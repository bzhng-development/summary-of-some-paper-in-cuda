# Configurable Foundation Models: Building LLMs from a Modular Perspective

**ArXiv:** [2409.02877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02877)

## 🎯 Pitch

LLMs already possess an inherent modular structure—even without special training, FFN neurons self-organize into sparse, functionally specialized partitions where 80% have near-zero impact per input and distinct subsets handle distinct skills like coding vs. math. This paper shows such \"emergent bricks\" can be surgically combined, updated, or expanded like plugins, promising LLMs that adapt per-task without running the full model.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **configurable foundation models**, a framework that decomposes monolithic LLMs into functional units called *bricks*—**emergent bricks** that arise spontaneously during pre-training as specialized neuron partitions, and **customized bricks** (plugins) that are deliberately constructed via post-training to inject new tasks, knowledge, or modalities. The authors formalize four brick-oriented operations—routing and retrieval (selecting relevant bricks per instruction, e.g., MoE gating), combination (merging bricks by weighted averaging or sequential stitching), updating (editing factual knowledge by locating and modifying specific neurons), and growing (expanding model capacity by adding new experts or layers)—and conduct an empirical analysis on Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, finding that FFN layers exhibit sparse activation (80% of neurons have near-zero impact on a given input), functional specialization (neurons highly predictive of specific capabilities like coding or math reach functionality scores above 0.8), and modular partitioning (distinct neuron subsets serve distinct capabilities with low cross-functionality overlap). The paper establishes that current LLMs already possess an implicit brick structure exploitable for efficiency and composability, positioning configurable foundation models as a paradigm shift from training monolithic models to collaboratively constructing and sharing functional bricks.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Monolithic LLMs Are Inefficient and Brittle

The fundamental issue this paper tackles is the **inflexibility of monolithic LLM architectures** in the face of three practical deployment trends. These trends are not hypothetical — they are already reshaping how LLMs are used and will only intensify as models become more capable:

1. **Deployment on end devices.** As LLMs move from centralized datacenters to smartphones, laptops, and wearables, the computational cost of running a full 7B+ parameter model for every query becomes prohibitive. A monolithic model that requires substantial GPU memory and FLOPs cannot feasibly serve as a personal assistant on a consumer device. The paper notes that "the use of monolithic LLMs that require substantial computational resources is gradually becoming infeasible" (Section 1). This is not merely about model compression for its own sake — it is about enabling LLMs to operate in resource-constrained environments where they can provide the most personal value.

2. **Widespread application across multiple domains.** Different domains, users, and even individual instructions demand different knowledge and capabilities. Storing all world knowledge in a single monolithic model and activating all parameters for every query leads to **redundant computation**: neurons responsible for French translation fire even when the user asks a Python programming question. Worse, knowledge from different domains can conflict. The paper points out that "conflicts between different domain knowledge may even result in sub-optimal performance" (Section 1). A model forced to reconcile contradictory information across domains in a shared parameter space may produce degraded outputs compared to a system that cleanly separates domain-specific processing.

3. **Rapid evolution in new scenarios.** The world changes continuously. New programming languages emerge, scientific discoveries render old facts obsolete, and user needs evolve. Monolithic models trained once and deployed statically cannot adapt — retraining a full model from scratch or even fine-tuning all parameters is computationally prohibitive at scale. The paper emphasizes that LLMs must "evolve efficiently and continuously, learning new knowledge and skills while avoiding forgetting existing knowledge" (Section 1). Catastrophic forgetting, where updating a model on new data degrades its performance on previously learned tasks, is a well-documented failure mode of full-parameter fine-tuning.

These three trends share a common root cause: **monolithic models couple computation to capacity**. Activating the model means activating everything it knows, regardless of relevance. Updating the model means risking destabilization of all its capabilities. The paper's central argument is that this coupling is an architectural choice, not a necessity — and that decomposing models into functional bricks can break it.

### Why This Problem Matters: Efficiency, Composability, and Sustainability

The practical implications extend beyond academic curiosity. Consider three concrete scenarios that motivate the research:

**Scenario 1: Personalized on-device assistants.** A user wants their phone's AI to handle coding questions (requiring Python and Java knowledge), translate occasional French messages, and compose emails in their personal style. A monolithic model must hold all these capabilities simultaneously and pay the full computational cost per query. A configurable model would store each capability as a separate brick — load the coding bricks when asked about Python, the translation brick for French messages, and the writing-style brick for emails — activating only what is needed. The paper estimates that only "around 5% of the neurons are with nonzero activation values for 90% of the input" (Section 2.1.1, citing prior work), suggesting massive potential efficiency gains.

**Scenario 2: Continuous knowledge updates without catastrophic forgetting.** When a new research paper invalidates a previously held scientific claim, a monolithic model requires either a costly full retraining or risks generating outdated information. A configurable model could isolate the specific knowledge brick responsible for that claim and update it independently, leaving other knowledge intact. The paper's discussion of knowledge editing — "editing the knowledge encoded in the emergent bricks of LLMs" by locating and modifying "knowledge neurons" (Section 3.3.1) — directly addresses this use case.

**Scenario 3: Collaborative model development.** Currently, improving an open-source LLM typically requires access to substantial compute and the full pretraining corpus. The configurable paradigm envisions a different workflow: a shared core model plus community-contributed bricks. A medical researcher could train a specialized diagnostic brick on private patient data, share the brick (not the data), and others could combine it with their own bricks for different tasks. The paper explicitly frames this as "a novel paradigm for developing foundation models, wherein the shift moves from training a whole model to training individual bricks" (Section 5.2).

Beyond these scenarios, the paper addresses a theoretical gap: while modularity is a foundational principle in neuroscience (the modularity of mind hypothesis), software engineering (component-based design), and industrial manufacturing (assembly from modular components), it has been largely neglected in the design of foundation models, which are overwhelmingly trained and deployed as monoliths (Section 1). The paper aims to bridge this gap by demonstrating that modularity can be both an analytical lens for understanding existing models and a design principle for building better ones.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies several lines of prior work that touch on modularity but each lacks a unified vision or practical framework.

#### Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT): Modules Without a System

Methods like adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019), LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), prefix tuning (Li & Liang, 2021), and prompt tuning (Lester et al., 2021) have demonstrated that task adaptation requires updating only a tiny fraction of parameters. These are the **customized bricks** in the paper's framework. The paper acknowledges this extensive literature (Section 2.2.3, Table 2) but identifies a critical shortcoming: these methods have been developed and studied **in isolation**. LoRA modules for coding, knowledge bricks for medical facts, and modality adapters for vision are all constructed using different training procedures, inserted at different points in the model, and evaluated on different benchmarks. There is no unified protocol for constructing, sharing, and composing them. As the paper states, "currently, different training methods, datasets, and insertion points are required for plugins with different capabilities. Discussing the construction of different types of plugins from a unified perspective could greatly benefit the future development of numerous plugins" (Section 2.2.5).

Moreover, PEFT methods are typically treated as **one-off adaptations** — train a LoRA module for task X, deploy it, done. The paper argues this underutilizes their potential. They should be viewed as persistent, reusable components in a growing library of capabilities: "the essence of fine-tuning LLMs is to customize bricks, which can fully supplement and stimulate knowledge and capabilities for LLMs to meet specific requirements" (Section 2.2).

#### Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): Modularity as a Byproduct, Not a Principle

MoE architectures (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022b) are the most prominent existing example of modular LLM design. Each expert is a sub-network (typically an FFN), and a gating mechanism routes each token to a subset of experts. The paper acknowledges that MoE "invisibly formalize LLMs into a pre-defined structure akin to combining bricks, where each expert functions as a distinct and specialized brick" (Section 2.1.2). Studies have shown that MoE experts do develop functional specialization — some handling syntax, others factual knowledge, etc. (Zhang et al., 2023c; Zoph et al., 2022).

However, the paper identifies two limitations of existing MoE work. First, **the modular structure is imposed architecturally**, not discovered. MoE models pre-allocate a fixed number of experts with uniform architecture, rather than allowing functional modules to emerge at varying granularities based on the data. This is a top-down design choice that may not match the natural modularity of the problem domain. Second, **MoE research has focused on scaling model capacity without increasing compute**, not on the broader vision of configurable models. The routing mechanism is typically a simple learned linear projection optimized for load balancing, not for semantic retrieval of relevant capabilities. The experts are not designed to be independently updated, shared across models, or combined in novel ways — they are inseparable components of a single trained model.

#### Model Analysis and Interpretability: Observations Without Operationalization

A substantial body of work has demonstrated that LLM parameters exhibit modular properties. The paper extensively reviews this literature (Section 2.1.1):

- **Knowledge neurons:** Dai et al. (2022) and Meng et al. (2022) showed that specific neurons in FFN layers store factual knowledge triples like (Paris, capital of, France), and manipulating these neurons can edit the model's factual knowledge.
- **Skill neurons:** Wang et al. (2022a) identified neurons whose activations are highly predictive of task labels, and perturbing them selectively degrades corresponding task performance.
- **Linguistic neurons:** Gurnee et al. (2023) and Voita et al. (2023) found neurons that correlate with linguistic features like n-grams, syntactic structure, and position.
- **Sparse activation:** Zhang et al. (2022c) and Li et al. (2023c) demonstrated that only a small fraction of neurons have non-zero activations for any given input — typically 5-10% in ReLU-based models.
- **Emergent modularity in MoE:** Zhang et al. (2023c) showed that MoE experts specialize into distinct functional roles (semantic understanding, knowledge storage, task execution) during pretraining.

These findings collectively suggest that LLMs already possess a latent brick structure. The problem, as the paper frames it, is that **this structure has been studied primarily for interpretation, not for operationalization**. Researchers locate knowledge neurons to understand *where* facts are stored, but they do not then package those neurons into a reusable "geography knowledge brick" that can be shared, updated, or combined with other bricks. The observation that models are modular has not translated into a practice of building models modularly.

#### Model Merging and Multi-Model Systems: Ad Hoc Composition

Recent work on model merging (Wortsman et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2023) and multi-model systems (Alayrac et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a) has demonstrated that models can be combined post-hoc to achieve composite capabilities. Weight averaging can create a model that performs well on multiple tasks by interpolating between fine-tuned variants. Multi-agent systems can have different LLMs play different roles, communicating through text.

The paper views these as positive existence proofs for composability but identifies two gaps. First, **the composition happens at the model level**, not at the sub-model level. Two entire fine-tuned models are merged, even though only a fraction of their parameters differ. Two entire LLM agents communicate, even though only a subset of their capabilities are relevant to the interaction. This is coarse-grained and wastes computation. Second, **these are ad hoc solutions**. There is no general framework for decomposing a complex instruction into sub-tasks, selecting appropriate bricks for each sub-task, and composing their outputs. The paper's brick operations — routing and retrieval, combination, updating, and growing — aim to provide this general framework.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's contribution is not a new model architecture, training algorithm, or benchmark. It is a **conceptual framework and a systematic taxonomy** that reframes existing fragmented research through the lens of configurable modularity. The authors are explicit about this:

> "In this paper, we offer a comprehensive overview and investigation of the construction, utilization, and limitation of configurable foundation models." (Abstract)

The paper positions itself as filling the gap between **observation and operationalization**. Prior work has observed that LLMs exhibit modular properties (sparse activation, functional specialization, expert routing). This paper asks: given that modularity exists, how do we build a practical system that exploits it? The answer involves four moves:

**First, rename and reframe.** The paper deliberately coins the term **brick** to replace the overloaded term *module*. A module in deep learning can mean a Transformer layer, an attention head, an adapter, an entire pre-trained model, or a functional sub-network — the ambiguity is counterproductive. A *brick* is defined precisely as "a collection of function-specific neurons" (Section 1, Table 1), with explicit acknowledgment that granularity can range from a single neuron to an entire model. This terminological clarity is not cosmetic — it enables the discussion of brick operations that apply across granularities.

**Second, unify two sources of modularity.** The paper introduces the emergent/customized distinction to reconcile two separate research traditions. The interpretability literature studies how modularity emerges spontaneously during pretraining (emergent bricks). The PEFT literature studies how modularity can be deliberately constructed after pretraining (customized bricks). The paper argues these are complementary halves of a single configurable model: emergent bricks provide the base capabilities, and customized bricks extend them. Neither tradition previously acknowledged the other as part of a unified system.

**Third, define the operations.** Prior work treated routing (in MoEs), merging (in model soups), editing (in knowledge editing), and growing (in continual learning) as independent research problems. The paper argues they are four fundamental operations on a shared brick abstraction, and that progress on each can inform the others. For example, a better understanding of brick specialization (from the emergent bricks literature) could improve routing decisions; better routing could improve brick combination; and so on.

**Fourth, provide empirical grounding.** The paper does not merely propose a taxonomy — it validates that the posited brick structure actually exists in current models. The experiments on Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Section 4) are designed to answer three skeptical questions: (1) Are neurons actually sparse? (Yes: 80% have near-zero impact, Figure 6-7.) (2) Do neurons specialize? (Yes: specific neurons achieve functionality scores above 0.8 for coding, math, and translation, Figure 8.) (3) Are specialized neurons partitioned into distinct groups? (Yes: neurons for different functionalities show low overlap, Figure 9-10.) These experiments transform the configurable model from an aspirational vision into something demonstrably grounded in current model behavior.

In summary, the paper's core intellectual move is to **convert a set of scattered empirical observations about LLM modularity into a coherent design paradigm with defined abstractions, operations, and evaluation criteria**. It does not claim to have solved all the problems — Section 5 is devoted to open challenges — but it provides the vocabulary and framework for the community to tackle them systematically rather than independently.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is primarily a **conceptual framework and empirical analysis paper** — it does not propose a new model architecture or training algorithm to be implemented from scratch. Instead, it builds a **taxonomy and operational framework** for understanding how Large Language Models can be decomposed into functional units called *bricks*, and defines four fundamental operations (routing, combining, updating, growing) that enable dynamic configuration of models by composing these bricks.

The problem it solves is the **fragmentation of modularity research** — observations about sparse activation, functional specialization, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and model merging exist in disconnected literatures. The "shape" of the solution is a **unified vocabulary and set of abstractions** that reinterprets existing techniques as instances of brick construction and brick operations, plus an empirical methodology for detecting brick-like structure in current models. The technical contribution is therefore **definitional, taxonomic, and analytical**, not algorithmic.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The paper defines a configurable foundation model as a system with four major component categories:

1. **Emergent Bricks** — Functional neuron partitions that arise spontaneously during pre-training. These are detected, not designed: they exist as sub-networks within the FFN layers of pre-trained models, discovered through activation analysis (e.g., knowledge neurons that encode factual triples, skill neurons that predict task labels, or MoE experts that specialize in semantic vs. syntactic processing). They form the base capability substrate.

2. **Customized Bricks (Plugins)** — Tiny neural modules deliberately constructed via post-training to inject new capabilities, knowledge, or modalities that the base model lacks. Examples include LoRA low-rank adapters for task adaptation, prefix embeddings for document knowledge, and cross-modal projectors for vision-language understanding. These are designed to be pluggable and reusable across different base models or tasks.

3. **A Brick Repository** — The library of all available bricks (both emergent and customized) that a configurable model can draw upon. For emergent bricks, this is the set of functional neuron groups identified within the model's existing parameters. For customized bricks, this is a growing collection of independently trained plugins.

4. **Brick Operations Engine** — Four fundamental operations that manipulate bricks to configure a model for a specific instruction:

   - **Routing and Retrieval:** Selecting which subset of bricks to activate for a given input, based on instruction requirements. For emergent bricks (e.g., MoE experts), this uses a learned gating function that scores each brick per token. For customized bricks (e.g., knowledge plugins), this uses embedding-based retrieval to find relevant external knowledge.

   - **Combination:** Fusing multiple bricks into a composite capability, either through parameter weighted averaging (element-wise interpolation of bricks with identical structure, e.g., merging two LoRA modules for different tasks) or brick stitching (sequential concatenation where the output of one brick feeds into another, e.g., an image encoder feeding into a language model).

   - **Updating:** Modifying the knowledge or behavior encoded in a brick without retraining everything else. For emergent bricks, this involves locating the specific neurons that encode a target fact and editing their weights. For customized bricks, this involves retraining or injecting a new plugin that overrides existing behavior.

   - **Growing:** Expanding the brick repository to accommodate new capabilities. In pre-training, this means adding new experts to an MoE model or increasing the width/depth of a dense model. In post-training, this means constructing a new customized brick for each new task or domain.

Information flows as follows: an instruction arrives → the routing/retrieval operation selects relevant bricks from the repository → bricks are combined (if multiple are needed for a composite task) → the selected bricks process the input → the output is produced. When new capabilities are needed or existing knowledge becomes outdated, the updating and growing operations modify the repository without disrupting other bricks.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

The remaining subsections unpack the framework in this order, which mirrors how a practitioner would engage with the paradigm:

- **First, the formal definition of bricks and how they are constructed** (§ 3.4.1–3.4.2): because before operating on bricks, we need to know what they are and where they come from. This covers the empirical evidence for emergent modularity (sparse activation, functional localization) and the training procedures for customized bricks (LoRA, adapters, knowledge plugins).

- **Second, brick granularity** (§ 3.4.3): because bricks can exist at different scales (single neuron, neuron group, layer, full model), and the choice of granularity determines what operations are meaningful and what computational tradeoffs exist.

- **Third, routing and retrieval** (§ 3.4.4): because this is the first operation applied in a configurable model — before computing anything, the system must decide which bricks to use. This covers trainable routers (MoE gating), fixed routers (hash-based, domain-based), and embedding-based retrieval for customized bricks.

- **Fourth, combination** (§ 3.4.5): because once bricks are selected, they must work together to produce a coherent output. This covers parameter weighted averaging (for homogeneous bricks) and brick stitching (for heterogeneous bricks), including how to choose weighting coefficients and how to determine stitching order via planners.

- **Fifth, updating** (§ 3.4.6): because after deployment, bricks become outdated and need modification. This covers knowledge editing (locating and modifying emergent knowledge neurons) and plug-and-play injection (adding new customized bricks that override old behavior).

- **Sixth, growing** (§ 3.4.7): because over time, entirely new capabilities are needed that no existing brick provides. This covers progressive parameter expansion during pre-training and construction of new task-specific plugins during post-training.

This order follows the lifecycle of a configurable model: define the bricks → select them → combine them → maintain them → expand them.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **conceptual and empirical framework paper** whose core idea is that decomposing LLMs into functional bricks — emergent partitions formed during pre-training and customized plugins constructed post-hoc — enables a new paradigm of configurable model usage where capabilities can be dynamically assembled, independently updated, and efficiently scaled.

---

#### 3.4.1 The Emergent Brick: How Functional Modularity Arises During Pre-Training

The paper's foundational observation is that pre-trained LLMs are not monolithic blobs of parameters — they internally organize into functionally specialized sub-networks through the pre-training process itself. The paper calls these **emergent bricks** and provides two converging lines of evidence for their existence: activation sparsity and function localization.

**Activation Sparsity: Only a Few Neurons Matter Per Input**

Activation sparsity refers to the empirical finding that for any given input token, only a small fraction of neurons in the FFN layers produce non-zero (or significantly non-zero) outputs. The "activation" of a neuron is its intermediate output after the non-linear activation function in the FFN. The paper defines this formally:

For a standard FFN layer computing `$\text{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = \text{FFN}_O(\text{FFN}_I(\mathbf{x})) = \mathbf{W}_O(\text{FFN}_I(\mathbf{x})) + \mathbf{b}_O$`, the intermediate representation `$\text{FFN}_I(\mathbf{x}) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{ff}}}$` is a vector of `$d_{\text{ff}}$` neuron activation values. "Sparsity" means that this vector is mostly zero (or near-zero) — for a ReLU-activated model, the paper cites prior work (Zhang et al., 2022c) showing that "only around 5% of the neurons are with nonzero activation values for 90% of the input" in a fine-tuned T5-Large model (Section 2.1.1).

For non-ReLU models (like modern Llama architectures using SiLU or GELU activations), the paper notes that activations are rarely exactly zero but many are close to zero. The paper's own experiments on Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (both using non-ReLU activations) confirm this: Figure 6(a) and 7(a) show the cumulative distribution function of normalized activation values across all 32 layers. The paper reports that "the normalized indicators for 80% neurons are lower than 0.2" (Section 4.3). This means the vast majority of neurons contribute minimal signal to the FFN output.

The practical significance: if 80% of neurons can be masked (their activations set to zero) with negligible loss increase, then for each input, the model is effectively using only ~20% of its FFN parameters. The paper experimentally verifies this: Figures 6(b) and 7(b) show that "when the proportion of masked neurons is low (<70% when using activation values as indicators, <80% when using output magnitudes as indicators), there is almost no decline in the model performance" (Section 4.3).

The paper also introduces a second sparsity metric: **output magnitude**, defined as `$||\text{FFN}(\mathbf{x})_i||_2$` — the L2 norm of the contribution of neuron `$i$` to the final FFN output. This directly measures impact on the output vector rather than intermediate activation values. Figures 6(c-d) and 7(c-d) show that output magnitude yields even higher apparent sparsity: more neurons have near-zero impact, and a higher percentage can be masked before performance degrades. The paper notes this finding "encourages further research to identify more effective indicators for assessing neuron usefulness" (Section 4.3).

**Why sparsity matters for the brick framework:** If neurons were uniformly activated for every input, decomposing the model into functional bricks would be pointless — you would need all bricks for every computation. Sparsity is the enabling condition: it means that only a small, input-dependent subset of parameters is actually working at any moment, which is exactly what you would expect if the model had organized into specialized bricks that activate conditionally.

**Function Localization: Specific Capabilities Live in Specific Neurons**

Beyond mere sparsity, the paper marshals evidence that the few active neurons are not randomly distributed — they are specialized for particular functions. The paper reviews three categories of localized functions found in prior work (Section 2.1.1):

**Knowledge neurons** (Dai et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2022): Factual associations like (Paris, capital of, France) are encoded in specific FFN neurons. The evidence is causal: zeroing the activation of these knowledge neurons suppresses the model's ability to recall the corresponding fact, while amplifying their activation strengthens it. The paper notes that "manipulating the activations or weights of these 'knowledge neurons' can effectively edit the knowledge-related predictions of LLMs" (Section 2.1.1).

**Skill neurons** (Wang et al., 2022a; Panigrahi et al., 2023): Neurons whose activations are highly predictive of whether the input belongs to a specific task type. These are discovered by training probes: for a given task (e.g., sentiment analysis), a classifier trained on a neuron's activation pattern across examples can predict the task label with high accuracy. Perturbing these neurons selectively degrades performance on that task while leaving other tasks unaffected — a direct demonstration of functional specialization.

**Linguistic neurons** (Gurnee et al., 2023; Voita et al., 2023): Neurons that correlate with surface-level linguistic features like n-gram patterns, syntactic structure, or token position. The paper also cites more recent work finding "language regions" in LLMs — clusters of neurons specialized for processing specific languages in multilingual models (Zhao et al., 2023a; Tang et al., 2024).

The paper's own experiments directly test functional specialization on Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 using seven capabilities: Coding, Math, Linguistic, Knowledge, Translation, Ethics and Moral, and Writing (Table 3). The methodology works as follows:

**Step 1: Define functionality labels.** For each chat instance `$(p_i, r_i)$` from the Infinity-Instruct dataset, assign a binary label `$y_i^f \in \{0, 1\}$` indicating whether the prompt `$p_i$` requires capability `$f$` to answer correctly. For example, "How can we select unique elements from a list in Python?" gets `$y_i^{\text{coding}} = 1$` and `$y_i^{\text{translation}} = 0$`. The paper manually selects 1,000 instances per capability that belong exclusively to one type (Section 4.2) to avoid confounding.

**Step 2: Compute per-neuron activation values.** For a given neuron `$n$` (one of the `$L \times d_{\text{ff}}$` neurons across all layers), collect its activation values across all tokens of all instances. For instance `$(p_i, r_i)$` with `$l_i$` tokens, the activation vector is `$\mathbf{A}_i = \{|a_{i,0}|, ..., |a_{i,l_i}|\}$`. The instance-level activation is the average of these absolute values.

**Step 3: Compute functionality score.** For each neuron and each capability, compute the Average Precision (AP) score:

$$\text{FuncScore}(n, f) = \text{AvgPrecision}(\{\mathbf{A}_0, ..., \mathbf{A}_n\}, \{y_0^f, ..., y_n^f\})$$

where `$\text{FuncScore}(n, f) \in [0, 1]$` is the functionality score of neuron `$n$` for capability `$f$`, `$\mathbf{A}_i$` is the average absolute activation of neuron `$n$` on instance `$i$`, and `$y_i^f$` is the binary label indicating whether instance `$i$` requires capability `$f$`.

**What it computes:** This is the area under the precision-recall curve when treating neuron activation as a predictor of whether the capability is needed. A high score means that when neuron `$n$` fires strongly, `$f$` is usually required, and when `$n$` is quiet, `$f$` is usually not required — the neuron's activation pattern is discriminative for that capability.

**Why this form:** Average Precision is used instead of simple correlation because it captures ranking quality — a neuron that consistently activates more strongly on capability-relevant inputs than on irrelevant inputs gets a high score even if the absolute activation values vary. Alternatives like Pearson correlation would assume a linear relationship, which is not expected for sparse binary-like activation patterns. AP is also threshold-free, avoiding arbitrary discretization choices.

**Findings:** Figure 8 shows the distribution of functionality scores across layers for both models. Key results:

- "The best functionality scores across these seven functionalities are significantly higher than those of randomly activated neurons and the mean functionality scores" (Section 4.4). For Coding, Math, and Translation, the best neurons achieve scores above 0.8 in most layers, indicating strong specialization.
- "The functionalities of Coding, Math, and Translation can achieve functionality scores higher than 0.8 in most layers" because "instructions for these functionalities require LLMs to understand or generate sequences distinctly different from the English natural language" — these are structurally distinct tasks that presumably recruit different neural circuits.
- There is a "large gap between the best functionality scores and the best 5‰ scores" — the drop-off is steep, meaning only a tiny handful of neurons per layer are highly predictive of each capability. This aligns with the activation sparsity finding: both the number of activated neurons per input AND the number of specialized neurons per capability are small.

The paper further validates specialization through a perturbation study (Figure 9). For each capability, the top 5% of neurons by functionality score are pruned (their activations set to zero), and the model's perplexity is evaluated on all seven capabilities. The results show that "values on the diagonal are generally higher than those off the diagonal" — pruning coding-specialized neurons increases perplexity on coding tasks (diagonal entry: +112% for Llama) much more than on other tasks (off-diagonal entries: +6-10%). This is a direct causal demonstration that the specialized neurons are functionally important for their associated capability and not others.

**Two Categories of Emergent Bricks: Human-Defined vs. Self-Organized**

The paper distinguishes between two types of emergent bricks based on how their structure arises (Section 2.1.2–2.1.3):

**Human-defined emergent bricks** follow the explicit architectural decomposition of the Transformer: attention heads, FFN layers, entire Transformer blocks. These are "explicitly designed by humans" (Section 2.1.2) — we know where each layer boundary is, where each attention head starts and ends. The paper argues that even though these are not designed with specific functional assignments, end-to-end training causes them to become functionally specialized: some layers handle syntactic parsing, others handle semantic composition; some attention heads attend to local context, others to long-range dependencies. The paper cites evidence that "Fan et al. (2020) show that it is possible to reduce the model computational costs by selecting only some of the layers" and "Michel et al. (2019) find that simply using one attention head can achieve comparable performance to the full model on certain tasks" (Section 2.1.2). Modular training — where different modules are trained separately with predefined functional roles — is an alternative approach exemplified by MoE models, where each expert is explicitly designated as a distinct brick with learnable specialization.

**Self-organized emergent bricks** do not respect human-defined architectural boundaries. They emerge from the interaction between multiple human-defined components during training. The canonical example (Zhang et al., 2022c): within a single FFN layer, some neurons consistently activate together while others remain inactive for the same inputs. These co-activating neurons form an implicit cluster — a self-organized brick — that functions as a collective unit. The paper notes that Zhang et al. (2023c) demonstrated these clusters "possess high productivity for specific functions and any perturbations to them can lead to drastic degradation of the performance in the corresponding function" (Section 2.1.3). Liu et al. (2023d) extended this to find "input-dependent subsets of neurons in feed-forward networks and attention heads to yield performance comparable to employing the entire model," suggesting cross-layer self-organized bricks that span both FFNs and attention mechanisms.

The distinction matters because self-organized bricks are typically **finer-grained and more functionally pure** than human-defined bricks. A single FFN layer (human-defined) may contain neurons for multiple unrelated functions, but a self-organized cluster within that layer is likely dedicated to a single function. This makes self-organized bricks more useful for precise operations like knowledge editing (update just the cluster, not the whole layer) and efficient inference (activate only the clusters needed, not the whole layer).

**How Granularity Determines Brick Capability**

The paper explicitly discusses brick granularity (Section 2.3) as a crucial design dimension, identifying four levels:

- **Solitary neuron granularity:** A single row/column in a weight matrix. Experiments show individual neurons can encode specific patterns (part-of-speech tags, word senses), factual knowledge triples, or even multiple related concepts (polysemous neurons). This is the finest possible decomposition but may be too brittle — "polysemous neurons capturing multiple concepts or word senses widely exist" (Section 2.3.1), meaning a single neuron often serves multiple functions, violating the "one brick, one function" ideal.

- **Neuron group granularity:** Collections of tens to thousands of neurons that function as a sub-layer. This includes MoE experts (each an entire FFN sub-layer), LoRA adapters (low-rank matrices acting on specific weight matrices), and the self-organized clusters described above. Neuron groups can display "more complex behaviors than solitary neurons" (Section 2.3.2) while still being small enough to be efficient. Most customized bricks operate at this granularity.

- **Layer granularity:** An entire Transformer layer. Layers show functional progression: lower layers handle local, lexical features; middle layers encode syntactic structure; higher layers capture task-specific semantics. This is a convenient abstraction for operations like early exiting (stop at an intermediate layer) or layer dropping, but it is coarse — each layer contains many functions.

- **Full model granularity:** Entire pre-trained models treated as bricks in a multi-model system. This is the level at which most existing multi-model work operates (e.g., Flamingo combining a vision encoder with an LLM, or multi-agent systems where each agent is a separate LLM).

The paper argues that "coarser-grained bricks with more parameters are better suited for addressing complex tasks" while finer-grained bricks handle more atomic functions (Section 2.3.5). The key open question is the mapping between granularity and capability: "Scaling laws, the empirical studies on the relationships between task performance, model scale, dataset size, and computation, may provide insights into the granularity-ability association" (Section 2.3.5). The paper also notes that granularities have "potential inclusive relationships" — a full model brick contains layer bricks, which contain neuron group bricks, which contain solitary neurons — suggesting a hierarchical brick organization where operations can target the appropriate level.

#### 3.4.2 The Customized Brick: Constructing Functional Modules Through Post-Training

While emergent bricks arise spontaneously during pre-training, **customized bricks** (also called *plugins*) are deliberately constructed after pre-training to add capabilities the base model lacks. The paper's key insight is that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), knowledge injection, and modality adaptation — typically studied as separate techniques — are all instances of the same underlying operation: building a small neural module that can be plugged into a frozen base model.

**Why Customized Bricks Are Possible: The Low Intrinsic Dimension of Fine-Tuning**

The feasibility of customized bricks rests on a critical empirical finding: LLM adaptation requires updating surprisingly few effective parameters. The paper formalizes this through the concept of **intrinsic dimension** (Section 2.2.1).

The intrinsic dimension of an optimization landscape is "the minimal number of free variables required to well define the optimization problem" (Section 2.2.1). In simpler terms: if you can find a low-dimensional subspace of the parameter space that contains a good solution, the problem's intrinsic dimension is at most the dimension of that subspace. Li et al. (2018) proposed estimating this by randomly projecting the full parameter space into lower-dimensional subspaces and checking whether any subspace contains parameters that achieve good performance.

Applied to LLM fine-tuning, Aghajanyan et al. (2021) found strikingly low intrinsic dimensions:

> "the fine-tuning of PLMs has a very low intrinsic dimension (∼200 for RoBERTa) and pre-training implicitly minimizes the intrinsic dimension. It has also been observed that larger models tend to have lower intrinsic dimensions." (Section 2.2.1)

A RoBERTa model with hundreds of millions of parameters can be meaningfully fine-tuned in a subspace of only ~200 dimensions. Larger models have even lower intrinsic dimensions. Qin et al. (2021) further found that "the many tasks can also be reparameterized into a shared universal low-dimensional subspace" — meaning there exists a common low-dimensional space where parameters for many different tasks all live.

**What this means for bricks:** If task adaptation requires navigating a ~200-dimensional space, then a brick with a few hundred parameters (rather than hundreds of millions) can, in principle, encode all the new capability needed. This is the theoretical justification for why tiny LoRA modules, adapters, and prefix embeddings can match full fine-tuning performance.

**Three Families of Customized Bricks Based on Structure**

The paper taxonomizes existing PEFT methods into three structural categories (Section 2.2.3), each representing a different way to parameterize the brick:

**1. Addition-based bricks** introduce new parameters into the model architecture. The two most prominent examples:

- **Adapter tuning** (Houlsby et al., 2019): Insert small bottleneck layers after attention or FFN sub-layers. An adapter consists of a down-projection `$\mathbf{W}_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$`, a non-linearity (typically ReLU or GELU), and an up-projection `$\mathbf{W}_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$`, where `$r \ll d$` is the bottleneck dimension (e.g., `$r=8$` or `$r=64$` for a `$d=4096$` model). For an input `$\mathbf{h} \in \mathbb{R}^d$`, the adapter computes `$\mathbf{h}' = \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{W}_{\text{up}}(\sigma(\mathbf{W}_{\text{down}}\mathbf{h}))$`. The residual connection ensures the adapter can learn the identity function, preserving pre-trained behavior.

- **Prompt tuning** (Lester et al., 2021): Instead of inserting parameters into layers, prepend learnable continuous embeddings `$\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times d}$` to the input sequence, where `$p$` is the prompt length (e.g., 20-100 tokens). The model processes these embeddings alongside the input tokens, and only these `$p \times d$` parameters are updated. This is "human interaction-friendly" because different prompts can be swapped without modifying model internals (Section 2.2.3).

**2. Specification-based bricks** do not add new parameters — they designate a subset of existing parameters as trainable while freezing the rest. The simplest example is **BitFit** (Zaken et al., 2022), which only optimizes the bias vectors `$\mathbf{b}$` inside all linear layers. In a Transformer, biases constitute a tiny fraction of total parameters (typically <1%), yet BitFit can recover most of full fine-tuning's performance. The paper notes that "when the model scales to billions of parameters, the performance gap introduced by the design differences becomes negligible" (Section 2.2.3) — meaning that arbitrarily selecting which existing parameters to train can work almost as well as carefully designed additions.

**3. Reparameterization-based bricks** rewrite existing layer computations into a parameter-efficient form. The canonical example is **LoRA** (Hu et al., 2022), which models the weight update `$\Delta\mathbf{W}$` for a pre-trained weight matrix `$\mathbf{W}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$` as a low-rank factorization:

$$\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}_0 + \Delta\mathbf{W} = \mathbf{W}_0 + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$$

where `$\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$` and `$\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$`, with `$r \ll \min(d, k)$` (e.g., `$r=8$` or `$r=16$`). Only `$\mathbf{A}$` and `$\mathbf{B}$` are trained; `$\mathbf{W}_0$` is frozen. The forward pass computes `$\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{W}_0\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$`.

**What this computes:** The original linear projection plus a low-rank correction. The correction is the composition of two narrow matrices — `$\mathbf{A}$` projects the input down to `$r$` dimensions, and `$\mathbf{B}$` projects back up to `$d$` dimensions. This constrains the update to live in an `$r$`-dimensional subspace, which is precisely the low-intrinsic-dimension property described above.

**Why this form:** The factorization exploits the empirical finding that weight updates during fine-tuning are low-rank. Full-rank updates would have `$d \times k$` degrees of freedom; LoRA uses only `$r \times (d + k)$` — for a typical `$4096 \times 4096$` matrix with `$r=16$`, this is ~131K parameters vs. ~16.8M, a >100x reduction. The factorization also enables efficient multi-task serving: different LoRA modules (`$\mathbf{A}_1, \mathbf{B}_1$` for task 1; `$\mathbf{A}_2, \mathbf{B}_2$` for task 2) can be swapped in/out without modifying the base model. The paper notes this as a key advantage: LoRA modules are "separately trained" and "can be combined for complex tasks" (Section 3.2).

**Training-free bricks** are a fourth category where no gradient updates are performed at all. Instead, task capability is encoded in the model's **representation space**. The paper cites recent work showing that "the demonstrations in in-context learning can be transformed into a function vector with simple representation arithmetic" — for example, subtracting the hidden state of a prompt without task instruction from the hidden state of the same prompt with task instruction yields a "task vector" (Liu et al., 2023c; Todd et al., 2023; Hendel et al., 2023). Adding this vector to the hidden states of a new input steers the model toward performing the task, without any weight modification. These representation-level bricks are the most lightweight possible — zero parameters, purely additive operations on activations.

**Knowledge Bricks: Packaging External Information as Parameters**

Beyond task capabilities, customized bricks can encode **knowledge** — facts, entities, domain expertise, or document content — in a pluggable form. The paper distinguishes between structured and unstructured knowledge bricks (Section 2.2.4):

**Structured knowledge graph (KG) bricks** encapsulate entity and relation information from knowledge bases like Wikidata. The core technical challenge is bridging the representation gap between KG embeddings (typically trained with translational objectives like TransE, where `$\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{r} \approx \mathbf{t}$`) and LLM token embeddings. The paper describes the approach of Zhang et al. (2023b): "learn a neural projection to bridge the gap between KG embeddings and token embeddings in pre-trained models." Formally, given a KG entity embedding `$\mathbf{e} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{KG}}}$`, a learned projector `$f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{KG}}} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$` maps it to the LLM's token embedding space, and the projected vector is prepended to the input sequence as additional token embeddings. The projector parameters `$\theta$` constitute the brick.

**Unstructured text bricks** encode the content of documents, articles, or other free-text sources. The paper describes two approaches. The first (Saad-Falcon et al., 2023) reuses intermediate activations: for a long document, the model processes it once and caches the hidden states from intermediate layers. During downstream inference, these cached activations are fed directly into upper layers, bypassing recomputation on the document text. The second (Xiao et al., 2023b) represents documents as learnable prefix tokens: "Xiao et al. (2023b) represent documents as prefix tokens and conduct self-supervised training to enable document bricks suitable for both inference and fine-tuning." The brick comprises a small set of embeddings `$\mathbf{P}_{\text{doc}} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times d}$` that, when prepended to any input, cause the model to condition on the document's content. Training uses a self-supervised objective where the model must reconstruct masked spans using the prefix as context.

**Modality Bricks: Bridging Perception and Language**

For multimodal capabilities, the paper describes two interface types for connecting pre-trained modality-specific models (vision encoders, audio processors) to LLMs (Section 2.2.5):

**Bricks with textual interface** convert non-textual data into text descriptions that the LLM can process natively. A video understanding system might use a captioning model to produce "a person is walking a dog in a park," then feed that text into the LLM alongside the user's question. Tool-augmented LLMs extend this: the LLM generates API calls to external models (e.g., "run object detection on image X"), receives structured text output, and reasons over it. The advantage is zero training — no parameter updates needed. The disadvantage is "inevitable information loss" from converting rich perceptual signals into text, which can "considerably impact the model's performance."

**Bricks with continuous interface** learn a neural projector that maps modality-specific features into the LLM's continuous representation space. The paper identifies two predominant architectures:

- **Attention-based bridging** (Alayrac et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023a): A set of learnable query vectors `$\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times d}$` cross-attend to the modality encoder's output features `$\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{mod}}}$`. The output of cross-attention is a fixed-size set of `$q$` vectors that can be inserted as "soft prompts" at any position in the LLM's input sequence. This adaptively extracts the most relevant information from variable-length modality features.

- **MLP-based bridging** (Liu et al., 2023a; Zhu et al., 2023): A simple multi-layer perceptron `$f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{mod}}} \to \mathbb{R}^{d}$` projects each modality feature individually. These projected features are then treated as additional token embeddings. The paper notes this is "powerful for modalities bridge" despite its simplicity.

The continuous interface eliminates information loss but introduces two challenges: (1) it requires multi-modal aligned training data (image-text pairs, audio-transcription pairs) to learn the projector, and (2) the projectors "sometimes suffer from fine-grained information loss" because their limited capacity (a few million parameters) must compress rich perceptual features into the LLM's representation space (Section 2.2.5).

**The Plugin Challenge: Independent Training Breeds Incompatibility**

The paper identifies a critical limitation of current customized brick practice: "since different types of plugins are trained independently, combining them during the inference stage can lead to out-of-distribution (OOD) problems" (Section 2.2.5). For example, a LoRA module trained to perform sentiment analysis was trained with the frozen base model as context; a knowledge brick encoding medical facts was trained with a different training distribution. When both are inserted simultaneously, the model encounters a combination of parameter modifications it never saw during training — the interactions between the bricks are unmodeled. The paper frames this as a key open challenge for brick combination operations (Section 3.2) and for developing "unified training strategy" where "different training methods, datasets, and insertion points" for bricks are designed for mutual compatibility (Section 2.2.5).

#### 3.4.3 Brick Granularity: The Efficiency-Capability Tradeoff

Bricks can exist at any scale from a single neuron to an entire model. The paper emphasizes that choosing granularity is a **design decision with computational and functional consequences** (Section 2.3), not a natural property of the model.

**Solitary neurons** (Section 2.3.1) are the finest meaningful unit — a single row in the input projection or column in the output projection of a linear layer. In Transformer FFNs, each neuron computes a scalar activation that gates a corresponding column of the output weight matrix. The paper cites extensive evidence that individual neurons acquire specific functions: Karpathy et al. (2015) found neurons that track word position; Dalvi et al. (2019) found neurons specialized for part-of-speech tags; Li et al. (2016) found neurons that activate for specific word categories like electronic items or legislative terms; and knowledge neurons (Dai et al., 2022) encode factual triples. However, the paper also notes that "polysemous neurons capturing multiple concepts or word senses widely exist" — a single neuron might fire for both financial "bank" and river "bank," as well as for certain syntactic patterns. This polysemy limits the utility of solitary-neuron bricks for clean functional decomposition.

**Neuron groups** (Section 2.3.2) aggregate multiple neurons into a functional sub-layer. The paper identifies two major organization forms:

- **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) experts:** Each expert is an independent FFN sub-layer (typically with `$d_{\text{ff}}/N_e$` neurons where `$N_e$` is the number of experts). A gating mechanism routes each token to `$k$` experts (typically `$k=1$` or `$k=2$`). The paper emphasizes that MoE experts naturally develop specialization — some handle syntax, others handle factual knowledge, others semantic composition — even without explicit functional supervision. This is the most extensively validated form of neuron group brick.

- **PEFT modules:** Adapters, LoRA matrices, and prefix embeddings all operate at neuron group granularity. A LoRA module with rank `$r$` modifies the equivalent of `$r$` virtual neurons across two layers. These are explicitly designed for a specific function (task, knowledge type), unlike MoE experts which develop specialization emergently.

**Layers** and **full models** are coarser bricks that the paper discusses but positions as less novel — these are already standard modular units in multi-model systems and layer-pruning work. The novel contribution is recognizing that these granularities exist on a continuum, and that operations like routing and combination can potentially be applied at any level.

The paper explicitly frames granularity selection as an unresolved problem: "the alignment of diverse granularity levels with specific abilities remains an open question" (Section 2.3.5). It suggests that scaling laws may provide guidance — just as larger models need more data to train optimally, larger bricks may be needed for more complex capabilities. The paper also notes the hierarchical relationship: "bricks of coarser granularities can be viewed as a combination of multiple finer-grained ones" — a full model brick contains layer bricks, which contain neuron group bricks, which contain solitary neurons. This suggests the possibility of **hierarchical brick management** where operations target the appropriate granularity based on the scope of the change needed (e.g., update a solitary neuron to fix one fact; grow a neuron group to add a new task; stitch two models for multimodal fusion).

#### 3.4.4 Routing and Retrieval: Selecting Which Bricks to Activate

The paper identifies routing and retrieval as the first and most fundamental brick operation — before any computation occurs, the configurable model must decide which subset of available bricks to engage (Section 3.1). The operation takes different forms depending on whether the bricks are emergent (internal to the model, limited in number) or customized (external, potentially vast in number).

**Emergent Brick Routing: Selecting Sub-Networks Per Token**

For emergent bricks (typically MoE experts or identified functional sub-networks), routing is a **per-token selection problem**. Given a repository of `$E$` experts (bricks), a routing function `$R: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^E$` maps each token representation `$\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$` to a score vector over experts. The `$k$` experts with the highest scores are activated, and their outputs are combined (typically through a weighted sum using softmax-normalized scores).

The paper distinguishes between trainable and fixed routing (Section 3.1.1):

**Trainable routing** is the standard in MoE architectures like Switch Transformer (Fedus et al., 2022b) and GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2021). The routing function is a learned linear projection `$\mathbf{W}_r \in \mathbb{R}^{E \times d}$`:

$$\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{W}_r\mathbf{x}$$

where `$\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^E$` is the vector of scores for each expert. The top-`$k$` operation selects the `$k$` highest scores. For `$k=1$` (Switch Transformer), only one expert is selected:

$$e^* = \arg\max_e s_e$$

**What this computes:** For each token, a weighted vote over which expert(s) should process it, where the weights are learned during training. The gating network learns to route tokens based on their content — tokens from code-heavy contexts might be routed to expert 3, tokens with factual queries to expert 7.

**Why this form:** A linear projection is the simplest parameterized function from token representation to expert scores. The top-`$k$` selection enforces sparsity explicitly — only `$k/E$` of the parameters are used per token, decoupling total model capacity from per-token computation. The paper notes a key challenge: "this method can lead to multiple tokens being assigned to the same brick, resulting in an imbalance of FLOPs among different bricks." Load balancing losses and auxiliary objectives are typically added to prevent expert collapse where the router sends all tokens to one expert.

Alternatives address the load balancing problem at the architectural level:
- **Expert choice routing** (Zhou et al., 2022a): Instead of tokens choosing experts, each expert chooses its top tokens. This naturally balances load because each expert is assigned exactly the same number of tokens.
- **Soft MoE** (Puigcerver et al., 2023): Instead of hard top-`$k$` selection, use soft slots that compute weighted combinations of all token representations, then dispatch these slot representations to experts. This eliminates the discrete routing decision entirely.
- **Recurrent routing** (Qiu et al., 2024): Use a recurrent network across layers to make routing decisions that are consistent across depth, preventing the model from sending the same token to completely different expert sets at different layers.

**Fixed routing** eliminates learnable parameters entirely. The paper describes three approaches:

- **Hash-based routing** (Roller et al., 2021): A deterministic hash function `$h(\mathbf{x})$` maps each token to an expert index. The hash is pre-computed and perfectly balanced by construction.
- **Random routing** (Zuo et al., 2022a): Each token is randomly assigned to an expert. The paper cites the finding that "the behavior of route functions in SwitchTransformer is akin to random routing" — surprisingly, learned routing often degrades to near-random behavior during training.
- **Domain-based routing** (Gururangan et al., 2022): Tokens are routed based on the domain label of their document (e.g., legal, medical, news). This provides interpretable expert specialization but "requires domains of nearly equal size to maintain a balance across the different bricks."

The paper notes that trainable routing, despite its theoretical advantages, suffers from a fundamental explainability problem: "fully explaining the routing behavior remains a challenging task." You can see which expert was selected but not clearly understand *why*, which makes debugging and controlled manipulation difficult.

**Customized Brick Retrieval: Finding Relevant External Knowledge**

For customized bricks (especially knowledge bricks), retrieval addresses a different problem: the brick repository may be **enormous** (e.g., millions of entity bricks from Wikidata), and the goal is to retrieve the few bricks most relevant to the current input (Section 3.1.2).

**Entity-level retrieval** is the most common approach. For a structured KG, when an input mentions an entity (e.g., "Albert Einstein"), the system retrieves the corresponding entity brick that encodes Einstein's properties, relations, and context. The paper describes the mechanism: "entity linking as a retrieval method to incorporate specific entity brick into the current model" (Section 3.1.2). A linking model (trained separately) identifies entity mentions in text, maps them to KG entity IDs, and the corresponding pre-computed entity embedding is fetched and prepended to the input.

**Dense retrieval** is used when the brick repository is unstructured (e.g., Wikipedia paragraphs encoded as bricks). Cheng et al. (2023) use Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS): the input is encoded into a query vector `$\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^d$`, and the brick whose representation `$\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^d$` maximizes `$\mathbf{q}^\top\mathbf{b}$` is retrieved. The representations are learned so that relevant bricks are geometrically close to their associated queries.

**Task-level retrieval** (Zhao et al., 2024b) operates at a higher granularity: for a given input, identify which previously trained task bricks (LoRA modules for different tasks) are most relevant. The paper describes this as a "retrieve-then-compose framework" where "the model first retrieves several related task bricks based on the input instructions and averages these bricks for final computation." This treats task selection as a retrieval problem over a library of task-specific bricks.

**Granularity of Routing Decisions**

The paper identifies three granularity levels for routing/retrieval decisions (Section 3.1.3):

- **Token-level**: Decisions per token, as in standard MoE. This provides maximum flexibility — a single sentence about Python and French can route Python-related tokens to coding experts and French tokens to translation experts. The cost is "significant computation and communication costs when applied to every token and every layer."

- **Sentence-level**: A single routing decision for the entire input sequence. Domain-based routing (Gururangan et al., 2022) and instance-level task retrieval (Zhao et al., 2024b) operate at this level. This is more efficient but cannot handle mixed-capability inputs.

- **Task-level**: Decisions made once before training or inference for an entire task. This is appropriate when an entire dataset or deployment scenario requires the same capability — you pre-select the relevant bricks once and use them for all queries.

The paper argues that future configurable models will need **multi-level routing** that combines these granularities: "token-level information is empirically used in emergent bricks, while sentence-level and task-level information is more relevant to customized bricks. Chen et al. (2022) have made preliminary attempts to incorporate task-level information and token-level information into routing various emergent bricks" (Section 3.1.4).

The paper also introduces the distinction between **passive routing** (decisions made in advance at predetermined positions — e.g., always retrieve entity bricks at entity mentions) and **active routing** (the model itself decides when and where to retrieve). Active routing "can also address the efficiency problem by significantly reducing the frequency of retrieval" and "investigate whether current LLMs can determine when to retrieve bricks." This connects to recent work on tool-using LLMs that decide during generation when to call external APIs.

#### 3.4.5 Combination: Fusing Multiple Bricks into Composite Capabilities

The routing operation selects bricks; the combination operation makes them work together. The paper distinguishes two combination methods based on whether the bricks have identical structures (homogeneous) or different structures (heterogeneous) (Section 3.2).

**Parameter Weighted Averaging for Homogeneous Bricks**

When multiple bricks share the same architecture (e.g., two LoRA modules of the same rank, or two fine-tuned versions of the same model), they can be combined through element-wise weighted averaging of their parameters:

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{merged}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \boldsymbol{\theta}_i$$

where `$\boldsymbol{\theta}_i$` is the parameter vector of brick `$i$`, `$w_i \in \mathbb{R}$` is its combination weight, and `$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{merged}}$` is the merged parameter vector.

**What this computes:** A new brick whose parameters are a convex combination (or, more generally, a linear combination) of the source bricks' parameters. If brick A is good at sentiment analysis and brick B is good at topic classification, the merged brick `$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{merged}} = 0.5\boldsymbol{\theta}_A + 0.5\boldsymbol{\theta}_B$` should be decent at both.

**Why this works (when it works):** The paper invokes the concept of **linear mode connectivity** (Frankle et al., 2020): "if two models are initialized from the same well-trained parameters, a straightforward linear weighting can enable the merged model to exhibit superior performance." When two bricks are fine-tuned from the same pre-trained checkpoint, their loss landscapes are connected by a low-loss path — the linear interpolation between their parameters lies in a region of low loss. This is not true for models trained from different random initializations, which is why "linearly weighting parameters from two distinct training processes usually fail to yield satisfactory results" (Section 3.2.1).

**How weights are determined:** The paper describes several methods:

- **Uniform averaging** (`$w_i = 1/n$`): The simplest approach, used in Model Soups (Wortsman et al., 2022) for merging models fine-tuned with different hyperparameters. Works well when bricks are of similar quality and contribute complementary abilities.

- **Fisher-weighted averaging** (Matena & Raffel, 2022): Weight each parameter by its Fisher information — parameters that were important during brick `$i$`'s training get higher weight in the merge for that brick's contribution. This prevents a poorly-tuned brick from degrading parameters crucial to a well-tuned brick.

- **Optimization-based weighting** (Huang et al., 2023a; Jin et al., 2023): Treat the merging weights `$w_i$` as optimizable variables. Huang et al. (2023a) use combinatorial optimization to find weights that maximize performance on a target task; Jin et al. (2023) minimize the L2 distance between the merged parameters and the source parameters to keep the merged model close to all sources.

- **Task arithmetic** (Ilharco et al., 2023): Beyond addition, subtraction is also useful. Zhang et al. (2023a) "perform detoxification by subtracting a brick trained from toxicity instructions": `$\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{clean}} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{base}} - \lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{toxic}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{base}})$`. Similarly, Daheim et al. (2023) "mitigate hallucinations by subtracting a brick trained on hallucinated examples." This operationalizes the idea that capabilities can be added and removed through simple arithmetic on brick parameters.

**Brick Stitching for Heterogeneous Bricks**

When bricks have different architectures — an image encoder (CNN/ViT), a language model (Transformer decoder), and a speech recognizer (conformer) — parameter averaging is impossible. Instead, **brick stitching** connects them sequentially: the output of brick A becomes the input (or part of the input) for brick B (Section 3.2.2).

The core challenge is the **interface**: brick A's output representation must be translated into a form that brick B can consume. The paper identifies two interface types, already discussed in the context of modality bricks (Section 2.2.5):

- **Textual interface:** Brick A produces text, brick B reads text. This requires no training but loses information. For multimodal models, a captioning model converts images to text descriptions; the LLM reads the descriptions alongside the user query.

- **Continuous interface:** A learned projector maps brick A's output features into brick B's input embedding space. For a vision encoder producing features `$\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{vis}}}$` and an LLM expecting embeddings `$\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_{\text{LLM}}}$`, a projector `$g_\phi: \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{vis}}} \to \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{LLM}}}$` maps each visual feature to an LLM-compatible embedding. The projector parameters `$\phi$` constitute the interface brick.

**How stitching order is determined:**

- **Heuristic stitching:** The order is manually designed based on the inference pipeline. For visual question answering: vision encoder → projector → LLM → text decoder. For multi-agent systems: Agent 1 (planner) → Agent 2 (executor) → Agent 3 (critic). The paper notes this is "generally adopted for concatenating model-level bricks, which can independently accomplish specific inference steps" (Section 3.2.2). For finer-grained bricks, arbitrary concatenation typically fails because "fine-grained bricks tend to have abstract functions and are usually dependent on surrounding bricks." The paper mentions that Pan et al. (2023) successfully stitched layer-level bricks from models of different sizes by exploiting the linear transferability of hidden spaces between same-architecture models, and Akiba et al. (2024) use evolutionary search to discover optimal stitching architectures.

- **Planner-based stitching:** The stitching order is dynamically determined per instruction by a planner module. The paper describes the three-component architecture: (1) a **task planner** "responsible for decomposing an instruction into several sub-tasks"; (2) a **controller** "tasked with generating and receiving the signals for each sub-task and ultimately produces the final answer"; and (3) **multiple bricks** "where each brick handles a specific type of sub-task" (Section 3.2.2).

The planner can operate in two modes:

**Pre-execution planning:** The planner generates the full execution sequence before any bricks are invoked. This requires functional descriptions of each brick — the paper cites Hsieh et al. (2023) and Shen et al. (2023c), where the planner is prompted with documentation of available tools/bricks and their input-output specifications, then outputs a plan like "first use image captioning brick, then use knowledge retrieval brick, then use summarization brick."

**Reactive planning** (Yao et al., 2023a; Gao et al., 2023): The planner decides the next brick to call based on intermediate results. After the image captioning brick returns "a dog playing in a park," the planner might determine that the question "what breed is the dog?" requires a fine-grained visual analysis brick rather than a general knowledge brick. This is more robust but introduces sequential dependencies that increase latency.

The paper also discusses **search-based stitching** (Ye et al., 2023; Qin et al., 2023) where the planner generates multiple candidate brick sequences, tries them sequentially, and selects the one that produces the best result. This trades computation for reliability — analogous to beam search over brick sequences.

**Open challenges:** The paper highlights two major difficulties for brick combination. First, **combining fine-grained heterogeneous bricks** at the sub-model level remains largely unexplored — most heterogeneous stitching operates at the model level. The paper suggests that "parameter redundancies also exist between different models, for instance, both language and image models internally possess neural bricks responsible for understanding real-world concepts" (Section 3.2.3), implying that cross-modal fine-grained stitching could be more parameter-efficient. Second, the need for a **universal brick interaction interface** — a standardized input-output format that any brick can implement, ensuring arbitrary bricks can be connected without custom engineering.

#### 3.4.6 Updating: Modifying Brick Contents to Reflect Changing Knowledge

Knowledge in the world changes — historical facts are revised, scientific theories are updated, new entities emerge. The updating operation modifies existing bricks to keep them current without retraining the entire model. The paper focuses primarily on knowledge bricks, acknowledging that "the capabilities of other widely used bricks such as task and modality rarely require frequent updates" (Section 3.3.3).

**Locate-and-Update: Editing Emergent Knowledge Bricks In-Place**

The dominant paradigm for updating emergent bricks involves two phases: first localize which neurons encode the target knowledge, then modify those neurons' parameters (Section 3.3.1).

**Phase 1: Locating Knowledge Bricks.** The paper describes how the FFN-as-key-value-memory hypothesis (Geva et al., 2021) underpins localization. In this view, each FFN layer computes:

$$\text{FFN}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{\text{ff}}} \sigma(\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{k}_i) \cdot \mathbf{v}_i$$

where `$\mathbf{k}_i$` (a row of the input weight matrix) is a "key" that detects patterns in the input, and `$\mathbf{v}_i$` (a column of the output weight matrix) is the "value" that gets added to the representation when the key is activated. A knowledge tuple (subject, relation, object) is stored when: (1) a key `$\mathbf{k}_i$` fires strongly for the subject entity's representation, and (2) the corresponding value `$\mathbf{v}_i$` encodes information that biases the output toward the object entity.

The paper cites two localization methods:
- **Integrated gradients** (Dai et al., 2022): Attribute the model's prediction of the object (e.g., "Paris" for the prompt "The capital of France is") to individual neurons by integrating gradients along the path from a baseline input to the actual input. Neurons with high attribution scores are designated as knowledge neurons.
- **Causal intervention** (Meng et al., 2022): Systematically corrupt the activation of individual neurons and measure the impact on factual recall. If zeroing neuron `$i$` causes the model to fail at recalling "Paris" but not other facts, neuron `$i$` is causally implicated in storing that specific fact. Meng et al. (2022) found that "middle-layer FFNs are most responsible for fact recalling."

The paper notes a critical limitation revealed by Hase et al. (2023): "causal intervention identifies which brick carries the target knowledge, but manipulating the parameters of these bricks does not lead to superior performance." The neurons that *express* a fact (identified by causal intervention) may not be the ones where the fact is *stored* (where parameter editing is effective). This suggests "knowledge storage of even one entity or tuple involves multiple neurons and defining knowledge bricks at the neuron-group level and layer level may be more suitable" (Section 3.3.1).

**Phase 2: Updating Knowledge.** After locating the target neurons, the update replaces their value vectors `$\mathbf{v}_i$` to encode the new knowledge. The paper describes the approach of Meng et al. (2022) in detail:

Given a target fact `$(s, r, o^*)$` to be inserted (e.g., "The capital of France is Marseille" instead of Paris), and a set of unrelated facts `$(s_j, r_j, o_j)$` that should be preserved, the update finds a new weight matrix `$\hat{\mathbf{W}}_O$` (output projection of the FFN) that satisfies:

$$\hat{\mathbf{W}}_O = \arg\min_{\mathbf{W}} \sum_{j} \|\mathbf{W}\mathbf{k}_j - \mathbf{v}_j\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{W}\mathbf{k}_{s,r} = \mathbf{v}_{o^*}$$

where `$\mathbf{k}_j$` are the key vectors for preserved facts, `$\mathbf{v}_j$` are their corresponding values, `$\mathbf{k}_{s,r}$` is the key vector for the subject-relation pair being edited, and `$\mathbf{v}_{o^*}$` is the desired new value encoding the updated object.

**What this computes:** The minimum-norm update to the weight matrix that inserts the new fact while minimally perturbing the representation of other facts. The constraint ensures the new fact is encoded; the objective preserves existing knowledge.

**Why this form:** This is a constrained least-squares problem with a closed-form solution that can be computed efficiently without iterative optimization. The L2 norm objective minimizes parameter drift — the updated weights stay as close as possible to the original weights while satisfying the editing constraint. However, the paper notes that "a small change in the parameter space might not translate to a small change in the function space," which is why some methods use a KL divergence loss "with adversarial examples to minimize the change of predictions on unrelated inputs after the update" (Onoe et al., 2023; Padmanabhan et al., 2023; Mitchell et al., 2022a). This addresses the problem that a parameter update that is small in L2 norm might still significantly alter the model's behavior on inputs that share features with the edited fact.

**Injecting New Customized Bricks as an Alternative**

Instead of surgically editing emergent bricks, an alternative approach is to inject a new customized brick that overrides the outdated knowledge (Section 3.3.2). This avoids the localization problem entirely — you don't need to find which neurons store the old fact; you just add new parameters that encode the correct fact and route queries to them.

The paper describes several representative methods:
- **Entity-level injection** (dos Santos et al., 2022): Train a new entity embedding for the updated entity and prepend it to the hidden states after the input layer. When the updated entity is detected in the input (via entity linking), its new embedding overrides whatever the base model would have retrieved from its internal parameters.
- **Neuron-level injection** (Huang et al., 2023c): "Insert and train a new FFN neuron for every knowledge update." This directly adds a new key-value pair to the FFN memory — a new neuron whose key detects the subject entity and whose value encodes the updated object.
- **Representation-level injection** (Hernandez et al., 2023; Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023): Instead of modifying weights, modify activations. A "steering vector" is computed as the difference between the hidden representations evoked by two prompts — one that elicits the correct fact and one that elicits the incorrect fact. Adding this steering vector to the hidden states at inference time nudges the model toward the correct fact without any weight changes.

The paper notes that these injection methods "generally does not require knowledge attribution of neurons" and the bricks "are created on demand" — advantages over locate-and-edit for rapid, frequent updates where precise neuron localization would be too expensive.

#### 3.4.7 Growing: Expanding the Brick Repository for New Capabilities

While updating modifies existing bricks, **growing** adds entirely new bricks to the repository — either to increase the model's total capacity during pre-training or to accommodate new tasks and domains during post-training (Section 3.4). The paper identifies this as essential for "continual learning strategies aiming at enabling LLMs to acquire information from new data effectively and efficiently" while "avoiding catastrophic forgetting of existing capabilities."

**Growing During Pre-Training: Scaling Model Capacity**

The paper describes two paradigms for expanding a pre-trained model's parameter count without training from scratch:

**Dense parameter expansion** (Section 3.4.1) increases the width (hidden dimension `$d$`) or depth (number of layers `$L$`) of an existing dense model. The key challenge is initializing the new parameters so that the expanded model performs at least as well as the original, providing a good starting point for further training.

The paper describes the approach of Wang et al. (2023c; LiGO): "employs a linear mapping of parameters from an existing model to initialize a model with increased width and depth." Formally, if `$\mathbf{W}_{\text{old}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$` is a weight matrix in the original model, and the expanded model has dimensions `$d_{\text{out}}' > d_{\text{out}}$` and `$d_{\text{in}}' > d_{\text{in}}$`, then the new weight matrix is initialized as:

$$\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{W}_{\text{old}}\mathbf{B}$$

where `$\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}' \times d_{\text{out}}}$` expands the output dimension and `$\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{in}}'}$` expands the input dimension. These expansion matrices are learned to preserve the functional mapping — applying `$\mathbf{W}_{\text{new}}$` to appropriately padded inputs should produce approximately the same outputs as `$\mathbf{W}_{\text{old}}$`.

Wu et al. (2024) take a simpler approach: "frozen the original parameters and only train the expanded parameters with new training corpus to avoid forgetting." New parameters are initialized as identity or zero, so the model initially behaves identically to the original. Training only the new parameters ensures that old capabilities, encoded in frozen parameters, are never degraded.

**Sparse expert expansion** leverages MoE architecture: instead of expanding a single dense FFN, add new experts to the existing MoE layer. The paper describes how this is done in practice: "the growing operation is performed by increasing the number of experts and keeping the parameters of other layers and experts fixed." During inference, "each token will only select the most related experts for computation" (Section 3.4.1) via the unchanged routing mechanism. The existing experts continue handling the types of tokens they already specialize in; the new experts learn to handle tokens from the new domain. This architecture naturally avoids catastrophic forgetting because old experts' parameters are frozen.

Key examples cited: Li et al. (2022) trained domain-specific language models by starting with a base MoE and adding new experts for each new domain; Komatsuzaki et al. (2023) showed that dense checkpoints can be "sparsely upcycled" into MoE models by replicating the FFN into multiple experts and fine-tuning; Shen et al. (2023b) used MoE growth for continual domain adaptation.

**Growing During Post-Training: Continual Task Learning**

For post-training scenarios where new tasks arrive sequentially, the paper advocates **brick-per-task** approaches: construct a new customized brick for each new task rather than fine-tuning the entire model (Section 3.4.2).

The paper describes two mechanisms for managing multiple task bricks:

- **Episodic memory bricks** (Isele & Cosgun, 2018; Han et al., 2020): Maintain a small memory of representative examples from old tasks. When training on a new task, interleave the new task data with episodes (mini-batches) from the memory brick to rehearse old knowledge, preventing forgetting. The memory brick size is typically small (a few hundred examples per task) but sufficient for rehearsal.

- **Router-based selection** (Madotto et al., 2021; Song et al., 2023a): Train a router alongside each new task brick. The router learns to predict, given an input, which task brick should process it. During inference, the router selects the appropriate brick, and only that brick's parameters are used (alongside the frozen base model). This is effectively MoE routing applied to task-level bricks: each task brick is an "expert," and the router is the gating function.

The paper also discusses **progressive networks** (Mahabadi et al., 2021; Razdaibiedina et al., 2023) where each new task gets its own brick that receives inputs not only from the base model but also from previous task bricks, enabling positive transfer without interference.

**Factors Determining How to Grow**

The paper concludes the growing section with three decision criteria (Section 3.4.3):

- **Task complexity:** For simple additions (recognizing a new entity, using a new tool), a small plugin brick suffices. For injecting a large volume of new knowledge or substantially boosting general capability, larger-scale growth (e.g., adding multiple new MoE experts or increasing model depth) is appropriate.

- **Computation budget:** Though growing is more efficient than training from scratch, it is not free. MoE expert addition has minimal inference overhead (only the new expert is activated for relevant tokens); dense expansion increases per-token cost for all inputs. The paper notes that "strategies such as plugins, and sparse MoE architectures are representative approaches to model growth within acceptable expenses."

- **Application targets:** "Lightweight plugins prove highly advantageous in scenarios emphasizing user-oriented customization" (e.g., personal task adapters). For scaling "to achieve heightened general AI capabilities, the introduction of a more extensive parameter set through a direct increase in width and depth or other sophisticated architectures becomes imperative."

#### 3.4.8 Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

The paper makes several key architectural decisions in framing its brick-based paradigm:

- **Accepting both emergent and customized bricks rather than choosing one:** The paper explicitly argues that modularity has two complementary sources — spontaneous functional differentiation during pre-training (emergent) and deliberate construction of specialized modules post-hoc (customized). Neither alone is sufficient. Emergent bricks provide the base capability substrate but are hard to create de novo for new tasks; customized bricks are flexible but rely on the base model's frozen representations. Treating both as equal citizens in a brick repository enables a system that benefits from both self-organization and engineering.

- **Defining bricks as function-specific neuron collections rather than architectural units:** By anchoring the definition of a brick to functional specialization rather than architectural boundaries (layers, heads, etc.), the paper enables operations that cross architectural boundaries — a self-organized brick might span multiple layers, and a knowledge brick might be a subset of neurons within a single FFN layer. This functional definition is what makes the framework general enough to encompass both MoE experts (architecturally defined) and knowledge neurons (emergent partitions within an architectural unit).

- **Granularity-agnostic operations:** The four operations (routing, combining, updating, growing) are defined independently of brick granularity. A routing operation can select a single neuron, a neuron group, a layer, or a full model — the mechanism differs (top-k gating for neurons, dense retrieval for plugins, planner-based selection for models) but the abstraction is unified. This enables hierarchical configuration where operations target different granularities depending on the need.

- **Separating brick construction from brick utilization:** The paper cleanly separates the *construction* problem (Section 2: how bricks come to be) from the *operations* problem (Section 3: how bricks are used). This separation is methodologically important because it allows the operations framework to remain valid even as construction techniques evolve — new PEFT methods, new knowledge editing algorithms, or new MoE training recipes produce bricks that still participate in the same four operations.

- **Empirical validation on current models rather than requiring new architectures:** The paper's experiments on Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 demonstrate that the brick structure already exists in widely-deployed models. This is a strategic choice that makes the framework immediately applicable — you don't need to train a new "configurable model from scratch"; you can decompose an existing model using the analysis techniques in Section 4 and start operating on the discovered bricks.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Operationalizing LLM Modularity — From Isolated Observations to a Unified Operations Framework

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is converting a scattered set of empirical observations about LLM modularity into an **actionable design paradigm with defined operations**. This is not an incremental refinement of any single technique — it is a conceptual reframing that changes the question from "what modular properties do LLMs exhibit?" to "how can we systematically exploit those properties to build more efficient, composable, and sustainable models?"

**The pre-existing landscape was fragmented.** Prior to this paper, evidence for LLM modularity existed in disconnected research silos. The interpretability community had demonstrated sparse activation (Zhang et al., 2022c; Li et al., 2023c), knowledge neurons (Dai et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2022), skill neurons (Wang et al., 2022a), and emergent expert specialization in MoE models (Zhang et al., 2023c). The parameter-efficient fine-tuning community had developed LoRA (Hu et al., 2022), adapters (Houlsby et al., 2019), prefix tuning (Li & Liang, 2021), and a dozen other methods for constructing tiny task-specific modules. The model merging community had shown that fine-tuned variants could be averaged (Wortsman et al., 2022) or arithmetically combined (Ilharco et al., 2023). The knowledge editing community had developed locate-and-edit methods (Meng et al., 2022, 2023) for updating facts in place. The continual learning community had explored expert expansion for domain adaptation (Gururangan et al., 2022; Li et al., 2022).

These communities operated largely independently, with different terminologies, evaluation protocols, and assumptions about what "modularity" meant. A knowledge neuron paper did not cite LoRA papers; a model merging paper did not engage with MoE routing work. There was no shared vocabulary and no recognition that these were all manifestations of the same underlying phenomenon.

**The paper's reframing is the innovation.** By coining the term **brick** — "a functional unit composed of a group of function-specific neurons" (Table 1) — and taxonomizing all existing modular structures into exactly two categories (emergent bricks that arise during pre-training, customized bricks constructed post-hoc), the paper creates a **shared abstraction** that unifies these communities. More importantly, it defines **four primitive operations** — routing/retrieval, combination, updating, and growing — that apply across all brick types and granularities. This is the critical conceptual leap: modularity is not just a property to be observed, but a substrate to be *operated on*. The operations framework implies that progress on any one operation (e.g., better MoE routing) can benefit the others (e.g., more precise brick combination), because they share the same underlying abstraction.

**Why this matters beyond the paper.** The operations framework is a **generative research agenda**, not a closed contribution. It identifies what the field *can* do now (route MoE experts, merge LoRA modules, edit knowledge neurons) and what it *cannot* yet do (combine fine-grained heterogeneous bricks across modalities, perform universal brick interaction, actively route at multiple granularities). Each "cannot" is a well-defined research problem. This is what distinguishes a framework that merely taxonomizes existing work from one that organizes future work — Section 5's open problems emerge directly from the four operations, not from a generic "more research is needed" statement.

**Evidence:** The framework's explanatory power is validated by mapping diverse prior work into its categories (Table 2). Every row in that table — from activation sparsity analysis to modality bridge training to evolutionary model merging — is assigned a position in the brick construction/operation taxonomy. If the framework were ad-hoc or forced, this mapping would produce contradictions or gaps. The paper's ability to cleanly situate over 100 cited works within its vocabulary without apparent strain is indirect evidence of the framework's coherence.

**What distinguishes this from incremental advances.** The paper does not propose a new training algorithm, a new model architecture, or a new benchmark. Its contribution is the *language and conceptual structure* for thinking about modularity. This is a rarer and more difficult contribution type — it requires not just technical skill but the ability to see commonality across disparate research threads and distill it into clean abstractions. The closest analog is the introduction of the "pre-train then fine-tune" paradigm that organized NLP research in the BERT era, or the "scaling laws" framework that gave the field a vocabulary for reasoning about compute allocation. Both were conceptual contributions that outlasted individual architectures.

### Innovation 2: The Emergent/Customized Distinction as a Unifying Lens on Two Sources of Modularity

The paper's second key insight is that modularity in LLMs has **two fundamentally different origins**, and conflating them has been a source of confusion in the literature. By cleanly separating emergent bricks (arising spontaneously from the pre-training objective) from customized bricks (deliberately constructed via post-training), the paper resolves apparent contradictions between research findings and provides a unified account of how LLMs can be simultaneously modular-by-nature and modular-by-design.

**The confusion in prior work.** Before this paper, it was unclear whether LLM modularity was something you discovered or something you built. The knowledge neuron literature (Dai et al., 2022) suggested that factual knowledge stored in specific neurons was an emergent property of pre-training — you could find these neurons, but you couldn't control where they formed. The MoE literature (Fedus et al., 2022b) showed that imposing modular architecture (experts with gating) produced functional specialization, but it was unclear whether this specialization was a consequence of the architecture or would have emerged anyway in a dense model. The PEFT literature (Hu et al., 2022; Houlsby et al., 2019) demonstrated that tiny add-on modules could encode new capabilities, but did these modules exploit existing emergent structure or create entirely new functional pathways? The paper's distinction clarifies all of this: emergent bricks are what the model learns to organize during pre-training; customized bricks are what we add afterward to extend the model. Both are real, both are modular, but they arise through different mechanisms and have different properties.

**Why the distinction is non-obvious.** The paper itself notes in Section 5.1 that emergent and customized bricks can encode overlapping capabilities and knowledge, potentially conflicting with each other. An emergent brick might encode "the capital of France is Paris" from a 2022 pre-training corpus, while a customized knowledge brick might encode "the capital of France is Marseille" from a 2024 update. Both are "knowledge about French geography," but the emergent version is entangled with other facts learned during pre-training and harder to isolate and edit, while the customized version is a clean, pluggable module trained in isolation. Understanding that these are different *kinds* of bricks with different management requirements — not just different locations of the same thing — is essential for building practical configurable systems.

**Evidence for the distinction's validity.** The paper's empirical analysis (Section 4) directly probes emergent modularity — the functionality scores, sparse activation patterns, and perturbation results in Figures 8-10 are measurements of *emergent* structure in models that were never explicitly designed to be modular. These same models can then be augmented with customized bricks (LoRA modules, knowledge plugins, modality adapters) that coexist with the emergent structure. The paper doesn't experimentally verify the coexistence, but its framework provides the vocabulary for describing it.

**What this changes about how we think.** The distinction implies that the next generation of foundation models should be designed to *accommodate both sources of modularity*. Instead of the current paradigm — pre-train a dense monolith, then bolt on adapters as an afterthought — one could imagine pre-training with explicit "expansion slots" where customized bricks can later be inserted, or pre-training objectives that encourage the formation of well-separated emergent bricks that are easier to identify and manipulate. The paper's Section 5.1 explicitly calls for "detecting the underlying collision and redundancy between bricks" and "jointly routing and retrieving emergent and customized bricks," which are research problems that only make sense once the distinction is recognized.

### Innovation 3: Granularity-Agnostic Operations — The Insight That Brick Management Scales Across Five Orders of Magnitude

A subtle but powerful insight in the paper is that the four brick operations (routing, combining, updating, growing) are **independent of brick granularity**, and that recognizing this enables a unified treatment of problems that span from single-neuron knowledge editing to multi-model agent orchestration.

**The granularity spectrum.** The paper identifies brick granularities ranging from a solitary neuron (~1e0 parameters) through neuron groups (~1e2–1e4 parameters, as in LoRA modules or MoE experts), layers (~1e6–1e7 parameters), to full models (~1e9–1e11 parameters). This spans roughly five orders of magnitude in parameter count. The paper's key move is arguing that the *same four operations* apply at every level, even though the implementation mechanisms differ dramatically.

Consider routing. At the neuron level, routing is a top-k selection over activation values or a learned gating function (MoE). At the neuron group level, routing is embedding-based retrieval over a library of task adapters. At the full model level, routing is planner-based decomposition of a complex instruction into sub-tasks assigned to different agent models. These look nothing alike in implementation, but they solve the same abstract problem: given an input and a repository of capabilities, select the minimal subset needed. Recognizing this as *the same operation* at different granularities means that advances in one regime can potentially inform the others — for example, could the active routing ideas from tool-using LLMs (where the model decides when to call an external API) be applied to fine-grained neuron selection, where the model learns to dynamically activate expert sub-networks?

**Why this matters.** The granularity-agnostic framing is what makes the paper's framework genuinely scalable. Without it, building a configurable model would mean choosing a specific granularity (say, LoRA modules) and committing to it. With it, a configurable model can manage bricks at multiple granularities simultaneously — a full-model translation agent, a layer-level vision encoder, LoRA-level task adapters, and neuron-level knowledge updates — all within the same operational framework. The paper's Section 2.3.5 explicitly notes that "different levels of brick granularities and abilities have potential inclusive relationships" (coarser bricks contain finer ones), suggesting a hierarchical brick organization. The operations framework provides the interface for navigating this hierarchy.

**Evidence.** The paper does not experimentally demonstrate multi-granularity operation — this is a conceptual contribution, not an empirical one. The evidence is taxonomic: Table 2 catalogs prior work at every granularity level within each operation category, demonstrating that the mapping is exhaustive. The fact that the framework can accommodate work as disparate as BitFit (solitary-neuron specification for fine-tuning, Zaken et al., 2022) and Flamingo (full-model stitching for multimodal understanding, Alayrac et al., 2022) under the same operation taxonomy is indirect validation of its generality.

### Innovation 4: The Empirical Methodology for Detecting Brick Structure — From Interpretation to Operationalization

While the paper is primarily a conceptual framework, Section 4 introduces a specific **empirical methodology** for detecting brick-like structure in existing models that goes beyond prior interpretability work in two important ways. First, it targets decoder-only instruction-tuned models (Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3) rather than the encoder models (BERT) or encoder-decoder models (T5) that dominated prior sparsity and specialization analyses. Second, and more critically, it provides a protocol for **functionality-based neuron evaluation** that bridges the gap between observing modularity and using it operationally.

**What prior work did.** Prior studies on activation sparsity (Zhang et al., 2022c; Li et al., 2023c) measured how many neurons have non-zero activations per input, establishing that sparsity exists. Prior studies on functional specialization (Wang et al., 2022a) trained probes to predict task labels from neuron activations, establishing that neurons are predictive of tasks. Prior studies on MoE specialization (Zhang et al., 2023c) analyzed expert routing patterns and perturbation effects. But none of these provided a **standardized, transferable methodology** for: (1) mapping arbitrary capabilities (not just pre-defined task labels) to neuron subsets, (2) quantifying the degree of specialization via a scalar score, and (3) validating specialization through causal perturbation with cross-capability evaluation.

**What the paper adds.** The functionality score methodology (Section 4.1) is the key contribution. By defining a capability `$f$` as a set of data labels from a broad-coverage dataset (Infinity-Instruct, with thousands of fine-grained ability tags), computing per-neuron average precision scores against capability-relevant vs. capability-irrelevant instances, and then conducting a **cross-capability perturbation study** (prune neurons for capability A, evaluate on capabilities A through G), the paper establishes a rigorous protocol for answering three questions:

1. **Sparsity:** What fraction of neurons can be masked before performance degrades? (Answer: 70–80%, Figures 6–7.)
2. **Specialization:** Do specific neurons strongly predict specific capabilities? (Answer: Yes. The best neurons achieve functionality scores >0.8 for Coding, Math, and Translation. Figure 8.)
3. **Partition:** Are specialized neurons for different capabilities distinct? (Answer: Largely yes. The diagonal entries in Figure 9 dominate off-diagonal entries, showing that pruning coding neurons hurts coding much more than other capabilities. The neuron overlap similarity in Figure 10 shows low cross-capability overlap, typically <0.05 versus 0.05 random baseline.)

**The cross-capability perturbation design is particularly important** because it addresses a confound that simpler analyses miss. If pruning the top 5% of coding-specialized neurons also degrades translation performance by 200% (as happens for Mistral's translation neurons in Figure 9b), that indicates either: (a) the neurons are not truly specialized — they serve multiple capabilities, or (b) there exist "resident neurons" (Song et al., 2023b) that are frequently activated for most inputs and whose removal broadly degrades performance. The paper explicitly acknowledges this confound: "pruning neurons for knowledge in Llama and neurons for translation in Mistral significantly affect all other functionalities. This may be due to the presence of resident neurons in the FFN" (Section 4.4). Recognizing and flagging this confound is evidence of methodological rigor, not weakness — it distinguishes genuine functional specialization from spurious correlation driven by universally active neurons.

**Why this methodology matters for the brick framework.** The brick operations in Section 3 assume that bricks can be identified and manipulated. Routing needs to know which bricks are relevant to which inputs; updating needs to localize specific knowledge; growing needs to know what new bricks to add. The Section 4 methodology provides a concrete (if preliminary) answer to "how do we find the bricks?" for emergent structure. It transforms the brick framework from a purely conceptual proposal into something with an empirical grounding procedure.

**Limitations and significance.** The methodology is clearly preliminary — it uses 1,000 instances per capability (a small sample), relies on pre-annotated capability labels (not learned from unlabeled data), and identifies neurons by correlation rather than causal necessity (though the perturbation study partially addresses this). The paper acknowledges that "in the future, we will explore more effective methods to locate function-specific neurons" (Section 4.4). Nevertheless, the methodology represents a significant step beyond prior work by providing a **protocol rather than just an observation**. Future researchers can apply this same protocol to other models, other capabilities, and other granularities, accumulating evidence about where and how brick structure manifests.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the **Infinity-Instruct dataset** (hosted on HuggingFace), specifically the `7M` version as referenced in Section 4.2. This dataset consists of user prompts and model-generated responses, where each instance is annotated with thousands of fine-grained ability labels indicating which capabilities the prompt requires. For the sparsity, specialization, and partition experiments, the authors manually curate seven broad capability categories (Coding, Math, Linguistic, Knowledge, Translation, Ethics and Moral, Writing) by grouping specific data labels (Table 3). Since each instance may require multiple abilities and the goal is to analyze specialization across distinct capabilities, the authors retain only instances that belong **exclusively** to one of the seven capability types. From each capability type, they randomly sample **1,000 instances**, yielding a 7,000-instance evaluation corpus (Section 4.2).

- **Base models.** The experiments analyze two widely-used decoder-only instruction-tuned models: **Llama-3-8B-Instruct** and **Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3** (Section 4.2). These are chosen because they represent the dominant modern LLM paradigm — decoder-only architectures trained on large-scale corpora followed by instruction-tuning — and because they use non-ReLU activations (SiLU for Llama-3, GELU for Mistral), making them a more challenging testbed for activation sparsity analysis than the ReLU-based encoder or encoder-decoder models (BERT, T5) studied in prior work. Both models have 32 Transformer layers. The authors argue this choice demonstrates that the brick phenomena generalize beyond the specific architectures used in earlier modularity research.

- **Metrics.** Four distinct metrics are used across different experiments:

  - **Normalized activation value** (for sparsity analysis): For each neuron `$i$` in each FFN layer, the absolute activation value `$|\text{FFN}_I(\mathbf{x})_i|$` is collected across all tokens of all instances. These are normalized across all neurons and layers to produce a distribution. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of these normalized values reveals what fraction of neurons produce near-zero outputs (Figures 6a, 7a).

  - **Output magnitude** (for sparsity analysis): Defined as `$||\text{FFN}(\mathbf{x})_i||_2$` — the L2 norm of neuron `$i$`'s contribution to the final FFN output vector `$\text{FFN}_O(\text{FFN}_I(\mathbf{x})_i \cdot \mathbf{W}_{O[:,i]})$`. This directly measures the neuron's impact on the output, rather than its intermediate activation. The authors argue this achieves "a higher degree of sparsity" than raw activation values (Section 4.3). Cumulative distributions are shown in Figures 6c and 7c.

  - **Loss** (for masking experiments): The standard language modeling cross-entropy loss on the generation task, measured as the model's perplexity-based loss on the output tokens given the input prompts. The metric is `$\text{Loss} = -\log P(r_i | p_i)$` for prompt-response pairs. This is used to assess the impact of neuron masking: as increasing fractions of neurons with the lowest indicators (activation or magnitude) are masked (set to zero), the loss quantifies performance degradation. Results are shown as raw loss values in Figures 6b, 6d, 7b, 7d.

  - **Functionality Score** (for specialization analysis): For each neuron `$n$` and each capability `$f$`, the Average Precision (AP) score is computed:
    $$\text{FuncScore}(n, f) = \text{AvgPrecision}(\{\mathbf{A}_0, ..., \mathbf{A}_n\}, \{y_0^f, ..., y_n^f\})$$
    where `$\mathbf{A}_i$` is the average absolute activation of neuron `$n$` across all tokens in instance `$i$`, and `$y_i^f \in \{0, 1\}$` indicates whether instance `$i$` requires capability `$f$`. The AP score ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating the neuron's activation pattern is more discriminative for that capability — the neuron fires strongly on capability-relevant inputs and weakly on irrelevant ones. The paper reports the best functionality score per layer, the average score, and the best score among the top 5‰ of neurons per layer (Figure 8).

  - **Perplexity change** (for perturbation validation): After pruning the top 5% of neurons by functionality score for a specific capability, the percentage increase in perplexity is measured on each of the seven capabilities separately:
    $$\text{PPL Change} = \frac{\text{PPL}_{\text{pruned}} - \text{PPL}_{\text{original}}}{\text{PPL}_{\text{original}}} \times 100\%$$
    This is the metric reported in the perturbation heatmaps (Figure 9).

  - **Distribution similarity** (for partition analysis): For a given capability `$f$`, the set of top-5% neurons by functionality score is identified across all layers. For two capabilities `$f_i$` and `$f_j$`, the distribution similarity is computed as the Jaccard index (size of intersection divided by size of union) between their top-5% neuron sets. This produces a value between 0 and 1, where 0 indicates completely disjoint neuron sets and 1 indicates identical sets (Figure 10).

- **Baselines.** The paper's experiments are primarily **analytical rather than comparative** — they aim to characterize the modular structure of existing models rather than benchmark configurable models against monolithic baselines. However, three implicit baselines are used to contextualize the functionality scores:

  - **Random activation baseline:** The functionality score that would be obtained if neuron activations were randomly permuted across instances (i.e., if there were no genuine correlation between neuron firing patterns and capability requirements). This is shown as the "Functionality Score for Random Activation" line in Figure 8. The fact that best-neuron scores exceed this baseline by large margins (reaching >0.8 vs. random's ~0.3) validates that the high scores are not artifacts of the metric or data distribution.

  - **Average functionality score:** The mean AP score across all neurons in a layer for a given capability. Shown as the "Average Functionality Score" line in Figure 8. The gap between the best-neuron score and the average quantifies how concentrated specialization is — the larger the gap, the more specialization is concentrated in a few neurons rather than distributed across the layer.

  - **Best 5‰ functionality score:** The average AP score of the top 5 per mille (0.5%) of neurons in each layer. Shown as the "Best 5‰ Functionality Score" line in Figure 8. The gap between the single best neuron and the best 5‰ indicates how quickly specialization drops off — a large gap means specialization is concentrated in extremely few neurons.

  For the perturbation study (Figure 9), the implicit baseline is the **unpruned model's performance** on each capability. The diagonal vs. off-diagonal comparison serves as an internal baseline: if pruning coding-specialized neurons affects coding far more than other capabilities, the neurons are functionally specialized; if it affects all capabilities roughly equally, the identified neurons are likely general-purpose "resident neurons."

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Since the experiments are purely analytical (no training or inference-time strategy comparison), there is no generation budget in the traditional sense. The computational cost of the analysis itself is not explicitly accounted for, which is a limitation (see Critical Assessment). The paper notes that "to evaluate the sparsity of LLMs, we attempt to calculate the distribution of neuron activations and the impact of the neurons with low activation values" (Section 4.3) by running inference on the 7,000-instance corpus (1,000 instances per capability) through each full model. For the perturbation study, each of 7 pruning configurations (one per capability) is evaluated on 7,000 instances, yielding 49 forward passes per model. The difficulty estimation costs (computing functionality scores for all `$32 \times d_{\text{ff}}$` neurons) scale as `$O(L \cdot d_{\text{ff}} \cdot N \cdot T)$` where `$L$` is layers, `$d_{\text{ff}}$` is FFN intermediate dimension, `$N$` is the number of instances, and `$T$` is average tokens per instance.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation, train/test splits, or statistical significance testing in the conventional sense. The analysis is conducted on a **fixed, curated evaluation set** of 7,000 instances. The paper justifies this by noting that the Infinity-Instruct labels are used only for capability assignment, not for model training — the models are off-the-shelf. The perturbation study serves as the primary validation mechanism: rather than statistical tests on the functionality scores, the paper uses causal intervention (pruning specialized neurons) to verify that the identified neurons are genuinely causally important for their associated capability. The paper notes a methodological limitation: "in the future, we will explore more effective methods to locate function-specific neurons" (Section 4.4), implicitly acknowledging that the AP-based localization, while validated by perturbation, could be improved.

### Main Quantitative Results

The empirical analysis addresses three hierarchically nested questions: (1) Are LLM neurons sparsely activated? (2) Do individual neurons specialize in specific capabilities? (3) Are specialized neurons organized into distinct, non-overlapping partitions? The results are organized accordingly.

---

#### Sparsity: Only a Minority of Neurons Matter Per Input

The headline finding is that **approximately 80% of neurons contribute negligible signal to the FFN output for a given input**, and a large fraction can be masked with minimal performance degradation.

**Activation value distribution (Figures 6a, 7a).** The cumulative distribution functions of normalized activation values across all 32 layers for both models show that "the normalized indicators for 80% neurons are lower than 0.2" (Section 4.3). Specifically, the CDF curves for all layers are tightly clustered near the left edge, indicating that across all depths, only ~20% of neurons produce activation values above 0.2 in normalized scale. The distributions are "similar across different layers and two decoder-only models with different training data," which the paper interprets as evidence that "the sparsity issues widely exist in LLMs" and are not an artifact of a particular model architecture or training recipe.

**What this means concretely:** For any given token processed by any FFN layer in Llama-3-8B-Instruct or Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, roughly 80% of the neurons produce outputs that are within 20% of the maximum activation magnitude observed across all neurons. Given that the FFN layers contain `$d_{\text{ff}} = 14336$` neurons each (for Llama-3), this means approximately 11,500 neurons per layer are effectively idle for each token.

**Output magnitude distribution (Figures 6c, 7c).** The alternative sparsity metric — output magnitude — reveals even stronger sparsity. The CDF curves show that an even larger fraction of neurons have normalized output magnitudes below 0.2, and the curves are shifted further left compared to the activation value distributions. The paper notes that "compared to activation values, using output magnitudes as indicators results in a greater number of neurons with normalized indicators below 0.2, allowing more neurons to be masked without a performance drop. This indicates that output magnitudes can achieve a higher degree of sparsity" (Section 4.3). This finding makes intuitive sense: a neuron can have a moderate activation value but, if its corresponding output weight vector `$\mathbf{W}_{O[:,i]}$` has small magnitude, its actual contribution to the final output will be small. Conversely, a neuron with low activation but large output weights could still be impactful — but empirically, activation and output weight magnitudes appear to be positively correlated.

**Masking experiments (Figures 6b, 6d, 7b, 7d).** The functional significance of sparsity is validated through progressive masking. Starting from 0% masked (all neurons active), the paper increases the masked ratio (neurons with the lowest indicators are set to zero) and measures the loss on all seven capability categories. Key quantitative results:

- **Using activation values as the masking indicator** (Figures 6b, 7b): For Llama-3-8B-Instruct, "when the proportion of masked neurons is low (<70% when using activation values as indicators), there is almost no decline in the model performance." The loss remains approximately flat at ~2.0–2.5 across all seven capabilities until the masking ratio exceeds 70%, after which it begins to rise. For Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, the flat region extends to approximately 70% masking as well, though the baseline loss is higher (~2.5–3.5 depending on capability), and the loss increase at high masking ratios is more dramatic, reaching above 10.0 for some capabilities at 80% masking.

- **Using output magnitudes as the masking indicator** (Figures 6d, 7d): The flat region extends further — "when the proportion of masked neurons is low (<80% when using output magnitudes as indicators), there is almost no decline in the model performance." For Llama-3, the loss curves remain near baseline through 80% masking, then rise to ~2.5–3.0 at 90% masking. For Mistral, the curves are similarly flat through 80% masking, with losses rising to ~3.0–5.0 at 90% masking.

**Capability-specific differences in masking sensitivity.** While the paper's primary claim is that masking is broadly insensitive to capability, Figures 6b, 6d, 7b, and 7d show some capability-dependent variation. For Llama-3 (Figure 6b), all seven capabilities show remarkably similar masking curves — the lines are tightly clustered throughout the masking range. For Mistral (Figure 7b), Ethical, Coding, and Math show slightly higher loss at 80% masking (~8.0–10.0) compared to Knowledge and Linguistic (~4.0–6.0), suggesting that these capabilities may depend on a subset of "essential" neurons that are more uniformly activated — though the paper does not comment on this variation explicitly.

**Interpretation.** The sparsity results establish the necessary condition for the brick framework: if a large fraction of neurons can be ignored for any given input without performance loss, then it is plausible to decompose the model into functional bricks and activate only the relevant ones per instruction. The fact that 70–80% masking is tolerated suggests the effective computational redundancy in FFN layers is substantial — the model is over-provisioned with parameters relative to what any single input requires, which is exactly the setting where dynamic brick selection could yield efficiency gains.

---

#### Specialization: Specific Neurons Are Highly Predictive of Specific Capabilities

The headline finding is that **neurons across all layers exhibit strong functional specialization**, with the best neurons achieving functionality scores above 0.8 for Coding, Math, and Translation — far exceeding the random baseline (~0.3) and the layer-average score (~0.3–0.4).

**Per-layer functionality scores (Figure 8).** The paper presents side-by-side results for Llama-3-8B-Instruct (Figure 8a) and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Figure 8b). Each subplot corresponds to one of the seven capabilities, with layer number on the x-axis (0 to 31) and functionality score on the y-axis (0 to 1). Four curves are shown per subplot:

- **Best Functionality Score** (dark blue/teal): The maximum AP score across all neurons in that layer. This represents the most specialized neuron for that capability.
- **Best 5‰ Functionality Score** (lighter shade): The average AP score of the top 0.5% of neurons in that layer.
- **Average Functionality Score** (lightest shade): The mean AP score across all neurons.
- **Functionality Score for Random Activation** (dashed line): The score that would be obtained under random permutation of activations (chance level).

Key quantitative findings:

**Magnitude of specialization.** For Llama-3-8B-Instruct:
- Coding: The best functionality score exceeds **0.8** in most layers, with peaks above **0.85** in middle layers (layers 8–24). The best 5‰ score drops to ~0.5–0.6, indicating a steep concentration of specialization.
- Math: Similar pattern — best scores consistently above **0.8**, peaks near **0.9** in layers 10–20. The best 5‰ scores range from ~0.5–0.7.
- Translation: Best scores reach **0.8–0.9** in layers 5–25. The best 5‰ scores are ~0.5–0.6.
- Linguistic: Best scores are lower, plateauing around **0.55–0.65** in middle layers. The best 5‰ scores are ~0.4–0.5, only modestly above the average (~0.35–0.4).
- Knowledge: Best scores reach **0.45–0.55** in upper layers (15–31), lower than Coding/Math/Translation but still clearly above the random baseline.
- Ethical and Writing: Best scores are modest (~0.4–0.5 for Ethical, ~0.35–0.45 for Writing), with the best 5‰ and average curves being close together, suggesting specialization for these capabilities is weak or diffusely distributed.

For Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, the patterns are broadly similar: Coding, Math, and Translation show strong specialization (best scores >0.8), while Linguistic, Knowledge, Ethical, and Writing show weaker but still above-chance specialization. The paper emphasizes that "the functionalities of Coding, Math, and Translation can achieve functionality scores higher than 0.8 in most layers" and attributes this to the structural distinctiveness of these capabilities: "instructions for these functionalities require LLMs to understand or generate sequences distinctly different from the English natural language, hence the neurons activated show high specificity" (Section 4.4). Coding requires generating structured programming syntax; Math requires symbolic reasoning with numbers and equations; Translation requires producing text in a target language — all of which differ markedly from general English text generation.

**Concentration of specialization.** The gap between the best-neuron score and the best-5‰ score is substantial across all capabilities. For Llama-3 Coding, the best neuron achieves ~0.85 in layer 15 while the best 5‰ achieves ~0.55 — a drop of 0.3, indicating that the top several dozen neurons per layer account for most of the predictive power, with the remaining 99.5% of neurons contributing little. This steep drop-off confirms the picture from the sparsity analysis: not only are most neurons inactive per input, but most neurons are also non-discriminative for any given capability. Only a tiny elite subset — the "bricks" — carry capability-specific information.

**Layer-wise distribution.** Specialization is not uniform across depth. For Llama-3:
- Coding specialization peaks in middle-to-upper layers (8–28), with layers 0–5 and 29–31 showing lower best scores.
- Math specialization is concentrated in layers 5–25, with a sharp drop in the final few layers.
- Translation shows a broad plateau across layers 5–28, suggesting translation-relevant neurons are distributed across most of the model depth.
- Linguistic specialization peaks early (layers 3–15) and declines in upper layers, consistent with prior work showing that syntactic processing is concentrated in earlier transformer layers.
- Knowledge specialization is concentrated in upper layers (15–31), consistent with the hypothesis that factual knowledge is stored in higher-level semantic representations.

Mistral shows similar patterns but with more variability across layers — the best-score curves are noisier, possibly reflecting differences in training data or instruction-tuning procedure.

**Comparison to baselines.** The average functionality scores across all neurons in a layer are approximately 0.3–0.4 for most capabilities, only slightly above the random activation baseline of ~0.3. This reinforces the concentration finding: the average neuron is barely more predictive of any capability than random chance. The functional specialization is carried by a small minority of neurons per layer.

#### Partition: Specialized Neurons for Different Capabilities Are Distinct

The headline finding is that **neurons specialized for different capabilities form largely disjoint sets**, with low cross-capability overlap, suggesting the potential for clean modular decomposition.

**Perturbation study (Figure 9).** For each capability `$f$`, the top 5% of neurons (ranked by functionality score for `$f$`) are identified across all layers and pruned — their activations are set to zero. The model's perplexity is then evaluated on test data from all seven capabilities. The results are presented as heatmaps where:
- **Rows** indicate the capability whose specialized neurons were pruned.
- **Columns** indicate the capability being evaluated.
- **Cell values** show the percentage increase in perplexity relative to the unpruned model: `$(\text{PPL}_{\text{pruned}} - \text{PPL}_{\text{original}}) / \text{PPL}_{\text{original}} \times 100\%$`.

Key quantitative findings for **Llama-3-8B-Instruct** (Figure 9a):

- **Diagonal dominance:** The diagonal entries (pruning capability X, evaluating capability X) are generally the largest values in each row. For Coding, the diagonal is **+112%** — pruning coding-specialized neurons more than doubles perplexity on coding tasks. For Math, the diagonal is **+296%**. For Translation, **+147%**. For Linguistic, **+59%**. For Writing, **+444%**. For Knowledge, the diagonal is **+81%**, but notably this is not the dominant effect — off-diagonal impacts (e.g., +122% on Ethical, +79% on Coding) are comparable, suggesting that the identified "knowledge-specialized" neurons in Llama are less functionally pure, or that the knowledge capability is broadly entangled with other capabilities.

- **Off-diagonal sparsity:** Most off-diagonal entries are in the single digits to low teens. For instance, pruning coding neurons increases Ethic perplexity by only **+6%**, Knowledge by **+8%**, and Linguistic by **+7%**. This indicates that the top coding neurons are highly specific — removing them disrupts coding dramatically but leaves other capabilities largely intact.

- **Exceptions and confounds:** Two notable exceptions challenge the clean-specialization narrative:
  - **Knowledge → others:** Pruning knowledge neurons affects Coding (+79%), Math (+184%), Ethical (+122%), and Translation (+86%) substantially — in several cases as much or more than the diagonal (+81%). The paper attributes this to "the presence of resident neurons in the FFN (Song et al., 2023b), which are frequently activated for most inputs. Including these neurons when selecting for functional specificity results in a substantial impact on model performance" (Section 4.4). In other words, the top knowledge neurons by AP score may include broadly useful neurons that fire for many input types, not just knowledge-intensive inputs, because these resident neurons happen to have high activation variance that correlates spuriously with knowledge labels.
  - **Writing → Knowledge:** Pruning writing neurons produces a +184% increase in Knowledge perplexity, and +67% in Math — both larger than or comparable to the diagonal effects in those rows. This suggests cross-capability entanglement.

Key quantitative findings for **Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3** (Figure 9b):

- The diagonal pattern is less consistently dominant. For Coding, the diagonal is **+118%**, comparable to Llama. For Math, the diagonal is **+200%**. For Translation, the diagonal is **+377%** (stronger than Llama's +147%). For Ethical and Writing, the diagonals are extremely high (+1774% and +9243% respectively), reflecting highly concentrated specialization — but these enormous numbers may also indicate that the identified neurons are so critical to those capabilities that pruning them effectively destroys the model's ability, driving perplexity to near-random levels.

- However, Mistral shows more cross-capability interference than Llama. Pruning Translation neurons severely impacts Coding (+391%), Writing (+477%), and Knowledge (+381%) — suggesting that Translation neurons in Mistral are less functionally isolated and may serve multiple linguistic functions. Pruning Knowledge neurons impacts all capabilities substantially (off-diagonals range from +105% to +375%), mirroring the resident neuron confound seen in Llama.

**Neuron overlap analysis (Figure 10).** The paper computes the Jaccard similarity between the sets of top-5% neurons for each pair of capabilities. Results for both models:

- **Diagonal entries are 1.0** (by construction — a set compared with itself).
- **Off-diagonal similarities are predominantly low.** For Llama-3-8B-Instruct (Figure 10a), values range from **0.00 to 0.08**, with most in the 0.01–0.04 range. The random baseline for Jaccard similarity of two randomly selected neuron groups is reported as **0.05**. Coding-Linguistic shows 0.01, Coding-Knowledge shows 0.01, Math-Ethical shows 0.00. The highest off-diagonal similarity for Llama is **Translation-Linguistic at 0.08**, which exceeds the random baseline — the paper explains this as expected because "the need to ensure grammatical correctness in the output language during the translation process" means translation and linguistic neurons share some functional overlap.
- For Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Figure 10b), the pattern is similar: off-diagonals range from **0.00 to 0.09**, with most below 0.04. Translation-Linguistic again shows the highest similarity at **0.09**, above the random 0.05 baseline. Coding-Math shows 0.04, Knowledge-Linguistic shows 0.01, Writing-Ethical shows 0.00.

The paper concludes that "values on the diagonal significantly outperform those off the diagonal, indicating that neurons for different functionalities are distinctly different" (Section 4.5). The low cross-capability overlap is interpreted as evidence for **functional partitioning** — different capabilities recruit distinct neural subpopulations, suggesting that the model's parameter space is organized into capability-specific bricks that are largely independent.

**Synthesis of the three findings.** Taken together, the sparsity, specialization, and partition results paint a coherent picture of emergent modularity: (1) For any given input, only ~20% of neurons are meaningfully active (sparsity). (2) Those active neurons are not random — specific neurons are strongly tuned to specific capabilities (specialization). (3) The specialized neurons for different capabilities are largely distinct sets (partition). This tripartite structure — sparse per-input activation, concentrated per-capability specialization, low cross-capability overlap — is exactly what the brick framework requires: a model that has internally organized into functional bricks that can be selectively activated based on input requirements.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The experimental section is structured as a single integrated analysis rather than a set of ablations around a central method, so traditional ablation studies (e.g., "trying different hyperparameters for brick detection") are not present. However, the paper includes several implicit robustness checks and design variations that serve a similar function:

- **Alternative sparsity indicator (activation value vs. output magnitude):** The paper evaluates two different metrics for ranking neuron importance — raw activation magnitude (`$|\text{FFN}_I(\mathbf{x})_i|$`) and output magnitude (`$||\text{FFN}(\mathbf{x})_i||_2$`). This is effectively an ablation on the choice of sparsity metric. The finding that output magnitude achieves higher apparent sparsity (Figures 6c-d, 7c-d vs. 6a-b, 7a-b) and enables masking 80% vs. 70% of neurons before performance degrades suggests that activation magnitude alone underestimates true functional sparsity. The paper does not claim one metric is definitively superior, but notes this finding "encourages further research to identify more effective indicators for assessing neuron usefulness" (Section 4.3). This is a negative-but-informative result: the metric choice matters, and simple activation magnitude may be suboptimal.

- **Cross-model replication (Llama-3-8B-Instruct vs. Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3):** All three main findings — sparsity, specialization, and partition — are replicated across two independently trained models from different organizations (Meta vs. Mistral AI) with different architectures (Llama uses grouped-query attention and SiLU activation; Mistral uses sliding window attention and GELU activation). The qualitative replication of all patterns across both models strengthens the claim that emergent modularity is a general property of large decoder-only LLMs rather than an artifact of a specific training procedure. However, the quantitative differences (especially the stronger cross-capability interference in Mistral's perturbation study and the vastly different diagonal perplexity increases — e.g., Llama Coding +112% vs. Mistral Coding +118% are similar, but Writing +444% vs. +9243% differ enormously) suggest model-specific factors influence the "purity" of specialization, which the paper does not systematically analyze.

- **Multiple functionality score thresholds (best vs. best 5‰ vs. average):** The paper reports specialization at three different selection thresholds in Figure 8. This serves as a sensitivity analysis on the number of neurons considered "specialized." The steep drop from best to best 5‰ confirms that specialization is highly concentrated — changing the threshold from 1 neuron to ~72 neurons per layer (for Llama-3 with `$d_{\text{ff}}=14336$`, 5‰ = ~72 neurons) substantially reduces the average score, indicating that the majority of even the top fraction of neurons are not highly specialized. This is consistent with the sparsity results (only ~20% of neurons are active, and within those, only a fraction are capability-specific).

- **Different evaluation dimension (loss on all capabilities after capability-specific pruning):** The perturbation study in Figure 9 evaluates the effect of pruning on all seven capabilities, not just the target capability. This is a multi-dimensional evaluation that tests for unintended side effects. The paper does not prune at multiple thresholds (e.g., top 1%, top 5%, top 10%) to show how the diagonal-off-diagonal gap varies with pruning threshold, which would have been informative. A partial replication at 5% only is the sole reported configuration.

- **Note on inherently limited ablations:** Since the paper does not propose a new method (e.g., a new routing algorithm or knowledge editing technique) whose design choices could be ablated, traditional ablation studies are not applicable. The empirical contribution is characterization, not method validation. The closest analogs to ablations are the choices of metric (AP for specialization), indicator (activation vs. magnitude for sparsity), and model (Llama vs. Mistral for replication). The paper would be strengthened by ablations on other design choices — e.g., using different capability groupings, different instance selection criteria (exclusive vs. overlapping capability labels), or different perturbation fractions — but these are absent.

### Critical Assessment

**Does the empirical analysis actually support the paper's central framework claim?**

The paper's overarching claim is that LLMs can and should be viewed through a "modular perspective" — that they possess an internal brick structure enabling configurable model usage. The experiments demonstrate three necessary conditions for this claim: sparsity (few neurons active per input), specialization (specific neurons tuned to specific capabilities), and partitioning (specialized neurons form distinct groups). However, the gap between *demonstrating these conditions* and *demonstrating that LLMs can be operated as configurable brick systems* is substantial, and the paper's empirical analysis does not fully bridge it. Here is a precise accounting:

**What the experiments actually demonstrate:**

- **Sparsity is confirmed for non-ReLU decoder-only models.** The activation and output magnitude distributions (Figures 6–7) convincingly show that a large fraction of neurons contribute near-zero output for any given input, extending prior findings from ReLU-based encoder models (T5, BERT) to modern autoregressive LLMs. The masking experiments validate that this sparsity is functional — neurons can be removed without immediate performance degradation. This is a solid empirical contribution.

- **Correlational specialization is confirmed.** The AP-based functionality scores (Figure 8) demonstrate that specific neurons have activation patterns correlated with specific capability labels. The scores are well above chance, consistent across two models, and concentrated in a few neurons per layer. This is good evidence for statistical association between neuron firing and capability requirements.

- **Causal specialization is partially supported.** The perturbation study (Figure 9) shows that removing the top-5% capability-correlated neurons disproportionately degrades the target capability for Coding, Math, and Translation in Llama. However, the evidence is weaker for Knowledge, Ethical, and Writing (where off-diagonal effects are large), and the Mistral results show substantial cross-capability interference across all categories. The paper's attribution of cross-capability effects to "resident neurons" is a post-hoc explanation, not a tested hypothesis — the paper does not demonstrate that filtering out resident neurons would produce cleaner partition results.

- **Neuron-set overlap is low (Figure 10),** supporting the partition hypothesis. But this is a necessary but not sufficient condition for functional partitioning — two capabilities could use disjoint neuron sets but still share critical "hub" neurons not captured in the top-5% threshold, or could interact through attention mechanisms not analyzed here.

**What the experiments do NOT demonstrate (and the paper does not claim they do):**

- **Dynamic brick selection at inference time.** The paper demonstrates that if you *could* identify capability-relevant neurons and activate only them, you *could* reduce computation by 70–80% with minimal loss. But it does not implement such a system. There is no experiment showing that an actual routing mechanism (MoE gating, retrieval-based selection, or otherwise) can achieve this at inference time with realistic overhead. The gap between "these neurons are relevant in retrospect" and "we can identify and select them in advance" is non-trivial.

- **Generalization beyond the seven tested capabilities.** The paper analyzes exactly seven capability categories, manually curated from the Infinity-Instruct label set. Whether the sparsity/specialization/partition patterns hold for other capabilities (e.g., summarization, dialogue, reasoning about time, spatial reasoning, etc.) is untested. The choice of capabilities is reasonable but necessarily selective. A broader capability coverage would strengthen the generality claim.

- **Brick combination operations.** The analysis characterizes individual bricks but does not test whether combining bricks (e.g., simultaneous activation of coding and math bricks for a problem that requires both) produces coherent outputs. The perturbation study tests removal, not combination. Brick stitching, parameter averaging, and multi-brick routing are entirely unvalidated experimentally.

- **Brick updating or growing.** The paper provides no empirical evidence that identified bricks can be updated (e.g., editing a fact in a knowledge brick without affecting other bricks) or that new bricks can be grown and integrated. These operations are discussed extensively in Sections 3.3–3.4 but remain purely conceptual in the context of this paper.

**Genuine weaknesses and limitations:**

1. **The gap between correlational and operational modularity is unaddressed.** The paper's framework is avowedly operational — it defines bricks as units that can be routed, combined, updated, and grown. But the experiments only demonstrate *descriptive* modularity: neurons have functional correlates and can be retroactively identified. There is no experiment showing that any of the four operations actually work on these identified bricks. For example, could the paper take the "coding brick" (the top-5% coding-specialized neurons) and route only that brick to process a coding query, while routing the "translation brick" for a translation query, and achieve high performance with reduced computation? If not, the operational claim is aspirational, not demonstrated. This is the single largest gap between the framework and the evidence.

2. **The perturbation study confounds functional specialization with general neuron importance.** As the paper itself acknowledges, some identified "specialized" neurons may actually be resident neurons — neurons that are broadly important regardless of capability. The perturbation study cannot distinguish between: (a) a neuron that is genuinely specialized for coding (its removal hurts coding because it encodes programming-specific computations) and (b) a neuron that is important for all capabilities (its removal hurts coding because coding, like all tasks, depends on this general-purpose computation). The AP metric would assign a high score to both types if the neuron happens to have somewhat higher activation variance on coding inputs. The paper does not control for this confound (e.g., by subtracting out the average activation across all capabilities before computing AP). This likely explains the anomalously high off-diagonal effects for Knowledge and Writing.

3. **The capability categories are coarse and their labels may be noisy.** The paper uses "Coding" as a single capability, but coding encompasses Python programming, SQL queries, code documentation, bug fixing, etc. — distinct sub-capabilities that may recruit different neuron subsets. Similarly, "Knowledge" spans health knowledge, geographic knowledge, chemistry knowledge, literary knowledge, and a dozen other domains (Table 3). If these sub-capabilities use different neural circuits, lumping them into one category would obscure specialization and produce lower apparent functionality scores. The modest functionality scores for Knowledge (best ~0.45–0.55, compared to >0.8 for Coding) might reflect this lumping rather than genuinely weaker modularity for knowledge.

4. **Sample size per capability (1,000 instances) is small for robust neuron-level statistics.** With `$32 \times 14336 = 458,752$` neurons per model (for Llama-3), computing AP scores from 1,000 positive and 6,000 negative examples (for a specific capability) per neuron means the scores are based on relatively few data points. Variance in AP scores is not reported, and no confidence intervals or significance thresholds are provided for any of the functionality scores. A neuron with an AP of 0.8 based on 1,000 examples might have a 95% confidence interval of ±0.05 or more, meaning apparent differences between capabilities or layers may not be statistically reliable.

5. **The analysis is limited to FFN layers.** The paper explicitly states its focus on FFN layers because "feedforward layers in Transformer can be regarded as key-value memory networks and provide world knowledge for sequence understanding" (Section 4.1). However, attention layers also contain neurons (attention heads, value projections, output projections) that plausibly exhibit specialization. The exclusion of attention mechanisms means the declared picture of modularity is incomplete — a "coding brick" might span both FFN neurons and specific attention heads, and analyzing only FFNs would miss cross-mechanism interactions.

6. **No evidence that self-organized bricks respect functional boundaries better than human-defined bricks.** The paper devotes substantial theoretical discussion to self-organized vs. human-defined emergent bricks (Section 2.1.2–2.1.3) but the empirical analysis does not distinguish between the two — it operates at the solitary neuron level and then groups by capability-defined sets. It does not test whether these capability-defined sets correspond to self-organized clusters (co-activating neuron groups), or whether self-organized clusters are more functionally pure than human-defined architectural boundaries (e.g., comparing specialization within FFN layers vs. across layers). This is a missed opportunity to validate a key theoretical distinction.

7. **The capability exclusivity filtering may bias the results.** The paper retains only instances that belong exclusively to one capability type (Section 4.2). This removes instances requiring multiple capabilities (e.g., a prompt that asks for a Python script and also requires mathematical reasoning). In practice, many real-world instructions are multi-capability, and the bricks needed for such instructions might interact in ways that the exclusivity-filtered analysis cannot detect. The clean separation observed in Figures 9–10 may partially be an artifact of using exclusively single-capability instances for evaluation — when an input genuinely requires both coding and math, the corresponding bricks might show overlap that this experimental design cannot reveal.

**What would strengthen the empirical contribution:**

- **An end-to-end brick routing demonstration:** For a held-out set of capability-labeled prompts, use the top-5% functionality-scored neurons for each capability to construct "capability bricks." Then, for each prompt, activate only the brick corresponding to its capability label and evaluate performance (accuracy or perplexity) compared to the full model. This would directly test whether the identified bricks can be used for the routing operation the framework proposes.

- **Brick overlap analysis at multiple thresholds:** Figure 10 shows Jaccard similarity for top-5% neurons. Repeating the analysis at top-1%, top-2%, top-10%, and top-20% would reveal whether the partition finding is sensitive to the threshold choice. If low overlap holds across a range of thresholds, the partition claim is robust. If overlap increases sharply at higher thresholds (e.g., top-20%), it suggests the "distinct partition" view may only hold for the most extreme specialized neurons and that broader functional modules are more entangled.

- **Per-instance activation analysis:** For each instance, record which neurons fire above a threshold, and then compute the overlap between the activated sets for instances of the same capability vs. instances of different capabilities. This would test whether capability-specific activation patterns are consistent at the instance level (not just in aggregate via functionality scores).

- **Attention neuron analysis:** Extend the functionality score and perturbation methodology to attention head outputs, Q/K/V projections, and the attention output projection. Compare the degree of specialization in FFNs vs. attention mechanisms. This would provide a more complete map of brick structure.

- **Resident neuron control:** Identify neurons that are consistently highly active across all capability types (by measuring activation variance across capabilities) and exclude them from the specialization analysis. Repeat the perturbation study with resident neurons excluded to test whether the cross-capability interference in Figure 9 is driven by these broadly active neurons.

**Bottom line:** The empirical analysis provides solid evidence for the *necessary enabling conditions* of the brick framework — sparsity, specialization, and partitioning exist in modern LLMs. This is a genuine contribution that extends prior modularity findings to decoder-only instruction-tuned models and provides a replicable methodology. However, the experiments do not demonstrate that these conditions are *sufficient* for the configurable operations the framework proposes. The gap between "the model looks modular under analysis" and "the model can be operated as a composable brick system" remains open. The paper is candid about this in part — Section 5 is entirely devoted to open problems — but the empirical claims in Section 4 are sometimes presented as stronger validation of the framework than the experimental design justifies. A reader should understand that the experiments validate the *descriptive* claim (LLMs exhibit modular structure) but not the *prescriptive* claim (we can build better systems by treating LLMs as configurable brick assemblies). The latter requires future work that the paper's framework is designed to motivate.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Framework Is Not Empirically Validated as Operational — Only as Descriptive

The single most consequential gap in the paper is the disconnect between the *conceptual* claim — that LLMs can be operated as configurable systems of routable, combinable, updatable, and growable bricks — and the *empirical* evidence, which demonstrates only that neurons exhibit modular properties under retrospective analysis. The paper's experiments (Section 4) establish three necessary conditions for the brick framework: sparse activation (Figures 6–7), functional specialization (Figure 8), and modular partitioning (Figures 9–10). However, no experiment tests whether any of the four brick operations (Section 3) can be realized using the identified bricks.

The consequence is that the paper's central value proposition — "for a given instruction, unlike computing the monolithic LLMs, we can select part of bricks with specific functionalities for computation according to the instruction functionality requirements, which significantly benefits the computational efficiency" (Section 1) — remains an aspirational claim unsupported by implementation. A practitioner reading this paper cannot determine whether routing the top-5% coding-specialized neurons for a Python query would actually produce correct code, or whether the ~70–80% computation reduction suggested by the masking experiments (Section 4.3) would translate to wall-clock speedups after accounting for the overhead of per-input neuron selection. The paper does not measure inference time, throughput, or accuracy for any end-to-end brick routing system. The paper's own framing in Section 5 acknowledges this obliquely — every open problem in Section 5.3, 5.4, and 5.5 concerns gaps that must be filled before the framework becomes operational — but the empirical claims in Section 4 are presented as validation of the framework, when they validate only its descriptive priors.

The perturbation study (Figure 9) is the closest the paper comes to testing an operation: pruning capability-specialized neurons simulates deactivating a brick for that capability. But this is a destructive test (removing neurons and measuring degradation), not a constructive one (activating only the relevant subset and measuring preserved performance). Destructive tests establish that the neurons *matter* for their associated capability; they do not establish that the neurons are *sufficient* — that activating them alone, without the other 95% of neurons, would produce adequate outputs. The distinction is fundamental: a neuron can be necessary without being sufficient. The brick framework requires sufficiency (or near-sufficiency) for efficiency gains.

The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation experimentally. Section 5.2 discusses future work on brick construction protocols, Section 5.4 discusses efficient computing frameworks, and Section 5.3 discusses evaluation of configurable models — all implicitly acknowledging that the operational infrastructure does not yet exist.

---

### The Capability Specialization Analysis Uses a Single, Coarse-Grained Capability Taxonomy on a Small Sample

The empirical analysis in Section 4 maps neurons to exactly seven capability categories (Coding, Math, Linguistic, Knowledge, Translation, Ethics, Writing), derived by manually grouping thousands of fine-grained Infinity-Instruct labels (Table 3) and retaining only 1,000 instances per category that belong exclusively to one type (Section 4.2). This design imposes three interrelated constraints on the generality and resolution of the findings.

**First, the seven categories are coarse and likely confound sub-capabilities that recruit distinct neural circuits.** "Knowledge" aggregates health knowledge, geographic knowledge, physics knowledge, chemistry knowledge, literary knowledge, legal knowledge, biology knowledge, astronomical knowledge, psychological knowledge, economic knowledge, environmental knowledge, and religious studies knowledge (Table 3) — at least 13 distinct domains. If factual knowledge about chemistry and factual knowledge about law use different neuron subsets, lumping them into one "Knowledge" category will: (a) reduce the apparent functionality score for any single neuron (since a neuron that fires for chemistry but not law would have a weaker AP correlation with the broad label), and (b) produce the anomalous off-diagonal perturbation effects observed in Figure 9, where pruning Knowledge neurons affects multiple other capabilities substantially — perhaps because the "Knowledge" neuron set is itself a mixture of many domain-specific bricks that overlap with domain-adjacent capabilities.

The paper acknowledges this confound implicitly when it notes that Coding, Math, and Translation achieve much higher functionality scores (>0.8) than Knowledge (~0.45–0.55), Ethical (~0.4–0.5), or Writing (~0.35–0.45) (Section 4.4, Figure 8). It attributes the high scores for Coding, Math, and Translation to their structural distinctiveness from natural English — a plausible explanation. But it does not consider the alternative (or complementary) explanation that these three categories are more homogeneous sub-capability-wise than the others, and thus suffer less from the label-lumping confound. The paper would need to replicate the analysis at a finer capability granularity to disentangle these explanations; it does not.

**Second, the exclusive-capability filtering removes multi-capability instances from the analysis.** The paper states: "we retain only those instances that belong exclusively to one of the aforementioned types" (Section 4.2). This is methodologically clean — it prevents the confound of a single instance requiring, say, both coding and math, which would degrade the AP score for both — but it also means the analysis is conducted on a **non-representative sample** of single-capability prompts. In practice, many real-world instructions require multiple capabilities (e.g., "Write a Python script to solve this differential equation" requires Coding + Math; "Translate this French legal document into English" requires Translation + Knowledge). The clean separation observed in Figure 9 (diagonal dominance) and Figure 10 (low cross-capability overlap) may partially reflect the exclusive-capability evaluation protocol — when an input genuinely requires two capabilities simultaneously, the corresponding neuron sets might overlap substantially, and the current experimental design cannot detect this because such inputs are excluded. The brick framework envisions multi-brick routing for complex instructions (Section 3.2), but the empirical analysis characterizes bricks only in single-capability isolation.

**Third, the sample size (1,000 instances per capability, 7,000 total) is modest relative to the number of statistical tests.** For a model like Llama-3-8B-Instruct with `$32 \times 14336 = 458,752$` FFN neurons, computing functionality scores involves 458,752 AP calculations, each based on 1,000 positive and 6,000 negative examples (for a specific capability). The paper reports no confidence intervals, standard errors, or significance thresholds for any functionality score. A neuron with an AP of 0.8 from 1,000 examples has a non-trivial variance; differences between layers or between capabilities may not be statistically distinguishable, especially in the 0.4–0.6 range where many Knowledge, Ethical, Writing, and Linguistic best scores cluster (Figure 8). The paper attributes layer-wise patterns (e.g., "Linguistic specialization peaks early (layers 3–15) and declines in upper layers") without quantifying the uncertainty in those estimates.

The paper does not mitigate these constraints. Section 4.4 acknowledges that "in the future, we will explore more effective methods to locate function-specific neurons," but does not discuss expanding the capability taxonomy, including multi-capability instances, or increasing sample sizes. A practitioner seeking to apply the functionality score methodology to a new domain would need to independently validate the granularity choices and sample requirements.

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost (Computing Functionality Scores for All Neurons) Is Unaccounted for and Dominates the Computational Budget

The paper's experimental methodology for identifying bricks imposes a computational cost that is never quantified, budgeted, or amortized in any efficiency analysis. Computing the functionality score for a single neuron requires: (1) running full forward passes on 7,000 instances to collect per-token activations at all layers, (2) storing those activations (for Llama-3, this is `$7,000 \times \text{avg\_tokens} \times 32 \times 14336 \times \text{sizeof(float16)}$` — an enormous storage requirement), and (3) computing AP scores for each of 458,752 neurons against each of 7 capability labels. The paper provides no estimate of the FLOPs, memory, or wall-clock time this consumes.

The consequence is that the paper's efficiency claims — "the normalized indicators for 80% neurons are lower than 0.2" (Section 4.3) implying 80% computation reduction is possible — are presented without accounting for the cost of *knowing which 80% to mask*. The masking experiment (Figures 6b, 6d) shows that you *can* mask 70–80% of neurons without loss **if you know which ones to mask**. But the method for determining which ones to mask — the functionality score computation — requires full forward passes on a labeled evaluation corpus and per-neuron scoring that likely exceeds the cost of running the model unmasked on all target inputs. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be `$\text{cost}(\text{brick\_identification}) + \text{cost}(\text{sparse\_inference})$`, and the paper provides no evidence that this sum is less than `$\text{cost}(\text{dense\_inference})$`.

This is not a minor oversight — it is structurally analogous to the difficulty estimation problem that other test-time compute papers flag as a central limitation (e.g., generating 2,048 samples per question to bin by difficulty before applying the compute-optimal strategy). The fact that the paper does not flag this as a limitation in Section 5 (which discusses open problems) suggests the authors may not view it as within scope — the paper is a framework proposal, not an efficiency demonstration. But for a practitioner reading Section 4's headline numbers ("80% of neurons can be masked!"), the unstated cost of identifying those neurons is a critical caveat.

The paper partially acknowledges this implicitly by noting the need for "efficient brick computing frameworks" (Section 5.4) and "brick construction protocols" (Section 5.2), but does not address the specific cost of the functionality scoring methodology itself. No mitigation is attempted — no amortization scheme, no training of a predictor for neuron importance, no comparison of the identification cost to the inference savings.

### The Framework Excludes Attention Mechanisms from the Brick Analysis, Leaving Half the Model's Computation Uncharacterized

The paper's empirical analysis is restricted to feed-forward network (FFN) layers. The justification is: "feedforward layers in Transformer can be regarded as key-value memory networks (Geva et al., 2021) and provide world knowledge for sequence understanding. Therefore, we mainly focus on the feedforward layers for analysis" (Section 4.1). This is a reasonable scoping decision — FFNs constitute roughly two-thirds of a Transformer's parameters and have been the primary focus of prior modularity and knowledge localization work — but it means the paper's characterization of brick structure covers only part of the model.

The consequence is that the paper's empirical claims about sparsity, specialization, and partitioning apply only to FFN neurons, not to attention heads, Q/K/V projections, or attention output projections. In a standard Transformer, attention mechanisms also contain neurons (attention head outputs, value projections) that could exhibit specialization. Prior work has shown that attention heads specialize for syntactic relations, coreference, and positional patterns (Clark et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019). If attention neurons also form capability-specific bricks, then the paper's FFN-only analysis: (a) underestimates the total number of bricks in the model, (b) cannot characterize cross-mechanism brick interactions (e.g., a "coding brick" might span both specific FFN neurons and specific attention heads), and (c) cannot estimate the total computational savings achievable by sparse activation of all bricks, since attention computation (which scales quadratically with sequence length) is often the dominant cost for long inputs.

The paper does not flag this as a limitation. Section 2.1.3 mentions that "Liu et al. (2023d) dive deeper into the concept of self-organized bricks by exploring both Transformer feed-forward networks and muti-head attention layers," acknowledging that attention bricks exist, but the empirical methodology in Section 4 is not extended to attention. A practitioner interested in end-to-end brick-based inference efficiency would need to separately characterize attention brick structure, with no guidance from the paper on how to adapt the methodology.

No mitigation is attempted. The paper does not even report the fraction of total model parameters or FLOPs accounted for by the FFN analysis vs. the full model, which would help a reader calibrate the coverage of the empirical claims.

### No Demonstration of Brick Independence Under Realistic Conditions — the Perturbation Study Reveals Entanglement but Does Not Resolve It

The perturbation study (Section 4.4, Figure 9) is the paper's primary causal test of functional specialization: prune the top-5% of neurons by functionality score for capability A, then measure perplexity change on capabilities A through G. The ideal result — strong diagonal dominance with near-zero off-diagonal effects — would demonstrate that the specialized neurons for each capability are independent, such that removing one capability's brick does not affect others. The actual results reveal substantial cross-capability interference, which the paper attributes to "resident neurons" but does not empirically isolate or control.

For Llama-3-8B-Instruct (Figure 9a), pruning Knowledge-specialized neurons increases perplexity on Coding by +79%, on Math by +184%, on Ethical by +122%, and on Translation by +86% — in several cases matching or exceeding the diagonal Knowledge effect of +81%. For Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Figure 9b), pruning Translation neurons increases Coding perplexity by +391% and Writing by +477%, effects comparable to or larger than the diagonal Translation effect of +377%. Pruning Ethical neurons increases Math perplexity by +391% and Coding by +381%. These are not minor cross-contaminations — they indicate that the identified neuron sets are substantially entangled.

The paper's explanation is: "This may be due to the presence of resident neurons in the FFN (Song et al., 2023b), which are frequently activated for most inputs. Including these neurons when selecting for functional specificity results in a substantial impact on model performance. In the future, we will explore more effective methods to locate function-specific neurons" (Section 4.4). This is a post-hoc hypothesis, not a tested explanation. The paper does not: (a) identify which specific neurons in the top-5% set are resident neurons vs. genuinely capability-specific neurons, (b) measure how much of the off-diagonal effect is attributable to resident neurons, (c) re-run the perturbation study with resident neurons excluded, or (d) propose a modified functionality score that controls for baseline activation rates.

The consequence is that the partition claim — "neurons for different functionalities are distinctly different" (Section 4.5) — is only partially supported. The Jaccard similarity analysis (Figure 10) shows low set overlap (0.00–0.08 between most capability pairs), which seems to support the partition claim. But the perturbation study (Figure 9) shows that removing one capability's set often degrades other capabilities substantially, which contradicts the claim of functional independence. These two findings can be reconciled if: (a) the overlapping neurons (few in number, hence low Jaccard) are disproportionately important (hence high perturbation impact), or (b) the brick sets are distinct but functionally coupled — removing one brick's output disrupts downstream computation for other capabilities even though the neurons themselves are disjoint. The paper does not explore either possibility. For a practitioner hoping to use the identified neuron sets as independent bricks — e.g., activating only the Coding brick for a coding query — the perturbation results suggest that deactivating other bricks may still degrade performance in unpredictable ways, undermining the clean modular decomposition the framework assumes.

The paper's mitigation is aspirational: "in the future, we will explore more effective methods to locate function-specific neurons" (Section 4.4). No concrete approach is proposed, and the resident neuron confound remains unresolved in the current empirical evidence.

---

### The Framework Proposes Granularity-Agnostic Operations but Provides No Methodology for Selecting the Appropriate Granularity

The paper devotes an entire section (Section 2.3) to brick granularity, identifying four levels — solitary neuron, neuron group, layer, and full model — and arguing that "coarser-grained bricks with more parameters are better suited for addressing complex tasks" while "finer-grained bricks handle more atomic functions" (Section 2.3.5). It then defines all four brick operations (routing, combination, updating, growing) in granularity-agnostic terms (Section 3). However, the paper provides no criterion, heuristic, or empirical methodology for determining which granularity is appropriate for a given capability, operation, or deployment scenario.

This is a practical limitation because granularity choices determine the efficiency-capability tradeoff and the feasibility of operations. Consider knowledge editing (Section 3.3.1): if a fact is stored in a single knowledge neuron (solitary neuron granularity), editing requires modifying one row/column pair. If the fact is distributed across a neuron group, editing requires modifying tens or hundreds of neurons, and the locate-and-update procedure becomes substantially more complex. The paper notes that "the knowledge storage of even one entity or tuple involves multiple neurons and defining knowledge bricks at the neuron-group level and layer level may be more suitable" (Section 3.3.1), citing Hase et al. (2023). But this is an observation from prior work, not a finding of the paper, and no methodology is provided for determining the appropriate granularity for a given fact or capability.

Similarly, for routing: at what granularity should bricks be selected? Token-level routing (MoE-style) maximizes flexibility but incurs per-token overhead. Task-level routing is efficient but cannot handle multi-capability inputs. The paper notes this tension in Section 3.1.4 ("Better retrieval methods need to be designed to balance accuracy and efficiency") but does not provide guidance on how to make the tradeoff. The empirical analysis in Section 4 operates at solitary neuron granularity (each neuron gets an individual functionality score) and at neuron group granularity (top-5% sets are treated as capability bricks), but the relationship between these granularities — whether the top-5% neurons naturally cluster into co-activating groups, whether those groups map cleanly to the capability labels, whether operations are more effective at one granularity vs. another — is unexplored.

The consequence is that a practitioner attempting to implement any of the four operations on a real model faces an unguided design choice. Should they construct customized bricks as LoRA modules (neuron group granularity), train full-layer adapters (layer granularity), or merge entire fine-tuned models (model granularity)? The paper's framework says all are valid bricks and all support the same operations, but provides no empirical comparison of the tradeoffs. The section on granularity ends with: "Thus, it becomes necessary to conduct comparative analyses of the performance and costs for different brick granularities, which we leave for future work" (Section 2.3.5). This is an honest and important acknowledgment — the granularity question is genuinely open — but it means that one of the framework's central design dimensions is entirely unvalidated.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not propose a new model, a new training algorithm, or a new benchmark. It proposes a **vocabulary and a conceptual structure** for thinking about foundation models through the lens of composable functional units — bricks — and defines four primitive operations (routing, combining, updating, growing) that constitute a general grammar for configuring models. The magnitude of the contribution is therefore **a reframing rather than a paradigm shift** — the paper reorganizes existing fragmented research threads into a unified taxonomy and provides a language for describing problems that were previously studied in isolation. Whether this reframing eventually *enables* a paradigm shift (toward models designed and deployed as configurable brick assemblies rather than monoliths) depends on whether the community adopts the vocabulary and whether the operational claims — that bricks can actually be routed, combined, updated, and grown at scale — are validated by subsequent work. The paper itself provides only descriptive evidence (Sections 4.3–4.5) that the necessary enabling conditions exist; it does not demonstrate that the operations work in practice.

What is genuinely new is the **dual-source account of modularity**. By cleanly separating emergent bricks (functional neuron partitions that arise spontaneously during pre-training) from customized bricks (tiny neural modules deliberately constructed via post-training), the paper resolves a confusion that has quietly plagued the modularity literature: are LLMs modular by nature or by design? The knowledge neuron literature (Dai et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2022) studied modularity as a pre-training emergent property — things you find. The PEFT literature (Hu et al., 2022; Houlsby et al., 2019) studied modularity as an engineering practice — things you build. The MoE literature (Fedus et al., 2022b; Shazeer et al., 2017) sat in between — imposing modular architecture and observing that specialization emerged. These communities rarely cited each other and operated with incompatible assumptions. The paper's emergent/customized distinction provides a framework where all three literatures are studying the same phenomenon — bricks — arising through different mechanisms. This reconciliation is valuable because it converts a confusing set of parallel research threads into a coherent picture: pre-training builds the base brick structure; post-training extends it. Neither alone is sufficient, and future progress requires understanding how they interact (Section 5.1).

The paper also changes the **conversation around model efficiency** from a purely architectural question (how do we compress models or sparsify computation?) to a **systems question** (how do we manage a library of capabilities and dynamically compose them per instruction?). The empirical finding that 70–80% of FFN neurons can be masked per input without loss (Figures 6–7) is not new in kind — sparsity has been studied since Zhang et al. (2022c) — but the paper frames it not as a discovery about model internals but as an **inventory of exploitable redundancy**. The implication is different: rather than asking "how can we prune the model to be smaller?," the paper asks "how can we keep the model large (with all its diverse capabilities) but activate only the relevant subset per query?" This shifts the research agenda from one-time compression to dynamic capability management, which is a more natural fit for foundation models that must serve many users with heterogeneous needs.

Research directions that become more attractive after this paper include: (1) dynamic brick routing at inference time, which the MoE literature has explored only at token granularity with learned gating, but which could be extended to sentence-level and task-level routing using the functionality score methodology (Section 4.1) as a supervision signal; (2) brick combination methods that go beyond homogeneous parameter averaging (Wortsman et al., 2022) to heterogeneous stitching of fine-grained bricks across modalities and model families; and (3) continual learning as brick growth, where new capabilities are added as new customized bricks without modifying existing ones — a cleaner formulation of the catastrophic forgetting problem than replay-based or regularization-based approaches.

Research directions that become somewhat less attractive: (1) purely black-box interpretability methods that seek to explain model behavior without decomposing the model into functional units — the paper's framework implies that interpretability should be organized around brick-level analysis; (2) one-shot model compression techniques that produce a single smaller model — the configurable paradigm suggests maintaining a large repository of specialized bricks and activating subsets dynamically, which may dominate static compression in capability-preservation-per-FLOP; and (3) monolithic fine-tuning for multi-task adaptation — the paper's brick-per-task vision (Section 3.4.2) implies that constructing separate bricks for each task and routing among them is both more efficient (no interference) and more sustainable (bricks can be independently updated).

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**End-to-end brick routing with the functionality score methodology as a supervision signal.** The paper's Section 4.4 identifies neurons with high functionality scores for specific capabilities in an offline, post-hoc analysis. The immediate follow-up is to train a **predictive router** that, given only the input text, selects which neurons (or neuron groups) to activate *before* computing the FFN layers, and to evaluate whether this learned routing matches the offline-identified specialized neurons while maintaining model performance. Concretely: use the top-5% functionality-scored neurons for each of the seven capabilities as pseudo-labels for a lightweight router module (e.g., a small MLP or linear projection added before each FFN layer) trained to predict which neurons should be active for a given token. Measure: (a) routing accuracy against the offline-identified sets, (b) end-to-end task accuracy on the seven capabilities compared to the full model, and (c) actual wall-clock speedup on GPU hardware accounting for routing overhead. The negative result — that learned routers fail to recover the offline-identified brick structure or that routing overhead eats all the sparsity gains — would be equally informative, bounding the practical value of the framework.

**Resident neuron ablation to purify capability-specific brick sets.** The perturbation study (Figure 9) reveals substantial cross-capability interference: pruning Knowledge-specialized neurons degrades Coding (+79%) and Math (+184%) in Llama-3, and pruning Translation neurons degrades Coding (+391%) in Mistral. The paper's post-hoc explanation — resident neurons that are broadly active across all capabilities inflate the top-5% sets — is untested. A direct follow-up would: (a) identify resident neurons by measuring per-neuron activation variance *across* capability types (neurons with low variance — consistently active regardless of capability — are resident), (b) exclude them from the capability-specific sets, (c) recompute functionality scores and perturbation effects on the purified sets, and (d) test whether the diagonal-off-diagonal gap in Figure 9 widens. If successful, this would produce cleaner brick sets suitable for routing. If even purified neuron sets still show cross-capability interference, it would suggest that functional entanglement is deeper than the resident neuron hypothesis can explain — possibly because capabilities genuinely share neural circuits (e.g., mathematical reasoning required for both Coding and Math prompts), which would bound the achievable modular decomposition.

**Multi-granularity brick comparison: neuron-level vs. group-level vs. layer-level decomposition.** The paper argues that bricks exist at all granularities and that the four operations are granularity-agnostic, but provides no empirical comparison (Section 2.3.5 explicitly leaves this to future work). A systematic comparison would: (a) apply the functionality score methodology at neuron granularity (as in Section 4), (b) apply a similar methodology at neuron group granularity using co-activation clustering (groups of neurons that activate together for the same capability, as in Zhang et al., 2022c), and (c) apply it at layer granularity by scoring entire FFN layers for capability specialization. For each granularity, implement a routing operation (activate only the relevant units for a given capability) and measure the accuracy-vs-computation tradeoff curve. The hypothesis — consistent with the paper's claim that "coarser-grained bricks with more parameters are better suited for addressing complex tasks" (Section 2.3.5) — is that fine-grained routing enables greater computation savings but at a higher accuracy cost, while coarse-grained routing preserves more accuracy but saves less computation. Quantifying this tradeoff would directly inform deployment decisions: on-device settings with tight compute budgets might prefer neuron-level routing; server-side settings with latency SLAs might prefer layer-level routing.

**Cross-model brick transfer and stitching.** The paper's analysis identifies capability-specialized neurons independently in Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 (Figures 8–10). A natural question is whether these independently identified bricks are **functionally equivalent across models** — i.e., do the top-5% Coding neurons in Llama encode the same computational operation as the top-5% Coding neurons in Mistral? If so, bricks could be transferred between models. The experiment: (a) for a given capability, extract the activation patterns of the specialized neuron set from Model A, (b) train a small projector to map these activation patterns into the hidden space of Model B's corresponding layers, and (c) evaluate whether Model B can perform the capability using only the projected activations from Model A's brick (with Model B's other neurons masked). If successful, this would be the first demonstration of cross-model brick reuse and would support the paper's vision of a shared brick ecosystem (Section 5.2). The negative result — that bricks are model-specific and non-transferable — would suggest that brick construction protocols need to be standardized at the training stage, not discovered post-hoc.

**Capability-aware pre-training objectives that encourage cleaner brick formation.** The paper's analysis reveals that current models exhibit partial but imperfect modularity — specialization exists but is contaminated by resident neurons and cross-capability interference (Figure 9). A forward-looking research direction is to design **pre-training objectives that explicitly encourage the formation of well-separated functional bricks**. The experiment: add an auxiliary loss during pre-training that rewards sparse per-input activation (encouraging only a subset of neurons to fire) and penalizes neurons that are consistently activated across diverse input types (discouraging resident neurons). Compare the resulting model's functionality scores, perturbation diagonality, and neuron-set overlap to the standard pre-trained baseline. If successful, this would produce models with "brick-ready" internal structure, making post-hoc brick identification (Section 4) trivial and routing (Section 3.1) more reliable. This connects to the paper's call for "enhanced training strategies [that] explicitly encourage the emergence of self-organized emergent bricks" (Section 2.1.3).

**Planner-based brick stitching with LLMs as planners and bricks as tools.** The paper describes planner-based stitching (Section 3.2.2) where a task planner decomposes complex instructions into sub-tasks and assigns each to an appropriate brick. This is described conceptually but never implemented or evaluated. A concrete follow-up would: (a) train a library of capability-specific customized bricks (LoRA modules) for the 7 capabilities from Section 4, (b) use an LLM (the base model itself or a smaller planner model) to decompose multi-capability prompts into sub-tasks (e.g., "Write a Python script to solve this differential equation and explain the solution in French" → coding sub-task + math sub-task + translation sub-task), (c) route each sub-task to the appropriate brick, and (d) evaluate end-to-end accuracy on a multi-capability benchmark. The key experimental variable is the planning mechanism: compare pre-execution planning (full decomposition before any brick is invoked), reactive planning (decide next brick based on intermediate outputs), and search-based planning (try multiple brick sequences, select best). This would directly test the brick stitching operation, which the paper flags as underdeveloped for fine-grained bricks.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device personal assistants with capability-specific brick activation.** Modern smartphones and laptops run increasingly capable small LMs (7B parameters and growing) but remain compute-constrained — a full forward pass through all parameters costs battery life and introduces latency. The paper's empirical finding that 70–80% of FFN neurons can be masked per input with negligible loss (Figures 6–7) translates directly to power savings: if a personalized assistant can pre-classify an incoming query into a capability category (coding, translation, general knowledge), activate only the ~20% of neurons belonging to that capability's brick, and skip computation for the remaining ~80%, the per-query FLOP cost drops proportionally. The specificity scores in Figure 9 (e.g., Llama Coding diagonal +112% vs. off-diagonal +6–10%) suggest that for well-separated capabilities like coding, math, and translation, capability-specific routing should preserve most task accuracy. The practical challenge — unsolved by the paper — is the lightweight routing decision itself: the classifier must run faster than the computation it saves, likely requiring a tiny model or heuristic (e.g., keyword matching for "Python" or "def" → coding brick). This deployment scenario maps directly to the paper's stated motivation of "deployment on end devices" where "the use of monolithic LLMs that require substantial computational resources is gradually becoming infeasible" (Section 1).

**Modular knowledge updates for enterprise knowledge bases.** Organizations maintaining LLM-powered internal tools (legal document analysis, medical literature retrieval, technical support) face a continuous knowledge freshness problem: new regulations, research findings, and product specifications emerge weekly, but full model retraining is too expensive and fine-tuning risks catastrophic forgetting of other domains. The paper's updating operation (Section 3.3) — specifically, injecting new customized bricks that override outdated emergent knowledge — offers a targeted solution. A legal knowledge brick trained on the latest regulatory update can be plugged into the base model alongside existing bricks for contract law, intellectual property, and compliance, without touching those other bricks' parameters. The paper's description of "plug-and-play methods that inject knowledge into a frozen LLM by adding new bricks" (Section 3.3.2) directly supports this, though the paper provides no empirical validation of the injection process itself. The key advantage over retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is that the knowledge is encoded in the model's parameters and thus participates in multi-step reasoning, not just surface-level retrieval. The limitation — unvalidated by the paper — is whether multiple independently trained knowledge bricks can coexist without interference (the OOD combination problem flagged in Section 2.2.5).

**Collaborative open-source model development via a shared brick protocol.** The paper envisions a paradigm where "a shared core foundation model for open-source communities, enabling individuals to develop their bricks based on a unified protocol and openly share bricks for collective utilization" (Section 5.2). This maps to a concrete workflow: (1) a central organization releases a base model (e.g., Llama-4) along with a brick construction protocol — a standardized training recipe, module format, and insertion specification; (2) a medical researcher fine-tunes a clinical diagnosis brick on private hospital data using the protocol, shares only the brick (not the data); (3) a legal researcher independently builds a contract analysis brick; (4) a third user downloads both bricks, plugs them into their local base model, and routes medical queries to the diagnosis brick and legal queries to the contract brick. The paper's brick operations framework (Section 3) provides the conceptual blueprint for this workflow, though the infrastructure — universal brick formats, compatibility testing, conflict resolution between independently trained bricks — does not exist. The principle insight that makes this newly tractable is the emergent/customized distinction: the base model's emergent bricks handle general language understanding, while customized bricks add specialized capabilities without modifying the shared substrate, avoiding the coordination problem of distributed fine-tuning where different contributors' parameter updates conflict.

**Reduced bias and improved model safety through targeted brick repair.** The paper's traceability benefit (Section 2.4) — that decomposing models into interpretable bricks allows "the focus [to] shift to a brick-by-brick examination, allowing for the repair or replacement of bricks that may induce the model to generate unethical responses" — has a concrete safety application. If an LLM exhibits toxic or biased outputs for a specific capability (e.g., generating stereotypical associations when answering questions about certain demographic groups), the functionality score methodology (Section 4.1) could identify the neurons most predictive of those biased outputs. Those neurons could then be updated (changing their output weights to suppress the biased associations), deactivated (masked permanently), or replaced with a debiased brick trained on carefully curated data. The paper cites Lauscher et al. (2021) and Geva et al. (2022) as preliminary evidence that toxicity-related neurons can be identified and deactivated. The configurable framework adds the conceptual infrastructure: treat the toxic sub-network as a brick, operate on it in isolation (update or replace), and leave the rest of the model's capabilities intact — avoiding the whack-a-mole problem where debiasing fine-tuning fixes one bias but introduces another. The key open question — flagged in Section 5.3's discussion of coupling — is whether biased behaviors are truly isolated in specific bricks or are diffusely encoded across the model, in which case brick-level repair would be insufficient and the coupling metric (how much does modifying brick A affect brick B's behavior) becomes the critical diagnostic.
