# Expert-Specialized Fine-Tuning for Sparse Architectural Large Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2407.01906](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01906)

## 🎯 Pitch

Tuning just 5–15% of a Mixture-of-Experts model’s parameters can outperform full fine-tuning on downstream tasks while preserving general ability—because each task naturally concentrates its computation on a small, distinct subset of experts. This Expert-Specialized Fine-Tuning (ESFT) cuts parameter updates by up to 90% and training time by up to 30%, yet it matches or beats full fine-tuning across math, code, summarization, and legal tasks.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Expert-Specialized Fine-Tuning (ESFT)**, a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) large language models that selectively tunes only the experts most relevant to a downstream task while freezing all other parameters. Evaluated on DeepSeek-V2-Lite across six diverse tasks—including math (MetaMathQA), code (evol-codealpaca), intent recognition, summarization, legal judgment prediction, and low-resource translation—ESFT reduces trainable parameters by up to 90% and training time by up to 30% compared to full-parameter fine-tuning, while matching or surpassing FFT's downstream accuracy (e.g., ESFT-Gate achieves a 50.2 average vs. 51.0 for FFT and 44.9 for LoRA) and better preserving general-ability benchmarks. The effectiveness hinges on the finding that different tasks activate highly concentrated but task-distinct expert subsets, establishing that fine-grained expert specialization allows selecting only 5–15% of experts for tuning without sacrificing performance—provided the underlying MoE architecture segments experts at sufficient granularity.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: PEFT Methods Are Designed for Dense Models, Yet the Frontier Is Shifting to Sparse Architectures

The paper addresses a specific mismatch in the current LLM ecosystem: **parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques have been overwhelmingly developed and tested on dense-architecture models, while the industry's most capable and cost-efficient models increasingly use sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures.** This gap is not merely an oversight — the structure of MoE models is fundamentally different from dense models in ways that existing PEFT methods fail to exploit, and in some cases, actively work against.

To understand why this matters, consider what happens inside a dense transformer versus an MoE transformer during fine-tuning. In a dense model, every token passes through the same feed-forward network parameters. PEFT methods like LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) are designed for this reality: they add low-rank adaptation matrices to existing weight matrices, reducing the number of trainable parameters while preserving the dense model's uniform processing structure. But in an MoE model, tokens are **routed** to different subsets of experts based on learned affinity scores. Different tasks activate different combinations of experts — a property the paper demonstrates systematically in Section 3.2. This means that in an MoE model, the question "which parameters should we fine-tune?" has a natural, architecture-inherent answer: tune the experts that the task actually uses. Existing PEFT methods weren't designed to ask this question because dense models don't provide the structural signal to answer it.

The paper's central claim is that by exploiting this architectural property — what the authors call **expert specialization** — one can achieve fine-tuning that is simultaneously more parameter-efficient, more computationally efficient, and better at preserving general capabilities than either full-parameter fine-tuning or generic PEFT methods applied naively to MoE models.

### Why This Problem Is Important: Practical and Theoretical Significance

The importance of this work operates on two levels: immediate practical impact and deeper architectural understanding.

**Practical impact: the economics of LLM customization.** Large language model deployment increasingly follows a pattern where a single massive pretrained model is customized for dozens or hundreds of downstream applications — customer service chatbots, code assistants, domain-specific writing tools, translation systems, and so on. Each customization typically requires fine-tuning. If full-parameter fine-tuning is used, each customized variant requires storing a complete copy of the model's parameters (28.6 GB for the DeepSeek-V2-Lite model used in this paper, as shown in Section 5.2). For an organization maintaining 100 customized variants, that's 2.86 TB of storage — and the training cost scales similarly. This is the problem PEFT aims to solve, and the paper demonstrates that ESFT can reduce storage by up to 90% (to 2.57 GB for ESFT-Token) and training time by up to 30% (from 28.5 minutes for FFT to 19.8 minutes for ESFT-Token, Section 5.2), while matching downstream task performance.

But the practical significance goes beyond just cost. The paper also shows that ESFT **better preserves general capabilities** than full-parameter fine-tuning or LoRA. Table 2 reports that after fine-tuning on specialized tasks, ESFT-Token achieves an average general benchmark score of 61.5, versus 58.8 for FFT and 59.1 for LoRA — and some specific degradations are stark: FFT drops from 42.5 to 34.2 on IFEval (instruction following), a decline of 8.3 points, while ESFT-Token only drops to 40.7 (a decline of 1.8 points). For real-world deployments, the ability to customize a model for a specific task without breaking its general competence is arguably as important as the customization quality itself — a customer service model that becomes excellent at summarizing support calls but can no longer follow basic instructions or answer trivia questions is not practically useful.

**Theoretical significance: understanding how MoE models represent tasks.** Beyond the engineering benefits, the paper provides evidence for a specific mechanistic hypothesis about MoE models: that **expert routing is the mechanism by which these models achieve task specialization**, and that this specialization is both concentrated (few experts per task) and distinct (different experts per task). Figures 2 and 3 in Section 3.2 provide the empirical backbone for this claim. Figure 2 shows that a small number of experts handle the majority of gate values for any given task — the distribution drops sharply from the top-ranked expert to the tail. Figure 3 shows that the set of Top-6 experts shared between two different tasks is near zero (off-diagonal values are close to 0 in the heatmap), while the set shared between two samples of the *same* task is near 6 (the maximum possible, since only 6 non-shared experts are routed per token in this architecture). This is a specific, falsifiable claim about MoE internals: tasks are represented by **disjoint expert subsets**. If this finding generalizes across model families and scales, it has implications for how we design MoE architectures, how we route tokens, and even how we interpret model behavior.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies limitations in the existing PEFT landscape along two axes: the methods themselves, and the models they're applied to.

**Limitations of existing PEFT methods for MoE models.** The paper categorizes dense-model PEFT methods into three families (Section 2.1):

1. **Adding new parameters** (Adapters, Soft Prompts): These methods insert small trainable modules into the existing architecture or prepend learnable tokens to the input. In an MoE context, these additions don't interact with the routing mechanism at all — the new parameters are outside the expert system, so they neither leverage nor preserve the task-specific expert specialization that the pretrained model has learned.

2. **Selecting existing parameters** (structured or unstructured sparse fine-tuning): These methods fine-tune a subset of existing parameters based on criteria like magnitude or gradient-based importance. In principle, these *could* be applied to MoE models, but the selection criteria are generic — they don't use task-specific routing information to decide which parameters to tune. A parameter might be important for the model in general but irrelevant to the specific downstream task.

3. **Low-rank adaptation** (LoRA and variants): LoRA decomposes weight updates into low-rank matrices, reducing trainable parameters. When applied to MoE models, LoRA treats all experts uniformly — it adds low-rank adapters to every feed-forward layer regardless of whether those experts are activated by the downstream task. This means LoRA is training parameters in experts that the task rarely or never uses, which is wasteful and may introduce unnecessary interference with other tasks.

The paper's experimental results bear out these limitations. Table 1 shows that LoRA achieves an average downstream task score of 44.9, well below ESFT's 49.4–50.2 and FFT's 51.0. On specialized adaptation tasks like legal judgment prediction (17.1 → 39.7 for LoRA vs. 17.1 → 45.7 for ESFT-Token and 17.1 → 49.1 for ESFT-Gate) and low-resource translation (14.5 → 23.1 for LoRA vs. 14.5 → 35.2–36.2 for ESFT), the gap is particularly large. This suggests that LoRA's uniform treatment of experts is especially costly when adapting to tasks that are far from the model's pretraining distribution — precisely the scenario where expert specialization matters most.

**The gap in the MoE literature: understanding expert behavior during fine-tuning.** While there is considerable prior work on MoE architectures (Section 2.2 covers the major lineages from GShard to DeepSeekMoE), the paper identifies a specific empirical gap: **there is no systematic study of how expert routing behaves during fine-tuning for downstream tasks.** The MoE literature has focused on training stability (Dai et al., 2022), load balancing (Fedus et al., 2021; Lepikhin et al., 2021), and architecture design (fine-grained segmentation, shared expert isolation — the innovations of DeepSeekMoE, Dai et al., 2024). But the question of what happens to expert specialization when you fine-tune an MoE model for a specific task — and whether you can exploit that specialization for efficiency — has not been investigated.

This gap is particularly significant because full-parameter fine-tuning of an MoE model may actively **damage** the expert specialization that makes the architecture effective. The paper's analysis in Section 6.3 provides evidence for this: when all non-shared experts are trained (the "ALL" row in Table 3), general performance drops from 62.4 (vanilla model, no training) to 58.8, a decline of 3.6 points. When only task-relevant non-shared experts are trained ("Relevant, ×, ×" row), general performance only drops to 61.5, a decline of 0.9 points. This suggests that FFT is updating experts that the target task doesn't need, and those updates are overwriting capabilities those experts were responsible for in other tasks — a form of catastrophic forgetting mediated by the MoE routing structure.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions ESFT not as a competitor to existing PEFT methods in a general sense, but as a **method specifically enabled by and designed for the MoE architecture's expert specialization property.** This is a crucial distinction. The authors are not claiming that expert selection is a better parameter selection criterion than gradient magnitude or Fisher information in some absolute sense. Rather, they are claiming that the routing mechanism of a pretrained MoE model already encodes task-specific parameter importance — and that by simply reading out this signal (via gate scores or token selection ratios), one can achieve efficient fine-tuning without any additional learned importance estimators.

This positions ESFT at the intersection of two research traditions that have been largely separate:

- From the **PEFT tradition**, ESFT inherits the goal of reducing fine-tuning costs and the methodology of selecting a subset of parameters to train. But unlike prior PEFT work, the selection criterion is not generic (magnitude, gradient, Fisher) — it's task-specific and architecture-derived.

- From the **MoE tradition**, ESFT inherits the concept of expert specialization (each expert learns a specific subset of knowledge) and extends it from pretraining to fine-tuning. Prior MoE work established that experts specialize during pretraining; ESFT shows that this specialization can be preserved and exploited during customization.

The paper also makes an important implicit methodological claim: that **fine-grained expert segmentation is not just a training efficiency technique, but an enabler of downstream customization efficiency.** Section 6.4 (Figure 7) provides the key evidence: when experts are artificially grouped to simulate coarse-grained architectures (2 or 4 experts per group instead of the original fine-grained 66), ESFT's performance degrades more severely than FFT's. At a group size of 4, ESFT-Gate's MATH accuracy drops to approximately 15% (from ~23% at group size 1), while FFT drops less dramatically. The authors interpret this as evidence that coarse-grained experts mix knowledge from multiple domains, making it impossible to select a "math-relevant" expert without also selecting parameters entangled with unrelated knowledge — the very problem that fine-grained segmentation was designed to solve for pretraining, now shown to matter for fine-tuning as well.

This architectural dependence is both a strength and a limitation of the positioning. It means ESFT is not a general-purpose PEFT method that can be dropped into any model — it requires a sufficiently fine-grained MoE architecture. The authors are transparent about this, noting in the Limitations section that "due to the limitation of the availability of other fine-grained MoE models, our method was only tested on the DeepSeek-V2-Lite MoE model." But it also means that as more models adopt fine-grained MoE architectures (a trend the paper implicitly advocates for), the value of architecture-aware PEFT methods like ESFT increases.

Finally, the paper positions its contribution along two practical axes that are often in tension: **specialized performance and general capability preservation.** The standard PEFT narrative is that you trade some performance for efficiency — LoRA saves parameters but often underperforms FFT on the target task. ESFT challenges this tradeoff by showing that, on average, it can match FFT's specialized performance (50.2 vs. 51.0) while better preserving general capabilities (60.6 vs. 58.8). This is a specific empirical claim that, if replicated across models and tasks, would reposition architecture-aware PEFT from a compromise to a genuine improvement over full fine-tuning for MoE models.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper designs a **parameter-efficient fine-tuning method for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language models** that decides *which specific experts to train* based on how frequently the downstream task activates them, rather than training all parameters or adding new ones. It solves the problem of expensive customization for sparse-architecture LLMs by observing that different tasks naturally concentrate their computation in distinct, task-specific subsets of experts, and then exploits this observation by training only those activated experts (5–15% of total) while freezing everything else — achieving full-parameter fine-tuning performance at a fraction of the cost.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components organized as a pipeline:

1. **Pretrained MoE Transformer (DeepSeek-V2-Lite)** — the base language model containing fine-grained experts (66 non-shared experts per layer, plus shared experts that process all tokens). This model has already learned task-specific expert specialization during pretraining and alignment.

2. **Expert Relevance Scorer** — a lightweight module that takes a small sample of task data (32 sequences of length 4096) and computes, for each expert in each layer, a relevance score reflecting how often the downstream task routes tokens to that expert. Two scoring methods are proposed: average gate score (continuous affinity) and token selection ratio (binary selection frequency).

3. **Expert Selector** — uses the relevance scores to choose which experts to train in each layer, controlled by a threshold parameter `$p \in (0, 1]$` that sets the cumulative fraction of relevance to include. Experts whose cumulative relevance exceeds `$p$` are marked as trainable; all others (plus attention layers, embeddings, and shared experts) are frozen.

4. **Fine-Tuned Task Model** — the result of standard supervised fine-tuning, but with gradients only flowing through the selected experts. During inference, tokens continue to be routed to *any* expert (including frozen ones), but only the selected experts' parameters were updated during training.

Information flows as follows: downstream task data arrives → a small subset (32 samples) is used to compute expert relevance scores per layer → a threshold `$p$` selects the most relevant experts → the model is fine-tuned on the full training data, updating only the selected experts → the resulting model produces task-specific outputs while preserving general capabilities encoded in frozen experts.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal MoE layer computation (Equations 1–5), because understanding how tokens are routed to experts is prerequisite to understanding how ESFT selects them.
- **Second**, the probing experiments (Section 3.2) that establish *why* expert selection works — the empirical finding that expert routing is concentrated within a task and distinct across tasks.
- **Third**, the two expert relevance scoring functions — average gate score and token selection ratio — since they are the mechanism that translates routing behavior into a trainable subset.
- **Fourth**, the selection and fine-tuning procedure that converts relevance scores into a concrete training protocol.
- **Fifth**, the training configuration and hyperparameters, including the data mixing strategy and the handling of shared versus non-shared parameters — because these design choices interact with the core method in non-obvious ways.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical methods paper** whose core idea is that the routing mechanism of a pretrained MoE model already encodes which experts are important for a given task, and that reading out this signal via simple statistics over a small data sample is sufficient to select the optimal subset of experts for efficient fine-tuning.

#### Preliminaries: The MoE Layer Computation

Before explaining how experts are selected, the paper first establishes the standard Mixture-of-Experts formulation for transformers, and then the specific enhancements introduced by DeepSeekMoE that make expert specialization possible.

**Standard MoE Layer**

In a standard MoE transformer layer, the standard feed-forward network (FFN) is replaced with multiple expert FFNs operating in parallel. Each token is routed to a subset of these experts based on learned affinity scores. The computation at the `$l$`-th MoE layer for the `$t$`-th token is:

$$h^l_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( g_{i,t} \cdot \text{FFN}^n_i(u^l_t) \right) + u^l_t$$

$$g_{i,t} = \begin{cases} s_{i,t}, & s_{i,t} \in \text{TopK}(\{s_{j,t} \mid 1 \leqslant j \leqslant N\}, K) \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

$$s_{i,t} = \text{Softmax}_i \left( u^{l\top}_t e^l_i \right)$$

where `$N$` is the total number of non-shared experts, `$\text{FFN}^n_i(\cdot)$` is the `$i$`-th expert's feed-forward network, `$u^l_t$` is the input hidden state of the `$t$`-th token at layer `$l$`, `$e^l_i$` is the learned centroid vector for the `$i$`-th expert at layer `$l$`, `$s_{i,t}$` is the token-to-expert affinity score after softmax normalization, `$g_{i,t}$` is the effective gate value (equals `$s_{i,t}$` if the expert is among the top-K by affinity, `$0$` otherwise), and `$\text{TopK}(\cdot, K)$` selects the set of `$K$` highest affinity scores.

**What this computes:** Each token `$t$` at layer `$l$` is represented by a hidden vector `$u^l_t$`. The router computes, for every expert `$i$`, the dot product between the token's hidden state and that expert's learned centroid vector. The softmax normalises these dot products into a probability distribution (the affinities `$s_{i,t}$`). Only the top-K scoring experts are retained; all others have their gate value set to `$0$`. The output `$h^l_t$` is the sum of the outputs of the selected expert FFNs (each weighted by its gate value), plus a residual connection from the input `$u^l_t$`.

**Why this form:** The TopK sparsification is what makes MoE computationally efficient — the model has `$N$` experts but only `$K \ll N$` are activated per token, meaning the FLOPs per token scale with `$K$` rather than `$N$`. The softmax over dot products ensures each token selects experts based on a learned similarity metric between the token's representation and each expert's "specialisation centroid." The residual connection preserves gradient flow and allows the MoE layer to act as a refinement on top of the transformer's existing computation.

**DeepSeekMoE Enhancements**

DeepSeekMoE (Dai et al., 2024) introduces two architectural modifications that are critical for the expert specialisation that ESFT exploits:

$$h^l_t = \sum_{i=1}^{K_s} \text{FFN}^s_i(u^l_t) + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( g_{i,t} \cdot \text{FFN}^n_i(u^l_t) \right) + u^l_t$$

$$g_{i,t} = \begin{cases} s_{i,t}, & s_{i,t} \in \text{TopK}(\{s_{j,t} \mid 1 \leqslant j \leqslant N\}, K - K_s) \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where `$K_s$` is the number of shared experts and `$\text{FFN}^s_i$` denotes a shared expert.

**What this computes:** The MoE layer now has two types of experts: shared experts (`$\text{FFN}^s_i$`) that process *every* token regardless of routing (capturing common knowledge like grammar, basic syntax, and general world knowledge), and non-shared experts (`$\text{FFN}^n_i$`) that are selectively routed based on the TopK mechanism (capturing specialised, domain-specific knowledge). The number of routed non-shared experts is `$K - K_s$` rather than `$K$`, because the shared experts have already contributed their computation. Additionally, under fine-grained segmentation, each original expert is split into `$m$` smaller experts, with both `$N$` (number of non-shared experts) and `$K$` multiplied by `$m$` while keeping the same fraction of total parameters activated per token — meaning each individual expert learns a narrower, more specialised skill.

**Why this form:** Shared expert isolation reduces parameter redundancy. Without it, every expert would need to learn common operations independently, wasting capacity. By separating common knowledge into always-active shared experts, the non-shared experts can specialise more sharply — they only need to learn what makes their domain *different*, not the shared foundation. Fine-grained segmentation further amplifies this: when experts are smaller and more numerous, each one learns a narrower slice of knowledge, making the routing distribution over experts more peaked (fewer experts handle any given token) and the expert subsets per task more distinct. This is the architectural precondition for ESFT to work — without fine-grained segmentation, experts would be coarser, mixing knowledge from multiple domains, and selecting a "task-relevant" subset would inevitably include experts entangled with unrelated tasks.

#### Probing Task-Specific Expert Specialization

Before proposing ESFT, the paper empirically validates the core intuition: that MoE models route tokens from the same task to concentrated, task-distinct expert subsets. These probing experiments (Section 3.2) use the pretrained DeepSeek-V2-Lite model, which has already been through a carefully curated alignment phase that excludes math and code data. The tasks span general domains (math, code) and specialised domains (intent recognition, summarisation, legal judgment prediction, low-resource translation), as detailed in Section 4.1.

**Finding 1: Expert routing is concentrated within a task**

The authors investigate the distribution of normalised gate values per expert — computed by summing all gate values assigned to each expert across all tokens in a task's data, then dividing by the total gate values across all experts so the scores sum to 1. Figure 2 displays this distribution with experts sorted from highest to lowest normalised gate value.

The result (Figure 2): for every task studied, a small number of experts absorbs the majority of the normalised gate mass, while the tail of experts receives near-zero values. The shaded regions show variance across layers, indicating that this concentration holds consistently throughout the model depth, not just in aggregate.

**What this means:** If an expert receives a high normalised gate value, it means that the routing mechanism repeatedly selects it for tokens from this task. The sharp drop-off from the top few experts to the tail indicates that the model is *not* spreading computation uniformly across experts for a given task — it is channelling most of the work through a specialised subset.

The paper quantifies this claim in Section 6.1, where Figure 4 shows that across all six tasks, ESFT selects between 2 and 15 experts per layer out of 66 (roughly 3–23% of non-shared experts), and the average across tasks and layers is in the 5–15% range. This confirms that the concentration visible in Figure 2 translates into concrete trainability savings — you need only train the top fraction of experts to capture the overwhelming majority of the gate mass.

**Finding 2: Active experts vary significantly across tasks**

The authors investigate the overlap between expert sets used by different tasks. For each task, they independently sample two data batches from the training set and compute the set of Top-6 routed experts (the 6 non-shared experts with the highest gate values, since `$K - K_s = 6$` in this architecture) averaged across all 26 transformer layers. They then compute the size of the intersection between these expert sets — the number of experts that appear in the Top-6 for both samples.

Figure 3 presents the results as a heatmap. The diagonal values (same-task comparisons) are near 6, meaning that two independent samples from the same task share almost all of their Top-6 experts. The off-diagonal values (cross-task comparisons) are near 0, meaning that the Top-6 experts for e.g. Math have almost no overlap with the Top-6 experts for Code, or Intent, or Translation.

**What this means:** The expert routing is not just concentrated — it is *task-specific*. The model has partitioned its expert capacity such that different domains use disjoint expert subsets. Math problems repeatedly activate expert numbers, say, 12, 24, 31, 45, 53, and 58; translation tasks repeatedly activate expert numbers 3, 7, 19, 28, 41, and 63, with minimal overlap. This is the architectural basis for ESFT: if each task has a unique expert "fingerprint," then fine-tuning only that task's specific experts should improve that task without disturbing experts responsible for other tasks.

**What this is not:** Importantly, these experiments use the *pretrained* (aligned) model, not a fine-tuned one. The expert specialisation is a property that emerges during pretraining and alignment — the model learns to route different types of computation to different experts as a way of managing its capacity. ESFT is *reading out* this existing specialisation, not creating it.

#### Expert Relevance Score Functions

The core mechanism of ESFT is converting the routing behaviour into a per-expert relevance score that determines which experts to train. The paper proposes two scoring functions, each capturing a slightly different aspect of expert-task affinity.

Both functions operate on a small subset `$\mathcal{D}_s = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N_s}$` sampled from the training data `$\mathcal{D}$`, where `$x_i$` is the input sequence, `$y_i$` is the label, and `$N_s$` is set empirically to 32 samples, each with a fixed sequence length of `$L = 4096$` (Appendix C validates this sample size).

**Average Gate Score (ESFT-Gate)**

$$g^l_i = \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{j=1}^{N_s} \frac{1}{L_j} \sum_{k=1}^{L_j} g^l_{i,k}$$

where `$g^l_{i,k}$` is the gate value (Equation 2) assigned to expert `$i$` at layer `$l$` for the `$k$`-th token in the `$j$`-th training sample, and `$L_j$` is the length of sequence `$x_j$`.

**What it computes:** For expert `$i$` at layer `$l$`, average its gate value over all tokens in all sampled sequences. Because gate values are zero for experts not in the TopK, this average captures both *whether* the expert is selected (frequency) and *how strongly* it is selected (affinity magnitude). An expert that is always in the TopK with high affinity will receive a high average gate score; an expert that is sometimes selected with low affinity will receive a moderate score; an expert that is never selected will receive near-zero.

**Why this form:** The average gate score is a continuous, smoothly-varying measure of expert-task affinity that preserves magnitude information. This matters because two experts might be selected with the same frequency but different confidence levels — the one with consistently higher softmax affinity is arguably more "specialised" for the task. Using raw gate values rather than binarised selection indicators captures this nuance.

**Token Selection Ratio (ESFT-Token)**

$$r^l_i = \frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{j=1}^{N_s} \frac{1}{L_j} \sum_{k=1}^{L_j} \frac{\mathbf{1}\left( g^l_{i,k} > 0 \right)}{K}$$

where `$\mathbf{1}(g^l_{i,k} > 0)$` is an indicator function that equals `$1$` if the gate value is positive (i.e., the expert was among the TopK for that token) and `$0$` otherwise, and `$K$` is the number of experts selected per token (the TopK parameter).

**What it computes:** For expert `$i$` at layer `$l$`, compute the fraction of tokens for which this expert is selected, normalised by `$K$`. Since exactly `$K$` experts are selected per token (ignoring shared experts), the raw selection count would be `$0$` to `$K$` per token; dividing by `$K$` normalises this to a value between `$0$` (never selected) and `$1/K$` (if selected for a token, contributes `$1/K$`), with the sum over all experts equalling `$1$`.

**Why this form:** The token selection ratio treats all selections as binary — an expert is either in the TopK or not, regardless of its affinity magnitude. This is a more "democratic" measure: it counts how many tokens *use* the expert rather than how strongly. The paper's experiments show that ESFT-Token tends to select fewer experts than ESFT-Gate for the same threshold `$p$` (as seen in Figure 4, where ESFT-Token's per-layer expert counts are generally lower), and better preserves general performance (Table 2: ESFT-Token average 61.5 vs. ESFT-Gate average 60.6 on general benchmarks). This suggests that the binary selection criterion produces a more conservative, more precisely targeted expert subset.

**Design choice: why two functions?** The paper does not declare one function as universally superior. Instead, it treats them as alternatives that can be chosen based on task-specific experimental performance (Section 3.3: "Both methods can be chosen based on task-specific experimental performance"). Table 1 shows that ESFT-Gate performs better on Code and specialised tasks (43.3 vs. 41.5 on HumanEval, 49.1 vs. 45.7 on Law), while ESFT-Token performs better on Math (66.0 vs. 64.9 on GSM8K) and general ability preservation. The choice between them reflects a tradeoff: gate-based scoring includes magnitude information and tends to include more experts, which helps specialised performance but may risk more general-ability interference; token-based scoring uses only frequency and tends to be more conservative, preserving general ability at a slight cost to specialised gains.

#### Expert Selection and Fine-Tuning Procedure

Once relevance scores are computed for every expert in every MoE layer, the system selects which experts to train based on a cumulative threshold.

**Selection mechanism**

For each MoE layer `$l$`, the relevance scores `$R^l_i$` (either `$g^l_i$` or `$r^l_i$`) are sorted in descending order. Starting from the highest-scoring expert, the system accumulates scores until the cumulative sum reaches or exceeds the threshold `$p$`:

$$\sum_{i \in \mathcal{E}^l_s} R^l_i \geqslant p$$

where `$\mathcal{E}^l_s$` is the set of selected experts for layer `$l$` and `$p \in (0, 1]$` is a hyperparameter controlling the fraction of total relevance to include.

**What this computes:** For each layer independently, take the total relevance score across all non-shared experts (which sums to 1 by construction for the token selection ratio, and to the average gate mass for the average gate score), and select the minimum set of highest-scoring experts whose cumulative relevance reaches the fraction `$p$`. If `$p = 0.1$`, the system selects enough top experts to account for 10% of the total relevance mass in that layer. Because the relevance distribution is highly skewed (Figure 2), a small `$p$` typically captures only a handful of experts (2–15 out of 66, as shown in Figure 4).

**Why this form:** The cumulative threshold adapts the number of selected experts to the concentration of routing in each layer. Layers where routing is highly concentrated (few experts handle most tokens) will select fewer experts for a given `$p$`; layers where routing is more diffuse will select more. This is superior to a fixed count (e.g., "always train the top 5 experts per layer") because it respects the actual routing structure — if a task's routing is genuinely diffuse across many experts in some layers, training only 5 would miss important capacity; if routing is extremely concentrated, training 10 would waste parameters on irrelevant experts.

**Hyperparameter settings:** The paper uses `$p = 0.1$` for ESFT-Gate and `$p = 0.2$` for ESFT-Token as the primary experimental settings (Section 4.3). The difference reflects the scoring functions' properties: ESFT-Gate's continuous scores tend to be more concentrated (a few experts get very high gate averages), so a lower `$p$` still captures the key experts; ESFT-Token's binary scores are more uniform among frequently-selected experts, requiring a higher `$p$` to achieve comparable coverage. Section 6.2 explores a sweep of `$p$` values for the Math task, showing that performance saturates at `$p = 0.1$` for ESFT-Gate and `$p = 0.2$` for ESFT-Token, with diminishing returns beyond these points.

**Fine-tuning execution**

During training, only the parameters of the selected experts `$\mathcal{E}^l_s$` in each layer receive gradient updates. All other parameters remain frozen at their pretrained values:
- Non-selected non-shared experts
- All shared experts (`$\text{FFN}^s_i$`)
- All attention parameters (queries, keys, values, output projections)
- All layer normalisation parameters
- Token embeddings
- Language modelling head
- Router parameters (the expert centroids `$e^l_i$` and the TopK selection mechanism)

During both training and inference, tokens continue to be routed to **any** expert — the routing mechanism is unchanged. This means that a token may be routed to a frozen expert (which processes it using unchanged pretrained parameters) or to a trained expert (which processes it using updated, task-specialised parameters). The model's output is the sum over all routed experts, so the updated experts' contributions are blended with the frozen experts' preserved knowledge.

**Why freeze router parameters?** The paper does not explicitly discuss this, but freezing the router is a consequential design choice. If the router were trained, the expert selection set might shift — experts that were initially selected as "task-relevant" could become less relevant after routing changes, and experts that were initially frozen could become more relevant, creating a moving-target problem. Freezing the router preserves the task-specific routing pattern that motivated the expert selection in the first place, ensuring that the selected experts remain the ones that actually process the task's tokens throughout training.

**Inference behaviour:** The fine-tuned model is a standard autoregressive transformer — no special inference-time routing modifications. The only difference from the base model is that a subset of experts' parameters have been updated. This means ESFT introduces **zero inference overhead** — the model's FLOPs per token, latency, and memory footprint are identical to the base model. This contrasts with adapter-based PEFT methods, which add new parameters that must be computed at inference time (though the overhead is typically small).

---

#### Training Configuration and Data Strategy

**Data sampling for expert selection**

The expert relevance scores are computed from a subset `$\mathcal{D}_s$` of 32 samples, each with a sequence length of 4096 tokens (Section 3.3). Appendix C validates this sample size by showing that the Top-6 expert overlap between two independent samples of the same task reaches near-maximum (5–6 shared experts) when the sample size reaches `$2^{17}$` tokens (32 × 4096 = 131,072 tokens). Below this threshold, the overlap is lower and more variable.

**Why 32 samples?** This is an empirical finding, not a derived optimum. The key property is that the expert routing distribution stabilises — adding more samples does not meaningfully change which experts appear in the Top-K. The cost of computing relevance scores scales linearly with the sample size, so using the minimal sample size that achieves stable routing estimates is important for practical efficiency. At 131,072 tokens, the computation is a single forward pass through the model (to collect gate values, not to train), which is negligible compared to the subsequent fine-tuning.

**Data mixing strategy**

The paper uses a 1:1 ratio of downstream task data to alignment data during training for FFT and LoRA (Section 4.3), finding that this mixing is "highly effective in preserving general abilities obtained from the alignment phase." However, for ESFT, mixing alignment data is not beneficial — Appendix F (Tables 9 and 10) shows that ESFT with pure downstream data achieves comparable or better performance on both specialised and general tasks compared to ESFT with data mixing. The authors interpret this as evidence that "ESFT is inherently capable of adapting to downstream tasks without significant performance degradation in general tasks, even without added alignment data."

**Why this matters:** Data mixing is a standard technique to prevent catastrophic forgetting during fine-tuning. That ESFT doesn't need it suggests that the mechanism of parameter isolation (only training task-relevant experts) is a more fundamental solution to forgetting than data replay — frozen experts retain their capabilities regardless of what the trained experts learn. This is a significant practical advantage because it eliminates the need to curate and balance a mixing dataset, and it means the full training budget can be spent on the downstream task.

**Training hyperparameters (Section 4.3):**
- Batch size: 32
- Sequence length: 4096
- Maximum training steps: 500, with evaluation every 100 steps
- Learning rate for ESFT: `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`, selected from a sweep over `$\{1 \times 10^{-5}, 3 \times 10^{-5}, 1 \times 10^{-4}, 3 \times 10^{-4}\}$`
- Learning rate for FFT: `$3 \times 10^{-5}$` (different optimum)
- Learning rate for LoRA: `$1 \times 10^{-4}$` (different optimum)
- LoRA rank: 8, scaling: 2 (following Hu et al., 2021), applied to all parameters except token embeddings and language modelling head
- Hardware: 2 nodes of 8× Nvidia A100 PCIe GPUs

**Why the learning rate differs across methods:** ESFT uses a lower learning rate than FFT and LoRA. The paper doesn't provide an explicit justification, but the likely reason is that ESFT is updating fewer parameters, and those parameters are already well-aligned with the task (they are the experts the model already routes the task's tokens to). A lower learning rate prevents the updates from overshooting and disturbing the existing specialisation that made those experts relevant in the first place. FFT and LoRA are updating parameters that may be less task-aligned initially and thus benefit from larger updates.

---

#### Distinction from Alternative Design Choices

**Why not train the router?** If the goal is task specialisation, an obvious alternative would be to also train the routing mechanism so that tokens are routed *more* to the trained experts. The paper deliberately freezes the router. This preserves the pretrained routing distribution, which has been optimised over massive diverse data to balance expert utilisation and specialisation. Training the router on narrow downstream data risks collapsing routing to a subset of experts (load imbalance) and destroying the broad knowledge encoded in the full expert set. The paper's choice means ESFT works *within* the existing routing structure rather than modifying it.

**Why not train all non-shared experts?** Table 3 directly tests this: training all non-shared experts ("ALL" row, plus shared and non-expert parameters as in FFT) achieves specialised performance of 51.0 but general performance of only 58.8. Training only task-relevant non-shared experts ("Relevant, ×, ×" row) achieves 49.4 specialised and 61.5 general. The 2.7-point general-performance advantage of the restricted training set is the empirical justification for selectivity — the 1.6-point specialised-performance gap is the cost. A practitioner would choose based on whether they prioritise specialised ceiling or general robustness.

**Why not train shared experts?** The shared experts process all tokens and are designed to capture common, cross-domain knowledge. Training them on a narrow downstream task risks overwriting this common knowledge with task-specific patterns, degrading performance on everything else. Table 3 confirms this: comparing "Relevant, ×, ×" (no shared training, general=61.5) to "Relevant, ✓, ×" (shared trained, general=60.7) shows a 0.8-point decline in general performance with only a 0.4-point gain in specialised performance (49.4 → 49.8). Training shared experts is a net loss on the combined specialised+general average.

**Why not train attention or other non-expert parameters?** The paper does not ablate this directly in isolation, but Table 3 shows that adding non-expert parameter training to the relevant-non-shared-expert setting ("Relevant, ×, ×": 49.4/61.5 vs. "Relevant, ✓, ✓": 50.8/60.3) trades 1.2 general points for 1.4 specialised points. The ratio is approximately 1:1, whereas training relevant non-shared experts alone achieves a better ratio. The interpretation (Section 6.3) is that non-expert parameters (attention, embeddings) encode task-general computation patterns, and training them on narrow data causes disproportionate forgetting.

---

#### Practical Deployment Considerations

The method introduces two phases to the fine-tuning workflow:

**Phase 1: Expert selection (offline, one-time per task).** Sample 32 sequences from the task's training data, run one forward pass through the pretrained model to collect gate values, compute relevance scores per expert per layer, apply threshold `$p$` to determine the trainable subset. This phase costs approximately the compute of processing 131K tokens through the model with no backward pass — negligible compared to training.

**Phase 2: Fine-tuning (the main compute).** Run standard supervised fine-tuning with gradients masked for all parameters except the selected experts. The training time reduction (19.8 minutes for ESFT-Token vs. 28.5 minutes for FFT, from Section 5.2) comes from two sources: fewer parameters to update (smaller gradient computation and optimizer state) and the practical effect that ESFT converges in similar or fewer steps due to the selected experts already being well-aligned with the task.

**Storage implications.** After training, only the updated expert parameters need to be stored per task variant. If the base model is 28.6 GB and ESFT-Token trains 2.57 GB of parameters (Section 5.2), then maintaining 100 task-specific variants requires storing the 28.6 GB base model once, plus 100 × 2.57 GB = 257 GB of task-specific deltas, for a total of 285.6 GB. Full-parameter fine-tuning would require 100 × 28.6 GB = 2,860 GB — an order of magnitude more. This is the practical economic argument for ESFT in multi-task deployment scenarios.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing PEFT as Architecture-Aware Parameter Selection—Not Parameter Addition or Compression

The dominant PEFT paradigm for dense models has been anchored in two strategies: **add new, small trainable components** (Adapters, Soft Prompts) or **compress the update into a low-rank subspace** (LoRA). Both strategies treat the model as a monolithic black box whose internal structure—beyond the existence of weight matrices—is irrelevant to deciding *where* to place the trainable capacity. LoRA adds low-rank adapters uniformly to all attention and FFN layers regardless of their functional role; Adapters insert bottleneck modules at standardized positions regardless of what those layers compute.

ESFT makes a fundamental conceptual break from this tradition. It argues that the **MoE routing mechanism already encodes per-task parameter importance**—and that reading out this signal is a simpler, more effective selection criterion than any learned or heuristic importance estimator applied to a dense model. The insight is not "we found a better scoring function for selecting parameters." It's that **the architecture itself is the importance estimator**. The router's job during pretraining is to *assign computation to specialists*; ESFT repurposes this assignment as a *training-time selection mask*. No auxiliary model, no Fisher information computation, no gradient-based pruning—just forward-pass statistics over 32 samples.

This reframing is considerable because it changes the relationship between pretraining and fine-tuning. In the standard PEFT view, pretraining produces a good initialization, and fine-tuning adapts it—the two phases are sequential but conceptually independent. In ESFT's view, **pretraining not only sets the weights but also discovers the modular structure that fine-tuning should respect**. The routing distribution isn't an artifact to be preserved or ignored; it's the primary signal for how to customize the model. Fine-tuning becomes an act of *reading and respecting* the structure that pretraining built, rather than overwriting or supplementing it.

Comparing to prior parameter-selection methods makes the distinction clear. Structured pruning approaches (Guo et al., 2020; Vucetic et al., 2022) select parameters based on magnitude or gradient saliency—criteria that are task-agnostic and don't leverage any domain signal from the architecture. Diff Pruning (Guo et al., 2020) learns a sparse mask during fine-tuning, but the mask is optimized as part of training, not read out from pretrained routing behavior. ESFT's selection criterion—average gate score or token selection ratio—is computed in a single forward pass before training begins, with no optimization, no gradients, and no hyperparameter search beyond the threshold `p`. It exploits a signal that exists only in MoE architectures and has no analog in dense models.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond efficiency numbers. It suggests a design principle: **architectures that make specialization explicit during pretraining enable more efficient customization during deployment**. Fine-grained MoE isn't just about training cost; it's about creating models whose internal modularity can be read and exploited post hoc. If this principle generalizes, it implies that future architecture design should consider not just pretraining efficiency (FLOPs, stability, load balancing) but also *downstream inspectability*—how easily can a practitioner identify and isolate the subnetwork responsible for a given capability?

Evidence for this reframing comes less from a single table and more from the overall structure of results. The probing experiments (Figures 2 and 3) establish that the routing signal *exists*; the main results (Table 1) establish that selecting experts based on this signal *works* (ESFT matches FFT); the ablation replacing relevant experts with random ones (Table 4) establishes that the signal is *causal* (random selection degrades performance by 2.8–4.4 points on average, with catastrophic drops like −20.4 on Translation for ESFT-Gate). Taken together, these findings argue that the routing distribution isn't merely correlated with task relevance—it encodes it.

### Innovation 2: Expert Specialization as a Fine-Tuning Constraint, Not Just a Pretraining Property

The MoE literature has long observed that experts specialize during pretraining—this is the motivation for load-balancing losses, for fine-grained segmentation, and for shared expert isolation (Dai et al., 2024; Fedus et al., 2021). But prior work treated expert specialization as a **static architectural property**: you design the model so experts specialize during training, and then you deploy it. What happens to that specialization *during fine-tuning* was, before this paper, essentially unexamined.

ESFT introduces a new diagnostic claim: **full-parameter fine-tuning damages expert specialization, and this damage explains why FFT degrades general capabilities**. The evidence is in Table 3 and the surrounding analysis in Section 6.3. When all non-shared experts are trained (FFT), general benchmark performance drops from 62.4 to 58.8—a decline of 3.6 points. When only task-relevant non-shared experts are trained (ESFT-Token, "Relevant, ×, ×"), general performance drops only to 61.5—a decline of 0.9 points. The key comparison: both methods achieve similar specialized performance (51.0 for FFT vs. 49.4 for ESFT-Token, a gap of 1.6 points), but FFT pays a 2.7-point larger penalty in general ability.

What this says is that **the specialized-to-general performance tradeoff is mediated by *which* experts you train, not just *how much* you train**. FFT is updating experts that the downstream task doesn't use—experts responsible for capabilities in other domains—and those updates are overwriting useful behaviors. It's a form of catastrophic forgetting, but with a specific mechanistic interpretation: forgetting isn't a diffuse degradation of all knowledge; it's the targeted corruption of experts that were specialized for now-irrelevant tasks.

This is a more precise claim than the standard "fine-tuning causes forgetting" narrative. It identifies **where** in the model forgetting occurs (in the non-task-relevant experts, and particularly in shared experts and non-expert parameters) and **why** (because those parameters encode capabilities for other tasks that are overwritten by narrow-domain training signals). Table 3 quantifies this: training shared experts costs 0.8 general points with minimal specialized gain (61.5 → 60.7 general, 49.4 → 49.8 specialized); training non-expert parameters costs 1.2 general points for 1.4 specialized points (ESFT-Token vs. "Relevant, ✓, ✓"); training all non-shared experts costs 2.7 general points for 1.6 specialized points (ESFT-Token vs. "ALL"). The cost per specialized point is lowest for training relevant non-shared experts and highest for training all parameters indiscriminately.

The practical implication is a direct challenge to the default assumption that fine-tuning should update all parameters. For MoE models—and potentially for any model with explicit modularity—**selective freezing is not a compromise to save compute; it's a strategy to preserve capabilities that full-parameter training actively damages**. This inverts the standard PEFT framing. PEFT is typically presented as "we achieve almost FFT performance at lower cost." ESFT reframes it as "FFT achieves our performance at higher cost and with more collateral damage."

The deeper insight is about the **role of modularity in continual learning**. The MoE architecture, with its explicit decomposition into specialized experts and shared experts, creates a natural separation between "knowledge that should be updated for this task" (relevant non-shared experts) and "knowledge that should be preserved across tasks" (shared experts, irrelevant non-shared experts, attention, embeddings). A dense model has no such separation—all parameters are entangled, and selective fine-tuning requires post hoc importance estimation. The MoE architecture builds the separation into the model structure, and ESFT exploits it. This suggests that architectural modularity is a form of **built-in continual learning prior**—and that as models are deployed in more multi-task settings, architectures that make knowledge boundaries explicit will have an inherent advantage over monolithic dense models.

This contribution is conceptual (a new interpretation of what happens during fine-tuning) and diagnostic (a specific, testable claim about where forgetting occurs). It is not a metric gain per se—the numbers in Table 3 are ablation results, not benchmark records. But the reframing it provides—from "PEFT is a cost-saving approximation of FFT" to "FFT is a destructive over-update that PEFT can improve upon"—is a substantial shift in how the field should think about model customization for modular architectures.

### Innovation 3: The Granularity-Specialization-Tunability Link as an Architectural Design Principle

The paper's analysis in Section 6.4 establishes a three-way relationship that, to the authors' knowledge, has not been previously articulated: **finer-grained experts → sharper specialization → more effective selective fine-tuning**. This is not an incremental finding about hyperparameters; it's a claim about how architectural decisions at pretraining time constrain what's possible at fine-tuning time.

The experiment (Figure 7) is a simulation: take the fine-grained DeepSeek-V2-Lite (66 non-shared experts per layer) and artificially group experts into coarse-grained clusters of size 2 or 4, simulating what the model would look like if it had been trained with fewer, larger experts. As group size increases (experts become coarser), ESFT's performance on MATH degrades from approximately 23% (group size 1, the original fine-grained model) to approximately 18% (group size 2) to approximately 15% (group size 4). FFT degrades as well, but less severely—the gap between ESFT and FFT *widens* as experts become coarser. Simultaneously, the average number of trained experts increases from roughly 4–8 at group size 1 to roughly 8–16 at group size 2 to roughly 16–32 at group size 4—so ESFT is both performing worse *and* training more parameters as granularity decreases.

The interpretation: coarse-grained experts mix knowledge from multiple domains. When the model has only a few large experts, each expert must handle diverse token patterns—a single expert might process math tokens, code tokens, and summarization tokens simultaneously. Selecting that expert as "task-relevant" for math means also dragging along the code and summarization knowledge embedded in the same parameters. Training that expert on math data updates the code and summarization knowledge as well, causing interference. Fine-grained experts avoid this: when experts are small and numerous, the routing can assign *different* (though perhaps neighboring) experts to math, code, and summarization, and the selection mechanism can pick the math-specific expert without entangling the others.

This is the same logic that motivated fine-grained segmentation for pretraining (Dai et al., 2024)—allowing each expert to specialize more narrowly, reducing knowledge interference, improving parameter efficiency. What the paper shows is that **this logic extends to fine-tuning**. The architectural property that improves pretraining also improves customizability. This is not obvious a priori. One could imagine that fine-tuning a coarse-grained model might be equally effective—you select the expert that handles the task, and since that expert is larger, it has more capacity to adapt. The results suggest the opposite: the **selectivity** enabled by fine granularity matters more than the **capacity** of individual experts. Being able to isolate the exact subnetwork responsible for math, without including adjacent code-related parameters, is worth more than having a larger subnet with mixed responsibilities.

The broader implication is an architectural design principle: **expert granularity is a control variable for the precision of post-hoc module isolation**. If you know at design time that your model will be fine-tuned for many diverse downstream tasks (a multi-tenant deployment scenario), building it with finer-grained experts provides a form of **architectural disentanglement** that makes selective fine-tuning more effective. This is a different optimization target than typical MoE design, which prioritizes training stability, load balance, and FLOPs efficiency. It suggests that "how easily can this model be customized without damaging other capabilities?" should be a first-class evaluation criterion for foundation models, and that architecture choices directly affect the answer.

A counterpoint the paper does not fully explore: finer granularity means more experts overall, which increases the difficulty of the selection problem (more candidates to score) and potentially increases the fragility of the routing distribution (smaller experts may have less stable routing patterns). The paper's finding that 32 samples suffice for stable expert identification (Appendix C) provides some reassurance, but this may not hold at larger scales or with different routing mechanisms. The granularity-tunability link is established as an empirical phenomenon on one model family; whether it is a robust scaling law or a model-specific observation remains open.

The significance of this insight is that it creates a feedback loop between deployment requirements and architecture design. If the field moves toward large-scale multi-task fine-tuning (hundreds of customized variants from one base model), the optimal pretraining architecture is not just the one with the best perplexity-per-FLOP—it's the one whose internal modularity best supports selective adaptation. ESFT provides the first concrete evidence for this connection and a methodology for measuring it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on six tasks spanning two customization scenarios. For *model enhancement* (improving existing capabilities): Math uses MetaMathQA (Yu et al., 2023) for training, evaluated on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021a); Code uses the Python subset of evol-codealpaca (Luo et al., 2023) for training, evaluated on HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021). For *model adaptation* (learning unfamiliar tasks): Intent Recognition, Text Summarization, and Legal Judgment Prediction use competition datasets from BDCI challenges; Low-resource Translation uses the ChrEn Cherokee-English dataset (Zhang et al., 2020). Task examples appear in Appendix A (Table 5). For general ability evaluation, seven benchmarks are used: MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021b), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), CEval (Huang et al., 2023), and CLUEWSC (Xu et al., 2020).

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use DeepSeek-V2-Lite (DeepSeek, 2024), a fine-grained MoE model with 66 experts per transformer layer (including shared experts). The model is first trained on a curated alignment dataset that *excludes* math and code data, producing a "vanilla model" checkpoint that serves as the starting point for all fine-tuning experiments. This alignment phase activates capabilities across domains while keeping Math/Code ability at an elementary level to better verify performance gains from domain-specific training. The choice of DeepSeek-V2-Lite is driven by its fine-grained expert segmentation — at the time of the study, it was uniquely suitable among available models for testing whether expert specialization enables selective fine-tuning. The authors acknowledge in the Limitations that this constrains generalizability, as no other fine-grained MoE models were available for cross-validation.

- **Metrics.** Downstream task performance is measured differently per task type. For Intent Recognition (text-to-JSON), the metric is **exact match** between model output and reference answer. For Summarization, Legal Judgment Prediction, and Translation, **GPT-4 scoring** is used: GPT-4 (specifically `gpt-4-1106-preview`) assigns a score from 0 to 10 comparing the model's prediction to the reference answer, using detailed rubrics provided in Appendix G (Table 11). For Math and Code, standard accuracy metrics are used (GSM8K and MATH solve rates; HumanEval and MBPP pass@1). All evaluations use few-shot prompting. General ability benchmarks use their standard metrics as reported in their respective publications. Training efficiency is measured by **training time** (minutes per 500 steps on 2×8 A100 nodes) and **storage space** (GB of trainable parameters that must be stored per task variant).

- **Baselines.** Two primary baselines are compared: (1) **Full-Parameter Fine-Tuning (FFT)**: all model parameters are updated during training with learning rate `3e-5`. (2) **Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)** (Hu et al., 2021): low-rank matrices added to all parameters except token embeddings and the language modeling head, with rank 8, scaling 2, and learning rate `1e-4`. For all methods, a 1:1 ratio of task-specific data to alignment data is maintained during training to preserve general abilities — a ratio found effective for FFT and LoRA but not necessary for ESFT (as analyzed in Appendix F). The vanilla (untrained) model serves as an additional reference point. ESFT itself is evaluated in two variants: **ESFT-Token** (using token selection ratio as relevance score, `p=0.2`) and **ESFT-Gate** (using average gate score, `p=0.1`), both with learning rate `1e-5`.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute efficiency is measured along two dimensions: **training time** (wall-clock minutes for 500 steps with batch size 32 and sequence length 4096 on 2 nodes of 8× NVIDIA A100 PCIe GPUs) and **trainable parameters** (GB of storage required for the fine-tuned weights). The paper does not use "generations" as a compute metric since all methods perform standard supervised fine-tuning with the same batch size and sequence length. The efficiency advantage of ESFT comes from reduced backward-pass computation (fewer parameters receive gradients) and reduced optimizer state memory. For the efficiency sweep in Section 6.2, the hyperparameter `p` (for ESFT) and rank (for LoRA) serve as proxies for computational resource usage, with higher values corresponding to more trainable parameters and potentially better performance.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the main results, each task is trained once with maximum 500 steps, evaluated every 100 steps, and the best checkpoint (by downstream task performance) is reported. Table 2 reports standard deviations *across tasks* (i.e., the variance of a given method's general-ability scores across the four adaptation tasks), not across random seeds or data splits. No k-fold cross-validation is used for strategy selection, unlike the compute-optimal policy search in the reference example. The expert relevance scoring uses a fixed sample of 32 sequences (sequence length 4096), with Appendix C demonstrating that this sample size produces stable expert identification (Top-6 expert overlap between two independent samples saturates near 6 at `2^17` tokens). For the coarse-grained simulation experiment (Section 6.4, Figure 7), greedy expert grouping is used based on co-occurrence statistics computed from alignment data.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Downstream Task Performance: ESFT Matches FFT, Substantially Outperforms LoRA

Table 1 presents the core performance comparison across all eight tasks. The headline finding is that **ESFT achieves near-FFT performance while training only 5–15% of parameters**, and dramatically outperforms LoRA on specialized adaptation tasks.

**Aggregate averages (Table 1, "Average" column):** ESFT-Gate achieves 50.2 across all eight tasks, compared to 51.0 for FFT (a gap of 0.8 points, or 1.6%) and 44.9 for LoRA (a gap of 5.3 points, or 10.6%). ESFT-Token achieves 49.4, a gap of 1.6 points from FFT. Both ESFT variants substantially close the gap between LoRA and FFT, recovering approximately 87–93% of the FFT-LoRA performance difference.

**Model enhancement tasks (Math and Code):** On MATH, ESFT-Gate achieves 23.2 versus FFT's 23.4 and LoRA's 20.6 — essentially matching FFT. On GSM8K, ESFT-Token achieves 66.0 versus FFT's 66.4 and LoRA's 58.9 — again matching FFT. On HumanEval, ESFT-Gate achieves 43.3, which is actually the best result across all methods (FFT: 42.1, LoRA: 39.6, vanilla: 42.1). The vanilla model's already-decent performance on Code (42.1 on HumanEval, 44.6 on MBPP) explains why gains are modest — these are enhancement tasks where the base model has non-trivial capability, and fine-tuning provides marginal improvements of 0–3 points. The important finding is that ESFT achieves these marginal gains without degrading other capabilities, while FFT achieves similar gains at the cost of general-ability regression (as shown in Table 2).

**Model adaptation tasks (specialized domains):** This is where the gap between ESFT and LoRA becomes pronounced. On Intent Recognition, ESFT-Gate achieves 78.6 (FFT: 78.8, LoRA: 67.8) — LoRA trails by 11 points. On Summarization, ESFT-Gate achieves 65.8 (FFT: 69.4, LoRA: 64.7) — a tighter spread. On Legal Judgment Prediction, ESFT-Gate achieves 49.1 (FFT: 47.0, LoRA: 39.7) — notably, ESFT *exceeds* FFT on this task by 2.1 points while LoRA trails FFT by 7.3 points. On Low-resource Translation, ESFT-Token achieves 36.2 (FFT: 38.4, LoRA: 23.1) — LoRA's performance is dramatically worse, trailing ESFT by 13.1 points. The pattern is consistent: for tasks far from the model's pretraining distribution, LoRA's uniform treatment of experts fails to capture the specialized knowledge needed, while ESFT's targeted expert selection closely approximates FFT's ability to adapt.

**LoRA's underperformance pattern:** LoRA's weakness is most severe on tasks requiring specialized domain knowledge that the pretrained model lacks. Translation (LoRA: 23.1 vs. ESFT: 35.2–36.2, gap of ~12–13 points), Legal Judgment (LoRA: 39.7 vs. ESFT: 45.7–49.1, gap of ~6–9 points), and Intent Recognition (LoRA: 67.8 vs. ESFT: 75.6–78.6, gap of ~8–11 points) all show large ESFT advantages. On Math and Code — domains where the pretrained model already has some capability — LoRA's gap narrows (GSM8K: LoRA 58.9 vs. ESFT 64.9–66.0, gap of ~6–7 points; MATH: LoRA 20.6 vs. ESFT 22.6–23.2, gap of ~2–3 points). This suggests that LoRA's parameter-efficient mechanism is sufficient for *refining* existing capabilities but insufficient for *acquiring* new specialized knowledge — precisely the regime where selective expert training (which updates the domain-specific subnetworks directly) is most valuable.

---

#### General Ability Preservation: ESFT Retains Capabilities Better Than FFT and LoRA

Table 2 reports performance on seven general benchmarks, averaged across the four adaptation tasks (Intent, Summary, Law, Translation) with standard deviations capturing cross-task variance. The headline: **ESFT-Token achieves the best general-ability average (61.5), outperforming both FFT (58.8) and LoRA (59.1), and losing only 0.9 points from the vanilla model's 62.4.**

**Per-benchmark analysis:** The pattern is not uniform across benchmarks — some tasks are more sensitive to fine-tuning interference than others. On IFEval (instruction following), FFT suffers a catastrophic drop from the vanilla model's 42.5 to 34.2 (±4.1 across tasks) — a decline of 8.3 points. LoRA drops to 38.7 (±2.5), a decline of 3.8 points. ESFT-Token achieves 40.7 (±1.3), a decline of only 1.8 points — preserving 78% of the original capability while FFT preserves only 19%. On HellaSwag (commonsense reasoning), FFT drops from 74.0 to 67.9 (±3.8), while ESFT-Token achieves 72.3 (±3.6) — essentially preserving the original performance. On ARC-Challenge, ESFT-Token achieves 52.9 (±1.5) versus vanilla's 53.7 — a negligible decline of 0.8 points — while FFT drops to 48.4 (±2.4). On CLUEWSC, both ESFT variants match the vanilla model's 81.5 within sampling error (80.9 for ESFT-Token, 81.4 for ESFT-Gate), while LoRA drops substantially to 74.3 (±7.7) with very high variance across tasks.

**The cross-task variance is informative:** FFT shows high standard deviations on several benchmarks (±4.1 on IFEval, ±3.8 on HellaSwag, ±2.4 on ARC), indicating that different adaptation tasks cause different degrees of general-capability damage under full-parameter training. ESFT-Token consistently shows *lower* standard deviations (±1.3 on IFEval, ±1.5 on ARC, ±0.5 on MMLU), suggesting that by isolating updates to task-relevant experts, the method produces more predictable and uniform general-capability preservation regardless of which downstream task is being learned.

**ESFT-Gate vs. ESFT-Token on general ability:** ESFT-Token (61.5 average) outperforms ESFT-Gate (60.6) by 0.9 points on general benchmarks — a consistent but modest advantage. The gap is largest on HellaSwag (72.3 vs. 68.2, a 4.1-point difference) and smallest on MMLU (57.1 vs. 57.0). This aligns with the earlier observation that ESFT-Token selects fewer experts (Figure 4) and uses a binary selection criterion (token selection ratio) that produces more conservative expert subsets, leaving more experts frozen and their knowledge intact.

#### Computational Efficiency: ESFT Reduces Storage by Up to 90% and Training Time by Up to 30%

Figure 5 (Section 5.2) reports training time and storage space. **ESFT-Token trains in 19.8 minutes with 2.57 GB of trainable parameters; ESFT-Gate trains in 20.9 minutes with 3.20 GB; FFT requires 28.5 minutes and 28.6 GB; LoRA requires 16.5 minutes (trainable parameter storage not explicitly reported in GB, but implied to be small given the low-rank decomposition).**

**Training time reduction:** ESFT reduces training time by 30% compared to FFT (19.8 minutes vs. 28.5 minutes). This comes from two mechanisms, though the paper doesn't decompose them: fewer parameters to update means smaller gradient computation graphs and less optimizer state to maintain; additionally, the learning rate for ESFT (`1e-5`) is lower than FFT (`3e-5`), and the paper notes that training proceeds for a maximum of 500 steps with evaluation every 100 steps — ESFT may converge faster due to the selected experts already being well-aligned with the task. LoRA's 16.5 minutes is the fastest, but as shown in Table 1, this speed comes with substantially worse downstream performance.

**Storage reduction:** The storage comparison is the more dramatic finding from a deployment perspective. ESFT-Token trains 2.57 GB of parameters — only 9.0% of FFT's 28.6 GB. ESFT-Gate trains 3.20 GB — 11.2% of FFT. Table 3 provides additional granularity: training only task-relevant non-shared experts ("Relevant, ×, ×") requires 1.4 GB of trainable parameters; adding shared experts brings it to 1.85 GB ("Relevant, ✓, ×"); adding non-expert parameters brings it to 2.7 GB ("Relevant, ✓, ✓"). The storage savings translate directly to multi-tenant deployment scenarios: maintaining 100 task-specific variants with ESFT-Token would require 28.6 GB (base model) + 100 × 2.57 GB = 285.6 GB total; with FFT, it would require 100 × 28.6 GB = 2,860 GB.

**Table 3 context:** The "ALL" row (which is effectively FFT) trains 15.7B parameters, achieves 51.0 specialized and 58.8 general, for an average of 54.9. The "Relevant, ×, ×" row (ESFT-Token with the specific settings from this ablation) trains 1.4B parameters — **91% fewer** — and achieves 49.4 specialized and 61.5 general, for an average of 55.4. On the *combined* specialized-plus-general average, ESFT with 9% of the trainable parameters actually *outperforms* FFT (55.4 vs. 54.9). This is the paper's strongest single-number argument that selective expert training is not a compromise but an improvement.

#### Expert Selection Analysis: 5–15% of Experts Capture the Task-Relevant Signal

Figure 4 (Section 6.1) visualizes the number of experts selected for training across all 26 transformer layers and all six tasks. The key observations:

**Per-layer expert counts:** The average number of trained experts per layer ranges from 2 to 15 out of 66 non-shared experts. This represents 3–23% of non-shared expert parameters per layer, with the typical range being 5–15% (roughly 3–10 experts). Across all layers and tasks, ESFT consistently trains a small fraction of available experts. The paper notes that this corresponds to 75–95% fewer trainable parameters than FFT for the MoE layers.

**ESFT-Token vs. ESFT-Gate expert counts:** ESFT-Token generally selects fewer experts than ESFT-Gate, visible in Figure 4 where the ESFT-Token bars tend to be lower than ESFT-Gate bars for the same task and layer. This is consistent with the scoring function design: ESFT-Token's binary selection ratio produces a sparser relevance distribution (experts either are or aren't selected frequently), while ESFT-Gate's continuous gate scores spread relevance more evenly. Despite selecting fewer experts, ESFT-Token achieves comparable specialized performance (49.4 vs. 50.2, Table 1) and better general performance (61.5 vs. 60.6, Table 2), suggesting that the additional experts selected by ESFT-Gate contribute marginal specialized gains at the cost of modest general-ability interference.

**Task-dependent variation:** More specialized tasks tend to use fewer experts. Math and Translation — the two most domain-specific tasks — show the lowest expert counts per layer (typically 2–8). Tasks like Summarization and Intent Recognition, which involve more varied language patterns, tend to use more experts (5–15). The paper's interpretation: "our method's performances for these tasks exceed LoRA to the greatest extent, indicating that our method is especially suitable for more specialized tasks." For highly specialized tasks, the expert routing is more concentrated, making the relevant-expert subset more distinct and easier to isolate.

**Layer-dependent patterns:** For most tasks, the middle layers of the transformer show the fewest trained experts — expert distribution is more concentrated in these layers. The paper does not speculate on why middle layers exhibit higher concentration, but this is consistent with the general finding in transformer interpretability that middle layers encode more task-specific syntactic and semantic patterns while early and late layers handle more generic processing (token embedding refinement and prediction preparation, respectively).

#### Efficiency Tradeoff Analysis: ESFT Outperforms LoRA at Every Compute Level

Section 6.2 (Figure 6) explores how ESFT and LoRA perform under different efficiency settings on the Math task. For ESFT, the hyperparameter `p` controls the threshold; for LoRA, the rank controls capacity. The x-axis shows the average number of trained experts per layer (for ESFT) or the rank value (for LoRA). The y-axis shows both MATH accuracy (specialized ability) and the average of seven general benchmarks (general ability).

**Key finding 1: ESFT dominates LoRA at every comparable efficiency level.** Both ESFT-Token and ESFT-Gate achieve higher MATH accuracy than LoRA at any setting where the number of trained parameters is comparable. At the lowest resource settings (p=0.05 for ESFT, low rank for LoRA), ESFT maintains a clear specialized-ability advantage while showing more stable general ability. As resources increase, the gap narrows but never closes.

**Key finding 2: ESFT-Token and ESFT-Gate have different saturation points.** ESFT-Token reaches peak specialized ability at p=0.5 and peak general ability also at p=0.5, with approximately 7–8 trained experts per layer at this setting. ESFT-Gate reaches peak specialized ability at p=0.3 and peak general ability at p=0.1, with specialized performance actually *declining* slightly at higher p values (visible as a small downward slope in the MATH line beyond p=0.3). This suggests that ESFT-Gate, which uses continuous gate values, is more susceptible to including less-relevant experts at higher thresholds — the continuous scores mean that beyond the truly essential experts, the marginal expert added contributes noise rather than signal.

**Key finding 3: All methods show a performance-efficiency tradeoff, but the tradeoff curve differs.** FFT sits at the high-resource extreme (training all experts) and achieves MATH accuracy of approximately 23.4. ESFT-Token reaches comparable accuracy (approximately 22.6) at p=0.5, training roughly 8 experts per layer. Increasing p beyond 0.5 (up to and including FFT's full training) provides diminishing returns — the curve flattens. This is the empirical basis for the claim that most experts are not relevant to task performance (Section 6.1: "ESFT-Token and ESFT-Gate performance saturates at p=0.2 and p=0.1, respectively, indicating that most expert choices may be less relevant to task performance").

**Key finding 4: ESFT preserves general ability better than LoRA across the efficiency spectrum.** The general-ability line for ESFT-Token remains relatively flat across p values (holding steady around the vanilla model's level), while LoRA's general ability shows more degradation as rank increases. This is consistent with the earlier finding that ESFT's parameter isolation (freezing non-task-relevant experts) is inherently more protective of general capabilities than LoRA's distributed low-rank updates, which modify all layers and experts simultaneously.

#### Shared vs. Non-Shared Parameter Training: A Detailed Ablation

Table 3 (Section 6.3) is perhaps the paper's most informative single analysis, systematically varying which parameter groups are trained. The experiment compares configurations based on three binary choices: whether all non-shared experts or only task-relevant ones are trained; whether shared experts are trained; and whether non-expert parameters (attention, embeddings, gates) are trained.

**Finding 1: Specialized performance increases monotonically with trainable parameters.** The rank ordering by specialized ability is: "ALL" (15.7B params, 51.0) > "Relevant, ✓, ✓" (2.7B, 50.8) > "Relevant, ✓, ×" (1.85B, 49.8) > "Relevant, ×, ×" (1.4B, 49.4) > "×, ✓, ✓" (1.3B, 49.0) > "×, ✓, ×" (450M, 47.4). The correlation between trainable parameter count and specialized performance is near-perfect — each additional parameter group adds fractional performance gains. This is the standard "more parameters, better fit" pattern.

**Finding 2: General performance decreases as trainable *shared* parameters increase.** The key comparison: starting from "Relevant, ×, ×" (general=61.5) and adding shared expert training → "Relevant, ✓, ×" (general=60.7, a drop of 0.8 points). Adding non-expert training → "Relevant, ✓, ✓" (general=60.3, a further drop of 0.4 points). Training all non-shared experts → "ALL" (general=58.8, a catastrophic drop). The pattern is that training shared parameters is disproportionately damaging to general ability — the shared experts encode common knowledge, and overwriting them with narrow-domain updates causes broad degradation.

**Finding 3: It is better to train task-relevant non-shared experts than to train more parameters indiscriminately.** Compare "Relevant, ×, ×" (1.4B params, average=55.4) to "×, ✓, ✓" (1.3B params, average=54.5). Both configurations train roughly the same number of parameters, but the configuration that targets task-relevant non-shared experts achieves 0.9 points higher combined average. Compare "Relevant, ✓, ×" (1.85B, average=55.3) to "ALL" (15.7B, average=54.9) — ESFT with 12% of the parameters achieves a *higher* combined average than full-parameter training. This inverts the standard PEFT narrative.

**Finding 4: Training only shared experts is the worst use of parameters.** The "×, ✓, ×" configuration (train only shared experts, 450M params) achieves 47.4 specialized and 61.2 general — the lowest specialized performance among all training configurations despite achieving good general preservation. This confirms that shared experts encode general-purpose computation patterns that are not well-suited for domain-specific adaptation; training them on narrow data produces neither good specialization nor meaningful capability preservation beyond what freezing them would achieve.

Based on these findings, the paper proposes two deployment strategies (Section 6.3):
1. **Prioritize specialized ability:** Train task-relevant non-shared experts plus all shared parameters and non-expert parameters ("Relevant, ✓, ✓"). This maximizes specialized performance (50.8) at the cost of modest general-ability regression (60.3, a 2.1-point drop from vanilla).
2. **Balance specialized and general ability with computational efficiency:** Train only task-relevant non-shared experts ("Relevant, ×, ×"). This achieves the best specialized-to-general tradeoff (49.4/61.5) with the fewest parameters (1.4 GB), making it optimal for multi-tenant deployment scenarios.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Expert relevance score function validation (Table 4):** Replacing the experts selected by ESFT's relevance scores with an equal number of randomly selected experts causes substantial performance degradation across all tasks. For ESFT-Token, the average drop (∆ of rand) is −2.8 points across eight tasks, with severe drops on Translation (−13.5) and GSM8K (−3.7). For ESFT-Gate, the average drop is −4.4 points, with catastrophic degradation on Translation (−20.4) and substantial drops on HumanEval (−4.3) and Intent (−5.0). The random-expert baseline confirms that expert selection is causal, not merely correlated with task performance — the specific experts identified by the relevance scores are genuinely more important for the task than an equal number of arbitrarily chosen experts.

- **Fine-grained vs. coarse-grained expert segmentation (Figure 7):** Simulating coarse-grained experts by grouping fine-grained experts (using greedy co-occurrence-based grouping as described in Appendix B) reveals that ESFT's advantage over FFT depends critically on fine granularity. At group size 1 (the original fine-grained model), ESFT achieves MATH accuracy of approximately 22–23% for both variants. At group size 2, ESFT-Token drops to approximately 20% and ESFT-Gate drops to approximately 18%. At group size 4, both drop further to approximately 15%, while FFT drops only to approximately 20%. Meanwhile, the average number of trained experts rises from ~4–8 at group size 1 to ~16–32 at group size 4. The convergence of ESFT toward worse performance with *more* trained parameters as granularity decreases is the key evidence that coarse-grained experts entangle knowledge from multiple domains, making selective training both less effective (the selected expert contains irrelevant knowledge that gets corrupted) and less efficient (more experts must be trained to capture the task-relevant signal spread across coarser modules).

- **Sample size for expert relevance scoring (Appendix C, Figure 8):** The number of shared Top-6 experts between two independent data samples from the same task increases with sample size and saturates at approximately `2^17` tokens (32 samples × 4096 tokens = 131,072 tokens). Below this threshold, overlap is lower and more variable across tasks. At 131,072 tokens, all six tasks show 5–6 shared Top-6 experts (out of a maximum of 6), indicating that the expert routing distribution has stabilized and the identified expert subset is robust to sampling variation. This validates that 32 sequences of length 4096 are sufficient for the expert selection phase — using more data would increase the selection cost without meaningfully changing which experts are chosen.

- **Data mixing strategy (Appendix F, Tables 9 and 10):** For FFT and LoRA, mixing alignment data with task data at a 1:1 ratio improves general-task performance (FFT general average improves from 58.5 to 58.8; LoRA improves from 55.0 to 59.1) while causing minor downstream-task regression (FFT downstream average drops from 52.7 to 51.0; LoRA drops from 47.1 to 44.9). For ESFT, data mixing does *not* provide this benefit — ESFT-Token's general average slightly *decreases* from 62.3 to 61.5 with mixing, and ESFT-Gate decreases from 62.2 to 60.6. Downstream performance is essentially unchanged (ESFT-Token: 50.0 vs. 49.4; ESFT-Gate: 49.7 vs. 50.2). The interpretation: ESFT's parameter isolation already prevents catastrophic forgetting, so data mixing provides no additional regularization benefit. In fact, mixing alignment data may dilute the training signal for the selected experts without meaningfully protecting the frozen ones. This is a non-obvious and practically important finding — it means ESFT users can skip the data mixing step entirely and focus the full training budget on the downstream task.

- **Math and Code as general tasks after adaptation training (Appendix H, Table 8):** When models are fine-tuned on adaptation tasks (Intent, Summary, Law, Translation) and then evaluated on Math and Code benchmarks (which were not in the training data), FFT shows severe degradation: MATH drops from vanilla's 19.6 to 15.1 (±0.3), and GSM8K drops from 55.9 to 40.3 (±5.3). LoRA shows even worse degradation: MATH drops to 11.8 (±0.6), GSM8K drops to 36.1 (±4.4). ESFT-Token preserves near-vanilla performance: MATH at 19.4 (±0.8), GSM8K at 55.2 (±0.7). The average across four benchmarks: vanilla=40.5, FFT=31.5, LoRA=28.1, ESFT-Token=39.7, ESFT-Gate=39.8. Both ESFT variants lose less than 1 point on average, while FFT loses 9 and LoRA loses 12.4. This is further evidence that the experts responsible for Math and Code are largely disjoint from those used for Intent, Summary, Law, and Translation — freezing them during adaptation training preserves their capabilities intact.

- **Negative result: ReST^EM optimization degrades revision performance.** Not applicable to this paper — there is no reinforcement learning or iterative self-improvement component. However, the grouping experiment in Section 6.4 serves as an analogous negative result: attempting to apply ESFT to coarser-grained MoE architectures (which is a natural extension one might attempt) degrades performance disproportionately, showing that the method is architecture-dependent in a way that limits its immediate applicability to the broader MoE landscape.

- **ESFT-Gate vs. ESFT-Token tradeoff:** Across all experiments, ESFT-Gate tends to achieve slightly higher specialized performance (50.2 vs. 49.4 average in Table 1) while ESFT-Token achieves slightly better general-ability preservation (61.5 vs. 60.6 in Table 2). The consistency of this pattern suggests a genuine tradeoff between the two scoring functions: gate-based selection, which incorporates affinity magnitude, is more aggressive about including experts with strong but potentially broad task affinities, yielding better specialization at the cost of slightly more interference; token-based selection, which uses only binary frequency, is more conservative, yielding better preservation at the cost of slightly less adaptation capacity. The paper does not provide guidance on when to choose which, stating only that "both methods can be chosen based on task-specific experimental performance" (Section 3.3).

- **LoRA hyperparameter fairness:** The paper uses LoRA with rank=8 and scaling=2 applied to all parameters except embeddings and LM head, following the original LoRA paper. A potential concern is whether LoRA with higher rank or applied selectively to specific layers would close the gap with ESFT. The efficiency sweep in Figure 6 partially addresses this by varying rank up to 512 (at which point LoRA exceeds FFT's trainable parameter count). LoRA's performance saturates well before rank=512, suggesting that increasing LoRA capacity does not close the ESFT performance gap — it's not that LoRA has too few parameters, but that its uniform application across all experts is inherently less effective than ESFT's targeted selection.

### Critical Assessment

**Does ESFT match or surpass full-parameter fine-tuning performance?**

The paper's central claim is that ESFT "not only improves the tuning efficiency, but also matches or even surpasses the performance of full-parameter fine-tuning" (Abstract). The evidence partially supports this but requires careful qualification.

On the "matches" claim: across the eight-task average, ESFT-Gate achieves 50.2 versus FFT's 51.0 (Table 1), a gap of 0.8 points or 1.6%. Whether this qualifies as "matching" depends on one's tolerance — it is a small gap, but it is consistently in FFT's favor. On individual tasks, ESFT matches or exceeds FFT on HumanEval (43.3 vs. 42.1), Legal Judgment Prediction (49.1 vs. 47.0), and MBPP (41.8 vs. 42.2 — a near-tie). On other tasks, FFT maintains a lead: GSM8K (66.4 vs. 64.9–66.0), MATH (23.4 vs. 22.6–23.2), Intent (78.8 vs. 75.6–78.6), Summary (69.4 vs. 65.4–65.8), Translation (38.4 vs. 35.2–36.2). The "surpasses" claim — that ESFT beats FFT — is supported on 2 of 8 tasks (HumanEval, Legal Judgment Prediction), with ESFT-Gate achieving the best result on HumanEval.

The more defensible claim, based on the data, is that **ESFT achieves performance within a small margin of FFT while training ~90% fewer parameters and better preserving general capabilities**. The combined specialized-plus-general average (Table 3) shows ESFT-Token at 55.4 versus FFT at 54.9 — ESFT actually leads on this combined metric. The Abstract's framing somewhat overstates the specialized-performance claim in isolation, but the broader argument (that ESFT is a net improvement when considering the full deployment picture) is well-supported.

A genuine limitation: the paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for the differences between methods. The 500-question MATH test set and the GPT-4 scoring on specialized tasks both introduce variance, and without error bars, it's unclear whether the 50.2 vs. 51.0 difference is statistically meaningful or within noise. The standard deviations reported in Table 2 are cross-task, not cross-run — they don't capture the uncertainty from re-running the same experiment with different random seeds.

**Does ESFT better preserve general capabilities than FFT?**

This claim is strongly supported. Table 2 shows ESFT-Token achieving 61.5 versus FFT's 58.8 on general benchmarks, a 2.7-point advantage. Table 8 (Appendix H) provides even stronger evidence: ESFT preserves Math and Code capabilities nearly intact after adaptation training (average drop <1 point), while FFT loses 9 points and LoRA loses 12.4 points. The mechanism — parameter isolation prevents interference — is plausible and consistent with the ablation in Table 3, which shows that training non-task-relevant experts and shared parameters is the primary source of general-ability degradation.

However, one caveat: the paper compares ESFT without data mixing to FFT *with* data mixing (1:1 alignment-to-task ratio). Appendix F shows that FFT without data mixing achieves *better* downstream performance (52.7 vs. 51.0) but *worse* general performance (58.5 vs. 58.8 at the aggregate, though with high variance). The paper's main comparison uses data-mixed FFT, which slightly disadvantages FFT on specialized tasks and slightly advantages it on general tasks. A comparison against FFT without data mixing would show a larger downstream-performance gap (in FFT's favor) and a similar general-performance gap (in ESFT's favor). The choice of baseline doesn't invalidate the conclusion — ESFT still preserves general capabilities better — but it slightly shifts the specialized-performance comparison.

**Is fine-grained expert segmentation the enabling factor?**

The grouping experiment (Figure 7) provides convincing evidence that ESFT's effectiveness degrades as expert granularity decreases. At group size 4, ESFT's MATH accuracy drops to approximately 15% while FFT maintains approximately 20%. This is a stark finding: ESFT goes from being competitive with FFT at fine granularity to being substantially worse at coarse granularity.

However, the experiment has limitations. It uses a *simulation* — artificially grouping fine-grained experts post hoc — rather than comparing genuinely coarse-grained and fine-grained models trained from scratch. A truly coarse-grained model might develop different routing patterns and expert specializations that partially compensate for the reduced granularity. The authors acknowledge this: "due to the lack of parameter-wise and structurally aligned MoE models with different expert granularities, we used a simulation approach." The simulation tells us what happens when we degrade an existing fine-grained model's structure, but not what would happen in a world where MoE models were designed with coarser experts from the start. This is a meaningful limitation that the paper is transparent about.

A stronger test would require training two MoE models with identical total parameters and training data but different expert granularities (e.g., 8 experts vs. 64 experts, with the same total FFN parameters), then applying ESFT to both. The paper's single-model evaluation makes its granularity claim suggestive rather than definitive.

**Does expert routing genuinely reflect task-specific specialization?**

The probing experiments (Figures 2 and 3, Section 3.2) demonstrate that expert routing is concentrated within tasks and distinct across tasks. This is clean, well-visualized evidence. However, the paper measures "specialization" purely through routing statistics — it doesn't demonstrate that the experts routed to a given task actually *encode task-specific knowledge* in a causal sense. The random-expert ablation (Table 4) partially addresses this by showing that replacing selected experts with random ones degrades performance, but this shows that the selected experts are *important for the task*, not that they are *specialized to the task*. An expert could be important for many tasks simultaneously — it might encode general reasoning capabilities used across domains — and still pass the concentration test (it would have high gate values for multiple tasks).

The paper's definition of specialization is essentially statistical: an expert is "specialized" for a task if it receives a disproportionate share of gate values for that task's tokens and not for other tasks' tokens. This is a reasonable operational definition, but it's worth noting that it differs from a causal definition (an expert is specialized if ablating it damages the task and only that task). The paper doesn't perform causal ablation studies on individual experts to verify that they encode task-specific rather than task-general knowledge.

**Is the expert relevance score method robust?**

The random-expert baseline (Table 4) supports the validity of the relevance scores — random selection is significantly worse, with drops of 2.8–4.4 points on average and catastrophic failures on Translation. However, the paper does not compare against other principled expert selection methods that an informed practitioner might consider: selecting experts based on gradient magnitude after a few training steps, selecting based on Fisher information, or using a learned importance predictor. The only comparison is against random selection. This is a weak baseline — random selection is expected to be bad. The paper would be strengthened by showing that gate-based selection outperforms alternatives like "select the experts with the largest weight norms" or "select experts based on their importance to the pretraining loss."

**What about tasks where the vanilla model has near-zero capability?**

The adaptation tasks (Intent, Summary, Law, Translation) all show large improvements from fine-tuning — the vanilla model's performance is very low (Intent: 16.8, Law: 17.1, Translation: 14.5; Table 1). ESFT handles these tasks well, achieving large gains. A natural question is whether ESFT would work for tasks even further from the pretraining distribution — tasks where the vanilla model's routing distribution might not be informative because the model has no relevant experts to route to. The paper doesn't test such tasks. All adaptation tasks, while specialized, are still within the broad domain of natural language — text-to-JSON conversion, summarization, legal text, and translation are all text-in/text-out tasks that a general LLM has some exposure to during pretraining. ESFT's reliance on pretrained routing distributions means it might fail for genuinely novel tasks (e.g., a new modality, a new symbolic system, a task requiring knowledge the model was never exposed to) where the router has no basis for assigning tokens to relevant experts. The paper does not discuss this boundary condition.

**Single model family, single architecture**

The paper's most significant limitation, acknowledged by the authors, is that all experiments use DeepSeek-V2-Lite — a single model from a single model family with a specific MoE architecture (fine-grained experts with shared expert isolation). The findings may not transfer to:
- Coarse-grained MoE models (e.g., Mixtral with 8 experts) — the grouping experiment suggests ESFT would underperform.
- MoE models without shared expert isolation — the paper's analysis in Table 3 suggests that training shared experts is costly, but if the architecture doesn't separate shared and non-shared experts, the expert selection strategy might need modification.
- Models with different routing mechanisms (e.g., hash-based routing, learned routing without TopK sparsity).
- Models at different scales — it's unclear whether the 5–15% expert selection ratio scales with model size or is specific to the 16B-parameter scale of DeepSeek-V2-Lite.

The paper positions fine-grained expert segmentation as the key enabler, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite was, at the time of writing, one of the only publicly available models with this property. This is a defensible but significant constraint on generality.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Varying the number of fine-tuning steps beyond 500:** The paper uses a fixed maximum of 500 steps. Would ESFT's advantage over FFT grow or shrink with more steps? One might expect FFT to eventually overfit more severely, widening the general-ability gap.
- **Testing with different random seeds:** All results are single-run. Variance estimates would clarify which differences are meaningful.
- **Comparison against other PEFT methods for MoE:** The paper only compares against LoRA, the most popular PEFT method for dense models. MoE-specific PEFT methods (if any exist) or adaptations of dense PEFT methods (e.g., applying LoRA only to routed experts, or using adapter modules per expert) are not tested.
- **Expert selection with different sample sizes below 32:** Appendix C shows that 32 samples works, but the minimum viable sample size for each task is not determined — for highly concentrated tasks like Translation, perhaps 8 samples would suffice, further reducing the selection cost.
- **Cross-task expert overlap quantification:** Figure 3 shows near-zero overlap for Top-6 experts between tasks, but this is a binary overlap measure. A continuous measure (e.g., Jaccard similarity of the top-20% of gate-mass experts) would provide more nuance about partial overlaps that might matter for tasks with shared subtasks (e.g., legal summarization vs. general summarization).

**Conditional nature of the claims:**

The paper's claims hold under the following conditions, which should be made explicit:
1. The base model uses a fine-grained MoE architecture (on the order of ~60+ non-shared experts per layer) with shared expert isolation.
2. The downstream task is within the broad domain of the model's pretraining distribution (text-in/text-out) such that the router has learned meaningful expert assignments.
3. The practitioner has access to enough task data to compute stable expert relevance scores (approximately 32 sequences of 4096 tokens, or ~131K tokens).
4. The deployment scenario values both specialized performance and general-capability preservation — if only specialized performance matters and storage is unlimited, FFT remains marginally better.
5. The task's expert routing distribution is sufficiently concentrated that a small `p` (0.1–0.2) captures the majority of gate mass — for tasks with diffuse routing across many experts, ESFT would need a higher `p` and the efficiency advantage would shrink.

When these conditions are met, the paper provides convincing evidence that ESFT offers a favorable performance-efficiency-preservation tradeoff compared to both full-parameter fine-tuning and LoRA. When they are not met — particularly the fine-grained architecture requirement — the paper's own analysis suggests ESFT would underperform.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Single Model Family, Single Architecture: No Cross-Model Validation

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use exactly one base model: DeepSeek-V2-Lite, a fine-grained MoE architecture with 66 non-shared experts per layer and shared expert isolation. The authors are transparent about this constraint in the Limitations section:

> "due to the limitation of the availability of other fine-grained MoE models, our method was only tested on the DeepSeek-V2-Lite MoE model. The conclusions drawn from this model require further validation when applied to other contexts."

This is not just a "we didn't have time to run on more models" caveat — it's a structural limitation that follows directly from the paper's core claim. ESFT's effectiveness depends on fine-grained expert specialization, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite was, at the time of writing, one of the only publicly available models implementing this architecture (DeepSeekMoE's fine-grained segmentation with shared expert isolation, as described in Dai et al., 2024).

**The consequence.** A practitioner who wants to apply ESFT to a different MoE model — say, Mixtral 8×7B (8 experts, 2 activated per token) or DBRX (16 experts, 4 activated) — has no empirical guidance from this paper about whether the method will work. The grouping experiment in Section 6.4 (Figure 7) provides a *simulation* suggesting that coarser granularity degrades ESFT disproportionately, but a simulation is not the same as a genuine cross-model comparison. Coarse-grained models trained from scratch might develop different routing behaviors (e.g., experts that are more multi-purpose by design) that partially compensate for reduced granularity in ways the simulation cannot capture. Conversely, genuinely coarse models might show even worse ESFT degradation than the simulation predicts if their experts are less specialized than artificially-grouped fine-grained experts.

Beyond expert count, the paper provides no evidence about whether ESFT transfers across:
- Different routing mechanisms (learned TopK vs. hash-based vs. reinforcement-learning-based routing).
- Different shared expert configurations (models with no shared experts, models where shared experts are also routed rather than always-active).
- Different model scales (DeepSeek-V2-Lite is ~16B parameters; whether the 5–15% expert selection ratio holds at 70B or 200B+ scale is unknown).
- Different pretraining mixtures (DeepSeek-V2-Lite's alignment data explicitly excludes math and code; a model pretrained on a different data distribution may show different expert specialization patterns).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The grouping experiment (Figure 7, Section 6.4) is the primary attempt to probe architectural dependence. When the 64 non-shared experts are artificially grouped into 32 (group size 2) or 16 (group size 4) coarse-grained clusters, ESFT's MATH accuracy degrades from ~23% to ~18% to ~15%, while FFT degrades less severely (to ~22% and ~20%). The average number of trained experts per layer *increases* from 4–8 to 16–32 as group size increases — ESFT is training more parameters for worse performance. This strongly suggests that coarse-grained architectures are hostile to the method. However, the experiment uses greedy co-occurrence-based grouping (Appendix B) on an already-trained fine-grained model, which may not faithfully replicate the expert specialization patterns of a genuinely coarse-grained model trained from scratch.

**Mitigation status.** The authors acknowledge this as the paper's primary limitation and call for future work to validate on other architectures. No attempt is made to compensate for the single-model constraint — there are no experiments with LoRA applied to different MoE architectures, no comparisons of expert routing statistics across model families, and no theoretical argument for why the results should generalize. The grouping experiment is a partial mitigation (it suggests the boundary condition — fine granularity is necessary), but it is a simulation, not a validation. A practitioner considering ESFT for a non-DeepSeekMoE architecture would need to replicate the probing experiments (Figures 2 and 3) to determine whether their model exhibits the concentrated, task-distinct expert routing that ESFT relies on.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For in Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** ESFT requires computing expert relevance scores before fine-tuning begins. This involves running a forward pass of the full model on 32 sequences of length 4096 tokens (131,072 tokens total, as validated in Appendix C) to collect gate values, then computing per-expert statistics. The paper's headline efficiency numbers — 19.8 minutes training time, 2.57 GB storage for ESFT-Token (Section 5.2, Figure 5) — do not include this expert selection phase. The authors acknowledge this implicitly in Section 3.3 when describing the procedure but do not discuss its cost relative to the training budget.

**The consequence.** The true end-to-end cost of deploying ESFT is: (cost of expert selection forward pass) + (cost of fine-tuning the selected experts). For the settings in this paper, the forward pass cost is small relative to 500 training steps — processing 131K tokens through a 16B-parameter model is on the order of seconds, while 500 training steps at batch size 32 and sequence length 4096 is on the order of 20 minutes. So the overhead is negligible in absolute terms. However, this becomes less obvious in different deployment scenarios:

- **Very small fine-tuning budgets:** If a practitioner wants to fine-tune for only 50 or 100 steps (rather than 500), the expert selection forward pass becomes a larger fraction of total compute. The paper doesn't characterize how ESFT performs at very low step counts, so a practitioner operating in an extremely compute-constrained regime cannot estimate the break-even point where the selection overhead outweighs the training savings.

- **Multi-task deployment:** The paper frames the storage savings as a major advantage for multi-tenant scenarios (Section 5.2 highlights that ESFT-Token stores only 2.57 GB of parameters per task variant versus 28.6 GB for FFT). But the expert selection phase must be run *per task*, and for an organization deploying hundreds of task variants, the cumulative selection cost could become non-trivial. If each selection phase processes 131K tokens, and the organization deploys 100 tasks, that's 13.1M tokens of forward passes — equivalent to a non-trivial fraction of a single fine-tuning run.

- **Dynamic or evolving tasks:** If the downstream task distribution changes over time (e.g., a customer service model that needs to handle new product categories), the expert selection might need to be re-run. The paper provides no guidance on how stable the selected expert set is over time or how frequently re-selection is needed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None — the paper does not measure, report, or discuss the computational cost of the expert selection phase. Appendix C characterizes the *sample size* needed (32 sequences) but not the associated compute. Section 5.2's efficiency measurements (Figure 5) are for the fine-tuning phase only. This is not hidden — the paper clearly describes the two-phase procedure — but the lack of cost accounting means the headline "30% training time reduction" is an upper bound on the actual end-to-end savings.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not suggest cheaper alternatives for expert selection (e.g., using fewer samples, or estimating relevance from a fraction of the model's layers rather than all 26). Given the saturation curve in Appendix C (Figure 8), it is possible that 16 or even 8 samples would produce sufficiently stable expert identification for many tasks, which would cut the selection cost by 2–4×. But this is not tested. The paper also does not explore whether expert relevance scores could be cached or transferred across related tasks (e.g., if Intent Recognition and Summarization share some expert overlap, could selection be amortized?). A more thorough treatment would include the selection cost in the efficiency comparison and explore the tradeoff between selection accuracy (sample size) and selection cost.

### No Confidence Intervals or Statistical Significance: Single-Run Results Throughout

**The assumption or constraint.** Every performance number in the paper is reported from a single experimental run. Table 1 reports exact values (e.g., ESFT-Gate MATH accuracy 23.2) with no error bars. Table 2 reports standard deviations *across tasks* (the variance of a method's general-ability scores across the four adaptation tasks), which is a measure of cross-task consistency, not run-to-run variability. The paper does not mention running experiments with multiple random seeds, multiple data splits, or multiple initializations.

**The consequence.** A practitioner reading the paper cannot determine whether the reported differences between methods are statistically reliable or within noise. Several of the paper's key claims depend on small numerical differences:

- **"ESFT matches FFT performance":** ESFT-Gate's average specialized score is 50.2 versus FFT's 51.0 (Table 1, difference of 0.8 points or 1.6%). On a 500-question MATH test set, a difference of 0.8 percentage points corresponds to 4 questions — well within the range of sampling noise. The same applies to ESFT-Token's 49.4 versus FFT's 51.0 (difference of 1.6 points, or ~8 questions). Without confidence intervals, it is impossible to know whether ESFT is genuinely slightly worse than FFT on average (as the point estimates suggest) or statistically indistinguishable (which would be a stronger claim in ESFT's favor).

- **"ESFT better preserves general capabilities":** ESFT-Token's general-ability average of 61.5 versus FFT's 58.8 (Table 2, difference of 2.7 points) is larger but still modest relative to the cross-task standard deviations (£1.1 for ESFT-Token, £1.3 for FFT). On benchmarks like MMLU (57.1 vs. 55.5, difference of 1.6 points) or TriviaQA (66.7 vs. 65.9, difference of 0.8 points), the gaps are within typical run-to-run variance for LLM evaluation. The claim that ESFT preserves general capabilities better is *directionally* supported across seven benchmarks, but the magnitude of the advantage is uncertain.

- **"ESFT surpasses FFT on specific tasks":** ESFT-Gate beats FFT on HumanEval (43.3 vs. 42.1, difference of 1.2 points) and Legal Judgment Prediction (49.1 vs. 47.0, difference of 2.1 points). The HumanEval test set has only 164 problems — a 1.2-point difference corresponds to 2 problems. This could easily be sampling noise.

The efficiency numbers (training time, storage space) are deterministic given the model architecture and batch size, so no statistical uncertainty applies there. But the performance comparisons — which are the paper's primary scientific contribution — are reported without any quantification of uncertainty.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides some implicit evidence that certain differences are robust: the consistent pattern across tasks (ESFT outperforms LoRA on 7 of 8 tasks for ESFT-Token and 8 of 8 for ESFT-Gate, Table 1; ESFT better preserves general ability on 6 of 7 benchmarks for ESFT-Token and 5 of 7 for ESFT-Gate, Table 2) suggests that the ESFT advantage is unlikely to be pure noise. The random-expert ablation (Table 4) shows large performance drops (average -2.8 for ESFT-Token, -4.4 for ESFT-Gate) that are clearly outside any plausible noise range. The grouping experiment (Figure 7) shows monotonic degradation as group size increases, again unlikely to be noise. So the paper's qualitative findings (ESFT > LoRA, expert selection matters, granularity matters) are well-supported even without formal statistics. But the *quantitative* findings — how much better ESFT is, whether it genuinely matches or surpasses FFT — remain uncertain.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss statistical methodology, does not report confidence intervals, and does not acknowledge this as a limitation. Adding multi-seed experiments would increase computational cost by a factor of 3–5× (depending on the number of seeds), which may have been prohibitive given that each experiment already involves 500 training steps on 2 nodes of 8× A100 GPUs per task. However, even reporting evaluation-time variance (e.g., by bootstrapping the test set) would provide some indication of uncertainty without requiring additional training runs. The cross-task standard deviations in Table 2 are a partial substitute (showing that the method's general-ability preservation is consistent across different fine-tuning tasks), but they don't address run-to-run variability for a fixed task.

### The Generalization Gap: No Evidence on Tasks Beyond Text-Based Benchmarks

**The assumption or constraint.** All six downstream tasks evaluated in the paper are text-in/text-out tasks: math word problems, code generation, intent-to-JSON conversion, text summarization, legal judgment prediction, and translation. These tasks, while diverse in domain (mathematical reasoning, programming, legal analysis, low-resource translation), share a fundamental structure: they require processing natural language input and producing natural language (or structured text) output. The pretrained model (DeepSeek-V2-Lite) has been exposed to all of these modalities during its large-scale pretraining and alignment — even the specialized tasks (Intent, Law, Translation) are within the broad distribution of text-based tasks that a general LLM encounters during training.

**The consequence.** ESFT's core mechanism — reading out expert routing distributions from a pretrained model — assumes that the router has learned *some* meaningful assignment of tokens to experts for the downstream task. If the task is genuinely novel — something the pretrained model was never exposed to, or something in a modality the model wasn't designed for — the routing distribution may be uninformative. The router might assign tokens essentially randomly (since it has no learned basis for distinguishing task-relevant patterns), in which case ESFT's expert selection would be no better than random selection. Table 4 shows that random expert selection causes severe degradation (average -2.8 to -4.4 points, with catastrophic drops on Translation of -13.5 to -20.4). So in a regime where the router provides no signal, ESFT would collapse to the random-expert baseline — which is substantially worse than LoRA, and dramatically worse than FFT.

This is not a hypothetical concern. Consider tasks that are genuinely out-of-distribution for current LLMs:
- Processing a new structured data format the model was never trained on (e.g., a proprietary binary encoding, or a domain-specific diagramming language).
- Tasks requiring real-time interaction with external tools or APIs where the model must learn a calling convention from scratch.
- Multimodal tasks where the MoE model was pretrained on text only and must now handle image or audio inputs through a newly added encoder — the router has no basis for assigning the new modality's representations to existing experts.
- Tasks in a programming language or natural language that didn't appear in pretraining data.

For all such cases, the paper provides no evidence about whether ESFT would work, and the method's reliance on pretrained routing patterns makes failure plausible.

Even within the text domain, the paper's task selection has a specific property: the adaptation tasks (Intent, Law, Translation) all show very low vanilla-model performance (14.5–17.1 in Table 1), meaning the pretrained model has essentially no capability on these tasks. Yet ESFT succeeds at adapting to them, which suggests the router *does* provide a meaningful signal even for tasks the model cannot perform. This is somewhat surprising and not fully explained. One possibility: the router assigns tokens based on surface-level or syntactic features that correlate with task domain (e.g., legal terminology activates certain experts even if the model doesn't understand legal reasoning). If this is the mechanism, ESFT would work for text-based tasks with distinctive lexical features but might fail for tasks that are lexically similar to pretraining data but structurally novel (e.g., a new reasoning format using familiar vocabulary). The paper provides no analysis of *what features* drive routing for unfamiliar tasks, leaving practitioners to guess at the boundary conditions.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None — the paper does not discuss out-of-distribution generalization, does not test tasks outside the text modality, and does not analyze whether the routing signal for unfamiliar tasks is driven by surface features or deeper semantic properties. The observation that adaptation tasks with near-zero vanilla performance still show concentrated, task-distinct routing (visible in Figures 2 and 3, where Translation and Law both show sharp expert distributions despite low baseline accuracy) is intriguing but not explained. The paper also does not test tasks that are close relatives of the adaptation tasks but with subtle differences (e.g., translating Cherokee to Spanish rather than English, or predicting legal outcomes in a different jurisdiction) to determine whether the expert selection generalizes across related tasks or is task-instance-specific.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The authors do not claim that ESFT works for all tasks, and the paper's scope is clearly delimited to the six evaluated tasks. But the absence of any discussion of task boundaries — *when* the method is expected to work versus fail — is a gap for practitioners. A simple diagnostic is implied by the method itself: if the expert routing distribution for a new task is diffuse (no clear concentration), ESFT is unlikely to work well, and FFT or LoRA would be preferable. But the paper does not provide this guidance explicitly, nor does it test how to determine in advance whether a task will benefit from ESFT.

### Practical Guidance Gaps: Hyperparameter Selection and the Gate-vs-Token Choice

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper presents two expert relevance scoring functions — ESFT-Gate (average gate score) and ESFT-Token (token selection ratio) — and treats them as equally valid alternatives, stating that "both methods can be chosen based on task-specific experimental performance" (Section 3.3). The threshold `p` is set to 0.1 for ESFT-Gate and 0.2 for ESFT-Token based on a hyperparameter sweep on the Math task (Section 6.2), but the sweep is not shown for other tasks. The paper provides no principled guidance for how a practitioner should choose between ESFT-Gate and ESFT-Token, or how to set `p`, for a new downstream task.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying ESFT on a new task faces a non-trivial model selection problem. The two scoring functions show different behaviors:
- ESFT-Gate tends to achieve slightly higher specialized performance (50.2 vs. 49.4 average in Table 1) but slightly worse general-ability preservation (60.6 vs. 61.5 in Table 2).
- ESFT-Gate selects more experts than ESFT-Token for the same threshold (visible in Figure 4), which means higher training cost (3.20 GB vs. 2.57 GB, Section 5.2) and potentially more interference with general capabilities.
- The optimal `p` values differ: ESFT-Gate saturates at `p=0.1` for general ability and `p=0.3` for specialized ability on Math (Figure 6), while ESFT-Token saturates at `p=0.5` for both.

Without task-specific guidance, a practitioner must either run their own hyperparameter sweep (defeating some of the efficiency gains) or guess. An unlucky guess — e.g., using ESFT-Gate with `p=0.1` on a task where ESFT-Token with `p=0.2` would perform substantially better — could leave performance on the table. The random-expert baseline (Table 4) shows that poor expert selection costs 2.8–4.4 points on average, so the choice of scoring function and threshold is consequential.

The efficiency sweep in Section 6.2 (Figure 6) provides some guidance for the Math task, but it's unclear whether the saturation points generalize. The Math task has concentrated routing (few experts with high gate values, Figure 2), which might mean it reaches saturation at lower `p` than a task with more diffuse routing. A task like Summarization, which the paper notes uses more experts per layer (Figure 4), might require a higher `p` to capture the relevant signal — but this hypothesis is not tested. The paper's recommendation of `p=0.1` for ESFT-Gate and `p=0.2` for ESFT-Token is used across all six tasks without showing per-task validation that these values are near-optimal for each task individually.

Additionally, the paper does not provide guidance on when to prefer ESFT *at all* over LoRA or FFT. The results clearly show ESFT outperforms LoRA on all tasks, but the gap varies substantially: ESFT dominates on Translation (+12–13 points over LoRA) and Legal Judgment (+6–9 points), but is closer on Math (+2–6 points) and Summarization (+0.3–1.1 points). A practitioner with a task similar to Summarization — where LoRA nearly matches ESFT — might reasonably choose LoRA for its simplicity (no expert selection phase, no architecture-specific code, wider community support). The paper does not provide decision criteria beyond "run the experiment and see."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The efficiency sweep (Figure 6, Section 6.2) on the Math task provides the only systematic comparison of `p` values, and it is limited to one task. Figure 4 shows per-task, per-layer expert selection counts, but these are for fixed `p` (0.1 for Gate, 0.2 for Token) — it does not show how the selection counts or performance change as `p` varies for non-Math tasks. Tables 1 and 2 report performance at the single chosen `p` per method. The comparison between ESFT-Token and ESFT-Gate is implicit in Tables 1 and 2 but never systematically analyzed — Section 6.1 notes that "ESFT-Token generally employs fewer experts while better maintaining general performance, comparable to ESFT-Gate in tasks like Math, Intent, and Law," but this is an observation, not a prescriptive guideline.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The paper provides the efficiency sweep for Math, which at least demonstrates the methodology a practitioner would use to select hyperparameters. The recommendation of `p=0.1/0.2` is supported by the sweep on Math and used consistently across all tasks, yielding good results. The paper's finding that performance saturates at relatively low `p` (0.1–0.2) for Math is encouraging — it suggests the method is not overly sensitive to the exact threshold, at least for tasks with concentrated routing. However, the absence of per-task validation and the lack of decision criteria for choosing between ESFT-Gate and ESFT-Token represent a practical gap. A follow-up study characterizing how routing concentration (measurable via the gini coefficient or similar on the normalized gate distribution) relates to optimal `p` would directly address this limitation, but the current paper does not provide it.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a conceptual shift in how the field thinks about parameter-efficient fine-tuning for sparse architectures. Before this work, PEFT methods were designed for dense models and applied to MoE models without modification — treating the expert structure as an implementation detail rather than a source of signal. ESFT demonstrates that the MoE routing mechanism, which the field has primarily studied as a training efficiency technique (enabling larger models at lower FLOPs-per-token), serves a second purpose: it encodes a **task-specific parameter importance map** that can be read out with trivial computation (a single forward pass over 32 samples) and used to make fine-tuning both cheaper and less destructive to general capabilities.

This is not an incremental improvement to an existing PEFT recipe. It is a reframing of what PEFT *means* for modular architectures. In the standard PEFT view, the goal is to approximate full-parameter fine-tuning with fewer trainable parameters — the implicit assumption being that FFT represents the performance ceiling that efficient methods must approach. ESFT challenges this assumption directly. Table 3 shows that by training only task-relevant non-shared experts (1.4B parameters), the model achieves a combined specialized-plus-general average of 55.4, while FFT (15.7B parameters, all experts trained) achieves 54.9. The efficient method *exceeds* the performance of the full method on the metric that matters most for deployment — not just specialized accuracy, but the ability to acquire new skills without losing old ones. When the paper reports that ESFT-Gate achieves 50.2 average specialized performance versus FFT's 51.0 (Table 1), the 0.8-point gap in specialized performance is more than compensated by the 2.7-point advantage in general-ability preservation (Table 2). This inverts the standard PEFT narrative: selective training is not a compromise to save compute; it is a strategy that produces a genuinely better model for multi-task deployment.

The diagnostic contribution is equally important. The paper provides the first systematic evidence that **full-parameter fine-tuning of MoE models damages expert specialization** — and that this damage is the mechanism behind catastrophic forgetting of general capabilities. Prior work on fine-tuning described forgetting as a diffuse degradation: the model overfits to the new task and loses broad capabilities. ESFT localizes the damage. The ablation in Table 3 shows that training shared experts costs 0.8 general-ability points with negligible specialized gain; training non-expert parameters costs 1.2 general points for 1.4 specialized points; training all non-shared experts (regardless of task relevance) costs 2.7 general points. The degradation is not diffuse — it is concentrated in the parameter groups that encode cross-domain capabilities. This is a specific, mechanistic claim about *where* forgetting occurs in MoE architectures, and it provides a diagnostic lens that future work can apply to other modular architectures (mixture-of-attention, mixture-of-LoRA, modular routing more generally).

The paper also resolves a tension in the MoE literature about the purpose of fine-grained expert segmentation. Prior work (Dai et al., 2024) motivated fine-grained experts primarily as a pretraining efficiency technique — more experts with the same total parameters means more specialized knowledge per expert, reducing redundancy and improving perplexity-per-FLOP. ESFT shows that fine granularity has a second, perhaps equally important benefit: it makes the model **inspectable and selectively tunable at deployment time**. Figure 7 demonstrates that as expert granularity decreases (group sizes of 2 or 4), ESFT's performance degrades disproportionately — not just because fewer experts means less specialized knowledge, but because the coarser experts entangle knowledge from multiple domains, making it impossible to select a "task-relevant" expert without also selecting parameters that encode unrelated capabilities. This creates a feedback loop between architectural design and deployment requirements: if the goal is to support many customized variants from one base model (a multi-tenant deployment), then fine-grained experts are not just a nice-to-have for pretraining efficiency — they are a prerequisite for efficient, capability-preserving customization.

For the broader PEFT research community, this paper suggests that the era of architecture-agnostic PEFT methods may be coming to an end. As the frontier shifts from dense models to sparse, modular architectures, PEFT methods that exploit architectural structure will have inherent advantages over methods that treat the model as a black box. LoRA, the dominant PEFT approach, applies low-rank updates uniformly across all layers and all experts — the paper shows this leaves substantial performance on the table (44.9 average specialized performance vs. 50.2 for ESFT-Gate, Table 1), with the gap being largest on tasks far from the pretraining distribution (+12–13 points on Translation). This does not mean LoRA is obsolete — it remains simpler to implement, architecture-agnostic, and well-supported by tooling. But it does mean that for practitioners deploying MoE models, the question is no longer "should we use LoRA or FFT?" but "should we use LoRA, FFT, or an architecture-aware method like ESFT?" — and the paper makes a strong case that the architecture-aware option dominates on the combined specialized-general performance metric.

Perhaps most significantly, the paper establishes a methodology for studying expert specialization that goes beyond static probing. The probing experiments in Section 3.2 (Figures 2 and 3) demonstrate that expert routing is concentrated and task-distinct — but these are properties of the frozen model. The ablation experiments in Section 6.3 (Table 3) demonstrate what happens when you *train* different expert subsets — they show that the routing distribution is not just descriptive but **causally relevant** to fine-tuning outcomes. This methodology — (1) probe routing, (2) selectively train based on routing, (3) measure both specialized and general degradation — provides a template for future work on other modular architectures. The random-expert ablation (Table 4) adds the crucial causal evidence: replacing task-relevant experts with random ones degrades performance by 2.8–4.4 points on average, confirming that the routing distribution identifies genuinely important parameters, not merely correlated ones.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Cross-architecture validation of expert specialization as a fine-tuning signal.** The most immediate gap the paper leaves is the single-model limitation. A direct replication study would apply ESFT's probing methodology (Section 3.2) and selective training protocol to at least three MoE model families: a coarse-grained architecture (e.g., Mixtral 8×7B with 8 experts), a medium-grained architecture (e.g., DBRX with 16 experts), and the fine-grained DeepSeek-V2 family at different scales (e.g., DeepSeek-V2 at 236B total parameters versus DeepSeek-V2-Lite at 16B). For each, the study would measure: (1) the concentration of normalized gate values (analogous to Figure 2) across a standard set of tasks, (2) the cross-task expert overlap (analogous to Figure 3), and (3) the specialized-versus-general performance tradeoff when applying ESFT versus FFT versus LoRA. The key question: is there a threshold of expert granularity (e.g., at least 32 non-shared experts per layer) above which ESFT's advantages materialize, or is the relationship continuous? The grouping experiment in Figure 7 suggests a threshold effect (group size 4 is substantially worse than group size 2), but this is a simulation on a fine-grained model — genuine cross-architecture data would determine whether it is a real architectural constraint or an artifact of the simulation methodology.

**Cheap, dynamic difficulty estimation for expert relevance — and failure mode characterization.** The paper's expert selection phase uses a fixed 32-sample budget (131K tokens) validated in Appendix C. A practical follow-up would characterize the minimum sample size needed per task as a function of the routing concentration (e.g., Gini coefficient of the normalized gate distribution). If a task has extremely concentrated routing — like Translation, where Figure 2 shows a steep drop-off — perhaps 4–8 samples suffice, reducing the selection cost by 4–8×. Conversely, tasks with diffuse routing (like Summarization, which Figure 4 shows uses more experts per layer) might need more than 32 samples. The follow-up would sweep sample sizes from 1 to 64 across all tasks, measure the stability of the selected expert set (Jaccard similarity to the set selected with 256 samples), and correlate stability with routing concentration metrics. This would give practitioners a decision rule: measure your task's routing concentration in a few samples, and if it exceeds a threshold, use a smaller selection budget.

Equally important: characterizing when ESFT *fails*. The paper shows strong results on all six tasks, but these tasks share a property — they are text-based and within the broad distribution of LLM pretraining. A stress-test would evaluate ESFT on tasks deliberately designed to be out-of-distribution: (1) tasks in a constructed language with no pretraining exposure, (2) tasks requiring operations on a new structured format (e.g., a novel serialization protocol), (3) tasks that are lexically similar to pretraining data but structurally novel (e.g., solving math problems expressed in standard English but using a non-standard notation). The hypothesis: when the router has no basis for assigning tokens to experts, the routing distribution will be near-uniform, ESFT's expert selection will be effectively random, and performance will collapse to the random-expert baseline in Table 4 (which shows drops of 2.8–20.4 points depending on the task). Confirming or disconfirming this hypothesis would establish the boundary conditions for ESFT's applicability.

**Combining ESFT with LoRA for hybrid expert-and-rank efficient fine-tuning.** ESFT freezes non-selected experts entirely, which means those experts contribute zero capacity to the downstream task — even though they might contain partially relevant knowledge that a small update could exploit. A hybrid method would apply ESFT's expert selection (training the top 5–15% of experts at full rank) *and* apply low-rank LoRA adapters to the remaining experts (training a small number of additional parameters in the frozen experts). The hypothesis: the selected experts handle the core task-specific computation, while the LoRA adapters in frozen experts provide a small amount of additional capacity for edge cases where the router sends tokens to non-primary experts. The experiment would sweep the LoRA rank on frozen experts (from 0 — equivalent to pure ESFT — to 8) and measure whether the additional parameters provide gains beyond pure ESFT's saturation point (p=0.2 for ESFT-Token, p=0.1 for ESFT-Gate, as identified in Figure 6). The key metric: does the specialized-versus-general tradeoff curve shift outward, or do the LoRA adapters on frozen experts cause the same interference that ESFT was designed to avoid?

**Expert-level causal ablation to distinguish specialization from importance.** The paper defines expert specialization statistically: an expert is specialized for a task if it receives a disproportionate share of gate values. This conflates two properties: an expert could be *important* for a task (ablating it degrades task performance) without being *specialized* (the expert might also be important for many other tasks). A causal follow-up would perform expert-level ablation: for each task, independently zero out each of the top-10 experts (by gate score) and measure the performance drop on that task *and* on a panel of other tasks. The specialization score for expert i on task A would be: (drop in task A performance when ablating expert i) minus (average drop in other tasks' performance). If experts are genuinely specialized, the ablation effect should be task-specific — an expert important for Math should not be important for Translation. If the top experts are actually general-purpose (important for everything), the specialization narrative would need revision. This experiment would also identify whether the top 2–3 experts per task are disproportionately causal (explaining most of the task performance) or whether the effect is distributed across the 5–15% selected by ESFT.

**Scaling laws for expert selection: does the 5–15% ratio hold across model scales?** DeepSeek-V2-Lite has approximately 16B total parameters with 66 non-shared experts per layer. If the model were scaled to DeepSeek-V2's 236B parameters (with 162 experts, 8 active per token as described in DeepSeek, 2024), would the fraction of experts selected by ESFT remain at 5–15%, or would it change? Two competing hypotheses: (1) larger models with more experts might show even sharper specialization (more experts means each one learns a narrower domain), so the fraction of task-relevant experts would *decrease*; (2) larger models might route tokens more diffusely (more experts means the TopK selection spreads across more candidates), so the fraction would *increase*. A scaling study applying ESFT to DeepSeek-V2 at multiple scales (or simulating different expert counts by subsampling) would distinguish these hypotheses and provide practical guidance for future MoE architectures: if the fraction decreases with scale, ESFT becomes even more efficient on larger models; if it increases, the method's efficiency advantage diminishes.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Multi-tenant LLM deployment with per-customer customization.** This is the application the paper's storage numbers directly enable. An organization maintaining customized LLM variants for multiple clients (e.g., a legal-tech company serving different law firms, each needing a model fine-tuned on their jurisdiction's case law) faces the storage and training cost of maintaining separate models per client. With FFT, each variant requires storing 28.6 GB of parameters; with ESFT-Token, each requires 2.57 GB (Section 5.2). For 50 clients, the storage drops from 1.43 TB to 128.5 GB (plus one 28.6 GB base model — a total of 157.1 GB), a reduction of approximately 9x. Training cost per new client drops by 30% (19.8 minutes vs. 28.5 minutes per 500-step run), and the resulting model better preserves the general capabilities that all clients share (Table 2: ESFT-Token general average 61.5 vs. FFT 58.8). The practical workflow: deploy the base model once, run ESFT's expert selection phase (a single forward pass over 32 client-provided samples, taking seconds), fine-tune for 500 steps on client data, store the 2.57 GB delta, and load it alongside the shared base model at inference time. A new client can be onboarded in under 30 minutes of GPU time with minimal storage footprint.

**Domain-specific model enhancement without breaking instruction-following.** Table 2 shows that FFT causes a severe drop on IFEval (instruction following), from the vanilla model's 42.5 to 34.2 — a decline of 8.3 points. This is catastrophic for a model that must continue to serve as a general assistant while gaining domain expertise. ESFT-Token preserves IFEval at 40.7, a decline of only 1.8 points. This matters concretely for scenarios like: a hospital fine-tunes an LLM on medical literature to improve diagnostic suggestion quality, but the model still needs to handle appointment scheduling, patient communication, and general medical Q&A — all of which rely on instruction-following. FFT would produce a model that is better at diagnosis but worse at the routine tasks that constitute most of its usage. ESFT produces a model that gains the specialized medical knowledge (comparable to FFT's gains, as shown by the adaptation-task results in Table 1) while retaining the instruction-following and general reasoning capabilities that make it usable day-to-day. The specific guidance from Section 6.3: use the "Balanced" strategy ("Relevant, ×, ×" in Table 3) — train only task-relevant non-shared experts, skip shared expert and non-expert training, and skip data mixing (Appendix F shows it provides no benefit for ESFT and may slightly harm performance).

**Rapid prototyping of specialized models in low-resource settings.** The paper's results on low-resource translation (ChrEn, Cherokee-to-English, Table 1) demonstrate ESFT's particular strength on tasks where the vanilla model has near-zero capability (14.5 baseline accuracy). ESFT-Token achieves 36.2 and ESFT-Gate achieves 35.2, compared to FFT's 38.4 and LoRA's 23.1. For a linguistics researcher working with an endangered language with limited parallel text, ESFT offers a practical path: fine-tune a general-purpose MoE model on a few thousand sentence pairs, achieve reasonable translation quality, and do so without destroying the model's ability to serve as a translation tool for other language pairs (Table 8 shows ESFT preserves Math and Code capabilities nearly intact, suggesting other language-pair capabilities would be similarly preserved). The specific advantage over LoRA is decisive here (+12–13 points on Translation) and over FFT is practical (near-FFT performance with 91% less storage per language variant, enabling a single base model to support dozens of low-resource language pairs without multiplicative storage costs).

**Cost-efficient batch inference with adaptive per-task resource allocation.** While the paper focuses on fine-tuning efficiency, the expert selection methodology has implications for inference architecture. The finding that different tasks route tokens to distinct, concentrated expert subsets (Figure 3) suggests that at inference time, a task-specific model variant could *skip* experts that are known (from the ESFT selection phase) to be irrelevant to the incoming task. For a deployment handling a known, stable mix of task types (e.g., a customer service platform that routes incoming queries to intent recognition, summarization, or translation pipelines), the system could pre-compute which experts each task uses and load only those experts into GPU memory per task — reducing inference memory footprint and potentially latency. The specific numbers: if Intent Recognition uses approximately 8 experts per layer (from Figure 4) out of 66, loading only those 8 would reduce the MoE layer's memory footprint by approximately 88%. Whether this translates to wall-clock speedup depends on the inference framework's support for dynamic expert loading, but the paper provides the routing statistics that would make such an optimization possible. This is not something ESFT directly implements, but it is a direct consequence of the expert specialization finding that the paper documents.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper provides explicit guidance in Section 6.3 for choosing between ESFT configurations based on deployment priorities, which maps naturally to choosing ESFT versus alternatives:

**Prefer ESFT (Balanced, "Relevant, ×, ×" in Table 3) when:**
- You need both specialized-task improvement and general-capability preservation.
- Storage per task variant matters (multi-tenant deployment, on-device storage constraints).
- Your base model uses fine-grained MoE (approximately 60+ non-shared experts per layer) with shared expert isolation.
- You have at least 32 sequences of ~4096 tokens to compute stable expert relevance scores.
- The downstream task is text-based and within the broad domain of the model's pretraining distribution (the routing distribution will provide a meaningful signal).

**Prefer ESFT (Max Specialized, "Relevant, ✓, ✓" in Table 3) when:**
- Specialized task performance is the primary metric and general-capability preservation is secondary.
- You have sufficient storage budget to handle the additional trained parameters (2.7 GB per variant vs. 1.4 GB for the Balanced approach).

**Prefer FFT when:**
- The base model uses a coarse-grained MoE architecture (fewer than approximately 32 non-shared experts per layer). The grouping experiment in Figure 7 shows ESFT's advantage degrades substantially at group size 2 and collapses at group size 4 — FFT outperforms ESFT in these regimes.
- You need the absolute maximum specialized performance and general-capability regression is acceptable (e.g., a one-off evaluation with no multi-task requirements).
- The downstream task is far outside the pretraining distribution such that the routing mechanism provides no meaningful signal — though the paper does not test this boundary explicitly, the random-expert baseline in Table 4 (drops of 2.8–20.4 points) suggests ESFT would be unreliable in this regime.

**Prefer LoRA when:**
- Simplicity and tooling support are paramount — LoRA is widely implemented, requires no architecture-specific code, and needs no expert selection phase.
- The downstream task is closely related to the model's existing capabilities (model enhancement rather than model adaptation), where the LoRA-ESFT gap is relatively small (GSM8K: LoRA 58.9 vs. ESFT-Token 66.0, a 7.1-point gap; MATH: LoRA 20.6 vs. ESFT-Token 22.6, a 2.0-point gap — smaller than the 12–13 point gaps on Translation and Legal Judgment).
- You do not have access to the model's internal routing statistics (e.g., using a closed-source MoE API where gate values are not exposed).

These recommendations are grounded directly in the paper's empirical results: the architectural dependence from Figure 7, the specialized-versus-general tradeoff from Table 3, the ESFT-versus-LoRA gap as a function of task type from Table 1, and the random-expert degradation from Table 4. The paper does not provide a formal decision rule for choosing between ESFT-Gate and ESFT-Token, but the consistent empirical pattern — ESFT-Token for general-ability preservation, ESFT-Gate for specialized performance — provides a practical heuristic that aligns with the results in Tables 1 and 2.
