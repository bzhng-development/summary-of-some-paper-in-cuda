# KL for a KL: On-Policy Distillation with Control Variate Baseline

**ArXiv:** [2605.07865](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07865)

## 🎯 Pitch

On-policy distillation is a leading post-training method for reasoning models but suffers from unstable training due to high-variance gradient estimates. This paper shows that the value function for this problem equals the per-token reverse KL divergence, which is already computed in the forward pass—enabling a zero-cost, unbiased control variate baseline that eliminates the need for an extra critic model. The resulting method, vOPD, matches the accuracy of the most expensive full-vocabulary baseline while cutting runtime by over 57%.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **vOPD (On-Policy Distillation with a control variate baseline)**, a method that stabilizes On-Policy Distillation by reframing it as policy-gradient reinforcement learning and subtracting a closed-form value function — the per-token negative reverse KL divergence between student and teacher — as a detached control variate baseline, reducing gradient variance without biasing the estimator or requiring an additional critic model. Evaluated on mathematical and scientific reasoning benchmarks (MATH500, Minerva Math, AMC23, AIME24/25, SciKnowEval, GPQA-Diamond) using Qwen3 and Olmo-3 model families, vOPD delivers up to +6.2% absolute accuracy gains over vanilla OPD on MATH500 while matching the performance of the most expensive full-vocabulary OPD variant at up to 57.7% lower wall-clock time. The paper further shows that a top-k approximation of the baseline preserves both the unbiased gradient and the variance reduction regardless of k, establishing that a coarse estimate of the value function suffices for stable training — not because the approximation is precise, but because the baseline's role is to dampen destructively large negative-reward gradients at high-mismatch tokens where the loss signal is least informative.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: OPD Training Is Unstable in Practice

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is deceptively specific: **On-Policy Distillation (OPD) works remarkably well for post-training language models on reasoning tasks, but its training recipe is underdeveloped and remains unstable in practice.** This is not a hypothetical concern — OPD has become a dominant post-training paradigm in industrial-scale systems (Qwen3, GLM-5, Nemotron-Cascade2, DeepSeek-V4 all use it), so training instability directly translates into wasted compute, unpredictable convergence, and suboptimal final model performance at enormous scale.

The instability arises from a precise technical source. OPD minimizes the reverse KL divergence between student ($\pi_\theta$) and teacher ($\pi_T$) using a **single-sample Monte Carlo estimator** — it samples one token $y_t$ from the student at each position $t$, computes the per-token reward $r_t = \log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$, and uses that single scalar to form a gradient. This is computationally efficient: the backward pass touches only $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$ at the single sampled token. But single-sample Monte Carlo estimates carry high variance, and this variance manifests as **large, noisy gradients that destabilize training**. The paper notes that the OPD "optimization recipe remains underdeveloped" and "stabilization techniques are still immature relative to the recipes that drive successful RLVR training" (Section 1), citing specific stabilization methods like clipping, per-token loss normalization, and KL penalties that have been refined for RLVR but lack principled OPD analogs.

The practical consequence is that practitioners deploying OPD face a frustrating tradeoff: they know OPD is the right objective (it provides dense token-level supervision, enabling faster credit assignment than sparse-reward RLVR while avoiding exposure bias), but they cannot reliably train with it using the most computationally efficient single-sample estimator. Instead, they must either accept training instability or pay substantial computational overhead for more stable (but more expensive) alternatives.

### Why This Problem Matters: OPD Is the Default, But We Don't Know How to Train It Well

The significance of this problem extends well beyond academic curiosity because OPD has become **the default fine-tuning recipe when a strong teacher model is available**. The paper characterizes this landscape clearly:

**OPD offers a fundamental advantage over RLVR.** In RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards), the model receives a single scalar reward — correct or incorrect — only after generating a complete reasoning chain that may span thousands of tokens. This sparse supervision means the model must perform credit assignment over long sequences from a single terminal signal, requiring "large rollouts and prolonged training, making training progress painfully slow" (Section 1). OPD replaces this sparse terminal reward with dense, token-level signals derived from a teacher's probability distribution. At every token position, the student receives immediate feedback on whether its prediction aligns with the teacher's, enabling faster convergence and more sample-efficient training. As the paper notes, OPD "empirically matches RLVR accuracy with a fraction of the compute" (Section 1).

**OPD mitigates exposure bias.** Off-policy distillation — where the student is trained on static outputs generated by the teacher — suffers from a train-test mismatch: the student learns to mimic the teacher's distribution on the teacher's generated sequences, but at test time it conditions on its own outputs, accumulating errors over long generations (a phenomenon known as exposure bias, identified by Ranzato et al., 2016). OPD avoids this by sampling responses from the student itself during training, so the student learns to correct its own mistakes in the context of its own generation distribution. This is particularly important for long chain-of-thought reasoning where errors compound across many steps.

**OPD is already deployed at massive scale.** The paper catalogs the industrial adoption: Qwen3, GLM-5, Nemotron-Cascade2, and DeepSeek-V4 all incorporate OPD into their post-training pipelines. The Qwen3 technical report explicitly credits OPD as a key component of its reasoning enhancement. This means that instability in OPD training is not a minor annoyance — it directly impacts the quality of some of the most widely used open-weight language models. A principled stabilization method would have immediate practical impact across the open-source LLM ecosystem.

**The single-sample OPD estimator is the bottleneck.** Three variants of OPD exist, each representing different points on the bias-variance-compute Pareto frontier. The single-sample Monte Carlo estimator (which the paper calls "base OPD") is the lightest in compute — one backward pass per token position — but suffers from high gradient variance. This high variance is the specific technical problem the paper addresses. The existing stabilization methods for OPD are all variants of increasing the number of samples to reduce variance, but each introduces new costs or biases:

- **OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$** computes the per-token KL over the entire vocabulary (|V| ≈ 150k for Qwen3), eliminating variance but requiring a backward pass through the full vocabulary at every position — enormously expensive. This is the "most widely adopted fix" (Section 1), used in GKD (Agarwal et al., 2024), but its cost is prohibitive at scale.
  
- **OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$** restricts the KL to the student's top-k most probable tokens (k ≪ |V|, e.g., k = 20), reducing compute cost but **biasing the gradient** away from the true OPD objective because it ignores probability mass on the remaining |V| − k tokens. The paper explicitly notes that this approach "yields only marginal gains" over base OPD in practice (Section 1), a finding consistent with Li et al. (2026).

The core unsolved problem is therefore: **can we get the variance reduction of full-vocabulary OPD while retaining the computational efficiency of the single-sample estimator, without biasing the gradient?** This is a genuine technical gap — no existing method achieves this tradeoff.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies concrete limitations in both the existing OPD stabilization methods and in the broader RL-for-LLMs literature's handling of OPD.

**Full-vocabulary OPD is too expensive.** Computing the KL divergence over 150k vocabulary entries at every token position requires materializing and backpropagating through a distribution of that size. This effectively makes OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ as expensive as pretraining at each step, defeating the purpose of OPD as a lightweight post-training method. The paper quantifies this cost in Figure 3: at the 4B scale, OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ takes 77.7 seconds per training step compared to 25.1 seconds for base OPD — a 3.1× slowdown. This cost multiplies across the many training steps needed for convergence.

**Top-k OPD introduces bias without commensurate gains.** The paper's critique of OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ is subtle but important. By restricting the KL to a truncated support and renormalizing, OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ changes the optimization target from $\text{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_T)$ to $\text{KL}(\bar{\pi}_\theta \| \bar{\pi}_T)$. This bias is not merely theoretical — the paper shows empirically that OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ yields only marginal improvements over base OPD (Table 2: +0.4% average at 1.7B scale, essentially noise). Li et al. (2026) investigated this phenomenon and found that gains saturate at very small k, suggesting that the top-k truncation is removing precisely the tail probability mass that carries the most informative teacher signal. The bias-variance tradeoff here is unfavorable: OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ pays a non-trivial compute cost (30.96 seconds per step at 1.7B vs. 22.21 for base OPD in Figure 3) for negligible performance improvement.

**The RL interpretation of OPD is underutilized.** Several recent works have noted that OPD can be formulated as policy-gradient RL (Ko et al., 2026; Jin et al., 2026; Lu & Thinking Machines Lab, 2025), where the per-token reward is defined as $r_t = \log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$ and the objective gradient takes the standard REINFORCE form $\mathbb{E}[\sum_t r_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)]$. But while the RL framing is recognized, **the standard RL toolkit for variance reduction has not been applied to OPD**. This is a notable omission because policy-gradient methods have well-developed techniques for exactly this problem: subtracting a control variate baseline to reduce gradient variance without biasing the estimator. This principle underlies every major policy-gradient algorithm — A3C, PPO with GAE, GRPO, RLOO — but has been entirely absent from the OPD literature. The paper explicitly identifies this gap: "the baseline... has not been systematically explored for OPD, even though OPD admits a policy-gradient interpretation" (Section 5.2).

**Existing RL stabilization methods don't transfer directly.** The paper is careful to explain why modern LLM RL methods like GRPO and RLOO cannot be naively applied to OPD. GRPO and RLOO construct baselines by comparing rewards across multiple sampled responses from the same prompt — the "group-relative baseline." This works because the reward is a prompt-level scalar (answer correctness), so multiple complete trajectories can be generated and their rewards compared. In OPD, however, the reward is per-token, and the baseline must be estimated per-token position $c_t$. Generating multiple completions from each intermediate context would be combinatorially expensive. What's needed is a baseline that can be computed from the single forward pass that OPD already performs — and critically, the paper shows that such a baseline exists in closed form but has been overlooked.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution as **bridging the gap between RL variance reduction techniques and OPD by exploiting a mathematical coincidence specific to the OPD setting**. The coincidence is this: in general RL, the value function (the expected future reward from a given state) must be learned by training a separate critic model, adding complexity and potential instability. In OPD, however, the per-token reward has a specific functional form — $r_t = \log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$ — whose expectation under the student distribution has a known closed form:

$$V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t) = \mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|c_t)}[r_t(c_t, y_t)] = -\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t))$$

This is not a general property of policy-gradient RL; it is a direct consequence of the OPD reward definition. The value function is exactly the negative per-step reverse KL divergence, which is **already computed in the forward pass** as part of evaluating the OPD loss. This means vOPD can obtain the canonical variance-reducing baseline — the value function — at zero additional model capacity cost, with no learned critic, and with no additional forward or backward passes beyond what OPD already computes.

The paper positions vOPD not as a new objective or a competing training paradigm, but as an **estimator-level intervention** that changes **how** the gradient of the standard OPD objective is estimated, not **what** objective is optimized. This is a crucial distinction. vOPD optimizes $J_{\text{OPD}}(\theta)$ exactly — the same objective as base OPD and OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ — but estimates its gradient with lower variance than base OPD while maintaining lower computational cost than OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$. The unbiasedness is guaranteed by the general property of baseline subtraction in REINFORCE (Williams, 1992): any baseline $b(c_t)$ that does not depend on the sampled action $y_t$ leaves the expected gradient unchanged, since $\mathbb{E}_{y_t}[b(c_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)] = b(c_t) \cdot \nabla_\theta \sum_v \pi_\theta(v|c_t) = b(c_t) \cdot 0 = 0$.

The paper further positions the top-k approximation of the baseline (vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$) as a key insight about **what matters for variance reduction**. The crucial distinction from OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ is subtle but consequential: in vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$, the top-k KL is used as a **detached baseline subtracted from the single-sample reward**, not as the **loss function itself**. Because the baseline is independent of the sampled token $y_t$ (it depends only on $\pi_\theta$, $\pi_T$, and the deterministic top-k set $S_t$), the gradient remains unbiased regardless of how poorly the top-k approximation matches the full KL. The approximation quality affects only the degree of variance reduction, not the expected gradient direction. This is fundamentally different from OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ where the approximation biases the optimization target itself. The empirical finding that even k=5 provides sufficient variance reduction (Figure 2) supports the paper's framing: the baseline's role is to dampen the most extreme negative rewards, and a coarse approximation suffices for this purpose.

The paper also positions itself relative to the broader trend of **understanding OPD instability through the lens of heavy-tailed gradients**. Li et al. (2026) and Ko et al. (2026) have identified that OPD's training instability is driven by "high-mismatch tokens" — positions where the student assigns high probability to tokens the teacher considers very unlikely, producing large negative per-token rewards that dominate the gradient. The paper builds on this understanding by showing that vOPD's baseline is **largest precisely at these high-mismatch tokens**: when $\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_T)$ is large, the negative reward is paired with a large positive baseline shift, compressing the token's contribution toward zero. The paper's analysis in Section 4.3 (Figure 1) directly visualizes this: the long negative tail of the reward distribution is compressed toward zero in the advantage distribution, while positive-reward tokens are largely unchanged. This selective damping — reducing gradients on the least informative tokens while preserving gradients on the most informative ones — explains why vOPD can reduce gradient norms by 1–2 orders of magnitude (Figure 4) while simultaneously improving final accuracy. It is effectively acting as a regularizer that targets precisely the noise, not the signal.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

vOPD is a **gradient estimator** for On-Policy Distillation — not a new training objective or a new model architecture, but a different way of computing the gradient of the standard OPD loss that is both more stable and computationally cheaper than existing alternatives. The core problem is that the standard single-sample OPD gradient estimator has high variance that manifests as large, noisy gradient spikes (particularly at tokens where student and teacher strongly disagree), and the solution is to **subtract a value function baseline computed in closed form from the per-token reward**, converting it into a lower-variance advantage estimate without requiring a learned critic, additional forward passes, or any bias in the expected gradient.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture

The vOPD system has three major components operating within a single forward-backward pass:

1. **Student model (`$\pi_\theta$`)** — the language model being trained. It generates responses autoregressively, sampling one token `$y_t$` at each position `$t$` from its own distribution `$\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t)$`, where `$c_t = (x, y_{<t})$` is the concatenation of the prompt `$x$` and all previously generated tokens. The student is what gets updated.

2. **Teacher model (`$\pi_T$`)** — a frozen, stronger language model (typically a post-trained checkpoint of the same model family). It provides the target distribution against which the student is distilled. The teacher is only used in the forward pass to compute token-level log-probabilities; its parameters are never updated.

3. **Value baseline computer** — a computation (not a separate model) that evaluates the per-token negative reverse KL divergence `$-\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t))$` at each position using the already-computed student and teacher distributions. This value is treated as a constant (detached from the computation graph) and subtracted from the per-token reward to form the advantage.

**Information flow:** A prompt `$x$` enters the system → the student generates a response `$y$` token-by-token, computing `$\log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` at each step → the teacher computes `$\log \pi_T(y_t | c_t)$` at the same sampled token → the per-token reward `$r_t = \log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` is computed → the baseline `$b_t = -\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t))$` is computed from the full student and teacher distributions at `$c_t$` → the advantage `$a_t = r_t - b_t = r_t + \text{DKL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_T)$` is formed → the gradient `$a_t \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` is computed with the backward pass flowing only through the single sampled token `$y_t$`.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the OPD objective and its RL formulation** — understanding OPD as policy-gradient RL is the foundation on which vOPD builds, so we start with the reward definition, the policy-gradient decomposition, and why the single-sample estimator has high variance.
- **Second, the value function of OPD** — the core mathematical insight that the expectation of the per-token reward under the student equals the negative reverse KL, giving us a closed-form baseline requiring no learned critic.
- **Third, the vOPD gradient estimator** — how the value function is subtracted as a detached baseline to form the advantage, why this preserves unbiasedness, and why the backward pass remains single-token.
- **Fourth, the variance reduction mechanism** — a concrete analysis of how vOPD dampens the heaviest-tailed gradients at high-mismatch tokens, including the approximate variance reduction formula.
- **Fifth, the top-k baseline approximation** — how a coarser KL estimate further reduces compute cost, why it remains unbiased (unlike OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$), and the empirical finding that even k=5 suffices.
- **Sixth, the relationship to existing OPD variants** — a systematic comparison of vOPD$_{\text{full-V}}$, vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$, base OPD, OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$, and OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ along the axes of bias, variance, and compute cost.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodological paper** that introduces a new gradient estimator for an existing objective. The core idea is that On-Policy Distillation can be interpreted as policy-gradient reinforcement learning with a specific per-token reward function, and that this reward function has the special property that its expectation under the student policy — the value function — is available in closed form as the per-token reverse KL divergence, which is already computed during the forward pass.

---

#### The OPD Objective and Its RL Formulation

The paper begins with the standard definition of On-Policy Distillation. OPD trains a student model `$\pi_\theta$` to minimize the reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence between the student's distribution over responses and a frozen teacher model's distribution `$\pi_T$`:

$$J_{\text{OPD}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | x) \| \pi_T(\cdot | x)) \right]$$

where `$\mathcal{D}$` is a dataset of prompts, `$y$` is a complete response sampled from the student, and the KL divergence is computed over the entire response distribution (i.e., over the joint distribution of all token sequences conditioned on the prompt).

**What it computes:** the expected negative reverse KL between student and teacher, averaged over prompts from the training distribution. Minimizing the reverse KL encourages the student to place high probability on tokens and sequences the teacher considers probable, while the mode-seeking property of reverse KL (as opposed to forward KL) means the student can concentrate its probability mass on a subset of the teacher's high-probability region — which is desirable when the student has lower capacity than the teacher.

**Why this form:** reverse KL is chosen over forward KL `$\text{DKL}(\pi_T \| \pi_\theta)$` because forward KL is mean-seeking and would force the student to cover all modes of the teacher distribution, including low-quality modes, which is both harder for a smaller student and produces worse generations. Reverse KL is mode-seeking, allowing the student to specialize in the teacher's highest-quality generation modes. Additionally, the on-policy sampling `$y \sim \pi_\theta$` — where the student generates its own training data rather than training on teacher-generated outputs — mitigates exposure bias, the train-test mismatch where a model trained on static data must condition on its own outputs during inference and compounds errors over long sequences.

In practice, the expectation over full response distributions is intractable, so the objective is decomposed into per-token terms. For a response `$y = (y_1, \ldots, y_{|y|})$` of length `$|y|$`, the objective is optimized by maximizing the following token-level objective, where `$c_t = (x, y_{<t})$` is the context (prompt plus all previously generated tokens) at step `$t$`:

$$J_{\text{OPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log \frac{\pi_T(y_t | c_t)}{\pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)} \right]$$

where `$y_t$` is the token actually sampled from the student at position `$t$`, `$\pi_T(y_t | c_t)$` is the teacher's probability assigned to that token given the context, and `$\pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` is the student's probability assigned to that same token.

**What it computes:** for each position `$t$` in the generated response, the log-ratio of teacher probability to student probability at the sampled token. If the teacher assigns higher probability to `$y_t$` than the student does, this term is positive (rewarding the student for choosing that token). If the student assigns higher probability than the teacher, this term is negative (penalizing the student). The sum over all positions gives a sequence-level score, and the expectation is estimated by Monte Carlo sampling of complete responses from the student.

**Why this form:** this decomposition follows from the chain rule of probability applied to the reverse KL. The reverse KL over sequences `$\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(y|x) \| \pi_T(y|x))$` expands to `$\sum_t \mathbb{E}_{y_{<t} \sim \pi_\theta}[\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t))]$`, and the per-token term is the Monte Carlo estimate of this per-step KL using the single sampled token `$y_t$` rather than summing over the full vocabulary. This single-sample estimator is computationally efficient because it only needs to evaluate the student and teacher log-probabilities at one token per position.

Now comes the critical reformulation as policy-gradient reinforcement learning. The per-token reward is defined as:

$$r_t(c_t, y_t) = \log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$$

where `$c_t$` is the context (state in RL terminology), `$y_t$` is the action (the token chosen by the policy), and `$r_t$` is the immediate reward received for taking action `$y_t$` in state `$c_t$`.

**What it computes:** a scalar reward at each token position that is positive when the teacher prefers the student's chosen token more than the student itself does, and negative when the student is overconfident in a token the teacher considers unlikely. This reward is treated as a "fixed scalar with no gradient flowing through it" (Section 2.1), meaning it is detached from the computation graph — the student's parameters are updated to increase the log-probability of tokens that received positive reward and decrease the log-probability of tokens that received negative reward, but the reward value itself does not generate gradients back through the teacher probabilities.

With this reward definition, the gradient of the OPD objective takes the standard REINFORCE (policy-gradient) form:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{OPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \underbrace{\left( \log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right)}_{r_t(c_t, y_t)} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right]$$

where `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` is the score function (the gradient of the log-probability of the sampled token with respect to the model parameters), and `$r_t$` acts as a scalar multiplier (the "weight" of the gradient update in that direction).

**What it computes:** for each token position, the gradient pushes the student's parameters in the direction that increases `$\log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` — i.e., makes the student more likely to output `$y_t$` in context `$c_t$` — scaled by `$r_t$`. If `$r_t$` is positive (the teacher likes this token more than the student currently does), the update increases the student's probability of choosing it. If `$r_t$` is negative (the student is overconfident relative to the teacher), the update decreases the student's probability of choosing it. The expectation over `$y \sim \pi_\theta$` means this gradient is estimated by actually sampling responses from the student and averaging over those samples.

**Why this form:** this is the standard REINFORCE estimator, which is known to be an unbiased estimator of the policy gradient `$\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta}[R(y)]$` for any reward function `$R$`. The key advantage is that the backward pass touches only `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` at the single sampled token `$y_t$`, making it computationally efficient — there is no need to compute gradients through the entire vocabulary distribution. The key disadvantage is that single-sample Monte Carlo estimates have high variance, which manifests as noisy, large-magnitude gradient updates that destabilize training.

The paper explicitly identifies **where** this variance comes from. At tokens where the student and teacher distributions strongly disagree — what recent work calls "high-mismatch tokens" — the reward `$r_t$` can take large negative values. For example, if the student assigns probability 0.1 to a token the teacher assigns probability 0.001, then `$r_t = \log(0.001) - \log(0.1) \approx -6.9 + 2.3 = -4.6$`, a large negative number. This large negative scalar multiplies the score function gradient, producing a gradient update with large magnitude that dominates the batch gradient. Since the reward is noisy (it depends on a single sample from the student), these large gradients are also noisy, injecting instability into training. This is the specific technical problem that vOPD solves.

#### The Value Function of OPD

The paper's central mathematical insight is that the per-token reward in OPD has a special structure: its expectation under the student distribution — the value function in RL terminology — can be computed in closed form from the same student and teacher distributions already evaluated during the forward pass. By definition, the value function `$V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t)$` is the expected reward when following policy `$\pi_\theta$` from state `$c_t$`:

$$V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t) = \mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t)} \left[ r_t(c_t, y_t) \right]$$

where the expectation is over all possible tokens `$y_t$` that could be sampled from the student's distribution at context `$c_t$`, weighted by their probabilities `$\pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$`.

Substituting the definition of `$r_t$`:

$$V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t) = \mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t)} \left[ \log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right]$$

Expanding the expectation as a sum over the vocabulary `$\mathcal{V}$`:

$$V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(v | c_t) \left( \log \pi_T(v | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(v | c_t) \right)$$

$$= \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(v | c_t) \log \pi_T(v | c_t) - \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(v | c_t) \log \pi_\theta(v | c_t)$$

$$= -\text{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t), \pi_T(\cdot | c_t)) + \text{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t))$$

$$= -\text{DKL}\left( \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t) \right)$$

where `$\text{H}(p, q)$` is the cross-entropy between distributions `$p$` and `$q$`, and `$\text{H}(p)$` is the entropy of distribution `$p$`.

**What it computes:** the value function at context `$c_t$` is exactly the **negative per-step reverse KL divergence** between the student's full next-token distribution and the teacher's full next-token distribution. This is a scalar that summarizes how much the student and teacher disagree at this position — when the distributions are similar (low KL), the value is close to zero; when they strongly disagree (high KL), the value is a large negative number.

**Why this form is significant:** in general policy-gradient RL, the value function must be estimated — typically by training a separate critic model (a value network) that learns to predict expected future rewards from states. This adds model parameters, training complexity, and potential instability. In OPD, the value function has a **closed-form expression** that uses exactly the same quantities (the student and teacher distributions at `$c_t$`) that are already computed during the forward pass to evaluate the OPD loss. This means vOPD can use the canonical variance-reducing baseline — the value function — with zero additional model capacity, zero additional learned parameters, and no additional forward passes beyond what OPD already requires. The computation of `$\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t))$` requires summing over the vocabulary, which has `$\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)$` cost, but this is only a forward-pass cost (no gradients flow through it), so it is substantially cheaper than the full-vocabulary backward pass required by OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$.

The paper notes that this connection has been observed previously (Tang & Munos, 2025), but has not been exploited as a mechanism for variance reduction in OPD training. The value function being the negative reverse KL is not a coincidence of this particular reward — it follows directly from the definition of the reward as the difference of log-probabilities, which makes the reward a "proper scoring rule" whose expectation recovers the divergence.

---

#### The vOPD Gradient Estimator

With the closed-form value function identified, vOPD applies the standard REINFORCE baseline subtraction technique. The baseline `$b_t(c_t)$` is set to the negative per-step reverse KL:

$$b_t(c_t) = V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t) = -\text{DKL}\left( \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t) \right)$$

This baseline is subtracted from the per-token reward to form the advantage `$a_t(c_t, y_t)$`:

$$a_t(c_t, y_t) = r_t(c_t, y_t) - b_t(c_t) = \left( \log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right) + \text{DKL}\left( \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t) \right)$$

The vOPD gradient estimator is then:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{vOPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \underbrace{\left( r_t(c_t, y_t) + \text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t)) \right)}_{a_t(c_t, y_t)} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right]$$

**What it computes:** the same expected gradient as base OPD (the estimator is unbiased — proven below), but each token's gradient contribution is scaled by the **advantage** rather than the raw reward. The advantage is the reward shifted by the KL divergence: since the KL is always non-negative, this shift is always in the positive direction. Positive-reward tokens become slightly more positive; negative-reward tokens are pushed toward zero (or even become positive if the KL is larger than the magnitude of the negative reward).

**Why the baseline is detached:** the KL term `$\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t))$` is computed in the forward pass but is treated as a constant with respect to the gradient computation — it does not propagate gradients back through the student or teacher distributions. This is the standard "detached baseline" approach in REINFORCE: the baseline should depend on the state `$c_t$` but not on the sampled action `$y_t$`, and it should not contribute its own gradient. Detaching ensures that the baseline serves only to reduce variance, not to change the optimization objective.

**Unbiasedness proof (Section 2.2 and Appendix A.1):** for any baseline `$b_t(c_t)$` that is independent of the sampled action `$y_t$`, the expected contribution of the baseline term to the gradient is zero:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t)} \left[ b_t(c_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right] = b_t(c_t) \cdot \mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t)} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right]$$

Using the score function identity (the "log trick"):

$$\mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t)} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right] = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(v | c_t) \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(v | c_t)}{\pi_\theta(v | c_t)} = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(v | c_t) = \nabla_\theta \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(v | c_t) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0$$

Therefore `$\mathbb{E}[b_t(c_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)] = b_t(c_t) \cdot 0 = 0$`, and the expected gradient with baseline subtraction is identical to the expected gradient without it: `$\mathbb{E}[\nabla J_{\text{vOPD}}] = \mathbb{E}[\nabla J_{\text{OPD}}]$`. The estimator is unbiased.

**Why this estimator reduces variance:** the variance reduction is not merely an empirical observation — it follows from the properties of the optimal baseline established in the RL literature (Greensmith et al., 2004; Appendix A.2). The optimal scalar baseline `$b^*$` that minimizes the trace of the gradient variance `$\text{tr}(\text{Var}[g(b)])$` is a weighted expectation of the reward:

$$b^* = \frac{\mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta}[r_t(c_t, y_t) \|\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)\|^2]}{\mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta}[\|\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)\|^2]}$$

Under a weak-correlation approximation where the reward `$r_t$` and the gradient norm `$\|\nabla \log \pi_\theta\|^2$` are not strongly dependent (which the paper argues is reasonable because `$\log \pi_\theta(y_t)$` concentrates near zero while `$r_t$` variation is driven primarily by `$\log \pi_T$` — see Appendix A.3 and Figure 5), this optimal baseline simplifies to the ordinary expectation of the reward:

$$b^* \approx \mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta}[r_t(c_t, y_t)] = V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t)$$

This means the value function is approximately the optimal variance-reducing baseline, which is the standard justification for its use in actor-critic methods. The paper's contribution is not in proving this (it is standard in RL), but in recognizing that for OPD specifically, this optimal baseline is computable in closed form without a learned critic.

**Why the backward pass remains single-token:** the baseline `$b_t$` is computed during the forward pass using the full vocabulary, but it is detached — no gradient flows through the KL computation. The gradient computation `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` still only needs to evaluate the derivative at the single sampled token `$y_t$`. This is the key computational advantage over OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$`, which requires a backward pass through all `$|\mathcal{V}|$` vocabulary entries at every token position because it computes `$\sum_v \pi_\theta(v | c_t) r_t(c_t, v) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(v | c_t)$`. vOPD gets the variance reduction benefit of the full-vocabulary KL (through the baseline) while keeping the backward pass cost of single-token OPD.

#### The Variance Reduction Mechanism: Token-Level Reward Damping

The paper provides a detailed analysis of **why** subtracting the negative KL baseline reduces variance, connecting it to the observed phenomenon of heavy-tailed negative rewards in OPD training. This analysis appears in Section 3.1 and is supported by the derivation in Appendix A.3.

The per-step gradient estimators for OPD and vOPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ at a single token position are:

$$g_{\text{OPD}} = r_t(c_t, y_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$$

$$g_{\text{vOPD}_{\text{full-V}}} = \left( r_t(c_t, y_t) + \text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t)) \right) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$$

Under the weak-correlation approximation, the reduction in gradient variance (measured by the trace of the covariance matrix) is:

$$\text{tr}\left( \text{Var}[g_{\text{OPD}}] \right) - \text{tr}\left( \text{Var}[g_{\text{vOPD}_{\text{full-V}}}] \right) \approx \text{DKL}\left( \pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t) \right)^2 \cdot \mathbb{E}_{y_t \sim \pi_\theta}\left[ \|\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)\|^2 \right]$$

**What it computes:** the amount by which vOPD reduces the total variance (summed over parameter dimensions) of the per-step gradient, relative to base OPD. The reduction is the product of two terms: (1) the squared KL divergence between student and teacher at this context, and (2) the expected squared norm of the score function gradient.

**Why this form reveals the mechanism:** the variance reduction scales with `$\text{DKL}^2$`, which means it is **largest precisely at the contexts where the student and teacher distributions most strongly disagree**. These high-KL contexts are exactly where the raw reward `$r_t$` can take large negative values (because the teacher assigns low probability to tokens the student assigns high probability to, driving `$\log \pi_T(y_t | c_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` strongly negative). At these positions, the baseline `$b_t = -\text{DKL}$` is a large positive shift, which adds to the negative reward and compresses the advantage toward zero. At low-KL contexts (where student and teacher already agree), the baseline is small, and the advantage is nearly equal to the raw reward.

**Operational interpretation — selective damping:** vOPD acts as a context-dependent regularizer that dampens gradient contributions most strongly at the least informative positions. When the student and teacher strongly disagree (high KL), the raw reward is dominated by noise — the student happened to sample a token the teacher dislikes, but the student's alternative high-probability tokens might also be poor choices from the teacher's perspective (otherwise the KL wouldn't be large). The gradient update at such a position is uninformative for improving the student's overall distribution match to the teacher, and the large magnitude of the negative reward makes it a dominant source of gradient noise. vOPD's baseline suppresses these contributions. Conversely, at low-KL positions where the student and teacher largely agree, the raw reward carries informative signal about which specific tokens to up-weight or down-weight for fine-tuning the distribution, and vOPD leaves these contributions largely intact.

The paper validates this interpretation empirically in Section 4.3 (which we'll discuss more in the experiments section). Figure 1 shows that the long negative tail of the OPD reward distribution is visibly compressed toward zero in the vOPD advantage distribution, while positive-reward tokens are largely unchanged. The scatter plot shows that among tokens with similarly negative rewards, some are dampened almost to zero (those at high-KL contexts, where the KL shift is large) while others retain negative advantages close to their original values (those at low-KL contexts, where the KL shift is small). This selective behavior is what allows vOPD to reduce gradient norms by 1–2 orders of magnitude (Figure 4) while simultaneously improving final accuracy — it is removing noise, not signal.

#### The Top-k Baseline Approximation

While vOPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ adds no backward-pass cost, it still requires computing the full-vocabulary KL `$\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \pi_T(\cdot | c_t))$` at every token position, which costs `$\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)$` per token. For models with vocabularies of ~150k tokens (like Qwen3), this forward-pass cost, while much cheaper than a backward pass through `$|\mathcal{V}|$`, is still non-trivial. The paper proposes an approximation that reduces this to `$\mathcal{O}(k)$` where `$k \ll |\mathcal{V}|$`.

The approximation restricts the KL computation to the **student's top-k support**: the set `$S_t$` of the `$k$` tokens with the highest probability under `$\pi_\theta(\cdot | c_t)$`. The distributions are renormalized to sum to one over this restricted support:

$$\bar{\pi}_\theta(v | c_t) = \frac{\pi_\theta(v | c_t) \cdot \mathbb{1}[v \in S_t]}{\sum_{u \in S_t} \pi_\theta(u | c_t)}, \quad \bar{\pi}_T(v | c_t) = \frac{\pi_T(v | c_t) \cdot \mathbb{1}[v \in S_t]}{\sum_{u \in S_t} \pi_T(u | c_t)}$$

where `$\mathbb{1}[v \in S_t]$` is 1 if token `$v$` is in the top-k set and 0 otherwise. The approximate baseline is then:

$$\hat{b}_t(c_t) = -\text{DKL}\left( \bar{\pi}_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \bar{\pi}_T(\cdot | c_t) \right)$$

Substituting this into the vOPD gradient gives the **vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$** estimator:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{vOPD}_{\text{top-k}}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \underbrace{\left( r_t(c_t, y_t) + \text{DKL}(\bar{\pi}_\theta(\cdot | c_t) \| \bar{\pi}_T(\cdot | c_t)) \right)}_{a_t(c_t, y_t)} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t) \right]$$

**What it computes:** the same gradient estimator as vOPD$_{\text{full-V}}$, but with the baseline KL approximated using only the student's top-k tokens rather than the full vocabulary. The baseline is still detached, so the backward pass remains single-token. The computational cost per token is reduced from `$\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)$` to `$\mathcal{O}(k)$` for the baseline computation.

**Why this remains unbiased:** the critical property that distinguishes vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ from OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ is that the baseline `$\hat{b}_t(c_t)$` depends only on `$\pi_\theta$`, `$\pi_T$`, and the deterministic set `$S_t$` — it does not depend on the sampled token `$y_t$`. Because of this, the unbiasedness argument from Section 2.2 still applies: `$\mathbb{E}[\hat{b}_t(c_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)] = 0$` for any baseline independent of `$y_t$`, regardless of how accurately `$\hat{b}_t$` approximates the true value function. The approximation quality affects only the degree of variance reduction, not the expected gradient direction. The estimator is unbiased for **any** choice of `$k$` — even `$k=1$` would be unbiased (though it would likely provide minimal variance reduction).

**The crucial distinction from OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$:** in OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ (defined in Eq. 6–7), the top-k KL is used as the **loss function** — the optimization target itself is changed from `$\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_T)$` to `$\text{DKL}(\bar{\pi}_\theta \| \bar{\pi}_T)$`. This biases the gradient because the renormalized distributions omit probability mass on the `$|\mathcal{V}| - k$` tokens outside the top-k support. In vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$`, the top-k KL is used only as a **detached baseline** subtracted from the single-sample reward of the full (unrestricted) OPD objective. The optimization target remains `$\text{DKL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_T)$`; only the gradient estimator's variance reduction component is approximated. This is a fundamentally different role that leads to fundamentally different empirical behavior: OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ provides marginal gains over base OPD (Table 2: +0.4% average at 1.7B), while vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ provides gains comparable to the full-vocabulary baseline (Table 2: +3.2% average at 1.7B, essentially matching vOPD$_{\text{full-V}}$'s +3.3%).

**Hyperparameter selection and robustness:** the paper investigates the sensitivity of vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ to the choice of `$k$` (Section 4.3, Figure 2). They ablate `$k \in \{5, 20, 50, 100\}$` and the full-vocabulary baseline, finding that accuracy is "stable across" all values, with all substantially outperforming base OPD. The key finding is that **any nonzero k suffices**: even the coarsest approximation at `$k=5$` provides enough variance reduction to stabilize training. The mean squared error of the top-k KL relative to the full-vocabulary KL (Figure 2, right) shows that the approximation error is non-trivial at `$k=5$` but drops to near zero beyond `$k=20$`. Despite this approximation error at small k, the training accuracy is nearly identical across all k values. This supports the paper's interpretation that the baseline's role is **coarse variance reduction** — damping the largest negative rewards — rather than precise advantage estimation, and that a rough estimate of the KL is sufficient for this purpose. The default value used in experiments is `$k=20$`, following Li et al. (2026) who showed that OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ gains saturate beyond `$k=16$`.

#### Relationship to Existing OPD Variants

The paper provides a systematic comparison of the five OPD variants — base OPD, OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$, OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$, vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$, and vOPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ — along three axes: bias, variance, and compute cost. This comparison is summarized in Table 1 and elaborated in Section 3.3.

**Base OPD** uses the single-sample Monte Carlo estimator (Eq. 3). It is **unbiased** (the expected gradient equals the true gradient of the OPD objective), has **high variance** due to the single-sample estimate, requires a **single-token backward pass** (the gradient flows only through `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` at the sampled token), and has **no additional per-token KL cost** beyond computing the teacher and student log-probabilities at the sampled token. This is the computationally lightest variant but suffers from training instability.

**OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$** computes the per-token KL over the full vocabulary (Eq. 4–5). It is **unbiased** (the full-vocabulary expectation equals the true gradient), has **zero variance at each context `$c_t$`** (because it computes the exact expectation rather than a sample estimate), requires a **full-vocabulary backward pass** (the gradient flows through `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(v | c_t)$` for every `$v \in \mathcal{V}$`), and has **`$\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)$` additional per-token KL cost** in both forward and backward passes. This is the most computationally expensive variant but is maximally stable.

**OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$** restricts the KL computation and backward pass to the student's top-k support (Eq. 6–7). It is **biased** (the gradient no longer equals the true OPD gradient because it omits probability mass outside the top-k set and renormalizes), has **near-zero variance at each context** (it computes a nearly-exact expectation over the top-k support), requires a **k-token backward pass** (gradient flows through k tokens per position), and has **`$\mathcal{O}(k)$` per-token KL cost** in both forward and backward passes. Despite the added compute over base OPD, it yields only marginal performance gains due to the bias.

**vOPD$_{\text{full-V}}$** uses the full-vocabulary KL as a detached baseline (Eq. 10). It is **unbiased** (the baseline is independent of the sampled token), has **low variance** (the value baseline reduces variance proportional to `$\text{DKL}^2$`), requires a **single-token backward pass** (same as base OPD — the gradient only flows through the sampled token), but has **`$\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{V}|)$` per-token KL cost in the forward pass only** (the baseline KL computation is detached, so no backward pass through the vocabulary). This is the key tradeoff that makes vOPD attractive: it achieves low variance (nearly matching OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$'s stability) while adding only forward-pass cost, not backward-pass cost.

**vOPD$_{\text{top-k}}$** further approximates the baseline using the top-k support (Eq. 15). It is **unbiased** (the top-k approximation is still independent of the sampled token), has **low variance** (the approximation captures enough of the KL for effective variance reduction), requires a **single-token backward pass**, and has **`$\mathcal{O}(k)$` per-token KL cost in the forward pass only**. This is the most computationally efficient variant that achieves effective variance reduction — the paper's Figure 3 shows it is faster than vOPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ and substantially faster than OPD$_{\text{top-k}}$ and OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ at larger model scales.

**A revealing contrast — why OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ cannot benefit from a baseline:** the paper demonstrates an instructive connection that clarifies the relationship between these variants (Section 3.1). If one were to subtract the value baseline from the OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ gradient, the baseline contribution would vanish:

$$V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t) \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(v | c_t) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(v | c_t) = V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t) \cdot \nabla_\theta \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \pi_\theta(v | c_t) = V^{\pi_\theta}(c_t) \cdot \nabla_\theta 1 = 0$$

This means OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ already has zero variance at each context (it computes the exact expectation), so subtracting the expected reward does nothing — the baseline becomes useful only once the full-vocabulary expectation is replaced with a Monte Carlo estimate, as in vOPD. This illuminates the core insight: vOPD and OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ are **dual approaches** to the same variance reduction. OPD$_{\text{full-V}}$ eliminates variance by computing the exact expectation at the cost of a full-vocabulary backward pass. vOPD eliminates variance by subtracting an analytically computed baseline at the cost of a full-vocabulary forward pass only. Both achieve low variance, but vOPD moves the expensive computation from the backward pass (which determines training speed) to the forward pass (which is much cheaper).

#### Training Implementation Details

The paper implements vOPD on top of standard OPD infrastructure using parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA (Hu et al., 2022). The specific training configuration (detailed in Section 4.1 and Appendix B) uses the TRL library's MiniLLM implementation as a base, with the following hyperparameters:

- **Optimizer**: AdamW
- **Learning rate**: `$1 \times 10^{-5}$` for Qwen3 models, `$2 \times 10^{-5}$` for Olmo-3 models
- **LoRA rank**: 64, with `$\alpha = 128$`
- **Batch size**: 64 prompts per step
- **Maximum prompt length**: 1024 tokens
- **Maximum response length**: 2048 tokens (following recent work showing OPD response length need not be excessively long)
- **Rollout temperature**: 1.0 (the student samples at temperature 1.0 during training to maintain exploration)
- **Training epochs**: 1 for mathematical reasoning (14K samples from DAPO-Math-17K, English subset), up to 10 for scientific reasoning (1,890 samples from SciKnowEval chemistry subset)
- **Precision**: bfloat16
- **Hardware**: single NVIDIA H200 NVL or A100 NVL GPU

The paper notes that "when using Qwen as the teacher model, we disable its extended thinking mode" — this is an important practical detail because Qwen3's extended thinking mode produces much longer chain-of-thought sequences with special formatting tokens that would change the token-level distributions in ways not accounted for by the standard OPD objective.

The vOPD-specific modification to the training loop is minimal: after the forward pass computes both the student and teacher log-probabilities at each position, the per-token KL baseline is computed (either over the full vocabulary or over the top-k support) and subtracted from the per-token reward to form the advantage before computing the policy gradient. The baseline computation is implemented as a detached tensor operation, ensuring no gradient flows through the KL. The entire change amounts to a few lines of code in the loss computation, making vOPD straightforward to integrate into existing OPD pipelines.

**A note on the weak-correlation approximation (Appendix A.3):** the variance reduction formula in Eq. 11 relies on the approximation `$\mathbb{E}[r_t \|\nabla \log \pi_\theta\|^2] \approx b_t \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla \log \pi_\theta\|^2]$`, which assumes weak correlation between the reward and the squared gradient norm. The paper argues this is reasonable because `$\log \pi_\theta(y_t | c_t)$` concentrates near zero for tokens sampled from the student (the student tends to sample tokens it assigns high probability to, so `$\pi_\theta(y_t) \approx 0.1$` to `$0.9$`, making `$\log \pi_\theta$` range from roughly `$-2.3$` to `$-0.1$`), while the variation in `$r_t$` is driven primarily by `$\log \pi_T(y_t | c_t)$` which can range much more widely (from near 0 for tokens the teacher likes to very negative numbers for tokens the teacher assigns near-zero probability to). Figure 5 in Appendix A.3 confirms this empirically with scatter plots showing weak correlation between student log-probability and reward, but strong correlation between teacher log-probability and reward. This supports the approximation as a reasonable first-order analysis, not a precise equality.

**Design choice — why not a learned baseline?** The paper implicitly addresses this by emphasizing the closed-form nature of the value function. A learned baseline (a separate critic model) would introduce additional parameters, require its own training objective, potentially introduce bias if the critic is imperfect, and add computational overhead. The fact that the exact value function is analytically available from quantities already computed in the forward pass makes a learned baseline strictly inferior — it would be more expensive, more complex, and less accurate. This is the key advantage of applying control variates to OPD specifically, versus to general RL where the value function must be learned.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Exploiting a Domain-Specific Coincidence to Obtain a Closed-Form Optimal Baseline for Free

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is not the application of control variates to OPD — baseline subtraction is a textbook RL technique dating to Williams (1992) and Sutton et al. (1999). Rather, it is the **recognition of a mathematical coincidence specific to the OPD reward structure** that makes the optimal variance-reducing baseline available in closed form without a learned critic, and the framing of this coincidence as the key to resolving a persistent practical tension in OPD training.

The coincidence is this: the per-token OPD reward is defined as `r_t = log π_T(y_t | c_t) − log π_θ(y_t | c_t)`. Taking its expectation under the student distribution yields `−DKL(π_θ(· | c_t) || π_T(· | c_t))` — the negative per-step reverse KL. This is not a general property of rewards in RL; it follows specifically from the reward being the difference of log-probabilities of two distributions, making it what Tang & Munos (2025) identified as a "proper scoring rule" whose expectation recovers the divergence. In a generic RL problem, the value function must be learned by training a separate critic model, which introduces additional parameters, training objectives, approximation error, and computational overhead. In OPD, the value function is **already computed as a byproduct of the forward pass** — the student and teacher distributions at each context are materialized to evaluate the OPD objective anyway, so computing their KL costs only an additional aggregation step over the vocabulary dimensions already in memory.

What makes this a genuine insight rather than an obvious application is the **non-obvious dual use of a quantity already present**. Prior work on OPD computed the KL — either as the full-vocabulary loss (OPD_full-V, Agarwal et al., 2024) or as a top-k approximation in the loss (OPD_top-k, Li et al., 2026) — but always treated it as the **optimization target**. The conceptual move vOPD makes is to recognize that the same quantity can serve a **completely different role**: as a detached variance reducer that does not change what is being optimized, only how its gradient is estimated. This is a framing innovation, not a technical one — the mathematics of baseline subtraction is standard, but seeing that the KL is simultaneously the optimization target *and* the optimal baseline for its own Monte Carlo estimator is a genuinely novel perspective.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond the immediate performance gains. It establishes that **OPD has a structural advantage over generic RL for variance reduction** — the reward function is not arbitrary; it has a form that makes the value function analytically tractable. This suggests that OPD should be designed differently from generic RLHF or RLVR pipelines: rather than importing stabilization techniques developed for arbitrary reward functions (learned critics, group-relative baselines, GAE), OPD can exploit its specific mathematical structure to achieve the same variance reduction with dramatically lower computational cost. The paper's analysis of why OPD_full-V cannot benefit from a baseline (Section 3.1: the baseline contribution vanishes because the full-vocabulary gradient already has zero per-context variance) further illuminates this structural property — it shows that vOPD and OPD_full-V are dual realizations of the same variance elimination, with vOPD moving the expensive computation from the backward pass (full-vocabulary gradients) to the forward pass (full-vocabulary KL), where it is substantially cheaper.

This is a fundamental advance in understanding, not an incremental improvement. It changes the question from "how do we stabilize OPD?" (which prior work answered with costly full-vocabulary or biased top-k alternatives) to "what structure does OPD have that makes stabilization cheap?" The answer — that the value function is analytically computable from the forward pass — is a property of the problem that had been present but unrecognized since OPD was first formulated as policy-gradient RL by Lu & Thinking Machines Lab (2025) and Ko et al. (2026).

The evidence supports the practical value of this insight: vOPD_full-V achieves accuracy competitive with OPD_full-V (Table 2: 33.1% vs. 33.0% average at 1.7B) while being substantially faster (Figure 3: 30.57s vs. 41.12s per step at 1.7B; the gap widens to 33.46s vs. 77.66s at 4B). The dual-use insight transforms a computation that was previously in the backward pass (the expensive part of training) into one that remains in the forward pass (the cheap part), without changing the optimization objective.

---

### Innovation 2: Distinguishing the Role of the KL as Objective vs. Baseline, and Why This Matters for Bias

The paper's second key insight is a **diagnostic distinction** between two fundamentally different roles a quantity can play in a gradient estimator — as part of the objective (the target being optimized) versus as a baseline (a variance reducer subtracted from the reward) — and the demonstration that this distinction has profound consequences for the bias-variance-compute tradeoff. This is not a new mathematical distinction (it is standard in REINFORCE), but the paper's contribution is in **applying it to resolve a specific empirical puzzle in the OPD literature** and in showing that the distinction explains why superficially similar methods (OPD_top-k and vOPD_top-k) have dramatically different empirical behavior.

The puzzle is this: Li et al. (2026) observed that OPD_top-k, which restricts the KL computation to the student's top-k tokens and uses it as the loss, provides only marginal gains over base OPD despite adding compute. Their investigation found that gains saturate at very small k, suggesting the truncation was removing informative teacher signal. Yet vOPD_top-k, which uses the same top-k KL approximation but places it in a different position in the estimator (as a detached baseline rather than the loss), provides gains comparable to the full-vocabulary baseline across all tested k values (Figure 2, Table 5 ablation). The paper explicitly names this as the "crucial distinction" (Section 3.2):

> "While both methods compute KL with a top-k approximation, they place it in different positions of the estimator. OPD_top-k uses it as the loss, replacing KL(π_θ||π_T) with KL(π̄_θ||π̄_T), thus changing the optimization target and biasing the gradient. vOPD_top-k uses it as a detached baseline subtracted from the reward."

The insight generalizes beyond the specific top-k case. It reveals that **the bias introduced by approximating the objective is fundamentally different from the variance-reduction degradation introduced by approximating the baseline**. An approximate objective changes where the optimizer converges; an approximate baseline changes how noisily it gets there. The former can lead the optimizer to a systematically wrong solution (biased optimum), while the latter only affects the path (potentially slower convergence but same endpoint in expectation). This distinction explains the empirical results that would otherwise be puzzling: why can a k=5 baseline approximation that carries non-trivial KL estimation error (Figure 2, right) still match the full-vocabulary baseline in final accuracy? Because the approximation error in the baseline only affects variance reduction quality, and even a coarse variance reduction is sufficient to suppress the heavy-tailed gradient noise that destabilizes training. In contrast, the approximation error in OPD_top-k's objective systematically removes probability mass from the optimization target, biasing the solution.

This is a **diagnostic insight** with implications for future OPD research: it suggests that efforts to improve stability should focus on better baseline estimates (which can be coarse and need only capture the dominant KL structure) rather than on better objective approximations (which must be precise to avoid bias). It also provides a **unified explanation** for the empirical landscape of OPD variants: OPD_top-k fails not because k=20 is too small, but because approximating the objective is the wrong intervention — the objective is already correct; the problem is the estimator's variance, which should be addressed through baseline subtraction rather than objective modification.

The paper supports this interpretation with a controlled ablation (Figure 2): all tested k values (5, 20, 50, 100) and the full-vocabulary baseline produce nearly identical accuracy (32.3–33.4% average), all substantially above base OPD (29.8%). The observation that "even the coarsest approximation at k=5 provides enough variance reduction to stabilize training" (Section 4.3) is the empirical anchor for the claim that the baseline's role is coarse damping, not precise advantage estimation. This is a conceptual contribution — it changes how one thinks about what matters for OPD stabilization — supported by systematic evidence.

### Innovation 3: The Heavy-Tailed Gradient Diagnosis and Selective Damping as a Mechanism

While prior work (Li et al., 2026; Ko et al., 2026) had identified that OPD training instability is driven by "high-mismatch tokens" — positions where student and teacher strongly disagree — the paper makes this diagnosis **mechanistically precise** and shows that vOPD's variance reduction operates through a **selective damping mechanism** that is qualitatively different from generic stabilization techniques like gradient clipping.

The mechanism is grounded in the structure of the OPD reward distribution. The paper's Figure 1 visualizes a phenomenon that was previously described qualitatively: the per-token reward distribution has a "pronounced negative long tail" (Section 4.3). Tokens where the teacher assigns much lower probability than the student produce large negative rewards (e.g., -4.6 or lower), and these heavy-tailed negative values dominate the batch gradient because the REINFORCE estimator multiplies the reward directly into the gradient update. Gradient clipping — the standard stabilization technique in deep learning — would simply cap these large negative values at some threshold, uniformly suppressing all large-magnitude gradients. But the paper's analysis reveals that this would be counterproductive: **not all large negative rewards are equally noisy**.

The paper's scatter plot (Figure 1, right) shows that among tokens with similarly negative rewards, the vOPD advantage transformation splits them into two groups: some are dampened almost to zero (advantage near 0), while others retain negative advantages close to their original reward values. The split is determined by the per-context KL: tokens at high-KL contexts receive a large positive baseline shift that absorbs most of the negative reward, while tokens at low-KL contexts receive a small shift that leaves the reward largely intact. The paper's interpretation of this selectivity is the key conceptual contribution:

> "A large negative reward arises when the student assigns high probability to a token the teacher considers unlikely. Suppressing this token is likely to shift its mass toward the student's other high-probability candidates. In low-KL contexts, the teacher's density for these alternative tokens is also likely to be high, yielding an informative gradient... In high-KL contexts, however, these tokens may be less probable for the teacher, resulting in a harmful gradient that can be mitigated by the high baseline from vOPD." (Section 4.3)

This is a **context-dependent noise diagnostic**: the informativeness of a large negative reward is not determined by its magnitude but by the broader distributional match at that context. When distributions already largely agree (low KL), a single negative reward carries signal about which specific tokens to down-weight. When distributions broadly disagree (high KL), the same negative reward is just noise — suppressing that token won't help because the student's alternatives are also poor from the teacher's perspective. vOPD's baseline automatically implements this selectivity because the damping magnitude is exactly the per-context KL.

This mechanism is fundamentally different from gradient clipping, which operates on gradient magnitude independently of context. It is also different from the group-relative baselines in GRPO and RLOO, which compare rewards across completions but don't account for per-token distributional mismatch. The paper's gradient norm evidence (Figure 4) shows that vOPD reduces gradient norms by 1–2 orders of magnitude — a dramatically larger reduction than clipping could achieve without destroying signal — while simultaneously improving final accuracy (Table 2). This is the signature of a method that removes noise while preserving signal, in contrast to a method that uniformly attenuates both.

The approximate variance reduction formula (Eq. 11 in Appendix A.3) provides theoretical grounding for this mechanism: the variance reduction at each context scales with DKL², meaning it is largest precisely at the positions where the gradient is least informative. This is not a generic property of baseline subtraction in RL — it follows specifically from the OPD value function being the negative KL, which makes the baseline magnitude directly proportional to the distributional mismatch that causes the noise. The paper is careful to note the weak-correlation approximation underlying this formula, but the qualitative insight — that vOPD targets variance reduction at the least informative positions — is robust.

This is a **mechanistic insight** that advances understanding of OPD training dynamics beyond the descriptive "high-mismatch tokens cause instability" to a prescriptive "dampen gradients at high-KL contexts, preserve them at low-KL contexts." It explains why vOPD works without needing to be precise about the baseline value (hence the k=5 result), and it provides a diagnostic framework for future work on OPD stabilization: methods should be evaluated not just on aggregate gradient norm reduction, but on whether they selectively attenuate gradients at high-KL positions while preserving them at low-KL positions.

### Innovation 4: The Unbiasedness-Variance-Compute Taxonomy as a Design Principle

The paper's final contribution is less a single finding and more a **conceptual framework** for reasoning about OPD variants that emerges from the systematic comparison in Table 1 and Section 3.3. By organizing the five methods along three axes — gradient unbiasedness, gradient variance, and computational cost — the paper provides a **design-space map** that clarifies the tradeoffs and reveals vOPD as occupying a previously empty Pareto-optimal region.

Prior work on OPD stabilization had implicitly explored two extremes of this space. At one extreme, base OPD is cheap (single-token backward pass) but high-variance. At the other, OPD_full-V is zero-variance but expensive (full-vocabulary backward pass). OPD_top-k attempted to interpolate between these extremes by using a k-token backward pass, but at the cost of introducing bias — it moved to a different Pareto frontier where unbiasedness is sacrificed for moderate compute reduction. The paper's taxonomy makes explicit what was previously implicit: that the three axes are not independent, and that the existing methods all lie on a **biased-vs-cheap** or **unbiased-vs-expensive** tradeoff that leaves significant room for improvement.

vOPD occupies a previously empty region: **unbiased, low-variance, and cheap in the backward pass**. It achieves this by moving the expensive computation (full-vocabulary KL) from the backward pass to the forward pass, exploiting the detached baseline property. The taxonomy reveals why this region was unexplored: it requires recognizing that the backward pass cost and the forward pass cost can be decoupled for variance reduction purposes. OPD_full-V couples them — to get zero variance, you must pay full-vocabulary cost in both passes. OPD_top-k also couples them — both passes are restricted to k tokens. vOPD decouples them: the forward pass computes the full KL (for the baseline), but the backward pass touches only the single sampled token. This decoupling is only possible because of the baseline subtraction mechanism, where the variance-reducing quantity (the KL) is mathematically independent of the gradient computation (the score function at the sampled token).

The practical significance of this taxonomy extends beyond the specific methods compared. It provides a **design principle** for future OPD research: improvements should be evaluated against this three-axis framework, and the goal should be to push toward low-bias, low-variance, low-compute regions. For instance, the paper's finding that vOPD_top-k maintains unbiasedness at any k while reducing compute suggests that future work could explore even more aggressive baseline approximations — perhaps using only the top-1 token, or a simple entropy-based approximation — without introducing bias. The taxonomy also explains why certain approaches (like applying baselines to OPD_full-V) would be pointless: OPD_full-V already achieves zero per-context variance, so subtracting the expected reward contributes nothing (Eq. 13). This is a non-obvious insight that the taxonomy makes visible.

The wall-clock time results (Figure 3) anchor this taxonomy in practical terms. At the 1.7B scale, vOPD_top-k (30.76s) is 25.2% faster than OPD_full-V (41.12s) while matching its accuracy. At the 4B scale, the gap widens dramatically: vOPD_top-k (32.88s) is 57.7% faster than OPD_full-V (77.66s). This scalability advantage is not incidental — it follows directly from the decoupling principle: as model size grows, the backward pass cost (which scales with parameter count × vocabulary size for OPD_full-V) dominates, while vOPD's single-token backward pass cost scales only with parameter count. The taxonomy thus predicts that vOPD's advantage over OPD_full-V grows with model scale, which the paper confirms empirically.

This is a **framing contribution** rather than a technical one: the taxonomy does not introduce new mathematics, but it organizes existing knowledge in a way that reveals an unexploited opportunity (the unbiased-low-variance-cheap-backward region) and explains why that opportunity exists (because baseline subtraction decouples forward and backward pass costs). It converts a set of empirical observations about what works into a principled understanding of why it works and what else might work, which is the hallmark of a useful conceptual framework.

The evidence supporting this framework is the consistent pattern across all experimental settings (Tables 2, 3, 5): vOPD variants (both full-V and top-k) consistently achieve accuracy competitive with OPD_full-V while being closer to base OPD in computational cost, and OPD_top-k consistently underperforms despite higher cost than base OPD. This pattern holds across model scales (1.7B, 4B, 7B), model families (Qwen3, Olmo-3), teacher-student size mismatches (1.7B→0.6B), and reasoning domains (mathematics, science). The framework's explanatory power — its ability to predict these patterns from first principles (bias hurts, baseline subtraction helps, decoupling forward/backward cost gives the best of both) — is what elevates it from a descriptive table to a genuine conceptual contribution.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The primary mathematical reasoning training data is the **English subset of DAPO-Math-17K** (Yu et al., 2025), consisting of 14K training samples, used for a single epoch. Evaluation spans six benchmarks: **MATH500** (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2024), **Minerva Math** (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), **AMC23** (MAA, 2026), **AIME24/25** (MAA, 2026), **SciKnowEval** (Feng et al., 2024; chemistry subset with 75/5/20 train/eval/test split), and **GPQA-Diamond** (Rein et al., 2024). The diverse benchmark selection tests both in-domain mathematical generalization and out-of-domain scientific reasoning transfer.

- **Base models.** Experiments use three model configurations from two families. The primary setting distills **Qwen3-1.7B** (the post-trained teacher) into **Qwen3-1.7B-Base** (the pretrained student), mirroring industrial OPD configurations where a post-trained checkpoint is distilled back into its base model (Yang et al., 2025). Additional evaluations cover: (i) **Qwen3-4B into Qwen3-4B-Base** (testing scale), (ii) **Qwen3-1.7B into Qwen3-0.6B-Base** (testing cross-scale teacher-student mismatch), and (iii) **Olmo-3-7B-Think into Olmo-3-7B-Base** (testing model family generalization; Olmo Team, 2025). All student models are trained with LoRA (rank 64, alpha 128) using the TRL library's MiniLLM implementation, with rollouts at temperature 1.0 for training and temperature 0.6 with top-p 0.9 for evaluation.

- **Metrics.** Performance is measured via **accuracy (%)**: the fraction of evaluation questions where the model's final answer matches the ground truth. For MATH500 and Minerva Math, the paper reports **avg@8 and pass@8** (average accuracy and pass@k across 8 sampled solutions). For the smaller AMC23 and AIME benchmarks, it reports **avg@32 and pass@32** (32 samples to reduce variance on small test sets). The "Average" column in Tables 2 and 5 is the arithmetic mean of avg@n across the four mathematical benchmarks. For SciKnowEval and GPQA-Diamond, standard single-answer accuracy is reported. All evaluation uses vLLM for accelerated inference.

- **Baselines.** Five methods are compared throughout: (1) **Base OPD** — the standard single-sample Monte Carlo estimator (Eq. 3) with no stabilization; (2) **OPDₜₒₚ₋ₖ** — restricts the KL loss to the student's top-k tokens with renormalization, using k=20 following Li et al. (2026); (3) **OPD_full-V** — computes the full-vocabulary KL at every token position (Eq. 4–5), representing the most expensive but most stable existing approach (Agarwal et al., 2024); (4) **vOPDₜₒₚ₋ₖ** — the proposed method with top-k baseline approximation (default k=20); (5) **vOPD_full-V** — the proposed method with exact full-vocabulary baseline. The "student" row in each result table shows the pretrained model's performance before any OPD training.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** There is no explicit "generation budget" in the RLVR sense because OPD does not use test-time search or multiple rollouts per prompt during training. Instead, compute is accounted via **per-step wall-clock time** measured on identical hardware (single NVIDIA H200 GPU; Figure 3), capturing the total forward and backward pass cost for each method. The backward token count (Table 1) provides the theoretical cost decomposition: base OPD and both vOPD variants require a backward pass through only a single sampled token per position, OPDₜₒₚ₋ₖ through k tokens, and OPD_full-V through |V| ≈ 150k tokens. This makes wall-clock time the appropriate efficiency metric rather than a sample budget.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. All experiments use fixed train/test splits (DAPO-Math-17K English subset for training, standard benchmark test sets for evaluation) with a single training run per configuration. The paper reports point estimates without confidence intervals or error bars on accuracy metrics. The small test sizes (MATH500: 500 questions; AMC23/AIME: ~30 questions each; SciKnowEval chemistry test: 20 questions) mean that differences of a few percentage points may not be statistically robust, though the consistency of patterns across multiple benchmarks and model configurations provides some informal replication. The per-step wall-clock time measurements in Figure 3 include variance error bars derived from repeated step timing.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Mathematical Reasoning: vOPD Consistently Outperforms Base OPD and Top-k OPD, Matches Full-Vocabulary OPD

Table 2 reports the primary mathematical reasoning results across three model configurations. The headline finding is that **vOPD variants deliver substantial and consistent gains over base OPD across all settings, while matching the performance of the much more expensive OPD_full-V**.

**Qwen3-1.7B → Qwen3-1.7B-Base (Table 2, top section).** This is the primary setting. The student baseline achieves 20.6% average accuracy before any OPD training. Base OPD improves this to 29.8% average — a +9.2 percentage point gain from distillation. Against this base OPD baseline, vOPDtop-k achieves 33.0% average (a +3.2 point absolute improvement) and vOPDfull-V achieves 33.1% (a +3.3 point improvement). The gains are most pronounced on MATH500, where vOPDtop-k reaches 64.9% avg@8 versus 58.7% for base OPD (+6.2 points). On AIME24/25 — the hardest benchmark — vOPDfull-V reaches 6.2% avg@32 versus 4.8% for base OPD (+1.4 points). Crucially, the two vOPD variants achieve **nearly identical performance** (33.0% vs. 33.1% average), confirming the top-k baseline approximation captures the essential variance reduction.

OPDtop-k provides only marginal gains over base OPD: 30.2% average versus 29.8% (+0.4 points). This is consistent with Li et al. (2026)'s finding that top-k loss restriction yields minimal benefit, and confirms the paper's diagnostic: approximating the objective biases the gradient in ways that offset the variance reduction from computing a k-token expectation.

OPDfull-V achieves 33.0% average — effectively tied with both vOPD variants. This is the critical comparison: vOPD matches the performance of the most expensive stabilized method while requiring only a single-token backward pass rather than a full-vocabulary one. The wall-clock implications are shown in Figure 3 and discussed below.

**Qwen3-4B → Qwen3-4B-Base (Table 2, middle section).** Scaling to 4B parameters preserves the pattern. Base OPD reaches 42.9% average (up from 28.4% for the pretrained student). vOPDtop-k reaches 45.3% (+2.4 points over base OPD) and vOPDfull-V reaches 45.4% (+2.5 points). On MATH500, vOPDtop-k achieves 79.3% avg@8 versus 75.2% for base OPD (+4.1 points). OPDfull-V achieves 45.1%, again closely matched by both vOPD variants. OPDtop-k provides no gain (42.8% vs. 42.9%). The smaller relative gain over base OPD at 4B (+2.4 points) compared to 1.7B (+3.2 points) may reflect reduced gradient variance at larger scale (larger models may have more concentrated distributions, reducing the single-sample estimator's variance) or may simply reflect diminishing returns as accuracy approaches ceiling on some benchmarks.

**Olmo-3-7B-Think → Olmo-3-7B-Base (Table 2, bottom section).** Testing a different model family confirms generalization. Base OPD reaches 29.9% average (up from 20.2% pretrained). vOPDtop-k reaches 33.1% (+3.2 points over base OPD) and vOPDfull-V reaches 32.9% (+3.0 points). On MATH500, vOPDfull-V achieves 64.4% avg@8 versus 61.2% for base OPD (+3.2 points). OPDfull-V achieves 31.8% — slightly below both vOPD variants on average, though the differences are within the range expected from run-to-run variation on these test sets. OPDtop-k underperforms base OPD on average (29.5% vs. 29.9%), consistent with the bias hypothesis.

**Qwen3-1.7B → Qwen3-0.6B-Base (Table 5, cross-scale section).** Testing a size-mismatched teacher-student pair (1.7B teacher distilling into 0.6B student) shows the same pattern. Base OPD reaches 17.5% average. vOPDfull-V reaches 21.1% (+3.6 points), vOPDtop-k reaches 20.0% (+2.5 points). OPDfull-V reaches 20.6%, and OPDtop-k reaches 19.9% (a +2.4 point gain over base OPD — one of the few settings where OPDtop-k shows non-trivial improvement, possibly because the smaller student benefits more from even biased variance reduction when the single-sample estimator is extremely noisy). The vOPD variants' advantage persists, confirming that the control variate mechanism does not depend on matched teacher-student capacity.

**Ablation of top-k in vOPD (Table 5, bottom ablation section; Figure 2).** The paper sweeps k ∈ {5, 20, 50, 100} and the full-vocabulary baseline. All vOPDtop-k variants substantially outperform base OPD (29.8%): vOPDtop-5 reaches 33.4%, vOPDtop-20 reaches 33.0%, vOPDtop-50 reaches 32.6%, vOPDtop-100 reaches 32.3%, vOPDfull-V reaches 33.1%. The accuracy is "stable across" all k values, with vOPDtop-5 actually achieving the highest average (33.4% — though this difference from 33.0% at k=20 is almost certainly noise given the test set sizes). This is the key empirical finding supporting the paper's claim that "any nonzero k suffices" — even the coarsest baseline approximation provides sufficient variance reduction.

Figure 2 (right) measures the **mean squared error of the top-k KL estimate relative to the full-vocabulary KL**. For base OPD (which has no baseline), this equals the squared full-vocabulary KL itself (since zero baseline is subtracted). The approximation error is non-zero for all k but decreases monotonically with k, dropping to near-zero beyond k=20. Notably, k=5 carries non-trivial approximation error (~0.3 MSE on the plot) yet matches the accuracy of the full-vocabulary baseline. This supports the interpretation that the baseline's function is **coarse damping** of heavy-tailed negative rewards, not precise advantage estimation.

#### Wall-Clock Time: vOPDtop-k Offers the Best Accuracy-to-Compute Tradeoff

Figure 3 reports per-step wall-clock time measured on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU for both the 1.7B and 4B model scales. At **1.7B**, base OPD is the fastest (22.21s per step), followed by vOPDtop-k (30.76s, +38.5% overhead), vOPDfull-V (30.57s, +37.6%), OPDtop-k (30.96s, +39.4%), and OPDfull-V (41.12s, +85.1%). Both vOPD variants cluster at roughly 30.6s, substantially faster than OPDfull-V. At **4B**, the gaps widen: base OPD (25.05s), vOPDtop-k (32.88s, +31.3% overhead), vOPDfull-V (33.46s, +33.6%), OPDtop-k (48.76s, +94.7%), and OPDfull-V (77.66s, +210.1%). vOPDtop-k is now 57.7% faster than OPDfull-V, and the vOPD variants pull away from OPDtop-k as well.

The key comparison combines accuracy from Table 2 with speed from Figure 3. At 1.7B, vOPDtop-k achieves 33.0% average accuracy in 30.76s/step, versus OPDfull-V's 33.0% in 41.12s/step — **same accuracy, 25.2% less time**. At 4B, vOPDtop-k achieves 45.3% in 32.88s/step, versus OPDfull-V's 45.1% in 77.66s/step — **marginally better accuracy (by +0.2 points) with 57.7% less time**. This scalability advantage follows from the decoupling principle: OPDfull-V's cost scales with vocabulary size × model parameters in the backward pass, while vOPD's backward pass cost scales only with model parameters (the vocabulary-sized KL computation stays in the cheap forward pass). As model scale grows, the forward/backward cost ratio shifts in vOPD's favor, making it increasingly efficient relative to OPDfull-V.

OPDtop-k emerges as the worst accuracy-to-compute tradeoff: at 4B, it costs 48.76s/step (+94.7% over base OPD) while delivering only 42.8% average (+0.0 points over base OPD's 42.9%). It is simultaneously more expensive than vOPDtop-k (48.76s vs. 32.88s) and less accurate (42.8% vs. 45.3%). This is the empirical signature of paying for biased variance reduction that doesn't help.

#### Gradient Norm: vOPD Suppresses Destabilizing Gradients by 1–2 Orders of Magnitude

Figure 4 plots the gradient norm throughout training for base OPD and vOPDfull-V in the Qwen3-1.7B setting. vOPD maintains gradient norms 1–2 orders of magnitude lower than base OPD across the entire training trajectory. Base OPD's gradient norms spike in the 10⁰ to 10² range with substantial variation, while vOPD's norms stay consistently in the 10⁻² to 10⁰ range.

This is significant because the paper claims vOPD's variance reduction selectively suppresses **noise-dominated gradients** (at high-KL contexts) while preserving **signal-carrying gradients** (at low-KL contexts). If the reduced gradient norms came at the cost of suppressed signal, we would expect vOPD to train more slowly or converge to worse solutions. Instead, vOPD reaches **higher final accuracy** than base OPD (33.1% vs. 29.8% average, Table 2). This is the signature of variance reduction that removes noise rather than signal: the gradients are smaller in magnitude but more informative in direction, leading to both stability (lower norms, less fluctuation) and better outcomes (higher accuracy).

The paper explicitly contrasts this with what gradient clipping would do: "simple gradient clipping cannot replicate" vOPD's selective damping because clipping operates on gradient magnitude uniformly across all contexts, while vOPD's damping is largest at precisely the high-KL contexts where the gradient is least informative. The 1–2 order of magnitude reduction is far larger than what standard clipping thresholds (typically capping at norms of 1–10) would achieve without destroying all learning signal — clipping aggressively enough to match vOPD's reduced norms would zero out nearly all gradients and prevent learning entirely. vOPD achieves this reduction while improving learning, confirming the mechanism is selective.

#### Reward-to-Advantage Transformation: Visual Evidence of Selective Damping

Figure 1 provides token-level diagnostics from the first batch of 64 prompts (approximately 55K tokens) in the Qwen3-1.7B setting. The left panel shows the **frequency distributions** of per-token rewards (OPD) and advantages (vOPD). The OPD reward distribution exhibits a "pronounced negative long tail" — a substantial number of tokens receive rewards of -10 or lower, with some extending past -20. The vOPD advantage distribution is "visibly shifted rightward with the long tail compressed toward zero." The shift follows directly from the baseline: since DKL(πθ || πT) ≥ 0, the advantage at = rt + DKL can never be less than rt. The compression of the long tail is the visual signature of the variance reduction mechanism operating on the most destabilizing tokens.

Figure 1 (right) shows the **per-token scatter plot** of advantage (x-axis) versus reward (y-axis). All points lie on or to the right of the identity line y = x, confirming the baseline can only shift rewards positively. A key observation from the scatter is the **bifurcation of negative-reward tokens**: among tokens with similarly large negative rewards (e.g., rt ≈ -15), some have advantages near zero (points lying on or near the x-axis), while others retain large negative advantages close to their original reward values (points near y = x). This bifurcation is the empirical manifestation of the context-dependent damping mechanism. Tokens at high-KL contexts receive a large baseline shift (DKL is large), absorbing most of the negative reward and compressing the advantage toward zero. Tokens at low-KL contexts receive a small baseline shift, leaving the reward nearly intact. As the paper explains in Section 4.3:

> "In low-KL contexts, the teacher's density for these alternative tokens is also likely to be high, yielding an informative gradient with minimal influence from vOPD. In high-KL contexts, however, these tokens may be less probable for the teacher, resulting in a harmful gradient that can be mitigated by the high baseline from vOPD."

This scatter plot is the direct empirical evidence for the paper's core mechanistic claim. It shows that vOPD's transformation is not a uniform reward rescaling but a **context-dependent filtering** that preserves signal-carrying negative rewards (at low-KL contexts where down-weighting this token likely helps) while suppressing noise-dominated negative rewards (at high-KL contexts where suppressing this token doesn't help because the student's alternatives are also poor). The paper's Appendix A.3 and Figure 5 provide further support by showing that reward variation is driven primarily by the teacher log-probability (which can vary widely) rather than the student log-probability (which concentrates near zero for sampled tokens), supporting the weak-correlation assumption underlying the variance reduction formula.

#### Scientific Reasoning: Gains Generalize Beyond Mathematics

Table 3 reports results on SciKnowEval (chemistry) and GPQA-Diamond. The student baseline achieves 26.1% on SciKnowEval and 20.4% on GPQA-Diamond. Base OPD improves to 29.3% and 24.7% respectively. vOPDfull-V reaches 34.7% on SciKnowEval (+5.4 points over base OPD) and 28.4% on GPQA-Diamond (+3.7 points). vOPDtop-k achieves 33.2% (+3.9 points) and 28.6% (+3.9 points). OPDfull-V achieves 35.1% and 28.7% — slightly ahead of vOPD on SciKnowEval (+0.4 points over vOPDfull-V) but effectively tied on GPQA-Diamond. OPDtop-k shows marginal gains: 29.7% and 24.1% (+0.4 and -0.6 points respectively).

The overall pattern mirrors the mathematical reasoning results: both vOPD variants and OPDfull-V lead in performance, gaining roughly +4 percentage points over base OPD on both benchmarks, while OPDtop-k provides negligible benefit. The consistency across reasoning domains (mathematics and science) and benchmarks (MATH500, Minerva, AMC, AIME, SciKnowEval, GPQA-Diamond) supports the claim that vOPD's gains stem from a "general, principled control variate mechanism" rather than a domain-specific interaction.

The scientific reasoning experiments use a much smaller training set (1,890 samples from SciKnowEval chemistry) than the mathematical reasoning experiments (14K samples from DAPO-Math-17K), with training extended to up to 10 epochs rather than 1. The fact that vOPD's advantages persist under these different training conditions (smaller dataset, more epochs, different domain) further supports the generality of the method.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Top-k hyperparameter sensitivity (Table 5 ablation, Figure 2):** vOPDtop-k accuracy is stable across k ∈ {5, 20, 50, 100} and the full-vocabulary baseline, with all values substantially outperforming base OPD. Average accuracy ranges from 32.3% (k=100) to 33.4% (k=5), with vOPDfull-V at 33.1%. The variation across k values (±0.8 points) is within the expected noise for test sets of this size. The key finding is that **even k=5 suffices** — a coarse baseline approximation provides enough variance reduction to stabilize training. The MSE of the top-k KL estimate relative to full-vocabulary KL (Figure 2, right) shows non-trivial error at k=5 that a naive approach would consider unacceptable for precise value estimation, yet training accuracy is nearly identical across all k. This is the strongest evidence for the paper's claim that the baseline's role is coarse damping, not precise advantage estimation.

**Teacher-student size mismatch (Table 5, Qwen3-1.7B → Qwen3-0.6B-Base):** vOPD's gains persist when the teacher is larger than the student (1.7B vs. 0.6B). vOPDfull-V achieves 21.1% average vs. 17.5% for base OPD (+3.6 points), comparable to the gain at matched sizes (+3.3 points at 1.7B → 1.7B). This suggests the control variate mechanism does not depend on the student having similar capacity to the teacher — the variance reduction operates on the per-token reward distribution regardless of the absolute scale of the models.

**Model family generalization (Table 2, Olmo-3-7B):** vOPD's advantages transfer to a different model family (Olmo-3) with different architecture, pretraining data, and tokenizer. vOPDtop-k achieves 33.1% average vs. 29.9% for base OPD (+3.2 points), and vOPDfull-V achieves 32.9% (+3.0 points). The gains are comparable to the Qwen3-1.7B setting despite the different base model, confirming the method is not specific to the Qwen3 architecture or tokenizer distribution.

**Scale (Table 2, Qwen3-4B):** vOPD's relative gain over base OPD is somewhat smaller at 4B (+2.4 points for vOPDtop-k) than at 1.7B (+3.2 points), though still substantial. This may reflect reduced gradient variance at larger scale (larger models may have more peaked distributions, reducing the single-sample estimator's variance), or it may reflect ceiling effects on the easier benchmarks. The wall-clock advantage of vOPD over OPDfull-V, however, grows with scale (57.7% faster at 4B vs. 25.2% faster at 1.7B), making vOPD increasingly attractive at larger model sizes despite the slightly smaller relative accuracy gain.

**Training domain generalization (Table 3):** vOPD's gains transfer to scientific reasoning (+3.9 to +5.4 points over base OPD on SciKnowEval and GPQA-Diamond), trained with different data (SciKnowEval chemistry), different prompt format (JSON answer extraction vs. boxed math answer), and different training duration (up to 10 epochs vs. 1). This confirms the variance reduction mechanism does not depend on properties specific to mathematical reasoning data.

**Negative result — OPDtop-k consistently underperforms its compute cost (Tables 2, 3, 5):** Across all settings, OPDtop-k provides either marginal gains (+0.4% average at 1.7B) or no gain (42.8% vs. 42.9% at 4B) over base OPD, despite being substantially more expensive (Figure 3: 30.96s vs. 22.21s at 1.7B; 48.76s vs. 25.05s at 4B). This negative result is important because OPDtop-k was the prior "lightweight" stabilization method, and its failure motivates the search for alternatives like vOPD. The paper's diagnostic — that approximating the objective biases the gradient in ways that offset the variance reduction from computing a k-token expectation — is supported by this consistent empirical pattern.

**Implicit ablation — detached baseline vs. loss (Table 2, vOPDtop-k vs. OPDtop-k):** The contrast between vOPDtop-k (33.0% average) and OPDtop-k (30.2% average), both using k=20, is a direct ablation of **where** the top-k KL is placed in the estimator. Since both methods compute the same top-k KL, the ~2.8 point accuracy gap isolates the effect of using it as a detached baseline (unbiased variance reduction) versus using it as the loss (biased optimization target). This is the strongest single-experiment evidence for the paper's core claim that the role distinction (baseline vs. objective) is what matters.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: vOPD is an unbiased, lower-variance gradient estimator for OPD that requires no additional backward passes.

The experiments provide substantial evidence for the practical consequence of this claim — that vOPD trains stably and achieves higher accuracy than base OPD — but the **unbiasedness** is a theoretical property (proven in Appendix A.1) that cannot be directly verified by the reported experiments. The paper does not, for example, compare vOPD's converged solution to the true OPD optimum on a toy problem where that optimum is known, nor does it demonstrate that vOPD and OPDfull-V converge to identical solutions given enough training (they achieve similar final accuracies, but that could be consistent with differently biased estimators landing near the same point on the test metrics). The unbiasedness claim rests on the mathematical argument that the baseline is independent of the sampled action, which is correct for both vOPDfull-V and vOPDtop-k, but the experiments don't directly test this.

What the experiments *do* verify is the **variance reduction** claim: Figure 4 shows gradient norms reduced by 1–2 orders of magnitude; Figure 1 shows the reward distribution's negative long tail compressed in the advantage distribution; and the training stability improvements are implied by vOPD's higher final accuracy despite smaller gradient steps. The fact that vOPDtop-k remains unbiased at any k is a theoretical result, but the experiments support its practical consequence: stable training even with k=5 where the baseline approximation is imprecise.

A missing experiment that would strengthen the unbiasedness claim is a **convergence analysis**: does vOPD converge to the same minimum as OPDfull-V when both are trained to convergence on the same data? The paper's single-epoch training on 14K samples may not reach convergence, so the reported accuracies reflect a combination of optimization speed and final solution quality. If vOPD is truly unbiased, it should converge to the same solution as OPDfull-V given enough training; the paper's results are consistent with this (they achieve similar accuracy) but don't demonstrate it conclusively.

#### Claim 2: vOPD matches the performance of full-vocabulary OPD at substantially lower computational cost.

This claim is **well-supported** by the reported experiments, with appropriate nuance. Across all settings in Table 2, vOPDfull-V and OPDfull-V achieve nearly identical accuracy: 33.1% vs. 33.0% at 1.7B, 45.4% vs. 45.1% at 4B, 32.9% vs. 31.8% at Olmo-7B. The wall-clock measurements in Figure 3 show vOPDfull-V is 25.2% faster at 1.7B and 56.9% faster at 4B than OPDfull-V. vOPDtop-k adds further speed while matching accuracy: at 4B, vOPDtop-k achieves 45.3% (vs. OPDfull-V's 45.1%) in 32.88s/step (vs. 77.66s/step) — a 57.7% reduction.

However, the wall-clock measurements come with important caveats. The paper acknowledges that "wall-clock results reflect our implementation and are not definitive" (Section 6). The absolute speed of each method depends on implementation details — how efficiently the top-k selection is computed, whether the full-vocabulary KL is computed via a single matrix multiplication or a loop, memory access patterns, etc. A highly optimized OPDfull-V implementation (e.g., using fused kernels for the full-vocabulary backward pass) might close some of the gap. Conversely, vOPDtop-k's top-k selection itself has overhead (sorting 150k values per token position) that might be optimizable. The relative rankings are likely robust, but the exact percentage improvements should be taken as indicative rather than definitive.

A missing comparison is **total training time to reach a target accuracy**. The paper reports per-step wall-clock time and final accuracy, but different methods may converge at different rates — vOPD might require fewer steps to reach a given accuracy due to lower variance (faster convergence per step), which would compound the per-step speed advantage. Alternatively, if vOPD's reduced gradients require more steps to converge (smaller but more informative steps might need more iterations to cover the same distance in parameter space), the per-step advantage might partially offset. A learning curve (accuracy vs. wall-clock time) would resolve this and is a notable omission.

#### Claim 3: A top-k approximation of the baseline preserves gradient unbiasedness and variance reduction regardless of k.

This claim has two parts — **unbiasedness** (theoretical) and **variance reduction sufficiency** (empirical). The unbiasedness is proven mathematically for any k (Section 3.2) and doesn't require experimental validation — it follows from the baseline being independent of the sampled token, which is true regardless of how inaccurately the top-k KL approximates the full KL.

The empirical claim about variance reduction sufficiency is **strongly supported** by the k-ablation in Table 5 and Figure 2. All k values from 5 to 100 and the full-vocabulary baseline produce nearly identical accuracy (32.3%–33.4%), all well above base OPD (29.8%). The MSE measurements in Figure 2 (right) confirm that k=5 carries non-trivial approximation error relative to the full-vocabulary KL, yet this imprecision doesn't hurt training. This supports the paper's interpretation that only coarse variance reduction is needed.

However, the ablation only tests one training configuration (Qwen3-1.7B, DAPO-Math-17K, 1 epoch). At larger scales, longer training, or different data distributions, the sufficiency of k=5 might not hold — perhaps more precise baselines become necessary when training for more epochs (the benefit of variance reduction might compound over more steps) or when the student-teacher distribution mismatch is larger (making both the KL and its top-k approximation error larger in absolute terms). This is an acknowledged limitation (Section 6: "validating vOPD at a larger scale is a next step").

#### Claim 4: vOPD reduces gradient variance most strongly on destabilizing high-mismatch tokens, acting as a selective regularizer.

This is the paper's **mechanistic claim**, and the evidence is primarily correlational rather than causal. Figure 1 shows that the advantage distribution compresses the negative long tail — this is consistent with the mechanism but doesn't prove that the compressed tokens are specifically the ones that *cause* training instability. The gradient norm reduction (Figure 4) shows that vOPD trains with smaller gradients — this is consistent with variance reduction improving stability but could also result from a uniform downscaling of all gradients (which the paper argues it's not, based on the scatter plot's bifurcation pattern). The improved final accuracy confirms that the gradients, despite being smaller, are more effective — this rules out uniform downscaling as the explanation (which would either slow convergence or hurt final accuracy).

Stronger evidence for the **selective** nature of the damping would come from token-level analyses connecting the advantage transformation to downstream training dynamics: do tokens whose advantages were heavily damped by vOPD (near-zero advantage despite large negative reward) show different gradient update patterns than tokens whose advantages were preserved? Does the variance reduction correlate with per-token KL magnitude as predicted by Eq. 11? The paper provides the theoretical derivation (Appendix A.3) and the qualitative scatter plot bifurcation, but doesn't quantitatively verify the predicted proportionality between variance reduction and DKL².

The weak-correlation approximation underlying the variance reduction formula (Appendix A.3) is empirically motivated by Figure 5, which shows weaker correlation between reward and student log-probability than between reward and teacher log-probability. This supports the approximation's premise but is not a direct test of the formula's accuracy — measuring the actual gradient variance with and without the baseline at individual token positions (requiring multiple rollouts from the same context) would be a more direct validation, though computationally expensive.

#### Cross-cutting Concerns

**Single training run per configuration.** All results are single-point estimates without error bars or multiple seeds. Given the relatively small test sets (500 questions for MATH500, ~30 for AIME, 20 for SciKnowEval test), differences of 1–2 percentage points (e.g., vOPDtop-5's 33.4% vs. vOPDtop-100's 32.3% in the ablation) are likely within run-to-run variation. The consistency of patterns across multiple benchmarks and model configurations provides informal replication, but the lack of error bars means the precise numerical gains (e.g., "+3.2 points at 1.7B") should be interpreted with appropriate uncertainty. The gradient norm curves in Figure 4 are from single training runs — variance in these curves across seeds is unknown.

**Training data is modest (14K samples, 1 epoch).** The primary experiments train on only 14K mathematical problems for a single epoch. This is a relatively light distillation regimen compared to industrial-scale OPD (which may use millions of samples for multiple epochs). At larger data scales, the relative importance of gradient variance vs. bias may shift — with more data, the noise in the single-sample estimator averages out over more gradient steps, potentially reducing the advantage of variance reduction techniques. Conversely, with larger models and more training, the absolute wall-clock time savings from vOPD's cheaper backward pass compound. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and calls for larger-scale validation (Section 6).

**No comparison to RLVR baselines.** The paper motivates OPD as an alternative to RLVR (Section 1) and claims OPD "empirically matches RLVR accuracy with a fraction of the compute," but provides no direct OPD vs. RLVR comparison in the experiments. This is reasonable given the paper's scope (stabilizing OPD, not comparing OPD to RLVR), but it means the practical significance of OPD stabilization — whether stabilized OPD actually outperforms RLVR at the same compute budget — remains an extrapolation from prior work rather than a demonstrated result in this paper.

**No sequence-level KL or multi-sample baselines.** The paper focuses exclusively on the token-level REINFORCE formulation of OPD. Recent work has explored sequence-level KL objectives and multi-sample estimators (e.g., using multiple rollouts per prompt to estimate advantages, analogous to GRPO's group-relative baseline). The paper doesn't compare against these alternatives, and it's possible that a combination of vOPD's per-token baseline with multi-sample sequence-level estimators would yield further improvements. This is acknowledged as future work (Section 6: "considering sequence-level KL objectives is another potential extension").

**Missing convergence analysis.** The paper doesn't report training curves (accuracy vs. step) for different methods. If vOPD's variance reduction translates to faster convergence (reaching a given accuracy in fewer steps), the effective speedup over OPDfull-V would be larger than the per-step measurements indicate. Conversely, if vOPD's smaller gradient steps require more iterations, the per-step advantage might partially offset. Learning curves would clarify the net effect and are a notable omission for a paper whose core contribution is an optimization technique.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Single Scale, Single Data Regime: vOPD Has Only Been Tested on Small Models with Short Training Runs

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use models up to 7B parameters (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-0.6B, Olmo-3-7B) trained on modest datasets (14K mathematical problems for 1 epoch, 1,890 scientific reasoning problems for up to 10 epochs) with parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA. The paper explicitly acknowledges this scope limitation: "Experiments in this work are limited to models up to 7B parameters, and validating vOPD at a larger scale is a next step" (Section 6).

**The consequence.** The practical motivation for vOPD is industrial-scale OPD — the paper cites Qwen3, GLM-5, Nemotron-Cascade2, and DeepSeek-V4 as systems where OPD instability is a real problem. These systems involve models with hundreds of billions of parameters trained on millions of samples for many epochs. Several failure modes become plausible at scale that the current experiments cannot detect:

- **The relative advantage of variance reduction may diminish.** At larger data scales, the noise in the single-sample Monte Carlo estimator averages out over exponentially more gradient steps. If the primary benefit of vOPD is faster convergence per step (fewer noisy gradient updates needed to reach a given solution quality), then with enough data, base OPD may eventually catch up — the variance reduction becomes a *speed* advantage rather than an *asymptotic* advantage. The paper cannot distinguish these because it trains for only 1 epoch on 14K samples, which may not be sufficient for any method to converge.

- **The top-k approximation may degrade at scale.** The paper's key finding that k=5 suffices for variance reduction is derived from a single 1.7B model trained for 1 epoch. At larger model scales with larger vocabularies and more peaked distributions, the top-k KL approximation error might grow — or the absolute magnitude of the KL divergence might increase — in ways that change whether coarse baseline approximations remain sufficient. The paper's claim that "any nonzero k suffices" (Section 4.3) has only been verified in the small-model, short-training regime.

- **The wall-clock advantage over OPD_full-V may not scale as cleanly as suggested.** The paper argues that vOPD's advantage over OPD_full-V grows with model scale because vOPD's backward pass cost scales only with parameter count while OPD_full-V's scales with parameter count × vocabulary size (Section 3.3). This is correct asymptotically, but at very large scales, other factors dominate: communication overhead in distributed training, memory constraints that force different batch-size or gradient-accumulation strategies, and the fact that OPD_full-V's full-vocabulary backward pass may be efficiently parallelized across devices in ways that close the gap. The paper's scaling trend from 1.7B to 4B is suggestive but extrapolating to 70B or 400B models is speculative.

- **LoRA may obscure the full variance structure.** All experiments use LoRA with rank 64. LoRA reduces the effective parameter count and may concentrate gradient variance into the low-rank update matrices, changing how the per-token reward variance propagates to parameter updates. Full fine-tuning — the regime used in industrial OPD — would expose the full parameter gradient to the reward variance, potentially making vOPD's variance reduction more impactful, or alternatively, making base OPD's instability even worse than what the LoRA experiments suggest. The paper provides no full-fine-tuning results.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper tests two model scales (1.7B and 4B, Section 4.2, Table 2) and shows that vOPD's accuracy gains persist at 4B (+2.4 points over base OPD vs. +3.2 points at 1.7B). The wall-clock measurements (Figure 3) show an increasing speed advantage at 4B (57.7% faster than OPD_full-V) versus 1.7B (25.2% faster), consistent with the theoretical argument that vOPD's advantage scales with model size. However, these are only two points on a curve, with the largest model being 7B (Olmo-3, Table 2), and the trends are not monotonic (the accuracy gain at 7B is +3.2 points, back to the 1.7B level). All experiments use LoRA.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and frames larger-scale validation as future work (Section 6). It provides no extrapolation analysis, no theoretical argument for why the benefits should persist rather than diminish at scale, and no discussion of how distributed training might affect the wall-clock comparisons. This is the most significant limitation for practitioners evaluating whether to adopt vOPD in production OPD pipelines, because the paper's evidence is entirely from a scale regime where training instability is less costly and easier to work around.

### Difficulty Estimation for Real Deployment Is Not Addressed: The Teacher Model Assumption

**The assumption or constraint.** vOPD, like all OPD methods, requires access to a frozen, stronger teacher model that provides token-level probability distributions at every position during training. The paper's experiments use post-trained checkpoints (Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Olmo-3-7B-Think) as teachers distilling into their corresponding base models (Section 4.1). The paper briefly acknowledges this constraint: "As a distillation method, vOPD requires access to a stronger teacher" (Section 6).

**The consequence.** This is not a limitation of vOPD specifically — it inherits this constraint from OPD — but it has consequences for how broadly vOPD's stabilization benefits apply. OPD is already filtering for scenarios where a strong teacher exists, and vOPD improves training within that filtered set. However, the paper's framing positions vOPD as a solution to OPD instability in industrial post-training pipelines, and several of those pipelines use **self-distillation** — where a model distills from an earlier checkpoint of itself, or where a single model generates its own training data through repeated sampling and filtering. In these scenarios, there is either no strictly "stronger" teacher, or the teacher's probability distribution is not well-separated from the student's (since they share the same architecture and initialization). The paper does not evaluate whether vOPD's variance reduction mechanism functions correctly — or is even needed — when the student-teacher gap is small or when the teacher is not consistently more accurate than the student.

Additionally, the need for a frozen teacher model during training doubles the memory requirements: both the student (with LoRA adapters and optimizer states) and the full teacher model (inference-only) must reside in GPU memory simultaneously. For large-scale training where memory is already saturated by the student model and optimizer states, the teacher's memory footprint may constrain batch sizes or require model parallelism strategies that complicate the training setup. The paper's experiments with LoRA on models up to 7B fit comfortably on a single H200 GPU (as evidenced by the per-step wall-clock measurements in Figure 3), so this memory constraint is invisible in the reported results.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper tests one cross-scale setting: Qwen3-1.7B (teacher) distilling into Qwen3-0.6B-Base (student), where the teacher is ~2.8× larger than the student (Table 5). vOPD's gains persist in this setting (+3.6 points for vOPD_full-V over base OPD), suggesting the mechanism works when the teacher is substantially stronger. However, the paper does not test the reverse (a smaller or equal-capacity teacher), which would be relevant for self-distillation. The paper also does not test with teachers from different model families (e.g., distilling from Qwen3 into Olmo-3), which would have larger distributional gaps and potentially larger KL values, changing the variance reduction dynamics.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the teacher constraint and lists "extending [vOPD] to self-distillation settings" as future work (Section 6). It provides no analysis of how teacher quality affects vOPD's variance reduction — for instance, ablation studies varying teacher strength (using smaller or earlier-checkpoint teachers) to see whether the per-token KL baseline remains a good variance reducer when the teacher is weak. The memory overhead of running two models simultaneously is not discussed.

### No Convergence or Learning Dynamics Analysis: Point Estimates Without Training Curves

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports only final accuracy after fixed training (1 epoch for math, up to 10 epochs for science) and provides no training curves — accuracy or loss as a function of training steps or wall-clock time. The only training-dynamics measurement is the gradient norm plot in Figure 4, and even that is from a single training run without error bars or comparison to validation accuracy over time.

**The consequence.** Without learning curves, the paper cannot distinguish between two fundamentally different explanations for why vOPD achieves higher final accuracy:

- **Faster convergence:** vOPD reaches a given accuracy in fewer steps because its lower-variance gradients are more informative per step. If base OPD were trained for longer, it might eventually reach the same accuracy as vOPD, and vOPD's advantage would be primarily a speedup rather than an improvement in the achievable solution quality.

- **Better final solution:** vOPD converges to a genuinely different (and better) local minimum because the reduced variance allows the optimizer to navigate the loss landscape more effectively, avoiding noisy gradient steps that push the parameters into poor regions that are hard to escape.

These interpretations have very different practical implications. If vOPD provides only faster convergence, its value is a compute multiplier — you can train for fewer steps to reach the same accuracy — but with unlimited compute, base OPD catches up. If vOPD provides better final solutions, its value is a capability improvement that cannot be replicated by simply training base OPD for longer. The paper's single-epoch, fixed-step training design cannot distinguish these, and the authors do not discuss this ambiguity.

The gradient norm evidence (Figure 4) shows vOPD trains with 1–2 orders of magnitude lower gradient norms throughout the entire training trajectory. This is consistent with both interpretations: lower variance could mean more efficient progress (faster convergence) or could mean the optimizer settles into a different, better basin (better solution). The fact that vOPD achieves higher accuracy than base OPD at the same step count is consistent with faster convergence (base OPD hasn't converged yet and would catch up with more steps), while the fact that OPD_full-V — which has near-zero variance — achieves similar accuracy to vOPD is consistent with vOPD's variance reduction being sufficient to match the best possible OPD solution at this data scale (better solution).

The wall-clock measurements compound this ambiguity. The paper reports vOPD as being faster per step than OPD_full-V, but if vOPD requires the same number of steps to converge (while OPD_full-V might require fewer due to zero variance), the net training-time advantage could be smaller or reversed. Conversely, if vOPD converges in fewer steps than base OPD, the per-step speedup understates the total advantage.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The gradient norm plot (Figure 4) is the only training-dynamics data. All accuracy results are final-point estimates after fixed training duration. The paper never reports intermediate checkpoints, validation accuracy during training, or the number of steps required to reach a target accuracy. The single-epoch training on 14K samples may not be sufficient for convergence of any method, meaning the reported accuracies reflect a mixture of convergence speed and solution quality that cannot be disentangled.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this ambiguity or discuss the convergence-vs-solution-quality distinction. There is no suggestion of future work on learning dynamics analysis. This is a significant omission for a paper whose core claim is about optimization stability — understanding whether stability translates to faster convergence, better solutions, or both is central to evaluating the practical value of the method.

### Wall-Clock Time Measurements Are Implementation-Specific and Incomplete

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's efficiency claims rest on per-step wall-clock time measurements on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU (Figure 3), averaged over training steps. The paper acknowledges that these "reflect our implementation and are not definitive" (Section 6).

**The consequence.** The wall-clock measurements capture the specific implementation choices in the paper's codebase (built on TRL's MiniLLM implementation), and several aspects of these measurements may not transfer to other implementations or hardware configurations:

- **Top-k selection overhead:** vOPD_top-k requires finding the top-k tokens from a 150K-element distribution at every token position for every sequence in the batch. The efficiency of this operation depends heavily on the implementation — naive sorting is O(|V| log |V|) per position; efficient top-k selection algorithms can reduce this to O(|V| log k). The paper does not specify how top-k is implemented, and different implementations could change the relative speed of vOPD_top-k versus vOPD_full-V (which requires a full sum over the vocabulary, an O(|V|) operation that may be efficiently parallelized on GPU).

- **Full-vocabulary KL computation:** vOPD_full-V requires computing the KL over all |V| tokens in the forward pass. In the paper's implementation, this appears to be done as a full-vector operation (the per-token KL computation time in Figure 3: 30.57s for vOPD_full-V vs. 30.76s for vOPD_top-k at 1.7B — nearly identical). A highly optimized implementation might compute this full-vocabulary KL using a single fused kernel that is significantly faster than the per-token loop the paper may be using. Conversely, the top-k selection in vOPD_top-k introduces a sorting bottleneck that may not parallelize as well on GPU.

- **OPD_full-V backward pass optimization:** The paper reports OPD_full-V as taking 77.66s per step at 4B, but this includes a backward pass through |V| tokens at every position. Modern deep learning frameworks can fuse the backward pass through large softmax operations, and with sufficient GPU memory, this operation can be heavily parallelized. The paper does not report whether standard optimizations (e.g., torch.compile, flash-attention-style kernel fusion) are applied to any of the compared methods, and differential application of such optimizations could skew the relative timings.

- **Batch size and memory effects:** The per-step timings are measured on a single GPU with a batch size of 64 and maximum sequence length of 2048 tokens. At larger scales where model parallelism or gradient accumulation is required, the relative costs of forward-pass vocabulary operations versus backward-pass vocabulary operations may shift — communication overhead, pipeline bubbles, and memory access patterns change, and the simple FLOPs-based argument that vOPD beats OPD_full-V may not directly translate to distributed training wall-clock time.

- **Total training time vs. per-step time:** The paper reports per-step time but not total training time to reach a target accuracy. As discussed in the previous limitation, if different methods converge at different rates, the per-step comparison is insufficient to evaluate end-to-end efficiency.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 provides per-step wall-clock time for all five methods at two model scales, with error bars showing variance across steps. The measurements are taken on identical hardware and software, providing a fair within-paper comparison, but they reflect a single implementation and hardware configuration. The paper does not report GPU memory usage, which would affect batch size choices in practice.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags the implementation-specific nature of the wall-clock results (Section 6) and does not claim the specific speedup percentages are universal. The relative ordering of methods (base OPD fastest, vOPD variants in the middle, OPD_full-V slowest) is likely robust, but the exact magnitudes (e.g., "57.7% faster") should be treated as indicative rather than transferable. The paper suggests future work could "optimize vOPD_top-k to be faster than vOPD_full-V" (Section 6), acknowledging that the current implementation may not be optimal.

### No Statistical Rigor: Point Estimates from Small Test Sets Without Uncertainty Quantification

**The assumption or constraint.** All accuracy results in the paper are point estimates from fixed evaluation runs with no confidence intervals, standard errors, or multi-seed replication. The test sets vary substantially in size: MATH500 has 500 questions, Minerva Math has a few hundred, AMC23 and AIME24/25 have approximately 30 questions each, and SciKnowEval chemistry test has 20 questions (Section 4.1). The paper runs each configuration once and reports the single-run accuracy.

**The consequence.** On small test sets, sampling error can be substantial. For AIME24/25 with ~30 test questions evaluated with 32 samples each (avg@32), a difference of 1–2 percentage points (e.g., vOPD_full-V's 6.2% vs. OPD's 4.8% in Table 2, 1.7B setting) corresponds to the model getting only about 1 more question correct out of 30. This could plausibly arise from which specific AIME problems happen to be in the test set rather than from a genuine improvement in reasoning capability. Similarly, improvements on SciKnowEval (20 test questions) of 5.4 percentage points (Table 3: vOPD_full-V 34.7% vs. OPD 29.3%) represent getting roughly 1 more question correct.

The paper draws conclusions from averages across benchmarks (the "Avg." column in Table 2), which helps aggregate signal across test sets but does not address the underlying per-benchmark uncertainty. If the apparent gains on MATH500 and Minerva Math are genuine while the AIME and AMC gains are noise, the average still shows an improvement, but the improvement is not uniformly distributed across difficulty levels or reasoning types.

The gradient norm and reward distribution analyses (Figures 1, 4) are from a single batch of 64 prompts from a single training run. The shapes of these distributions — particularly the long negative tail of the reward distribution and its compression by vOPD — might vary substantially across different batches, different prompts, or different random seeds. The paper presents these as representative visualizations without quantifying the variance across runs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Consistency across benchmarks provides informal replication: vOPD outperforms base OPD on MATH500, Minerva Math, AMC23, AIME24/25, SciKnowEval, and GPQA-Diamond (Tables 2, 3, 5), across three model configurations and one cross-scale setting. This broad consistency makes it unlikely that the gains are entirely due to test-set noise, but the magnitude of individual benchmark gains remains uncertain. The paper does not report the statistical significance of any comparison, does not use multiple training seeds, and provides no error bars on accuracy measurements.

The gradient norm plot (Figure 4) shows a single training run per method. Gradient norms are known to be noisy during LLM training, and the paper's claim of "1–2 orders of magnitude" reduction would be more convincing with multi-seed averages showing that this reduction is consistent and not specific to a particular training trajectory. The paper's scatter plot (Figure 1, right) shows ~55K tokens from one batch — this is sufficient to visualize the bifurcation pattern qualitatively, but the claim that the pattern reflects a systematic mechanism rather than sampling noise would be strengthened by showing it holds across multiple batches or training stages.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this limitation or propose any uncertainty quantification. The consistent pattern across benchmarks and model configurations provides some informal confidence, but the lack of standard errors or multi-seed results means the reported numerical improvements — particularly on small benchmarks like AIME and SciKnowEval — should be interpreted as approximate rather than precise. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt vOPD based on, say, a reported +6.2% improvement on MATH500 would want to know whether that number is 6.2% ± 0.5% or 6.2% ± 3%.

### The Weak-Correlation Approximation Underlying the Variance Reduction Analysis Is Not Directly Validated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's theoretical analysis of variance reduction — the derivation of Eq. 11 in Appendix A.3 showing that vOPD reduces per-step gradient variance by approximately DKL² · E[||∇ log πθ||²] — relies on a "weak-correlation approximation" that assumes the per-token reward rt and the squared gradient norm ||∇ log πθ||² are not strongly correlated. This approximation allows the optimal baseline to simplify from a weighted expectation (Eq. 23) to the ordinary value function Vπθ(ct). The paper provides qualitative justification for this assumption (Figure 5: student log-probability concentrates near zero with weak correlation to reward, while teacher log-probability drives reward variation) but does not directly validate the formula.

**The consequence.** If the weak-correlation approximation fails in practice — if rt and ||∇ log πθ||² are strongly correlated at certain token positions — then the value function is not the optimal baseline, and vOPD may leave substantial variance on the table. More importantly, the formula in Eq. 11 would not accurately describe how much variance reduction vOPD achieves, making it an unreliable diagnostic for understanding when vOPD helps and when it doesn't.

The approximation's validity may depend on properties of the model and data distribution that change across training. Early in training, when the student distribution is far from the teacher, both the KL and the reward variance may be large, and the correlation structure might be different from late in training when the distributions have largely aligned. The paper's Figure 5 uses data from the first batch of training in the Qwen3-1.7B setting — this characterizes the early-training correlation structure but not how it evolves. If the correlation between rt and ||∇ log πθ||² changes over training, the variance reduction formula would need to be time-dependent, which the paper does not explore.

Additionally, the weak-correlation approximation is used in Appendix A.3 to derive the variance reduction formula, but that formula is never empirically verified. The paper shows that vOPD reduces gradient norms (Figure 4) and that vOPD improves accuracy (Table 2), but these are aggregate effects that could arise from mechanisms other than the specific DKL²-scaling predicted by Eq. 11. For instance, if the baseline simply clips all rewards to be non-negative (since at = rt + DKL ≥ 0 when DKL ≥ |rt| for negative rewards), that uniform clipping might account for the variance reduction without the DKL² scaling. The paper's claim that variance reduction "scales with DKL(πθ||πT)², largest at exactly the contexts where updates are least informative" (Section 4.3) is an interpretation consistent with the data but not directly tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 (Appendix A.3) shows scatter plots of reward versus student log-probability and reward versus teacher log-probability for ~55K tokens from the first training batch. The student log-probability shows a narrow range (roughly -15 to 0) with weak correlation to reward, while the teacher log-probability shows a wider range (roughly -25 to 0) with strong correlation to reward. This supports the premise that reward variation is driven by the teacher term rather than the student term, which in turn supports the weak-correlation assumption between reward and student-dependent quantities like ||∇ log πθ||². However, this is an indirect argument — the actual correlation between rt and ||∇ log πθ||² is never measured, the derivation's approximation steps are not empirically checked, and the predicted DKL² scaling of variance reduction is not directly verified by measuring per-context gradient variance with and without the baseline.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this being an approximation, using the "≈" symbol in Eq. 26 and labeling it "weak-correlation approximation" in Appendix A.3. The theoretical analysis is presented as providing qualitative insight into why vOPD works — dampening gradients at high-KL contexts — rather than as a precise quantitative law. The empirical results (particularly the k-ablation showing that even k=5 provides sufficient variance reduction) do not depend on the formula's exact quantitative accuracy; they only require the qualitative insight that variance reduction is largest at high-KL contexts, which is robust. The paper does not suggest future work to validate or refine the variance reduction formula, nor does it discuss the limitations of the approximation for understanding vOPD's behavior in edge cases (e.g., when ||∇ log πθ||² varies systematically with KL due to model architecture properties).

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

vOPD does not introduce a new training objective, a new model architecture, or a new distillation paradigm. It is a **gradient estimator** — a modification to how the gradient of the existing OPD objective is computed, not to what objective is optimized. This might seem like a narrow contribution, but its implications for how the field approaches OPD training are substantial because it resolves a persistent tension that had forced practitioners into an unsatisfying tradeoff: accept training instability (base OPD), pay prohibitive computational overhead for stability (OPD_full-V), or accept bias for marginal gains (OPD_top-k). vOPD occupies a previously empty region of the design space — unbiased, low-variance, and cheap in the backward pass — and in doing so, changes the conversation around OPD stabilization from "which compromise should we accept?" to "how much baseline precision do we actually need?"

The most significant conceptual shift is the **decoupling of forward-pass and backward-pass costs for variance reduction**. Prior to vOPD, the implicit assumption in OPD stabilization was that reducing gradient variance requires reducing the Monte Carlo sampling error *in the gradient computation itself* — hence OPD_full-V's full-vocabulary backward pass (zero per-context variance by computing the exact expectation) and OPD_top-k's k-token backward pass (reduced variance by averaging over k samples). Both methods couple the variance reduction mechanism to the backward pass, which is the expensive part of training. vOPD shows that this coupling is unnecessary: by subtracting a detached baseline computed in the forward pass, you can achieve nearly the same variance reduction while keeping the backward pass as cheap as single-sample OPD. This is not an incremental optimization — it is a **structural insight** that the variance-reducing quantity (the value function) is mathematically independent of the gradient computation (the score function at the sampled token), and that in OPD specifically, this quantity is available in closed form from the forward pass.

This insight reframes the problem. The question is no longer "how do we make the Monte Carlo estimator more accurate?" but rather "what is the minimal additional computation needed to dampen the specific noise that destabilizes training?" The paper's answer — a coarse KL estimate used as a detached baseline — is both principled (it's the canonical variance reducer from REINFORCE) and pragmatic (even k=5 provides enough KL signal for effective damping). This changes what "good enough" means for OPD stabilization: the baseline does not need to be a precise value estimate; it only needs to identify high-KL contexts where the gradient signal is least informative and suppress their contributions.

The work also **reconciles a puzzling empirical contradiction** in the OPD literature. Li et al. (2026) showed that OPD_top-k, which restricts the KL loss to the student's top-k tokens, yields only marginal gains over base OPD despite adding compute. This was interpreted as evidence that the tail of the teacher distribution carries important signal that top-k truncation destroys. vOPD's results suggest a different interpretation: the top-k approximation is not inherently harmful — it is harmful specifically when used as the loss (biasing the optimization target), but beneficial when used as a baseline (reducing estimator variance). The ~2.8 percentage point gap between vOPD_top-20 (33.0% average) and OPD_top-20 (30.2% average) at 1.7B in Table 2 is a direct ablation of this distinction, using the same k=20 top-k KL in two different estimator positions. This reconciles the prior finding: OPD_top-k's failure is not about k being too small, but about placing the approximation in the wrong position in the estimator. This diagnostic — that bias from objective approximation is fundamentally different from imprecision in baseline estimation — provides a unified framework for understanding why existing stabilization methods succeed or fail, and it redirects future research toward better baseline design rather than better objective approximation.

Finally, vOPD changes the **practical economics of OPD at scale**. The wall-clock measurements in Figure 3 show that vOPD's advantage over OPD_full-V grows with model size: at 1.7B, vOPD_top-k is 25.2% faster per step than OPD_full-V while matching accuracy; at 4B, it is 57.7% faster. This scaling trend follows from the decoupling principle — OPD_full-V's cost scales with vocabulary size × parameter count in the backward pass, while vOPD's backward pass cost scales only with parameter count. For industrial OPD pipelines (Qwen3, GLM-5, DeepSeek-V4) where training involves hundreds of billions of parameters, this difference could translate into millions of dollars of compute savings per training run, without sacrificing the stabilization that makes OPD viable in the first place. This is not just an academic improvement; it is a direct efficiency gain for the largest-scale LLM training operations currently in production.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling vOPD to 70B+ models with full fine-tuning.** The paper's experiments are limited to models up to 7B parameters trained with LoRA. The central claim — that vOPD's backward pass remains single-token while OPD_full-V's scales with vocabulary size — predicts an efficiency advantage that grows with model scale, but this has not been tested beyond 4B. A direct replication of the Table 2 experiments at the 70B scale (e.g., distilling Qwen3-70B into Qwen3-70B-Base, or Llama-4 Maverick into Llama-4 base) with full fine-tuning would test whether the per-step speedup extrapolates as the FLOPs analysis predicts, and whether the accuracy gains persist when training for multiple epochs on millions of samples rather than a single epoch on 14K. This experiment would also reveal whether full fine-tuning changes the variance structure relative to LoRA — specifically, whether exposing the full parameter gradient to the reward variance makes vOPD's variance reduction more impactful (because more parameters means more dimensions for noise to propagate through), or whether alternative stabilization techniques become necessary at scale. A strong result would be demonstrating that vOPD_top-k with k=20 enables stable full-parameter OPD training of a 70B model at wall-clock time comparable to base OPD with gradient clipping, but with final accuracy matching OPD_full-V.

**vOPD for self-distillation and online RLVR-OPD hybrids.** The paper explicitly flags self-distillation as future work (Section 6), and this direction has both practical importance and theoretical interest. In self-distillation, the teacher is an earlier checkpoint of the same model (or the same model with different sampling parameters), so the student-teacher distribution gap is typically smaller and evolves during training as the student improves. vOPD's variance reduction mechanism depends on the per-token KL — when the KL is small (student and teacher largely agree), the baseline is near zero and vOPD reduces to base OPD. This means vOPD would automatically scale its stabilization with the distributional gap: strong stabilization early in training when the gap is large, minimal overhead late in training when distributions align. A concrete experiment would be implementing self-distillation for reasoning models (e.g., distilling from a model that uses extended thinking into the same model with standard decoding) and comparing vOPD against base OPD and OPD_full-V on both training stability and final accuracy. A negative result — that vOPD provides no benefit because the student-teacher KL is never large enough to cause destabilizing gradient spikes — would establish a boundary condition on when vOPD is useful, which is as valuable as a positive result. A more ambitious variant would be integrating vOPD into RLVR pipelines that alternate between RLVR steps (sparse terminal reward) and OPD steps (dense token-level teacher signal), using vOPD to stabilize the OPD phase without adding the full-vocabulary overhead that would make such hybrid training prohibitively expensive.

**Combining vOPD with multi-sample or sequence-level baselines.** The paper focuses entirely on per-token, single-sample estimation. Modern RLVR methods like GRPO and RLOO construct baselines by comparing rewards across multiple complete rollouts from the same prompt (group-relative baselines), exploiting the fact that different completions of the same prompt share the same expected reward structure. In OPD, multiple rollouts from the same context are rarely generated because the context is per-token and varies with each sampled sequence. However, one could generate multiple independent continuations from the *same* intermediate context (a branching approach), compute the per-token reward for each continuation, and construct a group-relative baseline from the rewards of the sibling tokens. This would combine vOPD's per-token value baseline (which captures distributional mismatch) with a multi-sample baseline (which captures sampling noise within a given distribution). A concrete experiment would compare (a) standard vOPD, (b) multi-sample OPD with group-relative baseline (branching 4–8 continuations per context), and (c) vOPD with group-relative baseline, measuring both gradient variance and final accuracy at matched compute budgets. If the per-token KL baseline and the group-relative baseline capture orthogonal sources of variance (distributional mismatch vs. sampling noise), combining them could yield variance reduction beyond either alone, potentially enabling stable OPD training even at very high learning rates or with very small batch sizes. A negative result — that the two baselines are largely redundant — would simplify the design space by confirming that the per-token KL baseline captures the dominant variance source.

**Adaptive k for vOPD_top-k based on KL magnitude.** The paper's k-ablation (Figure 2) shows that even k=5 provides sufficient variance reduction at the 1.7B scale, but this result comes from a single training configuration. The optimal k likely depends on the magnitude of the full-vocabulary KL — when KL is large (student and teacher strongly disagree), more tokens may be needed to get a reasonable KL estimate; when KL is small, even k=2 might suffice. An adaptive scheme could dynamically select k at each token position based on a quick estimate of the KL magnitude (e.g., computing the KL over a small pilot set of tokens and expanding to larger k only if the pilot estimate exceeds a threshold). This would make vOPD_top-k nearly as cheap as base OPD on low-KL tokens (where little stabilization is needed) while scaling up the baseline precision on high-KL tokens (where precise damping matters). A concrete experiment would compare adaptive-k vOPD against fixed-k vOPD on a benchmark with diverse difficulty levels (such as MATH500, which has problems ranging from easy algebra to competition-level combinatorics), measuring both total wall-clock time and final accuracy. The hypothesis is that adaptive k achieves accuracy matching vOPD_full-V at wall-clock time approaching base OPD. A key measurement would be the distribution of selected k values — does the adaptive scheme actually choose larger k at positions that the paper identifies as high-mismatch? — providing a direct test of the paper's selective damping theory.

**vOPD for multi-modal and cross-lingual distillation.** All experiments are on English text reasoning benchmarks with models from the Qwen3 and Olmo-3 families. The OPD reward definition r_t = log π_T(y_t | c_t) − log π_θ(y_t | c_t) is domain-agnostic — it only requires teacher and student distributions over a shared vocabulary — so vOPD's variance reduction mechanism should transfer to any modality or language where OPD is applied. However, the KL structure (and hence the baseline's behavior) depends on the properties of the distributions. In vision-language models, the token distributions may be more peaked (the model is very confident about which visual token comes next), potentially making the single-sample estimator more stable to begin with and reducing the need for variance reduction. In cross-lingual distillation (distilling from an English-dominant teacher into a multilingual student), the student-teacher KL may be systematically larger for non-English tokens, making vOPD's damping more critical for those tokens. A concrete experiment would replicate the Table 2 setting but with a vision-language model (e.g., distilling from a post-trained LLaVA checkpoint into its base LLM, using image-grounded reasoning data) and with cross-lingual distillation (e.g., distilling Qwen3-1.7B into a multilingual variant on a mix of English and non-English reasoning problems). The key measurement would be whether vOPD's accuracy gains are uniform across modalities/languages or concentrated in subdomains with larger student-teacher distribution gaps. A finding that vOPD helps most for low-resource languages (where the teacher has weak priors and the student-teacher KL is large) would have direct practical implications for equitable deployment of distilled models.

**Stress-testing the unbiasedness claim with convergence analysis.** The paper proves that vOPD's gradient estimator is unbiased (Appendix A.1) — the expected gradient equals the true gradient of the OPD objective. This is a theoretical guarantee, but its practical consequence is that vOPD should converge to the same solution as OPD_full-V when both are trained to convergence on the same data. The paper's experiments train for a single epoch on 14K samples, which may not reach convergence, so the reported accuracies reflect a mixture of convergence speed and solution quality. A direct test of unbiasedness would be a convergence experiment: train base OPD, vOPD_top-k, and OPD_full-V on the same data for many epochs (or on a smaller dataset where overfitting is possible) and measure whether all three converge to the same training loss and the same validation accuracy. If vOPD converges to a different (better) solution than OPD_full-V, that would suggest the baseline is doing more than variance reduction — perhaps it acts as an implicit regularizer that changes the optimization landscape. If vOPD converges to the same solution but faster, that confirms the paper's framing as a pure estimator improvement. Either result is informative: the former would open new research into *why* the baseline regularizes, and the latter would cement vOPD as a drop-in speedup with no qualitative effect on the final model. The experiment should also measure gradient variance directly (by computing the empirical variance of gradient estimates across multiple rollouts from the same context) to verify the DKL² scaling predicted by Eq. 11, which the paper never directly tests.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient OPD in industrial post-training pipelines.** The paper explicitly names Qwen3, GLM-5, Nemotron-Cascade2, and DeepSeek-V4 as systems where OPD is a key component of the post-training recipe (Section 1). For teams operating these pipelines, the immediate practical implication of vOPD is a reduction in the GPU-hours required for the OPD phase of post-training. At the 4B scale, vOPD_top-k matches OPD_full-V's accuracy while reducing per-step wall-clock time by 57.7% (Figure 3). Extrapolating this to the 70B–400B models used in production, where per-step time can be hours on hundreds of GPUs, a ~50% reduction in the most expensive phase of OPD training would translate to hundreds of thousands of dollars in compute savings per training run. The implementation cost is minimal — vOPD requires adding a detached KL baseline computation to the existing OPD loss function, which is a few lines of code in standard training frameworks — so the barrier to adoption is low. The primary risk is that vOPD's benefits have not been validated at production scale with full fine-tuning, but the theoretical argument for why the advantage should scale (vocabulary-size backward pass cost grows with model size in OPD_full-V, but not in vOPD) provides a strong prior that the savings persist.

**Stabilizing OPD for smaller research teams and academic labs.** Base OPD is attractive for small-scale research because it is simple, on-policy (avoiding exposure bias), and provides dense token-level supervision, but its training instability makes it unreliable without careful hyperparameter tuning — gradient clipping thresholds, learning rate schedules, and batch size choices all interact with the gradient variance in non-obvious ways. As a result, smaller teams often avoid OPD entirely in favor of simpler off-policy distillation (which has known exposure bias issues) or RLVR (which is more stable but slower to converge). vOPD_top-k with its default k=20 provides stable OPD training out of the box, with the paper demonstrating consistent +2–4 percentage point accuracy improvements over base OPD across model families (Qwen3, Olmo-3), scales (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 7B), and domains (mathematics, science). For a PhD student or a small industry team wanting to distill a large open-weight model into a smaller one for a specific reasoning task, vOPD provides a reliable recipe that works without the hyperparameter fragility of base OPD or the GPU budget of OPD_full-V. The implementation can be done entirely within the HuggingFace TRL ecosystem (which the paper uses), making it accessible to anyone already using standard LLM training tooling.

**On-device distillation with compute-efficient training.** Distilling large models into smaller ones for on-device deployment is one of the primary use cases for OPD, as the paper notes in its motivation (Section 1: OPD "has been successful in industrial-level post-training such as Qwen3"). In this setting, the student model may be 0.5B–3B parameters (targeting mobile or edge devices), and the training budget is often constrained — not every deployment team has access to massive GPU clusters. vOPD's efficiency advantage is most impactful in this regime: at the 1.7B scale (already small by modern standards, but representative of on-device models), vOPD_top-k achieves 33.0% average accuracy on mathematical reasoning benchmarks in 30.76s per step, versus 41.12s for OPD_full-V — a 25% time savings per step (Figure 3). If training requires thousands of steps (the paper uses ~220 steps for one epoch on 14K samples at batch size 64), this translates to hours of wall-clock time saved on a single GPU. Combined with the accuracy gains over base OPD (+3.2 percentage points average), vOPD enables teams with modest compute to produce higher-quality distilled models in less time. The cross-scale result (1.7B teacher → 0.6B student, Table 5) is directly relevant to this use case, showing that vOPD's benefits persist even when the teacher is ~2.8× larger than the student — a common configuration when distilling from cloud-scale models to on-device models.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions vOPD against three named OPD variants — base OPD, OPD_full-V, and OPD_top-k — with clear tradeoffs along the axes of bias, variance, and compute cost (Table 1, Section 3.3). The decision rules emerge directly from the experimental results:

- **Prefer vOPD_top-k (the recommended default):** When you need stable OPD training with minimal computational overhead over base OPD, and you have access to a frozen teacher model. vOPD_top-k with k=20 provides accuracy essentially matching OPD_full-V (Table 2: 33.0% vs. 33.0% average at 1.7B; 45.3% vs. 45.1% at 4B) while being 25% faster per step at 1.7B and 58% faster at 4B (Figure 3). The gradient is unbiased (unlike OPD_top-k), the backward pass is single-token (unlike OPD_full-V), and the method requires no hyperparameter tuning beyond k=20 (which is robust across all tested values from 5 to 100, Figure 2). This is the best accuracy-to-compute tradeoff among all compared methods and should be the default choice for most OPD applications.

- **Prefer vOPD_full-V:** When absolute precision in the baseline estimate matters (e.g., for training with very large batch sizes where gradient noise is already low and even small residual variance could dominate), or when the additional cost of the full-vocabulary forward pass is negligible relative to other training costs. At 1.7B, vOPD_full-V and vOPD_top-k have nearly identical wall-clock time (30.57s vs. 30.76s) and accuracy (33.1% vs. 33.0%), so the choice between them is largely academic at this scale. At 4B, vOPD_top-k pulls slightly ahead in speed (32.88s vs. 33.46s), making vOPD_full-V harder to justify. The paper does not provide a regime where vOPD_full-V clearly outperforms vOPD_top-k in accuracy, so this is more of a conservative choice for practitioners who want the theoretical guarantee of the exact value baseline.

- **Prefer base OPD:** Only when implementation simplicity is paramount and training instability is tolerable — for example, quick prototyping where the training runs are short enough that occasional gradient spikes don't derail progress, or when the student-teacher KL is known to be very small (the distributions are already well-aligned, so the baseline adds negligible variance reduction while still incurring the forward-pass KL cost). Base OPD is 28% faster per step than vOPD_top-k at 1.7B (22.21s vs. 30.76s, Figure 3), but this speed advantage comes at the cost of training instability and 3 percentage points lower final accuracy (Table 2). This is a false economy in most settings — the accuracy loss outweighs the speed gain — but may be acceptable for exploratory work.

- **Avoid OPD_top-k:** The paper's results consistently show that OPD_top-k is dominated: it adds substantial compute over base OPD (39% more per-step time at 1.7B, Figure 3) while providing only marginal accuracy gains (30.2% vs. 29.8% average, Table 2), and it is simultaneously more expensive than vOPD_top-k (30.96s vs. 30.76s at 1.7B; 48.76s vs. 32.88s at 4B) while being less accurate (30.2% vs. 33.0% at 1.7B). OPD_top-k occupies a no-win region of the design space — it pays for bias without getting commensurate variance reduction — and there is no practical scenario where it is preferable over vOPD_top-k.

- **Prefer OPD_full-V:** Only if vOPD is unavailable and stability is critical. OPD_full-V remains the most stable OPD variant (zero per-context gradient variance), but its computational cost — 85% slower than base OPD at 1.7B, 210% slower at 4B (Figure 3) — makes it impractical at scale. After vOPD, the primary remaining justification for OPD_full-V is as a research baseline for measuring the variance reduction achieved by other methods, not as a practical training method.
