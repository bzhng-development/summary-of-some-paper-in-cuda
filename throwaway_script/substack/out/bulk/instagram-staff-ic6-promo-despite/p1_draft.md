Here are the takeaways from this Sash Zats interview (grew to Staff Engineer / IC6 at Meta despite 10 team switches in 9 years, originally worked on Newsfeed Delight, Instagram blockchain, and the pre-launch Threads prototype):

**First team: iOS on Newsfeed Delight**

Sash joined a team called Newsfeed Delight, whose premise was taking designers' prototypes—the ones "always stacked up in their desk but never shipped"—and just building them, "no metrics, no nothing." It was inside Facebook Newsfeed, and he laughs looking back that anyone hearing "newsfeed, no metrics" today would predict the team wouldn't survive six months. It didn't. But the value prop was pure fun: building joyful visual animations and Easter eggs. He describes his career through-line as simply not thinking about impact too well, which let him "pick the teams that are completely irrational and high risk and God knows if there are even rewards in there." The team was oriented around an internal metric called CAU ("cares about user"), a subjective survey asking whether people felt Facebook cared about them—exactly the kind of fuzzy, vibe-driven metric that fragile teams orient toward. Ryan asks what Sash was optimizing for in that choice. "Fun. You know, just pure fun." He reflects that these choices look lucky in retrospect, but they accumulate a toolbox of skills, people, and connections you can build on later. He gravitated toward the **zero-to-one space**—the ambiguous, poorly defined zones where many strong engineers are uncomfortable because there's no diagram, no spec. Coming from startups, Sash realized that comfort with ambiguity is an asset.

**What makes a good designer partner**

The best collaborations are a "constant dance" between design vision and real-world constraints—like what happens when the network fails or a data field is missing. Sash loves showing designers capabilities they might not know about: using the accelerometer to shift shadows, location to inform visuals. The tighter the iteration loop, the better. On unreleased hardware, he once built a hacky pipeline that rendered Figma designs directly onto a device so designers could move things around and see changes immediately. He also advocates starting a project by ignoring technical constraints entirely—"oh yeah, we're gonna build this crazy thing that you want"—and then trimming down to meet real-world limits like thermal envelopes or processing power. That way you begin with ambition and let constraints inform the product rather than preemptively killing ideas.

**Joining a hardware team (CTRL-Labs)**

Unable to find an appealing iOS team in New York, Sash saw a vague posting for a new hardware team and reached out. He had zero hardware or Android experience ("Android is terrifying for anyone who builds for iOS. God forbid you never go there. It's going to the dark side."). The team was building experimental hardware on top of AOSP. He describes the experience as "Alice in Wonderland—once you plunge into the rabbit's hole, you kinda go, this is really fun." The team sourced its own hardware components, with mobile engineers figuring out processors and Bluetooth chips. Sash, drawn to reverse engineering, investigated how Apple's messaging stack communicates with cars over Bluetooth using an extended protocol. He grabbed a Raspberry Pi, installed software to pretend it was a car dashboard, paired his phone, and started tracing packets—publicly documented pieces plus some reverse-engineered ones. None of this code was committed ("it's trash code and you don't need it in the repository"). The goal was to understand the **upper bound experience unbounded by any constraints**—what could they hope to achieve once productionized? This became a common theme in his career: prototype to find the ceiling first, then figure out how to productionalize.

**Two diffs in six months and surviving performance review**

That first half on the hardware team, Sash landed roughly two diffs. He explains the team was seeded with senior people and the job's premise was explicit: you'd be evaluated on **de-risking many directions, not on landing code**. This is how big tech creates incentives for risky projects—you're not guaranteed a promo, but "you're at least protected from the downside." He notes the PSC cycles evolved over time to balance frequent feedback with making people feel safe taking longer, riskier bets. The biggest difference between hardware and software teams, he says, is timelines. With software you can push continuously; with hardware, something has to go to the factory to be flashed onto the device. Deadlines are ruthless—"you cannot push them by one month, everything falls apart." You can have zero-day updates, but something has to be in the box.

**Joining the Instagram blockchain team**

Wanting to live outside the US, Sash tried to stay on the CTRL-Labs team remotely, but unreleased-hardware regulations made that impossible. He found a team in London just as Instagram was opening an office there—not just any team, but the Instagram blockchain team. ("Instagram used to have a blockchain team and for the brief but glorious period of time we were building all sorts of technologies.") Meta's earlier cryptocurrency effort had spun out, but the talent and technology remained in-house, and they began exploring decentralized directions within Instagram as a creator-forward product. They shipped a vanilla NFT product where you could upload NFTs with a "beautiful shimmering effect" and eventually create NFTs directly from Instagram. Sash quips about an internal group called "Facebook does what" where people posted screenshots of surprising features—food delivery, movie tickets—and the NFT work felt like that: a fun, crazy moment.

The more interesting piece came from a TED Talk by Adam Mosseri exploring the idea that creators shouldn't be bound to any single platform's policies—your follow-ship should exist outside any one platform, with platforms merely *recognizing* it. Sash was tasked with turning that talk into a product. He embedded deep inside the partnerships team and worked with people from Google, Spotify, Shopify, and Nike to build a decentralized creator-following protocol. The technical side was one thing; far more fascinating was the **business-incentive alignment problem**. Meta's business is ads, Spotify's is subscriptions, Shopify charges merchants—how do you create a product all of them want given completely different incentives? "As an engineer, I think you get to think about it either if you're on your own startup or if you're pretty senior. I was neither of those things and it was so fun." Then crypto winter hit and the whole effort shut down. But the insight stuck: companies more often fall apart for non-technical reasons, and there's a whole product-shaping dimension engineers rarely get involved in.

**Joining Instagram Threads pre-launch**

With the writing on the wall for crypto, Sash helped ship traditional Instagram products for a bit. Over Christmas break, a few people reached out asking if he was familiar with ActivityPub—the protocol behind Mastodon for decentralized social networking. He wasn't deeply into Mastodon, but he was curious. He spun up an internal Instagram server instance and a Mastodon instance, wrote "really simple and really horrible code" to resolve Instagram users from Mastodon, and got them talking. The lesson: **a concrete thing people can play with creates momentum** in a way that 500 decks cannot. It didn't materially de-risk anything—everyone knew it was technically possible—but it showed initiative and got people excited.

Next, he started stripping the Instagram app down to a text-only experience: removing images, blowing up caption text to be massive, hitting the internal Instagram server. You could squint and see Threads. Working with a senior backend engineer, Peter Cottle, they began chiseling out the mobile experience: a special composer for text-only posts (Instagram couldn't share a post without an image), a backend that wouldn't crash on image-less posts, a secret feature gate for employees only. Sash was the iOS engineer based in London while the rest of the team built up in New York and SF. Because their builds were separate from Instagram's main CI, they'd break constantly. He'd wake up London-time, spend the first three to four hours fixing everything so the US team could work when they woke up, then build features. The whole project went from first prototype to public launch in five or six months, driven by the window of opportunity while Musk was acquiring Twitter. The leadership-backed initiative meant "magically people would appear and they would be, let me solve this for you."

**Working with an exceptional engineer (Peter Cottle)**

Peter had deep familiarity with Instagram's codebase, knew every corner, and understood the fastest path to making posts work without images, creating new feeds, etc. Their collaboration was a "conversation with code": Peter would quickly land backend changes, Sash would test against them, and it just worked. Sash notes that **fluency with a codebase** is a superpower for moving fast—Meta's high-level primitives (privacy defaults, entity creation) make it even easier. Ryan quips that Peter was basically a copilot who doesn't hallucinate. The exchange illustrates how prototyping speed in a large codebase depends on having either deep personal knowledge or incredibly well-designed abstractions—ideally both.

**The power of visible prototyping**

Ryan observes a pattern: Sash was known for visible prototypes, so people came to him, but he also responded to outreach *with* prototypes rather than to-do-list promises. Sash agrees that responding with something concrete "shows the attitude." In a corporate world full of alignment meetings, someone taking initiative in ambiguity always stands out. He'd built a reputation partly by posting internal prototypes on Workplace—things he couldn't share externally because they used internal tech—which built a mental model among colleagues: "oh yeah, if you want someone for quick and dirty prototyping, that's the person to reach out." A prototype is the **highest-fidelity alignment tool**: text relies on both parties reconstructing the same mental model, an image is better, a video better still, and an interactive prototype leaves the least ambiguity. He's seen too many meetings where 20 minutes in, people realize they were talking about completely different products.

**AI prototyping teams and the FAQ prototype**

After Threads, Sash worked on a string of AI prototypes. His favorite: he took all of Facebook's public FAQ and help-center content about account safety, performed retrieval-augmented generation over it (GPT-3 era), and built a conversational interface where you could ask questions instead of searching—hallucination-prone but grounded in concrete data. He posted it internally and received roughly 50 reach-outs from people across the company he'd never encountered before, all recognizing that customer support was about to be transformed. The power wasn't being first—it was bridging the external world into the internal context. People in large companies "have their lives and they don't care to read Twitter every day," so bringing in pieces of what's newly possible and grounding them in internal data creates awareness and momentum inside the organization.

**Reflecting on 10 team switches and career growth**

Sash was hired as IC4 and eventually promoted to IC6, despite "my best efforts not to." He acknowledges the obvious: it's "way, way easier to level when you are in the same space"—you build connections, goodwill, and deep expertise. A friend from his first Newsfeed team stayed there nearly the entire time and progressed steadily. But Sash found that boring. Coming from startups, his mentality wasn't oriented around the PSC cycle. He chose projects for passion, not career calculus, and that's what let him work long hours sustainably. The breadth paid off differently: by spreading himself across domains and posting prototypes widely, he built a network that brought him opportunities like Threads. He also built a persona—"I have a few really good friends on infra teams and we have these ongoing jokes about the number of SEVs that I cause them"—but those same people would later come to him when they needed intel on a completely different corner of the app. Large corporate environments, he notes, disincentivize prototyping and exploration. Most people focus on measurable impact. But organizations need people willing to take risks with no particular expectation—those are the people who build resilience and avoid local maxima. Metrics run into Goodhart's law: the moment you elect a number as a metric, it stops being good because people game it; many important things aren't measurable at all, and you have to rely on taste.

**No real regrets on team switches**

Some team switches didn't pay off as much as he hoped, but he learned from all of them. Even the ones that didn't work built relationships—if you're a decent person who tries your best, you end up with a massive network. He only truly appreciated this after leaving Meta: how many people reached out offering introductions, asking what he was doing, offering to be hired into whatever he builds next. "It's definitely a really, really fun adventure and you get to meet so many cool people that it's just hard to overestimate."

**Why leave Meta**

After nine years, Sash wanted to return to the startup pace where his preferred speed of moving is "the natural pace." It makes no financial or practical sense, but he's exploring a few ideas. In a startup, "there is no meeting, there is no partner team that I can blame on the failure. You're so close to the wire that you just try it out." The risk is bounded: it's a **two-way door**, not a one-way door—you can always go back to big tech. He's in a life stage where he's lucky enough to take the risk, and it's an experiment in discovering his actual worth and whether his ideas hold up in the unbounded wild.

**Advice to younger self**

A manager once told him something from a consultancy framework that reframed his thinking: instead of fixing every shortcoming, **focus on your strengths**—make sure weaknesses don't get in your way, but position yourself where your strengths shine. "That was a relatively eye-opening sort of advice." Whatever that means in your specific case, it's probably how you'll be most successful long-term. The real impact, as a Facebook VP once wrote, comes not from taking something poor and making it passable, but from taking something great and making it excellent.
