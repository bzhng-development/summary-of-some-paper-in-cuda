# Learn Your Reference Model for Real Good Alignment

**ArXiv:** [2404.09656](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.09656)

## 🎯 Pitch

Updating the reference policy during offline alignment—rather than keeping it frozen—eliminates the overoptimization that plagues DPO, IPO, and KTO, raising Llama3 win rates by up to 15 points on Arena-Hard. This simple change makes offline methods competitive with online RLHF without reward model hacking.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Trust Region** alignment methods—TR-DPO, TR-IPO, TR-KTO—that dynamically update the reference policy during offline alignment training rather than keeping it fixed, using either soft updates (progressively blending the current policy into the reference at each step) or hard updates (periodically replacing the reference with a full copy of the current policy). Evaluated on the Anthropic-HH dialogue and Reddit TL;DR summarization datasets with Pythia models (2.8B–12B), as well as on AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard with Llama3-8B, the TR methods achieve substantial win rate improvements over their vanilla counterparts—for example, gains of 9.5, 15.1, and 2.3 percentage points on Arena-Hard for TR-DPO, TR-IPO, and TR-KTO relative to DPO, IPO, and KTO baselines, respectively. By analyzing the relationship between KL divergence from the SFT policy and human-centric quality metrics, the paper demonstrates that TR methods reduce overoptimization, maintaining higher response quality at equivalent divergence levels and establishing that the gains from relaxing the reference-policy constraint materialize only when the reference is updated to track the evolving policy rather than remaining static.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Overoptimization in Offline Alignment Without a Reward Model

The central puzzle this paper tackles is that **offline alignment methods suffer from a degradation phenomenon that, on its surface, shouldn't exist**. In traditional RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback), overoptimization has a clear mechanism: you train a reward model on human preferences, then optimize a policy against that reward model using PPO. Because the reward model is an imperfect proxy for true human preferences, optimizing too aggressively against it eventually causes the policy to exploit the reward model's blind spots — it finds responses that score highly under the learned reward but are actually low-quality according to humans. This is well-characterized by Gao et al. (2022) and Coste et al. (2024), who show that policy quality initially improves with KL divergence from the reference policy, then degrades.

But offline methods like DPO, IPO, and KTO **don't use an explicit reward model at all**. DPO reparameterizes the RLHF objective so that the policy itself implicitly represents a reward function — you train directly on preference pairs without ever training a separate reward model. KTO goes further, using a prospect-theoretic loss that only requires knowing whether a response is desirable or undesirable, not pairwise comparisons. The optimization is done entirely on a fixed dataset of pre-collected preferences, without any online sampling from the policy.

Given this, you might expect overoptimization to disappear. There's no reward model to exploit, no online feedback loop where the policy can drift into regions the reward model hasn't seen. Yet **Rafailov et al. (2024a) demonstrated that the same overoptimization pattern emerges in offline methods**: as training progresses and the policy diverges from the reference, the quality of generated responses eventually degrades. They attribute this to a different mechanism — not reward model exploitation, but the increasing probability of Out-of-Distribution (OOD) data. As the policy moves away from the reference (which is typically the SFT model trained on high-quality outputs), it begins assigning probability mass to sequences that were never seen during training, and these OOD sequences tend to be low-quality.

This is a critical practical problem because **offline alignment methods are widely used precisely to avoid the complexity of online RLHF**. DPO and its variants are simpler to implement, more stable to train, and computationally cheaper than running PPO with a reward model. If they suffer from the same degradation as online methods, the field has traded implementation complexity for a different set of pathologies without fully understanding the tradeoff.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Reference Policy Is the Wrong Anchor

The paper identifies a deeper issue lurking beneath the overoptimization problem: **the standard practice of keeping the reference policy fixed throughout training creates an inappropriate constraint**. In all offline alignment methods — DPO, IPO, KTO — the loss function includes an implicit KL divergence penalty that anchors the trained policy to the reference model. This reference is typically the SFT model (the model after supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations), and it remains frozen during alignment.

The flaw in this design is subtle but consequential. The SFT model represents the starting point of alignment — it's the model that can generate plausible responses but hasn't been shaped by human preferences. Anchoring to it makes sense early in training, when the policy hasn't learned anything about preferences and needs a regularizer to prevent catastrophic deviation. But as training progresses, the policy learns to produce better responses than the SFT model. Keeping the reference fixed means the policy is perpetually penalized for moving away from a model that, by the end of training, is **demonstrably worse** than the policy itself.

The paper articulates this tension in Section 3.1 through an analysis of gradient dynamics. Examining the DPO objective's Hessian (Equation 7), the authors show that the loss landscape flattens as training progresses. The sigmoid term $\sigma(s)$ — where $s = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}$ — approaches 0 as the policy learns to distinguish chosen from rejected responses. This vanishing curvature means that if the policy enters a phase where probabilities of in-domain (ID) data are decreasing (a precursor to overoptimization), it becomes **progressively harder to escape**, because the gradient signal weakens precisely when strong corrective updates are needed.

This suggests that the reference policy isn't just a regularizer — it's an active participant in training dynamics. If it remains static, it can trap the policy in suboptimal regions of the loss landscape. If it could be updated to track the improving policy, the optimization would be "reset" periodically, restoring curvature and allowing continued progress without the probability-mass leakage that causes overoptimization.

From a practical deployment perspective, this matters enormously. Wang et al. (2024) found that **greater divergence from the reference policy does not always worsen results** — in fact, some of the best-performing models they studied had moved quite far from their reference in KL terms. This is a direct challenge to the conventional wisdom that the KL penalty must be carefully tuned to prevent divergence. It suggests that the problem isn't divergence per se, but divergence from a **stale** reference that no longer represents a good policy. If the reference could be updated, the KL penalty would measure deviation from a moving target that better reflects the current best-known policy, potentially allowing greater exploration without the associated degradation.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three categories of prior work, each with specific limitations that the Trust Region approach addresses:

**Standard offline alignment (DPO, IPO, KTO) with fixed reference.** All three methods — DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), IPO (Azar et al., 2023), and KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) — share the same architecture: an implicit or explicit KL penalty relative to a frozen SFT-based reference model. The paper demonstrates empirically (Figure 5) that all three exhibit the same overoptimization pattern when $\beta$ is set too low: as KL divergence from the SFT policy increases, human-centric quality metrics initially improve, then plateau, then decline. The vanilla methods reach their peak HC scores at relatively low KL values and then degrade. The paper's hypothesis is that this degradation is not inevitable — it's an artifact of the reference policy becoming stale, creating a penalty that pushes in the wrong direction.

**Attempts to tune $\beta$ as a solution.** The standard response to overoptimization in offline methods is to treat $\beta$ as a hyperparameter controlling the tradeoff between alignment and divergence. By increasing $\beta$, you strengthen the KL penalty, keeping the policy closer to the reference and (hopefully) within the regime where quality is still improving. But this is fundamentally a constraint-based approach — it **limits** how much the policy can improve rather than **enabling** further improvement. The paper's analysis in Figure 5 shows that vanilla methods with higher $\beta$ (and thus lower KL divergence) don't reach the peak HC scores that TR methods achieve. Simply turning up the regularization knob trades away potential gains. The paper's key insight is that the problem isn't the magnitude of divergence — it's that the reference is the wrong thing to diverge from.

**Game-theoretic and online methods that coincidentally update the reference.** The paper notes in Section 2 that some recent approaches — specifically, Nash equilibrium-based methods like those of Rosset et al. (2024) and Munos et al. (2024) — implicitly update a reference policy during training. In Nash-MD and related algorithms, the policy is optimized against an opponent that represents the previous iteration of the policy, creating a natural "moving target" effect. However, these are **inherently online methods**: they require sampling from the policy during training to construct the game-theoretic updates. The paper explicitly states that these "cannot be directly applied to the offline setup." The Trust Region approach can be understood as bringing this reference-updating insight into the purely offline domain, where no online sampling is available.

**The absence of a mechanistic understanding.** Perhaps the most important gap the paper addresses is conceptual. Prior to this work, the overoptimization phenomenon in offline methods was observed (Rafailov et al., 2024a) but not mechanistically connected to training dynamics. The paper's Hessian analysis in Section 3.1 and Appendix A provides a specific hypothesis: vanishing curvature in the loss landscape causes the optimization to get stuck in a regime of decreasing ID probabilities, and updating the reference "resets" this curvature. This transforms overoptimization from a mysterious empirical nuisance into a phenomenon with a plausible mechanistic explanation, which in turn suggests a principled intervention (updating the reference) rather than a heuristic one (increasing $\beta$).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames updating the reference policy as a **Trust Region** approach, deliberately evoking the language of trust-region optimization methods (Schulman et al., 2017). In classical trust-region methods, you optimize a local approximation of the objective function within a bounded region around the current iterate, then update the iterate and re-center the trust region. The TR methods proposed here operate analogously: for each training step $i$, the optimization is "anchored" to reference policy $\pi^{(i-1)}_{\text{ref}}$ (the previous iterate) rather than the fixed SFT model. When the reference is updated — either continuously via soft blending or periodically via hard replacement — the trust region moves, allowing the policy to continue improving without the penalty becoming misaligned.

The paper explicitly distinguishes its contribution from simply proposing a new loss function. Rather, it presents a **modification to the training procedure** that can be applied orthogonally to DPO, IPO, or KTO. The two update strategies (soft and hard) represent different points on a spectrum: soft updates provide smooth, continuous reference tracking at the cost of requiring an additional hyperparameter $\alpha$; hard updates provide discrete, episodic resets with the hyperparameter $\tau$ controlling frequency. Both are shown to work, though with different optimal settings depending on the base method and task.

The paper also positions itself carefully relative to the broader alignment landscape. It doesn't claim to eliminate overoptimization entirely — Figure 5 shows that TR methods still eventually plateau, just at higher KL values and higher HC scores. The claim is more specific: **at any given level of KL divergence from the SFT policy, TR methods achieve higher quality than their vanilla counterparts**. This is a Pareto improvement — you get strictly more alignment benefit per unit of divergence — rather than a fundamental solution to the overoptimization problem. The distinction matters because it correctly implies that TR methods extend the "useful range" of divergence but do not make the problem disappear.

Finally, the paper connects its approach to a broader research direction: the idea that **overoptimization arises from probability mass leaking to OOD sequences**, not from imperfect reward modeling in the traditional sense. The toy MDP experiments (Figure 2, Appendix Figures 8–9) are specifically designed to isolate this mechanism. In a tiny Markov Decision Process with only 9 possible trajectories (3 of which are in-domain, 7 OOD), the paper shows that DPO and IPO cause the probability of OOD trajectories to increase over training, while TR-DPO and TR-IPO direct probability mass primarily to the chosen in-domain trajectory and reduce OOD probabilities. This is clean, controlled evidence for the hypothesis that the reference-updating mechanism works by preventing probability leakage, not by any more complex interaction. For KTO, the pattern is different — the vanilla method already increases chosen-trajectory probability in this toy setup — which the paper flags as an observation that requires further investigation but doesn't undermine the practical benefits of TR-KTO on real tasks (as shown in the subsequent experiments).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper proposes a training-procedure modification—not a new loss function—that can be applied to existing offline alignment methods (DPO, IPO, KTO) to reduce overoptimization by periodically or continuously updating the reference policy during training rather than keeping it frozen. The system solves the problem that offline alignment methods degrade in quality as they diverge from a stale reference policy, and the solution takes the form of two simple update rules (soft blending and hard replacement) that keep the reference tracking the improving policy, thereby "resetting" the optimization landscape and preventing the probability-mass leakage to out-of-distribution sequences that causes overoptimization.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three major interacting components:

1. **The Policy** (`$\pi_\theta$`): The language model being aligned, initialized from the SFT checkpoint. It generates responses and is updated via gradient descent on the chosen offline alignment loss (DPO, IPO, or KTO).

2. **The Reference Policy** (`$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`): A second copy of the model maintained in memory during training. Its role is to serve as the anchor for the implicit KL penalty in the alignment loss. Unlike standard methods where `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is frozen at the SFT checkpoint, in TR methods it is periodically or continuously overwritten with the current policy's parameters (via a stop-gradient operation), making it a moving target.

3. **The Alignment Loss (DPO/IPO/KTO)**: The standard offline loss function, which takes both `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` as inputs. The loss remains mathematically unchanged from its vanilla form—the only difference is that `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is not a constant but a time-varying quantity driven by `$\pi_\theta$`.

Information flows in a loop: at each training step, the policy `$\pi_\theta$` generates log-probabilities for the chosen and rejected responses in the preference batch; these are compared against `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`'s log-probabilities for the same sequences through the alignment loss; gradients update `$\pi_\theta$`'s parameters; and then—depending on the update strategy—`$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` either receives a weighted blend of its old parameters and `$\pi_\theta$`'s new parameters (soft update) or waits for a scheduled interval and then copies `$\pi_\theta$` entirely (hard update). The reference never receives gradient updates directly; the `$\texttt{sg}$` (stop-gradient) operation ensures that.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal motivation through Hessian analysis (Section 3.1 in the paper), which explains *why* a fixed reference causes vanishing curvature and why updating it should help—this provides the theoretical justification for the method.
- **Second**, the soft update mechanism (Equation 8), which blends parameters continuously at every step, including the hyperparameter `$\alpha$` and its effect on training dynamics.
- **Third**, the hard update mechanism (Equation 9), which replaces the reference wholesale at intervals `$\tau$`, including the design rationale for episodic versus continuous updates.
- **Fourth**, the integration of these update mechanisms into DPO, IPO, and KTO, creating TR-DPO, TR-IPO, and TR-KTO, with attention to how each base method's loss function interacts differently with a moving reference.
- **Fifth**, the toy MDP validation (Figure 2), which isolates the OOD probability-leakage mechanism and demonstrates that TR methods redirect probability mass to chosen in-domain trajectories rather than OOD ones.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methodology paper** whose core idea is that the overoptimization observed in offline alignment methods arises from the reference policy becoming a stale anchor, and that two simple parameter-update strategies—soft blending and hard replacement—can mitigate this by keeping the reference tracking the evolving policy, thereby preventing the optimization from "getting stuck" in a regime where in-domain sequence probabilities decrease and probability mass leaks to out-of-distribution sequences.

#### Why the Standard Approach Fails: Vanishing Curvature in the DPO Loss Landscape

The paper builds its motivation on an analysis of the gradient and Hessian of the DPO objective, providing a mechanistic hypothesis for why a fixed reference policy leads to overoptimization. While the DPO loss (Equation 2) superficially seems to only require that the ratio `$\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}$` exceeds `$\log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}$`, the actual optimization dynamics are more subtle.

The gradient of the DPO loss (Equation 6 in the paper) has the form:

$$\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\beta \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}\right) \nabla_\theta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)} \right]$$

where `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the logistic sigmoid function, `$\beta$` is the temperature parameter controlling the strength of the KL penalty, `$\pi_\theta$` is the policy being trained, `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is the reference policy, `$\mathcal{D}$` is the dataset of preference pairs `$(x, y_w, y_l)$`, with `$x$` being the prompt, `$y_w$` the preferred response, and `$y_l$` the dispreferred response.

**What it computes:** This is the gradient of the DPO loss with respect to the policy parameters. It has the structure of a weighted sum over training examples, where each example's contribution is scaled by `$\sigma(s)$`—a number between 0 and 1 that represents how far the current policy is from the reference in terms of the relative log-probability of chosen versus rejected responses. When `$s$` is zero (the policy and reference assign the same relative probabilities), `$\sigma(0) = 0.5$` and the gradient has full magnitude. As training progresses and the policy learns to strongly prefer chosen over rejected relative to the reference, `$s$` becomes negative and `$\sigma(s)$` approaches 0, scaling down the gradient.

**Why this form matters:** The sigmoid scaling `$\sigma(s)$` acts as an adaptive learning rate that decays as the policy improves. This is not inherently problematic—it's a natural consequence of the logistic loss—but it interacts dangerously with the Hessian.

The Hessian of the DPO loss (derived in Appendix A, Equation 11) is:

$$\nabla^2_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \sigma(s) \nabla^2_\theta s + \sigma(s)(1 - \sigma(s)) \nabla_\theta s (\nabla_\theta s)^\top \right]$$

where `$s = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}$` is the same argument to the sigmoid as in the gradient, `$\nabla^2_\theta s$` is the Hessian of `$s$` with respect to the policy parameters, and `$\nabla_\theta s$` is its gradient.

**What it computes:** This is the matrix of second derivatives of the DPO loss—it describes the curvature of the loss landscape around the current parameter setting. The Hessian has two terms: the first `$\sigma(s)\nabla^2_\theta s$` captures the curvature of the log-probability ratio `$s$` scaled by the sigmoid, and the second `$\sigma(s)(1-\sigma(s))\nabla_\theta s (\nabla_\theta s)^\top$` is a rank-1 outer product that contributes positive curvature in the direction of the gradient.

The critical observation is that **both terms contain `$\sigma(s)$` as a multiplicative factor**. As training progresses and `$s$` becomes more negative (the policy increasingly distinguishes chosen from rejected), `$\sigma(s) \to 0$`, causing the entire Hessian to approach zero. This is the vanishing curvature problem: the loss landscape becomes flat precisely when the policy is making progress, which means that:

- **If the policy enters a "bad" regime** where probabilities of in-domain (ID) data are decreasing (as observed empirically by Pal et al., 2024 and Rafailov et al., 2024a), the small gradients and flat curvature make it **difficult to escape**. The optimization lacks the "steering signal" to correct course because the loss is nearly insensitive to parameter changes.
- **The reference policy controls `$s$`**: the term `$-\beta \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}$` in `$s$` means that if `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is fixed, `$s$` will inevitably become more negative as `$\pi_\theta$` improves, driving `$\sigma(s) \to 0$`. If we could **reset `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`** to the current policy, `$s$` would jump back to zero, restoring `$\sigma(s) = 0.5$` and re-establishing curvature in the loss landscape.

**Why this analysis supports updating the reference:** This is the core theoretical justification. Updating the reference is not merely a heuristic—it is a mechanism for periodically "resetting" the optimization curvature. When we set `$\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_\theta$`, the term `$\log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}$` becomes identical to `$\log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}$`, so `$s = 0$` and `$\sigma(0) = 0.5$`. The gradient and Hessian return to full strength. This prevents the optimization from becoming trapped in the low-curvature regime where ID probabilities can monotonically decrease without corrective pressure.

In plain language: **a fixed reference causes the training signal to gradually turn itself off**. By periodically replacing the reference with the current policy, you turn the signal back on, giving the optimizer the curvature it needs to continue making meaningful updates.

#### The Soft Update Mechanism: Continuous Reference Blending

The first update strategy is the **soft update**, which blends the reference policy and the current policy at every training step. The update rule (Equation 8) is:

$$\pi_{\text{ref}} \xleftarrow{\texttt{sg}} \alpha \pi_\theta + (1 - \alpha) \pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{prev}}$$

where `$\alpha \in [0, 1]$` is a weighting factor, `$\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{prev}}$` is the reference policy's state before this update, `$\pi_\theta$` is the current policy after the gradient step, and `$\texttt{sg}$` denotes the stop-gradient operation that prevents gradients from flowing through the update into `$\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{prev}}$`.

**What it computes:** This is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the policy parameters, applied at every optimization step. The reference policy becomes a smoothed, lagged version of the training trajectory. At `$\alpha = 0$`, the reference never updates (equivalent to vanilla methods). At `$\alpha = 1$`, the reference becomes exactly the previous step's policy (equivalent to a hard update at every step). Intermediate values produce a gradual blending where the reference "chases" the policy with a speed controlled by `$\alpha$`.

**Why this form:** Parameter-space interpolation of fine-tuned models is justified by the empirical finding that fine-tuned checkpoints lie in a region where linear interpolation is meaningful (Rofin et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2023). Since both `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` are initialized from the same SFT checkpoint, they remain in the same loss basin, and their parameters can be meaningfully combined via weighted averaging. Using an EMA rather than a direct copy provides three advantages:

1. **Smoothness**: The reference changes gradually, avoiding sudden jumps that could destabilize training. The policy receives a slowly shifting anchor rather than periodic shocks.
2. **Noise reduction**: The EMA acts as a temporal filter, smoothing out high-frequency noise in the policy updates. The reference represents a more stable estimate of the "current best policy" than any individual checkpoint.
3. **Single hyperparameter**: `$\alpha$` controls the entire tradeoff between reference stability and tracking speed. Small `$\alpha$` (e.g., 0.1) keeps the reference close to the original SFT model; large `$\alpha$` (e.g., 0.8) lets it rapidly track the evolving policy.

The stop-gradient operation `$\texttt{sg}$` is crucial: it means that while `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` receives parameter values from `$\pi_\theta$`, the computational graph does not propagate gradients from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` back to `$\pi_\theta$`. The reference is updated in a purely "read-only" fashion with respect to the loss. If gradients were allowed to flow through the update, the training objective would become recurrently dependent on its own past states, creating a fundamentally different (and likely unstable) optimization problem.

**Operational mechanics:** At each training step:

1. Compute the alignment loss using `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{prev}}$`.
2. Backpropagate through `$\pi_\theta$` only, updating its parameters via the optimizer.
3. After the optimizer step, compute `$\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{new}} = \alpha \pi_\theta^{\text{new}} + (1 - \alpha) \pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{prev}}$` element-wise across all model parameters.
4. Set `$\pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{prev}} \leftarrow \pi_{\text{ref}}^{\text{new}}$` for use in the next step.

The TR variants are denoted as TR-DPO`$\alpha$`, TR-IPO`$\alpha$`, and TR-KTO`$\alpha$`, where `$\alpha$` specifies the blending weight. The paper sweeps `$\alpha$` values in `$[0.1, 0.8]$` (Table 6, Figures 4 and 11), finding that `$\alpha = 0.6$` consistently performs well across methods on the Anthropic-HH dataset with Pythia 2.8B, while `$\alpha = 0.8$` performs best on the larger Llama3 models for general benchmarks (Table 7). Values of `$\alpha$` greater than 0.9 caused training instability and were excluded from results.

#### The Hard Update Mechanism: Periodic Reference Replacement

The second update strategy is the **hard update**, which replaces the reference policy entirely with the current policy at fixed intervals. The update rule (Equation 9) is:

$$\pi_{\text{ref}} \xleftarrow{\texttt{sg}} \pi_\theta$$

executed at every `$\tau$` training steps, where `$\tau \in \mathbb{N}$` determines the frequency of replacement.

**What it computes:** This is a discrete, episodic update. For `$\tau$` consecutive steps, the reference remains frozen while the policy optimizes against it. At step `$\tau, 2\tau, 3\tau, \ldots$`, the reference is wholly replaced with the current policy, resetting `$s$` to zero and restoring full curvature to the loss landscape. Between updates, the training dynamics are identical to the vanilla method.

**Why this form:** Hard updates represent a different point on the design spectrum from soft updates. Rather than providing continuous, smooth tracking, they provide sharp, periodic resets. This design has several complementary rationales:

1. **Explicit "trust region" semantics**: Between updates, the policy optimizes within a well-defined region around the reference (the KL penalty is measured relative to a specific, unchanging anchor). When the reference is replaced, the trust region shifts to center on the new policy state, and a new optimization phase begins. This is directly analogous to classical trust-region methods in optimization, where a local model is optimized within a bounded region, then the center point is updated and the region redefined.
2. **Larger jumps may be beneficial**: Soft updates produce small, incremental reference changes. Hard updates allow the reference to make substantial jumps, potentially "skipping over" suboptimal intermediate states. If the policy has improved significantly over the `$\tau$` steps since the last update, the new reference will reflect that improvement immediately rather than gradually.
3. **Natural experiment design**: `$\tau$` provides a clean, interpretable hyperparameter—the update frequency—that directly controls how often the optimization landscape is "reset." This is easier to reason about than the continuous blending weight `$\alpha$` in some contexts.
4. **Computational simplicity**: Hard updates require no element-wise parameter blending, only a parameter copy operation. This is slightly faster than soft updates (the paper reports 2.87% vs. 3.99% training time overhead for TR-DPO hard vs. soft on Llama3, as noted in Appendix D.3).

The stop-gradient operation `$\texttt{sg}$` plays the same role as in soft updates: the reference parameters are overwritten with the values of `$\pi_\theta$` but gradients are not propagated through this operation. The reference is a "snapshot" of the policy at the moment of replacement.

**Operational mechanics:** At each training step:

1. Compute the alignment loss using `$\pi_\theta$` and the current `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`.
2. Backpropagate through `$\pi_\theta$` only.
3. If the current step number `$\text{step} \bmod \tau = 0$`: copy all parameters from `$\pi_\theta$` to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. Otherwise, leave `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` unchanged.
4. Proceed to the next step.

The TR variants are denoted as TR-DPO`$\tau$`, TR-IPO`$\tau$`, and TR-KTO`$\tau$`, where `$\tau$` specifies the update interval. The paper sweeps `$\tau$` values of `$64, 128, 256, 512, 1024$` (powers of 2, starting at `$2^6$`; note that `$2^5 = 32$` was used for Llama3 general benchmarks). On Anthropic-HH with Pythia 2.8B, `$\tau = 512$` and `$\tau = 1024$` provide statistically significant improvements (Figure 4a, Table 12). On general benchmarks with Llama3, the optimal `$\tau$` shifts to smaller values: `$\tau = 32$` performs best (Table 2), reflecting the larger model size and different training dynamics.

**Why `$\tau$` must be tuned per task and model size:** The paper explicitly notes that "the optimal hyperparameters `$\alpha$` and `$\tau$` can vary across different tasks and model sizes" (Section 4.2). This is because `$\tau$` controls the tradeoff between allowing sufficient optimization within each trust region (larger `$\tau$` gives more steps for the policy to exploit the current reference before resetting) and preventing the policy from entering the low-curvature OOD-leakage regime (smaller `$\tau$` resets more frequently, keeping `$s$` closer to zero on average). Larger models may converge faster, requiring more frequent resets (smaller `$\tau$`) to maintain curvature. The paper's ablation study (Table 6 for Pythia, Table 7 for Llama3) provides empirical guidance but no analytic formula for the optimal `$\tau$`.

#### Gradient Dynamics Under Updates: Restoring the Training Signal

Section 3.1 hypothesizes that updating the reference should "reset" the optimization process and increase the curvature of the loss landscape. Appendix J provides direct evidence for this mechanism by tracking the DPO gradient coefficient `$\sigma(s)$` during training.

**What is measured:** The sigmoid term `$\sigma(s) = \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}\right)$` is the scalar that multiplies the gradient in Equation 6. When `$\sigma(s) = 0.5$` (at `$s = 0$`), the gradient has full magnitude. As training progresses and `$s$` becomes negative, `$\sigma(s) \to 0$` and the gradient vanishes.

**For vanilla DPO (Figure 17):** The gradient coefficient `$\sigma(s)$` starts near 0.5 and monotonically decreases over 1,200 training steps, reaching approximately 0.45. This gradual decline is the vanishing curvature effect—the training signal steadily weakens as the policy diverges from the fixed reference.

**For TR-DPO`$\alpha$` with soft updates (Figure 17a):** Higher values of `$\alpha$` (0.6, 0.9) maintain the gradient coefficient closer to 0.5 throughout training. With `$\alpha = 0.9$`, `$\sigma(s)$` oscillates near 0.50-0.51, never declining substantially. With `$\alpha = 0.6$`, the decline is milder than vanilla DPO (reaching roughly 0.48 versus 0.45). With `$\alpha = 0.3$`, the pattern is similar to vanilla DPO.

**For TR-DPO`$\tau$` with hard updates (Figure 17b):** The gradient coefficient exhibits a sawtooth pattern—`$\sigma(s)$` decreases between updates, then jumps back to 0.5 each time the reference is replaced. With `$\tau = 64$` (frequent updates), `$\sigma(s)$` oscillates rapidly near 0.5. With `$\tau = 256$`, the sawtooth deepens—`$\sigma(s)$` drops further between updates but still resets. With `$\tau = 512$`, the drops between updates are larger, but the average `$\sigma(s)$` remains higher than vanilla DPO.

**Why this matters:** This empirical measurement directly confirms the theoretical hypothesis. Vanilla DPO's training signal decays monotonically. Both update strategies counteract this decay: soft updates by continuously pulling the reference toward the policy (keeping `$s$` small), hard updates by periodically resetting `$s$` to zero entirely. The gradient coefficient never reaches the critically low values where optimization would become trapped.

The paper notes (Appendix J) that after a hard update, "the real value of the gradient scale becomes 0.5, as the argument of the sigmoid becomes zero. The graph does not reflect this because smoothing of values is used for better visibility." This means the sawtooth in Figure 17b is actually even sharper than shown—the true `$\sigma(s)$` resets exactly to 0.5 at each update, but exponential smoothing in the plotting obscures the instantaneous jumps.

#### Design Rationale: Why Two Update Strategies?

The paper presents both soft and hard updates not as competing alternatives but as two instantiations of the same underlying principle—update the reference to track the policy—with different tradeoffs:

**Soft updates (TR-`$\alpha$`)**
- *Mechanism:* Continuous blending at every step.
- *Hyperparameter:* `$\alpha$` controls the blend rate.
- *Advantage:* Smooth, no sudden changes; acts as an EMA, reducing noise.
- *Disadvantage:* The reference is always a lagged, smoothed version of the policy, never exactly matching it; `$\alpha$` interacts with learning rate in potentially complex ways.
- *Analogy:* Like a momentum term in optimization—the reference is a running average.

**Hard updates (TR-`$\tau$`)**
- *Mechanism:* Discrete replacement at fixed intervals.
- *Hyperparameter:* `$\tau$` controls the update frequency.
- *Advantage:* Clean "trust region" semantics; the reference is exactly the policy at the moment of reset; simpler to reason about.
- *Disadvantage:* Can cause abrupt changes in the loss landscape; `$\tau$` must be tuned per task and model size.
- *Analogy:* Like checkpointing in reinforcement learning—the reference is a snapshot taken at regular intervals.

The paper's results suggest that both mechanisms work effectively but that the optimal choice depends on the specific setting. On task-specific datasets (Anthropic-HH, Reddit TL;DR) with Pythia models, both strategies perform comparably at their optimal hyperparameters (`$\alpha = 0.6$`, `$\tau = 512$`). On general benchmarks with Llama3, hard updates with `$\tau = 32$` and soft updates with `$\alpha = 0.8$` produce the strongest results, with TR-IPO`$\alpha$` achieving the largest gains (29.5% LC win rate vs. 14.4% for vanilla IPO on AlpacaEval 2, as shown in Table 2).

#### Integration with DPO, IPO, and KTO: Creating the TR Methods

The update mechanisms are applied identically to all three base alignment methods, producing six TR variants: TR-DPO`$\alpha$`, TR-DPO`$\tau$`, TR-IPO`$\alpha$`, TR-IPO`$\tau$`, TR-KTO`$\alpha$`, TR-KTO`$\tau$`. The base loss functions are not modified—only the reference policy input to these losses changes over time.

**TR-DPO:** Uses the standard DPO loss (Equation 2):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}\right) \right]$$

where `$\mathcal{D}$` is the dataset of preference triples `$(x, y_w, y_l)$` (prompt, chosen response, rejected response), `$\beta$` controls the divergence penalty strength, and `$\sigma$` is the logistic sigmoid.

The only difference from vanilla DPO is that `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is not a constant (the SFT model) but a variable updated via either Equation 8 or Equation 9. The paper uses `$\beta = 0.05$` for DPO and TR-DPO on task-specific datasets (selected as "optimal trade-off between HC metrics and KL divergence" per Section 4.2). For the KL-vs-quality analysis in Section 4.4, multiple `$\beta$` values are swept (0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3) to trace out the Pareto frontier.

**TR-IPO:** Uses the standard IPO loss (Equation 3):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IPO}}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \left( \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)} - \frac{1}{2\beta} \right)^2 \right]$$

where the term `$\frac{1}{2\beta}$` is a target margin that the log-ratio difference should match. IPO replaces the logistic loss of DPO with a squared-error loss, which the paper notes has different Hessian dynamics (Appendix A.2) but the same fundamental issue—the term involving `$\nabla^2_\theta t$` (where `$t = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)} - \frac{1}{2\beta}$`) diminishes as `$t \to 0$`, causing curvature reduction. The paper uses `$\beta = 0.01$` for IPO and TR-IPO on task-specific datasets.

The IPO Hessian (derived in Appendix A.2) is:

$$\nabla^2_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{IPO}} = 2 \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ t \nabla^2_\theta t + \nabla_\theta t (\nabla_\theta t)^\top \right]$$

As the policy learns to satisfy the margin `$\frac{1}{2\beta}$`, `$t$` approaches zero, and the first term `$t \nabla^2_\theta t$` vanishes. The second term `$\nabla_\theta t (\nabla_\theta t)^\top$` does not vanish, but the overall curvature decreases. Updating the reference resets `$t$` (since changing `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` changes the target), restoring curvature through the first term.

**TR-KTO:** Uses the standard KTO loss (Equation 4):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{KTO}}(\pi_\theta, \pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \lambda_w \sigma\left(z_{\text{ref}} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}\right) + \lambda_l \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} - z_{\text{ref}}\right) \right]$$

where `$z_{\text{ref}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} \right]$` is a reference-dependent baseline representing the expected log-ratio, and `$\lambda_w$` and `$\lambda_l$` are loss-aversion coefficients controlling the asymmetry between chosen and rejected responses. The paper uses `$\beta = 0.05$` for KTO and TR-KTO on task-specific datasets.

KTO differs from DPO and IPO in that it does not require paired preferences—it only needs to know whether each response is desirable (`$y_w$`) or undesirable (`$y_l$`), with the prospect-theoretic weighting handling the asymmetry. The paper notes (Appendix A) that KTO's Hessian is not derived analytically due to "the presence of nested expectations" (`$z_{\text{ref}}$` is an expectation over the policy's own distribution). The empirical results (Figure 5c, Figure 15) nonetheless show the same overoptimization pattern and the same benefit from TR updates.

**Why the loss functions remain unchanged:** This is a deliberate design choice that distinguishes the TR approach from alternative proposals. Rather than modifying the objective function (e.g., by adding new regularization terms or changing the divergence measure), the TR methods modify the **training procedure**—specifically, the value of one input to the existing loss. This means:

- TR methods inherit all theoretical properties of the base losses (e.g., DPO's implicit reward parameterization).
- Implementation requires only adding reference-update logic to the training loop, not deriving new gradients or stability conditions.
- The approach is orthogonal to most other improvements—TR updates could be combined with length normalization (Park et al., 2024), rejection sampling (Liu et al., 2024b), or other orthogonal modifications.

#### Toy MDP Validation: Isolating the OOD Probability-Leakage Mechanism

To validate the hypothesis that TR methods reduce overoptimization by preventing probability mass from leaking to out-of-distribution (OOD) sequences, the paper constructs a minimal Markov Decision Process (MDP) experiment adapted from Rafailov et al. (2024a). This is described in Section 3.2 and detailed in Appendix B.

**The MDP structure (Figure 7):** The environment is a depth-3 deterministic decision tree. The initial state (depth 0) has three possible actions: `$a_0, a_1, a_2$`. Each action leads to a new state at depth 1, which again has three actions, leading to depth-2 states. All depth-2 states transition to a single terminal state. An action is a sequence of three choices (e.g., `$(a_0, a_0, a_0)$`, `$(a_1, a_1, a_0)$`), producing exactly `$3^3 = 27$` possible trajectories, of which 7 are used as OOD (since the terminal state is reached after 3 actions, and all leaf nodes go to the same terminal state, the total trajectory count is effectively limited). The paper states: "Two trajectories used as a pair are considered In-Domain (ID), the other seven (including the one that was in the SFT, but did not form part of the pair) are Out-of-Domain (OOD)."

**Wait—let me re-read the paper's description more carefully.** Appendix B states: "we used a set of three trajectories for the SFT stage: `$(a_0, a_0, a_0)$`, `$(a_1, a_1, a_0)$`, and `$(a_2, a_2, a_2)$`. For the alignment stage, we used a set consisting of only one pair: `$(a_1, a_1, a_0) \succ (a_0, a_0, a_0)$`. Both trajectories in the pair are included in the SFT dataset." Counting: there are 27 possible trajectories (3 choices per step, 3 steps). The SFT set has 3 trajectories. The preference pair has 2 trajectories, both from the SFT set. So the ID set is those 2 trajectories—the chosen one `$(a_1, a_1, a_0)$` and the rejected one `$(a_0, a_0, a_0)$`. The third SFT trajectory `$(a_2, a_2, a_2)$` is not part of the preference data, so it is effectively OOD along with all 24 remaining unseen trajectories. The paper says "the other seven" — this appears to be an error or an oversimplification; with 3 actions, 3 steps, and all states having 3 actions, there are 27 trajectories, and removing the 2 ID ones leaves 25, not 7. The figure's simplified structure likely has fewer reachable states, but the exact count is not fully specified. The key point is: **the OOD trajectories vastly outnumber the ID trajectories**, and the question is whether the alignment process inadvertently assigns probability mass to them.

**Model architecture:** A Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) with hidden size 3 and output size 3, predicting the next action given the sequence of previous actions encoded as one-hot vectors of dimension 4 (3 actions + a "beginning of sequence" token). This is deliberately tiny to enable precise analysis of probability distributions over the full trajectory space.

**Training protocol:** The RNN is first trained on the SFT dataset for 50 epochs, then on the single preference pair using one of the alignment losses (DPO, IPO, KTO, or their TR variants) for 200 epochs. For TR methods, `$\alpha = 0.6$` (soft update) and `$\tau = 8$` (hard update) are used. The experiment is repeated with 100 different random seeds to compute mean and standard deviation of trajectory probabilities.

**What is measured (Figures 2, 8, 9):** For each method variant, the paper tracks three quantities over the 200 training steps:

- **OOD trajectory probabilities (top row):** The sum of probabilities assigned to all OOD trajectories (those not in the preference pair). This is the key metric for overoptimization—an increase here means probability mass is leaking away from the data the model should be learning from.
- **ID trajectory probabilities (bottom row):** The probabilities of the chosen trajectory `$(a_1, a_1, a_0)$` and the rejected trajectory `$(a_0, a_0, a_0)$`. Ideally, the chosen probability should increase and the rejected probability should decrease.

**Results for DPO vs. TR-DPO (Figure 2):**

- **Vanilla DPO (Figure 2a):** The OOD trajectory probability (top) **increases from approximately 0.0 to 0.4** over training, with wide variance across seeds. The ID chosen probability (bottom, blue) decreases from roughly 0.35 to 0.20, and the ID rejected probability (bottom, orange) decreases from roughly 0.35 to 0.12. Both ID probabilities decrease, but the rejected decreases faster, creating the desired preference ordering—**yet a substantial portion of the probability mass (0.4) has moved to OOD trajectories**. This is the overoptimization phenomenon: the policy learns to satisfy the pairwise preference constraint, but does so partly by dumping probability onto sequences it has never seen, which is exactly the mechanism hypothesized by Rafailov et al. (2024a).

- **TR-DPO`$\alpha$` with `$\alpha = 0.6$` (Figure 2b):** The OOD probability (top) **decreases** from roughly 0.1 to nearly 0.0. The ID chosen probability (bottom, blue) **increases** from 0.30 to 0.55, and the rejected probability (bottom, orange) decreases from 0.55 to 0.35. Not only is probability mass not leaking to OOD sequences—it is being conserved and redirected to the chosen ID trajectory.

- **TR-DPO`$\tau$` with `$\tau = 8$` (Figure 2c):** Very similar to the soft update case. OOD probability decreases to near 0; chosen probability increases substantially; rejected probability decreases. The hard update achieves essentially the same qualitative outcome as the soft update.

**Results for IPO vs. TR-IPO (Figure 8):** The pattern mirrors DPO. Vanilla IPO shows OOD probability increasing to roughly 0.3, with both ID probabilities decreasing. TR-IPO (both soft and hard) shows OOD probability decreasing and chosen ID probability increasing (from ~0.3 to ~0.5-0.6).

**Results for KTO vs. TR-KTO (Figure 9):** Here the story is different. Vanilla KTO **already shows** the desirable pattern—OOD probability remains near zero throughout training, chosen probability increases, and rejected probability decreases. The paper states: "In this toy example, we do not observe the effect of overoptimization for either KTO or TR-KTO: the probability of the chosen trajectory increases, which prevents the probability mass from spreading to OOD examples." This is an interesting observation that the paper flags but does not fully resolve—it notes that on real tasks, KTO still benefits from TR updates (Figure 5c, Table 2), suggesting that the toy MDP is too simple to capture all aspects of the overoptimization phenomenon for KTO.

**Why this experiment matters:** The toy MDP provides clean, controlled evidence for the paper's central hypothesis. It isolates the mechanism—probability mass redistribution from ID to OOD—in a setting where:

- The full state space is enumerable, so OOD probability can be measured exactly (unlike in real language models where the sequence space is astronomically large).
- The preference data is minimal (one pair), so there is no confounding from dataset size or complexity.
- The base model is tiny (RNN with hidden size 3), so training is fast and reproducible across 100 seeds, enabling statistical analysis.
- The contrast between DPO/IPO (which leak probability to OOD) and their TR variants (which don't) is stark and directly supports the theoretical motivation.

The finding that KTO behaves differently in the toy MDP but still benefits from TR on real tasks is an open puzzle that the paper does not resolve. It suggests that overoptimization in KTO may involve mechanisms beyond simple OOD probability leakage, or that the toy MDP's training dynamics don't fully capture the real-task phenomenon.

#### Practical Implementation Details

**Training hyperparameters (Appendix D, Table 3):** All experiments use consistent settings unless noted. For Pythia and Llama3 models: maximum token length of 1024 (Pythia) or 2048 (Llama3); 1 training epoch; learning rate of `$6.0 \times 10^{-6}$` for SFT and `$1.0 \times 10^{-6}$` for alignment (both baseline and TR methods); AdamW optimizer with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`; batch size 128; linear learning rate decay with 100 warmup steps; maximum gradient norm of 2; DeepSpeed ZeRO optimization; Flash Attention 2 for memory efficiency. Training runs on 8 NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, taking 3–12 hours depending on model size.

**Computational overhead (Appendix D.3):** The paper measures the additional training time introduced by TR methods on the UltraFeedback dataset (single epoch, Llama3). Relative to their vanilla counterparts:
- TR-DPO: +3.99% (soft), +2.87% (hard)
- TR-IPO: +6.93% (soft), +3.32% (hard)
- TR-KTO: +7.44% (soft), +3.65% (hard)

The soft update overhead is larger because it requires element-wise parameter blending at every step, whereas hard updates only require a copy operation at intervals. Hard updates are uniformly faster. The paper notes that "peak GPU memory usage remains comparable to standard training setups without precomputing adjustments, simplifying implementation and ensuring compatibility with existing workflows."

**Parameter initialization:** Both `$\pi_\theta$` and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` are initialized from the same SFT checkpoint. This initialization is crucial for the soft update to be meaningful—the paper cites Rofin et al. (2022) and Ilharco et al. (2023) as evidence that fine-tuned models remain in a parameter regime where linear interpolation is valid.

**Hyperparameter selection strategy:** The paper uses `$\alpha = 0.6$` and `$\tau = 512$` as default values for the task-specific experiments (Section 4.2), acknowledging that "the optimal hyperparameters `$\alpha$` and `$\tau$` can vary across different tasks and model sizes" but selecting these values "to ensure a fair comparison across methods while maintaining a consistent computational budget." An ablation study (Appendix E, Tables 6 and 7) confirms that tuning `$\alpha$` and `$\tau$` per task and model can yield further improvements, but the default values already provide statistically significant gains over baselines across the evaluated settings.

**Early stopping and checkpoint selection:** The paper trains for exactly 1 epoch on all datasets (Table 3), with linear learning rate decay throughout. No early stopping based on validation loss is applied for the alignment stage. For SFT, the same protocol is used. This is a practical simplification that avoids the complexity of per-method early-stopping criteria but means that some checkpoints may be suboptimal relative to best-validation-loss selection.

**Dataset sizes (Appendix D, Table 4):**
- Anthropic-HH: 160,800 training, 8,552 validation examples
- Reddit TL;DR SFT: 41,947 training, 11,941 validation (after deduplication)
- Reddit TL;DR Preference: 73,396 training, 21,198 validation
- UltraChat-200k: 207,865 training, 23,110 validation
- UltraFeedback: 61,135 training, 2,000 validation

The Reddit TL;DR dataset is preprocessed to remove duplicates in the SFT portion, "retaining only uniquely preferred summaries."

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Two update strategies (soft and hard) rather than one**: Provides complementary tradeoffs—soft updates offer smooth, continuous tracking for stable training, while hard updates offer clean trust-region semantics and lower computational overhead. Both are shown to work, giving practitioners flexibility.
- **Parameter-space blending for soft updates** rather than output-distribution averaging: Leverages the empirical finding that fine-tuned models permit meaningful linear interpolation in parameter space (Rofin et al., 2022; Ilharco et al., 2023), and is computationally efficient (element-wise weighted sum).
- **Stop-gradient on reference updates** rather than allowing gradient flow: Critical for stability—if `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` received gradients, the training dynamics would become recurrent, with the reference's updates depending on its own past states, creating a fundamentally different optimization problem.
- **Loss functions unchanged** rather than modified: Orthogonal improvement that composes with future advances; TR modifications can be applied to any alignment method with an implicit or explicit reference-policy constraint.
- **Single-epoch training** rather than multi-epoch with early stopping: Simplifies the experimental protocol and avoids per-method early-stopping decisions that could confound comparisons.
- **Default hyperparameters `$\alpha = 0.6$` and `$\tau = 512$`** selected as robust defaults: Not claimed to be universally optimal, but provide consistent gains across the evaluated settings, establishing a practical baseline.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Overoptimization as a Curvature-Vanishing Problem Rather Than a Regularization Problem

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is to diagnose overoptimization in offline alignment not as a problem of *insufficient regularization* (the dominant framing in prior work), but as a problem of *vanishing curvature in the loss landscape caused by a stale reference policy*. This is a genuinely novel diagnostic that shifts the solution strategy from constraining divergence (tune β higher, stay closer to the reference) to enabling further improvement (update the reference so the optimization signal doesn't decay).

Prior work — both in online methods (Gao et al., 2022; Coste et al., 2024) and in the offline analysis by Rafailov et al. (2024a) — treated overoptimization as an inevitable consequence of moving too far from the reference. The standard response was to tune β, the KL penalty coefficient, treating it as a hyperparameter that controls a tradeoff: lower β allows more divergence and potentially better alignment, but also risks overoptimization. This framing implicitly assumes that the reference policy is the correct anchor for the KL penalty throughout training, and that moving too far from it is inherently dangerous.

The TR paper identifies a different mechanism entirely. By analyzing the Hessian of the DPO loss (Equation 11, Appendix A), the authors show that the term σ(s) — which multiplies both the gradient and the curvature — approaches zero as the policy improves relative to a fixed reference. This means the optimization landscape becomes flat: gradients shrink, second derivatives vanish, and the optimizer loses the ability to correct its trajectory. If the policy happens to enter a regime where in-domain probabilities are decreasing (as empirically observed by Pal et al., 2024), it becomes "stuck" there because the signal to reverse course has faded.

The innovation is not the Hessian derivation itself — Hessians of alignment losses are well-studied — but rather the **conceptual reframing** it enables. The problem is not that the policy has diverged too far from the SFT model. The problem is that the SFT model, frozen in place, creates a loss landscape whose curvature decays over time, making the optimization progressively less responsive. The solution is therefore not to constrain the policy more tightly, but to **move the reference so that curvature is restored**. This transforms the reference policy from a fixed anchor into a controllable parameter of the optimization dynamics — something you update to maintain healthy training, not something you cling to for safety.

The strength of this reframing is that it explains a puzzle that the regularization-centric view could not: why do Wang et al. (2024) find that greater divergence from the reference does not always worsen results? Under the regularization view, more divergence should be monotonically correlated with degradation. Under the curvature view, divergence per se doesn't cause degradation — the loss of curvature does, and if you periodically restore curvature (by updating the reference), you can diverge further without paying the overoptimization price. Figure 5 provides direct evidence for this: TR methods achieve higher human-centric (HC) metric scores at the same KL divergence, and they reach peak HC at higher KL values than vanilla methods can tolerate. The Pareto frontier shifts outward.

This is a fundamental (not incremental) contribution because it changes what researchers should try to optimize. Rather than searching for better divergence constraints or more sophisticated regularizers, the curvature perspective suggests investigating how to maintain healthy training dynamics — for instance, through reference update schedules, adaptive β, or loss landscapes designed to preserve curvature.

---

### Innovation 2: The Toy MDP as a Diagnostic That Isolates OOD Probability Leakage as the Overoptimization Mechanism (for DPO and IPO)

The paper constructs a minimal Markov Decision Process experiment (Section 3.2, Appendix B) that cleanly demonstrates that overoptimization in DPO and IPO manifests as a specific, measurable phenomenon: probability mass leaks from in-domain (chosen and rejected) trajectories to out-of-distribution trajectories that were never seen during preference training. The TR modifications prevent this leakage, redirecting probability mass to the chosen trajectory instead.

Prior work by Rafailov et al. (2024a) hypothesized this mechanism based on observing decreasing probabilities of both chosen and rejected sequences in real LLM training, but they could not directly measure OOD probabilities because the full sequence space of a language model is astronomically large. The toy MDP, with its tiny state space (27 possible trajectories, 2 ID and 25 OOD), makes the hypothesis directly testable: you can compute the exact probability assigned to every trajectory at every training step.

What makes this an insight rather than just a validation experiment is the **clean negative result it enables for KTO**. Figures 8 and 9 (Appendix B) show that DPO and IPO exhibit the leakage pattern — OOD probability increases, ID probabilities decrease — while TR-DPO and TR-IPO reverse it. But KTO (Figure 9) is different: even vanilla KTO keeps OOD probability near zero and increases chosen-trajectory probability throughout training, without any TR modification. And yet, on real tasks (Figure 5c, Table 2), KTO still benefits from TR updates.

This is a diagnostic contribution, not a performance claim. The toy MDP reveals that the overoptimization mechanisms of DPO/IPO and KTO are **qualitatively different** — DPO and IPO overoptimize via OOD probability leakage (at least in this minimal setting), while KTO's overoptimization on real tasks must involve something else that the toy MDP doesn't capture. The paper does not resolve this puzzle, but identifying it is valuable: it tells future researchers that KTO needs a different overoptimization analysis than DPO/IPO, and that the OOD-leakage hypothesis (while powerful for DPO/IPO) is not a universal explanation for all offline alignment methods.

The toy MDP also serves as a conceptual tool for the field. It is simple enough to be fully understood — an RNN with 3 hidden units, 3 SFT trajectories, 1 preference pair — yet rich enough to exhibit the core pathology. This makes it replicable and extensible; other researchers can use the same setup to test new alignment methods or hypotheses about overoptimization mechanisms in a controlled environment before scaling to real LLMs.

---

### Innovation 3: The Reference Update as an Orthogonal, Training-Procedure-Level Intervention Compatible with Any Reference-Based Loss

The paper proposes that updating the reference policy during training is not a new loss function, not a new regularizer, and not an architectural change — it is a **modification to the training loop** that can be applied to DPO, IPO, KTO, and (by extension) any future alignment method that includes an implicit or explicit reference-policy constraint. This is a conceptual contribution about the *level* at which the intervention operates.

Prior work on improving offline alignment has overwhelmingly focused on the loss function. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) replaced RLHF's two-stage reward-model-plus-PPO pipeline with a single loss. IPO (Azar et al., 2023) replaced DPO's logistic loss with a squared-error formulation. KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) replaced pairwise preferences with prospect-theoretic unary signals. SimPO (Meng et al., 2024) removed the reference model entirely. RSO (Liu et al., 2024b) added statistical rejection sampling. Each of these is a change to *what objective is optimized*.

The TR approach is different in kind: it changes *how the optimization is conducted* without touching the objective function. The DPO loss (Equation 2) remains identical after the TR modification — the only difference is that π_ref is not a constant. This is an insight about modularity: the reference policy is a tunable component of the training procedure, not an immutable part of the problem definition. Just as optimization research separates the choice of objective from the choice of optimizer, alignment research can separate the choice of alignment loss from the choice of *how the reference is managed during training*.

This modularity has practical implications that constitute an insight about research methodology. Because TR updates are orthogonal to the loss function, they compose with essentially any future improvement in alignment objectives. If someone invents a better loss than DPO that still uses a reference policy, TR updates can be applied to it immediately. If someone discovers that a different divergence measure (beyond reverse KL) works better for a particular task, TR updates can wrap around it. The paper implicitly argues that reference-policy management should be a first-class design dimension in alignment method development, not an afterthought.

The two update strategies (soft and hard) further demonstrate that this dimension admits a range of design choices — continuous vs. discrete, smooth vs. episodic — each with different tradeoffs in stability, computational overhead, and hyperparameter sensitivity. The paper does not claim that one strategy is universally superior; it establishes that the dimension exists and that interventions along it produce consistent gains. This is an incremental contribution in mechanics but a fundamental one in conceptual framing.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on four datasets spanning two categories. For **task-specific** evaluation: Anthropic-HH (Bai et al., 2022), a dialogue alignment dataset with 160,800 training and 8,552 validation examples where preferred responses are selected based on helpfulness and harmlessness; and Reddit TL;DR summarization (Stiennon et al., 2020), with 41,947 training examples for SFT (after deduplication to retain only uniquely preferred summaries) and 73,396 training preference pairs. For **general-purpose** evaluation: UltraChat-200k (Ding et al., 2023a), an instruction-following dataset with 207,865 training and 23,110 validation examples used for the SFT stage; and UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023), a binarized preference dataset with 61,135 training and 2,000 validation examples used for the alignment stage. All details appear in Appendix D, Table 4.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses two model families at different scales. For task-specific experiments, **Pythia** models (Biderman et al., 2023) at 2.8B, 6.9B, and 12B parameters are used, with SFT checkpoints obtained by training on preferred texts for each dataset (following Rafailov et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024b). For general benchmarks, **Llama3** (AI@Meta, 2024) at 8B parameters is used in two settings: the Base setting, where Llama3-Base is SFT-trained on UltraChat-200k then aligned on UltraFeedback; and the Instruct setting, where Llama3-Instruct generates 8 responses per UltraFeedback prompt and the best/worst are selected by PairRM (Jiang et al., 2023) to form adaptation preference pairs (following Tunstall et al., 2023; Meng et al., 2024). The Pythia models were chosen to match prior work's evaluation protocol; Llama3 was chosen as a contemporary, capable base model.

- **Metrics.** Four evaluation frameworks are employed. **AutoSxS Evaluation**: GPT-4-0125-preview serves as a proxy judge comparing responses from two models on 500 test samples, reporting Win%, Tie%, and Lose% (prompts provided in Appendix N). **General Benchmark Evaluation**: AlpacaEval 2 (Li et al., 2023) reports length-controlled (LC) win rate and raw win rate (WR); Arena-Hard (Li et al., 2024) reports WR with 95% confidence intervals, both using GPT-4-1106-preview as judge. **Human-Centric (HC) Metrics**: coherence, factual correctness, level of detail, helpfulness, and harmlessness are scored on a 1–10 scale by GPT-4-0125-preview on a specialized subset of 150 test samples with provocative content from Anthropic-HH (prompts in Appendix N.3), with aggregate results reported via Probability of Improvement (PoI; Agarwal et al., 2021). **KL Divergence**: measured between the trained policy and the original SFT policy using the full Anthropic-HH test set (Equation 5) to quantify how far the policy has moved from its starting point. Additionally, downstream task performance is evaluated via MixEval (Ni et al., 2024) across knowledge, reasoning, and domain-specific benchmarks (Appendix F), and jailbreak robustness is assessed using GPTFuzz (Yu et al., 2023) and ReNeLLM (Ding et al., 2023b) via the EasyJailbreak framework (Appendix G).

- **Baselines.** Three standard offline alignment methods serve as baselines: **DPO** (Rafailov et al., 2023), **IPO** (Azar et al., 2023), and **KTO** (Ethayarajh et al., 2024). Each is trained with a fixed reference policy (the SFT model) and serves as the direct comparison point for its TR variant. The SFT model itself is also reported as a pre-alignment baseline. For the general benchmarks, baseline values for DPO, IPO, and KTO on Llama3 were taken from Meng et al. (2024), as the paper replicated their experimental setup exactly.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a generation budget in the standard sense (no test-time search or sampling-based evaluation is performed). Instead, computational cost is measured in **training steps** and **wall-clock time**. All models are trained for exactly 1 epoch on their respective datasets (Table 3). The computational overhead of TR methods is measured as the percentage increase in training time relative to the vanilla baseline on UltraFeedback with Llama3 (Appendix D.3): TR-DPO adds 3.99% (soft) / 2.87% (hard), TR-IPO adds 6.93% (soft) / 3.32% (hard), TR-KTO adds 7.44% (soft) / 3.65% (hard). Peak GPU memory usage is reported as comparable to standard training.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For AutoSxS comparisons on 500-sample test subsets, Fisher's exact test is applied to determine whether TR methods' win rates are statistically significantly different from the 50% baseline (Table 12). For HC metrics, Probability of Improvement (PoI; Agarwal et al., 2021) is used with confidence intervals — a method is considered statistically significantly better if its confidence interval does not cross the 0.5 probability threshold (Figures 6, 14, 16). For Arena-Hard, 95% confidence intervals are reported. Two-fold cross-validation is not used for strategy selection (unlike the reference example paper); instead, hyperparameters α and τ are selected based on a sweep on the Pythia 2.8B model (Figures 4, 6, Table 6, Appendix Figures 11–12) and then carried forward to larger models and other methods. An ablation study (Appendix E, Tables 6–7) confirms sensitivity to these choices but establishes that the selected defaults (α = 0.6, τ = 512 for task-specific; α = 0.8, τ = 32 for general benchmarks) provide consistent improvements.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Task-Specific Results: TR-DPO on Anthropic-HH and Reddit TL;DR

The headline finding from the task-specific experiments is that **TR-DPO with both soft and hard updates outperforms vanilla DPO across Pythia models at 2.8B, 6.9B, and 12B scales, on both the Anthropic-HH dialogue and Reddit TL;DR summarization tasks**, with the gains passing Fisher's exact test for statistical significance at the selected hyperparameters.

**Hyperparameter sweep on Pythia 2.8B (Figure 4, Table 12).** On the Anthropic-HH dataset with Pythia 2.8B (β = 0.05):

- **Soft updates (TR-DPOα):** Win rates over DPO range from 38.2% to 42.8% as α varies from 0.1 to 0.8 (Figure 4a). The peak is at α = 0.5 (42.8% win, 21.8% tie, 35.4% lose) and α = 0.6 (42.4% win, 25.2% tie, 32.4% lose). Both α = 0.5 (p = 0.0098) and α = 0.6 (p = 0.0007) pass Fisher's exact test at the reported significance level. Performance degrades at α = 0.8 (39.2% win, 19.2% tie, 41.6% lose), indicating that too-aggressive reference tracking can be counterproductive.

- **Hard updates (TR-DPOτ):** Win rates increase monotonically with τ from 64 (29.6% win, 19.4% tie, 51.0% lose — significantly *worse* than DPO) to 1024 (42.8% win, 22.6% tie, 34.6% lose). The strongest statistically significant results are at τ = 512 (41.8% win, 24.0% tie, 34.2% lose; p = 0.0079) and τ = 1024 (p = 0.0047). The poor performance at τ = 64 suggests that updating too frequently destabilizes training — the reference changes before the policy can make meaningful progress within each trust region.

On the Reddit TL;DR summarization dataset with Pythia 2.8B (β = 0.05, Figure 4b):

- **Soft updates:** Performance is more sensitive to α on summarization than on dialogue. α = 0.6 achieves 46.4% win, 11.8% tie, 41.8% lose (p = 0.0488, just at the significance boundary). α = 0.3 achieves 46.0% win but with p = 0.1131 (not significant). α = 0.7 drops sharply to 37.8% win, and α = 0.8 collapses to 19.0% win — the reference is updating too aggressively for this task.

- **Hard updates:** τ = 512 achieves 47.4% win, 10.6% tie, 42.0% lose (p = 0.0491), and τ = 256 achieves 42.4% win. Smaller τ values underperform: τ = 64 yields only 30.4% win. Unlike Anthropic-HH, the paper does not report τ = 1024 results for Reddit TL;DR in the AutoSxS table (Table 12 notes it as not reported).

**Scaling to larger Pythia models (Table 1).** Using the selected hyperparameters (α = 0.6, τ = 512) and optimal β values (β = 0.05 for DPO/KTO, β = 0.01 for IPO), the paper evaluates TR-DPO, TR-IPO, and TR-KTO against their vanilla counterparts on Pythia 6.9B and 12B:

- **TR-DPO:** On Anthropic-HH, win rates are 35.0% (2.8B, α = 0.6), 39.6% (2.8B, τ = 512); 35.0% (6.9B, α = 0.6), 39.6% (6.9B, τ = 512); 40.0% (12B, α = 0.6), 39.4% (12B, τ = 512). On Reddit TL;DR: 46.4%/47.4% (2.8B), 40.0%/49.4% (6.9B), 43.8%/42.0% (12B). The paper describes these as improvements using the phrase "the bolded results indicate where method with α = 0.6 or τ = 512 outperforms base method" — and indeed, all bolded win rates exceed the corresponding tie+lose percentages that would indicate parity. However, the win rates are not uniformly high: several configurations on 6.9B and 12B show only modest advantages (e.g., TR-DPO α = 0.6 on 6.9B Anthropic-HH has 35.0% win, 24.4% tie, 40.6% lose — actually losing more than winning). The paper does not apply Fisher's test to these larger-model results individually, so the statistical significance at these scales is not established.

- **TR-IPO:** On Anthropic-HH (β = 0.01): 43.2%/45.4% win for soft/hard at 2.8B; 39.0%/41.0% at 6.9B; 34.2%/36.0% at 12B. On Reddit TL;DR: 43.2%/45.8% (2.8B), 39.2%/52.5% (6.9B — the hard update at 6.9B shows the largest single gain for IPO), 39.4%/47.4% (12B). The hard update at 6.9B on Reddit TL;DR is particularly striking: 52.5% win vs. 38.2% lose, a clear and substantial margin.

- **TR-KTO:** On Anthropic-HH (β = 0.05): 37.4%/40.4% (2.8B), 36.6%/35.8% (6.9B — hard update loses more than it wins), 37.6%/40.8% (12B). On Reddit TL;DR: 46.2%/45.0% (2.8B), 47.0%/50.8% (6.9B), 41.8%/50.8% (12B). KTO shows the most variability — on Anthropic-HH at 6.9B, the hard update actually underperforms (35.8% win vs. 37.8% lose), suggesting that the default hyperparameters are not optimal for this particular combination.

**The paper reports these improvements using the bolded-values convention**, noting where TR outperforms the baseline. The phrase "we notice improvements in quality across the most evaluated scenarios with these selected hyperparameters" (Section 4.2) is accurate but understates the variability — several configurations show only marginal wins or even slight losses. This is consistent with the paper's acknowledgment that "the optimal hyperparameters α and τ can vary across different tasks and model sizes."

#### General Benchmark Results: AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard on Llama3-8B

The headline finding from the general benchmark experiments (Table 2, Figure 1) is that **TR methods consistently and substantially outperform their vanilla counterparts on both AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard across both Base and Instruct settings**, with TR-IPOα achieving the largest gains: 29.5% LC win rate vs. 14.4% for vanilla IPO on AlpacaEval 2 (Base setting), a gain of 15.1 percentage points.

**Llama3-Base setting (SFT on UltraChat, aligned on UltraFeedback, using α = 0.8 and τ = 32 for TR methods, results in Table 2):**

- **AlpacaEval 2 (LC win rate):** DPO achieves 18.2%; TR-DPOα reaches 27.3% (+9.1 points) and TR-DPOτ reaches 27.7% (+9.5 points). IPO achieves 14.4%; TR-IPOα reaches 29.5% (+15.1 points) and TR-IPOτ reaches 28.1% (+13.7 points). KTO achieves 14.2%; TR-KTOα shows only a marginal gain to 14.7% (+0.5 points) while TR-KTOτ improves to 16.5% (+2.3 points). The SFT baseline is 8.5% — all alignment methods improve substantially over SFT, and TR methods improve substantially over their vanilla counterparts except for TR-KTOα. Standard deviations for LC are 0.7–0.9 percentage points, so the gains are multiple standard deviations in magnitude. However, comparing across methods: TR-IPOα (29.5%) slightly edges out TR-DPOτ (27.7%), and both dramatically outperform all KTO variants. This ordering is non-obvious — IPO is the weakest vanilla method (14.4%) but benefits most from TR, becoming the strongest TR variant.

- **Arena-Hard (WR):** DPO achieves 15.9%; TR-DPOα reaches 21.3% (+5.4 points, 95% CI [-1.8, 2.2]) and TR-DPOτ reaches 20.2% (+4.3 points). IPO achieves 17.8%; TR-IPOα reaches 19.4% (+1.6 points) and TR-IPOτ reaches 21.1% (+3.3 points). KTO achieves 12.5%; TR-KTOα reaches 14.9% (+2.4 points) and TR-KTOτ reaches 14.3% (+1.8 points). The WR values are all lower than LC values (as expected, since Arena-Hard is a more difficult benchmark), and the confidence intervals are relatively wide (approximately ±2 points), meaning that the pairwise differences between TR variants (e.g., TR-DPOα at 21.3 vs. TR-DPOτ at 20.2) are not statistically distinguishable. The pattern of TR-IPO doing remarkably well on AlpacaEval but less dramatically on Arena-Hard is interesting and not discussed.

**Llama3-Instruct setting (off-the-shelf Llama3-Instruct as SFT, aligned on PairRM-selected best/worst from UltraFeedback, same α = 0.8 and τ = 32, results in Table 2):**

- **AlpacaEval 2 (LC):** DPO achieves 40.3%; TR-DPOα reaches 43.5% (+3.2 points) and TR-DPOτ reaches 42.8% (+2.5 points). IPO achieves 35.6%; TR-IPOα reaches 43.6% (+8.0 points) and TR-IPOτ reaches 42.6% (+7.0 points). KTO achieves 33.1%; TR-KTOα reaches 37.9% (+4.8 points) and TR-KTOτ reaches 41.3% (+8.2 points). In the Instruct setting, the baseline numbers are much higher (the Instruct model starts from a stronger SFT), and the gains are smaller in absolute terms but still substantial for IPO and KTO. TR-KTOτ shows a remarkable +8.2 point LC gain over vanilla KTO, making it the strongest KTO variant in this setting. The SFT baseline in this setting is 24.9%, so all methods provide large improvements over the starting point.

- **Arena-Hard (WR):** DPO achieves 32.6% (95% CI [-2.4, 2.3]); TR-DPOα reaches 34.7% (+2.1 points) and TR-DPOτ reaches 32.4% (-0.2 points — essentially tied with vanilla DPO within confidence intervals). IPO achieves 30.5%; TR-IPOα reaches 33.8% (+3.3 points) and TR-IPOτ reaches 34.5% (+4.0 points). KTO achieves 26.4%; TR-KTOα reaches 29.2% (+2.8 points) and TR-KTOτ reaches 30.9% (+4.5 points). The confidence intervals are again wide (~2–3 points), so many pairwise comparisons are not definitive, but the consistent pattern of TR methods exceeding their vanilla counterparts is clear.

The paper does not deeply analyze why the Base setting shows larger relative gains than the Instruct setting. A plausible explanation (not offered by the paper) is that the Base SFT model (trained only on UltraChat) starts further from optimal alignment, leaving more room for improvement, whereas the Instruct model (trained by Meta with extensive alignment) is closer to the Pareto frontier and thus sees smaller absolute gains. The fact that TR methods still improve over vanilla methods in the Instruct setting — where the baseline is already heavily aligned — is strong evidence that the benefit of reference updating is not limited to low-quality starting points.

#### Overoptimization Analysis: KL Divergence vs. Human-Centric Quality

The headline finding from the overoptimization analysis (Figure 5) is that **at any given level of KL divergence from the SFT policy, TR methods achieve higher mean Human-Centric (HC) metric scores than their vanilla counterparts, and TR methods can sustain quality improvements to substantially higher KL values before degradation sets in**. This is the paper's central evidence that TR methods reduce overoptimization — not by preventing divergence, but by making divergence more productive.

**Experimental setup.** Pythia 2.8B is trained on Anthropic-HH with DPO, IPO, and KTO at multiple β values (0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3) to produce different KL divergence levels. TR variants use α = 0.6 and τ = 512. HC metrics (coherence, correctness, level of detail, helpfulness, harmlessness) are scored by GPT-4-0125-preview on a specialized subset of 150 test samples with provocative content from Anthropic-HH, on a 1–10 scale (prompts in Appendix N.3). The mean across all five metrics is plotted against KL divergence from the SFT policy measured on the full Anthropic-HH test set.

**DPO vs. TR-DPO (Figure 5a):**

- Vanilla DPO reaches its peak mean HC score of approximately 6.90 at a KL divergence around 0.05 (β = 0.05). As KL increases further (lower β), the HC score declines: at KL ≈ 0.10 (β = 0.03), HC ≈ 6.75; at KL ≈ 0.35 (β = 0.01), HC has dropped to approximately 6.50. The curve shows the classic overoptimization pattern: initial improvement, then degradation.
- TR-DPOα (α = 0.6) achieves HC ≈ 6.92 at KL ≈ 0.08 (β = 0.05), then continues to improve: HC ≈ 7.02 at KL ≈ 0.17 (β = 0.03), and HC ≈ 7.10 at KL ≈ 0.42 (β = 0.01). The curve is monotonically increasing over the tested range — it never turns downward.
- TR-DPOτ (τ = 512) shows a similar pattern: HC ≈ 7.00 at KL ≈ 0.15 (β = 0.03) and HC ≈ 7.08 at KL ≈ 0.35 (β = 0.01).
- At the same KL values, TR methods consistently score higher. For instance, at KL ≈ 0.35, vanilla DPO has HC ≈ 6.50 while TR-DPOα has HC ≈ 7.10 — a difference of roughly 0.6 points on a 1–10 scale across five metrics.

**IPO vs. TR-IPO (Figure 5b):**

- Vanilla IPO's behavior is striking: it peaks very early at HC ≈ 6.80 near KL ≈ 0.01 (β = 0.05), then degrades rapidly. At KL ≈ 0.05 (β = 0.03), HC has fallen to 6.60; at KL ≈ 0.15 (β = 0.01), HC ≈ 6.45. IPO is the most overoptimization-prone of the three vanilla methods — its useful range of KL divergence is extremely narrow.
- TR-IPOα (α = 0.6) pushes the peak much further: HC ≈ 7.15 at KL ≈ 0.18 (β = 0.01), and HC ≈ 7.05 at KL ≈ 0.12 (β = 0.007). TR-IPOτ (τ = 512) achieves HC ≈ 7.20 at KL ≈ 0.22 (β = 0.007). The improvement is dramatic — TR-IPO achieves substantially higher peak quality than vanilla IPO, and at KL values where vanilla IPO has already degraded severely, TR-IPO is still improving.

**KTO vs. TR-KTO (Figure 5c):**

- Vanilla KTO shows a gentler overoptimization curve than DPO or IPO: it peaks at HC ≈ 6.85 near KL ≈ 0.05 (β = 0.05) and declines to HC ≈ 6.45 at KL ≈ 0.45 (β = 0.01). The degradation is slower and more monotonic.
- TR-KTOα (α = 0.6) and TR-KTOτ (τ = 512) both improve over vanilla KTO but by smaller margins than in the DPO and IPO cases. TR-KTOα achieves HC ≈ 6.95 at its peak (KL ≈ 0.25, β = 0.01); TR-KTOτ reaches HC ≈ 6.88 at its peak. The curves are consistently above vanilla KTO but the gap is narrower — roughly 0.1–0.2 HC points versus 0.5–0.6 for DPO and IPO.

**Key observations from Figure 5:**

1. The Pareto frontier shifts outward for all three methods. TR variants dominate their vanilla counterparts — they achieve higher HC at the same KL (vertical improvement) and can reach higher KL before degrading (horizontal extension).
2. IPO benefits most dramatically from TR, transforming from the most overoptimization-prone vanilla method to the highest-peak-HC TR method. This suggests that IPO's sensitivity to reference staleness is particularly acute, which is consistent with its squared-error loss having different curvature dynamics (Appendix A.2).
3. KTO benefits least from TR in the KL-vs-HC analysis, even though it shows clear gains on AutoSxS and general benchmarks. This is consistent with the toy MDP finding (Figure 9) that KTO does not exhibit OOD probability leakage in the minimal setting, suggesting its overoptimization mechanism differs from DPO/IPO's and may be less directly addressed by reference updating. The paper does not resolve this tension.

**Probability of Improvement analysis (Figure 6 for TR-DPO; Figures 14, 16 for TR-IPO and TR-KTO, discussed in Appendix I).** The PoI metric (Agarwal et al., 2021) quantifies the probability that a randomly selected HC metric score from the TR method exceeds that from the vanilla method, with confidence intervals:

- **TR-DPOα vs. DPO (Figure 6a):** PoI exceeds 0.5 (indicating TR is better) for all α values tested. The confidence intervals do not cross 0.5 for α = 0.5 (PoI ≈ 0.53), α = 0.6 (PoI ≈ 0.53), and α = 0.7 (PoI ≈ 0.52), denoting statistical significance. α = 0.1–0.4 and α = 0.8 show PoI above 0.5 but with confidence intervals crossing 0.5 — numerically better but not statistically significant.
- **TR-DPOτ vs. DPO (Figure 6b):** τ = 512 (PoI ≈ 0.55) and τ = 1024 (PoI ≈ 0.54) have confidence intervals entirely above 0.5, establishing statistical significance. τ = 64, 128, 256 show PoI above 0.5 but with intervals crossing the line.
- **TR-IPO (Figure 14, Appendix I.2):** α = 0.5, 0.6, 0.7 and τ = 256, 512, 1024 are statistically significant. The PoI values for TR-IPO are numerically higher than for TR-DPO (some approaching 0.55), consistent with IPO benefiting more from TR.
- **TR-KTO (Figure 16, Appendix I.3):** Only α = 0.6 and τ = 512 are statistically significant. This is the narrowest window of significance among the three methods, consistent with KTO showing the smallest gains in the KL-vs-HC analysis.

#### Probability Mass Analysis on Real Data

To bridge the toy MDP results (showing OOD probability leakage in DPO/IPO) and the real LLM results (showing HC improvements from TR), the paper examines the log-probabilities of chosen and rejected sequences during training of Pythia 2.8B on Anthropic-HH (Appendix C, Figure 10). This is a creative attempt to partially observe the OOD leakage phenomenon in a real setting where the full OOD space cannot be enumerated.

**Setup.** To compare methods at equivalent KL divergence from the SFT policy, β values are selected such that TR-DPO (α = 0.6, τ = 512) and vanilla DPO reach the same KL but with significantly different HC metrics (per Figure 5a): β = 0.03 for TR methods, β = 0.01 for DPO. At these settings, both methods have diverged roughly equally from the SFT model, but TR methods have higher HC quality.

**Results (Figure 10).** At each training step, the batch-averaged log-probability of chosen texts (10a) and rejected texts (10b) is plotted:

- **Chosen text probabilities (10a):** TR-DPOα and TR-DPOτ maintain chosen log-probabilities around -135 to -140 throughout training (higher values = higher probabilities). Vanilla DPO's chosen log-probabilities decrease from roughly -135 at step 0 to approximately -170 by step 1200 (lower values = lower probability). The gap widens progressively over training.
- **Rejected text probabilities (10b):** Both vanilla DPO and TR methods decrease rejected probabilities over training, but TR methods maintain higher absolute probabilities. At step 1200, vanilla DPO's rejected log-probabilities are approximately -205, while TR-DPO variants are around -185 to -195.

**Interpretation.** The paper argues that higher probabilities for both chosen and rejected texts in TR methods indicate "less redistribution of probability mass onto OOD texts" (Appendix C). Since the policy is a probability distribution, if chosen and rejected sequences (both in-domain) retain higher probability under TR methods, less probability mass has been forced onto OOD sequences — the exact opposite of what vanilla DPO does in the toy MDP. The paper explicitly notes: "even though the probabilities of rejected texts remain higher for TR methods, these methods demonstrate better performance... it is more crucial to prevent probability leakage to OOD examples during training than to minimize the probability of rejected texts." This is a subtle but important point: aggressively suppressing rejected sequences is not the sole objective — maintaining a healthy overall probability distribution that doesn't leak mass to OOD is equally or more important.

**Limitation.** This analysis is suggestive but not conclusive. The log-probability of ID sequences is only an indirect proxy for OOD probability leakage — the paper cannot directly measure OOD probabilities because the sequence space is too large. The evidence is consistent with the hypothesis but does not prove it. Alternative explanations (e.g., TR methods simply find better optima in the ID space without any OOD involvement) cannot be ruled out.

#### Diversity Analysis: HC vs. Self-BLEU

To assess whether TR methods' quality improvements come at the cost of reduced output diversity, the paper measures Self-BLEU (Zhu et al., 2018) against mean HC metrics for all three method families across β values (Appendix K, Figure 18).

**Key finding.** At the same level of Self-BLEU (i.e., same output diversity), TR methods achieve higher HC metric values than their vanilla counterparts. The curves for TR-DPO (Figure 18a), TR-IPO (Figure 18b), and TR-KTO (Figure 18c) are consistently above and to the left of their vanilla counterparts — the Pareto frontier is shifted. This demonstrates that the HC improvements from TR are not simply a consequence of the model becoming less diverse (which can artifactually increase certain metrics by making outputs more generic). The paper also notes (Appendix K) that TR-DPO "consistently produces shorter texts" than vanilla DPO, with Figure 19 showing that at comparable KL divergence, TR-DPO outputs are roughly 10–20 tokens shorter on average. This is relevant because Park et al. (2024) identified that DPO can generate overly long outputs — TR-DPO partially mitigates this length bias as a side effect.

#### Downstream Task and Jailbreak Robustness

While not central to the paper's claims, Appendices F and G provide additional evaluation dimensions:

- **Downstream tasks (MixEval, Tables 8–10):** On Llama3-Base, alignment generally improves performance on reasoning and comprehension tasks (e.g., HellaSwag improves from 32.8% to 62.3–66.6% for DPO/TR-DPO) but sometimes degrades factual knowledge (e.g., MMLU changes modestly from 66.7% SFT to 69.6–72.2% across methods). TR methods do not consistently outperform vanilla methods on these metrics — the patterns are mixed and task-dependent. On Llama3-Instruct, the already-strong baseline shows modest improvements or occasional small regressions after alignment. These results are presented transparently but are not flagged as strong evidence for TR methods' superiority on downstream task performance specifically.

- **Jailbreak robustness (Table 11):** Both vanilla and TR methods are highly vulnerable to GPTFuzz and ReNeLLM attacks, with Attack Success Rates (ASR) typically 94–100%. The paper concludes: "none of the methods show significant robustness to jailbreak attacks... robustness against jailbreak attacks is an orthogonal issue to the overoptimization problem addressed in this work."

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Soft update hyperparameter α on Pythia 2.8B Anthropic-HH (Figure 4a, Table 6).** Sweeping α ∈ [0.1, 0.8] in increments of 0.1 reveals that performance follows an inverted-U shape: win rates increase from α = 0.1 (40.0%) to a peak at α = 0.5–0.6 (42.8–42.4%), then decline at α = 0.7 (42.2%) and α = 0.8 (39.2%). Values α ≥ 0.9 caused training instability and were excluded. The optimal range of α = 0.5–0.7 is relatively broad, suggesting the method is not hypersensitive to this parameter, but the drop at α = 0.8 indicates that too-aggressive blending (the reference tracking the policy too closely) is counterproductive. For IPO (Table 6), the pattern is similar with a peak at α = 0.5–0.7 (43.2–46.4% win). For KTO, most α values ≤ 0.3 perform catastrophically (78.6–88.0% lose), with only α = 0.4–0.6 showing meaningful wins (35.8–37.4%). This is a stark finding: TR-KTO is much more sensitive to α than TR-DPO or TR-IPO, and with suboptimal α, it degrades performance substantially below the baseline.

**Hard update hyperparameter τ on Pythia 2.8B Anthropic-HH (Figure 4a, Table 6).** Sweeping τ ∈ {64, 128, 256, 512, 1024} shows monotonic improvement with increasing τ for DPO: win rates rise from 29.6% (τ = 64, actually losing more than winning) to 42.8% (τ = 1024). The poor performance at small τ supports the interpretation that updating too frequently prevents the policy from making meaningful progress within each trust region — the reference is reset before the optimization can exploit it. For IPO, τ = 128 already achieves 46.8% win, and τ = 512 achieves 45.4% — performance is less sensitive to τ once above a threshold of ~128. For KTO, only τ = 512 achieves a clear win (40.4% win vs. 33.6% lose); τ = 64 and τ = 128 perform poorly (2.6% and 30.8% win, respectively), and τ = 1024 degrades to 11.8% win. KTO's sensitivity to τ mirrors its sensitivity to α — the method requires careful tuning of the reference update parameters.

**TR hyperparameters on Llama3-8B general benchmarks (Table 7, Appendix E).** The ablation for the larger model and different task reveals that the optimal hyperparameters shift:

- **Soft updates (α):** On AlpacaEval 2 with Llama3-Base, the optimal α for TR-DPO is 0.8 (27.25% LC, vs. 18.20% baseline). For TR-IPO, α = 0.8 achieves 29.48% LC — the highest single LC score in the ablation. For TR-KTO, α = 0.6 achieves 20.70% LC, with α = 0.8 dropping to 14.66% (barely above the 14.20% baseline). The optimal α is higher for Llama3 (0.7–0.8) than for Pythia (0.5–0.6), suggesting larger models can tolerate or benefit from more aggressive reference tracking.

- **Hard updates (τ):** For Llama3, the optimal τ is much smaller than for Pythia. τ = 32 achieves 27.71% LC for TR-DPO and 28.08% for TR-IPO — dramatically better than τ = 16 (26.36%, 25.11%). As τ increases further (64, 128, 256), performance monotonically decreases back toward baseline levels. For TR-KTO, τ = 64 and τ = 128 achieve the best results (~20.3% LC), while τ = 16 performs poorly (11.78%) and τ = 256 is moderate (20.70%). The optimal τ for Llama3 (32–64) is an order of magnitude smaller than for Pythia (512), which the paper does not explicitly explain. Possible factors: Llama3 is larger and may converge faster per step, meaning it benefits from more frequent resets; the UltraFeedback dataset is larger than Anthropic-HH (61k vs. 161k training examples), so there are fewer total steps in one epoch, making each step correspond to a larger fraction of training; or the general-benchmark task requires different optimization dynamics.

**β sensitivity within TR methods (Figure 5).** By sweeping β across {0.01, 0.03, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3} and plotting HC vs. KL, the paper implicitly validates that TR methods' benefits are not an artifact of a particular β choice. For all β values, the TR curves lie above the vanilla curves (or are comparable at very low KL where both methods are close to the SFT model). This is a robustness check: the improvement is not specific to one regularization strength.

**Soft vs. hard comparison at equivalent performance (Figures 4, 6; Tables 1, 2).** Across both task-specific and general benchmarks, soft and hard updates produce comparable peak performance when their respective hyperparameters are well-tuned. For Pythia 2.8B on Anthropic-HH, the best soft (α = 0.6, 42.4% win) and best hard (τ = 1024, 42.8% win) are nearly identical. For Llama3-Base on AlpacaEval 2, the best soft (TR-DPO α = 0.8, 27.25% LC) and best hard (TR-DPO τ = 32, 27.71% LC) are also similar. This suggests that the choice between soft and hard updates is primarily a matter of convenience and hyperparameter tuning preference, not a fundamental performance tradeoff — both mechanisms successfully implement the core idea of reference updating.

**Negative result: ReST^EM revision model (from the reference example paper — not present in this TR paper).** The TR paper does not include an equivalent negative-result ablation where a more complex optimization procedure degrades performance. The closest is the observation that α ≥ 0.9 and τ ≤ 16 caused "instability in both training and generation processes" (Appendix E), which is noted but not deeply analyzed. The jailbreak results (Table 11) showing that TR methods do not improve adversarial robustness are a minor negative result — the intervention targets overoptimization, not adversarial vulnerability, and the paper is transparent about this limitation.

**Diversity-quality tradeoff not sacrificed (Figure 18).** The Self-BLEU vs. HC analysis confirms that TR methods' quality improvements do not come at the expense of diversity — at the same Self-BLEU, TR methods score higher on HC metrics. This is a non-trivial check because many alignment improvements (e.g., simply increasing β to reduce divergence) can reduce output diversity.

**Generated-text length (Figure 19).** An unexpected finding: TR-DPO produces consistently shorter outputs than vanilla DPO at comparable KL divergence, except for one anomalous configuration (TR-DPOα with β = 0.01, where "repetitive word generation" causes increased length). The paper presents this as a side benefit — DPO is known to produce overly verbose outputs (Park et al., 2024), and TR-DPO partially mitigates this without explicit length normalization.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: TR methods "effectively mitigate overoptimization, enabling models to maintain strong performance even when substantially deviating from the initial reference policy."**

This claim is the paper's central thesis and is **strongly supported** by Figure 5, which shows that TR variants of all three methods achieve higher HC quality at equivalent KL divergence and can sustain quality improvements to higher KL values before (or without) degrading. The evidence is most compelling for DPO and IPO, where the vanilla methods show clear overoptimization curves (quality rises then falls as KL increases) while TR variants show monotonic or near-monotonic improvement over the tested range. For KTO, the effect is present but weaker — the vanilla method degrades more gently, and the TR variants provide only modest improvement.

However, the claim's scope needs careful qualification. The paper shows mitigation of overoptimization as measured by **GPT-4-judged human-centric metrics on the Anthropic-HH dataset with Pythia 2.8B**. Whether the same pattern holds on other datasets, with other model families, or with actual human evaluators (rather than GPT-4 proxies) is not established. The AutoSxS and general benchmark results (Tables 1, 2) show that TR methods outperform vanilla methods, but this demonstrates **better performance**, not necessarily **reduced overoptimization** — the improvement could arise from other mechanisms (e.g., better optimization dynamics leading to a better optimum, even if overoptimization per se is not the limiting factor for vanilla methods on those tasks). The paper's causal claim — that updating the reference specifically addresses the OOD probability-leakage mechanism — is supported by the toy MDP (Figure 2) for DPO and IPO but only indirectly by the real-data log-probability analysis (Figure 10), which shows higher ID probabilities under TR but cannot directly confirm reduced OOD probability.

The phrase "substantially deviating" is well-supported: TR methods reach KL values of 0.3–0.4 from the SFT policy while maintaining or improving quality, whereas vanilla methods degrade by KL ≈ 0.1–0.2.

**Claim 2: TR methods "surpass conventional methods" on "helpful and harmless dialogue, as well as summarization."**

**Supported** for the specific experimental setup tested (Pythia models on Anthropic-HH and Reddit TL;DR, evaluated by GPT-4 AutoSxS on 500 samples). Table 1 shows that at the selected hyperparameters, TR methods generally achieve win rates above 50% (i.e., they are preferred more often than the vanilla baseline), with some configurations reaching 47–52% win rates. However, the evidence is weaker than the claim suggests in several respects:

- **Not all configurations surpass the baseline.** TR-DPO α = 0.6 on Pythia 6.9B Anthropic-HH has 35.0% win, 24.4% tie, 40.6% lose — it loses more than it wins. TR-KTO τ = 512 on Pythia 6.9B Anthropic-HH has 35.8% win, 26.4% tie, 37.8% lose — also losing. The paper's statement is accurate for the best configurations but overstates the consistency across model sizes and methods.
- **Statistical significance is only established at 2.8B.** Table 12 reports Fisher's exact test p-values for Pythia 2.8B, where α = 0.6 (p = 0.0007) and τ = 512 (p = 0.0079) on Anthropic-HH are statistically significant. For larger models (6.9B, 12B), no statistical tests are reported — the claim of improvement rests on numerical win rates without confidence quantification.
- **The 500-sample test set** is modest. With 500 comparisons, a win rate of 42.4% vs. 32.4% lose (TR-DPO α = 0.6 on Anthropic-HH 2.8B) is statistically significant (p = 0.0007), but a win rate of 39.0% vs. 37.8% lose (TR-IPO α = 0.6 on Anthropic-HH 6.9B) would not be. The paper does not report which of the Table 1 results pass significance tests, making it difficult to assess which claimed "improvements" are distinguishable from noise. The bolded-values convention ("bolded results indicate where method with α = 0.6 or τ = 512 outperforms base method") only indicates numerical superiority, not statistical reliability.

**Claim 3: "Significant improvements in general-purpose assistant setups with the Llama3 model on the AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard benchmarks."**

**Supported** for the configurations reported in Table 2. TR-DPO achieves 27.3–27.7% LC on AlpacaEval 2 vs. 18.2% for DPO (Base setting), a gain of 9+ points with standard deviations of 0.8 points — this is clearly significant. TR-IPO shows even larger gains (29.5% vs. 14.4%, +15.1 points). On Arena-Hard, the improvements are more modest (e.g., TR-DPO 21.3% WR vs. 15.9% for DPO, +5.4 points), and the confidence intervals are wide enough that some pairwise comparisons (e.g., TR-DPOα vs. TR-DPOτ) are not distinguishable.

However, there are important limitations:

- **The baseline values are taken from Meng et al. (2024), not re-measured.** The paper states "The baseline values for the base methods were taken from Meng et al. (2024), as we replicated their experimental setup." This means the comparison is between TR methods trained in this study and vanilla methods trained in a prior study. If there were any subtle differences in training infrastructure, data preprocessing, or evaluation protocol, they would confound the comparison. Reproducing the vanilla baselines within the same experimental setup would have been cleaner.
- **Only one model family (Llama3-8B) is tested.** The generalizability to other model scales (e.g., Llama3-70B) or architectures is unknown.
- **The Instruct setting results** are less dramatic — TR-DPO improves from 40.3% to 43.5% LC (+3.2 points) — and TR-DPOτ on Arena-Hard actually matches the baseline (32.4% vs. 32.6%). The claim of "significant improvements" is more qualified in this setting.
- **TR-KTO shows mixed results on AlpacaEval 2 Base.** TR-KTOα (14.7% LC) barely exceeds vanilla KTO (14.2%), and the standard deviations (0.7–0.8) mean this difference may not be significant. The claim of improvement for TR-KTO is weaker than for TR-DPO and TR-IPO.

**Claim 4: "TR alignment methods reduce overoptimization by analyzing KL divergence and Human-Centric metrics, enabling the trained models to maintain better performance even as they diverge from the initial reference policy. At the same level of KL divergence, TR methods consistently achieve higher HC metrics compared to their classical counterparts."**

**Strongly supported** by Figure 5, with the qualification that "consistently" means "across the tested β values and for the Pythia 2.8B model on Anthropic-HH." The curves are visually unambiguous for DPO and IPO — the TR variants dominate the vanilla variants in the KL-HC plane. For KTO, the dominance is present but the gap is narrow. The PoI analysis (Figures 6, 14, 16) adds statistical rigor: for the best hyperparameter settings, the probability that TR produces better HC scores than vanilla exceeds 0.5 with confidence intervals that do not cross the line, confirming significance at those settings. However, the PoI analysis is only shown for Pythia 2.8B on Anthropic-HH — for other model sizes and tasks, the KL-HC relationship is not analyzed.

**Genuine weaknesses:**

- **The Hessian-curvature hypothesis is plausible but not directly tested.** The paper's theoretical motivation (Section 3.1) argues that updating the reference prevents vanishing curvature, and Appendix J shows that the gradient coefficient σ(s) stays higher for TR methods. But this is a measurement of the *gradient scale*, not the *curvature* (Hessian). The paper does not actually compute Hessian eigenvalues or condition numbers to verify that curvature is restored. The gradient-coefficient measurement is consistent with the hypothesis but does not prove the specific mechanism — it shows that TR methods maintain a stronger training signal, which could be beneficial for many reasons beyond curvature restoration.
- **Difficulty estimation / prompt heterogeneity is ignored.** Unlike the reference example paper, which conditions its entire allocation strategy on estimated prompt difficulty, this paper treats all prompts uniformly — the same KL penalty and reference-update schedule apply to easy and hard prompts alike. It is plausible that overoptimization behavior differs by prompt difficulty (as the reference paper found for test-time compute), but the TR paper does not explore this dimension. A difficulty-conditioned TR strategy (e.g., updating the reference more aggressively for easy prompts where the SFT model is a poor anchor) could be more efficient.
- **The OOD leakage mechanism is demonstrated in a toy MDP but only indirectly probed in real models.** The real-model evidence (Figure 10, Appendix C) shows higher ID probabilities under TR, which is consistent with reduced OOD leakage but does not directly measure it. The hypothesis that overoptimization in real LLMs is caused by probability mass shifting to OOD sequences remains plausible but unproven. Alternative mechanisms — e.g., the policy learning spurious stylistic features that GPT-4 judges penalize, or the optimization getting stuck in sharp minima that generalize poorly — could also explain the HC degradation at high KL, and TR methods might help with those as well without involving OOD sequences.
- **No comparison to alternative overoptimization mitigation strategies.** The paper frames TR as a solution to overoptimization but does not compare it to other approaches that might address the same problem — e.g., dynamically scheduling β (starting high, annealing down), using ensembles of reference models, adding explicit OOD-detection penalties, or using different divergence measures (forward KL, Jensen-Shannon). Without these comparisons, it is unclear whether TR is the most effective intervention or simply one of several equally effective ones.
- **The gap between AutoSxS win rates and statistical significance is not fully bridged at larger scales.** Table 1 reports many win rates in the 35–42% range (with ties at 20–28% and losses at 32–40%), which appear modest. The paper does not report whether these specific configurations pass statistical tests, and the sample size (500) means that a 40% win rate with 25% ties and 35% losses may not be statistically distinguishable from parity. The strongest results are at 2.8B (where Fisher's test is applied), and the largest gains on general benchmarks (Table 2), where standard deviations and confidence intervals are reported and the improvements are large relative to noise.

**What would strengthen the paper:**

- **Direct measurement of OOD probability in real models:** perhaps by tracking the probability of a held-out set of sequences known to be poor (e.g., nonsensical completions, toxic outputs) during training, to see if vanilla methods increase their probability while TR methods suppress it.
- **Hessian eigenvalue analysis:** computing the top eigenvalues or condition number of the DPO Hessian at checkpoints during vanilla and TR training to directly verify the curvature-restoration hypothesis.
- **Comparison to β scheduling:** a simple baseline where β starts at 0.1 and is linearly annealed to 0.01 over training — does this achieve similar benefits without reference updating?
- **Breakdown by prompt difficulty / response quality:** does TR help more on prompts where the SFT model's responses are already good (where moving away from the reference is risky) or on prompts where the SFT model performs poorly (where divergence is necessary)?
- **Human evaluation beyond GPT-4 proxies:** while GPT-4 evaluation is standard in the field, a small-scale human study (even on 100 examples) would validate that the HC improvements measured by GPT-4 correspond to actual human preferences, especially since GPT-4 itself may have idiosyncratic preferences that interact with the alignment methods being evaluated.

**Conditional nature of the claims.** The paper's strongest claims — that TR methods reduce overoptimization and achieve higher quality at the same KL divergence — are well-supported for DPO and IPO on the specific tasks, models, and evaluation protocols tested. The claims are weaker for KTO and at larger model scales where statistical testing is absent. The generalizability to other model families, tasks, and evaluation methods (actual human judgment vs. GPT-4 proxy) remains open. The paper is transparent about most of these limitations (Section 5: Limitations and Future Work), noting the reliance on automatic evaluation and the need for future work on "generalization of our methods to other domains, modalities, and especially smaller datasets."

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Is Assumed Solved for the Toy MDP but Undefined and Unmeasured in Real LLM Experiments

The paper's central mechanistic hypothesis — that overoptimization arises from probability mass leaking to out-of-distribution (OOD) sequences and that Trust Region methods prevent this leakage — is directly validated only in the toy Markov Decision Process (MDP) experiment (Section 3.2, Appendix B, Figure 2). In the toy MDP, the full trajectory space is enumerable (27 possible sequences), so the probability assigned to OOD trajectories can be computed exactly and tracked throughout training. The results are clean: vanilla DPO and IPO increase OOD probability to ~0.3–0.4 of the total probability mass, while TR-DPO and TR-IPO direct probability to the chosen in-domain trajectory instead.

However, in real language model experiments, the sequence space is astronomically large, and the paper cannot directly measure OOD probabilities. The analysis in Appendix C (Figure 10) attempts to address this gap by tracking the log-probabilities of chosen and rejected (in-domain) sequences during training of Pythia 2.8B on Anthropic-HH. The finding — that TR methods maintain higher probabilities for both chosen and rejected texts than vanilla DPO at equivalent KL divergence — is **consistent with** reduced OOD leakage but does not directly demonstrate it. Higher ID probabilities could arise from TR methods simply finding better optima in the ID space without any difference in OOD behavior. The paper acknowledges the indirectness of this evidence only implicitly, stating that "it is unfeasible to visualize the entire OOD text space in real-world scenarios" (Appendix C) and that the results "could suggest a reduced tendency to overoptimization" (emphasis added on the hedging language).

**The consequence:** The paper's causal claim — that TR methods work specifically because they prevent OOD probability leakage — remains a plausible hypothesis supported by controlled toy experiments but unverified in real LLM training. If the mechanism in real models is different (e.g., TR methods might improve optimization dynamics in ways unrelated to OOD sequences, such as escaping sharp minima or maintaining healthier gradient signal-to-noise ratios), then the toy MDP provides only an analogy, not a proof. This matters for practitioners because understanding the mechanism affects how they should generalize: if OOD leakage is the core problem, then other interventions that explicitly penalize OOD sequences (e.g., adding an entropy bonus on in-domain data, or training a discriminator to detect and penalize OOD outputs) might complement or replace TR methods. If the mechanism is instead about curvature restoration in the loss landscape, then alternative approaches like Hessian-aware optimization or adaptive learning rates might be equally or more effective. The paper does not distinguish between these possibilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Toy MDP results (Figures 2, 8, 9) show clear OOD leakage for DPO/IPO and its prevention by TR. Real-model log-probability analysis (Figure 10, Appendix C) shows higher ID probabilities for TR but cannot directly confirm reduced OOD probability. The gradient coefficient analysis (Figure 17, Appendix J) shows TR methods maintain stronger training signals, consistent with the curvature-restoration hypothesis but also consistent with other mechanisms.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not attempt to measure OOD probabilities in real models or propose a method for doing so. It does not compare TR to alternative interventions that would target OOD leakage directly. It notes only that the toy MDP results "support our initial hypothesis on updating the reference policy" (Section 3.2). In the Limitations section (Section 5), the paper does not flag this gap — the discussion of limitations focuses on generalization to other domains, sensitivity to hyperparameters, and reliance on automatic evaluation, but not on the unverified mechanistic hypothesis. The paper's claim of having "linked reward overoptimization to gradient dynamics that decrease in-domain sequence probabilities" (Section 5) overstates the strength of the evidence — the link is demonstrated in a toy setting and is consistent with, but not confirmed by, the real-model results.

### The Hyperparameters α and τ Are Task- and Model-Size-Dependent Without a Principled Selection Method, and Suboptimal Choices Can Degrade Performance Below the Baseline

The paper introduces two update strategies, each with a hyperparameter (α for soft updates, τ for hard updates), and the experimental results demonstrate that performance is sensitive to these choices in ways that vary across tasks, model sizes, and base alignment methods. The paper explicitly acknowledges this: "the optimal hyperparameters α and τ can vary across different tasks and model sizes" (Section 4.2). However, the paper does not provide a method for selecting α or τ without running a full hyperparameter sweep — a sweep that requires training multiple models and evaluating them, which multiplies the computational cost of alignment by the number of candidate hyperparameter values.

**The consequence:** For a practitioner wanting to use TR methods, the hyperparameter sensitivity creates a significant practical burden. The ablation studies reveal several failure modes:

- **KTO is especially sensitive.** On Pythia 2.8B Anthropic-HH (Table 6), TR-KTO with α = 0.1 achieves only 11.0% win vs. 78.6% lose — dramatically worse than the vanilla KTO baseline. With τ = 64, TR-KTO achieves 2.6% win vs. 88.0% lose. For KTO, most α and τ values produce *worse* results than vanilla KTO, with only a narrow window around α = 0.6 and τ = 512 showing improvement. A practitioner who guesses the wrong hyperparameter for KTO could end up with a substantially degraded model.

- **The optimal hyperparameters shift across settings.** For Pythia 2.8B on task-specific data, the best α is 0.5–0.6 and the best τ is 512–1024 (Figures 4, 6; Table 6). For Llama3-8B on general benchmarks, the best α shifts to 0.7–0.8 and the best τ drops to 32–64 (Table 7). The paper does not explain this shift — it could be due to model size, model architecture, dataset size, task type, or interactions between these factors. Without a principle for predicting which hyperparameters will work, each new deployment requires a fresh sweep.

- **The hyperparameter sweep itself is expensive.** Evaluating 8 values of α and 5 values of τ (as the paper does in Table 6) on a single task and model size requires training 13 separate models (8 soft-update variants + 5 hard-update variants), each for a full epoch. On Llama3-8B with UltraFeedback (Table 7), this would mean training ~13 models at several hours each on 8×A100 GPUs. For a practitioner with limited compute, this sweep may cost more than the final training run, making TR methods less attractive than simply tuning β on the vanilla method.

- **Values at the extremes cause instability.** The paper reports (Appendix E) that "α = 0.9 and τ values smaller than 16 caused instability in both training and generation processes, leading to their exclusion from the tables." This means the usable range of hyperparameters is bounded, and approaching the boundary risks training collapse — a failure mode that vanilla DPO/IPO/KTO do not have.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Tables 6 and 7 (Appendix E) provide the ablation data for Pythia 2.8B on Anthropic-HH and Llama3-8B on AlpacaEval 2. Table 1 shows that the default values (α = 0.6, τ = 512), while generally effective, produce some configurations that lose more than they win (e.g., TR-DPO α = 0.6 on Pythia 6.9B Anthropic-HH has 35.0% win vs. 40.6% lose). The gradient coefficient analysis (Figure 17) provides some intuition about why different α and τ affect training dynamics differently but does not yield a selection rule.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges the sensitivity in Section 4.2 and Section 5 (Limitations), noting that "large τ values in hard updates may not scale well, indicating the need for more flexible, data-adaptive approaches." However, it proposes no solution — no heuristic for selecting α or τ, no adaptive schedule (e.g., starting with small α and increasing it), and no relationship between optimal hyperparameters and measurable quantities like model size, dataset size, or initial KL divergence. The suggestion that "tuning hyperparameters for specific tasks and model configurations can further enhance performance" (Appendix E) frames the sensitivity as an opportunity rather than a limitation, but the practical cost of that tuning is not discussed. A data-adaptive approach (e.g., triggering a hard update when the gradient coefficient σ(s) drops below a threshold) is mentioned as future work but not developed.

### All Evaluations Rely on GPT-4 as a Proxy Judge, with No Human Validation, and the Paper's Own Prompt Design May Interact with the Alignment Methods Being Evaluated

The paper's primary evaluation frameworks — AutoSxS for task-specific results (Section 4.1.4), AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard for general benchmarks (Section 4.3), and the Human-Centric (HC) metric scoring (Section 4.4) — all use GPT-4 as a proxy for human judgment. The specific judge model is GPT-4-0125-preview for AutoSxS and HC metrics, and GPT-4-1106-preview for AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard. No human evaluation is conducted, even on a small subset of examples.

The paper acknowledges this limitation directly: "Our results rely on automatic evaluation by GPT-4, raising questions about the suitability of such methods for assessing alignment techniques and whether more appropriate evaluation methods exist" (Section 5, Limitations and Future Work). This is a candid admission, but the limitation runs deeper than a generic concern about automatic evaluation — there are specific reasons it may affect the paper's claims.

**The consequence:** GPT-4 is itself an aligned model trained with RLHF (or similar techniques), meaning it has been optimized to prefer certain response characteristics — helpfulness, harmlessness, correctness, appropriate detail, etc. When GPT-4 evaluates outputs from DPO/IPO/KTO-trained models, it is judging whether the outputs satisfy **its own** learned preferences. This creates a potential circularity: both the alignment methods being evaluated and the judge model were designed to optimize similar preference dimensions. If TR methods happen to produce outputs that align more closely with GPT-4's specific preferences (e.g., a particular style of helpfulness, a particular level of detail), they may score higher without necessarily being preferred by actual human users.

The paper's HC metric evaluation (Appendix N.3) adds a further concern. The evaluation prompt explicitly instructs GPT-4 to score coherence, correctness, detail, and helpfulness **independently of ethical or safety considerations**:

> "DO NOT TAKE into account ethical, moral, and other possibly dangerous aspects of the assistant's response when assessing the scores of coherence, correctness, detail, and usefulness, even if the information might be dangerously incorrect. The score should be unbiased!"

This instruction is designed to disentangle quality from safety, but it may have unintended effects. By telling the judge to ignore harmfulness when scoring other dimensions, the prompt creates an evaluation regime that differs from how humans typically assess responses — humans do not easily compartmentalize "this is coherent and detailed but dangerous" into separate judgments. The paper reports HC metrics as a mean across all five dimensions including harmlessness, but the instruction could artificially inflate scores on dimensions like helpfulness for responses that provide detailed but unsafe information. Whether TR methods benefit from this effect relative to vanilla methods is unknown, but the evaluation protocol introduces a variable that is absent in standard human preference assessment.

Furthermore, the specific prompts used for AutoSxS and HC evaluation (Appendix N) are paper-authored and not validated against human judgments. Small differences in prompt wording can significantly affect GPT-4's preferences (as documented in the broader literature on LLM-as-judge reliability). The paper does not report any calibration of its GPT-4 evaluation against human judgments on even a small subset of the test data.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** All quantitative results in Sections 4.2, 4.3, and 4.4 are based on GPT-4 evaluation. The paper does not report any human evaluation. The HC metric prompt (Appendix N.3) includes the instruction to ignore ethical considerations for non-harmlessness dimensions, quoted above. The jailbreak evaluation (Appendix G, Table 11) uses separate evaluators (Roberta for GPTFuzz, GPT-4 for ReNeLLM) but has no bearing on the main evaluation protocol's validity.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges the limitation in one sentence in Section 5 but does not attempt to mitigate it — no human evaluation is conducted, no calibration against human judgments is reported, and no alternative evaluation framework is explored. The reliance on the same judge model (GPT-4) across all evaluation dimensions means that any systematic bias in GPT-4's preferences would affect all reported results uniformly, and the paper provides no way to detect or correct for such bias.

### The Claims of Overoptimization Reduction Are Demonstrated on a Single Model Family (Pythia) with a Single Dataset (Anthropic-HH), and the General Benchmark Results on Llama3 Test Alignment Quality but Not Overoptimization Dynamics

The paper's central empirical contribution is the demonstration that TR methods reduce overoptimization — specifically, that they achieve higher human-centric quality at equivalent KL divergence from the SFT policy and can sustain quality improvements to higher KL values. However, this demonstration is restricted to a single experimental setup: Pythia 2.8B trained on Anthropic-HH, with HC metrics scored by GPT-4 on a specialized 150-example subset, analyzed across multiple β values (Figure 5). The paper does not replicate the KL-vs-quality analysis on any other model (not even Pythia 6.9B or 12B), any other dataset, or any other model family.

**The consequence:** The paper's title claims that TR methods work "for real good alignment," and the abstract states that they "effectively mitigate overoptimization." But a skeptical reader could ask: is this a general property of TR methods, or a specific interaction between Pythia 2.8B's training dynamics and the Anthropic-HH dataset? Several factors could make this setup special:

- **Pythia models are relatively weak compared to contemporary LLMs.** Pythia 2.8B achieves far lower quality than Llama3-8B on the same tasks. A weaker base model may have more room to "leak" probability to OOD sequences because its output distribution is less concentrated on high-quality text. Stronger models may behave differently.
- **Anthropic-HH focuses specifically on helpfulness and harmlessness in dialogue.** Overoptimization patterns may differ for summarization (where the output format is more constrained), for instruction-following (where the task distribution is broader), or for code generation (where correctness is more objective).
- **The HC metric analysis uses a "specialized subset of 150 test samples with provocative content."** This is a deliberately adversarial selection — the subset is chosen to surface safety-relevant behaviors. Overoptimization may be more or less pronounced on this subset than on a random sample, and the paper does not show how the KL-HC relationship changes with sample selection.

The general benchmark results on Llama3-8B (Table 2, Figure 1) show that TR methods outperform vanilla methods on AlpacaEval 2 and Arena-Hard, but these evaluations measure **alignment quality** (via win rates against a reference model), not **overoptimization dynamics**. A method could achieve higher win rates without affecting the overoptimization curve at all — for example, if it simply found a better optimum at the same KL divergence. The paper does not plot KL-vs-quality curves for Llama3-8B, so the claim that TR methods "reduce overoptimization" on general benchmarks is unsupported — only the claim that they "improve alignment quality" is supported.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 5 (KL vs. HC) is only for Pythia 2.8B on Anthropic-HH. Table 1 extends task-specific results to Pythia 6.9B and 12B but only reports AutoSxS win rates, not KL-vs-quality analysis. Table 2 reports Llama3 results on general benchmarks but does not include KL divergence measurements or HC metric analysis. The PoI analysis (Figures 6, 14, 16) is only for Pythia 2.8B on Anthropic-HH.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. It presents the KL-HC analysis for Pythia 2.8B (Section 4.4) and the general benchmark results for Llama3 (Section 4.3) as separate validation of the method, without noting that the overoptimization claim is only directly tested in the former. The Limitations section (Section 5) mentions "generalization of our methods to other domains, modalities, and especially smaller datasets" as future work but frames this as extending the method's applicability rather than validating the central overoptimization claim across settings. A practitioner deploying TR methods on a new model or task cannot assume, based on the paper's evidence, that the overoptimization reduction observed on Pythia 2.8B + Anthropic-HH will transfer.

### The TR Method Does Not Address Jailbreak Robustness or Factual Knowledge Preservation, and in Some Cases May Marginally Degrade These Properties, Even as Alignment Quality Improves

The paper includes two appendices (F and G) that evaluate dimensions of model behavior beyond alignment quality: downstream task performance on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks (MixEval, Appendix F) and robustness to jailbreak attacks (GPTFuzz and ReNeLLM, Appendix G). These evaluations are presented transparently but reveal that TR methods do not improve — and in some cases may slightly degrade — these properties.

**Jailbreak robustness (Appendix G, Table 11):** Across both vanilla and TR methods, Attack Success Rates (ASR) are 94–100% for all configurations tested. TR-DPO, TR-IPO, and TR-KTO show ASR values that are within 1–3 percentage points of their vanilla counterparts, with no consistent direction of improvement. The paper states: "none of the methods show significant robustness to jailbreak attacks... robustness against jailbreak attacks is an orthogonal issue to the overoptimization problem addressed in this work." This is an honest admission, but it has practical consequences: a practitioner deploying TR-DPO instead of DPO to improve alignment quality will not get any additional defense against adversarial prompts, and if the TR model diverges further from the SFT policy (as Figure 5 shows it does), it might even become slightly more vulnerable in ways the current evaluation does not detect (the paper tests only two attack methods on one model configuration).

**Downstream task performance (Appendix F, Tables 8–10):** The MixEval results on Llama3-Base show that alignment generally improves reasoning and comprehension tasks (e.g., HellaSwag improves from 32.8% SFT to 62.3–66.6% for DPO/TR-DPO) but sometimes degrades factual knowledge tasks. On MMLU, the SFT baseline achieves 66.7%; DPO achieves 69.6% (+2.9 points) while TR-DPOα achieves 70.5% (+3.8 points) and TR-DPOτ achieves 69.9% (+3.2 points). These are marginal differences within the noise of the benchmark. On GPQA, the SFT baseline achieves 25.0%; most alignment methods including TR variants maintain 25.0%, but KTO-based methods drop to 0.0%. On WinoGrande, the pattern reverses: SFT achieves 50.0%; DPO and IPO maintain 50.0%, but TR-DPO variants drop to 25.0%. These fluctuations are not flagged or discussed in the main text, and the paper does not analyze whether they represent meaningful degradation or statistical noise. The sample sizes for these benchmarks are not reported in the paper, making it impossible to assess whether a drop from 50.0% to 25.0% on WinoGrande is significant or within expected variance.

**The consequence:** These results suggest a potential tradeoff that the paper does not explore: TR methods may improve alignment quality (as measured by AutoSxS, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard) while having neutral or slightly negative effects on factual knowledge retention and adversarial robustness. For a practitioner, this means that choosing TR-DPO over DPO is not a strict Pareto improvement — it trades off small potential degradations in some dimensions for clear gains in alignment. Whether this tradeoff is worthwhile depends on the application: a customer-facing chatbot may prioritize alignment quality over trivia accuracy, while a research assistant may need to preserve factual knowledge.

The paper's framing of these results as evidence that "the effect of preference optimization varies across tasks" (Appendix F) understates the concern. The more specific worry is that **the mechanism by which TR methods achieve better alignment — allowing the policy to diverge further from the SFT model — might inherently risk forgetting or distorting knowledge that was present in the SFT model but is not reinforced by the preference data**. The SFT model was trained on demonstrations that cover a broader distribution than the preference pairs used for alignment; moving further from the SFT model could erode capabilities that are not represented in the alignment data. The paper does not investigate this hypothesis.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Appendix F (Tables 8–10) provides the downstream task numbers. Appendix G (Table 11) provides jailbreak ASR. The paper notes in both appendices that the effects are mixed and that jailbreak robustness is orthogonal, but it does not analyze whether the degradations (WinoGrande from 50% to 25% for TR-DPO, GPQA from 25% to 0% for KTO variants) are statistically significant or practically meaningful given benchmark variance.

**Mitigation status:** The paper acknowledges the jailbreak limitation explicitly ("robustness against jailbreak attacks is an orthogonal issue") and suggests future work on "separate, dedicated strategies" for adversarial robustness. It does not acknowledge the potential knowledge-forgetting tradeoff. The downstream task evaluation is presented as supplementary information without analysis of whether the observed degradations are real or noise. The paper does not propose or test any mitigation for knowledge loss — e.g., mixing SFT data into the alignment stage, using a smaller TR update rate to stay closer to the SFT model, or applying elastic weight consolidation.

### The Computational Overhead of Reference Updates Is Modest in Relative Terms but Multiplies When Hyperparameter Sweeps Are Required, and the Paper Does Not Account for This Total Cost

The paper reports that TR methods add 2.87–7.44% training time overhead relative to vanilla methods, depending on the base method and update strategy (Appendix D.3). On a single training run, this is modest — training that takes 10 hours with vanilla DPO takes approximately 10.3–10.7 hours with TR-DPO. However, this measurement only captures the cost of one training run with fixed hyperparameters. The practical cost of deploying TR methods includes the hyperparameter sweep needed to find good α or τ values, which multiplies the training cost by the number of candidate values tested.

**The consequence:** Consider a practitioner who wants to use TR-DPO on a new dataset and model. Based on the paper's results, they cannot assume that α = 0.6 and τ = 512 (the defaults from Pythia + Anthropic-HH) will be optimal — the optimal α shifted from 0.6 to 0.8 when moving from Pythia to Llama3, and the optimal τ shifted from 512 to 32. To find good hyperparameters, the practitioner must sweep at least a few values of α (the paper tests 8) and τ (the paper tests 5), for each base method (DPO, IPO, or KTO). That is 13 training runs for a single method on a single task, or ~26 runs if both soft and hard updates are evaluated. At ~3–12 hours per run on 8×A100 GPUs (the paper's reported training times, Appendix D.1), the sweep alone costs 78–312 GPU-hours for one method on one task.

This cost is not accounted for anywhere in the paper. The headline result — "TR-DPO improves win rate by 9.5 points on Arena-Hard" — is achieved after sweeping 8 α values and 5 τ values (plus baseline training) on Llama3-8B. A practitioner who trains one model with α = 0.6 and gets suboptimal performance has not "failed to use TR correctly" — they have failed to invest the necessary hyperparameter optimization budget. The paper does not provide a cheaper way to select hyperparameters (no heuristic, no transfer learning from smaller models, no online adaptation).

Furthermore, the hyperparameter sensitivity interacts with dataset size. The paper trains for exactly 1 epoch on all datasets (Table 3). For smaller datasets, the total number of training steps is smaller, and the optimal τ (measured in steps) may need to be smaller to achieve the same number of updates per effective "training duration." The paper notes that "large τ values in hard updates may not scale well, indicating the need for more flexible, data-adaptive approaches" (Section 5), implicitly acknowledging that τ is tied to dataset size, but provides no guidance on how to adjust it.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The training time overhead percentages are reported in Appendix D.3 for a single Llama3-8B training run on UltraFeedback. The ablation Tables 6 and 7 show the number of hyperparameter values tested. The paper does not report total compute used for all experiments (including sweeps and baselines), which is standard practice in many empirical ML papers and would help practitioners estimate the full cost of adopting TR methods.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not acknowledge the hyperparameter sweep cost as a limitation. It presents the overhead as "moderate" (Appendix D.3) in the context of a single run. The suggestion for future work on "more flexible, data-adaptive approaches" (Section 5) gestures toward reducing the reliance on fixed α or τ, but no concrete direction is proposed that would reduce the sweep burden. A practitioner reading the paper could easily underestimate the total compute required to achieve the reported gains by overlooking the implicit cost of hyperparameter selection embedded in the experimental design.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **conceptual reframing** rather than a paradigm shift: it repositions the reference policy in offline alignment from a fixed anchor — an immutable component of the problem definition — to a controllable parameter of the training dynamics. The magnitude of this shift is modest but genuine. The field had largely settled on a model where the SFT checkpoint serves as the permanent reference, with β tuned to manage the divergence tradeoff. This paper argues that this model is unnecessarily restrictive, and that updating the reference is not merely a heuristic trick but addresses a specific, diagnosable pathology: vanishing curvature in the loss landscape as the policy improves relative to a stale reference.

The reframing matters because it **redirects research attention from loss-function design to training-procedure design**. Prior work on offline alignment overwhelmingly focused on improving the objective function — DPO replaced RLHF's two-stage pipeline, IPO replaced the logistic loss with squared error, KTO introduced prospect-theoretic weighting, SimPO removed the reference entirely. Each of these is a change to *what* is optimized. The TR approach identifies a different lever: *how* the optimization is conducted, through the management of the reference policy over time. This is a complementary axis of improvement that composes with future advances in loss-function design. If someone invents a better alignment loss than DPO that still uses a reference, TR updates can be applied to it immediately.

The work also **reconciles a seemingly contradictory finding** in the literature. Wang et al. (2024) observed that greater divergence from the reference policy does not always worsen results — a finding that is puzzling under the standard regularization view, where more divergence should monotonically increase overoptimization risk. The TR framework explains this: divergence per se is not harmful; divergence from a *stale* reference is harmful because it causes the optimization signal to decay. If the reference tracks the policy, divergence is continually "reset," and the policy can move further from the SFT model without entering the low-curvature regime where overoptimization takes hold. Figure 5 provides direct evidence: TR methods reach higher KL values than vanilla methods (0.3–0.4 vs. 0.05–0.15) while maintaining or improving quality. This transforms the divergence penalty from a constraint to be respected into a dynamic that can be managed.

The paper also provides **a new diagnostic tool** for the field: the toy MDP experiment (Figure 2, Appendix B) that isolates OOD probability leakage as the overoptimization mechanism for DPO and IPO. This minimal testbed — an RNN with 3 hidden units, 3 SFT trajectories, 1 preference pair — is simple enough to be fully understood and replicated, yet rich enough to exhibit the core pathology. It enables precise measurement of OOD probabilities that is impossible in real LLMs. The finding that KTO does not exhibit OOD leakage in this toy setup (Figure 9) even though it still benefits from TR on real tasks (Figure 5c) is an unresolved puzzle that the field should investigate — it suggests that overoptimization mechanisms differ across alignment methods, and that the OOD-leakage hypothesis (while powerful for DPO/IPO) is not universal.

Perhaps most consequentially, the paper **elevates reference-policy management to a first-class design dimension**. Before this work, a practitioner implementing DPO would set π_ref to the SFT model and never think about it again. After this work, the choice of whether and how to update π_ref becomes a design decision alongside the choice of loss function, β value, and training dataset. The paper shows that this dimension matters: the difference between vanilla IPO (14.4% LC on AlpacaEval 2) and TR-IPOα (29.5% LC, Table 2) is 15.1 percentage points — over a 2× improvement — from changing nothing except whether the reference updates. This magnitude of gain from a non-loss-function change suggests that training-procedure interventions have been underexplored relative to objective-function innovations, and the TR results should encourage more work in this direction.

A research direction that becomes **less attractive** is the search for ever-smaller β values as the primary mechanism for improving alignment. The paper shows (Figure 5) that simply lowering β on vanilla methods causes quality to degrade after a relatively low threshold (KL ≈ 0.05–0.10 for DPO, even lower for IPO). The TR approach demonstrates that the problem is not the magnitude of divergence but the reference's staleness, making "tune β lower" a fundamentally limited strategy. Future work on improving alignment through divergence should focus on making divergence productive (by updating the reference) rather than simply allowing more of it.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Adaptive reference-update schedules triggered by loss curvature.** The paper's Hessian analysis (Section 3.1, Appendix A) argues that updating the reference restores curvature by resetting σ(s) to 0.5. But the current methods use fixed schedules — α for continuous blending, τ for periodic replacement — that require per-task hyperparameter sweeps. A natural extension is to make the update decision data-dependent: monitor σ(s) (or a direct estimate of Hessian curvature, such as the trace or top eigenvalue) during training, and trigger a reference update when curvature drops below a threshold. This would eliminate the need to tune α or τ, automatically adapting the update frequency to the training dynamics of each model-task combination. A concrete experiment: train TR-DPO with a curvature-triggered update rule (update when σ(s) < 0.3, for instance) on the same Pythia 2.8B + Anthropic-HH setup as Figure 5, and compare the KL-vs-HC curve against the best fixed-α and fixed-τ configurations. If the adaptive schedule matches or exceeds the performance of the best hand-tuned hyperparameter, it would provide both a practical improvement and stronger evidence for the curvature hypothesis.

**Combining TR with β scheduling to disentangle divergence magnitude from divergence productivity.** The paper shows that TR methods enable productive divergence to higher KL values (Figure 5), but it does not explore whether the *path* of divergence matters independently of the *endpoint*. A natural experiment: compare two training runs that reach the same final KL divergence from the SFT model, one using vanilla DPO with a fixed low β and one using TR-DPO with a higher β that is gradually annealed. If the TR run achieves higher HC quality at the same KL, it would demonstrate that the productivity of divergence depends on the training dynamics (reference updating, β scheduling) and not just the final distance from the SFT model. This would strengthen the paper's central claim that overoptimization is a training-dynamics problem rather than a regularization problem. The paper already has the infrastructure to run this experiment — it trains models at multiple β values for Figure 5 — and would only need to add a β-annealing schedule (e.g., linear decay from β = 0.1 to β = 0.01) to the existing protocol.

**Direct measurement of OOD probability leakage in real LLMs via held-out negative sequences.** The paper's mechanistic hypothesis — that overoptimization arises from probability mass leaking to OOD sequences — is validated in the toy MDP but only indirectly probed in real models (Figure 10, Appendix C). A direct test would construct a held-out set of sequences that are known to be poor (e.g., nonsensical completions, toxic outputs, off-topic responses) and track their average log-probability under the policy during vanilla and TR training. If vanilla DPO increases the probability of these negative sequences as training progresses (and as KL diverges), while TR-DPO decreases or maintains their probability, it would provide direct evidence for the OOD-leakage mechanism at scale. The negative-sequence set could be constructed from: (a) the rejected responses in the Anthropic-HH test set, (b) synthetically generated low-quality completions (e.g., using a weaker model's outputs), or (c) adversarially constructed prompts designed to elicit degenerate behavior. A strong follow-up would measure OOD probability on multiple negative-sequence sets to ensure the result is not an artifact of a particular construction, and would correlate OOD probability with HC metric degradation to establish a causal relationship.

**Extending TR to online alignment methods and comparing against Nash-MD approaches.** The paper notes (Section 2) that game-theoretic methods like Nash-MD (Munos et al., 2024) and Direct Nash Optimization (Rosset et al., 2024) implicitly update a reference policy during training but are inherently online — they require sampling from the policy. The TR approach brings reference-updating into the purely offline domain. A natural next step is to compare TR methods against these online approaches in a controlled setting: train on the same preference data, but allow the online methods to sample additional responses from the policy during training while restricting TR methods to the fixed offline dataset. The question is whether offline TR methods can approach the performance of online reference-updating methods without the additional sampling cost, or whether online sampling provides benefits beyond reference updating that offline methods cannot replicate. The Anthropic-HH and UltraFeedback datasets would support this comparison, since they provide both a fixed preference dataset (for offline training) and prompts that could be used for online sampling. A strong result would show that TR-DPO with hard updates (τ tuned per task) matches or exceeds Nash-MD on HC metrics while using only the fixed dataset, demonstrating that reference updating captures much of the benefit of online game-theoretic training without its computational cost.

**Stress-testing TR on tasks where the SFT model is already near-optimal.** The paper evaluates TR on tasks where the SFT baseline is far from optimal — Anthropic-HH dialogue quality improves substantially with alignment, Llama3-Base SFT achieves only 8.5% LC on AlpacaEval 2. The theoretical motivation (Section 3.1) suggests that TR methods should be *most* beneficial when the SFT model is a poor reference (because the curvature vanishes more quickly as the policy rapidly diverges), and *least* beneficial when the SFT model is already strong (because the policy doesn't need to diverge much). This predicts that on tasks where the SFT model achieves near-ceiling performance, TR might provide negligible gains or even hurt by allowing unnecessary divergence. A negative-result stress test would validate this prediction: take a heavily instruction-tuned model (e.g., Llama3-Instruct, which achieves 24.9% LC before any alignment on this paper's setup) and apply TR-DPO with the same protocol. If the gains are substantially smaller than on Llama3-Base (as Table 2 already suggests — TR-DPO improves from 40.3% to 43.5% LC in the Instruct setting vs. 18.2% to 27.3% in the Base setting), and if the KL-vs-HC curve shows minimal or zero benefit from TR, it would confirm that TR's value is conditional on the reference model being a genuine bottleneck. This would help practitioners decide when TR is worth the additional complexity.

**Investigating the KTO anomaly: why does vanilla KTO not exhibit OOD leakage in the toy MDP, yet still benefit from TR on real tasks?** The paper's toy MDP results reveal a genuine puzzle: DPO and IPO leak probability mass to OOD sequences in the minimal setting, and TR prevents this leakage. But KTO (Figure 9) already keeps OOD probability near zero and increases chosen-trajectory probability without any TR modification — vanilla KTO looks "optimal" in the toy setup. Yet on real tasks (Figure 5c, Table 2), KTO still benefits from TR updates, albeit less dramatically than DPO or IPO. This suggests that KTO's overoptimization mechanism in real LLMs is qualitatively different from DPO/IPO's, and is not captured by the toy MDP. A targeted investigation would: (a) scale up the toy MDP (more trajectories, more preference pairs, more complex dependencies) until vanilla KTO begins to exhibit overoptimization, to identify what property of real training triggers it; (b) analyze the KTO loss's gradient and Hessian (which the paper notes is "difficult" due to nested expectations in `z_ref`, Appendix A) to understand why KTO's curvature dynamics differ; and (c) test whether TR-KTO's benefits on real tasks are mediated by the same OOD-leakage mechanism (by tracking negative-sequence probabilities, as proposed above) or by a different mechanism entirely. Resolving this puzzle would deepen the field's understanding of overoptimization beyond the DPO/IPO case and potentially lead to KTO-specific interventions that are more effective than generic reference updating.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient alignment for organizations with limited compute budgets.** The paper's results provide a concrete recipe for improving alignment quality without increasing model size or training data volume. On Llama3-Base, applying TR-DPO with soft updates (α = 0.8, a single additional hyperparameter to tune) improves AlpacaEval 2 LC win rate from 18.2% to 27.3% — a ~50% relative improvement — at a computational overhead of only 3.99% training time (Appendix D.3). For a team that has already invested in pretraining or obtaining a base model, this is an unusually favorable cost-benefit ratio: less than 4% more training compute for 9+ percentage points of alignment quality. The method requires no additional data, no model architecture changes, and no online sampling infrastructure. The primary practical cost is the hyperparameter sweep to find good α or τ, but the paper's ablation studies (Tables 6, 7) provide reasonable starting points: α = 0.6–0.8 and τ = 32–512, with the larger values for smaller models and the smaller values for larger models. A practitioner could test 3–4 values of α and 3–4 values of τ (6–8 total runs) to find a configuration that substantially outperforms the vanilla baseline, making the total cost modest relative to the pretraining investment.

**Improving open-source instruction-tuned models with minimal additional training.** The Llama3-Instruct results (Table 2) demonstrate that TR methods can improve already-aligned models without starting from scratch. On top of the off-the-shelf Llama3-Instruct model, TR-DPOα improves AlpacaEval 2 LC from 40.3% to 43.5% (+3.2 points) and TR-IPOα improves from 35.6% to 43.6% (+8.0 points). These gains are achieved by: (a) generating 8 responses per UltraFeedback prompt from the Instruct model itself, (b) selecting the best and worst using PairRM, and (c) running TR alignment on these self-generated preference pairs. This is a lightweight, self-contained pipeline: no external preference data is needed, no reward model training is required, and the alignment stage trains for only 1 epoch on ~61k examples. For teams maintaining open-source model deployments (e.g., fine-tuned Llama variants for specific applications), TR offers a way to push alignment quality further without the complexity of online RLHF or the cost of collecting new human preference data.

**Alignment of smaller models for on-device or edge deployment.** The paper's Pythia results (Table 1) show that TR methods improve alignment at scales from 2.8B to 12B parameters, with some of the largest relative gains at the smallest scale. For applications where model size is constrained — on-device assistants, privacy-preserving local inference, embedded systems — getting the most alignment quality out of a small model is critical. TR-DPO on Pythia 2.8B achieves 42.4% win rate over vanilla DPO on Anthropic-HH (p = 0.0007, Table 12), and TR-IPO achieves 43.2–45.4% win. These improvements come without increasing the model footprint, inference latency, or memory requirements — the trained model is identical in architecture and size to the vanilla baseline, only the training procedure differs. The computational overhead (3–7% training time) is absorbed during the one-time alignment stage and does not affect deployment.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate a detailed decision rule comparing TR against named alternatives at the method level (e.g., "prefer TR-DPO over SimPO when..."). The contribution is positioned as an orthogonal modification to the training procedure that composes with the choice of base alignment loss (DPO, IPO, KTO), not as a competitor to those losses. The evidence supports the following practical guidance, grounded in the paper's ablation results:

**Prefer TR over the vanilla version of the same method (DPO, IPO, KTO) when:**
- You are training on a task where the SFT model is far from optimal and substantial divergence is expected (the Base setting in the paper, where SFT achieves only 8.5% LC on AlpacaEval 2, is a clear case).
- You have the computational budget for a modest hyperparameter sweep (3–5 values each of α or τ) to find task-appropriate settings — the paper shows that poorly-chosen hyperparameters can underperform the baseline, especially for KTO (Table 6).
- Your evaluation metric is alignment quality (win rates, human-centric metrics) rather than factual knowledge retention or adversarial robustness, where TR shows neutral or mixed effects (Appendices F, G).

**Be cautious about TR when:**
- The base alignment method is KTO — the hyperparameter sensitivity is high (Table 6: α = 0.1–0.3 causes 75–79% lose rates vs. vanilla KTO), and the quality gains are smaller than for DPO/IPO in the KL-HC analysis (Figure 5c).
- The SFT model is already strongly aligned (the Instruct setting in Table 2, where gains shrink to 2–3 LC points) — the additional divergence enabled by TR may not translate to meaningful quality improvements.
- Compute for hyperparameter sweeps is severely limited — a single training run with the wrong α or τ can produce a model worse than the vanilla baseline (e.g., TR-DPO α = 0.8 on Pythia 2.8B Reddit TL;DR achieves only 19.0% win, Figure 4b), so guessing is risky.
- You need to preserve specific factual knowledge from the SFT model — the paper's downstream task evaluation (Appendix F, Tables 8–10) shows occasional degradations (e.g., WinoGrande from 50% to 25% for TR-DPO variants) that are not well-understood and not addressed by the method.
