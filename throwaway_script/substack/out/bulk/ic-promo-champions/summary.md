Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (Staff+ engineer at Meta, author of The Peterman Post newsletter, on the hidden role of champions in IC promotions):

**How the promotion packet works behind the scenes**

When your manager believes you're close to a promotion, they compile a packet using the organization's standard template. One often-overlooked section is dedicated to **champion quotes**—written endorsements from higher-level ICs who have context on your work. The manager reaches out to those senior engineers and asks a specific question: "Are you supportive of Ryan's promotion to IC6? If so, could you share a 2-3 sentence statement of your support for his promotion?" The more senior and the more relevant that champion is to the work being evaluated, the more credibility their quote carries when the packet lands in calibration. Ryan notes that at some companies, these people are called "Sponsors" rather than champions, but the function is the same.

**What champions actually do in the calibration room**

Beyond written quotes, champions can have real influence during the calibration discussion itself. If people around the table raise questions about the technical difficulty or the quality of your implementation, those champions are "the first to speak up and provide context." Ryan is careful not to oversell this: "don't think it'll cause a promotion to happen that shouldn't." A weak packet won't get carried by a strong champion. But if your packet is on the fence, their input "can give your work the credibility it needs to go through." The dynamic he sees most often: managers aren't close enough to the code and the implementation details to have a strong independent opinion, so they "usually defer to the senior ICs" when evaluating technical difficulty and quality. That deference turns champions into a tiebreaker function—not the main event, but the edge that tips a borderline case. The most common pattern he's observed is senior ICs backing up the technical difficulty and implementation quality of the work, areas where managers simply lack the depth to push back.

**Leaving it to chance vs. derisking intentionally**

Ryan didn't know any of this when he was going up for his own promotions. "I was lucky to work with high-level engineers by chance, so champions were easy for my manager to find." In fact, when he was first promoted to Staff, his manager told him he had a few people to thank—and he was appreciative but didn't actually know what role they had played. It's "not something that people talk about," so he only learned the mechanics later in his career. But he's now explicit: you don't need to leave it to chance. Three deliberate moves he recommends: (1) **Seek out projects with higher-level engineers**—if you do excellent work alongside them, "you will learn a ton and earn yourself some champions" as a natural byproduct. (2) **Find a mentor who is at the next level**—if you're struggling to identify someone, ask your manager to help pair you. (3) **Share who your champions are with your manager** ahead of time—this gives them a head start when writing the packet; "the more context your manager has, the smoother the process will be." The closing note is characteristically measured: many people get promoted to Staff without ever learning this, "so you don't need to focus too much on this." It's not a required lever—just "something to be mindful of and plan for with your manager." Champions turn out to be "another good reason to work with engineers who are stronger than you are."

---

His **champion quotes** framing and the "don't think it'll cause a promotion that shouldn't" caveat both reflect the same underlying instinct: surface the hidden machinery of IC promotions without over-rotating on any single lever. The tiebreaker function he describes—managers deferring to senior ICs on technical depth—gives teeth to his quieter advice that **seeking out projects with higher-level engineers** is as much about earning credible voices in the room as it is about learning. His own story (being told he had people to thank, and not knowing why) is the origin point for the whole piece: a pattern he only named in retrospect, then turned into a derisking playbook for others.
