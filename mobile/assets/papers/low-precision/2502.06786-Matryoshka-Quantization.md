## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Matryoshka Quantization (MatQuant)**, a multi-scale training technique that jointly optimizes a single model across multiple integer precision levels—int8, int4, and int2—by leveraging the nested (Matryoshka) structure of integer data types, where smaller bit-widths are contained within the most significant bits of larger ones (slicing an int8 model's MSBs directly yields int4 or int2). Applied to the FFN parameters of Gemma-2 2B/9B and Mistral 7B using OmniQuant and QAT as base quantization algorithms, MatQuant produces int8 and int4 models within 0.5% of independently trained baselines while delivering 4–7% higher accuracy for int2 models—with an additional 6% gain when an extra bit represents outliers at an effective 2.05-bit precision—all from a single stored model that also enables free interpolative extraction of untrained precisions like int6 and int3. The approach establishes that int2 quantization can be substantially improved through multi-scale co-training with higher precisions, while the int4 and int8 models remain quality-neutral, though all gains are demonstrated exclusively on LLM feed-forward network quantization with learning-based methods.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: One Model Per Precision Is Both Inefficient and Inflexible

The fundamental challenge this paper addresses is structural inefficiency in how we deploy quantized large language models. When practitioners want to serve an LLM at different precision levels—say, int8 for high-quality inference in a datacenter with generous memory, and int4 or int2 for edge deployment with tight constraints—current methods force them to **train, store, and maintain separate models for each target bit-width**. Each precision is treated as an independent optimization problem, producing a collection of distinct models rather than a single, versatile one. This has several concrete costs:

- **Storage bloat**: maintaining int8, int4, and int2 variants of a model like Gemma-2 9B means storing three separate weight matrices—approximately 9B parameters × (1 + 0.5 + 0.25) bytes = 15.75 GB in a naive scenario, versus a single 9 GB int8 model that could serve all three.
- **Training redundancy**: each precision requires its own quantization pass (whether post-training calibration or quantization-aware training), wasting compute on learning the same underlying weight structure multiple times.
- **Deployment rigidity**: if a serving environment's available memory changes (e.g., due to load fluctuations or hardware heterogeneity), the system is locked into whichever precision models were pre-trained; there's no graceful degradation or elastic adaptation.

This is not simply a matter of convenience—it reflects a deeper missed opportunity. Integer data types possess an **inherent nested (Matryoshka) structure**: an 8-bit integer contains within its most significant bits a valid 4-bit integer and a valid 2-bit integer. The bit representation is hierarchical by construction:

```
int8:  [b7 b6 b5 b4 b3 b2 b1 b0]
int4:  [b7 b6 b5 b4] — the top 4 MSBs of int8
int2:  [b7 b6] — the top 2 MSBs of int8
```

Despite this natural nesting, **existing quantization methods largely ignore it**. When OmniQuant or QAT quantizes a model to int4, the resulting weight distribution is optimized solely for 4-bit fidelity; slicing the top 2 bits from this int4 model (or from an int8 model) to get an int2 model produces a severe accuracy collapse. Section 4 shows that slicing an int8 OmniQuant baseline to int4 drops Gemma-2 9B accuracy from 74.59% to 72.26%, and slicing to int2 collapses it from 74.59% to 38.11%—a 36-point drop. The nested structure exists in theory, but standard quantization doesn't exploit it.

### Why This Problem Matters: Latency, Memory, and the Autoregressive Bottleneck

The paper grounds this problem in the specific economics of LLM inference. As they note in Section 1:

> "Due to autoregressive nature of LLMs, decode latency tends to dominate inference cost. Decode latency itself is dominated by communication cost of transferring model weights from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) to the SRAM or due to transferring weights/activations in a distributed cluster."

This is the **memory-bandwidth bottleneck** that has become the defining constraint for LLM deployment. In autoregressive decoding, each token generation requires reading the entire model's weights from memory. For a 9B-parameter model at bfloat16, that's ~18 GB of weights that must stream through the memory bus for every single token. Quantizing to int8 halves this; int4 quarters it; int2 reduces it by 8×. The latency and energy savings are therefore directly proportional to the compression ratio—**if** the quantized model retains accuracy.

The practical tension is captured by the int2 regime specifically. The paper observes:

> "quantizing to extremely low precisions like int2 is known to be highly inaccurate"

This is the central deployment dilemma: int2 offers the most dramatic efficiency gains (8× fewer bits than bfloat16, 4× fewer than int8) but suffers from catastrophic accuracy loss under standard quantization methods. Gemma-2 9B drops from 74.38% average task accuracy (bfloat16) to 60.24% under the best int2 baseline in Table 1—a 14-point gap that is often unacceptable in production. Consequently, practitioners either (a) avoid int2 entirely, leaving significant memory savings on the table, or (b) maintain separate int4 and int8 models and route queries accordingly—precisely the multi-model maintenance burden the paper critiques.

The importance extends beyond any single model. As the LLM ecosystem fragments across open-weight models (Gemma, Mistral, Llama), cloud APIs, and on-device deployments, the **heterogeneity of deployment environments** means that different users need different precision levels for the same underlying model. A single quantized model that can dynamically serve int8, int4, or int2 depending on the deployment context would dramatically simplify the model supply chain.

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper organizes prior quantization work along two axes: learning-free vs. learning-based methods (Section 2). Both have structural limitations that MatQuant addresses.

#### Learning-Free Quantization: Fast but Brittle at Low Bit-Widths

Learning-free methods—GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022), AWQ (Lin et al., 2023), SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023), QuIP (Chee et al., 2024), SqueezeLLM (Kim et al., 2024)—perform quantization using small calibration datasets without gradient descent on model weights. Their core principle is to find a mapping from full-precision weights to quantized values that minimizes some reconstruction error (layer-wise L2 output error for GPTQ, activation-aware scaling for AWQ).

The paper acknowledges their strengths: they are inexpensive, require limited data, and work well at higher bit-widths (int8, int4). But at the int2 regime, the paper is unambiguous about their failure:

> "While learning-free methods are inexpensive and work well at higher bit-widths, they are often suboptimal in the low-precision regime, which benefits greatly from learning-based techniques."

The reason is fundamental: int2 has only 4 possible values per weight (0, 1, 2, 3 in unsigned representation). The quantization error introduced by mapping a continuous weight distribution into 4 buckets is enormous, and no amount of clever calibration—short of actually updating the weights to be more quantization-friendly—can fully compensate. Learning-free methods lack the degrees of freedom to reshape the weight distribution itself; they can only choose how to map a given distribution, not change it.

#### Learning-Based Quantization: Better Quality but Single-Precision

Learning-based methods—QAT (Jacob et al., 2018), OmniQuant (Shao et al., 2023), LLM-QAT (Liu et al., 2024a), EfficientQAT (Chen et al., 2024), SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2024b)—use gradient descent to optimize either the model weights themselves (QAT) or auxiliary scaling/shifting/rotation parameters (OmniQuant, SpinQuant) to minimize quantization-induced error.

The paper focuses its integration on two representative learning-based algorithms:

- **QAT** (Section 3.1.1): directly updates model weights $W_F$ using end-to-end cross-entropy loss with a straight-through estimator (STE) to propagate gradients through the non-differentiable quantization operator. The optimization is:

$$\min_{W_F} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \in [N]} \mathcal{L}_{CE}(F(x_i; Q_{MM}(W_F, c)), y_i)$$

where $Q_{MM}(W_F, c)$ is the $c$-bit MinMax-quantized version of the weights. This is powerful because it can reshape the weight distribution to be quantization-friendly, but it's expensive—requiring end-to-end training over millions of tokens—and the paper notes it can overfit to small calibration datasets (Section 4.2).

- **OmniQuant** (Section 3.1.2): does not update model weights. Instead, it learns auxiliary parameters—two scaling coefficients $\gamma$ and $\beta$ that modify the quantization range, and per-channel shift ($\delta$) and scale ($s$) parameters for the FFN's affine projections—by minimizing layer-wise L2 reconstruction error:

$$\min_{\gamma, \beta, \delta, s} \|F_l(W^l_F, X_l) - F_l(Q_{\text{Omni}}(W^l_F), X_l)\|_2^2$$

This is much cheaper than QAT (operates on limited data, trains only auxiliary parameters) and achieves comparable accuracy at higher precisions. The paper uses OmniQuant as its primary base algorithm because it represents the practical sweet spot between cost and quality that practitioners actually use.

The critical limitation shared by **all** these methods—both learning-free and learning-based—is that they **treat each target precision as an independent optimization problem**. A model quantized to int4 via OmniQuant has no relationship to the same model quantized to int2; they are separate artifacts, with separate training runs, separate weight representations, and separate storage. The nested bit structure of integer data types is unused.

#### The Sliced Baseline: Evidence the Nested Structure Is Neglected

The paper provides direct evidence for this neglect through the "Sliced int8" baseline that appears throughout its tables. This baseline takes an int8-quantized model (trained by OmniQuant or QAT) and simply slices out the most significant $r$ bits to produce an $r$-bit model, as described by the slicing operator $S(q_c, r)$ in Equation 6.

If existing quantization methods naturally preserved the nested structure, this slicing operation should produce reasonable int4 and int2 models. It does not. Table 1 shows:

- **int4 sliced from int8** (Gemma-2 9B OmniQuant): 72.26% accuracy vs. 74.33% baseline trained explicitly for int4—a 2-point gap, but still functional.
- **int2 sliced from int8** (same model): 38.11% accuracy vs. 60.24% baseline—a **22-point gap**, collapsing to near-random performance.

This is the smoking gun: the int8-quantized model, optimized only for 8-bit fidelity, arranges its most significant bits in ways that are catastrophic for 2-bit extraction. The bits that carry discriminative information for an 8-bit model are not necessarily the ones that matter for a 2-bit model, because the optimizer was never told to care about the 2-bit view.

#### Multi-Scale Training: The Conceptual Precursor

The paper explicitly positions itself within the multi-scale training lineage (Section 2), citing:

- **Nested dropout** (Rippel et al., 2014) and **slimmable networks** (Yu et al., 2018) in computer vision, which trained a single network that could operate at multiple widths by nesting smaller sub-networks within a larger one.
- **Matryoshka Representation Learning** (Kusupati et al., 2022), which extended this nested training idea to representation dimensions, learning embedding vectors where the first $k$ dimensions form a valid (if less accurate) representation for any $k$—enabling elastic deployment.
- **MatFormer** (Devvrit et al., 2023), which applied nested sub-network training to Transformer architectures, showing that jointly training multiple model depths within a single model produced a smooth accuracy-vs-compute trade-off.

The paper draws a direct conceptual parallel: just as Matryoshka representation learning nests smaller embedding dimensions within larger ones, MatQuant nests smaller bit-widths within larger ones. The key difference—and what makes this paper novel—is the application domain. Prior multi-scale training focused on **model capacity** (number of parameters, embedding dimensions). This paper applies the nested training philosophy to **numerical precision**, which is a different axis entirely because the nesting is not architectural but **inherent to the number representation itself**.

The only prior work on multi-precision training the paper cites is Yu et al. (2019), which trained a single ConvNet that could perform well at any bit-width. However:

> "the experiments were limited to ConvNets and small Neural Networks. In this paper, we extend the idea of nested precision to LLMs and show that it indeed works at scale."

This is a non-trivial extension. LLMs have attention mechanisms, much larger parameter counts, and different failure modes under quantization (outlier channels, activation outliers) compared to ConvNets. The paper also claims to be the first to demonstrate **interpolative bit-width extraction** (int6, int3) without training for those precisions, and to enable layer-wise Mix'n'Match of different precisions for free—capabilities not shown in prior multi-precision work.

### How MatQuant Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is not to invent a new quantization algorithm from scratch, but to introduce a **training paradigm that wraps around existing learning-based quantization methods**. This is made explicit in Section 3.2:

> "MatQuant is a general purpose framework to develop a single model that can do well at any precision. It is a multi-scale training technique that works with most learning-based quantization schemes like QAT and OmniQuant."

The core insight is that the optimization objective should be **multi-scale from the start**. Instead of optimizing for a single precision $c$:

$$\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(Q(\theta, c))$$

MatQuant jointly optimizes over a set of target precisions $R = \{r_1, r_2, ..., r_K\}$ by slicing each nested precision from the largest bit-width:

$$\min_{\theta} \sum_{r \in R} \lambda_r \cdot \mathcal{L}(S(Q(\theta, c), r))$$

where $S(\cdot, r)$ extracts the $r$-bit model from the $c$-bit quantized weights (Equation 7). This forces the optimizer to arrange the MSBs such that they form valid lower-precision models, **while still maintaining the full-precision quality through the joint loss**.

The paper identifies three specific value propositions that distinguish MatQuant from prior approaches:

1. **Single model, multiple precisions**: One training run produces a model that can be served at int8, int4, or int2—no separate storage or training needed. This is the efficiency argument.

2. **Better int2 through cross-precision regularization**: The int4 and int8 loss terms act as a regularizer on weight placement. The paper shows (Section 5.3, Table 5) that even when targeting only int2, training with an 8-bit representation where loss is computed only on the top 2 MSBs—what they call "Single Precision MatQuant"—outperforms training an explicit int2 model. The six extra bits provide degrees of freedom to position the 2 MSBs for maximum discriminability.

3. **Free interpolation and elastic deployment**: Models trained only on {8, 4, 2} can be sliced to previously unseen bit-widths like int6 and int3 with minimal accuracy loss (Tables 1-2). Furthermore, different layers can use different precisions (Mix'n'Match, Section 4.3), producing hundreds of models with different accuracy-memory trade-offs—all extracted from the same stored weights with zero additional training.

The paper also draws an important distinction that addresses a potential criticism: MatQuant is **not merely a training trick to improve int2**. While it does dramatically improve int2 (4-7% depending on base algorithm and model), it is designed as a *deployment flexibility* mechanism first. The int2 improvement is a beneficial side effect of multi-scale co-training—the joint loss reshapes the quantized weight distribution to be more favorable for low-bit extraction (Figure 1c shows the right-shifted distribution). But the primary contribution is the single-model, multi-precision paradigm itself.

### The Subtext: A Better Way to Do Low-Bit Quantization

There is an important implicit argument running through the paper that surfaces most clearly in Section 5.3 (Single Precision MatQuant): **multi-precision training may be a fundamentally better way to do low-bit quantization, even when you only care about one precision**. The finding that training an 8-bit model with loss computed only on the top 2 bits outperforms training an explicit 2-bit model (Table 5: 64.02% vs. 60.24% for Gemma-2 9B OmniQuant int2) is not just a neat trick—it suggests that the optimization landscape for low-bit quantization is better navigated in a higher-dimensional space with a projection to the target bit-width, rather than in the constrained low-bit space directly.

The weight distribution plot in Figure 1c supports this: MatQuant's training shifts more weights into higher-valued quantized buckets, which the paper hypothesizes is more informative for the int2 model (which only has 4 possible values, so maximizing the use of all 4 matters). The int8 and int4 models, with 256 and 16 possible values respectively, can absorb this shift with minimal accuracy loss because they have sufficient representational capacity to compensate. This is an elegant demonstration of **overparameterization as a training aid**: the excess bits in int8 provide optimization headroom that benefits the nested low-bit model, without the low-bit model paying the storage cost of those bits at inference time.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper introduces a **training wrapper** rather than a new quantization algorithm — given an existing learning-based quantization method like OmniQuant or QAT, MatQuant augments its training objective so that a single set of model weights, quantized once at the highest target precision (int8), can be sliced to produce functional models at lower precisions (int4, int2) that match or exceed independently trained baselines. The problem it solves is the "one model per precision" deployment burden, and the shape of the solution is **joint optimization over multiple bit-widths simultaneously**, using the nested integer bit structure as a structural prior that forces the optimizer to arrange the most significant bits to be directly usable at coarser granularities.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four interconnected components, though two are external (provided by prior work) and two are MatQuant's novel contributions:

1. **Base Quantization Algorithm (external)** — either OmniQuant (learns auxiliary scaling/shifting parameters via layer-wise L2 reconstruction) or QAT (directly updates model weights via end-to-end cross-entropy with straight-through estimation). This provides the fundamental quantization function $Q(\cdot, c)$ that maps floating-point weights to $c$-bit integers.

2. **Nested Bit Slicing Operator** (MatQuant's structural innovation) — the function $S(q_c, r)$ that, given a $c$-bit quantized weight vector $q_c$, extracts an $r$-bit model by keeping only the $r$ most significant bits, rounding the $(r+1)$-th bit, clamping overflow, and scaling back to the original bit-width. This is the mechanism that makes one model serve multiple precisions.

3. **Multi-Scale Loss Aggregator** (MatQuant's training innovation) — instead of computing loss at a single precision, MatQuant quantizes weights at the highest precision (int8), slices out each target precision ($r \in R$) using the operator above, computes the base algorithm's loss independently for each sliced precision, and sums them with per-precision weights $\lambda_r$. This is what forces the optimizer to care about all bit-widths simultaneously.

4. **Training Loop** (inherited from the base algorithm) — gradient descent over either model weights (QAT) or auxiliary parameters (OmniQuant), using straight-through estimation to propagate gradients through the non-differentiable quantization and slicing operations.

Information flows as follows: training data enters → the base quantization algorithm quantizes the full-precision weights to int8 → the slicing operator extracts int4 and int2 views from the int8 representation → each view is fed through the model's forward pass → the base algorithm's loss is computed separately for each precision view → losses are summed with per-precision weights $\lambda_r$ → gradients flow back through the slicing operation and quantization step (using STE) to update either the model weights or the auxiliary quantization parameters → the cycle repeats.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the quantization primitives (MinMax, QAT, OmniQuant)** — because MatQuant wraps these algorithms, the reader needs to understand exactly what loss landscape each base method operates on before seeing how MatQuant modifies it.
- **Second, the nested bit-slicing operator $S(q_c, r)$** — this is MatQuant's core structural mechanism; understanding its precise rounding and clamping behavior explains why bit-width interpolation works and the "Extra Precision" phenomenon of Section 7.
- **Third, the MatQuant training objective (Equation 7)** — the joint loss function that combines the base algorithm's objective across multiple sliced precisions, including the role of the $\lambda_r$ weights and the interplay with co-distillation.
- **Fourth, design choices and their justifications** — why train at int8 as the parent precision rather than int16, why the specific rounding behavior in slicing, why the default $\lambda$ ratios favor int2, and why Single Precision MatQuant works as a standalone technique.
- **Fifth, the deployment mechanisms (slicing, interpolation, Mix'n'Match)** — how the trained model is actually used at inference time, including extracting untrained precisions and layer-wise heterogeneous precision assignment.

#### The Quantization Primitives: MinMax, QAT, and OmniQuant

MatQuant is not a standalone quantization algorithm — it is a meta-objective that wraps around an existing learning-based quantization method. To understand MatQuant's design choices, the reader must first understand the two base algorithms it integrates with, and the common quantization function they share.

**MinMax Quantization.** Both QAT and OmniQuant build on the same fundamental quantization operator, which maps a real-valued weight vector $w$ to $c$-bit integers using uniform quantization with an affine transformation (scale and zero-point). The paper defines this in Equation 1:

$$Q_{MM}(w, c) = \text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{w}{\alpha} + z \right\rceil, 0, 2^c - 1\right)$$

where:

$$\alpha = \frac{\max(w) - \min(w)}{2^c - 1}, \quad z = -\frac{\min(w)}{\alpha}$$

Here $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a vector of full-precision (bfloat16) weights in a single quantization group (typically a row or column of a weight matrix), $c$ is the target bit-width (e.g., 8, 4, or 2), $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^+$ is the scaling factor that maps the weight range to the integer range $[0, 2^c-1]$, and $z \in \mathbb{R}$ is the zero-point offset that aligns the minimum weight value with zero in the integer domain.

**What it computes:** each full-precision weight $w_i$ is divided by the scale $\alpha$ (normalizing it to the integer domain), shifted by the zero-point $z$ (so that the minimum weight maps to 0), rounded to the nearest integer via $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$, and then clamped to the valid range $[0, 2^c-1]$ for an unsigned $c$-bit integer. The output $Q_{MM}(w, c)$ is a vector of integers in $[0, 2^c-1]$, which can be stored using $c$ bits per element. To recover an approximation of the original weight during the forward pass, these integers are de-quantized: $\hat{w} = \alpha \cdot (Q_{MM}(w, c) - z)$.

**Why this form:** uniform affine quantization is the standard for hardware-efficient inference because: (1) the scale $\alpha$ and zero-point $z$ are stored once per quantization group (not per-element), adding negligible overhead; (2) integer matrix multiplication with uniform quantization maps directly to hardware-supported integer dot products (e.g., int8 GEMM on GPUs); and (3) the affine transform handles asymmetric weight distributions (weights not centered at zero) through the zero-point, which is important for activations after ReLU or other non-negative nonlinearities.

**Quantization Aware Training (QAT).** QAT directly optimizes the model's full-precision weights $W_F$ to minimize the end-to-end task loss when the forward pass uses quantized weights. The paper formalizes this in Equation 2:

$$\min_{W_F} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \in [N]} \mathcal{L}_{CE}\left(F(x_i; Q_{MM}(W_F, c)), y_i\right)$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{CE}$ is the standard cross-entropy loss, $F(\cdot; Q_{MM}(W_F, c))$ is the model's forward pass using $c$-bit quantized weights, and $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^N$ are the training input-output pairs.

**What it computes:** for each training batch, the model weights are quantized to $c$ bits, the forward pass is computed using the quantized (and then de-quantized for the actual arithmetic) weights, the cross-entropy between the model's output distribution and the ground-truth labels is computed, and gradients are back-propagated to update the full-precision weights $W_F$. The key enabler is the **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)**: during backpropagation, the quantization operator's gradient (which is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the rounding boundaries) is replaced with the identity function $\frac{\partial Q_{MM}(w)}{\partial w} \approx 1$. This means the gradients flow through the quantization step as if it were transparent, allowing the full-precision weights to be updated even though the forward pass used their quantized versions.

**Why this form:** QAT directly optimizes for task performance under quantization constraints. The full-precision weights $W_F$ are free to move into arrangements that are more quantization-friendly — for example, clustering weights near quantization bin centers to minimize rounding error, or adjusting the weight distribution to use more of the available integer range. This makes QAT the most powerful quantization method in principle, but also the most expensive: it requires end-to-end gradient computation over the full model and a large training dataset (the paper uses 100M tokens from C4 for LLM QAT).

A subtle but important detail: QAT maintains two copies of each weight — the stored full-precision weight $W_F$ (used for gradient updates) and the quantized integer weight $Q_{MM}(W_F, c)$ (used in the forward pass and for final model storage). Only the quantized weights are needed at inference time; the full-precision weights are a training artifact. This dual representation is what enables MatQuant's multi-scale training, because the full-precision weights provide the optimization headroom while the quantized-and-sliced weights determine the deployed precision.

**OmniQuant.** Unlike QAT, OmniQuant does not update the model weights themselves. It freezes $W_F$ and instead learns auxiliary parameters — scaling and shifting coefficients — that adapt the quantization ranges and the FFN activations to minimize quantization error. The paper defines OmniQuant's modified quantization in Equation 3:

$$Q_{\text{Omni}}(w, c) = \text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{w}{\alpha} + z \right\rceil, 0, 2^c - 1\right)$$

$$\alpha = \frac{\gamma \cdot \max(w) - \beta \cdot \min(w)}{2^c - 1}, \quad z = -\frac{\beta \cdot \min(w)}{\alpha}$$

where $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$ are per-channel (or per-group) learnable scaling parameters that modify the effective quantization range. The standard MinMax quantization is recovered when $\gamma = \beta = 1$. By learning $\gamma$ and $\beta$, OmniQuant can shrink the quantization range (to reduce the step size and lower rounding error for concentrated weights) or expand it (to capture important outlier weights without clipping).

OmniQuant also introduces per-channel shift and scale parameters for the FFN's affine transformations, defined in Equation 4:

$$X W + b \rightarrow ((X - \delta) \oslash s) \cdot Q_{\text{Omni}}(W \odot s) + b + \delta \cdot W$$

where $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ is the input activation matrix to an FFN layer, $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_o}$ is the weight matrix for that affine projection, $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d_o}$ is the bias vector, $\delta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a learnable shift vector (per input channel), $s \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a learnable scale vector (per input channel), $\oslash$ is element-wise division, and $\odot$ is element-wise multiplication.

**What it computes:** the input activations $X$ are first shifted by $\delta$ and scaled by $s$ to reduce outlier magnitudes in the activation distribution. Simultaneously, the weights $W$ are inversely scaled by $s$ (element-wise multiplication with $s$, then quantized via $Q_{\text{Omni}}$). After the matrix multiplication, the bias is corrected by $\delta \cdot W$ to account for the activation shift. The net effect is that the quantization burden is redistributed: large activation values are reduced (by scaling them down), and the corresponding weight channels are scaled up to compensate — but because weights are quantized, the quantization range can be adjusted via $\gamma$ and $\beta$ to accommodate the scaled weights without clipping.

**OmniQuant's objective** is then layer-wise L2 reconstruction, defined in Equation 5:

$$\min_{\gamma, \beta, \delta, s} \|F_l(W^l_F, X_l) - F_l(Q_{\text{Omni}}(W^l_F), X_l)\|_2^2$$

where $F_l(\cdot)$ is the forward pass through a single Transformer layer (attention block followed by FFN block), $W^l_F$ are the full-precision weights of that layer, and $X_l$ is the layer's input from a small calibration dataset. The optimization is done independently per layer (not end-to-end), using only 128 examples with sequence length 2048 from C4 — a tiny fraction of what QAT needs.

**Why this form:** OmniQuant achieves QAT-competitive accuracy at a fraction of the cost because: (1) it only optimizes a handful of auxiliary parameters per layer (typically $\gamma, \beta$ per weight group, plus $\delta, s$ per FFN input channel), not the full weight matrix; (2) the layer-wise L2 objective is cheap to compute (single forward pass, no backpropagation through the full model); and (3) the reconstruction error is a good proxy for downstream task performance, especially with the shift/scale transformation that addresses activation outliers. This efficiency is what makes OmniQuant the "practical" base algorithm for MatQuant — if MatQuant required expensive QAT-level training, its adoption would be limited. The paper's design of making MatQuant work with OmniQuant (96%+ of the experiments) is a deliberate choice to target the deployment regime practitioners actually use.

A critical distinction: OmniQuant's auxiliary parameters ($\gamma, \beta, \delta, s$) are **per-precision**. The $\gamma$ and $\beta$ learned for int8 quantization are different from those learned for int4, because the optimal quantization range depends on how many bins are available. This means that in standard OmniQuant, training int8, int4, and int2 models requires three separate training runs with three separate sets of auxiliary parameters. MatQuant fundamentally changes this by forcing a **single set of auxiliary parameters** (for the int8 parent precision) to work well for all child precisions jointly.

#### The Nested Bit-Slicing Operator: $S(q_c, r)$

The mechanism that makes MatQuant possible at inference time is the slicing operator, defined in Equation 6:

$$S(q_c, r) = \text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{q_c}{2^{c-r}} \right\rceil, 0, 2^r - 1\right) \times 2^{c-r}$$

where $q_c \in [0, 2^c-1]$ is a $c$-bit quantized integer (the output of $Q_{MM}$ or $Q_{\text{Omni}}$ at precision $c$, e.g., int8), $r$ is the target bit-width (e.g., 4 or 2), and $2^{c-r}$ is the bit-shift factor (e.g., $2^{8-4} = 16$ for int8 → int4, or $2^{8-2} = 64$ for int8 → int2).

**What it computes:** the operator extracts an $r$-bit model from a $c$-bit model in three steps:

1. **Right-shift by $c-r$ bits:** $\frac{q_c}{2^{c-r}}$ (integer division). This discards the $c-r$ least significant bits, keeping only the $r$ most significant bits. For int8 → int4, this is dividing by 16, moving the top 4 bits to the bottom 4 positions.

2. **Round and clamp to $r$ bits:** $\lfloor q_c / 2^{c-r} \rceil$ rounds to the nearest integer (which implements rounding based on the most significant discarded bit — if the $(r+1)$-th MSB is 1, we round up; if 0, we round down), then $\text{clamp}(\cdot, 0, 2^r-1)$ prevents overflow (e.g., if rounding pushes a value to $2^r$, it is clamped to $2^r-1$).

3. **Left-shift back by $c-r$ bits:** multiplication by $2^{c-r}$. This restores the value to the original int8 scale, so that the resulting $r$-bit integer occupies the same numerical range as the parent int8 quantized weight. For example, an int8 value of 192 becomes an int2 value of $3 \times 64 = 192$ (since $192/64 = 3$, which is a valid int2 value).

The paper provides a concrete example: the unsigned int8 value 53. In binary, 53 = 00110101₂. The first two MSBs are 00₂ = 0. Naively slicing them would give 0. However, the bit corresponding to $2^5 = 32$ (the $(r+1)$-th MSB from the top, since $r=2$ and we're looking at bit position 5 counting from the MSB at position 7) is set. The rounding step detects this: $53/64 = 0.828$, which rounds to 1. So the sliced int2 value is 1 (which, when left-shifted, becomes 64). This rounding behavior is deliberate: as noted in Appendix A, it "pushes values to higher buckets as we expect them to be more informative," following the finding from Wanda/SparseGPT-style pruning (Sun et al., 2024) that larger-magnitude weights are generally more important.

**Why this form:** the slicing operator has three design properties that are essential for MatQuant's deployment flexibility:

- **No data movement or re-quantization:** extracting an $r$-bit model from a stored $c$-bit model requires only bitwise operations (shift, mask, possibly a small lookup table for rounding). This means it can be done at load time or even dynamically per-layer, with negligible cost. The alternative — storing separate quantized models — would require loading different weight arrays from memory, defeating the single-model efficiency.

- **Integer arithmetic compatibility:** the output $S(q_c, r)$ is still an integer in the same range as the parent $c$-bit quantization ($[0, 2^c-1]$), because the left-shift restores the scale. This means the sliced model can use the exact same de-quantization formula ($\hat{w} = \alpha \cdot (S(q_c, r) - z)$) as the parent model, with the same $\alpha$ and $z$. No per-precision recalibration is needed — the scale and zero-point from int8 training are reused directly.

- **Deterministic and differentiable (via STE):** the slicing involves only division, rounding, clamping, and multiplication — all of which are either differentiable or can be handled with straight-through estimation during training. This is what allows MatQuant to optimize through the slicing operation during training: the forward pass uses sliced quantized weights, and the backward pass treats the slicing as transparent (gradients flow to the full $c$-bit quantized weights, which in turn flow to the full-precision weights or auxiliary parameters depending on the base algorithm).

The clamping in the third step (preventing overflow after rounding) is particularly important and the paper discusses it in detail in Appendix A and Section 7. Consider an int8 value of 240. Dividing by 64 (for int2 extraction) gives $240/64 = 3.75$, which rounds to 4. But int2 only supports values 0, 1, 2, 3. Without clamping, $4 \times 64 = 256$ would be outside the int8 range and would effectively use a 9th bit. The clamping step ensures $4 \rightarrow 3$, so the output is $3 \times 64 = 192$ — a valid int8 value corresponding to int2 bucket 3. This is the **correct** behavior for standard integer arithmetic.

However, the paper's "Errata" (Section 7) reveals that the initial draft had a bug: it used Equation 8 without the clamping step, allowing the $r$-bit model to occasionally produce values of $2^r$ (e.g., 4 for a 2-bit model), which requires an extra bit to represent. This bug turned out to be serendipitous: the extra bucket acts as a sparse outlier representation, and for int2 models, it provides a 5-6% accuracy boost (e.g., Gemma-2 9B int2 accuracy jumps from 63.35% with proper clamping to 68.52% with the "Extra Precision" bug, as shown in Tables 5 and 30 of the Appendix). The paper hypothesizes that this extra bucket captures outlier weights that would otherwise be severely distorted by int2 quantization, and that even a single extra bit is sufficient because only a small fraction of weights need the extra representational capacity.

#### The MatQuant Training Objective

With the quantization primitive $Q(\cdot, c)$ (from the base algorithm) and the slicing operator $S(q_c, r)$ defined, MatQuant's training objective follows naturally. The paper states it in Equation 7:

$$\min_{\theta} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i \in [N]} \sum_{r \in R} \lambda_r \cdot \mathcal{L}\left(F(S(Q(\theta, c), r), x'_i), y'_i\right)$$

where:

- $\theta$ is the set of parameters being optimized — for QAT, $\theta = W_F$ (the full-precision model weights); for OmniQuant, $\theta = \{\gamma, \beta, \delta, s\}$ (the auxiliary quantization parameters per layer).
- $c$ is the highest (parent) precision, fixed at 8 in all experiments (int8 is the largest integer precision commonly supported in hardware and training frameworks).
- $R = \{r_1, r_2, ..., r_K\}$ is the set of target bit-widths. The paper defaults to $R = \{8, 4, 2\}$, reflecting the standard integer precision hierarchy.
- $Q(\theta, c)$ is the base algorithm's quantization function applied at $c$ bits — this produces a $c$-bit quantized weight vector from either the full-precision weights (QAT) or the auxiliary-parameter-modified quantization (OmniQuant).
- $S(Q(\theta, c), r)$ slices the $c$-bit quantized weights to $r$ bits using the operator from Equation 6.
- $\mathcal{L}$ is the loss function of the base algorithm: for QAT, $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{CE}$ (cross-entropy with ground-truth labels, so $x'_i = x_i$, $y'_i = y_i$); for OmniQuant, $\mathcal{L} = \|\cdot\|_2^2$ (layer-wise L2 reconstruction, so $x'_i = X_l$ is the layer input, $y'_i = F_l(W^l_F, X_l)$ is the full-precision layer output serving as the reconstruction target).
- $\lambda_r \in \mathbb{R}^+$ is the loss weight for precision $r$, controlling how much the optimizer prioritizes each precision level.

**What it computes:** for each training example or calibration sample, the following happens:

1. The model weights are quantized to $c$ bits (int8) using the base algorithm: $q_c = Q(\theta, c)$.
2. For each target precision $r \in R$, the int8-quantized weights are sliced to $r$ bits: $q_r = S(q_c, r)$.
3. The model's forward pass is computed using $q_r$: for QAT, this is the full model output $F(x_i; q_r)$; for OmniQuant, this is the single-layer output $F_l(q_r, X_l)$.
4. The loss is computed between this output and the target: for QAT, cross-entropy with the label; for OmniQuant, L2 distance to the full-precision layer output.
5. The losses from all target precisions are summed, weighted by $\lambda_r$.
6. Gradients flow backward through the slicing operation (STE, identity gradient), through the $c$-bit quantization (STE, identity gradient), to update $\theta$.

**Why this form:** the joint optimization over multiple precisions has several critical properties that single-precision training lacks:

- **Shared MSB optimization:** the gradient signals from all precision levels propagate to the same $c$-bit quantized weights. The int8 loss encourages the full 8-bit representation to be accurate; the int4 loss encourages the top 4 MSBs to form a good 4-bit model; the int2 loss encourages the top 2 MSBs to form a good 2-bit model. The optimizer must find a compromise: arrange the 8 bits such that all three views work well. Because int8 has 256 possible values and int2 has only 4, the int8 representation has significant redundancy — there are $256/4 = 64$ different int8 values that all map to the same int2 value. The optimizer can use this redundancy to position the int2 bin boundaries optimally without destroying int8 accuracy.

- **Implicit regularization against overfitting:** the multi-scale objective acts as a form of structured regularization. A weight configuration that produces good int8 accuracy might have the top 2 MSBs in an arrangement that is terrible for int2 (this is exactly what happens with the "Sliced int8" baseline — the int8 model, optimized only for 8 bits, has uninformative top-2 MSBs). The int2 loss term penalizes such configurations, forcing the MSBs to carry discriminative information. This regularization is "free" in the sense that it doesn't require additional data or hyperparameters beyond the $\lambda_r$ weights.

- **Asymmetric precision weighting:** the paper defaults to $(\lambda_8, \lambda_4, \lambda_2) = (0.1, 0.1, 1.0)$ for Gemma models (Appendix B). This heavily weights the int2 loss — the optimizer cares 10× more about the 2-bit model's accuracy than the 8-bit model's. The logic (Section 5.1, Table 3) is that int8 and int4 models, with their large number of quantization bins, are relatively easy to optimize — even a moderately suboptimal bin arrangement still produces acceptable accuracy. Int2, with only 4 possible values, is extremely sensitive to which weights map to which bins. A weight that falls on the boundary between int2 bins 1 and 2 can map to either, and that single choice might determine whether an entire channel is effectively "on" or "off." By weighting the int2 loss higher, MatQuant ensures the optimizer resolves these critical bin assignments correctly, even if it means slightly suboptimal int8 bins.

- **Single set of auxiliary parameters:** for OmniQuant, $\theta = \{\gamma, \beta, \delta, s\}$ is shared across all precisions. This is fundamentally different from standard OmniQuant, which would learn separate $\gamma^{(8)}, \beta^{(8)}$ for int8, $\gamma^{(4)}, \beta^{(4)}$ for int4, and $\gamma^{(2)}, \beta^{(2)}$ for int2. In MatQuant, there is one $\gamma$ and one $\beta$ per weight group, and they must serve all three precisions simultaneously. This constraint forces the quantization range to be chosen such that it works across bit-widths — likely a narrower range than what would be optimal for int8 alone, to ensure the reduced bin resolution at int4 and int2 doesn't cause excessive information loss. The paper doesn't explicitly analyze this trade-off, but it is implicit in the architecture: the $\gamma, \beta$ are optimized to minimize the weighted sum of reconstruction errors at all precisions, not any single one.

**Training hyperparameters** (Appendix B for OmniQuant, applicable to QAT with adjustments):

- **Data:** 128 examples with sequence length 2048 from the C4 dataset, for a total of 262,144 tokens per training run (10M tokens for all models except the int2 baseline which uses 20M tokens, following Shao et al., 2023's finding that int2 benefits from longer training).
- **Batch size:** 4 for OmniQuant (small, because each example is a full 2048-token sequence and the forward pass is per-layer).
- **Learning rate:** constant $1 \times 10^{-3}$ for OmniQuant; for QAT, linear warmup to $1 \times 10^{-5}$ over 150 steps followed by cosine decay.
- **Optimizer:** AdamW with default betas (presumably 0.9, 0.999, though not explicitly stated for QAT).
- **Hardware:** TPUv5e chips.

For QAT experiments specifically:
- 100M tokens from C4, batch size 16, sequence length 8192, single epoch (for FFN-only quantization).
- 300M tokens from C4, batch size 16, single epoch (for FFN+Attention quantization).

For co-distillation experiments (Section 5.2): training uses 8.3M tokens (shorter than the main experiments, since distillation provides additional supervision signal).

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Parent precision selection: why int8?** The paper always quantizes at $c = 8$ (int8) and slices down to lower precisions. This choice is not arbitrary:

- int8 is the highest integer precision with widespread hardware support (NVIDIA Tensor Cores, Google TPUs, Apple Neural Engine). Training at int8 means the parent model can be served directly on current hardware.
- The gap between int8 (256 bins) and int2 (4 bins) provides substantial optimization headroom. A single int8 bin corresponds to $1/256 \approx 0.4\%$ of the weight range — fine enough that repositioning a weight by a few bins doesn't materially affect int8 accuracy. But that same repositioning can move the weight across an int2 bin boundary (which covers $1/4 = 25\%$ of the range), dramatically affecting the int2 model.
- int16 would provide even more headroom but would double the parent model's memory, defeating the "single stored model" advantage. The parent model must itself be compact enough to be practical.

**Rounding behavior in slicing: why round-to-nearest with upward bias?** Appendix A explains the deliberate choice to round up when the $(r+1)$-th MSB is 1, and round down when it's 0 (standard round-half-up). The justification — "we expect [higher buckets] to be more informative" — is based on the well-established finding in model pruning that larger-magnitude weights are more important for model accuracy. By rounding borderline values up, the slicing operator biases the low-precision model toward keeping more weights in higher-magnitude bins (which correspond to larger absolute weight values after de-quantization), preserving the model's most influential parameters. A round-half-down strategy would systematically reduce weight magnitudes, potentially "turning off" important parameters by pushing them to lower bins.

**The clamping necessity:** the clamp in Equation 6 is essential even in the correct implementation because the rounding can overflow. For int8 → int2: $255/64 = 3.98$, which rounds to 4. Without clamping, this would produce an invalid int2 value. With clamping, it's corrected to 3. This affects only the very top of the int8 range — values near 255 — which are relatively rare in practice (most weights cluster near zero), but the clamping ensures correctness for all inputs.

**Co-distillation (Section 5.2):** MatQuant optionally incorporates knowledge distillation from the highest-precision nested model (int8) to lower-precision nested models. The paper explores configurations like $[8, 4, 8 \rightarrow 2]$ (train with int8 loss, int4 loss, and distillation from int8 to int2) and $[8, 4, 2, 8 \rightarrow 4; 2]$ (train with all three direct losses plus distillation from int8 to both int4 and int2). The distillation target is the output distribution (logits) of the int8 model on the same input. This effectively tells the lower-precision model: "I don't care how you arrange your limited bins, as long as your output matches what the high-precision model would produce." This is a softer constraint than the ground-truth label, and Table 4 shows it benefits int2 (0.97% accuracy gain with OmniQuant) while minimally affecting int8 and int4.

#### The Deployment Mechanisms: How MatQuant Models Are Served

Once a MatQuant model is trained (with OmniQuant or QAT), the stored artifact is:

- For **OmniQuant**: the full-precision model weights $W_F$, plus one set of auxiliary parameters ($\gamma, \beta, \delta, s$) per layer, optimized for int8.
- For **QAT**: the full-precision weights $W_F$ only (auxiliary parameters exist only during training as part of the quantization function; the final model stores the int8 quantized weights $Q_{MM}(W_F, 8)$ directly).

At inference time, the model can be served at any precision $r$ through the following procedure:

1. **Quantize to int8**: $q_8 = Q(W_F, 8)$ using the stored auxiliary parameters (OmniQuant) or by direct quantization of the stored int8 weights (QAT).
2. **Slice to $r$ bits** (if $r < 8$): $q_r = S(q_8, r)$ using Equation 6.
3. **De-quantize for computation**: $\hat{W} = \alpha \cdot (q_r - z)$, using the int8 scale $\alpha$ and zero-point $z$. Note that the scale and zero-point do not change when slicing — the left-shift in $S(q_c, r)$ ensures the sliced values remain in the same numerical range as the parent int8 weights, so the same de-quantization parameters apply.

This means the model can serve int8 by skipping step 2, int4 by slicing with $r=4$, int2 by slicing with $r=2$, and — crucially — int6 or int3 by slicing with $r=6$ or $r=3$ even though those precisions were **never explicitly trained**. This is the "interpolative behavior" the paper demonstrates (Section 3.2.1, Tables 1-2): because the optimization forced the 8 MSBs to form a coherent hierarchy at the {8, 4, 2} granularities, the intermediate granularities (6 and 3) benefit from a kind of smooth interpolation. The 6-bit model, for example, uses the 6 MSBs — the same top 4 bits optimized for int4, plus 2 additional bits that were optimized as part of the int8 representation but were never directly supervised as a 6-bit unit. Because the joint training arranges the entire 8-bit representation to be informative (int8 loss) while ensuring the top 4 bits work well (int4 loss) and the top 2 bits work well (int2 loss), the top 6 bits end up "accidentally" good.

**Layer-wise Mix'n'Match (Section 4.3):** Beyond homogeneous slicing, MatQuant enables heterogeneous precision across layers — different layers use different bit-widths — producing models at any effective bit-width between 2 and 8 bits per FFN parameter. The paper explores four strategies for assigning precisions to layers given a target average bit-width:

- **Pyramid**: initial and final layers at int2, middle layers at int8, with int4 as intermediate.
- **Reverse Pyramid**: initial and final layers at int8, middle layers at int2.
- **Increasing**: bit-width increases monotonically from first to last layer.
- **Decreasing**: bit-width decreases monotonically from first to last layer.

The Pyramid strategy "consistently outperformed the others" (Appendix B), and the paper's hypothesis is that middle layers contain the most task-critical representations (they are furthest from both input and output, doing the deepest semantic processing), so they benefit most from higher precision, while initial and final layers (which handle token embedding projection and final classification) can tolerate lower precision.

Figure 2 demonstrates the result: by trying different combinations of per-layer precisions, one can extract hundreds of models from a single trained MatQuant checkpoint, each with a different average bits-per-FFN-parameter and corresponding task accuracy. Importantly, these models require zero additional training — they are pure post-hoc slicing decisions applied at model load time. For the Gemma-2 9B model trained with MatQuant + OmniQuant, the Mix'n'Match curve densely spans the accuracy-vs-bits trade-off, with some sub-4-bit mixed-precision models outperforming the homogeneous 4-bit sliced model.

#### Single Precision MatQuant: When You Only Want int2

Section 5.3 explores what happens when the multi-scale objective is collapsed to a single precision: $R = \{2\}$, i.e., the optimizer only sees the loss from the int2 view. The other precisions (int8, int4) are still implicitly present because the int2 model is extracted by slicing an int8-quantized model — but their losses are not included in the optimization.

The finding is counterintuitive but powerful: Single Precision MatQuant **outperforms** both the standard int2 baseline and (sometimes) the full MatQuant with $R = \{8, 4, 2\}$. Table 5 shows:

- Gemma-2 9B, OmniQuant int2: Baseline 60.24%, MatQuant 63.35%, Single Precision MatQuant **64.02%** (+3.78% over baseline, +0.67% over MatQuant).
- Mistral 7B, OmniQuant int2: Baseline 59.74%, MatQuant 62.75%, Single Precision MatQuant **63.58%** (+3.84% over baseline).

However, the int4 and int8 models extracted from the Single Precision MatQuant checkpoint suffer significant accuracy degradation (Tables 23 and 24 in Appendix G show Gemma-2 9B int8 dropping from 74.59% to 73.80% and int4 dropping from 74.33% to 73.01% for OmniQuant). This makes sense: the optimizer, freed from the constraint of preserving int4 and int8 accuracy, aggressively rearranges the lower 6 bits (the bits that are discarded in the int2 slicing) purely to optimize the placement of the top 2 MSBs. The dropped bits can take on any values — including values that would be catastrophic for int4 or int8 performance — as long as they don't interfere with the top 2 bits' discriminability.

The paper's interpretation (Section 5.3):

> "gradient descent is free to tune these six additional bits to improve the overall quality of the int2 model"

is an elegant framing: the six lower bits act as a **continuous relaxation** of the int2 quantization problem. Instead of optimizing a 2-bit model directly (where each weight must choose one of 4 values and gradients have to squeeze through that tiny bottleneck), the optimizer works in an 8-bit space where each weight has 256 possible values, and the "int2-ness" is enforced only by the fact that the loss is computed on the sliced (2-bit) version. The extra bits provide smooth pathways through the optimization landscape — a weight can gradually drift across int2 bin boundaries by accumulating small changes in the lower bits, rather than having to jump discontinuously from one 2-bit value to another.

This is conceptually related to **stochastic rounding** and **noisy quantization** techniques that add noise during training to smooth the quantization operator, but MatQuant achieves the smoothing structurally (through excess bits) rather than stochastically (through added noise).

**Weight distribution analysis:** Figure 4 (Appendix G) shows the weight distribution for Single Precision MatQuant on Gemma-2 9B int2. Compared to the baseline, the distribution is shifted to the right — more weights occupy the higher-valued int2 bins. The paper explains this: because the int2 loss term is the only objective, the optimizer has maximum freedom to push weights into the most informative bins for a 2-bit representation. The 8-bit representation provides headroom (many different int8 values map to the same int2 value) that the optimizer exploits to achieve this bin rebalancing.

#### Extension to All Transformer Weights (FFN + Attention)

The paper's primary experiments quantize only the FFN parameters ("weight only quantization within the parameter-intensive FFN blocks of the Transformer layer," Section 4). This is justified by the FFN being "the main latency block" in autoregressive decoding — the attention mechanism's cost scales with sequence length, but FFN cost scales with model dimension and accounts for roughly 2/3 of the total parameters in standard Transformer architectures.

However, Section 5.3 and Table 6 extend MatQuant to quantize all weights (FFN + Attention) using QAT. The results reveal a more nuanced picture:

- At int8, int4, and int6: MatQuant performs on par with baselines (e.g., Gemma-2 9B int4: Baseline 72.98% vs. MatQuant 74.01%).
- At int2 and int3: the baseline QAT is **unstable** — the paper marks baseline entries for int2 and int3 with dashes ("—"), indicating they failed to converge or produced invalid results. MatQuant and Single Precision MatQuant, however, are "very stable" and produce functional models.

This instability in low-bit QAT for all weights is a known challenge in the literature (the paper cites Chen et al., 2024; Du et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a, which use warm-starting, distillation, or auxiliary losses to stabilize training). MatQuant's multi-scale training provides inherent stabilization: the higher-precision loss terms (int8, int4) prevent the optimizer from diverging, even when the int2 loss alone would be too sparse or noisy for convergence. This is another example of the regularization benefit of joint multi-scale training, beyond just the deployment flexibility.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The "Train at High Precision, Deploy at Low Precision" Paradigm as a Quasi-Continuous Relaxation

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is not merely that you can slice a quantized model to lower bit-widths—this is trivially true of any integer representation. The insight is that **training a model at high precision with loss supervision only on the low-precision projection produces better low-precision models than training at the target precision directly**. This inverts the standard quantization logic.

Prior work in quantization treats the target bit-width as a constraint to be optimized under: you quantize to int2 by training or calibrating an int2 model. The implicit assumption is that the optimization should occur in the space of the final representation—if you want 2-bit weights, you should optimize over 2-bit weights. This seems tautologically correct, and it is what QAT, OmniQuant, GPTQ, and every other method the paper cites does.

MatQuant shows this assumption is false for low-bit regimes. Single Precision MatQuant (Section 5.3, Table 5) trains an int8 model but computes loss only on the sliced int2 view ($R = \{2\}$, i.e., $λ_8 = λ_4 = 0, λ_2 = 1$). The optimizer works in an 8-dimensional discrete space (256 possible values per weight) but is evaluated only on a 2-dimensional projection (4 possible values). The result: +3.78% over the int2 baseline for Gemma-2 9B with OmniQuant (64.02% vs. 60.24%), and +0.67% over even the full multi-scale MatQuant.

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental gain. It reveals that the 2-bit optimization landscape is pathologically hard to navigate directly—the gradient signal through a 4-value quantization bottleneck is too sparse and discontinuous for effective learning—but becomes tractable when embedded in an 8-bit space where the lower 6 bits act as a smooth, continuous proxy. The optimizer can gradually drift a weight from representing int2 bin 1 to bin 2 by accumulating small changes in the lower bits (e.g., moving from int8 value 64 to 65 to 66... to 128), rather than having to make a single discontinuous jump from 1 to 2 in int2 space. The paper articulates this as "gradient descent is free to tune these six additional bits to improve the overall quality of the int2 model," but the deeper conceptual move is recognizing that **overparameterization during training is beneficial even when the deployment representation is severely constrained**.

This connects to a broader trend in deep learning—overparameterization as a training aid (wider networks train faster, lottery tickets, etc.)—but applies it to numerical precision rather than architectural capacity. It is analogous to training a binary neural network by optimizing in floating-point with a STE, but the mechanism here is different: the excess bits are not just for gradient propagation (the STE already handles that), they provide **representational slack** that the optimizer uses to resolve complex bin-assignment trade-offs. The int8 space has 64 different representations for every int2 value; the optimizer can temporarily use this redundancy to explore different weight configurations before committing to a final int2 mapping.

This insight has practical significance beyond the paper's immediate results: it suggests that quantization research should focus less on designing better low-bit optimizers and more on designing better **high-bit training schemes with low-bit projections**, since the optimization is fundamentally easier in the higher-dimensional space. It also explains why QAT (which maintains full-precision weights as a training proxy) tends to outperform post-training methods at low bit-widths—it's not just the additional training data, but the continuous relaxation provided by the full-precision weights.

### Innovation 2: Multi-Scale Quantization as Implicit Bit-Aware Regularization

The paper's second conceptual contribution is reframing multi-scale training from a "deployment convenience" into a **regularization mechanism that produces strictly better low-bit models**. Standard regularization (weight decay, dropout) penalizes model complexity to improve generalization. MatQuant's multi-scale loss penalizes **bit-structure incoherence**: it punishes any arrangement of the int8 MSBs that would produce poor lower-precision models when sliced.

This is a novel form of regularization because the penalty is not on the weight values themselves but on the **hierarchical informativeness of the bit representation**. A weight configuration that achieves perfect int8 accuracy might have the top 2 MSBs in an arrangement that carries essentially no information about the task—this is exactly what the "Sliced int8" baseline demonstrates, where int2 accuracy collapses to 38.11% from an int8 model that achieves 74.59% (Table 1, Gemma-2 9B). The int8 optimizer, given only an int8 loss, has no reason to organize the MSBs hierarchically; it treats all 8 bits as a flat encoding. The int2 loss term in MatQuant forces the optimizer to discover weight arrangements where the **bit significance aligns with the task structure**—the most important information must reside in the top 2 bits, the next most important in bits 3-4, and the fine-grained details in bits 5-8.

Prior work on multi-scale training (slimmable networks, Matryoshka representation learning, MatFormer) uses nested structures as a way to get flexible deployment. The regularization benefit is present but secondary—it's usually framed as "the larger sub-network regularizes the smaller one" in a general sense. MatQuant makes the regularization **explicitly about the bit hierarchy**, and the paper demonstrates its power by comparing MatQuant to Single Precision MatQuant: MatQuant (with all three loss terms) achieves 63.35% int2 accuracy, while Single Precision MatQuant (int2 loss only) achieves 64.02% (Table 5). The int8 and int4 loss terms slightly constrain the optimizer, reducing the peak int2 performance by 0.67%, but they buy the ability to serve int4 and int8 models. This is a clean demonstration of a **regularization-accuracy trade-off in bit space**: the multi-scale objective trades a small amount of int2 performance for a large amount of deployment flexibility.

The paper also provides mechanistic evidence for this regularization through the weight distribution analysis (Figure 1c). MatQuant's training shifts the quantized weight distribution to the right—more weights occupy higher-valued int2 bins. This is the optimizer's response to the multi-scale objective: to make the int2 model (with only 4 bins) as informative as possible, it pushes weights into higher-magnitude bins, which are more discriminative. The int8 and int4 models, with their larger bin counts, can absorb this shift because they have enough representational capacity to compensate for a slightly suboptimal bin distribution. The regularization is therefore asymmetric: it disproportionately benefits the most constrained precision (int2) at minimal cost to the less constrained ones (int4, int8).

This is a **diagnostic contribution** as much as a methodological one. It transforms our understanding of why certain quantization approaches fail at low bit-widths: it's not just that 2 bits are inherently insufficient, but that standard training doesn't organize the weight bits in a way that makes those 2 bits carry maximal information. The remedy is not a better quantization formula but a training objective that explicitly values bit-level hierarchical structure.

### Innovation 3: The Discovery That a Single Extra Bit Captures Outliers at Low Precision

Section 7 (Errata) documents what initially appears to be a bug but reveals a genuinely insightful finding: **at extremely low precisions like int2, adding a single extra bit—used sparsely and only for outlier values—provides a disproportionate accuracy gain (~5-6%) that far exceeds what the bit budget would naively suggest**.

The "bug" was omitting the clamping operation in the slicing operator (Equation 8 vs. Equation 6). This allows the rounding step to occasionally produce a value of $2^r$ (e.g., 4 for a 2-bit model) instead of clamping it to $2^r-1$ (3). The result is a model that uses $2^r + 1$ possible values instead of $2^r$, requiring an effective bit-width of $r + \epsilon$ (e.g., 2.05 bits on average, as shown in Table 7 for Gemma-2 9B: 2.050 bits). The fraction of parameters that use this extra bucket is "very small" (Section 7), yet the accuracy jump is dramatic: Gemma-2 9B int2 under Extra Precision MatQuant reaches 68.52% vs. 63.35% for standard MatQuant (Table 30)—a +5.17% gain for roughly 0.05 additional bits per parameter.

This finding is significant because it provides **empirical evidence for a long-hypothesized phenomenon in quantization**: that the primary obstacle to extreme compression is not the average-case information loss but the worst-case loss on outlier weights. Works like LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022), SpQR (Dettmers et al., 2023), and SqueezeLLM (Kim et al., 2024) have argued that a small fraction of outlier weights must be kept at higher precision, while the bulk of weights can be aggressively compressed. MatQuant's Extra Precision variant validates this hypothesis at int2: a single additional representational value (the 5th bucket in a nominally 2-bit model) is sufficient to capture the weight outliers that would otherwise be catastrophically distorted by forcing them into one of the 4 int2 bins.

What makes this a **diagnostic insight** rather than just a bug report is the asymmetry: the benefit of the extra bucket is enormous at int2 but negligible at int4 and int8 (Table 7: Gemma-2 9B int4 goes from 73.83% to 73.88%, a 0.05% gain). This confirms that the outlier problem is **specific to extremely low bit-widths** where the bin resolution is so coarse that a single outlier can dominate a quantization group's scaling factor and distort all other weights in that group. At int4 (16 bins) and int8 (256 bins), the existing bins provide enough range to accommodate outliers without severe collateral damage.

The paper's reframing—calling this "Extra Precision" rather than treating it as a bug—is itself a conceptual contribution. It suggests that future low-bit quantization formats should support **sparse precision extensions**: a base representation at the target bit-width (e.g., 2 bits for 99% of weights) with occasional extra-bit flags for outlier values. The paper hypothesizes that "even a single bit is enough to capture several of these outliers," which opens up hardware-software co-design questions about how to efficiently support such sparse-precision formats. Current hardware supports uniform precision (int2, int4, int8); MatQuant's result suggests that supporting **near-uniform precision with sparse exceptions** could unlock significant accuracy gains at minimal hardware cost.

The co-distillation results in Table 8 reinforce this: applying knowledge distillation from the int8 model to the 2.050-bit model (Extra Precision) yields 70.17% accuracy (up from 68.52% without distillation), suggesting that the extra bucket is most effective when the optimizer is explicitly told to match the high-precision model's behavior on the outlier weights.

### Innovation 4: Free Interpolative Precision Extraction as Evidence of Learned Bit Hierarchy

The paper demonstrates that models trained on {8, 4, 2} bits can be sliced to previously unseen precisions like int6 and int3 with accuracy matching explicitly trained baselines (Tables 1-2). This "interpolative behavior" is not just a convenient property—it is **evidence that MatQuant's training induces a genuine hierarchical structure in the bit representation**, where each additional bit of precision adds information in a roughly monotonic way.

Prior multi-scale methods (slimmable networks, Matryoshka embeddings) typically only work at the specific scales they were trained for. A slimmable network trained at widths {0.25×, 0.5×, 1.0×} does not guarantee good performance at 0.375×—you would need to train that width explicitly or accept suboptimal results. MatQuant's ability to extract a functional int3 model from {8, 4, 2} training without ever seeing a 3-bit loss term means the optimizer has learned something stronger than just "make the 2-MSB and 4-MSB views good." It has learned to arrange all 8 bits in an approximately **monotonic importance ordering**, where bit 7 is most critical, bit 6 next, and so on down to bit 0. When you take 3 MSBs, you get the three most important bits, which naturally form a good 3-bit model.

This is a **structural discovery about optimizer behavior under nested objectives**. The optimizer, faced with loss terms at {8, 4, 2}, could in principle find a solution where bits 5-8 are terrible but bits 1-4 somehow compensate for the int8 loss—satisfying the {8, 4, 2} constraints without producing a smooth hierarchy. That it doesn't—that int6 and int3 work well—suggests that smooth hierarchical solutions are a strong attractor in the optimization landscape when the loss terms are nested. This is a non-trivial empirical finding with theoretical implications for understanding how neural networks represent information across bit levels.

The practical significance is substantial: it means that one training run can produce an essentially continuous spectrum of precision-quality trade-offs through pure post-hoc slicing, without the combinatorial cost of training every possible bit-width. Combined with Mix'n'Match (different precisions per layer), the number of extractable models is combinatorial, all from a single stored checkpoint. This transforms the deployment question from "which precision should I quantize to?" into "given my hardware constraints, what slicing and mixing strategy gives me the best accuracy?"—a much more flexible and cost-effective paradigm.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses the C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2020) for both training/calibration and evaluation. For OmniQuant experiments, 128 examples with a sequence length of 2048 are sampled for training, totaling 262,144 tokens (10M tokens for all models except the int2 baseline, which uses 20M tokens following Shao et al., 2023). For QAT experiments, a fixed set of 100M tokens from C4 is sampled for FFN-only quantization, and 300M tokens for FFN + Attention quantization. Perplexity is computed on the C4 validation set, while downstream accuracy is measured across six tasks: ARC-c, ARC-e (Clark et al., 2018), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), and Winogrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020). The choice of C4 for calibration rather than task-specific data is deliberate: it tests whether the quantization method generalizes beyond the calibration distribution.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments use three Transformer-based LLMs: Gemma-2 2B, Gemma-2 9B (Gemma-Team, 2024), and Mistral 7B (Jiang et al., 2023). These span a range of model scales (2B to 9B parameters) and architectures (Gemma and Mistral families), providing evidence that MatQuant is not specific to one model design. The paper focuses primarily on quantizing FFN parameters ("weight only quantization within the parameter-intensive FFN blocks," Section 4), justified by the FFN being the dominant latency block in autoregressive decoding. Extension to all Transformer weights (FFN + Attention) appears in Section 5.3 and Table 6.

- **Metrics.** Two complementary metrics are reported throughout: (1) **Task Average** — the mean zero-shot accuracy across the six downstream tasks listed above, providing a direct measure of how quantization affects the model's practical capabilities; (2) **log perplexity (log pplx)** — the log perplexity on the C4 validation set, measuring the model's language modeling quality under quantization. Task average is reported as a percentage (%, higher is better), while log perplexity is a unitless scalar (lower is better). Using both metrics guards against the possibility that quantization creates models with good language modeling scores but poor downstream behavior (or vice versa) — a known failure mode in quantization research.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against four baselines for each precision level:
  - **Sliced int8 (OmniQuant or QAT):** An int8 model trained with the base algorithm, then naively sliced to the target bit-width using the operator in Equation 6. This tests whether the nested structure is naturally preserved without multi-scale training — it is not, as shown by the dramatic accuracy collapse at int2.
  - **Baseline (explicitly trained):** A model trained independently for the specific target precision using the same base algorithm (OmniQuant or QAT) with standard single-precision optimization. This is the primary competitive baseline and represents the current best practice for producing a model at a given bit-width.
  - **bfloat16:** The full-precision model with no quantization, serving as an upper bound on achievable accuracy.
  - **Majority voting / other baselines:** The paper does not use majority voting or other selection-based baselines, as quantization quality is measured on individual forward passes.
  The "Baseline" models are trained with the same hyperparameters as the MatQuant models (same data, same number of tokens) to ensure fair comparison, with the exception that the int2 Baseline is trained for 20M tokens (vs. 10M for other precisions) following the recommendation of Shao et al. (2023) that int2 benefits from longer optimization.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Unlike LLM sampling papers, this work does not use "generations" as a compute metric. Compute is measured indirectly through **effective bits per parameter** for the Mix'n'Match experiments (Section 4.3, Figure 2), calculated as the average bit-width across FFN layers when different layers use different precisions. All main experiments measure quality at fixed bit-widths (8, 4, 2 bits per FFN parameter), so the comparison is at equal storage/memory cost. Training cost is reported in tokens processed (10M, 20M, 100M, 300M tokens) and hardware is specified (TPUv5e chips). The paper does not attempt to account for the cost of difficulty estimation or any other pre-processing — because quantization is a one-time offline cost, not a per-query inference cost, the relevant comparison is storage and latency at deployment time, which is proportional to bit-width.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no cross-validation or statistical significance testing reported for the main results. The paper reports single-run numbers averaged across six downstream tasks, without confidence intervals or multiple training seeds. This is standard practice in the quantization literature (OmniQuant, GPTQ, AWQ, etc. all report single runs) but limits the ability to assess whether observed differences (e.g., 63.35% vs. 60.24% for Gemma-2 9B int2) are statistically robust or within run-to-run variance. The Mix'n'Match experiments (Section 4.3) do explore hundreds of layer-wise precision configurations, and the reported "best" models are presumably selected post-hoc from this search, which introduces a selection bias — the Mix'n'Match curves in Figures 2 and 3 should be interpreted as the Pareto frontier of achievable performance, not as a single-model result.

### Main Quantitative Results

The results are organized around the two base quantization algorithms (OmniQuant and QAT) and three model scales, with additional experiments on interpolation, Mix'n'Match, and extensions to all Transformer weights.

---

#### MatQuant with OmniQuant (Section 4.1, Table 1)

**Headline result:** MatQuant + OmniQuant produces int8 and int4 models within 0.5% of independently trained baselines while generating int3 and int6 models for free (via slicing) that match explicitly trained baselines. For int2, MatQuant outperforms the baseline by 1.04–3.11 percentage points depending on the model.

**Table 1** presents the full results across Gemma-2 2B, Gemma-2 9B, and Mistral 7B, with Task Average (%, higher is better) and log perplexity (lower is better) at each precision.

**Int8 results:**
- Gemma-2 2B: Baseline 68.25% → MatQuant 68.02% (−0.23%)
- Gemma-2 9B: Baseline 74.59% → MatQuant 74.05% (−0.54%)
- Mistral 7B: Baseline 73.77% → MatQuant 73.65% (−0.12%)

The int8 MatQuant models are essentially quality-neutral relative to the explicit int8 baselines. The gaps are small (≤0.5%) and within what could plausibly be run-to-run variance. Log perplexity shows similarly small degradation: Gemma-2 9B goes from 2.418 to 2.438 log pplx. The int8 baseline is close to bfloat16 for all models (e.g., Gemma-2 9B bfloat16: 74.38% vs. int8 Baseline: 74.59% — the quantized model slightly exceeds the full-precision one, a known phenomenon where quantization can have a regularizing effect).

**Int4 results:**
- Gemma-2 2B: Baseline 67.03% → MatQuant 66.58% (−0.45%)
- Gemma-2 9B: Baseline 74.33% → MatQuant 73.83% (−0.50%)
- Mistral 7B: Baseline 73.62% → MatQuant 73.06% (−0.56%)

Again, int4 MatQuant is within 0.5% of the explicit baseline across all models. Log perplexity degradation is modest: Gemma-2 9B from 2.451 to 2.491. The "Sliced int8" baseline (slicing the int8 model to int4 without multi-scale training) shows a larger drop: Gemma-2 9B 72.26% (vs. 74.33% baseline) — a ~2-point gap, demonstrating that standard int8 training does not preserve int4 quality in the MSBs, though the degradation is much less severe than at int2.

**Int2 results — the headline gain:**
- Gemma-2 2B: Baseline 51.33% → MatQuant 52.37% (+1.04%)
- Gemma-2 9B: Baseline 60.24% → MatQuant 63.35% (+3.11%)
- Mistral 7B: Baseline 59.74% → MatQuant 62.75% (+3.01%)

This is the paper's primary quantitative claim: MatQuant substantially outperforms explicit int2 training. The gains range from 1–3 percentage points across models and are consistent across both Task Average and log perplexity (e.g., Gemma-2 9B log pplx: 3.292 → 3.187). The "Sliced int8" baseline collapses: Gemma-2 9B 38.11%, Mistral 7B 37.29% — roughly 22–25 points worse than MatQuant and near-random performance. This quantifies just how poorly standard int8 training organizes the MSBs for 2-bit extraction: the int8 model's top 2 bits carry essentially no information about the task.

**Int6 and int3 interpolation results (bottom of Table 1):**
- Int6: MatQuant (sliced) vs. explicit Baseline is within ~0.5% for all models (e.g., Gemma-2 9B: MatQuant 73.92% vs. Baseline 74.23%). The model was never trained with an int6 loss term, yet slicing to int6 matches explicit training.
- Int3: MatQuant (sliced) vs. explicit Baseline is also within ~0.5–1% (e.g., Gemma-2 9B: MatQuant 72.87% vs. Baseline 73.23%). Again, no int3 training, but the interpolation works.
- Compare these to "Sliced int8": int3 accuracy from a standard int8 model is 54.18% (Gemma-2 9B) — a 19-point gap vs. MatQuant's 72.87%. The same pattern holds for all models: MatQuant's interpolation is not just slightly better, it's a qualitative shift from "broken" to "functional."

**Key observations from Table 1:**
- The gains are largest at int2, moderate at int3/int4/int6 (where MatQuant matches baselines), and negligible at int8. This follows the "regularization benefit" pattern: the most constrained precision benefits most from multi-scale training.
- The gap between "Sliced int8" and MatQuant grows as bit-width decreases: 2 points at int4, ~15 points at int3, ~22 points at int2. MatQuant's training explicitly forces the optimizer to make the MSBs informative at coarse granularities.
- Log perplexity trends mirror Task Average, confirming the improvements are not an artifact of the downstream task selection.
- Mistral 7B int4 "Sliced int8" shows a catastrophic 38.51% accuracy — far worse than the 72.26% for Gemma-2 9B — suggesting model-specific sensitivity to how int8 training arranges the MSBs. MatQuant recovers this to 73.06%.

---

#### MatQuant with QAT (Section 4.2, Table 2)

**Headline result:** MatQuant + QAT shows the same pattern as OmniQuant but with larger int2 gains (4.46–7.02 percentage points) and a notable caveat: QAT overfits to the C4 calibration data, improving log perplexity while sometimes degrading downstream task accuracy compared to OmniQuant.

**Table 2** mirrors Table 1's structure with QAT as the base algorithm.

**Int8 results:**
- Gemma-2 2B: Baseline 67.82% → MatQuant 67.44% (−0.38%)
- Gemma-2 9B: Baseline 74.17% → MatQuant 74.52% (+0.35%)
- Mistral 7B: Baseline 73.48% → MatQuant 72.58% (−0.90%)

MatQuant matches or slightly trails the QAT baselines at int8, with the Mistral 7B showing a larger drop than with OmniQuant. Log perplexity shows improvement over bfloat16 for all models (e.g., Gemma-2 9B bfloat16: 2.418 → QAT int8: 2.29) — QAT is compressing the model in a way that reduces perplexity, but this doesn't always translate to downstream accuracy.

**Int4 results:**
- Gemma-2 2B: Baseline 67.03% → MatQuant 66.59% (−0.44%)
- Gemma-2 9B: Baseline 73.26% → MatQuant 73.24% (−0.02%)
- Mistral 7B: Baseline 72.13% → MatQuant 71.99% (−0.14%)

Int4 MatQuant with QAT is essentially identical to baselines. The "Sliced int8" baseline does better with QAT than with OmniQuant (e.g., Gemma-2 9B: 73.36% sliced vs. 73.26% baseline), suggesting QAT's direct weight optimization partially preserves the MSB structure even without explicit multi-scale training.

**Int2 results — the largest gains in the paper:**
- Gemma-2 2B: Baseline 47.74% → MatQuant 52.20% (+4.46%)
- Gemma-2 9B: Baseline 56.02% → MatQuant 62.29% (+6.27%)
- Mistral 7B: Baseline 54.95% → MatQuant 61.97% (+7.02%)

These are substantially larger gains than with OmniQuant (+4–7% vs. +1–3%). The QAT int2 baselines are worse than their OmniQuant counterparts (e.g., Gemma-2 9B QAT baseline: 56.02% vs. OmniQuant baseline: 60.24%), suggesting QAT struggles more with direct int2 optimization — and MatQuant's multi-scale training compensates more effectively. The gap between MatQuant QAT and MatQuant OmniQuant at int2 is small (e.g., Gemma-2 9B: 62.29% QAT vs. 63.35% OmniQuant), meaning MatQuant effectively closes the QAT disadvantage at int2.

**Int6 and int3 interpolation:**
- Int6: MatQuant again matches baselines within ~0.5% for all models.
- Int3: MatQuant matches baselines within ~1% (e.g., Gemma-2 9B: MatQuant 70.41% vs. Baseline 69.9% — MatQuant slightly exceeds the baseline). The "Sliced int8" int3 is much worse (Gemma-2 9B: 68.70% vs. MatQuant 70.41%), though the gap is smaller than with OmniQuant.

**Overfitting observation:** The paper explicitly notes in Section 4.2:

> "While OmniQuant only trains the auxiliary parameters needed for quantization, QAT also updates the weight parameters. This potentially results in severe overfitting to the C4 subset used in the experiments."

Evidence: QAT improves log perplexity dramatically (e.g., Gemma-2 9B int2: 2.660 for MatQuant QAT vs. 3.187 for MatQuant OmniQuant) but downstream task accuracy does not correspondingly improve (Gemma-2 9B int2: 62.29% QAT vs. 63.35% OmniQuant). The paper warns that "users are better off using resource-friendly methods like OmniQuant" unless high-quality diverse training data is available — a pragmatically honest assessment that strengthens credibility.

#### Layerwise Mix'n'Match (Section 4.3, Figure 2)

**Headline result:** By assigning different precisions (int8, int4, int2) to different FFN layers, MatQuant produces hundreds of models with effective bit-widths between 2 and 8 bits, densely spanning the accuracy-vs-cost trade-off, all extracted from a single trained checkpoint with zero additional training.

**Figure 2** plots the Pareto frontier for Gemma-2 9B trained with MatQuant + OmniQuant. The x-axis is "Effective bits per FFN parameter" (the weighted average of layer precisions), and the y-axis is Task Average. The key comparison points are:
- The **Baseline** curve (points at 2, 4, 8 bits): explicit single-precision OmniQuant models.
- The **MatQuant** curve (same points): the homogeneous sliced models from MatQuant.
- The **MatQuant-Interp.** curve (points at 3, 6 bits): sliced interpolation to untrained precisions.
- The **Mix'n'Match** curve (dense scatter): heterogeneous layer precision assignments.

The Mix'n'Match curve densely populates the region between 2 and 8 bits. Critically, at sub-4-bit effective widths, Mix'n'Match models outperform the homogeneous 4-bit sliced model. For example, a Mix'n'Match configuration with an effective 3.5 bits per FFN parameter achieves higher accuracy than the 4-bit homogeneous MatQuant model. This means a deployment with a memory budget below 4 bits per parameter can achieve better accuracy by mixing int2 and int4 layers than by uniformly using int4.

The paper explores four Mix'n'Match strategies (Appendix B): Pyramid (int2 at start/end layers, int8 in middle, int4 in between), Reverse Pyramid (int8 at start/end, int2 in middle), Increasing, and Decreasing. The Pyramid strategy "consistently outperformed the others," with the hypothesis that middle layers do the deepest semantic processing and benefit most from higher precision. All models shown are post-hoc extractions — the training itself is unchanged, only the layer-wise slicing decisions at inference time differ.

**Figure 3** (in the Errata, Section 7) shows the equivalent Mix'n'Match curve for Extra Precision MatQuant on Gemma-2 9B. Here, the int2 model has an effective 2.050 bits (due to the unclamped slicing bug allowing an extra bucket for some weights). The Mix'n'Match curve with Extra Precision MatQuant is Pareto-optimal — it dominates the baseline at nearly all bit-widths, and "sometimes even improving on the bfloat16 model accuracy." This is a striking result: a mixed-precision model with less than 8 bits per parameter can exceed the full-precision model's accuracy, likely because the quantization acts as a beneficial regularizer (similar to how int8 models sometimes outperform bfloat16) while the mixed precision preserves critical information in key layers.

#### Weighting Ablation for MatQuant (Section 5.1, Table 3)

**Headline result:** Higher relative weight for $λ_2$ is essential for good int2 performance; increasing $λ_4$ and $λ_8$ to improve int8/int4 often degrades int2, revealing a direct trade-off in the multi-scale objective.

**Table 3** sweeps the loss weights $(λ_8, λ_4, λ_2)$ across four configurations for Gemma-2 2B and 9B, and Mistral 7B, with OmniQuant as the base. The paper's default is $(0.1, 0.1, 1.0)$ for Gemma models. The sweep tests $(0.1, 0.1, 1.0)$, $(0.2, 0.2, 1.0)$, $(0.3, 0.3, 1.0)$, and $(0.4, 0.4, 1.0)$ — progressively increasing the int8 and int4 weights relative to int2 (which stays fixed at 1.0).

**Gemma-2 9B results (representative):**
- **Int8**: accuracy varies from 74.05% at $(0.1, 0.1, 1.0)$ to 73.84% at $(0.4, 0.4, 1.0)$ — a 0.21% decline as int8 weight increases. Counterintuitively, increasing int8's loss weight does not reliably improve int8 accuracy; the relationship is non-monotonic.
- **Int4**: accuracy is fairly stable, ranging from 73.83% at $(0.1, 0.1, 1.0)$ to 74.32% at $(0.4, 0.4, 1.0)$ — a 0.49% improvement. Higher int4 weight does help int4, modestly.
- **Int2**: accuracy drops from 63.35% at $(0.1, 0.1, 1.0)$ to 61.98% at $(0.4, 0.4, 1.0)$ — a 1.37% decline. The int2 model is clearly sensitive to how much the optimizer prioritizes higher precisions.
- **Optimal per-precision weights:** The best int8 comes at $(0.1, 0.1, 1.0)$ (74.05%), the best int4 at $(0.4, 0.4, 1.0)$ (74.32%), and the best int2 at $(0.3, 0.3, 1.0)$ (64.10%) — different configurations optimize different precisions. The paper's default $(0.1, 0.1, 1.0)$ represents a "balanced" choice that favors int2 while keeping int8/int4 acceptable.

**Gemma-2 2B and Mistral 7B** show similar patterns (full results in Appendix Tables 15, 16, 17). The key qualitative finding is that the $λ$ weights provide a controllable knob for trading off accuracy between precision levels — a practitioner who primarily cares about int2 can use $(0, 0, 1)$ (Single Precision MatQuant), while one who needs all three can tune $(λ_8, λ_4, λ_2)$ to balance the objectives. This is a practical design lever, not just an ablation curiosity.

---

#### Co-distillation Ablation (Section 5.2, Table 4)

**Headline result:** Adding knowledge distillation from the int8 model to the lower-precision nested models improves int2 accuracy by up to 0.97% with OmniQuant, while minimally affecting int8 and int4.

**Table 4** tests four distillation configurations on Gemma-2 9B with both OmniQuant and QAT:
- `[8, 4, 2]`: standard MatQuant with no distillation (baseline).
- `[8, 4, 8 → 2]`: int8 and int4 losses, plus distillation from int8 to int2.
- `[8, 4, 2, 8 → 2]`: all three direct losses, plus int8-to-int2 distillation.
- `[8, 4, 2, 8 → 4; 2]`: all three direct losses, plus distillation from int8 to both int4 and int2.

**OmniQuant results:**
- **Int8**: ranges from 74.05% (no distillation) to 72.76% (`[8, 4, 8 → 2]`) — adding distillation while removing the int2 direct loss drops int8 accuracy.
- **Int4**: ranges from 73.83% to 72.65% — similar pattern; distillation doesn't help int4.
- **Int2**: baseline 63.35%; `[8, 4, 2, 8 → 4; 2]` achieves 64.32% — a +0.97% gain. The configuration that distills from int8 to both int4 and int2 (while keeping all direct losses) gives the best int2 result.
- Log perplexity: int2 improves from 3.187 to 3.227 — slightly worse perplexity but better downstream accuracy, another instance of the perplexity-accuracy decoupling.

**QAT results:**
- **Int8**: baseline 74.52%; best configuration 74.87% (`[8, 4, 2, 8 → 2]`), a small gain.
- **Int2**: baseline 62.29%; best configuration 62.70% (`[8, 4, 2, 8 → 2]`), a +0.41% gain — smaller than with OmniQuant.

The key finding is that co-distillation specifically benefits the most constrained precision (int2), consistent with the paper's broader narrative that lower bit-widths benefit disproportionately from additional training signal. The distillation provides a softer, higher-quality target than the ground-truth labels alone, and int2 — with its severe representational bottleneck — gains the most from this improved supervision.

**Table 8** (in the Errata) extends co-distillation to Extra Precision MatQuant (2.050 bits effective), showing even larger gains: Gemma-2 9B int2 goes from 68.52% (no distillation) to 70.17% (`[8, 4, 2, 8 → 2]`), a +1.65% gain. The extra bucket for outliers makes the distillation signal even more valuable.

---

#### Single Precision MatQuant (Section 5.3, Table 5)

**Headline result:** Training with only the int2 loss but using int8 as the intermediate representation (Single Precision MatQuant, $R = \{2\}$) outperforms both the explicit int2 baseline and sometimes the full MatQuant with $R = \{8, 4, 2\}$, confirming that high-bit optimization with low-bit projection is fundamentally better than direct low-bit optimization.

**Table 5** compares int2 performance across three methods: Baseline (explicit int2 training), MatQuant (multi-scale {8, 4, 2}), and Single Precision MatQuant (MatQuant with $λ_8 = λ_4 = 0, λ_2 = 1$).

**OmniQuant results:**
- Gemma-2 2B: Baseline 51.33%, MatQuant 52.37%, S.P. MatQuant **53.42%** (+2.09% over baseline, +1.05% over MatQuant)
- Gemma-2 9B: Baseline 60.24%, MatQuant 63.35%, S.P. MatQuant **64.02%** (+3.78% over baseline, +0.67% over MatQuant)
- Mistral 7B: Baseline 59.74%, MatQuant 62.75%, S.P. MatQuant **63.58%** (+3.84% over baseline, +0.83% over MatQuant)

**QAT results:**
- Gemma-2 2B: Baseline 47.74%, MatQuant 52.20%, S.P. MatQuant 52.08% (−0.12% vs. MatQuant, still +4.34% over baseline)
- Gemma-2 9B: Baseline 56.02%, MatQuant 62.29%, S.P. MatQuant **62.66%** (+6.64% over baseline, +0.37% over MatQuant)
- Mistral 7B: Baseline 54.95%, MatQuant 61.97%, S.P. MatQuant 61.48% (−0.49% vs. MatQuant, still +6.53% over baseline)

The QAT results are mixed — Single Precision MatQuant beats MatQuant on Gemma-2 9B but slightly trails on Gemma-2 2B and Mistral 7B — suggesting the multi-scale regularization from int4/int8 losses sometimes helps int2 convergence in QAT (perhaps by stabilizing training, as noted in Section 4.2's overfitting discussion).

The critical finding is that **every** Single Precision MatQuant result dramatically exceeds the explicit int2 baseline. The smallest improvement is +2.09% (Gemma-2 2B OmniQuant); the largest is +6.64% (Gemma-2 9B QAT). This is the paper's strongest evidence that the "optimize at high precision, evaluate at low precision" paradigm is fundamentally superior to direct low-bit optimization.

**Tables 23 and 24** (Appendix G) reveal the cost: the int4 and int8 models extracted from Single Precision MatQuant are degraded. Gemma-2 9B OmniQuant: int8 drops from 74.59% (Baseline) to 73.80% (S.P. MatQuant sliced to int8); int4 drops from 74.33% to 73.01%. The optimizer, freed from int4/int8 constraints, sacrifices those precisions to squeeze maximum performance from the int2 view.

---

#### FFN + Attention Quantization (Section 5.3, Table 6)

**Headline result:** When quantizing all Transformer weights (not just FFN), baseline QAT becomes unstable at int2 and int3 and fails to converge, while MatQuant and Single Precision MatQuant produce functional models, demonstrating that multi-scale training stabilizes low-bit optimization in large parameter spaces.

**Table 6** extends QAT to FFN + Attention parameters for Gemma-2 9B and Mistral 7B.

**Int8 and int4:** MatQuant performs on par with baselines. Gemma-2 9B int8: Baseline 74.61%, MatQuant 74.85% (+0.24%). Int4: Baseline 72.98%, MatQuant 74.01% (+1.03%). The "Sliced int8" baseline is reasonable at these precisions.

**Int2:** The baseline QAT entry is marked with a dash ("—"), indicating training failure. The paper states:

> "we found int2 and int3 to be very unstable while quantizing both, the FFN and the Attention parameters. ... In the naive setup of minimizing the loss with respect to the ground truth, we find QAT to be very unstable at lower precisions."

Single Precision MatQuant achieves 45.69% (Gemma-2 9B) — far below the FFN-only results but functional. MatQuant achieves 44.19%. While these accuracies are poor relative to FFN-only quantization (~63%), the key point is that MatQuant **enables** int2 quantization of full Transformer weights where the baseline **fails entirely**. The stabilization likely comes from the higher-precision loss terms providing gradient signal that prevents divergence.

**Int3:** Same pattern: baseline fails ("—"), Single Precision MatQuant achieves 67.68% (Gemma-2 9B), MatQuant achieves 63.63%. Again, MatQuant makes training possible where the baseline cannot converge.

**Int6:** MatQuant matches baseline: Gemma-2 9B 74.57% vs. 74.65%.

The results reinforce the paper's narrative that multi-scale training is not just about deployment flexibility — it's a **training stabilization technique** for low-bit regimes, particularly when the parameter space is large (all Transformer weights) and the optimization landscape is poorly conditioned.

#### Extra Precision MatQuant (Section 7, Tables 7, 8)

**Headline result:** The unclamped slicing operator (Equation 8, a bug in the initial draft) creates a 2.050-bit model that dramatically outperforms the properly clamped 2-bit model (+5.17% for Gemma-2 9B with OmniQuant), revealing that a single extra bit for outlier representation provides disproportionate accuracy gains at extreme low precision.

**Table 7** compares MatQuant (clamped, Equation 6) with Extra Precision MatQuant (unclamped, Equation 8) for Gemma-2 2B, 9B, and Mistral 7B with OmniQuant. The "Avg. Bits" column shows the effective bit-width: 8, 4.022, 2.050, 6.018, and 3.029 bits for Extra Precision vs. exactly 8, 4, 2, 6, 3 for standard MatQuant.

**Gemma-2 9B results (representative):**
- **Int8:** MatQuant 74.05% vs. Extra Precision 73.97% (−0.08%) — negligible difference.
- **Int4 (4.022 bits):** MatQuant 73.83% vs. Extra Precision 73.88% (+0.05%) — negligible.
- **Int2 (2.050 bits):** MatQuant 63.35% vs. Extra Precision **68.52%** (+5.17%) — dramatic gain.
- **Int6 (6.018 bits):** MatQuant 73.92% vs. Extra Precision 74.21% (+0.29%) — small.
- **Int3 (3.029 bits):** MatQuant 72.87% vs. Extra Precision 72.93% (+0.06%) — negligible.

The pattern is clear: the extra bucket matters enormously at int2, but its benefit rapidly diminishes at higher precisions. This precisely quantifies the outlier hypothesis: at int2, the 4 available bins are so coarse that a small number of outlier weights would be catastrophically distorted, and the single extra bucket captures them; at int4 (16 bins) and above, there are enough bins to accommodate outliers without the extra bucket.

**Log perplexity:** Int2 improves from 3.187 to 2.823 — a 0.364 reduction, consistent with the accuracy gain.

**Table 8** adds co-distillation to Extra Precision MatQuant: the 2.050-bit model reaches 70.17% with the `[8, 4, 2, 8 → 2]` configuration — a +6.82% gain over the standard MatQuant int2 of 63.35% (and +1.65% over Extra Precision MatQuant without distillation).

The paper hypothesizes:

> "this additional bucket helps with capturing the outliers and thus leads to a significant performance boost. As highlighted by recent work (Dettmers et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024), it is crucial to store certain outliers full precision. Interestingly, we show that even a single bit is enough to capture several of these outliers, especially for low bit quantization."

This finding is a **serendipitous validation** of the outlier-channel hypothesis from the quantization literature, with the novel twist that a single extra bit (used sparingly) suffices for int2.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Loss re-weighting ($λ_r$):** Sweeping $(λ_8, λ_4, λ_2)$ from (0.1, 0.1, 1.0) to (0.4, 0.4, 1.0) reveals a trade-off: higher int8/int4 weights modestly improve int4 accuracy (+0.49% for Gemma-2 9B OmniQuant from (0.1,0.1,1) to (0.4,0.4,1)) but degrade int2 (−1.37% over the same sweep). The optimal configuration depends on the deployment priority; no single weighting dominates all precisions (Table 3 and Appendix Tables 15–17).

**Co-distillation targets:** Distilling from int8 to int2 provides the largest int2 benefit (+0.97% for OmniQuant, Table 4); distilling to both int4 and int2 helps slightly less than distilling only to int2. Removing the direct int2 loss and relying solely on distillation (`[8, 4, 8 → 2]`) degrades int2 performance relative to keeping both signals — direct supervision matters. The benefit is larger with Extra Precision MatQuant (+1.65%, Table 8).

**Single Precision MatQuant vs. Baseline vs. full MatQuant:** This is the paper's most important ablation (Table 5). It isolates the effect of multi-scale regularization: Single Precision MatQuant (int2 loss only, int8 representation) always massively outperforms the explicit int2 baseline, proving that optimizing in int8 space with int2 projection is superior to optimizing directly in int2 space. Full MatQuant (with int4/int8 losses) sometimes trails Single Precision MatQuant in int2 accuracy (by 0.67% for Gemma-2 9B OmniQuant) but provides functional int4/int8 models — Single Precision MatQuant's int4/int8 are degraded (Appendix Tables 23, 24).

**QAT overfitting:** The paper explicitly notes that QAT improves log perplexity but not always downstream accuracy compared to OmniQuant (Section 4.2). Gemma-2 9B int2: QAT MatQuant log pplx 2.660 vs. OmniQuant MatQuant log pplx 3.187 (QAT is much better), but Task Average: 62.29% vs. 63.35% (OmniQuant is better). This is a negative result that the paper transparently reports, highlighting the risk of overfitting to the C4 calibration subset.

**QAT int2/int3 baseline failure for FFN+Attention:** When quantizing all weights, baseline QAT fails to converge at int2 and int3, while MatQuant and Single Precision MatQuant succeed (Table 6). This is a robustness ablation showing that multi-scale training prevents training collapse in large parameter spaces at extreme low precision.

**Sliced int8 baseline:** This is the key diagnostic baseline appearing in every table. It shows that standard single-precision training does not preserve the nested bit structure: int4 sliced from int8 Omniquant drops ~2% (Gemma-2 9B: 74.59% → 72.26%), int3 drops ~19%, and int2 collapses completely (74.59% → 38.11%). These numbers quantify the problem MatQuant solves.

**Mix'n'Match strategy comparison:** Appendix B compares Pyramid, Reverse Pyramid, Increasing, and Decreasing strategies for layer-wise precision assignment. Pyramid (int2 at start/end, int8 in middle) consistently outperforms alternatives, confirming that middle layers benefit most from higher precision. This is a design choice ablation that validates the intuition about layer importance.

**Extra Precision vs. standard MatQuant across all bit-widths and models:** Table 7 uniformly shows that the extra bucket helps only at int2 (and to a much lesser extent int3), confirming that the outlier problem is specific to extremely low bit-widths. This is a robustness check for the claim that the extra precision matters specifically for outlier-dominated regimes.

**Co-distillation + Extra Precision interaction:** Table 8 shows that co-distillation benefits Extra Precision MatQuant more than standard MatQuant: +1.65% for Extra Precision int2 vs. +0.97% for standard int2 (OmniQuant, Gemma-2 9B). This suggests the outlier-representing extra bucket makes the int2 model better able to leverage the high-precision teacher's signal.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper are thorough for the scope they define — quantization of FFN parameters in 2B–9B LLMs using two learning-based algorithms — but they demonstrate a narrower set of claims than the paper's framing sometimes implies.

**Claim: MatQuant produces int8 and int4 models with comparable accuracy to independently trained baselines.** *What was tested:* Tables 1 and 2 show MatQuant int8/int4 within 0.5% of explicit baselines across three models and two base algorithms, with the occasional larger deviation (Mistral 7B QAT int8: −0.90%). *What was not tested:* Whether "comparable accuracy" holds at larger model scales (13B, 70B, 405B), where quantization dynamics may differ; whether it holds for models with different architectural features (Mixture of Experts, different normalization schemes, different activation functions); whether the int8/int4 models are truly "comparable" under more rigorous statistical testing (confidence intervals, multiple seeds). The claim is supported for the specific setting tested, but the paper's language occasionally generalizes beyond this. The single-run reporting (standard in the quantization literature, but still a limitation) means small differences like 0.5% may be within run-to-run noise.

**Claim: MatQuant significantly outperforms standard int2 quantization (4–7%).** *What was tested:* Tables 1, 2, and 5 robustly demonstrate this across all three models, both base algorithms, and multiple configurations (Single Precision, co-distillation, Extra Precision). The gains range from +1.04% to +7.02% depending on the specific setup. *What was not tested:* Whether these gains hold when int2 is evaluated on tasks beyond the six chosen downstream benchmarks; whether the gains persist under different calibration data distributions (C4 is web text — would code-heavy or math-heavy distributions show the same gains?); whether the gains are due to MatQuant specifically or to the additional effective training signal from multi-scale optimization (a training budget-matched comparison where the baseline is given proportionally more tokens is not performed). The int2 gains are the strongest and most consistently demonstrated result in the paper.

**Claim: Free interpolative extraction of untrained precisions (int6, int3).** *What was tested:* Tables 1 and 2 show sliced int6/int3 matching explicit baselines to within ~1%. *What was not tested:* How this interpolative behavior scales with the gap between trained precisions. The paper trains {8, 4, 2} and tests {6, 3} — gaps of 2 and 1 bits from trained precisions. Would {8, 2} training (skipping int4) still allow good int4 extraction? Would {8, 6, 4, 2} training improve int3/int5/intermediate precision quality? The interpolation claim is supported for the specific granularities tested but the limits of interpolation are unexplored.

**Claim: Mix'n'Match enables dense accuracy-vs-cost trade-offs.** *What was tested:* Figures 2 and 3 show Pareto frontiers from hundreds of layer-wise precision combinations for Gemma-2 9B with OmniQuant. *What was not tested:* Whether the optimal Mix'n'Match configuration transfers across models or must be searched per-model; whether the search procedure (which selects the best post-hoc) introduces selection bias that inflates the apparent Pareto frontier; whether the gains hold under actual hardware deployment (mixing precisions across layers may have unexpected memory alignment or kernel launch overhead). The Mix'n'Match claim is a proof-of-concept demonstration, not an exhaustive characterization.

**Claim: Extra Precision (2.05-bit) models capture outliers and boost int2 performance.** *What was tested:* Table 7 shows ~5% gain for int2 when the clamping is omitted, with minimal effect on higher precisions. *What was not tested:* Whether this extra bucket can be implemented efficiently in hardware; how the sparse extra-bit weights interact with memory layout and cache lines; whether the fraction of weights using the extra bucket can be controlled or predicted. The paper treats this as a serendipitous finding rather than a fully developed technique, and the claims appropriately reflect this.

**Significant weaknesses and omissions:**

1. **Single calibration dataset (C4).** All training and perplexity evaluation uses C4, a web-text corpus. The paper does not test whether MatQuant's benefits are specific to the C4 distribution or generalize to other domains (code, math, multilingual text, conversation). If the calibration data distribution matters — and the paper's own observation about QAT overfitting suggests it does — then the reported numbers may not reflect deployment performance where the inference distribution differs from C4.

2. **No hardware deployment benchmarks.** The paper motivates MatQuant through deployment flexibility and memory-bandwidth arguments but never measures actual inference latency, throughput, or memory usage on hardware. The effective bits-per-parameter metric is a proxy for memory cost, but real deployment involves kernel launch overheads, memory alignment constraints, and the cost of the slicing operation itself (even if negligible, it's unmeasured). A single end-to-end latency number for a MatQuant model served at different precisions would substantially strengthen the practical claims.

3. **The FFN-only scope is both a strength and a limitation.** The paper explicitly focuses on FFN quantization because FFN dominates latency, and this focus enables clear experiments. But the full model results (Table 6) for FFN+Attention show dramatically lower int2 accuracy (45.69% for Gemma-2 9B vs. 64.02% FFN-only with Single Precision MatQuant), suggesting the attention weights are much harder to quantize at low bit-widths. The paper does not investigate why, or whether MatQuant's multi-scale training could be specifically adapted for attention (e.g., different $λ$ weights for attention vs. FFN layers).

4. **No comparison to learning-free methods at matched bit-widths.** The paper compares MatQuant only to the same base algorithm (OmniQuant or QAT) without multi-scale training. It does not compare MatQuant's int2 to, say, GPTQ int2 or QuIP int2, which would contextualize whether MatQuant's gains are relative to a weak baseline or represent genuine state-of-the-art. The Sliced int8 baseline shows that naive slicing fails catastrophically, but that's a low bar — the relevant question is how MatQuant int2 compares to the best available int2 quantization, regardless of method.

5. **Limited model diversity.** Three models from two families (Gemma-2 2B/9B, Mistral 7B) is reasonable but does not establish generality. Llama-family models (which have different weight distributions, different normalization, and different training data) are conspicuously absent, despite being the most widely deployed open-weight LLMs. The paper would be stronger with even a single Llama result.

6. **No statistical reporting.** Single-run results without confidence intervals or error bars make it impossible to assess whether improvements like +0.67% (Single Precision MatQuant vs. MatQuant) are meaningful or within noise. The six-task average provides some robustness to per-task variance, but run-to-run training variance (different random seeds, different C4 samples) is unmeasured.

7. **The "free interpolation" claim overstates the cost.** Extracting int6 and int3 from MatQuant is "free" in the sense of requiring no additional training, but it does require implementing the slicing operation (Equation 6) in the inference pipeline and ensuring the int8 de-quantization parameters are valid for the sliced bit-width. The paper does not discuss whether the same $α$ and $z$ from int8 remain optimal for int6/int3, or whether recalibration would improve interpolation quality. If simple recalibration (like recomputing $α$ for the sliced weights) would help, the interpolation results are a lower bound — but if the int8 scale is genuinely sufficient, that's a stronger claim that should be justified.

**What experiments would have strengthened the paper:**

- **Cross-domain evaluation:** testing MatQuant models on code generation, math reasoning, or multilingual benchmarks to verify that C4-calibrated quantization transfers.
- **Hardware latency benchmarks:** even a single measurement of tokens-per-second for int8/int4/int2 homogeneous and Mix'n'Match configurations on a standard GPU would validate the deployment efficiency claims.
- **Training budget-matched comparison:** the int2 baseline uses 20M tokens vs. MatQuant's 10M tokens — a concession that int2 needs more training. A strong comparison would give the int2 baseline access to the same total optimizer steps as the multi-scale MatQuant model (since MatQuant computes loss at three precisions, it effectively gets 3× the gradient signal per token). Does the int2 baseline catch up if given 30M tokens? The paper doesn't test this.
- **Ablation on parent precision:** the paper uses int8 as the parent. Would int16 as parent further improve int2 (more optimization headroom) or would the gap to int2 become too large (256× more values, the optimizer loses the connection between parent and child)? This would test the limits of the "continuous relaxation" hypothesis.
- **Llama-2 or Llama-3 results:** even a single Llama model would substantially broaden the evidence base and address the most obvious generalizability concern.
- **Multiple random seeds:** even 3 seeds would allow basic statistical testing of whether observed accuracy differences are reliable.

**Overall assessment:** The paper's experiments solidly support its core technical claims for the specific setting studied (2B–9B models, FFN-only quantization, C4 calibration, OmniQuant/QAT base algorithms). The int2 improvements are robust and practically significant, and the interpolative/extractive capabilities are genuinely novel in the LLM quantization context. However, the claims about deployment flexibility and hardware efficiency are aspirational — they follow logically from the architecture but are not empirically validated. The paper is best understood as a **method contribution with strong empirical validation in a controlled setting**, not as a deployment-ready system demonstrated at scale. The transparency about limitations (QAT overfitting, the initial bug, the cost of difficulty estimation — though this paper doesn't use difficulty estimation, the point is about honest self-assessment) strengthens credibility and appropriately scopes the contribution.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Entire Evaluation Rests on a Single Text Corpus (C4) with No Cross-Domain Validation

**The assumption or constraint.** All MatQuant training, calibration, and perplexity evaluation uses the C4 dataset—a web-text corpus consisting primarily of internet pages. The downstream evaluation benchmarks (ARC-c, ARC-e, BoolQ, HellaSwag, PIQA, Winogrande) are standard commonsense reasoning tasks, but they are not diverse domain-shifted tests. The paper does not evaluate MatQuant on code generation, mathematical reasoning, multilingual text, long-form dialogue, or any domain where the weight distribution of the model might interact differently with quantization. The introduction motivates MatQuant through deployment flexibility and latency reduction, which implies diverse real-world usage, yet the evaluation remains entirely within a single calibration distribution.

**The consequence.** The practical risk is **distributional brittleness**: MatQuant's multi-scale training forces the optimizer to arrange the int8 MSBs in a hierarchically informative way, but the "informativeness" of a given bit arrangement is relative to the training distribution. If the C4 calibration data happens to produce weight distributions where the hierarchical structure naturally aligns with quantization-friendly patterns, MatQuant will look excellent—but a practitioner deploying on code-heavy or math-heavy inference workloads may see int2 accuracy collapse far below the reported 63.35% (Gemma-2 9B OmniQuant, Table 1). The paper itself provides indirect evidence for this concern: the QAT experiments (Section 4.2, Table 2) show that QAT **overfits to the C4 subset**, achieving dramatically better log perplexity than OmniQuant (2.660 vs. 3.187 for Gemma-2 9B int2) while producing worse downstream task accuracy (62.29% vs. 63.35%). The paper explicitly acknowledges this: "QAT also updates the weight parameters. This potentially results in severe overfitting to the C4 subset used in the experiments." If the calibration distribution can induce overfitting within the same training paradigm, MatQuant's generalization to inference-time distributions that differ from C4 is entirely unverified.

Additionally, the six downstream tasks are all English-language multiple-choice benchmarks that test commonsense reasoning. They do not stress the model's generative capabilities, factual recall, or multi-step reasoning—all of which are critical for production LLM deployments. A model that scores 63.35% on commonsense QA might produce gibberish on a code completion task when quantized to int2. The paper's evaluation suite cannot distinguish between these scenarios.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The QAT overfitting observation in Section 4.2 and Table 2 is the only direct evidence of calibration-distribution sensitivity. The paper acknowledges this: "users are better off using resource-friendly methods like OmniQuant" unless high-quality diverse data is available. But this warning applies only to the choice between QAT and OmniQuant—the paper does not extend the concern to MatQuant's calibration distribution more broadly. The downstream evaluation results (Tables 1, 2, 9–14, 15–29) show consistent patterns across six tasks, but these six tasks are all drawn from the same commonsense reasoning domain. There is **no evidence whatsoever** on MatQuant's performance under domain shift—no code benchmarks, no math benchmarks, no multilingual evaluations, no retrieval-augmented generation tasks. The paper does not measure this limitation; it is an unacknowledged gap.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation, propose cross-domain evaluation, or even acknowledge the single-corpus scope as a constraint. The calibration pipeline uses C4 exclusively, and all experiments use the same C4-derived calibration data. A practitioner reading the paper would have no way to estimate how much of the reported int2 gain would persist on their specific deployment distribution. The natural mitigation—evaluating MatQuant on a held-out domain (e.g., training on C4, testing on GitHub code or arXiv papers)—is not performed.

---

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Renders Mix'n'Match Deployment Search Impractical for Per-Model Optimization

**The assumption or constraint.** The Mix'n'Match capability (Section 4.3, Figures 2 and 3) is presented as a key deployment advantage: from a single trained MatQuant checkpoint, a practitioner can extract hundreds of layer-wise precision configurations with varying accuracy-vs-cost trade-offs, all at zero additional training cost. However, **identifying the optimal Mix'n'Match configuration for a given hardware budget and accuracy target requires evaluating these hundreds of configurations on a validation set**. The paper's Mix'n'Match curves show the Pareto frontier—the best achievable accuracy for each effective bit-width—but finding that frontier requires exhaustively evaluating layer-wise precision assignments and selecting the best post-hoc.

This is a form of **difficulty estimation cost** analogous to what other test-time compute papers acknowledge. The paper does not account for this search cost in its deployment efficiency claims. A practitioner who wants to deploy a MatQuant model at, say, 3.5 effective bits per FFN parameter must either (a) trust that the Pyramid strategy identified in Appendix B generalizes across models and hardware, or (b) run their own search, which means quantizing and evaluating dozens or hundreds of precision configurations on their validation data.

**The consequence.** The "zero additional training cost" claim for Mix'n'Match is technically true—no gradient steps are needed—but it understates the practical cost of model selection. For a 9B-parameter model with 42 Transformer layers (typical for Gemma-2 9B), each layer can be assigned one of 3 precisions (int8, int4, int2), yielding $3^{42} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{20}$ possible configurations. Even a structured search (e.g., the Pyramid/Reverse Pyramid/Increasing/Decreasing heuristics in Appendix B, plus random sampling) requires evaluating on the order of hundreds of configurations on a validation set. Each evaluation requires slicing the model to the configuration's per-layer precisions (cheap) and running inference on a validation dataset (expensive—potentially hours of GPU time for a 9B model on a full evaluation suite).

Moreover, the optimal Mix'n'Match configuration may be **model-specific and calibration-data-specific**. The paper finds that the Pyramid strategy works best for Gemma-2 9B with OmniQuant, but there is no guarantee this transfers to Mistral 7B, Llama-3 8B, or other architectures. A practitioner deploying a new model family would need to re-run the configuration search, amortizing the development cost across many inference queries only if the deployment volume is high. For low-volume or single-user deployments, the search cost might exceed the inference savings.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no measurement of the Mix'n'Match search cost. Appendix B describes the four strategies explored (Pyramid, Reverse Pyramid, Increasing, Decreasing) but does not report how many total configurations were evaluated, what validation set was used for configuration selection, or how long the search took. The Mix'n'Match curves in Figures 2 and 3 show only the **selected best configurations**—the Pareto frontier—without indicating how many configurations were tested to find them or how sensitive the frontier is to the search budget. The paper does not report whether the optimal configuration for Gemma-2 9B transfers to Gemma-2 2B (same architecture family, different scale), which would partially address the generalizability concern.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates this by identifying a consistent heuristic (Pyramid strategy: int2 at start/end, int8 in middle, int4 in between) that "consistently outperformed the others" (Appendix B). A practitioner could adopt this heuristic without running a full search, accepting that it may be slightly suboptimal. However, the paper does not measure the gap between the Pyramid heuristic and the true Pareto frontier—it's possible that an exhaustively searched frontier would reveal substantially better configurations. The paper suggests no method for cheaply predicting the optimal Mix'n'Match configuration from model properties, calibration data statistics, or a small number of probe evaluations. The limitation is acknowledged only implicitly, through the omission of search cost from any reported metric.

### The Single Model, Single Architecture, Single Quantization Scope Limits Generalizability Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's empirical validation covers three models—Gemma-2 2B, Gemma-2 9B, and Mistral 7B—for FFN-only quantization, with FFN+Attention results in Table 6 only. The authors state they "believe [Gemma-2] is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an assertion, not a demonstrated fact. The model families tested share architectural features: both Gemma-2 and Mistral are dense Transformer models with GeGLU activations in the FFN, pre-norm LayerNorm, and rotary position embeddings. Llama-family models (which use SwiGLU, RMSNorm, and different weight initialization), Mixture-of-Experts architectures, and non-Transformer architectures are not evaluated.

Furthermore, the scope of quantization is limited to weights only, with the primary focus on FFN parameters (Section 4). Activation quantization, KV-cache quantization, and quantization of the embedding layer are not addressed. The paper justifies the FFN focus by noting that the FFN is "the main latency block"—which is true in the sense that FFN parameters dominate total parameter count (~2/3 of a standard Transformer), but attention weight quantization is equally important for memory-constrained deployments, and activation quantization is critical for compute-bound inference scenarios.

**The consequence.** There are multiple failure modes that a practitioner faces when generalizing MatQuant beyond the paper's scope:

- **Architectural sensitivity.** Different activation functions (SwiGLU vs. GeGLU) produce different weight distributions, which affect the quantization error landscape. Models with different normalization placements (post-norm vs. pre-norm) propagate quantization errors differently through layers. Weight tying between embedding and output layers (common in smaller models) creates coupling that MatQuant's per-layer OmniQuant optimization doesn't account for. The paper provides no evidence that the int2 gains (+4–7%) transfer to Llama, Phi, or other widely deployed model families.

- **Scale sensitivity.** The three models tested span 2B to 9B parameters—a 4.5× range. Modern LLM deployment spans a much wider range: from 1B edge models to 405B datacenter models. Quantization behavior at 70B+ scales may differ qualitatively: larger models are often more compressible (overparameterization provides redundancy), but they also exhibit more extreme outlier channels (Dettmers et al., 2022) that could change the outlier-handling dynamics that MatQuant's Extra Precision variant exploits. The paper provides no scaling trend analysis—there's no plot of int2 gain vs. model size, which would help practitioners extrapolate to larger models.

- **FFN-only vs. full-model disparity.** Table 6 reveals that when attention parameters are included, int2 accuracy drops dramatically: Gemma-2 9B Single Precision MatQuant goes from 64.02% (FFN-only, Table 5) to 45.69% (FFN+Attention, Table 6)—a ~18-point collapse. This suggests that attention weight quantization at int2 is fundamentally harder, and MatQuant's multi-scale training does not fully resolve the difficulty. A practitioner who reads the headline int2 numbers (63.35%, Table 1) and assumes they apply to full-model quantization will be severely disappointed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FFN+Attention results in Table 6 are the only evidence of scope sensitivity, and they are stark: baseline QAT fails entirely at int2 and int3 (marked "—"), and even MatQuant struggles to reach usable accuracy (45.69% int2 for Gemma-2 9B). This is measured and reported transparently, but the paper does not analyze *why* attention weights are harder to quantize or whether MatQuant's weighting scheme ($\lambda_r$) could be adapted per-block (different weights for FFN vs. attention) to improve results.

There is no evidence for cross-architecture generalization at all—no Llama results, no MoE evaluation, no results on models with different normalization schemes. The paper's "representative" claim is unsubstantiated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the FFN-only scope explicitly (Section 4: "our primary focus is on weight only quantization within the parameter-intensive FFN blocks") and provides FFN+Attention results as an extension (Section 5.3, Table 6). The QAT instability at low bit-widths for full-model quantization is documented, and MatQuant's stabilization effect is shown. However, the paper does not propose architecture-specific adaptations, evaluate additional model families, or provide guidance for practitioners deploying MatQuant on non-Gemma models. The limitation is partially acknowledged (in scope definition) but the generalizability gap is not explicitly discussed as a limitation.

---

### Hardware Deployment Reality Is Unaddressed: Latency, Memory Alignment, and Kernel Support

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper motivates MatQuant entirely through deployment efficiency—Section 1 frames the problem as the "communication cost of transferring model weights from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) to the SRAM," and Section 3.2.1 discusses deploying MatQuant models in environments with different hardware constraints. However, **no hardware measurements are reported**. The paper uses "effective bits per parameter" as a proxy for memory cost and "accuracy vs. bits" trade-off curves as a proxy for deployment quality, but never measures actual inference latency, throughput, memory footprint, or energy consumption on real hardware.

This is a significant gap because the deployment claims rest on assumptions that may not hold in practice:

- **Slicing overhead.** Extracting an int2 model from an int8 model requires the bit-slicing operation $S(q_c, r)$ (Equation 6). While this is argued to be cheap (bitwise shifts, masking, rounding), the paper provides no measurements. In a latency-critical autoregressive decoding loop, even small per-layer overheads accumulate across 42 layers × hundreds of tokens. If slicing must be performed dynamically (e.g., per-query precision selection), the cost may be non-trivial.

- **Memory alignment.** int2, int3, and int6 are not natively supported by most hardware's memory subsystems. GPUs and TPUs read memory in chunks (e.g., 32-byte cache lines on NVIDIA GPUs). Storing a 2-bit value requires packing multiple weights into a single byte and unpacking them during computation, which introduces bit-manipulation instructions that are not free. The Extra Precision variant (2.05 bits) adds the complexity of a sparse extra-bit representation—some weights need to be stored with an additional bit, which disrupts uniform packing and may require sparse storage formats that are slower to access.

- **Mix'n'Match kernel complexity.** Layer-wise heterogeneous precision requires the inference engine to dispatch different matrix multiplication kernels for different layers. If layers 1–5 use int2, layers 6–30 use int8, and layers 31–42 use int4, the inference runtime must support all three kernel variants and switch between them mid-inference. This is feasible but adds engineering complexity and may cause GPU kernel launch overhead that partially offsets the memory savings.

- **Kernel availability.** The paper states (Section 5.4) that "custom-implemented CUDA kernels can support various low-precision bit-widths, like int2 and int3." This is true in principle (works like QuIP and GPTQ provide such kernels), but it means MatQuant's deployment readiness depends on third-party kernel support that may not exist for all hardware platforms (TPUs, Apple Silicon, edge accelerators). The paper provides no benchmarks with these custom kernels.

**The consequence.** The headline deployment flexibility claims—"a single model that can dynamically serve int8, int4, or int2 depending on the deployment context" (Executive Summary)—are aspirational. A practitioner who attempts to deploy MatQuant based on the paper's accuracy numbers alone may discover that:

- int2 inference is not 4× faster than int8 in practice because of unpacking overhead and the lack of optimized int2 GEMM kernels on their target hardware.
- The Extra Precision variant (2.05 bits), which provides the best accuracy, requires a sparse storage format that existing quantization runtimes (e.g., llama.cpp, vLLM, TensorRT-LLM) do not support.
- Mix'n'Match configurations, while Pareto-optimal in theory, cannot be efficiently served because the runtime expects homogeneous precision and switching precisions per-layer incurs unacceptable overhead.

The paper's accuracy-vs-bits curves (Figures 2, 3) may not translate to accuracy-vs-latency or accuracy-vs-throughput curves in any straightforward way.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **zero** hardware measurements. There are no latency numbers, no throughput benchmarks, no memory profiling results, no kernel benchmarks. The deployment considerations section (5.4) discusses hardware support in general terms (mentioning that int8 and int4 have native support, int2/int3 require custom CUDA kernels) but does not profile any specific implementation. The Mix'n'Match discussion (Section 4.3) and associated figures (2, 3) plot accuracy against effective bits per parameter—a storage metric, not a latency or throughput metric.

The Extra Precision variant's deployment requirements are acknowledged as a challenge: "custom CUDA kernel would be required to enable sparse additions of these additional bits to the model weights." But no such kernel is developed or benchmarked.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the hardware gap partially in Section 5.4:

> "MatQuant can enable further research on hardware software co-design to effectively support elastic bit-widths on-the-fly during inference."

This frames the hardware challenge as future work rather than a resolved component of the method. The paper does not attempt to measure or bound the deployment overhead, provide reference kernel implementations, or characterize the gap between theoretical memory savings and actual wall-clock improvements. For a paper whose primary contribution is deployment flexibility, the absence of any deployment measurement is a significant limitation. A practitioner must treat the deployment claims as **unvalidated hypotheses** rather than demonstrated capabilities.

### The Int2 Accuracy Gains Depend on a Strong Baseline Weakness—MatQuant Closes a Gap in OmniQuant/QAT but May Not Exceed Competing Int2 Methods

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline claim is that MatQuant "significantly outperform[s] their individually trained counterparts" at int2, with gains of 4–7% for QAT and 1–3% for OmniQuant (Section 1, Tables 1 and 2). These numbers compare MatQuant against **the same base algorithm (OmniQuant or QAT) trained explicitly for int2**. The paper does not compare MatQuant int2 against the best available int2 quantization from the broader literature—GPTQ int2, QuIP int2, SpQR int2, or any other learning-free or hybrid method.

This matters because OmniQuant and QAT are not necessarily the strongest int2 quantization baselines. The paper's own results show that OmniQuant int2 (Gemma-2 9B: 60.24%, Table 1) and QAT int2 (56.02%, Table 2) leave a large gap to bfloat16 (74.38%)—a 14–18 point deficit. Meanwhile, methods like QuIP claim state-of-the-art int2 quantization with substantially smaller accuracy gaps on similar model scales. The paper does not benchmark MatQuant against these methods, so the practitioner cannot determine whether MatQuant's int2 (63.35% OmniQuant, 62.29% QAT) represents genuine progress toward closing the int2 gap or merely a better way to use a weak base algorithm.

**The consequence.** The risk is that MatQuant's int2 gain is **partially an artifact of a weak baseline** rather than a fundamental improvement in int2 quantization. If the best competing method (QuIP, SpQR, etc.) achieves 65% on Gemma-2 9B int2, then MatQuant's 63.35% is not state-of-the-art—it's competitive but not superior. The paper's framing ("up to 7% higher accuracy") would be misleading in that context, because a practitioner would compare against the best available int2 method, not against OmniQuant/QAT baselines.

Furthermore, the Single Precision MatQuant result (Table 5)—which shows that training int2 via int8 projection outperforms direct int2 QAT/OmniQuant—may be a general property of any high-bit training scheme, not specific to MatQuant. If GPTQ or QuIP could similarly benefit from "train at int8, evaluate at int2" (e.g., by doing QAT at int8 with int2 projection loss as a post-processing step), the relative advantage of MatQuant would shrink.

The comparison gap also affects the Extra Precision claim. The 2.050-bit model achieves 68.52% (Table 7, Gemma-2 9B), which is substantially closer to bfloat16 (74.38%). But without knowing what, say, a 2.05-bit variant of QuIP or SpQR would achieve, the paper cannot claim that this represents the best achievable quality at this bit budget.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **no comparisons to any non-OmniQuant, non-QAT int2 method**. The related work section (Section 2) discusses GPTQ, AWQ, QuIP, SqueezeLLM, and other learning-free methods, but these are never used as baselines in the experiments. The "Sliced int8" baseline (which collapses to ~38% at int2) demonstrates that naive slicing fails, but this is a straw-man comparison—no practitioner would serve a sliced int8 model at int2 and expect it to work. The relevant comparison is against the best available int2 quantization, regardless of method family.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this comparison gap. The consistent framing is that MatQuant outperforms "independently trained baselines" and "standard int2 quantization"—where "standard" implicitly means OmniQuant or QAT. The omission of learning-free int2 baselines is not discussed as a limitation. A practitioner must independently benchmark MatQuant against competing methods to determine whether the int2 gains represent a genuine advance or a base-algorithm-specific improvement.

### The Revision Model-Like "Correct-to-Incorrect" Problem Exists for MatQuant's Slicing: Sliced Models Share De-Quantization Parameters Optimized for int8

**The assumption or constraint.** MatQuant's deployment procedure (Section 3.4) specifies that when extracting an $r$-bit model from the stored int8 model, the same de-quantization parameters—the scale $\alpha$ and zero-point $z$ from Equation 1—are reused without recalibration. The slicing operator $S(q_c, r)$ in Equation 6 left-shifts the $r$-bit value back to the int8 numerical range, so the sliced integers occupy the same scale as the original int8 weights, and the paper states that no per-precision recalibration is needed.

However, the scale $\alpha$ and zero-point $z$ are optimized during training for the **int8 quantized weight distribution**. When the model is sliced to int2, the effective weight distribution changes substantially—the int2 model can only take 4 possible values (or 5 in the Extra Precision variant), which changes the empirical min, max, and distribution of the de-quantized weights. The int8-optimized scale may be suboptimal for the int2 weight distribution, similar to how a verification model's calibration degrades when the proposal distribution shifts.

**The consequence.** The accuracy of sliced models may be **unnecessarily degraded** by using int8-optimized de-quantization parameters. A simple post-hoc recalibration—recomputing the optimal scale $\alpha$ and zero-point $z$ for the sliced int2 weights using the same MinMax formula (Equation 1) on a small calibration set—could potentially improve int2 accuracy without any change to the training procedure. Conversely, if such recalibration does *not* improve accuracy, that would be strong evidence that MatQuant's training has successfully arranged the int8 weights such that the int8 scale is genuinely optimal for all nested precisions—a stronger claim than what the paper currently asserts.

The paper's interpolation results (int6, int3) implicitly test this assumption: the fact that int6 and int3 work well with int8 de-quantization parameters suggests the assumption holds for moderate bit-width gaps. But the gap from int8 to int2 is much larger, and the Extra Precision variant (where some weights use a 5th bucket that maps to int8 value 256, outside the nominal int8 range) creates a distribution mismatch that the int8-optimized scale may not handle well.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **no ablation on de-quantization parameter recalibration**. Every reported number for sliced models uses the int8-optimized $\alpha$ and $z$ without modification. There is no experiment showing that recalibrating $\alpha$ and $z$ for each target precision does or does not improve accuracy. The interpolation results (Tables 1, 2: int6 and int3 matching baselines) provide indirect evidence that recalibration may be unnecessary for moderate gaps, but this is not explicitly tested or stated. The Extra Precision variant (Section 7) introduces the possibility of weight values at $2^r \times 2^{c-r}$ (e.g., 4 × 64 = 256 for int2), which is larger than the maximum int8 value (255) and would be clipped by the standard de-quantization formula unless the scale is adjusted.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this as a limitation. The de-quantization parameter reuse is presented as a feature ("the exact same de-quantization formula ... with the same $\alpha$ and $z$"), not as a potential accuracy bottleneck. No future work is suggested on per-precision calibration. A practitioner deploying MatQuant may find that a simple post-hoc recalibration step (costing a few forward passes on calibration data) yields a non-trivial accuracy improvement, particularly for int2—but the paper provides no guidance on whether this is worth doing.
