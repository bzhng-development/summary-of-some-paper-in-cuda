## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **LUFFY** (Learning to reason Under oFF-policY guidance), a framework that augments on-policy reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) by incorporating off-policy reasoning traces from a stronger teacher model — dynamically balancing imitation of high-quality demonstrations with self-driven exploration through a **Mixed-Policy GRPO** objective combined with **policy shaping via regularized importance sampling** (re-weighting gradients to emphasize low-probability actions that represent capabilities the model has yet to acquire). Evaluated on six math benchmarks (AIME24/25, AMC, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench) using Qwen2.5-Math-7B as the base model, LUFFY achieves an average gain of +6.4 points over prior RLVR methods and +6.2 points on out-of-distribution tasks, while also succeeding in training LLaMA-3.1-8B on hard datasets where on-policy RLVR completely fails — establishing that off-policy guidance transcends the fundamental capability boundaries of on-policy RL only when policy shaping prevents the entropy collapse that would otherwise cause the model to latch onto superficial imitation rather than acquiring generalizable reasoning behaviors.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: On-Policy RLVR Cannot Transcend a Model's Initial Capabilities

Recent breakthroughs in large reasoning models (LRMs) like OpenAI-o1, DeepSeek-R1, and Kimi-1.5 have demonstrated that sophisticated reasoning behaviors — multi-step chain-of-thought, self-reflection, self-correction — can emerge through reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR). The training is straightforward: the model generates solutions, receives a binary reward based on whether the final answer matches the ground truth, and updates its policy to maximize that reward. This simplicity is precisely what makes the "aha moment" (DeepSeek-R1's documentation of emergent self-reflection) so compelling — complex cognitive behaviors arise from a minimal reward signal without human demonstration or dense supervision.

However, the paper identifies a **fundamental limitation** in how RLVR operates. All current RLVR approaches are **on-policy**: the model learns exclusively from solutions it generates itself. During training, the policy model $\pi_\theta$ samples $N$ solutions per question, computes advantages based on reward scores within that batch (GRPO's group computation, Eq. 2), and updates parameters to increase the probability of correct solutions while decreasing the probability of incorrect ones.

The problem: **reinforcement learning under this setting amplifies existing behaviors rather than introducing genuinely novel cognitive capacities.** The paper cites recent work (Zhao et al., 2025; Yue et al., 2025) demonstrating this bound:

> "on-policy RL is bounded by the base LLM itself. In essence, reinforcement learning under this setting amplifies existing behaviors rather than introducing genuinely novel cognitive capacities."

The mechanism is straightforward. If the base model never generates correct solutions for a particular class of problems — if its pass@1 rate is effectively zero — then every on-policy rollout receives a reward of zero. The advantage computation in GRPO (Eq. 2) produces near-zero advantages for all samples (since all rewards are zero or near-zero, the mean is approximately zero, and normalization by standard deviation amplifies noise rather than signal). There is **no gradient signal pushing the model toward correctness** — the model cannot learn because it never sees what success looks like. On-policy RL can only optimize within the region of solution space that the base model already explores with non-trivial probability.

Concrete evidence for this limitation appears in Gandhi et al. (2025), which the paper cites:

> "Recent study corroborates this constraint, demonstrating that models like Llama 3.2 quickly reach performance plateaus under RL training precisely because they lack certain foundational cognitive behaviors necessary for further advancement."

This means on-policy RLVR has a hard ceiling determined by the base model's pretraining. If the base model cannot produce even one correct solution for a problem class, RLVR provides zero benefit — the model spins its wheels, sampling incorrect solutions and receiving no useful feedback. This is not merely an efficiency problem; it is a **capability boundary** that on-policy methods cannot cross.

### Why This Problem Matters

The limitation has both practical and theoretical significance:

**Practical significance: scaling RLVR to weaker models and harder tasks.** If on-policy RLVR only amplifies existing behaviors, then the technique's effectiveness is gated by the quality of the base model. Organizations training on weaker foundation models (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B rather than Qwen2.5-Math-7B) or on harder problem distributions may find that RLVR simply doesn't work — training rewards collapse to zero (as the paper demonstrates in Figure 4) and no amount of compute fixes the problem. This creates a Matthew effect where strong models get stronger through RL while weak models cannot bootstrap themselves.

**Theoretical significance: the exploration-exploitation dilemma in LLM post-training.** The problem connects to a fundamental tension in reinforcement learning: how do you explore behaviors you haven't yet discovered? On-policy RL provides no mechanism for directed exploration beyond random sampling from the current policy. If the policy assigns near-zero probability to correct reasoning patterns, random sampling will never discover them. Off-policy RL in traditional domains (DQN, SAC) addresses this through experience replay — learning from trajectories generated by previous policies or even entirely different policies. But extending this to LLM training presents unique challenges: the action space is enormous (the token vocabulary), trajectories are long (thousands of tokens), and distribution shift between policies can be severe enough to destabilize training.

**Economic significance: reducing dependence on large teacher models.** If off-policy guidance works, it means smaller models can acquire reasoning capabilities from larger models through training, rather than requiring the large model at inference time. This has implications for on-device deployment, cost-efficient inference, and democratizing access to reasoning capabilities.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies four categories of prior work, each with specific limitations that motivate LUFFY:

**1. Pure on-policy RLVR methods (SimpleRL-Zero, Oat-Zero, PRIME-Zero, OpenReasonerZero).** These are the current state-of-the-art for reasoning RL training. They all operate within the on-policy paradigm, generating rollouts from the current policy and updating based on those rollouts. While these methods have achieved impressive results — Oat-Zero reaching 43.7 average on six math benchmarks with Qwen2.5-Math-7B — they are fundamentally bounded by the base model's initial capabilities. The paper's On-Policy RL replication achieves 45.5 average, showing the ceiling of this approach with the specific base model and training data used.

**2. Supervised fine-tuning on off-policy traces (SFT).** The most straightforward way to incorporate external guidance is simply fine-tuning on high-quality reasoning traces from a stronger model (e.g., DeepSeek-R1). This is what DeepSeek-R1's own distillation pipeline does. However, the paper demonstrates that SFT has critical weaknesses:

- **Rigid imitation.** SFT causes the model to learn superficial patterns rather than generalizable reasoning. The paper shows (Appendix F, Figure 10) that SFT generations are nearly twice as long as LUFFY's on average (4,646 vs. 2,832 tokens) and often contain "excessively long and ultimately unproductive reasoning traces" on incorrect outputs.
- **Poor out-of-distribution generalization.** SFT achieves 44.1 on in-distribution math benchmarks but only 47.5 on out-of-distribution tasks (Table 1). More tellingly, as temperature increases during inference, SFT's pass@8 accuracy *degrades* while both on-policy RL and LUFFY improve (Figure 12), indicating SFT has memorized reasoning patterns rather than learned to explore.
- **Test-time brittleness.** SFT performs comparably to LUFFY at near-deterministic decoding (temperature 0.1) but collapses at higher temperatures (0.6–1.0), failing to discover additional correct reasoning paths. This aligns with prior findings (Chu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025) that SFT tends to memorize rather than generalize.

**3. Two-stage approaches (SFT + RL).** Combining SFT pre-training with subsequent RL fine-tuning (a natural hybrid) partially addresses SFT's generalization weaknesses. However, the paper identifies specific failure modes:

- **Excessive training cost.** The SFT model internalizes the teacher's verbosity — generating unnecessarily long reasoning traces that dramatically increase the computational cost of the RL roll-out stage. SFT+RL requires 130 GPU-hours × 8 GPUs compared to LUFFY's 77, despite achieving worse performance (48.2 vs. 50.1 average on math benchmarks).
- **Worse out-of-distribution performance.** SFT+RL achieves only 44.8 on out-of-distribution tasks, substantially worse than on-policy RL's 57.3 and LUFFY's 57.8. The rigid imitation learned during SFT persists through RL training, limiting the model's ability to adapt to novel problem distributions.
- **Data inefficiency.** SFT+RL uses 135K off-policy traces compared to LUFFY's 64K, yet achieves worse results.

**4. Multi-task training (RL + SFT loss).** Adding SFT loss as an auxiliary objective during RL training seems appealing — it combines on-policy exploration with off-policy imitation in a single stage. However, the paper shows this performs worst of all off-policy baselines (40.1 on math benchmarks, 46.0 on OOD). The model appears to suffer from conflicting gradients: the SFT objective pulls toward rigid imitation while the RL objective pushes toward exploration, and neither works well.

### The Missing Piece: Why Can't We Just Do Off-Policy RL?

The paper frames the gap as a fundamental algorithmic challenge. Off-policy RL algorithms exist in the traditional RL literature (DQN, SAC, TD3), but extending them to LLM training for reasoning is non-trivial for several reasons:

**Distribution shift.** When learning from trajectories generated by a different policy $\pi_\phi$ (the teacher, e.g., DeepSeek-R1), importance sampling must correct for the mismatch between $\pi_\theta$ (current policy, e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B) and $\pi_\phi$. The importance weight $\pi_\theta(\tau) / \pi_\phi(\tau)$ can be extremely small when the teacher assigns high probability to tokens that the student assigns near-zero probability. These small weights lead to high-variance gradient estimates — a well-known problem in importance sampling.

**Entropy collapse.** The paper identifies a specific failure mode: when combining on-policy and off-policy objectives, the model converges rapidly but prematurely. Figure 2 (left) shows that Mixed-Policy GRPO without policy shaping causes generation entropy to collapse much faster than on-policy RL alone. The mechanism: the model quickly learns to reinforce off-policy tokens that are *already likely* under its own policy, while ignoring off-policy tokens that represent genuinely novel reasoning capabilities. The model achieves high reward by imitating surface-level patterns (formatting, phrasing) from the teacher without internalizing the deeper reasoning structure.

> "When combining both learning off-policy and on-policy signals, the model tends to quickly converge toward reinforcing off-policy tokens that are also likely in the on-policy $\pi_\theta$ distribution, and ignoring off-policy tokens that are deviated from the model's original policy, i.e., low-probability tokens that may represent essential reasoning capabilities the model has yet to acquire."

This is the crucial insight: **naive off-policy learning causes the model to hack the objective by learning only the easy-to-imitate tokens** — those already within its distribution — while ignoring the hard-to-imitate tokens that encode genuine reasoning capabilities.

**Tokenization mismatch.** When the teacher and student use different tokenizers (common when distilling from DeepSeek-R1 to Qwen), computing $\pi_\phi(\tau_{j,t})$ exactly is impossible because the token sequences don't align. The paper addresses this by setting $\pi_\phi = 1$ for computational efficiency, which simplifies the importance weight to $\pi_\theta(\tau_{j,t})$ — but this removes the calibration that importance sampling is meant to provide, making the off-policy gradient noisier.

### How LUFFY Positions Itself

LUFFY is positioned as a **first framework for principled off-policy learning within the RLVR paradigm.** It does not claim to invent off-policy RL for LLMs from scratch — the paper acknowledges that importance sampling for off-policy policy gradients is standard (Sutton et al., 1999; PPO, 2017). Rather, LUFFY's contribution is:

1. **Identifying the specific failure mode** of naive off-policy integration (entropy collapse, superficial imitation of high-probability tokens).

2. **Proposing a concrete mechanism to address it** — policy shaping via regularized importance sampling, which re-weights gradients to amplify learning from low-probability actions.

3. **Demonstrating that this mechanism enables capabilities beyond on-policy RL's ceiling** — specifically, training LLaMA-3.1-8B on hard datasets where on-policy RL fails entirely (training rewards collapse to zero in Figure 4), while LUFFY achieves stable reward improvement.

The paper explicitly frames this as **transcending the exploration-exploitation tension** that dominates prior work:

> "LUFFY achieves a dynamic and adaptive equilibrium between imitation and exploration."

This framing is important because it positions LUFFY not as a replacement for on-policy RLVR but as an augmentation that **fills the capability gap** — use off-policy guidance when on-policy exploration is insufficient, use on-policy exploration when the model can already generate correct solutions. The mixed-policy group computation (Eq. 4) implements this adaptively: when the model's own rollouts fail (all receive reward 0), off-policy traces receive positive advantages and drive learning; when the model succeeds (some rollouts get reward 1), on-policy rollouts dominate and the model explores its own solution space.

### The Broader Context: Why Now?

The paper doesn't explicitly discuss this, but the timing is significant. The release of DeepSeek-R1 in January 2025 provided the community with both (1) a strong open-source reasoning model whose outputs can serve as off-policy traces, and (2) evidence that RLVR works at scale. However, the distillation pipeline — SFT on R1's outputs — while effective for creating R1-Distill models, doesn't produce the same kind of self-improving reasoning that the full RL-trained R1 exhibits. LUFFY offers a path to combine the best of both: learning from R1's reasoning patterns (through off-policy guidance) while preserving the exploration and self-improvement capabilities that make RLVR powerful (through mixed-policy training and policy shaping).

This positions LUFFY as a bridge between two paradigms: pure distillation (fast, but produces rigid models) and pure RLVR (produces flexible, self-improving models, but bounded by base capabilities). The bridge is particularly valuable for weaker base models — where pure RLVR hits its capability ceiling and pure SFT produces brittle memorization, LUFFY's balanced approach enables genuine capability acquisition that generalizes to novel problems.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents an **algorithmic framework** — not a deployed system — that modifies the GRPO reinforcement learning algorithm to incorporate reasoning traces generated by a stronger, external model (off-policy data) alongside the model's own self-generated solutions (on-policy data), using a dynamically weighted objective and a gradient re-weighting mechanism to prevent the model from converging prematurely to superficial imitation. The core problem is that on-policy RLVR can only amplify behaviors the base model already exhibits — it cannot teach genuinely new reasoning capabilities — and LUFFY solves this by injecting off-policy guidance in a way that forces the model to learn from unfamiliar, low-probability actions (the "hard parts" of reasoning) while still preserving the self-driven exploration that makes RLVR produce generalizable behaviors rather than memorized patterns.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The LUFFY training loop has five major components that interact during each update step:

1. **Policy Model ($\pi_\theta$)** — the LLM being trained (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B). It generates on-policy rollouts (solutions to math problems) and receives gradient updates from the combined objective.

2. **Off-Policy Data Source ($\pi_\phi$)** — a stronger reasoning model (DeepSeek-R1) that provides pre-generated, high-quality reasoning traces. These traces are static — they are not generated during LUFFY training but come from a pre-existing dataset (OpenR1-Math-220k, filtered to 45K prompts with correct answers). For computational efficiency, the teacher's token-level probability $\pi_\phi$ is treated as 1 everywhere, avoiding tokenization mismatch issues.

3. **Mixed-Policy Group Advantage Computation** — given a prompt, the system samples 7 on-policy rollouts from $\pi_\theta$ and retrieves 1 off-policy trace from $\pi_\phi$, pools their rewards (all 0 or 1 based on answer correctness), and computes a normalized advantage for each rollout. Because off-policy traces are always correct (reward 1), they receive positive advantages when the on-policy rollouts fail, and near-zero advantages when on-policy rollouts succeed.

4. **Mixed-Policy GRPO Objective (Eq. 5)** — two parallel policy gradient terms: an off-policy term that uses importance sampling $\pi_\theta / \pi_\phi$ to correct for distribution mismatch when learning from the teacher's traces, and an on-policy term that uses the standard GRPO clipped objective for the model's own rollouts. These are summed and normalized by total token count.

5. **Policy Shaping Function $f(\cdot)$ (Eq. 6)** — a transformation applied to the importance sampling ratio in the off-policy term only, replacing the linear weight $\pi_\theta / \pi_\phi$ with $f(\pi_\theta / \pi_\phi) = (\pi_\theta / \pi_\phi) / (\pi_\theta / \pi_\phi + \gamma)$. This re-weights gradients to amplify learning signals for tokens where the student assigns low probability (actions far from its current distribution that encode novel reasoning capabilities) and dampens signals for tokens the student already assigns high probability (superficial patterns it could imitate easily without learning deeper reasoning).

Information flow per training step: a batch of prompts is sampled → for each prompt, the policy model generates 7 on-policy rollouts (temperature 1.0) and the off-policy trace is retrieved from the pre-computed dataset → Math-Verify scores all 8 rollouts (1 if the extracted answer matches the ground truth, 0 otherwise) → advantages are computed via group normalization (Eq. 4) → the mixed-policy GRPO loss with policy shaping (Eq. 6) is computed token-by-token, summing the shaped off-policy term over the teacher's trace and the clipped on-policy term over the model's rollouts → gradients flow back to update $\pi_\theta$.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the **Mixed-Policy Group Advantage Computation** (Eq. 4), because it is the mechanism by which off-policy traces receive learning signals — understanding how advantages are assigned reveals *when* LUFFY imitates versus *when* it explores.
- **Second**, the **Mixed-Policy GRPO Objective** (Eq. 5) and its importance sampling terms, because this is the mathematical core that extends GRPO to handle two distributions — we need the convergence theory (Theorem 1) to understand why this form is sound.
- **Third**, the **policy shaping function $f(\cdot)$** (Eqs. 6–8), because this is LUFFY's key innovation that prevents entropy collapse — the gradient analysis shows exactly *how* re-weighting changes what the model learns.
- **Fourth**, the **practical design choices** (setting $\pi_\phi = 1$, removing the off-policy clip, the $\gamma$ hyperparameter, the rollout ratio 7:1), because these implementation details determine whether the theoretical framework actually works in practice.
- **Fifth**, the **training recipe and hyperparameters**, because reproducing LUFFY requires knowing the learning rate, batch sizes, dataset construction, and system prompt.

This is primarily an **algorithmic contribution paper** whose core idea is that off-policy reasoning traces can be integrated into GRPO through a shaped importance-sampling objective that forces the model to learn from low-probability actions, thereby preventing the entropy collapse that would otherwise cause the model to superficially imitate the teacher without acquiring generalizable reasoning capabilities.

#### Mixed-Policy Group Advantage Computation

The starting point for LUFFY is GRPO's group-based advantage estimation (Eq. 2 in the paper). In standard GRPO, for a single question $q$, the model $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ samples $N$ solutions $\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_N$, each receives a scalar reward $R(\tau_i) \in \{0, 1\}$ from Math-Verify, and the advantage for solution $i$ is:

$$A_i = \frac{R(\tau_i) - \text{mean}(\{R(\tau_1), \ldots, R(\tau_N)\})}{\text{std}(\{R(\tau_1), \ldots, R(\tau_N)\})}$$

where $R(\tau_i)$ is the binary reward (1 if the extracted answer matches the ground truth, 0 otherwise), $\text{mean}(\cdot)$ is the average reward across the $N$ solutions in the group, and $\text{std}(\cdot)$ is the standard deviation across the same group.

**What it computes:** for each solution, the advantage is the number of standard deviations its reward is above or below the group average. A correct solution in a group where most solutions are incorrect receives a large positive advantage (it is much better than average). A correct solution in a group where most solutions are correct receives a near-zero advantage (it is not special). An incorrect solution receives a negative advantage whose magnitude depends on how many other solutions are correct.

**Why this form:** group normalization removes the need for a separately trained value function (critic), which would be expensive and potentially inaccurate for long reasoning traces. By normalizing within each question's group, the advantages are automatically calibrated to question difficulty — on hard questions where all solutions are wrong, advantages are near zero (no learning signal, which is the failure mode LUFFY addresses); on easy questions where most solutions are right, correct solutions don't get reinforced unnecessarily.

**LUFFY's modification (Eq. 4):** instead of sampling all $N$ solutions from the on-policy model $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, LUFFY constructs a mixed group:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{R(\tau_i) - \text{mean}(G_{\text{on}} \cup G_{\text{off}})}{\text{std}(G_{\text{on}} \cup G_{\text{off}})}$$

where $G_{\text{on}} = \{R(\tau_i) \mid \tau_i \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\tau), i = 1, \ldots, N_{\text{on}}\}$ is the set of rewards from the model's own rollouts (7 in practice), and $G_{\text{off}} = \{R(\tau_j) \mid \tau_j \sim \pi_\phi(\tau), j = 1, \ldots, N_{\text{off}}\}$ is the set of rewards from the teacher's pre-generated traces (1 in practice).

**What it computes:** the advantage for each rollout — whether on-policy or off-policy — is its reward z-scored against the combined pool of both on-policy and off-policy rewards. Since the off-policy trace is always correct (reward 1, because the dataset is filtered to include only traces where DeepSeek-R1 produced the correct answer), it acts as a "reference correct solution" in the group.

**Why this form:** this mixed-pool normalization creates an **adaptive signal** that depends on how well the model is currently performing on that specific question. Consider two scenarios:

- **When the model fails on a question** (all 7 on-policy rollouts are incorrect, rewards all 0): the group contains seven 0s and one 1. The mean is 1/8, the standard deviation is non-zero (because there is variance — one solution is different from the others). The off-policy trace receives a strongly positive advantage (roughly +2.6 standard deviations above the mean). The on-policy rollouts all receive slightly negative advantages. **Effect:** the model is pushed to imitate the teacher on this question — the off-policy trace dominates the gradient.

- **When the model succeeds on a question** (say, 5 of 7 on-policy rollouts are correct): the group contains six 1s and two 0s. The mean is 6/8 = 0.75. The off-policy trace's advantage is positive but small (about +0.6 standard deviations above the mean). Some on-policy rollouts also receive positive advantages. **Effect:** the off-policy trace provides a weak signal; the on-policy rollouts provide the dominant learning signal; the model explores its own solution space without being pulled strongly toward the teacher.

This adaptivity — the off-policy trace matters most when the model is lost and matters least when the model is competent — is what LUFFY means by "dynamic and adaptive equilibrium between imitation and exploration." It is not a fixed mixing weight; it emerges from the group computation.

The paper uses $N_{\text{on}} = 7$ and $N_{\text{off}} = 1$ for a total of 8 rollouts per prompt. The choice of 7:1 ratio is described as "ensure fairness" — matching the total number of rollouts (8) used in the On-Policy RL baseline (which uses 8 on-policy rollouts). The paper does not ablate this ratio, but the mechanism suggests that using more off-policy traces would strengthen the imitation signal, while using fewer would weaken it. One off-policy trace per question is sufficient because it only needs to provide a reference correct solution when the model fails.

#### Mixed-Policy GRPO Objective

Having computed advantages for both on-policy and off-policy rollouts, LUFFY defines a composite objective function that sums two policy gradient terms. The standard GRPO objective (Eq. 3) for on-policy rollouts is:

$$J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^N |\tau_i|} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_i|} \text{CLIP}(r_{i,t}(\theta), \hat{A}_i, \epsilon) - \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}]$$

where $r_{i,t}(\theta) = \pi_\theta(\tau_{i,t} \mid q, \tau_{i,<t}) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\tau_{i,t} \mid q, \tau_{i,<t})$ is the per-token importance sampling ratio between the current policy and the policy that generated the rollout, $\hat{A}_i$ is the advantage from Eq. 4, $\epsilon$ is the clip parameter, and $\beta D_{\text{KL}}$ is a KL penalty keeping the policy near a reference. The CLIP function is:

$$\text{CLIP}(r, A, \epsilon) = \min(r \cdot A, \text{clip}(r; 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \cdot A)$$

**What CLIP computes:** if the importance ratio $r$ has moved outside the interval $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ (meaning the current policy assigns substantially different probability than the old policy), the gradient is clipped — the update treats the ratio as if it were clamped to the nearest boundary. If $A > 0$ (good action), we don't let the ratio exceed $1+\epsilon$ (prevents overly aggressive increases in probability). If $A < 0$ (bad action), we don't let the ratio fall below $1-\epsilon$ (prevents overly aggressive decreases).

**Why this form:** the clip creates a trust region — the policy cannot change too much in a single update relative to the policy that generated the data. This stabilizes training by preventing destructive large updates when a rollout happens to be very high or very low probability under the new policy compared to the old one.

**LUFFY's Mixed-Policy objective (Eq. 5):** LUFFY extends this by adding a parallel term for off-policy rollouts:

$$J_{\text{Mixed}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{Z} \left( \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{off}}} \sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_j|} \text{CLIP}(\hat{r}_{j,t}(\theta, \phi), \hat{A}_j, \epsilon)}_{\text{off-policy objective}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{on}}} \sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_i|} \text{CLIP}(r_{i,t}(\theta), \hat{A}_i, \epsilon)}_{\text{on-policy objective}} \right)$$

where $Z = \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{off}}} |\tau_j| + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{on}}} |\tau_i|$ is the total number of tokens across all rollouts (normalizing so the loss is per-token, not inflated by longer traces). The two importance sampling ratios differ:

- $r_{i,t}(\theta) = \pi_\theta(\tau_{i,t} \mid q, \tau_{i,<t}) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\tau_{i,t} \mid q, \tau_{i,<t})$ — the standard on-policy ratio, where the denominator is the pre-update model that actually generated the rollout.
- $\hat{r}_{j,t}(\theta, \phi) = \pi_\theta(\tau_{j,t} \mid q, \tau_{j,<t}) / \pi_\phi(\tau_{j,t} \mid q, \tau_{j,<t})$ — the off-policy ratio, where the denominator is the teacher's probability.

**What it computes:** for each token in each rollout, the objective computes whether that token should become more or less probable. For on-policy tokens, the decision uses the clipped ratio relative to the model's recent policy — this is standard GRPO. For off-policy tokens (from the teacher), the decision uses the clipped ratio relative to the teacher's policy — this corrects for the fact that the teacher's trajectory may have very different probability under the student than under the teacher.

**Why this form:** mixing on-policy and off-policy objectives in a single sum, normalized by total token count, creates a natural curriculum. Early in training, when the model assigns low probability to the teacher's tokens, the off-policy ratio $\hat{r}_{j,t}$ is small, but the off-policy advantage $\hat{A}_j$ is large (because the model can't solve the problem itself). These two effects partially offset, providing a moderate but consistent learning signal. Later in training, when the model's on-policy rollouts succeed, the on-policy advantages become large while off-policy advantages shrink, and the model transitions naturally to self-driven exploration.

**What is NOT in this objective: the KL penalty and format rewards.** The paper states: "We remove the KL loss term by setting $\beta = 0$ and set the entropy loss coefficient to 0.01. Following Dr.GRPO, we remove the length normalization and standard error normalization of GRPO loss for all experiments." This is consistent with recent practice (SimpleRL-Zero, Oat-Zero) that found KL penalties unnecessary for reasoning RL. The approach also includes "no format or length reward" — the only reward is answer correctness from Math-Verify.

#### Convergence Theory (Theorem 1)

The paper provides a theoretical guarantee that the mixed-policy gradient estimator converges, addressing the concern that importance sampling from a different distribution might produce unstable or biased updates.

**Formal statement (Theorem 1):** Suppose the objective $J \in \mathcal{J}_n$ (the class of finite-sum Lipschitz smooth functions), has $\sigma$-bounded gradients, and the importance weight $w = \pi_\theta / \pi_\phi$ is clipped to the bounded interval $[w, \bar{w}]$. Let the learning rate at step $k$ be $\alpha_k = \alpha = c / \sqrt{K}$ where:

$$c = \sqrt{\frac{2(J(\theta^*) - J(\theta^0))}{L \sigma^2 \bar{w} w}}$$

and $\theta^*$ is an optimal solution, $\theta^0$ is the initial parameters, $K$ is the total number of iterations, and $L$ is the Lipschitz smoothness constant. Then the iterates of the algorithm satisfy:

$$\min_{0 \leq k \leq K-1} \mathbb{E}[\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|^2] \leq \sqrt{\frac{2(J(\theta^*) - J(\theta^0)) L \bar{w}}{K w}} \sigma$$

where $\mathbb{E}[\|\nabla J(\theta_k)\|^2]$ is the expected squared gradient norm at iteration $k$ (a measure of how close the algorithm is to a stationary point — zero gradient means a local optimum has been reached), $(J(\theta^*) - J(\theta^0))$ is the total potential improvement from the initial to optimal objective value, and $\sigma$ is the bound on gradient norm.

**What it states operationally:** the minimum expected squared gradient norm over all $K$ iterations shrinks at a rate of $O(1/\sqrt{K})$. In practical terms, this means the algorithm is guaranteed to find a point where the gradient is very small (a stationary point, which for nonconvex optimization means at least a local optimum or saddle point) after sufficiently many iterations, with the convergence rate degrading gracefully with the weight bounds — if the importance weights have a large spread (large $\bar{w}/w$ ratio), convergence is slower but still guaranteed.

**Why this form:** the proof (Appendix B.1) follows the standard stochastic gradient descent analysis for nonconvex Lipschitz smooth functions (Reddi et al., 2016), with the key modification being that the gradient estimator uses importance-weighted samples. The boundedness assumption $w \in [w, \bar{w}]$ is critical — without it, a single extremely small or large importance weight could blow up the gradient variance and break convergence. The paper satisfies this by clipping importance weights (implicitly through the CLIP operation for on-policy, and through the shaping function's bounded behavior for off-policy).

**Practical implication:** the theorem justifies using a constant learning rate $\alpha = c/\sqrt{K}$ when the total number of steps $K$ is known in advance, or a decaying learning rate $\alpha_k \propto 1/\sqrt{k}$ when it is not. The total complexity to reach an $\epsilon$-accurate solution (where the gradient norm is at most $\epsilon$) is $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ iterations — standard for SGD-based nonconvex optimization.

**What the theorem does NOT cover:** the analysis assumes the off-policy distribution $\pi_\phi$ is well-defined and the importance weights are bounded. In LUFFY's practical implementation, $\pi_\phi$ is set to 1 (not a real probability distribution), which means the importance weight is simply $\pi_\theta(\tau_{j,t})$ — always in $[0, 1]$ and thus trivially bounded. The theorem's assumption that $\mathbb{E}[\pi_\theta / \pi_\phi] = 1$ does not hold with this simplification, but the practical consequence is that the gradient steps are conservative (weights at most 1) rather than aggressive, which is safer for training stability.

#### Policy Shaping via Regularized Importance Sampling

The mixed-policy GRPO objective successfully incorporates off-policy guidance, but the paper identifies a critical failure mode: **entropy collapse**. Figure 2 (left) shows that Mixed-Policy GRPO without shaping causes the generation entropy to drop much faster than On-Policy RL — after about 200 steps, entropy approaches zero, meaning the model's policy has become nearly deterministic and exploration has ceased.

**The mechanism of entropy collapse:** the paper explains (Section 3.2) that the model "hacks" the mixed-policy objective by selectively learning from off-policy tokens that are already high-probability under its own policy $\pi_\theta$. These tokens — which represent surface-level patterns like "Let's think step by step," "First, we need to understand," formatting conventions — are easy to further reinforce because the model already assigns them high probability. The model achieves high reward (and thus high advantage-weighted loss reduction) by making these already-likely tokens even more likely, while ignoring off-policy tokens that have low probability under $\pi_\theta$ — the tokens that encode genuine reasoning capabilities (specific mathematical transformations, non-obvious intermediate steps, verification checks) that the model has not yet acquired.

**Why this is "hacking":** the objective is being minimized (the model achieves lower loss), but the desired behavior is not being learned. The model becomes more confident in the reasoning patterns it already knew, rather than acquiring new ones. The high-probability, low-information tokens dominate the gradient because they have larger importance weights — $\pi_\theta$ is already large for them, so the gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta$ receives a large multiplier from the advantage (since CLIP won't clip weights near 1.0 much). The low-probability tokens have small importance weights (since $\pi_\theta$ is small), so their gradients are proportionally smaller, even though these are precisely the tokens that encode novel capabilities.

**Policy shaping function (Eq. 6):** LUFFY addresses this by replacing the importance sampling ratio $\hat{r}_{j,t}(\theta, \phi)$ in the off-policy term with a non-linear transformation $f(\hat{r}_{j,t}(\theta, \phi))$:

$$J_{\text{SHAPING}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{Z} \left( \sum_{j=1}^{N_{\text{off}}} \sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_j|} f(\hat{r}_{j,t}(\theta, \phi)) \cdot \hat{A}_j + \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{on}}} \sum_{t=1}^{|\tau_i|} \text{CLIP}(r_{i,t}(\theta), \hat{A}_i, \epsilon) \right)$$

where $f(x) = x / (x + \gamma)$ with $\gamma = 0.1$, and all variables retain their previous definitions.

**What $f(x) = x/(x+\gamma)$ does:** it is an increasing, concave function that passes through the origin ($f(0) = 0$) and asymptotes to 1 ($f(x) \to 1$ as $x \to \infty$). For small $x$ (low-probability tokens where $\pi_\theta$ is small), $f(x) \approx x/\gamma$ — the weight is approximately linear but amplified by $1/\gamma = 10$. For large $x$ (high-probability tokens where $\pi_\theta \approx 1$), $f(x) \approx 1/(1 + \gamma) \approx 0.91$ — the weight is compressed and can never exceed 1, regardless of how large the importance ratio gets.

**Why this shape:** the concave form creates the re-weighting behavior shown in Figure 2 (middle and right). The original linear importance sampling (blue line in the middle panel) gives equal relative importance to all tokens — if token A has probability 0.9 and token B has probability 0.1, the gradient multiplier for A is 9× larger than for B. The shaped function $f(x)$ (orange line) compresses high-probability weights (at probability 0.9, $f \approx 0.9/(0.9+0.1) = 0.9$) while boosting low-probability weights (at probability 0.1, $f \approx 0.1/(0.1+0.1) = 0.5$). The effective gradient weighting (Figure 2, right) shows that the shaped function assigns substantially larger gradients to low-probability actions: at probability 0.1, the shaped gradient weight is approximately 0.15 versus approximately 0.05 for the unshaped version — a 3× amplification.

**Gradient analysis (Eqs. 7–8):** the paper derives the gradient of the shaped off-policy objective:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{SHAPING-OFF}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\phi} \left[ f'(\pi_\theta) \cdot \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_\phi} \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta \cdot \hat{A}_j \right]$$

where $f'(\pi_\theta)$ is the derivative of the shaping function. Since $f'(x) = \gamma / (x + \gamma)^2$, we have:

- When $\pi_\theta$ is small (low-probability token), $f'(\pi_\theta) \approx \gamma/\gamma^2 = 1/\gamma = 10$ — the gradient is amplified 10× relative to the unshaped version.
- When $\pi_\theta$ is large (high-probability token), $f'(\pi_\theta) \approx \gamma/(1 + \gamma)^2 \approx 0.1/1.21 \approx 0.083$ — the gradient is dampened.

Further decomposing to per-action gradients (Eq. 8):

$$\left| \frac{\partial J_{\text{SHAPING-OFF}}(\theta)}{\partial M_\theta(\tau'_{j,t})} \right| \leq \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\phi} \left[ |f'(\pi_\theta)| \cdot \pi_\theta \cdot (1 - \pi_\theta) \cdot |\hat{A}_j| \right]$$

where $M_\theta(\tau'_{j,t})$ is the output logit for a specific action/token $\tau'_{j,t}$. The bound is $\pi_\theta(1 - \pi_\theta)$ times the shaping derivative, times the advantage magnitude. For the standard linear shaping ($f'(\pi_\theta) = 1$), the gradient is small when $\pi_\theta \to 0$ or $\pi_\theta \to 1$ — the model learns little from tokens it's very uncertain about or very certain about. With LUFFY's shaping, $|f'(\pi_\theta)|$ is large precisely when $\pi_\theta$ is small, partially compensating for the $\pi_\theta(1 - \pi_\theta)$ factor and enabling meaningful learning from low-probability, high-uncertainty actions.

**Why this prevents entropy collapse:** by amplifying gradients for low-probability tokens, the shaping function creates a "restoring force" that encourages the model to maintain some probability mass on diverse actions. Even as the model becomes more confident in certain reasoning patterns, the shaping function ensures that unfamiliar tokens from the teacher continue to receive non-trivial learning signals, preventing the policy from collapsing to a deterministic, low-entropy distribution. This is visible in Figure 6: Mixed-Policy without shaping plateaus around step 200 and eventually converges with On-Policy RL; with shaping (LUFFY), accuracy continues to improve steadily, and entropy (Figure 5, right) remains elevated throughout training.

**Variance analysis (Appendix B.2):** the paper provides an informal analysis showing that the shaped importance weights have lower variance than the original weights. Using a first-order Taylor expansion around $x = 1$:

$$\text{Var}[f(x)] \approx \left( \frac{\gamma}{(1 + \gamma)^2} \right)^2 \text{Var}[x]$$

Since $(\gamma/(1+\gamma)^2)^2 \ll 1$ for $\gamma \in (0, 1)$, the shaped weights have substantially lower variance. A special-case analysis assuming $p(x) = e^{-x}$ (reasonable because importance weights tend to be small and decay exponentially for unlikely tokens) gives $\text{Var}[f(x)] = 1 - 2\gamma^2 e^\gamma E_3(\gamma) - \gamma^2 e^{2\gamma} E_1(\gamma)^2 < 1 = \text{Var}[x]$, confirming the variance reduction. Lower variance in importance weights means more stable gradient estimates and smoother training.

#### Practical Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Setting $\pi_\phi = 1$ (the teacher's probability is treated as 1 everywhere).** The paper states: "In practice, to facilitate direct integration of high-quality demonstrations from large, powerful models (e.g., DeepSeek-R1), we adopt $\pi_\phi = 1$ for computational efficiency." This means the off-policy importance ratio simplifies to $\hat{r}_{j,t} = \pi_\theta(\tau_{j,t} \mid q, \tau_{j,<t})$ — just the student's probability of the teacher's token.

**Why this choice:** computing $\pi_\phi(\tau_{j,t})$ would require running the teacher model (DeepSeek-R1) forward on every off-policy token during training to get its token-level probabilities, which is computationally infeasible — DeepSeek-R1 is a much larger model and running it as a "probability oracle" during training would dominate the cost. Additionally, DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen2.5-Math-7B use different tokenizers, so exact token-level alignment is impossible without complex token-mapping procedures. Setting $\pi_\phi = 1$ is equivalent to assuming the teacher is "maximally confident" in all its token choices — this is not correct in a probabilistic sense, but it bounds the importance weight to $[0, 1]$, making it conservative (the student is never told to increase probability beyond what it naturally assigns, only to match what the teacher did).

**Consequence: no clip on off-policy term.** The paper states: "We omit the clip operation for the off-policy rollouts, as the clip operation will be imbalanced when $\pi_\phi = 1$." With $\pi_\phi = 1$, the off-policy importance weight is always in $[0, 1]$. The CLIP operation in standard GRPO is designed to bound weights that can exceed 1 (when the new policy assigns higher probability than the old policy). Since the off-policy weight can never exceed 1 (the student cannot assign higher probability than 1 to any token), clipping would only affect the lower bound ($1 - \epsilon$). But since weights are already naturally bounded in $[0, 1]$, clipping provides no benefit and could arbitrarily suppress learning from tokens where $\pi_\theta$ is naturally small.

**Removing the on-policy clip (Figure 8):** the paper further removes the clip operation from the on-policy term as well. The justification: "LUFFY experiences more frequent clipping compared to On-Policy RL, which can suppress learning from high-quality off-policy traces." Figure 8 shows that the clip ratio (fraction of tokens where the importance ratio exceeds $1 \pm \epsilon$ and gets clipped) is higher for LUFFY than On-Policy RL throughout training. This is because the off-policy guidance pulls the policy in directions that deviate from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ more rapidly than pure on-policy learning — exactly the behavior the clip was designed to prevent, but which is desirable when incorporating external knowledge. Removing the clip "allows greater flexibility in updating toward unfamiliar yet effective actions, thereby unlocking the model's capacity to better integrate off-policy reasoning behaviors."

**Choice of $\gamma = 0.1$ (Figure 9):** the paper sweeps $\gamma \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5\}$ and finds that $\gamma = 0.1$ achieves the best average accuracy (50.1 on the six math benchmarks). Higher values of $\gamma$ make the shaping function more aggressive — $f(x) \approx x/\gamma$ for small $x$, so $\gamma = 0.2$ would give only 5× amplification versus 10× for $\gamma = 0.1$. Lower values (0.05) give even stronger amplification (20×) but appear to over-amplify low-probability tokens, potentially causing the model to overfit to rare but non-generalizable teacher patterns. The optimal $\gamma = 0.1$ balances amplification of novel capabilities with stability.

**The 7:1 on-policy to off-policy ratio:** the paper uses 7 on-policy rollouts and 1 off-policy trace per prompt, totaling 8 rollouts (matching the baseline's 8 on-policy rollouts). The paper doesn't ablate this ratio, but the mechanism suggests that 1 off-policy trace is sufficient because it only needs to provide a reference point for advantage computation — when the model fails, one correct example is enough to create a positive advantage. More off-policy traces would strengthen the imitation signal but potentially reduce exploration.

**Entropy loss coefficient 0.01:** the paper includes a small entropy bonus in the loss (coefficient 0.01) to explicitly encourage exploration. This is orthogonal to the policy shaping mechanism — the entropy bonus penalizes low-entropy distributions directly, while policy shaping re-weights gradients to prevent entropy from collapsing in the first place. The combination provides both a "pull" (shaping amplifies low-probability token gradients) and a "push" (entropy bonus directly penalizes determinism).

#### Training Recipe and Hyperparameters

**Dataset construction:** the training data comes from OpenR1-Math-220k, a public dataset of math problem prompts with solutions generated by DeepSeek-R1. The paper starts with the default 94K-prompt subset, filters out generations longer than 8192 tokens, and filters out solutions where Math-Verify determines the extracted answer does not match the ground truth. This yields 45K prompts, each paired with one verified-correct off-policy reasoning trace from DeepSeek-R1. The dataset is purely mathematical — no formatting rewards or process rewards are used.

**Difficulty subsets (Section 5.2, Appendix C.1):** for the experiments with LLaMA-3.1-8B, the paper creates two subsets:
- **Easy set:** questions where DeepSeek-R1's correct solution is ≤ 2K tokens (7.3K prompts)
- **Hard set:** questions where DeepSeek-R1's correct solution is ≤ 4K tokens (25.4K prompts)

The intuition: "the more tokens needed for Deepseek-R1 to generate a correct answer, the more difficult the question is." These subsets are used to test whether LUFFY can train models on harder problems where on-policy RL fails.

**Rollout and update configuration:**
- Rollout batch size: 128 prompts per step
- Update batch size: 64 prompts per gradient step
- Rollouts per prompt: 7 on-policy + 1 off-policy = 8 total
- Temperature for rollout generation: 1.0 (maximum entropy sampling)
- Evaluation temperature: 0.6
- Max generation length: implicitly bounded by the context window (16384 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B after rope theta extension from 10000 to 40000)

**Optimization:**
- Optimizer: AdamW (specific betas and weight decay not stated beyond "learning rate is constantly set as 1e-6")
- Learning rate: $1 \times 10^{-6}$ for all RL-trained models
- Training steps: 500 for standard LUFFY, 860 for LUFFY† (matching SFT+RL GPU hours)
- GPU configuration: 8 A100 GPUs
- Implementation: based on verl (rollout generation via vLLM)

**Model modifications for Qwen2.5-Math-7B:** since the base model has a 4096 context window but off-policy traces can be lengthy, the paper extends the context window to 16384 and changes the rope theta from 10000 to 40000 (rotary position embedding base frequency — larger values allow better extrapolation to longer sequences).

**System prompt (Appendix C.2):** all Qwen2.5-series models use a standardized prompt that instructs the model to structure its output into "Thought" (using `<think>\n` and ` response\n` tags) and "Solution" sections, with the final answer enclosed in `\boxed{}`. The prompt explicitly asks for "analyzing, summarizing, exploring, reassessing, and refining your thought process through multiple iterations." For LLaMA-3.1-8B, a simplified prompt is used ("Answer: Let's think step by step.") because the model "cannot follow such an instruction" — an important practical detail showing that the system prompt's effectiveness depends on the base model's instruction-following capability.

**Reward function:** Math-Verify, a rule-based verifier that extracts the answer from `\boxed{}` (or equivalent formatting) and compares it to the ground truth using SymPy (symbolic mathematics library). The reward is 1 if the extracted answer is mathematically equivalent to the ground truth, 0 otherwise. There is no format reward, no length reward, and no process reward — only outcome correctness.

**What is NOT used:** the paper explicitly removes several components common in other RLVR implementations:
- No KL penalty ($\beta = 0$)
- No length normalization in GRPO loss
- No standard error normalization in GRPO loss
- No format or length reward
- No process reward model (PRM)
- No value function (critic)

The paper argues these removals follow recent best practices (Dr.GRPO, SimpleRL-Zero) that found them unnecessary or harmful for reasoning RL. The simplicity of the reward signal — pure outcome correctness — is preserved from standard RLVR, with the off-policy augmentation being the only addition.

**Evaluation protocol:**
- For AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC (small test sets): avg@32 (average accuracy over 32 sampled solutions per question)
- For MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench: pass@1 (single greedy or temperature-0.6 sample)
- Out-of-distribution benchmarks (ARC-c, GPQA-diamond, MMLU-Pro): pass@1 with shuffled multiple-choice options
- Significance testing: paired bootstrap resampling with 1000 samples, testing whether LUFFY significantly outperforms baselines at $p < 0.05$

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Off-Policy Guidance as Capability Expansion, Not Just Sample Efficiency

The dominant framing of off-policy RL in the broader literature — and even in the LLM post-training community — is **sample efficiency**: use data from any policy to get more gradient steps per environment interaction. In classical RL (DQN, SAC, TD3), the benefit of off-policy learning is that you can replay old experiences rather than discarding them after one update. The paper departs from this framing entirely. It argues that off-policy data serves a fundamentally different purpose in the RLVR context: **it expands the set of behaviors the model can learn, rather than merely making learning faster within an already-explored region.**

This distinction matters because sample efficiency is an optimization concern — you could achieve the same final performance with more on-policy samples, just at greater cost. Capability expansion is a *representational* concern — no amount of additional on-policy sampling can teach behaviors the model never generates. The paper provides direct evidence for this distinction in Figure 4 and Section 5.2: LLaMA-3.1-8B trained with on-policy RL on the Hard training set sees training rewards collapse to zero — the model never generates correct solutions, so it receives no positive gradient signal and learns nothing. Additional on-policy samples would not help; the model is stuck at a fixed point where the policy gradient is identically zero because all advantages are zero. LUFFY, by injecting a single correct off-policy trace per prompt, breaks this deadlock — not by making learning faster, but by making it *possible*.

Prior work on distillation (SFT from DeepSeek-R1 traces, as in DeepSeek-R1's own pipeline) implicitly recognized that external data can teach new capabilities. But distillation is not framed as RL — it's supervised learning that memorizes the teacher's outputs. The paper's framing positions off-policy guidance as a **learning signal that enables reinforcement, not a substitute for it**. The model doesn't memorize the teacher's trace; it uses the trace's positive advantage to get gradient information about which high-level reasoning patterns lead to correctness, then continues to explore its own variations through the on-policy objective. The fact that LUFFY outperforms SFT by 6.0 points on in-distribution math (50.1 vs. 44.1, Table 1) and by 10.3 points on out-of-distribution tasks (57.8 vs. 47.5) demonstrates that off-policy guidance as an RL signal produces generalization that pure imitation cannot.

This is a **fundamental reframing** of off-policy learning's role in LLM post-training, not an incremental improvement. It recasts the exploration problem — previously treated as an intractable search over an enormous action space — as a *guided* exploration problem where external demonstrations provide direction without constraining the final solution distribution to the teacher's.

---

### Innovation 2: Entropy Collapse as THE Central Failure Mode of Naive Off-Policy Integration

The paper makes a **diagnostic contribution** that the field had not articulated before: the primary obstacle to combining on-policy and off-policy objectives is not distribution shift (the classical importance sampling concern), but **selective imitation of high-probability tokens that causes premature entropy collapse**. This is conceptually distinct from standard off-policy RL challenges. In classical RL, the challenge is that data from a different policy produces biased or high-variance gradient estimates because the importance weights $\pi_\theta / \pi_\phi$ have high variance when the behavior and target policies diverge. The standard solution is to bound importance weights (e.g., through clipping in PPO) or to use techniques like retrace.

The paper shows that in the LLM context, the problem is subtler and more specific. The model can hack the mixed-policy objective by reinforcing off-policy tokens that are *already likely* under its own distribution — surface-level patterns like "Let's analyze this step by step" — while ignoring tokens that are genuinely novel. The evidence is Figure 2 (left): Mixed-Policy GRPO without shaping causes generation entropy to collapse **faster** than on-policy RL alone (entropy drops to near zero by step ~200 vs. ~400 for on-policy). Yet Mixed-Policy achieves higher early accuracy (Figure 6, green line peaks early), creating an illusion of success that masks the underlying failure: the model hasn't learned deeper reasoning, it has just become more confident in the easy parts of the teacher's traces.

This diagnostic matters because it explains *why* prior attempts at combining SFT and RL (SFT+RL, RL with SFT loss) underperform expectations. Those methods expose the model to off-policy data but provide no mechanism to prevent the model from selectively imitating surface patterns. The result — visible in Appendix F, Figure 10 — is that SFT-trained models produce excessively long outputs (averaging 4,646 tokens vs. 2,832 for LUFFY) that superficially resemble the teacher's verbosity without capturing the teacher's reasoning depth. SFT+RL partially recovers from this (improving from SFT's 44.1 to 48.2 on math, Table 1), but the rigid imitation from SFT persists through RL training, limiting out-of-distribution generalization to 44.8 — far below on-policy RL's 57.3.

Prior work had observed that SFT leads to memorization rather than generalization (Chu et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025), but had not identified entropy collapse as the *mechanism* linking off-policy data exposure to poor generalization. The paper's identification of this failure mode — and its demonstration that policy shaping can prevent it — is a **diagnostic advance** that provides a concrete hypothesis for why future off-policy approaches succeed or fail: do they maintain exploration, or do they collapse to high-probability imitation?

---

### Innovation 3: Policy Shaping as a General Mechanism for Gradient Re-Weighting Under Distribution Mismatch

The policy shaping function $f(x) = x/(x+\gamma)$ is not, in itself, a complex mathematical innovation — concave transformations of importance weights have been studied in the importance sampling literature for variance reduction. What makes LUFFY's policy shaping **distinctive** is its role as a **curriculum mechanism** that operates at the token level, continuously adjusting which parts of the teacher's demonstrations the model learns from based on the model's current competence.

The key insight, formalized in the gradient analysis of Eq. 7–8, is that the shaping function's derivative $f'(\pi_\theta) = \gamma/(\pi_\theta + \gamma)^2$ acts as an **automatic difficulty-weighting scheme**. When the model assigns low probability to a teacher token ($\pi_\theta$ is small, indicating this is an unfamiliar reasoning pattern), the shaping derivative is large (~1/$\gamma$), amplifying the gradient. When the model assigns high probability ($\pi_\theta \approx 1$, indicating this is a pattern the model already knows well), the derivative is small (~$\gamma/(1+\gamma)^2$), dampening the gradient. The model doesn't need an explicit difficulty estimator or a hand-designed curriculum schedule — the shaping function implicitly creates a curriculum where the model focuses on the tokens it finds hardest to predict, which are precisely the tokens encoding reasoning capabilities beyond its current distribution.

This is fundamentally different from prior approaches to curriculum learning in LLM post-training, which typically operate at the **example level** (train on easy problems first, then hard) or the **objective level** (increase the weight of the RL term relative to SFT over time). LUFFY's curriculum operates at the **token level and is self-adjusting** — as the model becomes more confident in certain reasoning patterns (increasing $\pi_\theta$ for those tokens), the shaping function automatically reduces their gradient weight, shifting focus to remaining low-probability patterns. This creates a natural progression: early training focuses on making unfamiliar tokens merely plausible; later training refines the model's distribution over the full reasoning trace.

The connection to variance reduction (Appendix B.2) strengthens this claim. The shaped weights have lower variance than the original importance weights, which means the gradient signal from off-policy data is more stable — not just differently weighted, but *more reliable*. This is important because high-variance gradients from off-policy data could cause training instability (large, noisy updates that damage previously learned capabilities). The shaping function simultaneously amplifies learning from novel patterns while keeping the overall gradient variance manageable.

The significance of this innovation extends beyond LUFFY itself. The idea of using a concave transformation of importance weights to create an implicit token-level curriculum could be applied to other settings where models learn from heterogeneous demonstrations — multi-task learning from experts of varying quality, domain adaptation, or any scenario where some parts of a demonstration are more informative than others based on the learner's current state.

---

### Innovation 4: Empirical Proof That On-Policy RLVR Has a Hard Capability Ceiling (and Off-Policy Guidance Breaks It)

One of the paper's most consequential contributions is a clear, experimentally demonstrated **boundary condition** for on-policy RLVR: if the base model cannot generate correct solutions for a problem class (pass@1 effectively zero), on-policy RL provides **no benefit whatsoever** — training rewards collapse to zero and the model learns nothing. This is not merely an efficiency limit; it is an **absolute capability ceiling**.

Prior work had hinted at this limitation theoretically. Zhao et al. (2025) argued that "RL post-training amplifies behaviors learned in pretraining," and Yue et al. (2025) questioned whether RL "really incentivizes reasoning capacity beyond the base model." But these were largely observational studies — they showed that RL-trained models didn't exhibit fundamentally new behaviors, but couldn't experimentally isolate whether this was due to insufficient training, poor reward design, or an inherent limitation. The LLaMA-3.1-8B experiments in Section 5.2 and Figure 4 provide the cleanest isolation yet.

The experiment is simple but decisive: take LLaMA-3.1-8B (a weaker base model than Qwen2.5-Math-7B) and train it with on-policy RLVR on two datasets of different difficulty. On the Easy set (problems DeepSeek-R1 solves in ≤2K tokens), on-policy RL works — training rewards improve, the model learns. On the Hard set (problems requiring 2K–4K tokens for DeepSeek-R1), on-policy RL fails completely — training rewards collapse to zero, visible in Figure 4 (right panel) where the orange line (Llama-On-Policy-Hard) stays flat at zero while the purple line (Llama-LUFFY-Hard) rises steadily. The base model literally never generates a correct solution on the Hard set, so every rollout gets reward 0, every advantage is (approximately) 0, and no learning occurs.

This result is significant for two reasons. **First**, it provides a concrete diagnostic for practitioners: before investing in RLVR training, check whether the base model achieves non-zero pass@1 on the target distribution. If it doesn't, RLVR alone won't help — you need external guidance (like LUFFY) or a better base model. **Second**, it demonstrates that LUFFY genuinely **transcends** the on-policy ceiling rather than merely accelerating convergence within it. The model trained with LUFFY on the Hard set achieves non-trivial performance, proving that off-policy guidance enables learning of capabilities the base model could not discover through self-exploration alone.

This finding contrasts sharply with the narrative that "RL is all you need" for reasoning — a narrative implicitly advanced by the success of DeepSeek-R1, which showed impressive emergent behaviors from pure RLVR. The paper doesn't contradict DeepSeek-R1's results but provides a more precise picture: RLVR works spectacularly well when the base model **already possesses** latent reasoning capabilities that just need amplification, but fails when those capabilities are genuinely absent. DeepSeek-R1 started from DeepSeek-V3, a very strong base model; LUFFY's contribution is showing how to extend RLVR's benefits to weaker base models that lack pre-existing reasoning competencies. This is a **boundary-mapping contribution** that clarifies the scope of applicability of the dominant paradigm.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary training data is a filtered subset of OpenR1-Math-220k, a public dataset of math problem prompts with DeepSeek-R1-generated reasoning traces. The paper starts with the default 94K-prompt subset, filters out generations longer than 8192 tokens, and retains only traces where Math-Verify confirms the extracted answer matches the ground truth — yielding 45K prompts, each paired with one verified-correct off-policy reasoning trace. For evaluation, the paper uses six in-distribution math benchmarks: AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC (all three from NuminaMath), MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021), Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024). Three out-of-distribution benchmarks test generalization: ARC-c (Clark et al., 2018, open-domain reasoning), GPQA-diamond (Rein et al., 2024, graduate-level science, denoted GPQA*), and MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024, reasoning-focused academic questions). For the LLaMA-3.1-8B experiments in Section 5.2, the paper creates two difficulty-stratified subsets: an Easy set (7.3K prompts where DeepSeek-R1's correct solution is ≤2K tokens) and a Hard set (25.4K prompts where the solution is ≤4K tokens), using solution length as a proxy for problem difficulty since longer traces indicate harder problems.

- **Base model(s).** The primary model is Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Yang et al., 2024), chosen because it is the standard base model in prior RLVR work (SimpleRL-Zero, Oat-Zero, PRIME-Zero, OpenReasonerZero), enabling direct comparison. To test generalizability, LUFFY is extended to Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (smaller scale), Qwen2.5-Instruct-7B (instruction-tuned rather than math-specialized), and LLaMA-3.1-8B (Meta, 2024, a weaker model for the capability-ceiling experiments). For Qwen2.5-Math-7B, the context window is extended from 4096 to 16384 tokens and the rope theta is increased from 10000 to 40000 to accommodate long off-policy traces; Qwen2.5-Instruct-7B and LLaMA-3.1-8B require no such modifications.

- **Metrics.** For AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC — which have relatively small test sets — the paper reports avg@32 (average accuracy over 32 independently sampled solutions per question), a standard metric that reduces variance from the stochastic decoding. For MATH-500, Minerva, and OlympiadBench, which have larger test sets, pass@1 (single-sample accuracy) is reported. For out-of-distribution benchmarks (ARC-c, GPQA*, MMLU-Pro), pass@1 is reported with multiple-choice options randomly shuffled to prevent contamination from memorized answer ordering. All answers are graded using Math-Verify, a rule-based verifier that extracts the final answer from \boxed{} formatting and checks mathematical equivalence against the ground truth using SymPy. The primary summary metric is the arithmetic mean across the six in-distribution benchmarks. Statistical significance is assessed via paired bootstrap resampling with 1000 samples, testing whether LUFFY's performance advantage over baselines is statistically reliable at p < 0.05.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against four categories of methods. **(1) Prior RLVR approaches:** SimpleRL-Zero (Zeng et al., 2025) trains Qwen2.5-Math-7B with rule-based reward; Oat-Zero (Liu et al., 2025) uses the same base model but removes standard deviation normalization in advantage computation and token-level normalization in the loss; PRIME-Zero (Cui et al., 2025) incorporates implicit process rewards from policy rollouts; OpenReasonerZero (Hu et al., 2025) is a recent open-source RLVR implementation. All four are built on Qwen2.5-Math-7B but differ in dataset composition, optimization strategies, and reward structures. **(2) On-Policy RL replication:** trained by the authors using Dr.GRPO with the same reward (Math-Verify only, no format or length rewards) and the same training data as LUFFY, using 8 on-policy rollouts per prompt — this is the direct apples-to-apples baseline that isolates the effect of adding off-policy guidance. **(3) Alternative off-policy learning methods:** SFT trains the model for 3 epochs on the same DeepSeek-R1 traces and prompts; RL w/ SFT Loss adds an SFT auxiliary loss during RL training on the off-policy traces; SFT+RL is a two-stage pipeline that performs 3 epochs of SFT followed by 500 steps of on-policy RL on held-out data. **(4) Instruct models:** Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct serves as a reference for the base model's instruction-tuned performance without RLVR.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Fair comparison is ensured by matching the total number of rollouts per prompt: On-Policy RL uses 8 on-policy rollouts, LUFFY uses 7 on-policy + 1 off-policy = 8 total. All RL-trained models use temperature 1.0 for rollout generation and are trained for 500 steps (except LUFFY†, trained for 860 steps to match SFT+RL's GPU hours). GPU hours are reported in Table 2 to account for wall-clock time differences: LUFFY requires 77 GPU-hours (× 8 A100 GPUs), SFT requires 24, SFT+RL requires 130, and RL w/ SFT Loss requires 133 — with the latter two suffering from excessively long generations induced by rigid SFT imitation that inflates the RL roll-out cost. For the FLOPs-matched comparison between LUFFY and other off-policy methods, Table 2 additionally reports data usage: LUFFY uses 64K on-policy samples (×7 rollouts each) + 64K off-policy samples, while SFT+RL uses 64K on-policy (×8) + 135K off-policy, making LUFFY both more compute-efficient and more data-efficient.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no cross-validation in the standard sense — the test sets are fixed benchmarks with publicly available splits, and all models are evaluated on the same test questions. The statistical significance protocol uses paired bootstrap resampling (Koehn, 2004) with 1000 resamples, comparing LUFFY against each baseline to determine whether observed differences exceed what would be expected from random sampling variation. The results in Table 1 mark LUFFY and LUFFY† with an asterisk to indicate "significantly better than baselines (p < 0.05)." The paper does not report confidence intervals on individual benchmark scores, only the bootstrap-based significance test on aggregate comparisons.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### In-Distribution Math Reasoning Performance (Table 1)

The headline result: LUFFY achieves an average score of 50.1 across the six in-distribution math benchmarks (AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench), outperforming the best prior RLVR method (OpenReasonerZero at 41.0) by +9.1 points and the authors' own On-Policy RL replication (45.5) by +4.6 points. LUFFY†, trained for additional steps to match SFT+RL's GPU hours, reaches 50.4 average.

On individual benchmarks, the pattern of improvement is uneven, which is informative:

- **AIME 2024:** LUFFY achieves 29.4, substantially below Oat-Zero's 33.4 — the best prior method on this specific benchmark. This suggests that on AIME 2024's specific problem distribution, pure on-policy RL with Oat-Zero's optimization choices (removing standard deviation and length normalization) is particularly effective, and off-policy guidance does not provide additional benefit. The paper does not explain this exception, but it may indicate that AIME 2024 problems are within Qwen2.5-Math-7B's capability range (so on-policy exploration is sufficient) and that Oat-Zero's optimization tweaks independently improve performance.

- **AIME 2025:** LUFFY achieves 23.1, dramatically higher than all baselines — the next best is SFT at 22.3, followed by SFT+RL at 23.1 (tied), with on-policy RL at only 15.3. The +7.8 point gain over on-policy RL on AIME 2025 is LUFFY's largest per-benchmark improvement, and the paper highlights this as evidence that LUFFY "demonstrates significantly greater advantage on the newly released AIME 25 test set." The interpretation: AIME 2025 problems, being newly released, are less likely to be in the base model's training data, so the model benefits more from exposure to DeepSeek-R1's reasoning patterns (via off-policy traces) compared to problems where the base model already has latent knowledge that on-policy RL can amplify.

- **AMC:** LUFFY at 65.6 vs. On-Policy RL at 62.0 — a solid +3.6 point gain.

- **MATH-500:** LUFFY at 87.6 vs. On-Policy RL at 84.4 — a +3.2 point gain, with SFT+RL slightly higher at 87.2. MATH-500 is a relatively saturated benchmark where ceiling effects may compress differences.

- **Minerva:** LUFFY at 37.5 — notably *below* On-Policy RL's 39.3. This is the only in-distribution benchmark where LUFFY underperforms its on-policy counterpart. The paper does not comment on this reversal, but it suggests that Minerva's problem distribution (which includes more scientific and quantitative reasoning beyond pure math) may not benefit from the specific off-policy traces, which are math-focused from DeepSeek-R1. The off-policy guidance may even be slightly misleading for Minerva-style problems.

- **OlympiadBench:** LUFFY at 57.2 vs. On-Policy RL at 46.8 — a massive +10.4 point gain, the largest absolute improvement on any benchmark. OlympiadBench contains Olympiad-level problems that likely require sophisticated multi-step reasoning, making off-policy guidance particularly valuable.

Comparing LUFFY against alternative off-policy methods reveals the advantage of the mixed-policy approach over pure imitation:

- **LUFFY vs. SFT (50.1 vs. 44.1):** A +6.0 point advantage. SFT performs competitively on AIME 2025 (22.3) and OlympiadBench (43.7) but substantially worse on AIME 2024 (22.2 vs. 29.4) and AMC (52.8 vs. 65.6). The pattern suggests SFT's memorized reasoning patterns are fragile — they help on problems resembling the training distribution but fail on problems requiring adaptive reasoning.

- **LUFFY vs. SFT+RL (50.1 vs. 48.2):** A +1.9 point advantage, with SFT+RL actually outperforming LUFFY on MATH-500 (87.2 vs. 87.6, essentially tied) and matching on AIME 2025 (23.1). The smaller gap here indicates that RL after SFT partially recovers from the rigidity of pure SFT, but the two-stage approach still falls short of LUFFY's integrated method.

- **LUFFY vs. RL w/ SFT Loss (50.1 vs. 40.1):** A +10.0 point advantage, the largest gap among off-policy methods. Multi-tasking RL and SFT objectives in a single stage performs substantially worse than either sequential combination or LUFFY's mixed-policy approach, likely due to conflicting gradient signals — the SFT loss pulls toward exact imitation while the RL loss pushes toward exploration.

The resource comparison in Table 2 contextualizes these gains: LUFFY achieves higher performance than SFT+RL (50.1 vs. 48.2) while using only 59% of the GPU hours (77 vs. 130) and less than half the off-policy data (64K vs. 135K traces). When LUFFY is trained for matching GPU hours (LUFFY†, 860 steps), it reaches 50.4 average, widening the gap to +2.2 points.

#### Out-of-Distribution Generalization (Table 1, right columns)

The headline: LUFFY achieves an average of 57.8 across the three out-of-distribution benchmarks (ARC-c, GPQA*, MMLU-Pro), substantially outperforming all baselines. The +6.2 point advantage over the best prior RLVR method (OpenReasonerZero at 51.6) and the +0.5 point advantage over On-Policy RL (57.3) demonstrate that off-policy guidance does not come at the cost of generalization — a concern that might arise if off-policy traces caused overfitting to the teacher's reasoning style.

The per-benchmark breakdown reveals important patterns:

- **ARC-c:** LUFFY at 80.5 vs. On-Policy RL at 82.3 — a slight decrease. ARC-c tests open-domain reasoning (science exam questions), which may rely more on factual knowledge than mathematical reasoning patterns, making off-policy traces from a math-focused model less beneficial.

- **GPQA*:** LUFFY at 39.9 vs. On-Policy RL at 40.4 — essentially tied. GPQA tests graduate-level science knowledge, again outside the math domain where off-policy traces are most relevant.

- **MMLU-Pro:** LUFFY at 53.0 vs. On-Policy RL at 49.3 — a +3.7 point gain. MMLU-Pro focuses on reasoning-intensive academic questions, which may benefit more directly from general reasoning patterns in the off-policy traces.

The most striking out-of-distribution comparison is LUFFY vs. SFT+RL: 57.8 vs. 44.8 — a **+13.0 point gap**. This is the paper's strongest evidence that SFT-based off-policy learning produces brittle, non-generalizing models, while LUFFY's mixed-policy approach preserves the generalization benefits of RL. The SFT+RL model, despite strong in-distribution performance (48.2, second only to LUFFY), collapses on out-of-distribution tasks, scoring only 72.4 on ARC-c (vs. LUFFY's 80.5), 24.2 on GPQA* (vs. LUFFY's 39.9), and 37.7 on MMLU-Pro (vs. LUFFY's 53.0). This pattern — strong in-distribution, weak out-of-distribution — is exactly what one would expect from a model that has memorized surface-level patterns rather than acquiring generalizable reasoning.

LUFFY† (extended training) achieves 61.8 average on OOD tasks, with a dramatic improvement on GPQA* (49.0 vs. LUFFY's 39.9, a +9.1 point jump), suggesting that additional training steps particularly benefit the most knowledge-intensive out-of-distribution benchmark.

#### Extension to More Models (Table 3, Figure 3)

LUFFY is tested on three additional base models to assess generalizability beyond Qwen2.5-Math-7B:

- **Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B:** LUFFY achieves 38.0 average across the six math benchmarks, compared to 31.9 for SFT (+6.1 points) and 30.0 for On-Policy RL (+8.0 points). The larger gap over on-policy RL for the smaller model (+8.0 vs. +4.6 for the 7B model) suggests that off-policy guidance is particularly valuable when the base model is weaker — the smaller model benefits more from exposure to the teacher's reasoning because its own exploration is more limited. The Instruct version (without LUFFY) achieves 35.7, meaning LUFFY provides a +2.3 point improvement over instruction tuning alone.

- **Qwen2.5-Instruct-7B:** LUFFY achieves 40.7, compared to 29.0 for SFT (+11.7 points) and 35.2 for On-Policy RL (+5.5 points). The massive gap over SFT (40.7 vs. 29.0) on an instruction-tuned (non-math-specialized) model highlights how SFT's rigid imitation fails when the base model lacks math-specific pretraining — the model cannot effectively memorize reasoning patterns for a domain it was not pretrained on. LUFFY's mixed-policy approach, by contrast, enables the model to selectively internalize useful patterns while maintaining its own generation style.

- **LLaMA-3.1-8B:** LUFFY achieves 13.2, compared to 5.9 for SFT (+7.3 points) and 9.6 for On-Policy RL (+3.6 points). All numbers are substantially lower than for Qwen2.5-based models, reflecting LLaMA-3.1-8B's weaker math pretraining. The Instruct model achieves 17.1 — notably higher than LUFFY's 13.2, which appears to contradict the claim that LUFFY improves over baselines. However, this comparison is misleading because the Instruct model's 17.1 likely reflects instruction-tuning on math data that the base LLaMA-3.1-8B did not receive; LUFFY starts from the base model and adds reasoning capabilities through off-policy RL. The fair comparison is LUFFY vs. On-Policy RL (13.2 vs. 9.6) and LUFFY vs. SFT (13.2 vs. 5.9), both of which show gains. The absolute numbers remain low because LLaMA-3.1-8B fundamentally lacks the mathematical knowledge that Qwen2.5-Math models were pretrained on — off-policy guidance can teach reasoning patterns but cannot inject missing factual or procedural math knowledge.

#### LLaMA-3.1-8B on Different Difficulty Levels (Section 5.2, Figure 4)

This experiment is the paper's most direct evidence that LUFFY transcends on-policy RLVR's capability ceiling. The setup: train LLaMA-3.1-8B (a weaker model) with both On-Policy RL and LUFFY on two datasets — an Easy set (7.3K prompts where DeepSeek-R1 solves problems in ≤2K tokens) and a Hard set (25.4K prompts requiring 2K–4K tokens).

**Easy dataset (Figure 4, left):** Both methods work. On-Policy RL training rewards rise from near-zero to approximately 0.2–0.25 over 500 steps. LUFFY training rewards rise similarly, with a slight advantage by the end of training. This is the regime where the base model can occasionally generate correct solutions (pass@1 > 0), so on-policy RL receives positive gradient signals and learns. The slight LUFFY advantage suggests off-policy guidance helps even when on-policy RL works, but the effect is modest.

**Hard dataset (Figure 4, right):** This is the critical finding. On-Policy RL training rewards collapse to zero and stay there — the flat orange line at 0 throughout all 500 steps. The base model never generates a correct solution on the Hard set; every rollout receives reward 0; all advantages are (approximately) 0; gradient updates are essentially noise. In contrast, LUFFY training rewards (purple line) rise steadily from near-zero to approximately 0.1–0.15 over 500 steps. The single off-policy trace per prompt provides a positive advantage that breaks the zero-gradient deadlock, enabling the model to learn even though it never independently produces a correct solution during training.

This result is described with precision: "on-policy reinforcement learning performs well on the Easy subset but fails on the Hard subset, where training rewards collapse to zero, since on-policy rollouts struggle to obtain positive feedback signals. In contrast, LUFFY achieves stable reward improvements on both datasets." The paper does not report final evaluation accuracy for these models (only training rewards), so we cannot quantify how much LUFFY improves test performance on the Hard set — only that it successfully trains where on-policy RL completely fails. This is the most important missing quantitative result in the entire paper: what accuracy does the LLaMA-3.1-8B LUFFY-trained-on-Hard model achieve on the math benchmarks? The training reward curve shows learning is occurring, but without evaluation scores, we cannot assess whether the learned capabilities are meaningful.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Components of LUFFY (Table 4):** The ablation decomposes LUFFY into its constituent parts, starting from bare Mixed-Policy RL (Eq. 5, without shaping or clip removal) and adding components incrementally:

- **Mixed-Policy RL alone:** 44.4 average across six benchmarks. This is already below On-Policy RL's 45.5 (Table 1), confirming that naive off-policy integration is counterproductive — the entropy collapse identified in Section 3.2 causes Mixed-Policy RL to actually perform *worse* than pure on-policy training, despite having access to additional high-quality data.

- **+ Policy Shaping:** 47.8 average, a +3.4 point improvement over Mixed-Policy RL alone. This recovers and exceeds On-Policy RL's performance (45.5 → 47.8), demonstrating that policy shaping is the critical component that makes off-policy guidance beneficial rather than harmful.

- **+ Shaping + NoClip (full LUFFY):** 50.1 average, an additional +2.3 point improvement. Removing the on-policy clip allows the model to make larger updates toward the teacher's distribution without being constrained by the trust region around $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, which is appropriate because the teacher's traces may be far from the current policy.

The ablation also tests whether policy shaping and clip removal benefit on-policy RL without off-policy data:

- **On-Policy RL + Shaping:** 42.0 average, *worse* than On-Policy RL alone (45.5). Policy shaping without off-policy data is counterproductive — the shaping function is designed to amplify gradients for low-probability tokens in the *teacher's* distribution, but when there is no teacher, it simply distorts the on-policy gradient in ways that don't help.

- **On-Policy RL + No Clip:** 44.9 average, slightly below On-Policy RL (45.5). Removing the clip from pure on-policy training doesn't help and may slightly hurt by allowing overly aggressive updates.

**Gamma parameter in policy shaping (Figure 9):** Sweeping $\gamma \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5\}$ on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, LUFFY achieves peak accuracy of 50.1 at $\gamma = 0.1$. Performance degrades on both sides: $\gamma = 0.05$ yields approximately 48.5, $\gamma = 0.2$ yields approximately 49.0, and $\gamma = 0.5$ yields approximately 47.0. The shape of the curve suggests a genuine optimum rather than saturation — the shaping function's amplification of low-probability tokens can be too weak (high $\gamma$, insufficient amplification, entropy still collapses) or too strong (low $\gamma$, over-amplification of rare tokens, model overfits to non-generalizable teacher patterns).

**Removing on-policy clip (Figure 8):** The clip ratio (fraction of on-policy tokens where the importance ratio exceeds $1 \pm \epsilon$ and gets clipped) is monitored during training. LUFFY (with off-policy data) experiences higher clip ratios than On-Policy RL — roughly 0.10–0.20 vs. 0.05–0.10 — because the off-policy guidance pulls the policy in directions that deviate more from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$. This provides empirical justification for the design choice: the standard clip would suppress exactly the updates that off-policy guidance is meant to enable.

**Removing on-policy clip from ablation (Table 4, bottom rows):** Adding Shaping or NoClip to On-Policy RL (without off-policy data) hurts performance: On-Policy + Shaping drops to 42.0, On-Policy + No Clip drops to 44.9. This confirms that these components are specifically designed for the off-policy setting and are not general-purpose improvements to GRPO.

**Training dynamics (Figure 5):** Three metrics are tracked over 500 steps for On-Policy RL and LUFFY:

- **Outcome training rewards (left panel):** LUFFY starts lower than On-Policy RL (around 0.12 vs. 0.15 at step 0), dips to approximately 0.10 around step 100, then rises and crosses above On-Policy RL around step 200, reaching approximately 0.28 vs. 0.24 at step 500. The early dip is interpreted as the model "adjusting to external guidance and potentially sophisticated cognitive behaviors."

- **Generation length (middle panel):** LUFFY's average generation length rises from approximately 1200 tokens at step 0 to 2200 at step 500, gradually approaching the off-policy traces' average length (around 2500–3000 tokens, shown as a dashed line). On-Policy RL's generation length stays relatively flat between 1200–1800 tokens. This provides evidence that LUFFY learns to produce more extensive reasoning, not just superficial imitation — the length increase is gradual, not abrupt (which would indicate copying the teacher's verbosity).

- **Generation entropy (right panel):** On-Policy RL entropy drops from approximately 0.40 to near-zero by step 200, indicating complete loss of exploration. LUFFY entropy starts lower (around 0.25) but drops more slowly and stabilizes around 0.10–0.15 with occasional fluctuations and increases (notably between steps 200–250, where entropy rises from 0.10 to 0.15 before declining again). The sustained non-zero entropy and fluctuations are evidence that policy shaping prevents complete collapse and enables ongoing exploration.

**Policy shaping effect on validation performance (Figure 6):** Validation accuracy (on MATH-500, sampled periodically during training) is tracked for three conditions: On-Policy RL, Mixed-Policy RL (no shaping), and Mixed-Policy RL + Shaping (LUFFY). Mixed-Policy without shaping achieves rapid early gains (reaching approximately 47% by step 100 vs. On-Policy RL's 42%) but plateaus by step 200 and eventually converges with On-Policy RL around 48–50%. LUFFY (with shaping) starts slightly slower but continues to improve throughout training, reaching approximately 52% by step 500. This is direct evidence that policy shaping's primary benefit is not faster initial learning but sustained improvement — without shaping, the model hits a ceiling caused by entropy collapse; with shaping, it continues to acquire new capabilities.

**LUFFY vs. SFT: generation characteristics (Appendix F, Figure 10):** On the combined in-distribution test set, LUFFY produces significantly shorter correct solutions on average (2,832 tokens) compared to SFT (4,646 tokens), with the length distributions showing LUFFY's correct solutions concentrated in the 1500–4000 token range while SFT's spread across 2000–8000+. For incorrect solutions, the contrast is even starker: SFT frequently produces excessively long, unproductive traces (many exceeding 6000 tokens), while LUFFY's incorrect solutions remain more moderate. This is evidence that LUFFY learns to reason efficiently rather than mimicking the teacher's verbosity.

**LUFFY vs. SFT: test-time exploration (Appendix F, Figure 12):** Pass@8 accuracy on the combined AIME 2024 + AMC set is measured as generation temperature varies from 0.1 to 1.0. All three methods (On-Policy RL, LUFFY, SFT) perform similarly at temperature 0.1 (approximately 72–74%). As temperature increases, On-Policy RL improves to approximately 78% at temperature 1.0, LUFFY improves to approximately 80%, while SFT *degrades* to approximately 66%. This is the paper's cleanest evidence that SFT memorizes reasoning patterns that collapse under stochastic decoding, while RL-based methods (both on-policy and LUFFY) learn to explore productively at higher temperatures, consistent with recent findings that "SFT memorizes, RL generalizes" (Chu et al., 2025).

**Similarity to teacher traces (Appendix F):** BLEU scores between model outputs and DeepSeek-R1 traces are computed on a held-out set: SFT = 57.5, LUFFY = 44.8, On-Policy RL = 8.8. This quantifies the imitation spectrum: SFT heavily copies surface patterns, On-Policy RL learns independently, and LUFFY strikes an intermediate balance — substantially more imitation than On-Policy RL but substantially less than SFT.

**RL w/ SFT Loss generation length dynamics (Appendix F, Figure 11):** Unlike LUFFY, where generation length increases gradually toward the off-policy average, RL w/ SFT Loss shows a rapid spike — generation length jumps from approximately 1500 to over 5000 tokens early in training and stays elevated, even exceeding the off-policy traces' average length. This helps explain why RL w/ SFT Loss underperforms (40.1 average): the model latches onto the teacher's verbosity as the primary signal, generating long but often incorrect traces, with the SFT loss dominating the RL signal.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments support LUFFY's three central claims — but with important boundary conditions and evidential gaps that the paper does not always acknowledge.

**Claim 1: "LUFFY achieves an average gain of +6.4 points across six math benchmarks against previous RLVR methods."** This is the headline claim from the abstract and is numerically true (50.1 vs. the best prior method, OpenReasonerZero at 41.0, a gap of +9.1; or vs. the On-Policy RL replication at 45.5, a gap of +4.6 — the +6.4 figure appears to average across multiple baselines or benchmarks in a way not precisely specified). However, the claim obscures substantial benchmark-level variance. On AIME 2024, LUFFY scores 29.4 vs. Oat-Zero's 33.4 — LUFFY **loses** to a prior method. On Minerva, LUFFY scores 37.5 vs. On-Policy RL's 39.3 — LUFFY **loses** to the on-policy baseline. On MATH-500, LUFFY's 87.6 vs. SFT+RL's 87.2 is essentially tied. The average improvement is driven disproportionately by OlympiadBench (+10.4 over On-Policy RL) and AIME 2025 (+7.8). Whether the +6.4 average represents genuine, consistent improvement or is inflated by a few outlier benchmarks is not discussed. Bootstrap confidence intervals on per-benchmark differences — which would quantify the uncertainty — are not reported; only aggregate significance tests appear.

**Claim 2: "LUFFY successfully trains weak foundation models where On-Policy RL fails."** The LLaMA-3.1-8B experiment on hard data (Figure 4, right) is compelling as a proof of concept: on-policy training rewards are literally zero for 500 steps while LUFFY's rise to ~0.1–0.15. However, this experiment is **incomplete in a critical way**: we see training rewards improving, but we do not see final evaluation accuracy. Training reward curves are intermediate signals — they show the model is receiving non-zero gradients and learning *something*, but not whether what it learns translates to meaningful test performance. A model could have rising training rewards (by learning to produce outputs that occasionally match the ground truth through shallow pattern matching) while still achieving near-zero test accuracy on genuinely novel problems. The paper should have reported the final six-benchmark average for the LLaMA-3.1-8B model trained on the Hard set, as it does for all other models. Without this, we cannot assess the **magnitude** of capability transcendence — only that some learning occurs where on-policy RL learns nothing.

Additionally, the claim that LUFFY "transcends the fundamental limitations of on-policy RLVR" (abstract) is supported only for LLaMA-3.1-8B on the specific Hard dataset. For Qwen2.5-Math-7B — the main model — on-policy RL achieves 45.5 average, which is not a failure. The transcendence claim is demonstrated only at the boundary where on-policy RL collapses entirely; it is not shown that LUFFY can teach capabilities that are *moderately* beyond the base model's reach (where on-policy RL gets some signal but plateaus). The paper would be stronger with experiments on an intermediate-difficulty dataset where on-policy RL makes partial progress but plateaus, showing that LUFFY breaks through the plateau.

**Claim 3: "Policy shaping prevents entropy collapse."** This claim is well-supported by the entropy curves in Figures 2 (left) and 5 (right), and by the validation accuracy trajectories in Figure 6. The mechanism is observable: mixing on-policy and off-policy objectives without shaping causes faster entropy collapse than on-policy alone; adding shaping sustains higher entropy and enables continued accuracy improvement after the unshaped version plateaus. The ablation (Table 4) confirms that Mixed-Policy RL without shaping performs *worse* than On-Policy RL (44.4 vs. 45.5), and adding shaping recovers and exceeds on-policy performance. However, the paper does not isolate whether policy shaping works primarily through **(a)** re-weighting low-probability tokens (the claimed mechanism) or **(b)** simply reducing the effective learning rate on off-policy data (since $f(x) \leq 1$ always, shaping effectively scales down the off-policy gradient magnitude for all tokens, with proportionally less scaling for low-probability ones). Distinguishing these would require an ablation that scales the off-policy loss weight without reshaping — e.g., a constant multiplier on the off-policy term — which is not tested.

**Claim 4: "LUFFY achieves superior out-of-distribution generalization."** The OOD results (Table 1, right) show LUFFY at 57.8 vs. On-Policy RL at 57.3 — a +0.5 point difference that is likely within statistical noise (no confidence intervals are reported for OOD benchmarks). The dramatic OOD gap is between LUFFY and the SFT-based methods (SFT+RL at 44.8, a +13.0 gap; SFT at 47.5, a +10.3 gap), not between LUFFY and On-Policy RL. This means the claim that LUFFY "demonstrates superior generalization capability" is true **relative to other off-policy methods** but not relative to on-policy RL — on-policy RL already generalizes well; LUFFY matches that generalization while adding in-distribution gains. The paper's abstract claim of "+6.2 points on out-of-distribution tasks" is against the best prior RLVR method (OpenReasonerZero at 51.6), not against on-policy RL, which is the more relevant baseline for testing whether off-policy guidance comes at a generalization cost.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Ablation of the on-policy to off-policy ratio:** The paper uses 7:1 on-policy to off-policy rollouts. What happens at 6:2, 5:3, or 4:4? The adaptivity argument (Section 3.4) suggests the ratio matters: more off-policy traces strengthen the imitation signal, which might help weaker models or harder datasets but hurt exploration. Without this ablation, we don't know whether 7:1 is near-optimal or whether LUFFY is robust to this choice.

- **Multiple teachers or off-policy data sources:** The paper uses only DeepSeek-R1 traces. Would traces from other strong models (e.g., Qwen-Max, GPT-4) produce similar gains? This would test whether the benefit is specific to DeepSeek-R1's reasoning style or general to any high-quality math reasoning traces.

- **Evaluation of the LLaMA-3.1-8B Hard-trained model on math benchmarks:** As noted above, training reward curves without final accuracy leave the capability transcendence claim incomplete.

- **Confidence intervals on per-benchmark and OOD results:** The paper reports significance only in aggregate, but the benchmark-level variance (LUFFY wins some, loses some) makes aggregate claims less informative than they appear.

- **Ablation of the shaping function form:** $f(x) = x/(x+\gamma)$ is one concave transformation among many. Would other concave functions (e.g., $\log(1+x)$, $\sqrt{x}$, $\min(x, c)$) work similarly or better? Testing only $\gamma$ values for a single functional form conflates the effect of concavity with the specific algebraic form.

- **Experiments on non-math reasoning domains:** All experiments are on math. The out-of-distribution benchmarks (ARC-c, GPQA, MMLU-Pro) test generalization to other knowledge domains but still involve reasoning. It's unclear whether LUFFY would help for code generation, logical reasoning, or other tasks where verifiable rewards exist.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **The test sets are small for some benchmarks.** AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 each have 30 questions. AMC has an unspecified but likely small number. Pass@1 or avg@32 on 30 questions has high variance — a difference of 2–3 correct answers can shift accuracy by 7–10 percentage points. This makes per-benchmark comparisons noisy and may explain some of the uneven results (e.g., LUFFY losing to Oat-Zero on AIME 2024).

- **The On-Policy RL baseline is a specific implementation choice.** The paper trains On-Policy RL with Dr.GRPO modifications (no length normalization, no standard error normalization, no KL penalty, entropy coefficient 0.01) and achieves 45.5 average. But Oat-Zero — which uses the same base model and similar optimizations — achieves 43.7 (Table 1). The 1.8-point difference between two "on-policy RL" implementations underscores how sensitive these methods are to hyperparameters. LUFFY's +4.6 advantage over the authors' On-Policy RL replication might shrink or grow against a differently-tuned on-policy baseline.

- **The SFT baseline may be undertrained or poorly configured.** SFT achieves only 44.1 average, lower than On-Policy RL's 45.5. But DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B — which is essentially SFT on R1 traces — achieves much higher performance (not reported in the paper, but publicly known to be competitive with or exceed LUFFY's numbers on some benchmarks). This suggests the paper's SFT configuration (3 epochs, learning rate 5e-5, based on open-r1/OpenR1-Qwen-7B) may not be the strongest possible SFT baseline, making LUFFY's comparison against SFT overly favorable.

- **Difficulty estimation for the Easy/Hard split uses a proxy (solution length) that may not capture true difficulty.** Problems where DeepSeek-R1 uses more tokens may involve more steps, more verification, or more exploration — but could also reflect inefficiency in R1's reasoning or stylistic verbosity. A problem that R1 solves verbosely might be easy for Qwen2.5-Math-7B; a problem R1 solves concisely might be hard. The paper does not validate that token length correlates with actual difficulty for the student model.

- **The paper does not compare against stronger distillation baselines.** Beyond SFT, there are more sophisticated knowledge distillation techniques (e.g., training on the teacher's logits, using the teacher's probability distribution rather than hard labels, sequence-level KD) that might provide stronger off-policy baselines than the three tested (SFT, RL w/ SFT Loss, SFT+RL). The failure of these specific baselines does not demonstrate that *all* distillation approaches are inferior to LUFFY.

- **GPU hour comparisons may be confounded by implementation efficiency.** Table 2 reports that SFT+RL requires 130 GPU-hours vs. LUFFY's 77. But the paper attributes SFT+RL's higher cost to "excessively long generations induced by rigid imitation from SFT." This means the cost difference is partly an artifact of SFT's poor behavior (generating unnecessarily long outputs that slow down roll-out generation), not an inherent efficiency advantage of LUFFY's algorithm. A better-implemented SFT baseline with length penalties or generation constraints might be both cheaper and better-performing.

Overall, the experiments convincingly demonstrate that LUFFY improves over the tested baselines, but they leave open questions about **(a)** whether the gains are robust to hyperparameter tuning of baselines, **(b)** whether the capability transcendence on LLaMA-3.1-8B translates to meaningful test accuracy, and **(c)** whether policy shaping's mechanism is genuinely about re-weighting low-probability tokens or more simply about reducing the effective off-policy learning rate. The paper's strongest contribution — the LLaMA-3.1-8B Hard dataset experiment — is also its most incomplete, lacking the evaluation data that would quantify the practical significance of breaking the on-policy ceiling.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Amortized in the Capability Ceiling Experiment

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper’s most compelling claim — that LUFFY transcends the capability boundaries of on-policy RL — rests on an experiment where LLaMA-3.1-8B is trained on a Hard dataset that on-policy RL completely fails to learn from (Section 5.2, Figure 4). However, the Hard dataset is constructed by **filtering for problems where DeepSeek-R1 can generate a correct solution within 4K tokens** (Appendix C.1). This means every training prompt in the Hard set has a pre-existing, verified-correct off-policy trace from a much stronger model. The scenario where LUFFY "succeeds where on-policy fails" is therefore one where external supervision from a capable teacher is already available for every single training example.

**The consequence.** This limitation tightly constrains the practical interpretation of the capability transcendence result. If a practitioner faces a problem distribution where their base model’s pass@1 is effectively zero, LUFFY can only help if they already have access to correct solutions for a substantial fraction of those problems — which requires either a stronger model that succeeds on them (exactly the capability gap they’re trying to bridge) or expensive human annotation. The experiment does not demonstrate that LUFFY can learn from a small number of off-policy traces and generalize to novel problems within the same difficulty class; every training prompt has its own off-policy trace. The scenario is more accurately described as **distillation from a capable teacher on hard problems** rather than **autonomous capability acquisition beyond a model’s initial reach**.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 (right) shows training rewards for LLaMA-3.1-8B on the Hard set: On-Policy RL is identically zero for 500 steps while LUFFY rises to ~0.1–0.15. But the paper does not report the evaluation accuracy of the resulting model on any math benchmarks. The paper measures only training rewards — an intermediate signal that confirms gradient flow, not final capability. Additionally, the Hard set is constructed by filtering OpenR1-Math-220k prompts to those where DeepSeek-R1’s correct solution is between 2K and 4K tokens, meaning every prompt is paired with a teacher-generated correct answer. The experiment does not test a setting where off-policy traces are available for only a subset of prompts and the model must generalize to other equally hard prompts without teacher guidance.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the circularity of requiring a teacher that already solves the hard problems in order to train a model on those problems, nor does it experiment with partial off-policy coverage (e.g., providing traces for 10% of hard prompts and testing whether LUFFY generalizes to the remaining 90%). The paper’s description of the Easy/Hard split focuses on solution length as a difficulty proxy, not on the teacher-dependence of the setup. This absence is particularly notable because the paper’s abstract frames LUFFY as "transcending the fundamental limitations of on-policy RLVR," which implies a more general capability expansion than "requires a teacher that already succeeds on every training example."

### Single-Domain Evaluation: All Results Are on Mathematics

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper — training, in-distribution evaluation, and even the "out-of-distribution" benchmarks — operates within or adjacent to mathematical reasoning. The training data is exclusively from OpenR1-Math-220k (math competition problems), the six in-distribution benchmarks (AIME 2024/2025, AMC, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench) are all mathematics, and the three out-of-distribution benchmarks (ARC-c, GPQA*, MMLU-Pro) involve reasoning over scientific and academic content that, while not pure math, still heavily depends on quantitative and logical reasoning skills that overlap with mathematical competency. The paper provides no evidence about whether LUFFY works for non-math domains — code generation, formal logic, multi-step planning, or any task where verifiable rewards exist but the reasoning patterns differ qualitatively from mathematical problem-solving.

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering LUFFY for a non-math reasoning domain has no information to guide their decision. The mechanisms that make LUFFY effective — learning to produce multi-step chains of reasoning, self-verification, structured exploration — may transfer to other domains, but they may not. Mathematical reasoning has specific properties (symbolic manipulation, well-defined correctness, standard proof strategies) that may make off-policy traces particularly informative in ways that don’t hold for, say, code debugging (where the space of correct solutions is more diverse) or ethical reasoning (where "correctness" is ambiguous). Without cross-domain evidence, the paper cannot claim that LUFFY is a general framework for off-policy guidance in RLVR — it can only claim effectiveness on math.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper’s entire evidence base is mathematical. The out-of-distribution benchmarks (Table 1, right columns) test generalization to different tasks, but these tasks (ARC-c: science exam questions; GPQA*: graduate-level science; MMLU-Pro: reasoning-focused academic questions) are still in the general neighborhood of STEM reasoning. The paper does not include any non-STEM evaluation (e.g., legal reasoning, creative writing, dialogue) nor any code generation benchmark (HumanEval, MBPP). The dataset construction section (Section 4) explicitly states the training data comes from NuminaMath and that DeepSeek-R1’s traces are math solutions. Section 7 (Conclusion) mentions that "future work may focus on extending LUFFY to broader domains or modalities," implicitly acknowledging the current scope limitation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation briefly in the future work statement (Section 7) but does not discuss it as a constraint on the current claims. The abstract and introduction present LUFFY as a framework for "Learning to reason under off-policy guidance" without qualifying the domain restriction. Given how central the math-only evidence is to every quantitative claim in the paper, the lack of explicit discussion of domain generalizability in the limitations section (Appendix A, which focuses on reward availability, model scale, and single-teacher setup) is a notable omission.

### The Off-Policy Distribution π_φ = 1 Simplification Is Not Empirically Justified

**The assumption or constraint.** LUFFY’s off-policy importance sampling ratio simplifies from $\pi_\theta(\tau_{j,t}) / \pi_\phi(\tau_{j,t})$ to $\pi_\theta(\tau_{j,t}) / 1 = \pi_\theta(\tau_{j,t})$ by setting the teacher’s token-level probability $\pi_\phi$ to 1 for all tokens (Section 3.1). The paper states this is done "for computational efficiency" and to "avoid the complexity caused by different tokenization between the on-policy and off-policy models." This is equivalent to assuming the teacher is maximally confident in every token choice — an assumption that is demonstrably false: real language models (including DeepSeek-R1) assign less than 1.0 probability to most tokens, and treating every teacher token as certain eliminates the variance-reducing calibration that importance sampling is designed to provide.

**The consequence.** Setting $\pi_\phi = 1$ has two potential failure modes. **First**, it makes the importance weight $\hat{r}_{j,t} = \pi_\theta(\tau_{j,t})$ always ≤ 1, meaning the off-policy gradient is systematically underweighted relative to a correctly normalized importance sampler — the model is always told to increase probability of the teacher’s tokens, but never by an amount that accounts for how confident the teacher actually was. Tokens where the teacher was uncertain (low $\pi_\phi$) receive the same treatment as tokens where the teacher was certain, potentially causing the student to overfit to noise in the teacher’s generation. **Second**, the variance properties derived in Appendix B.2 assume the original importance weight $x = \pi_\theta / \pi_\phi$ has expectation 1 (a standard requirement for unbiased importance sampling). With $\pi_\phi = 1$, this property does not hold because $\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\phi}[\pi_\theta(\tau) / 1] \neq 1$ in general — the expectation over teacher trajectories of student probabilities is arbitrary. The convergence theorem (Theorem 1) assumes bounded importance weights $w \in [w, \bar{w}]$, which is satisfied (since $\pi_\theta \in [0,1]$), but the theorem does not require unbiasedness, so it remains valid. Whether the practical gradient estimates are meaningful, however, depends on whether $\pi_\phi = 1$ is a reasonable proxy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **no ablation or analysis** of the $\pi_\phi = 1$ choice. There is no experiment comparing LUFFY with $\pi_\phi = 1$ against an alternative where $\pi_\phi$ is estimated (e.g., by running DeepSeek-R1 inference on the off-policy traces to get token probabilities, or by using a distilled approximation). There is no measurement of how much the $\pi_\phi = 1$ assumption distorts the importance weights compared to the true ratio. The paper simply states the choice and moves on. The convergence proof in Theorem 1 assumes bounded weights and clipped importance ratios, which is satisfied, but this is a theoretical guarantee about optimization convergence — it says nothing about whether the converged policy is a good policy (i.e., whether the gradient estimates are pointing in a useful direction). The ablation in Table 4 shows that the shaping function + NoClip combination improves performance, but this does not isolate the $\pi_\phi = 1$ assumption — it tests the combined effect of several design choices confounded together.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation or trade-off anywhere in the main text or appendices. The discussion in Section 3.1 presents $\pi_\phi = 1$ as a practical convenience that "preserves theoretical guarantees" without discussing what is lost. Given that one of the paper’s core claims is that importance sampling enables principled off-policy learning, the sweeping simplification of the importance weight’s denominator deserves explicit discussion as a gap between the theoretical framework and practical implementation.

### LLaMA-3.1-8B Hard-Set Results Are Incomplete: Training Rewards Without Evaluation Accuracy

**The assumption or constraint.** The experiment that most directly supports the paper’s central claim — that LUFFY "transcends the fundamental limitations of on-policy RLVR" (abstract) — is the LLaMA-3.1-8B training on the Hard dataset (Section 5.2, Figure 4). The key evidence is a training reward curve: on-policy RL rewards remain at zero for 500 steps, while LUFFY rewards rise to ~0.1–0.15. The paper presents this as proof that LUFFY succeeds where on-policy RL fails. However, **training reward is a process metric, not an outcome metric**. It measures whether the model is receiving non-zero gradient signals, not whether those signals produce a useful final model.

**The consequence.** Training rewards can be non-zero and increasing while the model learns behaviors that are ultimately unhelpful. For instance, the model might learn to produce outputs that happen to match the ground-truth answer through shallow pattern matching (e.g., guessing common answer formats, exploiting dataset biases) without acquiring generalizable reasoning — a form of reward hacking at the training distribution level. Without final evaluation accuracy on held-out benchmarks, we cannot distinguish between "the model is learning to solve hard math problems" and "the model is learning to produce surface patterns that occasionally yield correct answers on the training set." The paper reports final evaluation scores for every other model configuration (Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-Instruct-7B, LLaMA-3.1-8B on Easy data) but omits them for the one experiment that most directly supports the capability transcendence claim. This omission is the single largest evidential gap between what the paper claims and what it demonstrates.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 (right) shows training rewards over 500 steps — nothing else. There is no table or figure reporting the LLaMA-3.1-8B Hard-trained model’s accuracy on AIME, AMC, MATH-500, or any other benchmark. Table 3 reports final evaluation scores for LLaMA-3.1-8B trained with LUFFY (13.2 average across six benchmarks), but that model was trained on the Easy subset (0–2K token solutions, as stated in Appendix C.1: "The dataset used for LLaMA3.1-8B is the subset of OpenR1-Math-220k we used with Qwen2.5-Series models, selected by the length of DeepSeek-R1’s correct solution, i.e., 0-2k tokens (Easy training set described in Sec. 5.2)"). The LLaMA-3.1-8B LUFFY evaluation results in Table 3 correspond to the Easy-set training, not the Hard-set training. The Hard-set training is only evaluated via the intermediate reward curves in Figure 4.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not explain why final evaluation results for the Hard-trained model are absent, nor does it acknowledge this as a limitation. Given that the capability-transcendence claim features prominently in the abstract, introduction, and conclusion, the absence of the outcome metric that would quantify the magnitude of transcendence is a significant weakness. The most generous interpretation is that the Hard-trained model was evaluated and performed poorly, and the results were omitted; the most neutral interpretation is that the authors considered training reward curves sufficient evidence and did not run final evaluations. Either way, the paper’s strongest claim is supported by its weakest evidence.

### Policy Shaping’s Mechanism Is Confounded with Effective Learning Rate Reduction

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims that policy shaping via $f(x) = x / (x + \gamma)$ prevents entropy collapse by amplifying gradients for low-probability tokens (those where $\pi_\theta$ is small) while dampening gradients for high-probability tokens (Section 3.2). The gradient analysis (Eqs. 7–8) shows that the shaping derivative $f'(\pi_\theta) = \gamma / (\pi_\theta + \gamma)^2$ is larger when $\pi_\theta$ is small and smaller when $\pi_\theta$ is large — which is interpreted as a re-weighting mechanism that specifically targets novel capabilities. However, $f(x)$ has another property: **it compresses the entire off-policy gradient magnitude relative to linear importance sampling**, since $f(x) \leq x$ for all $x$ when $\gamma > 0$ (because $x/(x+\gamma) < x$ for $x > 0$). This means policy shaping acts as both a re-weighting function and an overall gradient scaler on the off-policy term.

**The consequence.** The observed benefits of policy shaping — sustained entropy (Figure 5, right), continued accuracy improvement beyond the Mixed-Policy plateau (Figure 6), and improved final performance (Table 4) — could be explained by either mechanism: **(a)** the claimed re-weighting toward low-probability tokens, or **(b)** simply reducing the effective learning rate on off-policy data, which slows down convergence and thereby prevents premature entropy collapse by keeping the model from overfitting to the teacher too quickly. These hypotheses have different implications. If (a) is correct, the specific concave shape of $f(x)$ matters, and $\gamma = 0.1$ represents a genuine optimal balance between amplification and stability. If (b) is correct, any mechanism that reduces the off-policy gradient magnitude would work similarly — a constant multiplier on the off-policy loss, a lower learning rate for off-policy parameters, or even simply training for fewer steps. The paper does not provide evidence that distinguishes these hypotheses.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The ablation in Table 4: Mixed-Policy RL (which uses linear importance sampling, effectively $f(x) = x$) achieves 44.4 average, while adding shaping achieves 47.8. This shows shaping helps, but does not distinguish re-weighting from gradient scaling. The gamma sweep in Figure 9 shows a performance peak at $\gamma = 0.1$, with degradation at both lower (0.05) and higher (0.2, 0.3, 0.5) values. This is consistent with the re-weighting hypothesis (too much amplification at low $\gamma$, too little at high $\gamma$) but also consistent with the gradient-scaling hypothesis (too little off-policy signal at high $\gamma$, too much at low $\gamma$ causing overfitting). The entropy curves in Figure 5 (right) show LUFFY sustaining higher entropy than On-Policy RL, but do not show what entropy would look like with a constant-scaled off-policy loss. The paper does not include an ablation that scales the off-policy loss by a constant factor without reshaping — the most direct test of whether the concave shape matters beyond its scaling effect.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents the re-weighting interpretation as established fact ("This mechanism encourages the model to preserve exploration throughout training," Section 3.2) without considering alternative explanations for the observed effects. The variance analysis in Appendix B.2 argues that shaped weights have lower variance than unshaped weights, which is a property of the concave transformation, but lower gradient variance does not distinguish between the re-weighting and scaling hypotheses because both produce lower-variance updates. This is a conceptual gap, not an experimental one — the paper does not articulate what would constitute evidence for the re-weighting hypothesis over the scaling hypothesis, and therefore does not design experiments to test it.

### No Ablation of the On-Policy to Off-Policy Rollout Ratio

**The assumption or constraint.** Throughout the paper, LUFFY uses exactly 7 on-policy rollouts and 1 off-policy trace per prompt, totaling 8 rollouts to match the On-Policy RL baseline’s 8 on-policy rollouts. The paper describes this choice as ensuring "fairness" (Section 4), but never tests whether the 7:1 ratio is optimal, robust, or even necessary. The mixed-policy advantage computation (Eq. 4) is the mechanism that creates the adaptive balance between imitation and exploration — when the model fails, the off-policy trace receives high advantage; when the model succeeds, on-policy rollouts dominate. The ratio of off-policy to on-policy traces determines the dynamic range of this adaptivity: more off-policy traces would increase the baseline correctness level in the group, reducing the advantage of any single off-policy trace when the model fails, but providing more diverse teacher demonstrations; fewer off-policy traces would make the off-policy advantage larger when the model fails (since a single correct trace in a larger group of zeros has a higher z-score), but provide less teacher signal.

**The consequence.** Without ablating this ratio, we do not know whether LUFFY’s performance depends critically on the 7:1 choice or is robust across a wide range. A practitioner training a model with different characteristics — a weaker base model that fails more often (where off-policy traces would have larger advantages, potentially causing over-imitation), or a stronger base model that succeeds more often (where off-policy traces would have near-zero advantages and contribute little) — has no guidance on whether to adjust the ratio. Furthermore, the ratio interacts with the policy shaping function: if off-policy advantages are larger (because off-policy traces are rarer in the group), the shaping function’s gradient re-weighting operates on larger raw gradients, potentially amplifying or dampening the effect. The paper’s claim of a "dynamic and adaptive equilibrium" is supported by the group computation mechanism, but the degree of adaptivity — how strongly the model is pulled toward imitation when it fails, and how quickly it transitions to exploration when it succeeds — depends on the ratio, and is uncharacterized.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no sweep, no ablation, and no analysis of the on-policy to off-policy ratio. Every LUFFY experiment uses 7:1. Table 2 reports data usage (64K off-policy traces), but this is a consequence of using 1 off-policy trace per prompt in a 45K-prompt dataset — the ratio is never justified or tested. The paper does compare against alternative off-policy methods that use different data-to-training ratios (SFT uses only off-policy data, RL w/ SFT Loss uses 7:1, SFT+RL uses nearly 2:1 off-policy to on-policy data across its two stages), but these comparisons confound the ratio with entirely different training algorithms, so no conclusion about the ratio itself can be drawn.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention the on-policy to off-policy ratio as a hyperparameter worth studying, nor does it discuss sensitivity to this choice in the limitations (Appendix A). The ratio is treated as a fixed design choice rather than a tunable parameter, despite being central to the adaptive mechanism that the paper claims is one of LUFFY’s key innovations. Future work could study the ratio’s effect, but the current paper provides no evidence that 7:1 is near-optimal or that LUFFY’s gains would persist at different ratios.
