Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (framing career compensation as an investment portfolio across big tech and startups):

**Compensation as an investment**

Ryan opens by reframing your compensation package not as a salary, but as an investment. Every offer you consider has three properties worth analyzing: expected value, variance, and liquidity. A big tech RSU grant and a startup equity package are just different assets in the same portfolio framework, and understanding the tradeoffs across these dimensions lets you make a deliberate choice rather than defaulting to conventional wisdom.

**Expected value: startup equity is not zero**

The common refrain is that big tech pays more — higher salaries, larger equity packages. People often dismiss startup equity as near-worthless, but "its expected value is not near zero." Ryan points to AngelList data plotting venture fund performance across the industry: even the worst median-case vintage year (2013) delivered a 17.5% IRR. While any single startup's equity is unlikely to pay out, the expected return across a large enough sample is meaningfully positive. He sharpens the point: if startup equity had no expected value, why would billions of dollars flow into venture capital when that capital could simply sit in big tech stocks?

Some startup equity can even have comparable expected value to big tech RSUs. The mechanism is the **"rich get richer" dynamic in private markets** — information is less accessible, so top venture funds with stronger networks and better deal flow enjoy a persistent competitive advantage. The best startup deals are competitive and cluster toward the strongest VCs because taking investment from a top fund signals success and attracts future employees and investors. So while big tech offers higher expected value in most cases, a strong offer from a startup backed by top-tier VCs can close that gap considerably.

**Variance: the real differentiator**

Variance is where the two paths diverge cleanly. "The smaller the company the larger the variance in the value of the equity." For most people, this high variance is a liability — you can only work at a limited number of companies across a career, so you may never see a substantial payout even if the expected value math holds in aggregate. You are not a fund; you don't get to diversify across a hundred startups.

But variance cuts both ways. For someone who wants a chance to "break into a new social class," high variance becomes an asset. Big tech compensation will make you well off, but "you will never be truly rich (e.g. +$30M net worth)." If you want a shot at that outcome, you need investments with higher variance. The variance isn't a bug; it's the feature you're buying.

**Liquidity and the big tech superset strategy**

Liquidity is where big tech equity shines — it's "as good as cash once you receive it." Startup equity, by contrast, is illiquid until near or at an exit. But this asymmetry creates an interesting arbitrage. Every time your big tech RSUs vest, you can sell them and invest the cash into startups. Since the total value of a big tech equity package is often higher than what a startup would grant you, "you'd own more equity in startups this way than if you just worked at them." Big tech equity can be a **strict superset of startup equity**: you get the liquidity, the higher dollar amount, *and* exposure to venture-like returns — without putting all your eggs in one basket.

The bottleneck is deal flow. "The hottest startups aren't going to let a random big tech employee invest in them." AngelList has democratized access but typically charges 20% carry, which cuts into the strategy's margins. It's also unclear whether you can access the absolute top deals through a platform when competition to get capital into those startups is so intense. Working at a hot startup directly solves this: you get access to deals you wouldn't otherwise see as an angel investor, and your network improves because people begin sharing deals with you to co-invest — especially if you're a founder with ties to your investors.

**The decision beyond compensation**

Big tech compensation makes more sense for most people. But if you land a strong offer from a top startup and you don't need near-term liquidity — say, for a loan or a home purchase — it's reasonable to consider the startup path. The compensation lens is clarifying, but it's not the whole picture. "Focusing on where you can get the most skill and career growth is a much more effective strategy for maximizing compensation," because senior-level packages dwarf entry-level ones no matter which company you join. The best long-term investment in your career is the learning curve itself.
