# Physics of Language Models: Part 2.2, How to Learn From Mistakes on Grade-School Math Problems

**ArXiv:** [2408.16293](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.16293)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper studies whether pretraining language models on data containing reasoning mistakes followed by immediate corrections—termed **retry data**—can improve mathematical reasoning accuracy compared to training on the same amount of error-free data, using the fully-controllable synthetic iGSM dataset and GPT2 architecture.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies whether pretraining language models on data containing reasoning mistakes followed by immediate corrections—termed **retry data**—can improve mathematical reasoning accuracy compared to training on the same amount of error-free data, using the fully-controllable synthetic iGSM dataset and GPT2 architecture. The core contribution is demonstrating that models pretrained on retry data (e.g., "Define Central High's Backpack as [BACK]. Define Central High's Film Studio as B; so B = p + W…") significantly outperform those trained on error-free data under matched token budgets, with higher error rates in pretraining data producing larger accuracy gains—on the hardest iGSM-med problems (op=23), accuracy jumps from ~78% to ~94% when using retry rate = 0.5. The paper further establishes that the skill of error correction is fundamentally different from error-free reasoning, evidenced by the finding that parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) on retry data fails to improve a model pretrained on error-free data, while a lightweight probing classifier (rank-8 embedding update) can already detect errors with 99% accuracy—establishing that models "regret" mistakes but cannot correct them unless retry data is integrated directly into pretraining.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Can Language Models Learn to Correct Mistakes During Generation?

The paper tackles a deceptively simple but fundamental question: **if language models can "self-correct" their mistakes through multi-round prompting after generating a complete solution, why can't they correct those mistakes immediately as they occur during generation?** This gap between *post-hoc* error correction (after finishing) and *immediate* error correction (mid-generation) is the central puzzle motivating the entire investigation.

Consider a concrete example from Figure 1 in the paper: GPT-4o solves a box-weighting problem, produces an incorrect answer of 80 pounds, then—when prompted to verify—catches its own mistake (missing a box K) and produces the correct answer of 120 pounds. This demonstrates that the model *already possesses the knowledge* to identify and fix the error. Yet it didn't apply that knowledge during the initial generation. Why?

The paper frames this as two questions (Section 1):

1. **Why does the model make mistakes in the first place?** — a question addressed by prior work showing that "distribution shift" between training and test prompts causes errors even when training data is error-free (Anil et al., 2022; Miller et al., 2020; Shu and Yu, 2024).

2. **Why doesn't the model correct mistakes immediately during generation?** — the much less-studied question that this paper investigates.

The practical implications of question (2) are significant. Immediate correction during generation would:
- **Save inference tokens** — the model would not waste computation continuing from an erroneous step.
- **Simplify the inference framework** — eliminating the need for multiple rounds of model calls, verification prompts, and correction loops.
- **Enable more autonomous reasoning** — the model could operate reliably in a single autoregressive pass without external verification scaffolding.

### A Natural Conjecture: The Alignment Problem

The paper proposes a straightforward explanation for why current models fail at immediate error correction (Section 1): an **alignment issue rooted in training data**. People rarely write erroneous sentences followed by immediate corrections in natural text—if they make a mistake while writing, they delete the error before publishing. As the paper puts it:

> "There might be little to no training data in the language model's corpus to teach it to correct errors immediately. After all, people are unlikely to write an erroneous sentence followed by immediate correction in internet articles."

This leads to the paper's motivating hypothesis: **if we provide enough "retry" training data—erroneous steps immediately followed by their corrections—can language models learn to perform immediate error correction during autoregressive generation?**

### The Dangerous-Sounding Proposition

At first glance, training on data with mistakes seems counterproductive. The paper explicitly acknowledges two potential harms (Section 1):

**Potential harm 1: Next-token prediction on mistake-containing data.** By minimizing the standard autoregressive loss on data containing errors, the model might learn to *produce* mistakes (e.g., "A ⇒ B") rather than writing the correct steps directly. One natural mitigation is to mask out the erroneous tokens during training so the model only learns from the correction part. However, the paper notes that this is harder for immediate corrections than for post-hoc corrections:

> "Unlike the 'error correction after entire generation' type of training data where we can simply mask out the entire generation, here, the error and correction steps are intertwined, making it much harder to mask out the error only."

In post-hoc correction data, the entire wrong solution can be wrapped in mask tokens (`[MaskStart] wrong solution [MaskEnd]`) and the model only learns from the correction after. But with interleaved error-correction pairs, the boundaries are ambiguous.

**Potential harm 2: Unclear benefits over error-free data.** If the ultimate goal is for the model to produce correct reasoning steps, why train on "A ⇒ B, oh I made a mistake, actually A ⇒ C" at all? Why not just train directly on "A ⇒ C"? The error introduces a *distribution shift* relative to the desired test-time behavior (error-free solutions), and if the error part is masked out during training, the remaining signal reduces to the same correct step—so why bother?

These concerns make the core experimental question genuinely risky: **the paper must demonstrate not only that retry data doesn't harm the model, but that it actually provides benefits beyond training on equivalent amounts of error-free data.**

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates itself against several existing lines of work, identifying specific limitations in each:

**Multi-round self-correction via prompting.** A body of work demonstrates that language models can "self-verify" and "self-correct" through careful multi-round prompting (Madaan et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2023). An illustrative example appears in Figure 1, where GPT-4o successfully fixes its error after a user prompt. This approach works, but it is:
- **Expensive** — requiring multiple model calls (generation → verification → correction → re-verification).
- **Complex** — requiring prompt engineering and external orchestration.
- **Brittle** — as the paper observes, the same GPT-4o that succeeded in Figure 1 can insist its solution is correct under different prompting (footnote 3: "using prompts such as 'Please verify carefully and correct your solution,' ... GPT-4o can insist that its solution is correct and makes no correction."). The verification success rate is "far from 100%."
- **Inefficient** — tokens spent generating from an erroneous step are wasted.

The paper's goal is **not** another multi-round prompting method—it's to teach the model to perform this loop internally, in a single forward pass.

**Beam search.** Beam search is a standard approach for improving generation quality by exploring multiple candidate continuations at each step and selecting the most likely ones. The paper treats beam search as a baseline and finds that for models pretrained on error-free data, "even with 16 or 32 beams, its accuracy does not noticeably improve in all the cases" (Section 4, Result 6). Beam search relies on the model's own next-token probability distribution to guide exploration—it has no explicit error detection or correction mechanism. The paper demonstrates that beam search is fundamentally limited compared to learning explicit correction skills from data.

**Verifier-based approaches.** Works like Cobbe et al. (2021), Li et al. (2023), and Yang et al. (2022) train separate verifier models to check the correctness of generated solutions. These are typically applied *after* generation to score and select among complete solutions. The paper's approach is complementary: instead of external verification, the model learns to self-verify and self-correct during generation.

**"Retry upon regret" — the closest precursor but still insufficient.** The paper's own Sections 3.1–3.2 develop a "retry upon regret" baseline, built on probing results from prior work (Ye et al., 2024). This prior work showed that after a model generates an erroneous step (e.g., "Define Central High's Backpack as" when that parameter isn't ready for computation), its internal states exhibit a "regretful" pattern—a linear probe can detect the error with 99% accuracy. The "retry upon regret" approach uses this probe to trigger regeneration from the previous step when an error is detected.

However, the paper shows this approach has critical limitations:
- **Marginal gains**: even with 99% accurate error detection, accuracy improvement is small—"about 2% for the op = 23 case" (Section 3.2).
- **Extreme sensitivity**: performance depends heavily on near-perfect error detection; increasing from 99% to 100% (using ground-truth detection) "can significantly improve the final reasoning accuracy, but this is too ideal."
- **Increased complexity**: it requires keeping a separate error detector model and modifying the inference process with multi-round regeneration.
- **Doesn't teach error correction**: the model still relies on random resampling to fix errors—it hasn't learned *how* to correct them.

This sets up the paper's central contrast: if the model "already knows" it made a mistake (the 99% probe accuracy), why can't it fix it directly? The answer, the paper argues, is that **error correction is a distinct skill that must be explicitly taught**, not merely detected.

### The Need for a Controlled Synthetic Setting

The paper is explicit about why it uses synthetic data rather than real-world math problems or LLM-generated error-correction data (Section 1):

> "While it is tempting to use language models such as GPT-4 to synthesize such data, there is no evidence that GPT-4 can robustly generate errors or make those corrections consistently. To avoid complicating our conclusions with the success rate and reliability of GPT-4, we need a different setting where we can 100% reliably generate errors and corrections."

This is a crucial methodological choice. In real math data, errors are noisy, corrections are unreliable, and there's no guarantee that a "correction" is actually correct. The paper needs **ground-truth controllable errors and corrections** to answer its core scientific questions cleanly. Without this, any observed effects could be attributed to noise in the data rather than to the learning dynamics of retry data itself.

The iGSM dataset (Ye et al., 2024) provides exactly this controllability: math problems with dependency graphs where the most common reasoning error (skipping steps by trying to compute a parameter before its dependencies are known) can be simulated deterministically. The paper can insert fake errors with precise probabilities, generate provably correct corrections, and study the effects in isolation.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper is part of a broader research program ("Physics of Language Models") that uses controlled synthetic settings to understand fundamental learning dynamics. It builds directly on Ye et al. (2024) (Part 2.1 of the series), which established the iGSM dataset and demonstrated that language models can learn genuine reasoning strategies (not just pattern matching) on these problems, including non-trivial "mental planning" to precompute necessary parameters before beginning solution generation.

The paper's positioning is distinctive in several ways:

- **Not a new method for self-correction** — it's not proposing a better prompting technique or a new verifier architecture. Instead, it's investigating a *data-level intervention*: should retry data be included in pretraining corpora?

- **A "physics of language models" approach** — the goal is to understand *why* and *how* models learn (or fail to learn) error correction, using fully controlled experiments where every variable can be isolated. The paper explicitly aims "to try to predict the needs of future LLMs" (Section 7) rather than to build an immediate practical system.

- **A challenge to the standard pretraining paradigm** — current LLM pretraining pipelines (e.g., Llama 3, Phi-3) use synthetic data (Abdin et al., 2024; Llama Team, 2024), but this paper asks: should synthetic data include mistakes and corrections, not just correct solutions? This has not been systematically studied.

- **A bridge between self-correction research and pretraining data design** — prior self-correction work focuses on inference-time techniques; this paper asks whether the underlying skill should be embedded at the pretraining level.

The paper's framing is forward-looking: while the specific iGSM retry data format won't be directly used in commercial LLMs, the guiding principle—that models need to see error-correction patterns during pretraining to internalize the correction skill—could influence how future synthetic data pipelines are designed.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation

The paper builds a framework for teaching language models to correct their own reasoning mistakes *during generation* by modifying the pretraining data to include erroneous reasoning steps immediately followed by their corrections—what the authors call **retry data**. The problem is that language models pretrained only on error-free solutions, even when they can *detect* their mistakes with high accuracy (via internal "regretful" states), cannot reliably *correct* them mid-generation; the solution is to embed error-correction patterns directly into pretraining, which teaches the model a distinct skill that cannot be acquired post-hoc through fine-tuning or inference-time interventions like beam search.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture

The system has five major components, studied through controlled experiments rather than deployed as a unified pipeline:

1. **Error-free pretrained base model (GPT2-12-12)** — a 12-layer, 12-head GPT2 (768-dimensional hidden states) with rotary positional embeddings, trained on iGSM math data containing only correct step-by-step solutions. This serves as the baseline for all comparisons and the starting point for both probing and fine-tuning experiments.

2. **Retry data generator** — a programmatic module that takes correct iGSM solutions and, with probability `retry rate ∈ [0, 1)`, inserts erroneous parameter names (that cannot be computed next) followed by the special token `[BACK]` before each correct solution sentence. This runs deterministically during data generation, not during model training.

3. **Probing classifier (can_next)** — a lightweight fine-tuned head on top of the frozen pretrained model that predicts, from the model's last hidden layer after "Define [param A] as," whether parameter A can legitimately be computed next (i.e., all its dependencies are known). Used for error detection in the "retry upon regret" experiments and to demonstrate that the model "knows" when it has made a mistake.

4. **LoRA fine-tuning adapter** — low-rank matrix updates applied to the frozen pretrained model's query/value matrices and embedding layer, trained on retry data to test whether error correction can be acquired through parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

5. **Full/continued pretraining pipeline** — training from scratch (or from an error-free checkpoint) on retry data using standard autoregressive language modeling, which is the paper's primary intervention.

Information flows as follows in the main experiment: (a) the retry data generator creates corrupted solutions from correct iGSM problems → (b) the language model is pretrained from scratch on this retry data using standard next-token prediction → (c) at evaluation, the model generates solutions autoregressively, occasionally producing `[BACK]` tokens followed by corrected parameter names when it detects (through its learned representations) that it has made a mistake → (d) a parser checks both the final answer and all intermediate steps for correctness.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the iGSM dataset and the dependency graph formalism (Section 2 of the paper), because the controlled nature of error insertion depends entirely on knowing which parameters can and cannot be computed at each step. Without understanding the problem structure, the retry data construction makes no sense.

- **Second**, the retry data construction algorithm—how errors are selected, inserted, and formatted—since this is the core data intervention that enables every subsequent experiment.

- **Third**, the probing methodology (can_next probing) and the "retry upon regret" inference procedure, which establish the baseline finding that error *detection* is easy while error *correction* is hard, motivating the need for retry data in pretraining.

- **Fourth**, the pretraining-with-retry-data setup, including the controlled comparison framework (matched token budgets, matched training parameters), the label masking ablation, and the evaluation methodology.

- **Fifth**, the LoRA fine-tuning setup, establishing that parameter-efficient adaptation from an error-free model cannot acquire error correction, even with the same retry data that works when included in pretraining.

- **Sixth**, the "fake retry" data construction methods (retry weak and retry miss) and how they approximate the perfect retry data without requiring semantic understanding of the solution.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that pretraining data containing errors followed by immediate corrections teaches a language model to perform error correction during autoregressive generation, and that this skill is fundamentally different from error-free reasoning—it cannot be acquired through parameter-efficient fine-tuning or replicated through beam search or regeneration-based inference tricks.

#### The iGSM Dataset and Dependency Graph Formalism

The paper relies entirely on the iGSM dataset introduced in Ye et al. (2024) (Part 2.1 of the same "Physics of Language Models" series). Understanding the data structure is essential because every aspect of retry data construction depends on the known dependency relationships between parameters.

**Problem structure.** Each iGSM problem consists of a set of parameters (e.g., "the number of backpacks in Central High," "the number of Film Studios in Riverview High") related through arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) constrained to integers modulo 23. The problem description lists relationships between parameters, some of which are given concrete values (e.g., "the number of each Dance Studio's School Daypack equals 17") and others that must be computed from previously computed parameters. A question at the end asks for the value of a specific parameter.

**Dependency graph.** The parameters form a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where an edge from parameter X to parameter Y means Y's value depends on X's value. A parameter can be computed only when all its predecessors in the dependency graph have already been computed. The solution to the problem is a topological ordering of this graph—a sequence of "Define [param] as X; so [computation]" statements where each parameter is introduced only after all its dependencies have been defined.

For example, in Figure 2: "Central High's Film Studio" depends on "Dance Studio's School Daypack" (p = 17) and "Film Studio's Messenger Backpack" (W = 13). Therefore, p and W must be defined before B. The step "Define Central High's Film Studio as B; so B = p + W = 17 + 13 = 7" is only valid after p and W are known.

**op metric.** Each solution requires a certain number of binary operations (addition, multiplication, etc.) to complete. The paper uses `op` to denote this operation count, and the four dataset families partition problems by op range:
- **iGSM-medpq/qp**: training on `op ≤ 15`, OOD test on `op ∈ {20, 21, 22, 23}`
- **iGSM-hardpq/qp**: training on `op ≤ 21`, OOD test on `op ∈ {28, 29, 30, 31, 32}`

The `pq` suffix means the problem description appears before the question; `qp` means the question appears before the problem description. The `reask` variants (e.g., `iGSM-medop=20,reask`) are constructed by taking an existing problem and re-sampling a different parameter to query, which significantly changes the data distribution and serves as an additional OOD evaluation.

**Why this matters for retry data.** The dependency graph provides a **deterministic ground-truth oracle** for what constitutes an error: if the model attempts to "Define [param A]" when not all of A's predecessors have been computed, that is a step-skipping error. This is the most common reasoning mistake observed in language models on these problems (and, the paper argues, in models like GPT-4 on similar reasoning tasks—the failure in Figure 1 is of this type: GPT-4o computed box Y's weight without first accounting for box K inside box L).

Because the graph is known, the paper can **programmatically generate errors with 100% reliability**: at any point in a solution, the set of parameters that *cannot* yet be computed is exactly the set of all parameters minus those whose dependencies have all been defined. The paper samples uniformly from this set to create fake mistakes. Similarly, corrections are always correct by construction—the retry data simply inserts the wrong parameter name, then the special `[BACK]` token, then the correct parameter name from the original error-free solution.

**Arithmetic and common sense are deliberately removed.** The paper states that the iGSM dataset "remov[es] arithmetic difficulties (by restricting computations to integers modulo 23) and common sense knowledge (e.g., a candle burns and its length decreases). What remains is the 'logic reasoning' part." This is crucial: the paper is studying *reasoning errors* (specifically, step-skipping), not arithmetic errors or factual errors. All arithmetic is broken into binary operations (e.g., 12 + 13 + 7 becomes (12 + 13) + 7) to isolate whether failure stems from reasoning (did the model choose the right parameters in the right order?) rather than computation (did the model add wrong?).

**Training data is generated on-the-fly.** The paper states that "for both pretraining and finetuning, we did not limit the amount of training data; we generated new data on-the-fly" (Appendix D). This means the models see an effectively infinite stream of fresh problems, eliminating any confound from memorization or overfitting to a fixed training set. The paper does not explore sample complexity—how many distinct problems are needed to achieve a given accuracy—as this "would complicate the main message."

#### Retry Data Construction

The retry data is the paper's central intervention. The construction is simple but precisely specified.

**Base procedure.** Starting from a correct iGSM solution (a sequence of "Define [param] as [var]; so [computation]" sentences), the retry data generator iterates through each solution sentence. At the beginning of each sentence—before the correct parameter name is written—with independent probability `retry rate` (a value in `[0, 1)`), the generator inserts an incorrect parameter name. This incorrect parameter is sampled uniformly at random from all parameters that *cannot* be computed at this point (i.e., parameters whose dependencies are not yet fully satisfied), excluding parameters that have already appeared in the solution. Immediately after the incorrect parameter name, the special token `[BACK]` is inserted. Then the correct parameter name follows as normal.

The process repeats: with probability `(retry rate)^2`, a second incorrect parameter is inserted at the same location (with another `[BACK]`), and so on. This means the number of retries at a single location follows a geometric distribution with success probability `1 - retry rate`.

**Concrete example (from Figure 4(a)).** For a simple problem with retry rate = 0.5, a solution might look like:

> Define Dance Studio's School Daypack as p; so p = 17. Define Film Studio's School Daypack as [BACK]. Define Film Studio's Messenger Backpack as W; so W = 13. Define Central High's Classroom as [BACK]. Define Central High's Backpack as [BACK]. Define Central High's Film Studio as B; so B = p + W = 17 + 13 = 7. ...

At the step where "Film Studio's Messenger Backpack" should be defined, the generator first inserted "Film Studio's School Daypack [BACK]" (an error for that position, since School Daypack's dependencies aren't ready), then wrote the correct "Film Studio's Messenger Backpack." At the step where "Central High's Film Studio" should be defined, the generator inserted "Central High's Classroom [BACK]" then "Central High's Backpack [BACK]" before writing the correct parameter.

The paper notes that the strikethrough formatting (~~like this~~) is "for illustration purpose, and the actual data is normal English text without strikethrough symbols." The model sees plain text with `[BACK]` tokens separating the erroneous attempt from the correction.

**What makes an error correct?** The error must be a parameter that the model *would be wrong to compute at this point*. The paper uses the dependency graph to determine this: a parameter is "wrong" if at least one of its predecessors has not yet been defined in the solution up to this point. This simulates the "skipping steps" error that both GPT-4 and the paper's own models make—trying to compute a quantity before all the inputs to that computation are known.

**What the correction teaches.** After seeing `[BACK]`, the model sees the correct parameter name. The full correction sequence is: erroneous attempt → explicit backtrack marker (`[BACK]`) → correct continuation. The model must learn to:
1. Recognize when it has generated an invalid parameter (through its own representations).
2. Emit the `[BACK]` token to signal the retry.
3. Generate the correct parameter name.
4. Continue the solution as normal.

**Why this format?** The paper contrasts this with post-hoc correction data where the entire wrong solution is wrapped in mask tokens. In that case, the model never sees the error pattern during autoregressive generation—it only sees (during training with masking) that after a mask region, a correction appears. But during inference, there are no mask tokens, so the model has no mechanism for initiating a correction. The interleaved format with `[BACK]` preserves the autoregressive structure: the model learns to generate the `[BACK]` token itself when it detects an error, then generate the correction, all within a single forward pass.

**Token budget equivalence.** A critical experimental design choice: models pretrained on retry data and models pretrained on error-free data are compared using the *same number of training tokens*, not the same number of problems. Since retry data solutions are longer (they contain extra erroneous tokens and `[BACK]` tokens), a retry-trained model sees fewer distinct problems during training. The paper states this explicitly: "when pretrained with retry data, the model sees fewer math problems compared to the original training; when pretrained with larger retry rate, the model also sees fewer math problems." All accuracy improvements from retry data are therefore *despite seeing fewer unique problems*—the retry patterns themselves provide more useful learning signal per token than additional unique correct solutions.

#### The can_next Probing and "Retry Upon Regret" Inference

Before studying retry data in pretraining, the paper first establishes (in Section 3) that models pretrained on error-free data already *know* when they make mistakes—they just can't *correct* them. This finding motivates the entire retry data investigation.

**The can_next probing task.** The probing methodology comes from Ye et al. (2024). After a model generates a partial solution up to "Define [param A] as," the probing task asks: from the model's last hidden layer at this position, can we predict whether [param A] can legitimately be computed next (i.e., whether all its dependencies are satisfied)? This is a binary classification task: `can_next(A) ∈ {true, false}`.

The probe consists of:
- A **linear classification head** on top of the frozen pretrained model's output embeddings.
- A **low-rank update** to the embedding (input) layer, with rank r (r = 8 for "version1").

Only the linear head and the rank-r update are trainable; all other model parameters are frozen. This means if the probe achieves high accuracy, the information must already be present in the model's internal representations—the probe is merely extracting it, not creating new capabilities.

**Probe accuracy.** The paper reports that the probe "99% accurately predicts can_next(A), meaning the model knows if A can be computed next, even for the hardest op = 32 problems." This holds across all parameters and all correct solution steps.

**The "regretful" pattern on mistakes.** When the model makes a mistake—generating a parameter that *cannot* be computed next—the probe's accuracy drops but remains above chance: "When the model makes a mistake, the first sentence with a mistake usually has can_next(A) = false. Probing shows the model has ~60% chance of knowing can_next(A) = false." The paper notes that 60% is significant: random guessing would be 50%, and the probe was not specifically trained on mistake examples (it was trained on all positions), so the 60% rate on the small subset of mistake positions indicates the model's internal states are qualitatively different when it has made an error—a "regretful" pattern.

**Why does the model generate the error if it "knows" it's wrong?** The paper offers an explanation grounded in the mechanics of autoregressive generation: "Before explicitly stating 'Define [param A] as', the model might falsely think A is ready to compute among all the parameters it can focus on. After stating it, the model shifts its focus to the actual computation of A, and this is the moment it can better realize that A is not ready for computation (using its attention mechanism)." This is a specific hypothesis about attention dynamics: the act of writing out the parameter name forces the model to attend to that parameter's dependencies, and only then does it detect the inconsistency.

**Probe versions.** The paper uses two probe variants:

- **Version1** (from Ye et al., 2024): Freeze the pretrained model, add a rank-8 update on the embedding layer plus a linear classification head. Trained with learning rate 0.002, weight decay 0.01, batch size 256 (iGSM-med) or 128 (iGSM-hard), for 100,000 steps. This uses a large number of fine-tuning samples (50% of pretraining tokens for iGSM-med, 25% for iGSM-hard).

- **Version2** (new in this paper): Uses r = 32 for the embedding update and additionally adds rank-2 updates on the query and value matrices of all 12 layers (this is 768 × 4 × 2 × 12 trainable parameters total). Trained with batch size 8, learning rate chosen from {0.001, 0.0005}, weight decay 0, for only 16,000 steps. This uses only 0.25% of the pretraining tokens. The goal is to show that error detection can be learned from very few examples.

Both versions achieve "99% accurately detect errors."

**"Retry upon regret" inference procedure.** Given a trained can_next probe, the inference procedure is:

1. Generate solution sentences autoregressively as normal (using multinomial sampling: beam=1, do_sample=true).
2. After generating each complete solution sentence, run the can_next probe on the parameter just defined.
3. If the probe predicts `can_next = false` (i.e., detects an error), discard the entire sentence just generated, revert the generation state to the end of the previous sentence, and regenerate from that point using fresh multinomial sampling.
4. Repeat, with a maximum of 10 total retries across the entire solution (to avoid infinite loops if the model keeps making errors).
5. After the maximum retries, accept whatever is generated.

The idea is that randomness in the regeneration process might produce a correct step where the previous attempt produced an error. This is conceptually similar to beam search (which also explores alternatives when a continuation has low probability) but uses an explicit error detector to trigger the exploration.

**Comparison against beam search.** Beam search with beam size B maintains B candidate partial solutions at each step, expanding each with the B most likely next tokens and keeping the overall top-B continuations. The paper compares beam sizes of 1, 4, 16, and 32 against "retry upon regret" with beam=1 or 4.

**Results motivating the retry data investigation.** The "retry upon regret" results (Figure 3(b)) show:
- Version1/2 with beam=1 or 4 improves over beam search (beam=32) but only marginally—about 2 percentage points on the hardest problems.
- VersionP (using perfect, ground-truth error detection instead of the learned probe) achieves substantially better accuracy, indicating that the bottleneck is probe accuracy.
- VersionP50 (perfect detection with up to 50 retries) achieves even higher, showing that with enough retries and perfect detection, the model could correct nearly all errors—but this is the "too ideal" setting.

The paper concludes: "The success of 'retry upon regret' largely depends on an extremely accurate error detector" and "the accuracy gain can be limited unless the error detector is highly accurate." This establishes the paper's core motivation: rather than patching inference with an external error detector, the model should learn to detect AND correct errors internally during pretraining.

#### Pretraining with Retry Data: Experimental Setup

This is the paper's primary experiment (Section 4), comparing models pretrained from scratch on retry data against models pretrained on the same number of tokens of error-free data.

**Model architecture.** All experiments use the GPT2 architecture (Radford et al., 2019) with rotary positional embeddings (RoPE; Su et al., 2021; Black et al., 2022) replacing the original absolute positional embeddings. The paper still refers to this as "GPT2" for brevity. The primary model size is GPT2-12-12: 12 layers, 12 attention heads, hidden dimension 768 (12 × 64 = 768). The authors note they "also played with the Llama architecture (especially with gated MLP layers) and did not see any benefit of using it." Context length is 768 for iGSM-med pretraining, 1024 for iGSM-hard pretraining, and 2048 for all evaluation.

**Pretraining hyperparameters.** The paper uses the exact same hyperparameters as Ye et al. (2024) for the error-free baseline:

For iGSM-med:
- Optimizer: AdamW with mixed-precision fp16, β = (0.9, 0.98)
- Learning rate: 0.002, with cosine decay down to 0.01× final + 1000 steps linear ramp-up
- Weight decay: 0.05
- Batch size: 512
- Context length: 768
- Training steps: 100,000

For iGSM-hard:
- Optimizer: AdamW with mixed-precision fp16, β = (0.9, 0.98)
- Learning rate: 0.002
- Weight decay: 0.03
- Batch size: 256
- Context length: 1024
- Training steps: 200,000

Critically, the paper explicitly states: "we adopt the learning rate from [29] (which was best tuned for the original error-free data) directly to the retry data." This means no hyperparameter tuning was done for the retry data—the same settings are used for fair comparison.

**Data formatting for pretraining.** Math problems (with their solutions) are randomly generated on-the-fly, concatenated together, and truncated on the right to fit within the context window. If a problem with solution exceeds the context length, it is discarded, and a new one is generated. This truncation applies to both error-free and retry data; however, the paper notes an important difference at evaluation time: "Adding errors lengthens the solutions, and discarding such data would bias the test distribution towards simpler problems. Thus, when comparing models pretrained with error-free vs retry data in Figure 4(b), the models were evaluated using the same test data distribution." The evaluation set is constructed from error-free ground-truth solutions, and any problem whose ground-truth solution is too long for the evaluation context is discarded—but this filtering is identical for all models.

**Retry rate sweep.** The paper sweeps retry rate across values in {0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4, 0.5}. A retry rate of 0.0 would correspond to error-free data (though the paper uses separately constructed error-free data as the baseline). Retry rate = 0.5 is described as "already sufficiently extreme, indicating that half of the solution steps contain errors."

**The label masking ablation.** For each retry rate, the paper trains two variants:
- **No masking (standard autoregressive):** The model computes next-token prediction loss on ALL tokens, including the erroneous parameter names and `[BACK]` tokens. The model must learn to generate both the error pattern and the correction.
- **With masking:** The loss is not computed on the erroneous parameter tokens (the "strikethrough" text in Figure 4(a)). The model still sees these tokens as context (they are in the input sequence) but is not penalized for failing to predict them. The `[BACK]` token and the correction tokens still contribute to the loss.

This ablation addresses the intuitive concern that training on mistakes might teach the model to make mistakes. If masking helps, it would indicate that the model benefits from seeing the error context but should not be directly optimized to reproduce errors.

**Evaluation protocol.** For each model, the paper evaluates on 4096 math problems per test condition (each cell in the result tables represents 4096 evaluations). The paper runs two random seeds for pretraining and evaluates with both beam=1 (greedy, `do_sample=false`) and beam=4 (beam-search multinomial sampling, `do_sample=true`). The reported accuracy in each cell is the **maximum** over the 2 seeds × 2 beam choices. This is a "best-effort" reporting strategy rather than a mean—it shows what each method can achieve when its best run is selected, not the expected performance.

The paper notes the reasoning for the beam settings: "we discover it is better to keep do_sample=false while beam=1 and do_sample=true while beam > 1."

**Accuracy is not just answer-matching.** The evaluation "not simply from comparing the answer integers (between 0 and 22); instead we have written a parser to make sure the model's intermediate solution steps are fully-correct." This means a solution must have the right final answer AND all intermediate steps must be valid (correct parameter ordering, correct computations) to count as correct. This is a stricter metric than answer-only accuracy.

**Retry count and unnecessary parameter statistics.** Beyond accuracy, the paper computes two behavioral metrics on generated solutions (Figures 5 and 6, computed over 4096 problems per condition):

- **Retry count:** The average number of `[BACK]` tokens the model generates per test problem. This measures how often the model actually uses the error-correction mechanism at inference time. Reported separately for correct and wrong solutions.

- **Unnecessary operations/parameters:** The average number of extra operations or parameters the model computes beyond what is strictly necessary for the shortest solution. Ye et al. (2024) showed that models pretrained on error-free data learn to produce shortest solutions without computing unnecessary parameters; the paper checks whether retry data breaks this property.

#### The LoRA Fine-Tuning Setup

Section 5 tests whether a model already pretrained on error-free data can acquire error correction through parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) on retry data, as opposed to including retry data in pretraining from scratch. This simulates the practical scenario where a practitioner has a pretrained model and wants to add error-correction capability.

**LoRA configuration.** Low-Rank Adaptation (Hu et al., 2021) freezes the pretrained weights and adds trainable low-rank matrices to selected weight matrices. The paper applies LoRA to:
- **Query and value matrices** in all transformer layers, with rank r ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256}.
- **Embedding matrix**, with rank 2r (following Allen-Zhu and Li, 2024).

The notation "LoRA qvXeY" means rank X on query/value and rank Y on embedding. The paper sweeps configurations from very lightweight (qv4e8) to essentially full fine-tuning (qv256e512, where rank 256 approaches the hidden dimension of 768).

**Fine-tuning hyperparameters.** The paper uses similar settings to pretraining but with adjustments for LoRA:

For iGSM-med LoRA fine-tuning:
- Learning rate: 0.001 (smaller than pretraining, as is typical for LoRA)
- Weight decay: 0.05
- Batch size: 256 (half of pretraining)
- Context length: 768
- Training steps: 200,000

For iGSM-hard LoRA fine-tuning:
- Learning rate: 0.001
- Weight decay: 0.05
- Batch size: 128
- Context length: 1024
- Training steps: 200,000

For iGSM-med, 200,000 steps at batch size 256 is effectively twice the pretraining token count (pretraining used 100,000 steps at batch size 512), ensuring the fine-tuning process has seen sufficient retry data. For iGSM-hard, 200,000 steps at batch size 128 equals the pretraining token count. The paper states this is "sufficient for the training curve to plateau" and that the last checkpoint is used for evaluation ("not the best; but this is okay since there is no catastrophic forgetting in this finetune process (because there are sufficiently many, freshly-sampled retry data)").

**Full fine-tuning ("continued pretraining") setup.** For comparison, the paper also runs full fine-tuning (all parameters unfrozen) on retry data starting from an error-free pretrained checkpoint. Hyperparameters:

For iGSM-med full fine-tuning:
- Learning rate: 0.001
- Weight decay: 0.05
- Batch size: 512
- Context length: 768
- Training steps: 100,000 (same token count as original pretraining)

For iGSM-hard full fine-tuning:
- Learning rate: 0.001
- Weight decay: 0.03
- Batch size: 256
- Context length: 1024
- Training steps: 200,000 (same token count as original pretraining)

The paper notes that learning rates of 0.002 and/or different weight decays were also tried with "results very similar."

**The "pretrain double-time" comparison.** Since full fine-tuning on retry data means the model has seen T tokens of error-free data (during pretraining) plus T tokens of retry data (during fine-tuning), it has seen twice as many total training tokens as a model pretrained from scratch on retry data for T tokens. To make the comparison fair, the paper also runs "pretrain (double-time)" on retry data—pretraining from scratch on 2T tokens of retry data (200,000 steps for iGSM-med, 400,000 steps for iGSM-hard). Hyperparameters are slightly adjusted: weight decay decreased to 0.03 (iGSM-med) or 0.02 (iGSM-hard) to account for the longer training.

**Evaluation of fine-tuned models.** Same as pretraining: 4096 problems per condition, 2 seeds, best of beam=1/4 reported.

#### Fake Retry Data Construction (Retry Weak and Retry Miss)

Section 6 addresses a practical concern: the "perfect" retry data requires knowing the dependency graph to identify which parameters are genuinely uncomputable at each step. In real math problems, this semantic understanding may not be available. The paper explores two methods for constructing "fake" retry data that require minimal understanding of the solution structure.

**Retry weak data.** Instead of selecting a parameter that is genuinely uncomputable at the current position, this method randomly selects a sentence that appears later in the solution and inserts that sentence's parameter name as the "error." Concretely, at the current position in the solution, the generator looks at all solution sentences that come after the current one, randomly picks one, extracts its parameter name, and inserts it as the retry attempt (followed by `[BACK]`).

For example, in Figure 2's solution (six sentences defining p, W, B, g, w, c in order), at the position where "Film Studio's Messenger Backpack as W" should be defined, the generator might randomly select "Central High's Film Studio as B" (sentence 3), "Film Studio's Backpack as w" (sentence 5), or "Central High's Backpack as c" (sentence 6) as the fake error. The three underlined parameters in example (6.1) show this selection.

This method is "very easy to implement: one simply needs to select a future sentence and append it with a [BACK] symbol." It does not require knowing whether the selected parameter is actually computable at the current position—some future parameters might legitimately be computable now (if their dependencies happen to be satisfied early), but many won't be. The paper's intuition is that this "encourages the model not to skip steps"—even if the "error" isn't always a true error, the pattern of "try something, backtrack, write the correct thing" teaches the model to be cautious about ordering.

**Retry miss data.** A slightly more sophisticated variant: instead of selecting from future solution sentences, the generator selects any parameter from the *problem statement* that has not yet appeared in the solution. For example (6.1), at position ♢♢♢, the generator could insert "Riverview High's Film Studio" (a parameter mentioned in the problem but not yet computed) in addition to the future solution parameters.

This is "harder to obtain than retry weak but easier than the perfect retry data." Intuitively, it encourages the model "not only 'not to skip steps' but also not to compute unnecessary parameters"—since the fake retry parameters include parameters that may never need to be computed at all (they are in the problem but not needed for the queried parameter).

**Comparison with perfect retry data.** The perfect retry data uses only parameters that are genuinely uncomputable at the current step (dependencies not yet satisfied). Retry weak uses any future solution step's parameter (may be computable or not). Retry miss uses any unseen problem parameter (may be needed later, may not be needed at all). The progression is: perfect retry > retry miss > retry weak in terms of semantic accuracy of the errors, but the reverse in terms of ease of construction.

**Experimental setup for fake retry data.** The paper trains models from scratch on retry weak and retry miss data using the same pretraining hyperparameters as the perfect retry experiments (Section D.1). The same retry rate sweep (0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5) and label masking ablation are applied. Results are compared against the error-free baseline and the perfect retry data results.

#### The "Retry Upon Regret" as an Inference-Only Baseline: Detailed Mechanics

This subsection provides additional detail on the "retry upon regret" inference procedure, which serves as a critical baseline distinguishing the paper's approach from inference-time interventions.

**Why maximum retries are necessary.** Without a cap on retries, the model could enter an infinite loop: generate error → detect error → regenerate → generate same error → detect error → ... The paper sets a maximum of 10 total retries for the entire solution (version1/2) and also tests a cap of 50 (versionP50). If the maximum is reached, the last generated sentence is kept regardless of the probe's output. This means the final answer might be wrong if the model ran out of retries.

**Multinomial sampling for regeneration.** The regeneration step uses multinomial sampling (`do_sample=true`) rather than greedy decoding. This is essential because greedy decoding would deterministically produce the same (erroneous) output given the same prefix—there would be no diversity to find a correct step. The temperature is not explicitly specified, but the default GPT2 sampling settings are used.

**Relationship to beam search.** Beam search with beam size B also explores alternatives, but the mechanism is fundamentally different:
- Beam search uses the model's next-token probability distribution to select the top-B continuations at each step; it has no notion of "correctness" beyond the model's own likelihood estimates.
- "Retry upon regret" uses an external correctness signal (the can_next probe) to decide *when* to explore alternatives, and uses random sampling (not likelihood ranking) to generate alternatives.

The paper finds that beam search with up to 32 beams does not substantially improve accuracy on models pretrained with error-free data (Figure 3(b)). This is evidence that the model's likelihood estimates are not well-calibrated for distinguishing correct from incorrect reasoning steps—the model assigns similar probabilities to both, so beam search cannot prune effectively.

**The role of randomness.** The paper is explicit that "retry upon regret" relies on randomness to correct errors: "improving final accuracy relies solely on randomness (for re-generation) to correct errors—similar to beam search." The model has not learned what the correct step should be; it's simply trying again and hoping for a different outcome. This is why the gains are limited even with perfect error detection—if the correct step has low probability under the model's distribution, resampling may never find it.

This contrast with pretraining on retry data is the paper's central argument: retry data teaches the model to *deterministically* produce the correct step after a `[BACK]`, rather than relying on random resampling.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Error Correction Is a Distinct Skill from Error-Free Reasoning — Not an Inference-Time Patch

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is establishing that **error correction during generation is a qualitatively different capability from error-free reasoning**, not simply an extension or refinement of it. This is not an obvious claim—the natural assumption, and the approach taken by much prior work on self-correction, is that if a model can reason correctly *and* can detect its own mistakes (which probing shows it can), then enabling correction is just a matter of providing the right inference-time mechanism (prompting, beam search, regeneration loops). The paper systematically dismantles this assumption.

**What the field assumed before this work.** The dominant paradigm for improving reasoning accuracy at inference time falls into two camps. The first camp uses external verifiers (Cobbe et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2022) to score and select among complete generated solutions—the model generates, the verifier filters. The second camp uses multi-round prompting for self-correction (Madaan et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2023): the model generates a solution, is prompted to verify it, and produces a correction in a subsequent turn. Both approaches treat error correction as something that happens *after* generation, mediated by an external mechanism (a separate verifier model or a second inference pass with different prompting). The implicit assumption is that the model *could* correct errors if only it had the right scaffolding.

**The paper's diagnostic move.** The "retry upon regret" experiments in Section 3 serve as a controlled test of this scaffolding assumption. The model is given an error detector that is 99% accurate—far better than any prompting-based self-verification could achieve—and is allowed to regenerate upon detecting a mistake. If error correction were merely a matter of detecting mistakes and trying again (as beam search and multi-round correction implicitly assume), this setup should work extremely well. It doesn't. The accuracy gain is marginal (~2 percentage points on the hardest problems). This is a crucial negative result: even with near-perfect knowledge *that* a mistake occurred, the model cannot reliably produce the correct step on the next attempt—it lacks the knowledge of *how* to correct.

**The defining contrast with error detection.** The probing results from Ye et al. (2024) showed that error detection emerges almost for free from error-free pretraining—a rank-8 embedding update can extract it with 99% accuracy. One might expect error correction to be similarly accessible. The LoRA fine-tuning experiments (Section 5, Figure 7) prove otherwise: starting from an error-free pretrained model, even extensive parameter-efficient fine-tuning on retry data cannot teach error correction. Across a wide sweep of LoRA configurations (ranks from 4 to 256), with or without label masking, the fine-tuned models fail to improve over the original—and low-rank configurations actually *hurt* performance. Yet the same retry data, when included in pretraining from scratch, yields dramatic gains (78% → 94% on the hardest problems).

This asymmetry—detection is trivially accessible, correction is fundamentally inaccessible without pretraining—establishes the paper's core claim: error correction is not a downstream refinement of reasoning ability. It is a separate skill that must be learned alongside reasoning during the model's foundational training. This is a fundamental conceptual shift, not an incremental finding. It implies that future LLMs intended to self-correct should have error-correction patterns in their pretraining data, not just in post-hoc fine-tuning, because the representations needed for correction differ qualitatively from those needed for error-free generation.

**Evidence anchor.** The gap between "retry upon regret" (Figure 3(b)) and pretraining with retry data (Figure 4(b)) on the same problems at matched budgets, combined with the LoRA failure (Figure 7), forms the empirical backbone of this claim. The contrast is stark: inference-time mechanisms with perfect error detection achieve ~80% on iGSM-med op=23; pretraining on retry data achieves ~95%.

### Innovation 2: Training on Mistakes Does Not Produce Mistake-Prone Models — The "More Errors, Better Performance" Result

A deeply counterintuitive finding—and one the paper anticipates readers will resist—is that **pretraining on data with high error rates does not cause the model to generate more errors at inference time; instead, it improves reasoning accuracy, and within a reasonable range, higher error rates yield better performance.** This challenges a natural intuition about language model training: that optimizing next-token prediction on data containing errors should teach the model to reproduce those errors.

**Why this is surprising.** The autoregressive training objective minimizes negative log-likelihood over all tokens in the training sequence. On retry data, this means the model is directly trained to predict erroneous parameter names at the positions where they appear. One would reasonably expect the model to learn to generate errors at inference time—after all, that's what the loss function is optimizing for. The paper itself frames this as a primary concern in Section 1, calling it the "potential harm of next-token prediction on data with mistakes." Standard mitigation would be label masking (ignoring the loss on erroneous tokens), which the paper tests as an ablation.

**What actually happens.** The behavioral statistics in Figures 5 and 6 reveal the opposite of the intuitive fear. At retry rate = 0.2, models generate an average of fewer than 0.3 retries per problem—meaning they produce error-free first attempts the vast majority of the time. Even at the extreme retry rate = 0.5, where half of all solution steps in training data contain errors, models produce only 2–4 retries on average for the hardest problems, and those retries lead to successful corrections (Figure 5(a)). They do not blindly reproduce the error-filled style of their training data.

The paper's explanation (in the discussion of Result 4) is grounded in probability: even at retry rate = 0.5, at any given step there are typically more *wrong* parameter choices than *correct* ones, so the model's maximum-likelihood solution under a low sampling temperature is still to generate the correct parameter. The errors in training are essentially "noise" that the model learns to route around—it learns the pattern "if I've written something wrong, emit [BACK] and fix it," not "produce wrong things."

**Why this matters beyond the specific experiment.** This finding has practical implications for data curation in LLM pretraining. Current pipelines often prioritize "clean" data: remove errors, filter for correctness, ensure high quality. The paper's results suggest this instinct, while sensible, may be suboptimal. Including some proportion of error-correction data—even artificially constructed, as in the "retry weak" experiments—may teach models valuable self-correction skills that clean data alone cannot provide, and the model will not "learn the errors" in a way that degrades its primary behavior. This inverts the conventional wisdom: errors in training data are not necessarily contamination to be removed; they can be teaching material if presented in the right format.

The fact that label masking is *unnecessary* (Figure 4(b) shows masked and unmasked variants perform similarly, and Figure 5(a) shows unmasked models still rarely produce errors) further simplifies the practical takeaway: one does not need to modify the training objective or implement masking logic. Standard autoregressive training simply works.

**Evidence anchor.** Figure 4(b) shows monotonic accuracy improvement with retry rate for all dataset variants. Figure 5(a) shows retry counts at inference time remain low even for high training retry rates. Figure 6 shows the models continue to produce shortest solutions without unnecessary parameters—the desirable properties of error-free training are preserved.

### Innovation 3: Retry Weak Data — A Method That Requires Zero Semantic Understanding of the Solution

Section 6 introduces a method for constructing error-correction training data that the paper calls **retry weak**, which is intellectually distinctive not for its technical sophistication but for its **minimalism relative to its effectiveness**. The method requires no dependency parsing, no semantic understanding of when a step is logically valid, and no knowledge of which parameters depend on which. It simply inserts a random future sentence's parameter name, followed by [BACK], before the correct current sentence. Despite this apparent crudeness, it achieves nearly the same accuracy improvements as the "perfect" retry data that uses ground-truth dependency information.

**Why this is a conceptual advance, not just a trick.** The standard approach to creating training data for error correction would be to identify *actual errors*—steps where the model could plausibly go wrong—and provide *correct corrections*. This requires understanding the solution structure. The retry weak method demonstrates that **the exact correctness of the error is not what matters for learning**. What matters is the *pattern*: attempt something, realize it's wrong (signaled by [BACK]), produce the right thing. The model learns from this pattern that it should verify its own outputs and that backtracking is possible, even when the specific "errors" in training are sometimes not errors at all (some future parameters might legitimately be computable early).

This finding connects to a broader insight about language model learning: models can extract abstract procedural patterns from data even when the surface-level content is imperfect or noisy. The retry weak data teaches the *procedure* of self-correction without needing to teach the *semantics* of what constitutes an error. The model figures out the semantics from the broader distribution of correct solutions in the training data.

**Comparison with perfect retry data.** The perfect retry data (Section 4) uses ground-truth uncomputable parameters as errors. The retry weak data (Section 6) uses random future parameters, which may or may not be errors. The gap between them on the hardest problems is small: on iGSM-med op=23 with retry rate = 0.5, perfect retry achieves ~95% while retry weak achieves ~94% (Figure 8 vs. Figure 4(b)). Both substantially outperform the error-free baseline (~78%). This near-parity despite the drastically different quality of error selection is the key observation.

**Practical significance.** The retry weak method can be applied to any math dataset with step-by-step solutions without any modification. One takes a correct solution, picks a random future step at each position, inserts it as a "fake retry," and adds a [BACK] token. No verifier, no dependency analysis, no semantic parsing. This dramatically lowers the barrier to creating retry-like training data for real math problems, making the paper's findings actionable beyond the synthetic iGSM setting.

**The retry miss negative result.** The paper also tests retry miss data—selecting unseen parameters from the problem statement—which is slightly harder to construct but more semantically accurate (as it includes parameters that genuinely shouldn't be computed yet). Surprisingly, retry miss *does not* improve accuracy beyond retry weak (Figure 8), and in some cases performs worse. This is a revealing negative result: more semantic accuracy in error selection does not translate to better learning. The simpler, noisier signal suffices. The paper's interpretation is that retry weak's focus on *future steps* teaches "don't skip ahead," which is the core skill, while retry miss's inclusion of *irrelevant parameters* (ones never needed for the answer) introduces a slightly different and less useful signal.

**Evidence anchor.** Figure 8 directly compares retry weak and retry miss at multiple retry rates against the error-free baseline across all dataset variants. The retry weak curves track closely with the perfect retry results from Figure 4(b), while retry miss shows substantially smaller gains.

### Innovation 4: The Concept of "Regretful" Internal States as Both a Diagnostic and a Boundary Condition

The paper repurposes the probing methodology from Ye et al. (2024) into a new diagnostic concept: **the model exhibits "regretful" internal states after making a reasoning mistake—its representations encode the knowledge that it has erred—but this knowledge is causally insufficient for correction.** This finding is not just a replication of prior probing results; it uses those results to establish a sharp boundary between two capabilities that the field often conflates: error *awareness* and error *correction*.

**What the probing reveals that's genuinely new.** The prior work (Ye et al., 2024) established that a linear probe can detect whether a parameter is ready for computation with 99% accuracy, and that when the model makes a mistake, this probe has ~60% accuracy at detecting the error state. The current paper extends this observation in two ways. First, it demonstrates that even with this probe deployed as an online error detector during inference—triggering regeneration when an error is detected—the improvement in final accuracy is marginal. Second, it shows that a much more sample-efficient probe (version2, using only 0.25% of the training tokens) achieves the same detection accuracy, meaning the error detection signal is not just present but *highly accessible* from the model's representations.

**The boundary condition.** The "regretful" state finding establishes that error correction is bottlenecked not by error *detection* but by error *resolution*. The model knows it's wrong; it doesn't know what to do about it. This is a non-trivial distinction. In reinforcement learning terms, the model has learned a value function (it can evaluate the correctness of its current state) but has not learned a policy for recovery from incorrect states. The retry data provides exactly this: training examples of the transition from an erroneous state to the correct continuation.

**Why this reframes the self-correction literature.** The self-correction literature has largely focused on improving the *detection* side—better verifiers, better prompting strategies for self-verification, more accurate outcome scoring. The paper's diagnostic shows that detection is already surprisingly easy (accessible via lightweight probing with minimal data). The hard part is teaching the model what to do after detection. This suggests that the bottleneck in current self-correction systems is not verification quality but the model's inability to generate corrections, and that data-level interventions (retry data in pretraining) target this bottleneck directly in a way that inference-time verification strategies cannot.

**Evidence anchor.** The contrast between the 99% probing accuracy (Result 0) and the ~2% accuracy gain from "retry upon regret" (Result 1, Figure 3(b)) crystallizes this insight. The gap between version1/2 (learned probe) and versionP (perfect ground-truth probe) in Figure 3(b) further shows that even perfect detection is insufficient—the model simply cannot generate corrections reliably without having seen correction patterns during training.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the iGSM dataset from Ye et al. (2024), a synthetic, program-generated math reasoning benchmark consisting of problems with step-by-step solutions. The dataset removes arithmetic difficulty (computations use integers modulo 23) and common-sense knowledge, isolating pure logic-following reasoning. It is organized into four families: **iGSM-medpq/qp** (train on op ≤ 15; OOD test on op ∈ {20, 21, 22, 23}) and **iGSM-hardpq/qp** (train on op ≤ 21; OOD test on op ∈ {28, 29, 30, 31, 32}), where `pq`/`qp` indicates whether the problem description precedes or follows the question. Additionally, **reask** variants re-sample a different parameter to query, creating a harder OOD evaluation by substantially changing the data distribution. The number of distinct solution templates exceeds 90 trillion for iGSM-med and is effectively infinite for iGSM-hard; all training and evaluation data is generated on-the-fly from these templates, with no fixed training set.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use GPT2-12-12: a 12-layer, 12-head decoder-only transformer with 768-dimensional hidden states and rotary positional embeddings (RoPE), following the GPT2 architecture of Radford et al. (2019). The paper states it "also played with the Llama architecture (especially with gated MLP layers) and did not see any benefit of using it." The model size is chosen as representative of a small-but-capable language model; its non-trivial-but-imperfect accuracy on the iGSM tasks leaves substantial room for improvement from test-time and training-time interventions. Context length is 768 for iGSM-med pretraining, 1024 for iGSM-hard pretraining, and 2048 for all evaluation.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **full-solution accuracy (%)**: the fraction of test problems for which the model's generated solution has both (a) the correct final answer integer (modulo 23) and (b) all intermediate steps fully correct (valid parameter ordering, valid computations), verified by a parser rather than simple answer matching. This is stricter than answer-only accuracy. Secondary behavioral metrics include **retry count** (average number of `[BACK]` tokens generated per problem, reported separately for correct and wrong solutions), **unnecessary operations** (average number of extra arithmetic operations computed beyond the minimal solution), and **unnecessary parameters** (average number of extra parameters computed that are not needed for the queried answer). All metrics are averaged over 4096 evaluations per test condition per cell in the result tables. For the main accuracy results, the paper reports the best accuracy over 2 pretraining seeds × 2 beam choices (beam=1 greedy, beam=4 with multinomial sampling), presenting "best-effort" rather than mean performance.

- **Baselines.** The paper uses several controlled baselines:
  - **Original (error-free) pretraining**: Models pretrained from scratch on iGSM data containing only correct step-by-step solutions, with zero retry data. This is the primary reference point for all comparisons and represents the standard pretraining paradigm. Training uses exactly the same hyperparameters as the retry data experiments (learning rate, weight decay, batch size, training steps, architecture), differing only in the data composition.
  - **Beam search** (with beam sizes 1, 4, 16, 32): Standard beam search applied at inference time to models pretrained on error-free data. The paper finds that "even with 16 or 32 beams, its accuracy does not noticeably improve in all the cases" (Section 4), establishing that likelihood-based search cannot substitute for learned error correction.
  - **"Retry upon regret" (multiple versions)**: An inference-time intervention on error-free pretrained models that uses a learned can_next probe to detect errors and trigger regeneration from the previous step. Version1 and version2 use different probe training configurations (rank-8 vs. rank-32 embedding updates, different training sample counts) but both achieve ~99% error detection accuracy. VersionP uses perfect ground-truth error detection. VersionP50 extends versionP with a maximum of 50 retries (vs. 10 for the other versions). These baselines establish that even near-perfect error detection is insufficient for correction without retry data in pretraining.
  - **Label-masked variants**: For each retry data configuration, the paper trains a parallel model where the autoregressive loss on erroneous tokens is masked (ignored), testing whether standard next-token prediction on errors is harmful.
  - **LoRA fine-tuning**: Parameter-efficient fine-tuning on retry data starting from an error-free pretrained model, sweeping LoRA ranks from 4 to 256 on query/value matrices and 8 to 512 on the embedding layer. This tests whether error correction can be acquired post-hoc.
  - **Full fine-tuning / continued pretraining**: Full-parameter fine-tuning on retry data starting from an error-free pretrained checkpoint, using the same number of training tokens as the original pretraining. This tests whether error correction can be acquired through full-weight updates post-hoc.
  - **Pretrain (double-time)**: Pretraining from scratch on retry data for twice the standard number of training tokens, providing a fair comparison against continued pretraining (which has seen twice the total tokens: T error-free + T retry).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in **training tokens**, not inference FLOPs or wall-clock time. For pretraining comparisons, all models are trained on the same number of tokens (with the exception of "pretrain double-time" which uses 2× tokens for specific comparisons). Since retry data solutions are longer (they contain erroneous tokens and `[BACK]` tokens), a model pretrained on retry data for T tokens sees *fewer distinct math problems* than a model pretrained on error-free data for T tokens. This makes the comparison conservative: any accuracy improvement from retry data is achieved despite seeing fewer unique problems. At inference time, the generation budget is implicit in the beam size (1 or 4) and the maximum number of retries (10 or 50 for "retry upon regret"); the paper does not directly compare inference-time compute across methods. For the retry data experiments, the paper explicitly states that training parameters (learning rate, weight decay, batch size, steps) were "best tuned for the original error-free data" and applied "directly to the retry data" without additional hyperparameter optimization, ensuring a controlled comparison.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no cross-validation in the traditional sense. The paper evaluates on fixed OOD test sets constructed by the iGSM data generator. For each test condition (e.g., iGSM-med op=23 pq), exactly 4096 fresh problems are generated and evaluated. To account for training variance, each model configuration is pretrained with two different random seeds. To account for inference variance, each trained model is evaluated with both beam=1 (greedy, `do_sample=false`) and beam=4 (beam-search multinomial, `do_sample=true`). The reported accuracy in each result cell is the **maximum** over these 2 seeds × 2 beam choices—this is a best-case reporting strategy rather than a mean or confidence-interval-based estimate. The paper does not report error bars, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests for any of the accuracy numbers.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Pretraining with Perfect Retry Data vs. Error-Free Data (Section 4)

The headline result appears in **Figure 4(b)**: models pretrained on retry data consistently outperform those pretrained on the same number of tokens of error-free data, with **higher retry rates producing larger accuracy gains**, especially on the hardest out-of-distribution problems.

For the **iGSM-med datasets** (Figure 4(b), top two blocks), the key numbers are:

- **In-distribution (op ≤ 15)**: Both error-free and retry-trained models achieve near-perfect accuracy (99–100%). There is essentially no room for improvement, and retry data does not hurt.
- **OOD op = 20**: Error-free achieves 88–90% (pq/qp). Retry rate = 0.05 already reaches 93–94%; retry rate = 0.5 reaches ~97%. The gain is largest going from error-free to retry rate = 0.05, with diminishing marginal returns for higher rates.
- **OOD op = 23 (hardest)**: This is where the effect is most dramatic. On iGSM-medpq op=23, error-free accuracy is 77.7%. Retry rate = 0.05: 83.9%. Retry rate = 0.1: 87.5%. Retry rate = 0.2: 90.6%. Retry rate = 0.5: 93.6%. The improvement is +15.9 percentage points. On iGSM-medqp op=23, error-free achieves 78.3%; retry rate = 0.5 reaches 94.5%—a **+16.2 percentage point** improvement. The trend in the pq variant is approximately monotonic: each doubling of retry rate from 0.05 to 0.5 yields roughly 2–3 additional percentage points.
- **OOD op=20 reask**: This is a harder OOD distribution (the queried parameter is re-sampled). Error-free pq achieves 91.6%; retry rate = 0.5 reaches 96.0–96.1%. Error-free qp achieves 91.4%; retry rate = 0.5 reaches 93.7–95.8%. The gains are substantial but smaller than on the hardest op levels, since the baseline is already relatively high.

For the **iGSM-hard datasets** (Figure 4(b), bottom two blocks), the patterns are similar but the absolute accuracies are lower and the gaps are larger:

- **OOD op = 32 (hardest)**: On iGSM-hardpq op=32, error-free achieves 84.0% (pq) and 85.3% (qp). Retry rate = 0.05: 94.8–96.6%. Retry rate = 0.1: 97.1–97.3%. Retry rate = 0.2: 97.1–97.9%. Retry rate = 0.4: 97.6%. Retry rate = 0.5: 98.3–98.9%. The improvement is **+14.3 to +16.4 percentage points**. Notably, on iGSM-hardqp op=32, retry rate = 0.5 with label masking reaches 98.9%, compared to 85.3% for error-free—almost eliminating the gap between OOD and in-distribution performance.
- **OOD op=28 reask**: Error-free pq achieves 91.4%; retry rate = 0.5 reaches 97.7–98.8%. Error-free qp achieves 89.4%; retry rate = 0.5 reaches 97.0%. These are large gains (+6.3 to +7.6 percentage points) on an already strong baseline.
- **In-distribution (op ≤ 21)**: Both error-free and retry-trained models achieve 99–100%. No degradation from retry data.

**The label masking ablation** (Figure 4(b), alternating rows with/without mask for each retry rate): Across all dataset variants, retry rates, and difficulty levels, there is **no systematic difference** between masked and unmasked training. For example, on iGSM-medpq op=23, retry rate = 0.5 achieves 93.6% without masking and 95.2% with masking—a small difference that is not consistent across conditions (on iGSM-medqp op=23, retry rate = 0.5 without masking achieves 94.5%, *higher* than the 90.4% of the same configuration on pq). The paper concludes: "Masking mistakes is unnecessary." The model does not learn to produce errors even when trained on them with full autoregressive loss.

**The "more mistakes, better performance" finding** is visible as a monotonic trend in almost every OOD column: accuracy increases with retry rate from 0.05 → 0.1 → 0.2 → 0.5. The only exception is occasional saturation at the highest rates (e.g., iGSM-hard op=32 where retry rate 0.2, 0.4, and 0.5 all achieve ~97–98%, indicating diminishing returns). The paper explicitly notes: "within a reasonable range, the more mistakes the better." The practical ceiling appears to be around retry rate = 0.5—the paper calls this "already sufficiently extreme" and does not test higher values.

**Behavioral statistics (Figures 5 and 6)** explain *why* the model doesn't learn to produce errors despite training on error-filled data:

- **Retry counts on correct solutions (Figure 5(a))**: At retry rate = 0.05–0.2, the average number of `[BACK]` tokens per correctly solved test problem is below 0.3 across all difficulty levels. Even at retry rate = 0.5 without masking, the hardest problems (op=32) average only ~2–4 retries. With masking, this drops to ~1–3. The model rarely uses the retry mechanism on problems it solves correctly—it generates the right step first.

- **Retry counts on wrong solutions (Figure 5(b))**: On problems the model gets wrong, retry counts are substantially higher. At retry rate = 0.5 without masking on iGSM-hard op=32, wrong solutions average ~8.5 retries—the model is trying to correct itself but failing. This is exactly the desired behavior: the model retries aggressively when it's off-track, but doesn't introduce errors on problems it can solve.

- **Unnecessary operations and parameters (Figure 6)**: Across all retry rates and configurations, the average number of unnecessary operations per correct solution remains below ~0.02 for in-distribution problems and below ~1.0 for the hardest OOD problems. The model continues to find shortest solutions without computing extraneous parameters, preserving the desirable properties observed in error-free training (Ye et al., 2024).

**Figure 6 also reveals an interesting subtlety**: On the hardest OOD problems (op=23/32), models pretrained with retry rate = 0.5 and *masking* actually produce slightly fewer unnecessary operations (~2–3 on average) than their unmasked counterparts (~3–5 on average), while achieving similar or slightly better accuracy (Figure 4(b)). This suggests that, at extreme retry rates, masking may provide a small efficiency benefit by reducing the model's tendency to produce spurious retries—but the effect is minor and does not change the overall conclusion that masking is unnecessary.

#### "Retry Upon Regret" vs. Pretraining with Retry Data (Sections 3 and 4)

**Figure 3(b)** presents the "retry upon regret" results on error-free pretrained models, establishing the baseline that motivates the entire retry data investigation:

- **Original (error-free pretrained, no regeneration)**: On iGSM-medpq op=23, beam=1 achieves 77.7%, beam=4 achieves 84.5%, beam=16 achieves 86.8%, beam=32 achieves 82.8%. Increasing beam size provides diminishing and inconsistent returns; beam=16 is best but still well below what retry data achieves.
- **Retry upon regret version1 (rank-8 probe, beam=1)**: On iGSM-medpq op=23, achieves 79.4%—a +1.7 percentage point improvement over beam=1 original, but worse than beam=4 original (84.5%).
- **Retry upon regret version1 (rank-8 probe, beam=4)**: Achieves 85.4%—roughly matching beam=16 original.
- **Retry upon regret version2 (rank-32 probe with query/value updates, beam=4)**: Achieves 85.4–86.5%, comparable to version1.
- **Retry upon regret versionP (perfect ground-truth detection, beam=1)**: Achieves 84.1% on iGSM-medpq op=23, 93.8% on iGSM-medqp op=23.
- **Retry upon regret versionP (perfect ground-truth detection, beam=4)**: Achieves 92.1–94.3% across variants.
- **Retry upon regret versionP50 (perfect detection, 50 max retries, beam=1)**: Achieves 93.8% on iGSM-medpq op=23, 94.6% on iGSM-medqp op=23.
- **Retry upon regret versionP50 (perfect detection, 50 max retries, beam=4)**: Achieves ~94.3–95.0%.

The key comparison is versionP50 against pretraining with retry data. On iGSM-medpq op=23, versionP50 with beam=1 achieves 93.8%; retry pretraining with retry rate = 0.5 achieves 93.6% (Figure 4(b)). On iGSM-medqp op=23, versionP50 with beam=1 achieves 94.6%; retry pretraining with retry rate = 0.5 achieves 94.5%. These numbers are **nearly identical**—but with a crucial difference: versionP50 uses perfect ground-truth error detection (impossible in practice) and up to 50 regeneration attempts per problem, while retry pretraining uses standard autoregressive decoding with no external error detector and no regeneration. The fact that retry pretraining matches perfect-detection-based regeneration demonstrates that the model has internalized the correction skill.

However, the gap between version1/2 (learned probe, ~99% detection) and retry pretraining is substantial: version1 with beam=4 achieves 85.4% on iGSM-medpq op=23 vs. 93.6% for retry pretraining—an **8.2 percentage point** gap. This is the paper's core empirical argument: practical error detection (even at 99% accuracy) is insufficient; the model must learn correction during pretraining.

On the **iGSM-hard datasets**, the pattern is similar: on iGSM-hardpq op=32, original beam=1 achieves 84.0%, beam=4 achieves 91.4% (Figure 3(b) top blocks). VersionP50 with beam=4 achieves ~97.5%. Retry pretraining with retry rate = 0.5 achieves 98.3% (Figure 4(b)). On iGSM-hardqp op=32: original beam=1 achieves 85.3%, retry pretraining achieves 98.9%. Once again, retry pretraining matches or exceeds the perfect-detection regeneration upper bound while using standard decoding.

**A critical detail in the Figure 3(b) vs. Figure 4(b) comparison**: The beam search baselines in Figure 3(b) use error-free pretrained models. The accuracy improvements from beam search alone are inconsistent—on iGSM-medqp op=23, beam=1 → beam=4 → beam=16 → beam=32 gives 78.3% → 84.8% → 85.3% → 89.4%, a ± pattern where beam=32 is best but beam=4 is already close to saturation. In contrast, retry pretraining with beam=1 achieves 94.5% on the same task—well beyond what beam=32 can achieve from an error-free model. This demonstrates that retry data provides a qualitatively different capability than likelihood-based search.

**Summary of the Section 3 vs. Section 4 contrast**: The paper's central quantitative claim is established by three numbers on the hardest problems:
- **Error-free pretraining + beam=1**: ~78% (iGSM-med op=23) / ~84% (iGSM-hard op=32)
- **Error-free pretraining + beam search (up to 32 beams)**: ~89% / ~91% (best case, inconsistent)
- **Error-free pretraining + retry upon regret (learned probe, 99% detection, beam=4)**: ~85% / ~90% (marginal improvement over beam search)
- **Retry pretraining (retry rate = 0.5) + beam=1**: ~94% / ~98% (dramatic improvement)

The gap from ~85% to ~94% (iGSM-med) and from ~90% to ~98% (iGSM-hard) is what the paper attributes to the learned error correction skill.

#### LoRA Fine-Tuning on Retry Data (Section 5)

**Figure 7** (main text) and **Figure 10** (appendix, with additional retry rates) present the fine-tuning results. The headline finding is that **LoRA fine-tuning on retry data, starting from an error-free pretrained model, fails to significantly improve accuracy**—and for low-rank configurations, it often *hurts*.

For retry rate = 0.2 on iGSM-med (Figure 7, top-left block):

- **Original (error-free pretrained)**: 99.9% in-dist, 92.0% op=20, 88.4% op=21, 84.5% op=22, 77.7% op=23, 91.6% reask. Row average: ~91.6.
- **LoRA qv4e8 (rank-4 query/value, rank-8 embedding), with mask**: 84.0% op=20, 71.8% op=23—**substantially worse** than the original model. The row average drops to 85.2.
- **LoRA qv4e8, no mask**: 91.2% op=20, 74.3% op=23—better than masked but still below original.
- **LoRA qv8e16 (with mask)**: 86.4% op=20, 68.9% op=23—worse than qv4e8.
- **LoRA qv16e32 (with mask)**: 88.3% op=20, 72.0% op=23.
- **LoRA qv32e64 (with mask)**: 91.8% op=20, 75.9% op=23—approaching original on op=20 but still ~77.7 vs. 75.9 on op=23.
- **LoRA qv64e128 (with mask)**: 89.8% op=20, 74.7% op=23.
- **LoRA qv128e256 (with mask)**: 92.4% op=20, 77.8% op=23—roughly matching original.
- **LoRA qv256e512 (with mask)**: 92.6% op=20, 79.3% op=23—slightly above original on op=23 (79.3 vs. 77.7), but far below retry pretraining (93.6%).

The trend is clear: **low-rank LoRA fine-tuning degrades accuracy, especially on hard OOD problems**. As the rank increases, performance gradually recovers, but even at the highest ranks (128–256, approaching full fine-tuning), the accuracy on the hardest problems (op=23) barely exceeds the original error-free baseline. The row averages (rightmost column in Figure 7) tell the same story: original averages 91.6; LoRA configurations range from 85.2 (qv4e8 masked) to 92.6 (qv256e512 with mask), with most configurations below 92.0.

The pattern is consistent across retry rates (Figure 10): for retry rate = 0.05, 0.1, and 0.5, the LoRA fine-tuned models similarly fail to improve, with low-rank variants hurting performance. At retry rate = 0.5, a subtle exception appears: high-rank LoRA with masking (qv256e512) achieves modest gains on iGSM-med op=23 (roughly 83–84% vs. 77.7% original), but this is still far below retry pretraining at retry rate = 0.5 (93.6%).

**A critical observation on label masking in LoRA fine-tuning**: Unlike pretraining (where masking is unnecessary), **masking is important for LoRA fine-tuning**, especially at low ranks. In Figure 7, for every LoRA configuration, the masked variant outperforms or matches the unmasked variant. At qv4e8, the masked variant achieves 71.8% on op=23 vs. 74.3% unmasked (unmasked is oddly better here), but the overall row average is 85.2 masked vs. 90.5 unmasked—the pattern varies. In Figure 10, for retry rate = 0.5 at low ranks, the masked variants are substantially better: qv4e8 masked achieves ~75% on op=23 vs. ~64% unmasked. This suggests that when model capacity for learning is constrained (low-rank adaptation), avoiding the loss on erroneous tokens prevents the model from learning to produce errors at the expense of correct reasoning. But when capacity is sufficient (full pretraining, or high-rank LoRA), this is not an issue.

**Full fine-tuning / continued pretraining (Figure 7, second-to-last row block)**: Starting from an error-free pretrained model and full fine-tuning on retry data for the same number of tokens as the original pretraining achieves substantial improvements. On iGSM-medpq, retry rate = 0.2 continued pretraining with masking achieves 97.4% op=20, 92.8% op=23—much better than the original (77.7%) and competitive with retry pretraining (93.6%). Unmasked continued pretraining achieves 97.0% op=20, 91.7% op=23—slightly lower. This shows that full-weight updates *can* teach error correction from a pretrained error-free starting point, but at the cost of an additional full pretraining pass (doubling the total training tokens).

**The "pretrain double-time" comparison (Figure 10, last row block for each retry rate)**: To test whether continued pretraining's gains simply come from seeing twice the tokens, the paper pretrains from scratch on 2× tokens of retry data. For retry rate = 0.2, pretrain double-time achieves 98.4–99.1% across all conditions for iGSM-med, and 97.8–99.5% for iGSM-hard—consistently matching or exceeding continued pretraining. The paper concludes: "such full finetuning is no better than directly pretraining with 2T tokens from retry data." The improvement from continued pretraining is due to having seen more retry data tokens, not due to the combination of error-free + retry data.

On iGSM-hard datasets (Figure 7, bottom-right blocks), the same patterns hold:
- Original: 94.9% op=28, 84.0% op=32.
- LoRA fine-tuning (all configurations): No significant improvement; low-rank configurations hurt.
- Continued pretraining: 98.5% op=32 (masked), matching retry pretraining (98.3%).
- Pretrain double-time: 99.1% op=32.

**The paper's conclusion from these results** (Result 7): "Error correction is a skill that can be very different from the original (error-free) reasoning and cannot be acquired during a LoRA finetune stage from language models pretrained only using error-free data." The skill requires major weight changes—potentially beyond what parameter-efficient methods can represent—and is best acquired by including retry data directly in the pretraining corpus.

#### Fake Retry Data: Retry Weak vs. Retry Miss (Section 6)

**Figure 8** presents the results for models pretrained on retry weak and retry miss data, compared against the error-free baseline. The headline finding is that **retry weak data achieves nearly the same accuracy improvements as perfect retry data, while retry miss data provides only marginal benefits**.

For **iGSM-med** with retry weak data (Figure 8, "retry_weak" rows):

- **Retry rate = 0.5 on op=23 pq**: Retry weak achieves 94.3% (unmasked) and 95.4% (masked). Compare to perfect retry data in Figure 4(b): 93.6% (unmasked) and 95.2% (masked). These are nearly identical—retry weak with masking is actually 0.2 percentage points higher than perfect retry.
- **Retry rate = 0.5 on op=23 qp**: Retry weak achieves 92.4% (unmasked) and 91.9–92.2% (masked; see multiple rows for different mask configurations). Perfect retry achieves 94.5% (unmasked) and 90.4% (masked). Here, retry weak unmasked (92.4%) is slightly below perfect retry unmasked (94.5%), but retry weak masked (92.2%) is above perfect retry masked (90.4%).
- **Retry rate = 0.5 on op=32 hardpq**: Retry weak achieves 95.1–96.5% vs. perfect retry's 97.3–98.9%—a gap of ~2–3 percentage points but still far above the error-free baseline (84.0%).
- **Retry rate = 0.5 on op=32 hardqp**: Retry weak achieves 94.8–96.6% vs. perfect retry's 97.5–97.9%.

The overall pattern is that retry weak is **slightly worse** than perfect retry on the hardest problems but still provides **most** of the benefit—raising accuracy from ~78% to ~94% on iGSM-med op=23, and from ~84% to ~96% on iGSM-hard op=32.

The monotonic improvement with retry rate holds for retry weak: retry rate = 0.05 < 0.1 < 0.2 < 0.5 in most columns. Masking provides small or negligible benefits, consistent with the perfect retry results.

For **retry miss data** (Figure 8, "retry_miss" rows):

- **Retry rate = 0.5 on op=23 pq**: Achieves 87.8% (Figure 8)—better than error-free (77.7%) but substantially worse than retry weak (94.3%) and perfect retry (93.6%).
- **Retry rate = 0.5 on op=32 hardpq**: Achieves 93.4–93.5%—better than error-free (84.0%) but worse than retry weak (95.4–96.5%).
- **At retry rate = 0.2**: Retry miss achieves 89.1% on op=23 pq (vs. 77.7% error-free, 90.6% perfect retry, 93.5% retry weak with mask). On op=32 hardpq, retry miss achieves ~89.5% (vs. 84.0% error-free, ~97.1% perfect retry, ~93.2% retry weak).

Retry miss provides measurable but modest improvements over error-free data, nowhere near the gains of retry weak or perfect retry. The paper's interpretation is that retry miss includes parameters that are genuinely never needed (irrelevant to the queried answer), which provides a less useful learning signal than retry weak's focus on future needed steps. Retry weak teaches "don't skip ahead to future steps you're not ready for," which is the core skill; retry miss adds noise by also suggesting the model shouldn't compute completely irrelevant parameters—but that's a weaker and less targeted lesson.

**A surprising finding about retry weak at low retry rates**: At retry rate = 0.05, retry weak performs *worse* than the error-free baseline on some conditions (e.g., Figure 8, iGSM-medqp op=22: retry weak 0.05 achieves 78.0% vs. error-free 84.5%). This dip at very low retry rates does not appear in the perfect retry data. The paper does not explain this phenomenon, but it suggests that when the retry signal is too sparse in retry weak data (which is noisier than perfect retry data), the model may not learn the correction pattern reliably, while the errors still consume tokens that could have been used for additional correct problems. At higher retry rates (0.2+), this effect disappears and substantial gains emerge.

**Behavioral statistics for fake retry data (Figure 11)**: Models pretrained on retry weak data tend to retry more at inference time than those pretrained on perfect retry data. At retry rate = 0.5 on iGSM-hard op=32, retry weak models average ~5.5 retries per correct solution (Figure 11(a)), compared to ~1–3 retries for perfect retry data (Figure 5(a)). This makes intuitive sense: retry weak's "errors" are sometimes not actual errors, so the model may learn a slightly less precise threshold for when to retry, resulting in more false-positive retries. However, despite this higher retry rate, retry weak models still achieve similar accuracy (Figure 8), and the unnecessary parameter counts remain comparable to perfect retry (Figure 11(b), similar to Figure 6).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Label masking on erroneous tokens (Figures 4(b), 5, 7, 8, 10)**: The paper runs masked and unmasked variants for nearly every experimental configuration. For pretraining from scratch (perfect retry data), masking provides negligible benefit—the unmasked models perform as well or better at most retry rates, and the behavioral statistics show unmasked models rarely produce errors at inference time. For LoRA fine-tuning, masking becomes important at low ranks, where unmasked models suffer significant degradation (Figure 7: qv4e8 masked achieves 84.0% op=20 vs. 91.2% unmasked; but masked is worse on op=23: 71.8% vs. 74.3%). This interaction between masking and model capacity is a non-obvious finding: when the model has limited trainable parameters, the error signal (learning to predict erroneous tokens) competes with the correction signal, and masking helps isolate the latter. When capacity is abundant (full pretraining), this competition is not a bottleneck.

- **Retry rate sweep (0.01 to 0.5, Figures 4(b) and 8)**: The paper demonstrates that accuracy improves monotonically with retry rate up to 0.5. Retry rate = 0.01 (not shown in the main figures but mentioned in the text) produces negligible gains, establishing that a non-trivial fraction of steps must contain errors for the model to learn the correction pattern. The diminishing returns at high retry rates (0.4–0.5) suggest that the optimal rate may be in the 0.2–0.5 range. The paper does not test rates above 0.5, noting that exploring "extreme failure settings" is "not particularly interesting."

- **Probe version comparison for "retry upon regret" (Figure 3(b))**: Version1 (rank-8 embedding update, large training set) and version2 (rank-32 embedding + query/value updates, 0.25% of training data) achieve comparable accuracy as error detectors and yield similar final accuracies when used for "retry upon regret." This establishes that error detection is not only accurate but *sample-efficient*—it can be learned from a tiny fraction of the data used for pretraining. Yet despite this efficiency, the downstream benefit for reasoning accuracy remains marginal, reinforcing that detection ≠ correction.

- **Maximum retries in "retry upon regret" (Figure 3(b), versionP vs. versionP50)**: Increasing the maximum retries from 10 to 50 (with perfect ground-truth error detection) further improves accuracy, indicating that more regeneration attempts can compensate for the model's inability to deterministically correct errors. However, this comes at proportional inference cost (up to 50× more tokens generated per problematic step), making it impractical.

- **Pretrain double-time vs. continued pretraining (Figure 10, bottom rows for each retry rate)**: This ablation tests whether combining error-free and retry data (through sequential training) provides benefits beyond simply training on more retry data tokens. For retry rate = 0.2 on iGSM-med, pretrain double-time (2T tokens of retry data) achieves 98.4% average across all conditions, while continued pretraining (T tokens error-free + T tokens retry) achieves 96.9%—the pretrain double-time is actually slightly better. The paper concludes that "such full finetuning is no better than directly pretraining with 2T tokens from retry data." There is no synergistic benefit from error-free pretraining before retry fine-tuning; what matters is the total quantity of retry data seen.

- **Beam size for evaluation (all figures, best of beam=1/4 reported)**: The paper reports the maximum accuracy over beam=1 (greedy) and beam=4 (multinomial beam search). The fact that beam=4 sometimes outperforms beam=1 indicates that the model's error correction is not perfectly deterministic—there is some benefit to sampling. However, the beam=1 results alone already show large improvements over error-free baselines, confirming that the correction skill is primarily deterministic (the model generates `[BACK]` and corrects itself without needing to explore multiple continuations).

- **Two random seeds (all figures)**: Every model configuration is trained with two different random seeds. The paper reports the best accuracy from the two seeds. While the individual seed values are not shown, the consistency of the trends across so many configurations (multiple retry rates, multiple dataset variants, multiple difficulty levels) makes it unlikely that the results are seed artifacts. However, the lack of reported variance means the reader cannot assess the stability of individual data points.

- **Architecture robustness (mentioned in Appendix D, not shown)**: The paper states it "also played with the Llama architecture (especially with gated MLP layers) and did not see any benefit of using it." This is a brief but important check: the results are not specific to the GPT2 architecture. However, no systematic comparison or quantitative results are provided, so the claim of architecture-independence is weakly supported.

- **Context length for pretraining vs. evaluation (Appendix D)**: The paper uses context length 768 for iGSM-med pretraining, 1024 for iGSM-hard pretraining, and 2048 for all evaluation. This tests length generalization—the model must handle solutions longer than any seen during training. The retry data models maintain their accuracy advantage at these extended lengths, showing that the correction skill generalizes across sequence lengths.

- **Data discarding at evaluation (Appendix D)**: For evaluation, problems with error-free ground-truth solutions longer than 768 (iGSM-med) or 1024 (iGSM-hard) tokens are discarded. Critically, this filtering is based on the error-free solution length, not the retry-augmented length, ensuring that all models are evaluated on the same test distribution. The paper explicitly notes: "Adding errors lengthens the solutions, and discarding such data would bias the test distribution towards simpler problems."

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "Models pretrained on retry data outperform those trained on the same amount of error-free data."** — This claim is **well-supported** by the comprehensive evidence in Figure 4(b). The improvements are consistent across four dataset variants, six retry rates, two label masking conditions, multiple difficulty levels, and both in-distribution and OOD settings. The magnitude is substantial: +16 percentage points on the hardest iGSM-med problems, +14 percentage points on iGSM-hard. However, the claim is demonstrated only for the specific synthetic iGSM dataset with GPT2-12-12. The paper does not test on real math data (GSM8K, MATH), larger models, or different reasoning domains. The "same amount of error-free data" comparison controls for token count but not for the *diversity* of problems seen: retry-trained models see fewer unique problems (because retry solutions are longer) but achieve higher accuracy. This raises the possibility that error-free training could be improved by increasing problem diversity rather than by introducing retry patterns—a comparison the paper does not explore.

**Claim 2: "Higher retry rates produce larger accuracy gains, and masking mistakes is unnecessary."** — **Supported with qualifications.** The monotonic trend in Figure 4(b) is clear for retry rates from 0.05 to 0.5. However, the paper tests only up to retry rate = 0.5, and the gains are already diminishing (0.4 to 0.5 on iGSM-hard op=32 shows +0.6% at best). The optimal retry rate may depend on model size, dataset complexity, and problem difficulty—none of which are explored. The "masking unnecessary" claim holds for pretraining from scratch but fails for LoRA fine-tuning (Figure 7, Figure 10), where low-rank configurations without masking perform substantially worse. The paper's conclusion that "masking mistakes is unnecessary" applies specifically to the pretraining-from-scratch setting with sufficient model capacity, not universally.

**Claim 3: "Error correction is a fundamentally different skill from error-free reasoning and cannot be acquired through LoRA fine-tuning."** — **Supported with impressive thoroughness.** The LoRA sweep in Figures 7 and 10 covers 7 rank configurations per retry rate, 4 retry rates, and both masking conditions—roughly 56 LoRA fine-tuning experiments per dataset variant. The consistent failure of low-rank LoRA (accuracy degradation) and the at-best-marginal improvement of high-rank LoRA (approaching but rarely exceeding the original) provide strong evidence. The contrast with full fine-tuning / continued pretraining (which succeeds) confirms that the issue is not the retry data itself but the parameter efficiency of the adaptation method. However, the paper does not test whether the failure is specific to LoRA or generalizes to other PEFT methods (prompt tuning, adapters, IA3). It also does not test whether a model fine-tuned on error-free data first (to reach the baseline) and then on retry data would benefit—all LoRA experiments fine-tune directly from the error-free pretrained checkpoint on retry data.

**Claim 4: "Retry weak data, requiring zero semantic understanding, achieves nearly the same improvements as perfect retry data."** — **Supported, with an interesting caveat.** Figure 8 shows retry weak at retry rate = 0.5 achieves 94–96% on the hardest problems, compared to 94–98% for perfect retry (Figure 4(b)). The gap is small (0–4 percentage points) and sometimes favors retry weak. The caveat is the performance dip at low retry rates (retry weak at 0.05 underperforms error-free on some conditions), which is not explained and could make retry weak risky if the retry rate is not tuned properly. The paper's recommendation to use retry weak is practical but would benefit from understanding why low retry rates fail for noisy retry data but not for perfect retry data.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **Single model size, single architecture family.** All experiments use GPT2-12-12 (~85M parameters). The paper does not test whether the benefits of retry data scale with model size, or whether larger models might learn error correction more readily from error-free data alone (making retry data unnecessary). The claim that these findings "predict the needs of future LLMs" (Section 7) is speculative without scaling experiments.

- **No comparison against data augmentation with additional error-free data.** The retry data uses more tokens per problem. An alternative baseline would be: given T tokens of error-free data plus the extra tokens that retry data would have consumed, train on T + extra tokens of error-free data (more diverse problems). This would isolate whether the benefit comes from the retry patterns specifically or simply from having more training signal (the retry tokens provide additional examples of parameter names and computation patterns, even if they're in "error" contexts). The paper's "pretrain double-time" comparison uses 2T tokens of retry data vs. continued pretraining, but never compares against 2T tokens of error-free data.

- **Best-case reporting masks variance.** All accuracy tables report the maximum over 2 seeds × 2 beam configurations. This inflates the reported numbers relative to expected performance and makes it impossible to assess whether differences between configurations are statistically meaningful. A 1–2 percentage point difference between retry rates or masking conditions could be noise.

- **Evaluation on 4096 problems per cell creates a data leakage concern.** The paper generates 4096 *fresh* problems on-the-fly for each evaluation. Since the data generator uses templates, there may be structural overlap between training and evaluation problems, even if the specific parameter values differ. The "reask" variants partially address this by changing the queried parameter, but do not eliminate the fact that training and test problems come from the same template distribution. True OOD evaluation on a different problem structure (e.g., training on iGSM-med and testing on iGSM-hard, rather than using each dataset's own OOD split) would be more convincing.

- **No baseline using an external verifier or ORM.** The paper compares against beam search and "retry upon regret" (which uses a learned probe), but does not compare against a separately trained outcome reward model (ORM) or process reward model (PRM) that scores and selects among candidate solutions. Given that verifier-based approaches are a dominant paradigm in math reasoning (Cobbe et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2023), the absence of this baseline is notable. It's possible that a strong verifier applied to error-free pretrained model outputs could match or exceed the retry pretraining results at the same total compute budget (training + inference).

- **The "retry upon regret" baseline uses the same probe that demonstrates "regret."** There is potential circularity: the probe was trained to detect errors on the same type of data the model was evaluated on, and the 99% detection accuracy is measured on the same distribution. The 60% detection rate on actual model mistakes is more relevant for the "retry upon regret" application, and this lower accuracy may partly explain the limited gains. The paper does not explore whether improving probe accuracy on mistake examples specifically (e.g., by targeted training) would close the gap with retry pretraining.

- **Retry data is entirely synthetic and deterministic.** The errors in retry data are programmatically inserted with uniform random selection, not generated by the model itself. This means the error distribution in training (random selection of uncomputable parameters) may not match the error distribution the model actually produces at inference time (which might be biased toward certain types of parameters or certain positions in the solution). The paper does not analyze whether the model's self-generated errors at inference time resemble the training errors. If they are substantially different, the model might be learning a correction skill that doesn't transfer to its own error patterns—though the strong accuracy results suggest this is not a major issue.

- **The "regret" interpretation is somewhat anthropomorphic.** The paper frames the probe's 60% accuracy on mistake positions as evidence that the model "regrets" its errors. But a 60% detection rate, while above chance (50%), is far from reliable—the model "knows" it made a mistake only slightly more often than not. The jump from 60% probing accuracy to the claim that the model "exhibits regretful behavior" is a strong interpretation of a weak signal. The more conservative reading is that the model's representations contain some information about step validity, which a trained classifier can partially extract, but the model's own generation process does not use this information effectively.

**Experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **Scaling model size**: Does retry data benefit larger models more, less, or equally? If larger models learn error correction more readily from error-free data, retry data might be unnecessary at scale—undermining the paper's practical recommendations.

- **Full fine-tuning with varying amounts of retry data**: The continued pretraining experiments use T tokens of retry data. How little retry data is needed for full fine-tuning to work? This would establish whether the difficulty with LoRA is about data quantity or about representational capacity.

- **Comparison against data augmentation**: Generate extra error-free training data equal in tokens to the retry data inflation, and compare. This would distinguish "more data" from "retry patterns specifically."

- **Breaking down the contributions of [BACK] token, error exposure, and correction structure**: What if the model sees only correct solutions with occasional `[BACK] [same correct param]` (no actual errors)? Or what if the model sees errors without [BACK], just concatenated? These ablations would identify which components of the retry data format are necessary.

- **Analysis of model internals**: The paper mentions that "when a mistake is made, the model needs to revise its internal computations to find alternative solutions" (Section 5) but does not analyze these computations. Probing the retry-trained model's internal states (similar to the can_next probing on error-free models) could reveal how the correction mechanism is implemented—does the model pre-compute alternative next steps? Does it use the [BACK] token to trigger a different attention pattern?

**Conditional nature of the claims:** The paper's claims hold for the specific setting tested: small GPT2 models (85M parameters), synthetic grade-school math problems with known dependency structures, and programmatically-inserted step-skipping errors. The claims do not necessarily extend to: (1) larger models where error correction might emerge from scale alone, (2) real math problems where errors are more diverse (arithmetic mistakes, misinterpretation, logical fallacies beyond step-skipping), (3) domains without clear step-by-step solution structures, (4) settings where retry data cannot be reliably constructed (no ground-truth dependency graph). The paper is explicit about these limitations in Section 7: "We do not claim that the synthetic data used here can directly aid in building future LLMs." The value is in establishing guiding principles, not in providing a drop-in solution for current systems.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Single model scale and architecture — no evidence that retry data benefits larger models

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use a single model: GPT2-12-12, a 12-layer, 12-head transformer with 768-dimensional hidden states — approximately 85 million parameters. The paper does not test any larger models, nor does it test any architecture other than GPT2 (the paper notes it "played with the Llama architecture... and did not see any benefit of using it," but provides no quantitative results for this). The paper explicitly frames its contribution as predictive: "the goal of this paper is to try to predict the needs of future LLMs" (Section 7). Yet the entire empirical foundation rests on a model three orders of magnitude smaller than frontier LLMs.

**The consequence.** The central question the paper raises — whether retry data should be included in pretraining corpora for large-scale LLMs — cannot be answered from the evidence presented. There are at least two ways scale could undermine the findings:

First, larger models may simply make fewer step-skipping errors on these reasoning tasks, reducing or eliminating the need for error correction. The paper itself notes that error-free pretrained models already achieve 99-100% accuracy on in-distribution problems (op ≤ 15 for iGSM-med, op ≤ 21 for iGSM-hard) — the benefits of retry data only appear on harder OOD problems. If scaling model size pushes the "in-distribution" frontier outward, the region where retry data helps might shrink or vanish.

Second, and more fundamentally, larger models might acquire error correction capabilities from error-free data alone through emergent meta-cognitive abilities. The paper's core claim is that error correction is "a skill very different from the original (error-free) reasoning" (Result 7, Section 5). But this claim is established only at one model scale. If sufficiently large models learn to plan alternative solution paths or detect and recover from errors without explicit retry data — as they appear to acquire other emergent capabilities — then the recommendation to include retry data in pretraining becomes unnecessary overhead.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The negative evidence is the absence of scaling experiments. The paper provides no results for GPT2-medium (345M), GPT2-large (774M), or any other larger configuration. The model size is stated only in Appendix D; the main text treats GPT2-12-12 as a universal proxy for "language models" without qualification.

The paper does provide one weak piece of suggestive evidence: the architecture check. The statement "we also played with the Llama architecture (especially with gated MLP layers) and did not see any benefit of using it" (Appendix D) implies that the results are not specific to the GPT2 architecture. However, this does not address the scale question — a same-sized Llama variant is still an ~85M parameter model.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not frame the lack of scaling experiments as a limitation, propose scaling studies as future work, or qualify its recommendations with caveats about model size. The conclusion advocates for adding retry data "to the pretrain data for language models to truly learn the capability to correct errors" (Section 7), stated as a general principle without scale qualification.

### 6.2 The cost of constructing retry data is not accounted for — perfect retry data requires ground-truth dependency graphs

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline results (Section 4, Figure 4(b)) rely on **perfect retry data**: programmatically generated errors where the inserted incorrect parameter is guaranteed to be uncomputable at the current step. Constructing this data requires **complete knowledge of the solution's dependency graph** — for each step in each training problem, the data generator must determine which parameters have all dependencies satisfied and which do not, then sample uniformly from the latter set. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by devoting Section 6 to cheaper alternatives ("obtaining math data with mistakes and corrections can be challenging in real life"), but the entire experimental methodology in Sections 3-5 uses perfect retry data exclusively.

The paper also does not account for the **computational cost of generating retry data**. While the iGSM data generator is programmatic and deterministic, constructing retry data requires running the dependency analysis on every generated problem. The paper treats this as free — "for both pretraining and finetuning, we did not limit the amount of training data; we generated new data on-the-fly" (Appendix D) — but the compute spent on data generation is not included in any cost model. This contrasts with the paper's careful accounting of training tokens (matching token counts between retry and error-free conditions), which only captures the downstream training cost.

**The consequence.** The perfect retry data results set an upper bound on what retry data can achieve, but this bound is **unrealizable in practice for real math problems**. Real math solutions do not come with formal dependency graphs. Determining whether a parameter can be computed at a given step requires semantic understanding of the mathematical dependencies — exactly the reasoning capability the model is being trained to acquire. This creates a chicken-and-egg problem: to train a model to correct reasoning errors, one needs data containing reasoning errors with correct corrections; to generate those corrections reliably, one needs a model that can reason correctly. The paper's use of programmatic data generation sidesteps this entirely, but the sidestep is only possible because of the synthetic setting.

The retry weak method (Section 6) partially addresses this by showing that random future-step insertion works nearly as well. But retry weak still requires **step-by-step solutions with identifiable steps** — a format not universally available in real math corpora. Many math solutions in web text are narrative, abbreviated, or structured differently. The paper does not test retry weak on solutions where steps are not clearly delineated.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 6 is the paper's primary acknowledgment of this limitation: "While it is possible to prepare 'perfect' retry data on synthetic iGSM datasets, obtaining math data with mistakes and corrections can be challenging in real life. For this reason, we explore the possibility of using more realistic approaches to augment math problems." The retry weak results (Figure 8) demonstrate that the dependency graph requirement can be relaxed, but the retry weak method still assumes: (a) solutions are structured as sequences of discrete steps, (b) each step defines exactly one parameter, and (c) future steps can be identified by simple positional indexing. None of these assumptions hold for arbitrary math text.

Figure 8 also reveals that retry weak at very low retry rates (0.05) can underperform error-free data (e.g., iGSM-medqp op=22: retry weak 0.05 achieves 78.0% vs. 84.5% for error-free). The paper does not explain this dip, but it implies that retry weak is not a free lunch — the retry rate must be tuned, and getting it wrong can degrade performance. This tuning requirement is an additional practical cost the paper does not discuss.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed by Section 6, but the mitigation itself introduces new constraints. The paper acknowledges the need for future work: "This can be done either through naively creating retry weak data like ours or using more advanced prompting methods to encourage an auxiliary model to rewrite math data into such a format. We cannot explore such follow-up directions due to GPU resource limitations" (Section 7). The paper explicitly defers the question of how to construct retry-like data for real math problems to future work.

### 6.3 The "retry upon regret" baseline uses a probe trained on the same distribution — the 99% detection accuracy may not hold in genuinely novel settings

**The assumption or constraint.** The can_next probing classifiers (version1 and version2) that enable the "retry upon regret" baseline are trained and evaluated on the **same distribution** of iGSM problems. Version1 uses 50% of pretraining tokens for fine-tuning (25% for iGSM-hard); version2 uses only 0.25%, but still draws training data from the identical distribution. The 99% error detection accuracy reported for these probes is measured on held-out problems from the **same problem templates**, not on OOD problems where the model's error patterns might differ.

This matters because the "retry upon regret" results serve as the paper's primary evidence that error detection is easy while error correction is hard — the asymmetry that motivates the entire retry data investigation. If the probes' 99% accuracy is inflated by distributional overlap between training and evaluation, the asymmetry may be less stark than the paper claims.

**The consequence.** If the probes are overfit to the training distribution (even mildly), then the practical error detection accuracy during "retry upon regret" inference on OOD problems is lower than 99%. The paper already shows that detection accuracy is the bottleneck: version1/2 probes (99% accuracy) yield only ~2% accuracy improvement over beam search, while versionP (100% ground-truth detection) yields ~8-10% improvement (Figure 3(b)). If real detection accuracy on OOD problems is, say, 95% rather than 99%, the "retry upon regret" gains would be even smaller or potentially negative (false positives trigger unnecessary regeneration, wasting compute and potentially introducing errors).

This limitation does not undermine the paper's main finding (retry data in pretraining works), but it weakens the supporting argument that "error detection is easy." The paper uses this argument to motivate why retry data is necessary — "if detection is already learned, why can't the model correct errors?" If detection is actually harder than the paper suggests, the argument's rhetorical force is reduced, though its logical structure (detection ≠ correction) remains valid.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report probe accuracy specifically on OOD problems. The 99% figure is cited from Ye et al. (2024), where it was measured across all problems in the iGSM test sets. The paper's own probing results (Section 3.1) are described qualitatively: "When A ranges over all possible parameters, the probing 99% accurately predicts can_next(A), meaning the model knows if A can be computed next, even for the hardest op = 32 problems." The phrase "all possible parameters" suggests this is measured across the full test distribution, including OOD problems. However, the probe was trained on in-distribution data; the paper does not report whether the probe was trained on iGSM-med and tested on iGSM-hard (cross-dataset generalization) or only within each dataset.

Additionally, the 60% detection rate on actual model mistakes (Section 3.1) is a more relevant metric for "retry upon regret" deployment: when the model actually makes an error (not just when the probe is tested on random positions), the probe detects it only 60% of the time. A 40% false negative rate on errors is a significant practical limitation — nearly half of all model mistakes go undetected and therefore uncorrected. The paper acknowledges this indirectly (the 60% figure is reported) but does not compute the downstream impact: what final accuracy could "retry upon regret" achieve if the probe's mistake-detection rate were higher?

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the sensitivity to probe accuracy as a core finding: "The success of 'retry upon regret' largely depends on an extremely accurate error detector — increasing the error detection success rate from 99% to 100% can significantly improve the final reasoning accuracy" (Section 3.2). This is framed as evidence for the paper's thesis (retry data is necessary) rather than as a limitation of the experimental setup. The paper does not discuss what probe accuracy would be achievable in practice for real-world math problems where the error types are more diverse and less well-defined.

### 6.4 Retry data is evaluated only on synthetic grade-school math — no evidence on real math benchmarks, code, or other reasoning domains

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses the iGSM dataset, which is designed to isolate "logic-following reasoning" by removing arithmetic difficulty (modulo 23 arithmetic), common-sense knowledge, and natural language variation. The problems have a rigid, templated structure: a list of parameter relationships in a specific format, a question asking for one parameter's value, and a step-by-step solution where each step defines exactly one parameter. The only type of error studied is **step-skipping**: attempting to compute a parameter before its dependencies are known.

The paper acknowledges this scope limitation: "While grade-school level math problems have many other difficulties (including arithmetic or common sense), following [29], we have focused on the (logic-following) reasoning aspect, which is one of the weakest aspects of GPT-4" (Section 7). It also notes that "it is unlikely that iGSM retry data will be directly used for pretraining future commercial-level LLMs" and frames the work as finding "guiding principles for necessary ingredients" (Section 7).

**The consequence.** The paper's findings may not transfer to real math reasoning in at least three ways:

First, **error diversity**. Real math errors include arithmetic mistakes (e.g., 17 + 13 = 30, off by one), unit confusion, misinterpretation of problem statements, applying the wrong formula, algebraic manipulation errors, and logical leaps that aren't cleanly categorizable as "step-skipping." The retry data format (wrong parameter → [BACK] → correct parameter) may not be appropriate for these other error types. An arithmetic error, for instance, doesn't involve the wrong parameter — it involves the wrong computation on the right parameters. The paper provides no evidence on whether retry data helps with non-step-skipping errors.

Second, **solution structure**. iGSM solutions have a uniform format: each step defines one parameter via a simple computation from previously defined parameters. Real math solutions vary widely in structure: some are narrative, some use equations with minimal text, some group multiple computations into a single step. The retry weak method (Section 6) relies on the ability to identify "future steps" to insert as fake errors; in unstructured solutions, there are no clear step boundaries.

Third, **domain specificity**. Even if retry data works for math reasoning, the paper provides no evidence on whether it transfers to other reasoning domains where step-skipping errors occur: code generation (calling a function before defining it), logical deduction (using a conclusion before establishing its premises), multi-step planning, or scientific reasoning. The paper's recommendations for LLM pretraining are stated generally ("we advocate for adding mistakes and corrections at the pretrain level," Section 7), but the evidence covers only one narrow reasoning domain.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The negative evidence is the complete absence of experiments on non-iGSM data. The paper does not test on GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, or any other benchmark. It does not even test on the arithmetic or common-sense variants of iGSM that Ye et al. (2024) described — those difficulties were explicitly removed. The paper's only connection to real LLM behavior is a single qualitative example (Figure 1) showing GPT-4o making a step-skipping error on a box-weighting problem, which the paper uses to argue that "the failure example in Figure 1 is also in this spirit" (Section 3). This anecdote illustrates that step-skipping errors occur in real LLMs, but does not demonstrate that retry data would help with them — GPT-4o corrects the error through multi-round prompting, not through a pretraining data intervention.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation in its conclusion: "We do not claim that the synthetic data used here can directly aid in building future LLMs" (Section 7). It frames the work as producing guiding principles rather than deployable solutions. However, the paper's strong prescriptive statements — "we strongly discourage using fine-tuning to teach a model to correct errors (or using beam search, or letting the model regenerate upon encountering a mistake) as these are not effective. We advocate for adding mistakes and corrections at the pretrain level" (Section 7) — are stated without qualification about domain specificity. A practitioner reading these recommendations might reasonably assume they apply to math reasoning broadly, but the evidence does not support that breadth.

The paper suggests future work: "using more advanced prompting methods to encourage an auxiliary model to rewrite math data into such a format. We cannot explore such follow-up directions due to GPU resource limitations." This acknowledges that the retry data format must be adapted for real data, but does not address whether the underlying learning dynamics would transfer once adapted.

### 6.5 The retry data evaluation uses best-case reporting (max over seeds and beam sizes) without statistical quantification

**The assumption or constraint.** Every accuracy number in Figures 3(b), 4(b), 7, 8, and 10 is reported as the **maximum** over 2 pretraining seeds × 2 beam configurations (beam=1 greedy, beam=4 multinomial). The paper states this explicitly in Appendix D.1: "we pretrain using two different random seeds, and evaluate with both beam=1/4; we then present the best accuracies in each cell with respect to the 2 seeds and 2 beam choices." No error bars, standard deviations, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests are reported anywhere in the paper.

The paper also evaluates on exactly **4096 problems per test condition** per cell. For the hardest OOD conditions (e.g., op=23 for iGSM-med, op=32 for iGSM-hard), differences between configurations are sometimes as small as 1-2 percentage points — corresponding to 40-80 problems out of 4096. The paper does not report whether differences of this magnitude are statistically significant given the two-seed variance.

**The consequence.** Several of the paper's finer-grained claims rest on small numerical differences that may not be reliable:

- The claim that "masking mistakes is unnecessary" (Result 2-3, Section 4) relies on masked and unmasked accuracies being comparable. In Figure 4(b), for iGSM-medpq op=23 at retry rate = 0.5, the unmasked variant achieves 93.6% while the masked variant achieves 95.2% — a 1.6 percentage point difference favoring masking. At retry rate = 0.4, unmasked achieves 91.5% and masked achieves 94.6% — a 3.1 percentage point difference. These gaps are large enough to matter practically, but the paper dismisses them as within the range of "no systematic difference." Without variance estimates, the reader cannot assess whether the 1.6 and 3.1 point gaps are noise or signal.

- The claim that retry weak at retry rate = 0.5 achieves "nearly the same accuracy improvements as perfect retry data" (Result 8, Section 6) depends on comparing Figure 8 against Figure 4(b). On iGSM-medqp op=23, retry weak (unmasked) achieves 92.4% while perfect retry (unmasked) achieves 94.5% — a 2.1 point gap. On iGSM-hardqp op=32, retry weak (unmasked, retry rate = 0.5) achieves ~95% vs. perfect retry's ~98% — a ~3 point gap. Whether these gaps are "nearly the same" is a judgment call that variance estimates would inform.

- The LoRA fine-tuning results (Figure 7) show an apparent non-monotonic relationship with rank: accuracy sometimes drops from rank-8 to rank-16, then recovers at rank-32. Without variance estimates, it's impossible to tell whether these fluctuations are real (indicating an interesting interaction between rank and learning dynamics) or noise from the two-seed sampling.

More broadly, the best-case reporting strategy inflates all reported numbers relative to expected performance. A practitioner deploying one of these models would see the *average* accuracy over random seeds, not the best of two. The paper's 94% on iGSM-med op=23 with retry pretraining might correspond to, say, 91-93% expected accuracy, which is still a large improvement over the 78% baseline but narrower than the headline numbers suggest.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's description of the evaluation protocol (Appendix D.1) is transparent about the max-over-seeds-and-beams procedure. The reader can see that this is the methodology. However, the paper provides no raw data, no per-seed breakdown, and no discussion of variance. The statement "since we care about the (relatively small) accuracy differences across models, we pretrain using two different random seeds" (Appendix D.1) acknowledges that the differences of interest are small, making the lack of variance quantification more consequential, not less.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss this as a limitation, provide variance estimates, or justify why max-over-seeds is an appropriate reporting strategy. The standard in the field varies: many papers report mean and standard deviation; some report max (arguing it shows what the method can achieve under optimal conditions). The paper does not engage with this methodological question.

The paper does not report a 38% correct-to-incorrect reversion rate — this figure is not present in any of the sections, figures, or tables of the provided paper content. ### 6.6 Important ablation missing: Does seeing errors WITHOUT corrections help, or is the [BACK] + correction structure essential?

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's retry data format combines three elements: (1) an erroneous parameter name, (2) a special `[BACK]` token, and (3) the correct parameter name. The paper never ablates these components to determine which are necessary. Does the benefit come from exposure to incorrect parameters (teaching the model what NOT to compute)? From the backtracking mechanism (teaching the model it can undo errors)? From the correction itself (teaching the model the right answer)? Or from some interaction of all three?

The paper does test one related ablation: label masking (whether the loss should be computed on the erroneous tokens). But this only asks whether the model should be *optimized* to predict errors — it doesn't ask whether seeing errors at all is beneficial. An informative ablation would be: pretrain on data where errors are inserted but NO correction follows (just `[BACK]` with nothing after, or the error token without any `[BACK]`), and measure whether the model still improves. This would distinguish "learning from errors" from "learning the correction procedure."

**The consequence.** Without these ablations, the paper cannot explain *why* retry data works. The introduction frames two possible mechanisms: (1) the model learns to avoid errors because it sees error patterns and their negative consequences, or (2) the model learns an explicit correction procedure that it deploys when needed. The behavioral statistics (low retry counts on correct solutions, Figure 5(a)) suggest that the model primarily avoids errors rather than correcting them — it rarely uses `[BACK]` on problems it solves correctly. This favors mechanism (1). But the paper's framing throughout emphasizes the correction procedure (e.g., "the model learns to automatically retry after detecting an error," Section 4). The actual learning mechanism has practical implications: if the benefit is primarily avoidance, then simpler interventions (e.g., training on data where errors are present but flagged, without explicit corrections) might work. If the correction procedure is essential, then the full retry format is necessary, and the paper's recommendations for data preparation are justified.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no experiments that isolate these components. The closest evidence is indirect:

- The low retry counts on correct solutions (Figure 5(a)) suggest the model has learned to avoid errors, not just to correct them. At retry rate = 0.5 on iGSM-hard op=32, models average only ~2-4 retries on correct solutions — meaning they solve these hard problems with almost no backtracking. The accuracy improvement comes from not making errors in the first place, not from correcting them.

- The failure of LoRA fine-tuning (Section 5) and "retry upon regret" (Section 3) to teach correction suggests that simple exposure to the correction pattern (fine-tuning) or the correction mechanism (regeneration) is insufficient — the learning must happen during pretraining. But this doesn't distinguish whether pretraining teaches avoidance or teaches a deep correction capability.

- The retry weak results (Section 6) show that the "errors" don't need to be true errors — random future steps work. This suggests the model is learning something about the *structure* of the data (don't skip ahead) rather than learning from semantically meaningful errors. But again, the mechanism is unclear.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss component ablations or acknowledge the ambiguity about the learning mechanism. The conclusion states that "it is critical to teach a model not to skip steps" (Section 7), which leans toward the avoidance interpretation, but the paper does not design experiments to test this hypothesis.
