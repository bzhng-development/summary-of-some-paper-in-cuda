# Neuron-Activated Graph Ranking: Training-Free Target-Oriented Data Selection via Sparse, Interpretable Neural Activation Patterns in LLMs

**ArXiv:** [2604.15706](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15706)

## 🎯 Pitch

Selecting pretraining data based on which neurons fire inside a frozen LLM yields a 23.5% performance collapse when just 0.12% of those neurons are silenced—revealing a sparse functional backbone. This neuron‑activated graph (NAG) ranking, which requires absolutely no training, outperforms embedding‑based methods by 5.3% accuracy on HellaSwag and works across unrelated domains like economics text boosting math reasoning.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Neuron-Activated Graph Ranking (NAG-based Ranking)**, a training-free and interpretable framework for target-oriented pretraining data selection that characterizes each input by identifying which sparse, high-impact neurons in an off-the-shelf LLM fire during inference. Using SmolLM3, Qwen3, and Llama-3.2 as backbone models, the method constructs a compact Neuron-Activated Graph (NAG) — a layer-wise collection of top-K activated neurons per input — and ranks candidate pretraining data by NAG overlap similarity to a small set of target examples (e.g., selecting documents whose neuron-activation patterns match those of HellaSwag training samples). Across six benchmarks under single-target settings, NAG-based Ranking improves target-oriented pretraining by 4.9% on average over random sampling and outperforms the state-of-the-art target-oriented baseline BETR by 5.3% accuracy on HellaSwag, while maintaining gains under a multi-target setting where it surpasses two baselines by 1.1% and 4.1% respectively. Deactivating NAG-selected neurons — only 0.12% of all model parameters — causes a 23.5% performance collapse, establishing that NAG captures a sparse "functional backbone" whose discriminative power yields utility-aligned rankings only when aggregated across all layers rather than restricted to the final layer (which incurs a 4.1% average drop).

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Quality Data Selection Is Underspecified for Target-Oriented Pretraining

Everyone agrees that "high-quality" pretraining data matters enormously for model performance — works such as phi-1 (Gunasekar et al., 2023), FineWeb (Penedo et al., 2024), and DCLM (Li et al., 2025) have demonstrated that curating pretraining corpora yields models that punch far above their weight class in terms of parameter count. However, the paper identifies a fundamental ambiguity in the current data selection paradigm: **what "quality" means is almost never defined with respect to the specific downstream capabilities a model is intended to acquire**. As the paper states in Section 1:

> "what makes 'high-quality' data remains surprisingly under-defined... high-quality data should align with targeted scenarios that enable LLMs to acquire desired capabilities efficiently — education, medicine, or specific research domains — while ruling out other irrelevant factors that do not contribute to such capabilities."

This is not merely a semantic quibble. Consider a concrete example from the paper's motivating Figure 1: a user wants a model to excel at mathematical reasoning (finding maxima of functions like $f(x) = xe^{-x}$). General-purpose quality heuristics might select Wikipedia articles about calculus because they are well-written, factually accurate, and educationally valuable. But Wikipedia articles are *expository* — they explain definitions and provide solved examples. They do not train the model to *perform* the multi-step symbolic reasoning required to solve novel problems. The underlying capability gap — knowing definitions versus executing reasoning chains — means the selected data may be high-quality by generic metrics yet poorly aligned with the target task. The paper frames this as a fundamental misalignment between general quality heuristics and target-specific utility.

This gap has real economic and practical consequences. In production settings where models are deployed for specific domains (customer support for a particular product line, medical coding, legal document analysis), the pretraining corpus is typically a massive, heterogeneous mixture of web data. Without a principled way to select the subset that actually drives target-domain performance, practitioners waste compute on data that contributes nothing to the capabilities they need — or worse, that introduces irrelevant patterns that interfere with target-task learning.

### Why This Problem Matters: Compute Efficiency, Interpretability, and the Training-Inference Tradeoff

The significance of this problem manifests along three dimensions:

**1. Compute efficiency is bottlenecked by data quality, not just quantity.** The modern LLM training paradigm has largely converged on the insight that data *curation* is as important as data *volume*. The Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) tell us how to optimally allocate compute between model size and token count, but they implicitly assume a fixed data distribution. In practice, the choice of which tokens to include — independent of how many — can produce models that achieve equivalent performance with dramatically less compute. Recent work on compute multipliers (Betker, 2023; Amodei, 2025) explicitly frames data quality improvements as multiplicative factors on effective compute: better data selection yielding a 2× compute multiplier means achieving the same performance with half the training FLOPs. For organizations training models at scale, moving from "random web crawl" to "target-aligned subset" represents perhaps the most cost-effective lever available — it requires no architectural changes, no algorithmic innovation, and no additional hardware, only a better criterion for which data to include.

**2. Existing target-oriented methods suffer from an interpretability bottleneck.** The paper identifies a specific weakness in current approaches to target-aligned data selection (discussed in detail below): they typically *distill* internal model signals — embedding similarities, loss values, perplexity — into opaque auxiliary classifiers or scalar scores. This distillation step, while necessary for scalability (you cannot run full forward passes through a large LLM to score billions of candidate documents), throws away information about *why* certain data is deemed relevant. The result is a black-box pipeline: the selected data improves downstream performance, but practitioners cannot diagnose why, cannot refine the selection criteria in a principled way, and cannot verify that the selection mechanism is capturing genuine capability-relevance rather than exploiting superficial correlations (e.g., semantic or stylistic similarity that happens to covary with the target domain but does not causally contribute to learning the target skill).

**3. The paper argues for a shift from representation-level to neuron-level reasoning about data utility.** Most existing methods — both general-quality and target-oriented — operate at the level of dense vector representations: last-layer embeddings, loss values, or classifier outputs. These are holistic summaries that conflate many different aspects of how a model processes an input. The paper's central insight is that model behavior is governed by a *sparse* subset of parameters, with different tasks relying on largely disjoint neuronal regions (building on prior work by Panigrahi et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; 2025). If this is true, then measuring input similarity via dense embeddings misses the critical structural information about which specific computational pathways are activated. Two inputs might have similar final-layer embeddings (and thus be considered similar by embedding-based methods) while engaging fundamentally different internal circuits — or conversely, might have different embeddings while relying on overlapping sets of neurons. The paper positions neuron-level analysis as a way to cut through this entanglement and capture the actual *computational signature* of a target capability.

### Existing Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper organizes prior work into two streams and identifies specific failure modes in each.

#### General-Quality Data Selection: Agnostic to Downstream Targets

A large body of work focuses on selecting data that is generically "good" without reference to any particular task. The paper categorizes these approaches into three subtypes and identifies their limitations:

**Classifier-based quality filtering** trains supervised or weakly-supervised models to distinguish high-quality text from noise, as exemplified by FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al., 2024) and DCLM (Li et al., 2025). The core weakness the paper identifies is label dependence: these classifiers require curated annotations defining what constitutes quality, and those annotations inevitably embed the biases and assumptions of the curators. For example, FineWeb-Edu's educational quality classifier was trained to recognize text with pedagogical value — but "educational value" is a specific, culturally contingent notion that may not align with the reasoning patterns needed for a mathematical problem-solving target. The paper explicitly tests this in Table 1: FineWeb-Edu's selected data outperforms random sampling by 2.5% on average, showing general quality signals help, but it underperforms NAG-based Ranking on benchmarks like HellaSwag (55.3% vs. 60.6%), XStoryCloze (65.9% vs. 70.0%), and XWinograd (76.2% vs. 80.1%) — exactly the benchmarks where target-specific reasoning patterns diverge from generic educational content.

**Heuristic-based filtering** applies hand-crafted rules: perplexity thresholds, language identification, deduplication, URL-based filtering, and so on (Wenzek et al., 2019; Rae et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2022; Abbas et al., 2023). The paper argues these are "coarse proxies of quality that are agnostic to the specific capabilities a model is expected to acquire" — they remove obvious noise but provide no signal about whether the surviving data teaches the right skills. A perfectly grammatical, on-topic, non-duplicate article about cooking recipes passes all heuristic filters but contributes nothing to mathematical reasoning ability.

**Learned scalar quality scores** use model outputs or losses to estimate data utility (Sachdeva et al., 2024; Wettig et al., 2024). The paper's critique here is more subtle: these signals "reflect only shallow, final-layer behavior" rather than the rich internal computation that determines what the model actually learns from a training example. A data point that yields low training loss may be low-loss precisely because it is easy and the model already knows how to process it — such data provides little learning signal despite appearing "high quality" by loss-based metrics.

The common thread across all three subtypes is that **general-quality methods overlook the internal computational structure of the model itself**. They treat the model as a black box whose outputs or losses can be read off, but whose internal processing — which neurons fire, which circuits activate — is irrelevant to data selection. The paper argues this is a missed opportunity: if different capabilities are implemented by different neurons, then the pattern of neuron activations on an input is a direct signature of which capabilities that input exercises, and this signature is more informative than any external quality label.

#### Target-Oriented Data Selection: Interpreting Targets Through Opaque Proxies

A more recent line of work explicitly aligns pretraining data with downstream tasks. The paper positions its contribution relative to these methods by identifying a shared weakness: they all distill task-relevant signals into compressed, opaque representations — typically auxiliary classifiers — that obscure the underlying mechanism of alignment.

**BETR (Mizrahi et al., 2025)** is the most direct point of comparison and the paper's primary target-oriented baseline. BETR measures similarity between candidate pretraining documents and target examples using last-layer embeddings from a pretrained LLM, constructs pseudo-labels based on similarity rankings, and trains a lightweight classifier to score the entire candidate pool. The paper identifies two specific failure modes:

- **Last-layer embeddings conflate surface features with task-relevant structure.** Prior work (Lyu et al., 2023; Skean et al., 2025) has shown that final-layer representations often entangle semantic content with stylistic and surface-level features — two documents may have similar embeddings because they share vocabulary, register, or topic, even if they engage fundamentally different reasoning patterns. This is particularly problematic for reasoning-heavy targets: a document that *discusses* mathematical concepts may have high embedding similarity to math problems without actually containing the reasoning patterns that train mathematical ability.

- **The compression into a classifier discards diagnostically useful information.** Even if the original embedding space contained rich task-relevant signals (which the paper doubts, given the last-layer limitation), the distillation into a lightweight classifier necessarily loses information helpful for understanding, debugging, or refining the selection. The classifier outputs a single scalar score — a document either looks task-relevant or not — but provides no insight into *which aspects* of the task it is matching or *why* certain documents rank higher than others.

The paper's empirical results support this critique. In the single-target setting (Table 1), BETR achieves a 3.9% average improvement over random, but NAG-based Ranking achieves 4.9% — a 1% absolute gap, with particularly large deltas on ARC-C (+1.7% over BETR) and HellaSwag (+3.1% over BETR). In the multi-target setting, BETR actually *degrades* relative to random on some benchmarks (TriviaQA drops 4.0%, HellaSwag drops 2.3%), while NAG maintains positive gains across the board. This multi-target degradation is particularly revealing: it suggests BETR's embedding-based similarity is not robust to the presence of multiple, diverse targets, likely because last-layer embeddings for different tasks interfere with each other when mixed.

**Perplexity-correlation methods** (SHUM et al., 2025; Thrush et al., 2025) define proxy signals correlated with downstream performance — typically LM loss or perplexity on target data — and train classifiers to predict these signals for candidate documents. The paper's critique is two-fold: (1) the proxy signal itself is a scalar summary that discards structural information about how the model processes the input, and (2) loss/perplexity on target data conflates multiple factors (data difficulty, domain match, model familiarity) that may not correspond to learning utility — a document can yield high loss because it is genuinely hard (good for learning) or because it is out-of-distribution noise (bad for learning), and these cases are not distinguishable from the scalar loss value.

**DAIG (Miyoshi et al., 2025)** trains an auxiliary model directly on target data and uses its predictions to score source data. The paper groups this with the other proxy-based approaches, noting that it still relies on the output behavior of a model rather than on the internal computational signatures of how that model processes different inputs.

Across all these target-oriented methods, the paper identifies a common architectural pattern: **target alignment is inferred indirectly through a compression step** (embedding → classifier, loss → classifier, auxiliary model → scores) that transforms rich, high-dimensional model behavior into a low-dimensional signal optimized for scalability. The paper argues this compression introduces an interpretability bottleneck: the learned signals "are opaque and hard to diagnose or refine, thus constraining how effectively the signals can be leveraged for further performance gains."

### The Conceptual Gap: From Representation Similarity to Neuron-Level Computation

Underlying the paper's critique of both general-quality and target-oriented methods is a deeper conceptual argument about what it means for two inputs to be "similar" for the purpose of pretraining. The paper argues that similarity of *representations* (embeddings, losses) is not the same as similarity of *computation* — two inputs can produce similar final representations through different internal pathways, or produce different representations through overlapping pathways.

The paper grounds this argument in the emerging literature on **sparse functional localization in neural networks**. Prior work (Panigrahi et al., 2023) demonstrated that fine-tuned models rely on task-specific subsets of parameters — different downstream tasks engage largely disjoint regions of the model. Zhao et al. (2024, 2025) extended this to show that model behaviors (multilingual processing, safety mechanisms) are governed by highly sparse, identifiable neuron sets. The implication for data selection is direct: if task capabilities are implemented by specific neurons, then two inputs that engage the *same* neurons are exercising the *same* capabilities, regardless of whether their surface-level representations look similar. Conversely, inputs with similar representations may engage entirely different neuronal circuits and thus train entirely different capabilities.

This is the conceptual leap the paper makes. Rather than asking "does this candidate document look similar to my target examples?" (embedding similarity) or "does this candidate document have similar loss characteristics?" (perplexity correlation), it asks: **"does this candidate document activate the same neurons as my target examples?"** This reframes data selection from a representation-matching problem to a computational-signature-matching problem. The hypothesis is that neuron-activation overlap is a more direct, more causally grounded measure of whether training on a candidate will improve the specific circuitry required for the target task.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions NAG-based Ranking as a resolution to the tension between existing approaches:

- **Against general-quality methods:** NAG is explicitly target-oriented — it selects data based on alignment with a specific downstream capability rather than generic "quality" heuristics. The paper demonstrates (Table 1) that this target orientation matters empirically: NAG outperforms FineWeb-Edu by 2.4% on average despite FineWeb-Edu's sophisticated quality classifier, with the largest gaps on benchmarks where general quality and target relevance diverge.

- **Against target-oriented methods:** NAG is interpretable and training-free — it uses raw neuron activations from off-the-shelf LLMs rather than compressing model behavior through auxiliary classifiers. This eliminates both the training cost (no classifier to train per target) and the interpretability bottleneck (the selection criterion is directly inspectable: which neurons are shared between candidates and targets). The paper's deactivation experiments (Section 4.1.1) provide causal evidence that NAG-selected neurons are genuinely critical for task performance — deactivating just 0.12% of neurons selected by NAG causes a 23.5% accuracy collapse, whereas deactivating random neurons has negligible effect. This is a type of validation that black-box classifier-based methods cannot provide.

- **Against embedding-based similarity (BETR specifically):** NAG operates on neuron-level overlap rather than vector-space proximity, and aggregates signals across all layers rather than relying on the final layer. The paper shows (Section 4.2.2) that restricting NAG to the final layer causes a 4.1% average performance drop, providing direct evidence that task-relevant signals are distributed throughout the model's depth and that last-layer-only methods (like BETR's embedding similarity) miss critical information. The layer-wise analysis (Figure 5) further reveals that different neuron types (UP projections vs. DOWN projections vs. attention projections) carry different amounts of task-relevant signal, with FFN UP projections being most effective — a level of granularity that embedding-based methods cannot access.

- **As a framework rather than a single method:** The paper emphasizes that NAG-based Ranking is a general approach that works with any off-the-shelf LLM as the extraction backbone. Table 1 shows consistent gains across three different model families (Qwen3-1.7B, Llama-3.2-3B, SmolLM3-3B) with variation of only 0.3% in average performance, suggesting the method captures universal properties of Transformer computation rather than model-specific quirks. The paper also demonstrates (Table 2) that NAG signals can be combined with existing quality-based methods (FineWeb-Edu) to achieve additive gains, positioning NAG as a plug-in component for existing data pipelines rather than a replacement for them.

The paper's ultimate ambition, articulated in the conclusion, is to "encourage further exploration of neuron-level interpretability for data selection, and more broadly, for understanding and steering the capabilities learned during large-scale pretraining." This positions neuron-level data selection not just as a practical tool for improving pretraining efficiency, but as a window into the mechanistic basis of capability acquisition — a bridge between the practical problem of data curation and the scientific problem of understanding how neural networks implement learned skills.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **training-free data selection pipeline** that takes a small set of target examples (e.g., 1,000 math problems), a large pool of unlabeled pretraining documents, and an off-the-shelf language model, and outputs a ranked subset of documents whose neuron-activation patterns most closely match those of the target examples. It solves the problem of "given a specific downstream capability I want my model to acquire during pretraining, which documents in my massive web crawl actually train that capability?" by reframing data selection from representation-matching to **computational-signature-matching**: rather than asking whether a candidate document's embedding is close to target embeddings (which can conflate surface features with task-relevant structure), it asks whether the candidate document activates the same sparse set of high-impact neurons in the reference LLM.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components, executed sequentially:

1. **Target Profile Construction**: A small set of target examples `D_target` (e.g., HellaSwag training samples) is fed through an off-the-shelf LLM in inference mode. For each input, the **Neuron Impact Calculator** (Section 2.1) computes the contribution of every neuron in every projection layer, and the **NAG Extractor** (Section 2.2) selects the top-K highest-impact neurons per layer to form a compact Neuron-Activated Graph (NAG). These per-example NAGs are aggregated via frequency counting into a **Target Profile** — a layer-by-neuron frequency map representing which neurons are consistently activated by target examples.

2. **Candidate NAG Extraction**: Every document in the candidate pool `D_pool` (150B tokens from RefinedWeb) undergoes the same forward-pass and NAG extraction procedure, producing one NAG per candidate document. This is a one-time cost: once extracted, NAGs are target-independent and can be reused for any target task.

3. **Similarity-Based Ranking**: For each candidate document `c`, its NAG is compared against the Target Profile using a **group similarity function** `Sim(c, D_target)` that averages, across layers, the overlap between the candidate's top-K neurons and the target's frequently-activated neurons. This produces a scalar relevance score `s(c)` for every candidate.

4. **Top-Fraction Selection**: All candidates are ranked in descending order of `s(c)`, and the top fraction `r_f` (e.g., 20%) by token count is selected to form the pretraining corpus `D_selected`. This subset is then used to train a new model from scratch.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the Neuron Impact definition (Section 2.1)** — how we quantify which neurons matter for a given input, since this is the atomic signal that everything else builds on.
- **Second, the NAG construction procedure (Section 2.2)** — how we organize per-neuron impact scores across layers into a compact, comparable graph structure, including the critical sparsity parameter `K` and its justification.
- **Third, the NAG-based similarity metrics and ranking procedure (Section 2.3)** — how we compare NAGs across inputs and against groups, derive the ranking criterion, and prove equivalence between the computationally efficient group similarity formulation and the intuitive pairwise average.
- **Fourth, the full data selection pipeline** — how these components fit together into a training-free, model-agnostic workflow with specific design choices (UP projection neurons, layerwise ratio `r_k = 0.3%`, filtering rate `r_f = 20%`) and their empirical justifications from the analysis in Section 4.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that neuron-level activation overlap, aggregated across all layers of an off-the-shelf LLM, provides a more causally grounded and interpretable signal for target-oriented data selection than embedding similarity or learned classifiers. The technical contribution is the full pipeline from neuron impact quantification through graph construction to similarity-based ranking, along with empirical validation of each design choice.

#### Defining and Computing Neuron Impact

The fundamental atomic operation in the NAG pipeline is quantifying **how much a single neuron contributes to processing a given input**. The paper needs a metric that is (a) computationally cheap enough to compute for every neuron in every layer on millions of documents, and (b) causally meaningful — neurons with high impact scores should genuinely be important for the model's computation on that input, not just correlated with it.

**What is a neuron?** The paper adopts the definition from PLND (Zhao et al., 2024): a neuron is a single column of a projection weight matrix in a Transformer. Specifically, for any projection layer with weight matrix `W ∈ R^{d_in × d_out}`, each of the `d_out` columns is treated as an individual neuron. The paper focuses on projection layers in both Attention (Q, K, V) and FFN (UP, DOWN) modules. For example, the FFN UP projection `W_up ∈ R^{d_model × d_internal}` contains `d_internal` neurons — one per column of the weight matrix. This column-as-neuron definition is important because it cleanly separates each neuron's contribution: when computing a layer's output `h_out = h_in^T W`, the contribution of neuron `k` is exactly `h_in^T W_{:,k}`, the dot product between the input vector and the `k`-th column of the weight matrix.

**The impact metric: local output change under deactivation.** The paper defines neuron impact as the magnitude of change in the *local layer output* when that neuron is deactivated:

$$Imp(N_k | h_{in}) = \| h_{in}^T W - h_{in}^T (W \setminus N_k) \|_2 = |h_{in}^T W_{:,k}|$$

where `h_in ∈ R^{d_in}` is the input activation vector to the projection layer, `W ∈ R^{d_in × d_out}` is the projection weight matrix, `W_{:,k}` is the `k`-th column of `W` (the neuron being evaluated), and `W \ N_k` denotes the weight matrix with the `k`-th column zeroed out.

**What it computes:** The norm (L2) of the difference between the layer's output with and without neuron `k`. Because deactivating neuron `k` simply removes its column from the computation, the output change is exactly `h_in^T W_{:,k}` — the scalar contribution of that neuron to the layer output — and its magnitude is the absolute value of that scalar. The result is a single non-negative number per neuron per layer per input.

**Why this form:** The paper explicitly notes that evaluating a neuron's effect on the *final model output* would be more behaviorally relevant but is computationally prohibitive — it would require a full forward pass per neuron. Instead, they adopt a local approximation: measure the change at the immediate output of the projection layer containing that neuron. This is a standard technique in the pruning and interpretability literature (it approximates the first-order Taylor expansion of the loss). To validate that this local proxy correlates with genuine behavioral importance, the paper provides an explicit validation experiment (Appendix C): they rank UP projection neurons by impact score, group them into bins of 50 neurons each (deactivating same-rank neurons across all layers), and measure the resulting change in model loss on 500 samples. The Pearson correlation between group mean impact and loss change is +0.71 ± 0.02, and deactivating the top 0.8% of neurons causes 159× more loss change than deactivating mid-rank neurons. This validation justifies using the local proxy: neurons with high local impact scores are indeed disproportionately important for the model's final behavior.

**Why not other metrics?** An alternative would be gradient-based importance (e.g., the magnitude of the gradient of the loss with respect to the neuron's output). However, gradient computation requires a backward pass, which is roughly 2× the cost of a forward pass and requires a loss function (which means you need labels or at least a language modeling objective). The paper's impact metric uses only a forward pass — it can be computed during standard inference with no labels, no backward pass, and no task-specific setup. This is what makes the method "training-free": the impact scores fall out of the same forward pass that produces the model's predictions, incurring only the cost of saving intermediate activations and computing column-wise magnitudes.

**Operational summary:** For a given input text, you run a standard forward pass through the reference LLM, record the input activation vector `h_in` at each projection layer, and compute `|h_in^T W_{:,k}|` for every neuron `k` in that layer. The result is a per-layer vector of neuron impact scores — one scalar per neuron — that quantifies how much each neuron contributed to that layer's computation on this specific input.

#### Constructing the Neuron-Activated Graph (NAG)

Having computed impact scores for every neuron on a given input, the next step is to organize them into a compact, structured representation that can be efficiently compared across inputs. This is what the Neuron-Activated Graph (NAG) provides.

**The core design decision: top-K selection per layer.** Rather than keeping all impact scores (which would produce a representation as large as the model itself — millions of floats per input — and would be dominated by noise from low-impact neurons), the NAG selects only the top-K highest-impact neurons per layer:

$$\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{(K)}(c) = \text{TopK}\left(\{I_{\ell,k}(c)\}_{k=1}^{d_{\ell}}\right) \subseteq \{1, \dots, d_{\ell}\}$$

where `I_{\ell,k}(c)` is the impact of neuron `k` in layer `\ell` for input `c`, `d_{\ell}` is the total number of neurons in layer `\ell`, and `\text{TopK}(\cdot)` returns the indices of the `K` largest elements.

**What it computes:** For each layer, sort all `d_{\ell}` neurons by their impact score (descending) and keep the indices of the top `K`. The result is a set of `K` neuron indices per layer — not their impact values, just which neurons they are.

**Why top-K indices rather than impact values:** The paper discards the actual impact magnitudes and keeps only the identity of which neurons were activated. This is a deliberate design choice: the hypothesis is that *which* neurons fire is more stable and more informative than *how strongly* they fire. Impact magnitudes are sensitive to input length, tokenization artifacts, and other nuisance variations; neuron identity is a discrete, structural signal. Keeping only indices also makes the NAG a compact set rather than a dense vector, enabling efficient set-based similarity operations and interpretable inspection (you can literally look at which neurons are shared between a candidate and the target).

**The full NAG definition:** The NAG for input `c` is the collection of layer-wise top-K neuron index sets:

$$\text{NAG}(c) = \left\{\mathcal{N}_1^{(K)}(c), \mathcal{N}_2^{(K)}(c), \dots, \mathcal{N}_L^{(K)}(c)\right\}$$

or equivalently as the set of layer-neuron index pairs:

$$\text{NAG}(c) = \left\{(\ell, k) \mid \ell \in \{1, \dots, L\}, k \in \mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{(K)}(c)\right\}$$

where `L` is the number of layers. The total size of the NAG is `L × K` elements — for the paper's default configuration with Qwen3-1.7B (L=28 layers, `d_internal = 6144` for UP projection, K=20), the NAG contains only 560 neuron indices, representing approximately 0.3% of all UP projection neurons (`20/6144 ≈ 0.33%` per layer).

**The sparsity parameter `K` and the layerwise neuron ratio `r_k`:** The paper parameterizes `K` via a layerwise ratio `r_k = K / d_{\ell}`, where `d_{\ell}` is the number of neurons in layer `\ell`. This ensures that models with different internal dimensions are compared on an equal footing — a `K=20` selection from a layer with 6144 neurons (0.33%) is comparable to a `K=30` selection from a layer with 11008 neurons (0.27%). The paper's default configuration uses `r_k = 0.3%` across all layers, which was determined empirically through a sweep (Figure 6, Section 4.2.3). The sweep shows that performance increases rapidly as `r_k` grows from near-zero, peaks at approximately 0.3%, and then plateaus or slightly declines for larger `r_k` — indicating that task-relevant signals are concentrated in a very sparse set of high-impact neurons, and including more neurons dilutes the signal with less discriminative, noisier neurons. At the extreme `r_k → 1`, the NAG would include all neurons, and the ranking would theoretically collapse toward random selection (since all inputs would have identical NAGs).

**Choice of neuron type: FFN UP projections.** The paper compares NAGs constructed from different projection layers — K, Q, V, O (attention projections), UP, and DOWN (FFN projections) — and finds that UP projection neurons yield the highest performance on HellaSwag (60.6% at `r_k=0.3%`, K=20, Figure 5). The authors hypothesize this is because expansion layers like UP projection operate in a higher-dimensional latent space (`d_internal > d_model`) that better isolates task-specific signals, whereas projection layers closer to the residual stream (DOWN projection, attention projections) tend to capture more compressed, mixed information. This is consistent with mechanistic interpretability findings that FFN layers store factual and procedural knowledge in their expanded intermediate representations, which can be more cleanly attributed to individual neurons.

**Layer coverage: all layers vs. final layer only.** The NAG by default aggregates neurons from all `L` layers. The paper explicitly compares this against a "last-layer only" variant (Section 4.2.2, Table 4) and finds a 4.1% average performance drop when restricting to the final layer, with particularly severe degradation on TriviaQA (22.3% → 15.1%) and HellaSwag (60.6% → 55.2%). This demonstrates that task-relevant signals are distributed across the full depth of the model — early and middle layers contribute information that is not available in the final layer's compressed representations. This finding is a direct empirical critique of methods like BETR that rely exclusively on final-layer embeddings.

**Operational summary:** For each input text, run a forward pass, compute impact scores for all UP projection neurons across all layers, select the top-K neurons per layer (where K is determined by `r_k` × layer dimension), and store only the (layer_index, neuron_index) pairs. The result is a sparse, discrete, interpretable signature of how the model processes that specific input.

#### NAG-Based Similarity: Comparing Inputs and Groups

With NAGs constructed for both target examples and candidate documents, the paper needs a way to quantify alignment — how similar is a candidate document's neuronal activation pattern to the pattern characteristic of the target task? The paper defines two forms of similarity: a pairwise metric for comparing individual inputs, and a group metric for comparing an input against an aggregated target profile.

**Pairwise NAG similarity.** For two inputs `c` and `c'` with NAGs constructed using the same `K` and the same neuron type across the same model, the similarity is defined as the Dice coefficient (F1 score) of their NAG sets:

$$\text{Sim}(c, c') = \frac{2 |\text{NAG}(c) \cap \text{NAG}(c')|}{|\text{NAG}(c)| + |\text{NAG}(c')|}$$

**What it computes:** Twice the size of the intersection divided by the sum of the sizes — the harmonic mean of precision and recall. Since both NAGs have the same total size `L × K` (each input selects exactly `K` neurons per layer across `L` layers), this simplifies to `|NAG(c) ∩ NAG(c')| / (L × K)` — the fraction of selected neuron positions where the two inputs agree.

**Why Dice rather than Jaccard or cosine similarity:** The Dice coefficient is symmetric, bounded in [0, 1], and handles the fixed-size sets naturally (the denominator is constant). Jaccard similarity `|A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|` would also be bounded in [0, 1] but would have a variable denominator, making it harder to interpret across comparisons. Cosine similarity is not directly applicable because NAGs are sets of discrete indices, not vectors in a continuous space. The Dice form preserves the set-based semantics while providing a normalized similarity in [0, 1] — 0 means no shared neurons, 1 means identical NAGs.

**Target profile: aggregating NAGs over a group.** To compare a single candidate against many target examples efficiently (without computing `|D_target|` pairwise similarities), the paper constructs an aggregated **target profile**: a frequency map over all layer-neuron pairs indicating how often each neuron is selected across the target set.

$$w_{\ell,k}(D) = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{c' \in D} \mathbb{1}[(\ell, k) \in \text{NAG}(c')]$$

where `D` is the set of target examples (or any group of inputs), `(\ell, k)` is a layer-neuron index pair, and `\mathbb{1}[\cdot]` is the indicator function (1 if neuron `k` in layer `\ell` is in the NAG of `c'`, 0 otherwise).

**What it computes:** For each neuron position in the model, the fraction of target examples for which that neuron appears in the top-K. Neurons consistently activated across many target examples will have `w` close to 1.0; neurons never activated will have `w = 0`. The result is a matrix (or dictionary) mapping `(layer, neuron_index)` → activation frequency in [0, 1].

**Group similarity: comparing a candidate to a target profile.** Given the target profile `{w_{\ell,k}(D_target)}`, the similarity of a candidate `c` to the target group is:

$$\text{Sim}(c, D_{\text{target}}) = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \frac{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{(K)}(c)} w_{\ell,k}(D_{\text{target}})}{\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\ell}} w_{\ell,k}(D_{\text{target}})}$$

where the outer sum averages over layers, the numerator sums the target activation frequencies for the neurons the candidate selects in that layer, and the denominator normalizes by the total activation mass in that layer.

**Key simplification of the denominator:** Because each target example selects exactly `K` neurons per layer, the sum of `w_{\ell,k}` over all `d_{\ell}` neurons in a layer equals exactly `K`:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{d_{\ell}} w_{\ell,k} = \sum_{k=1}^{d_{\ell}} \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{c' \in D} \mathbb{1}[(\ell, k) \in \text{NAG}(c')] = \frac{1}{|D|} \sum_{c' \in D} |\mathcal{N}_{\ell}^{(K)}(c')| = K$$

This means the denominator is constant across layers and across candidates — it eliminates any normalization artifacts that could arise from different layers having different numbers of neurons. The group similarity becomes a simple average: for each layer, count how many of the candidate's top-K neurons are also frequently activated by the target (weighted by their activation frequency), divide by K, and average across layers.

**Equivalence to average pairwise similarity.** The paper proves (Appendix D) that this group similarity is mathematically equivalent to the average of pairwise similarities between the candidate and each target example:

$$\text{Sim}(c, D_{\text{target}}) = \frac{1}{|D_{\text{target}}|} \sum_{c' \in D_{\text{target}}} \text{Sim}(c, c')$$

**Why this equivalence matters:** The frequency-based formulation avoids enumerating all `|D_target|` pairwise comparisons — you precompute the target profile once (O(|D_target| × L × K) operations), then score each candidate in O(L × K) time by looking up the precomputed frequencies. For a target set of 10,000 examples and a candidate pool of millions, this is the difference between feasible and infeasible. The proof confirms that this computational shortcut incurs no approximation error — it is exact, not a heuristic.

**Operational summary of the similarity computation:** (1) Aggregate the NAGs of all `|D_target|` target examples into a frequency profile (a dictionary from `(layer, neuron_index)` to `[0,1]`). (2) For each candidate document, extract its NAG (top-K neurons per layer). (3) For each layer, look up the target frequency of each of the candidate's K neurons, sum those frequencies, and divide by K (the constant denominator). (4) Average the resulting per-layer scores across all L layers. The output is a scalar in [0, 1] representing how strongly the candidate's neuronal activation pattern overlaps with the pattern characteristic of the target task.

#### The Full Data Selection Pipeline: From Raw Text to Ranked Corpus

The paper assembles the components above into a complete, training-free pipeline for target-oriented pretraining data selection. This subsection describes the end-to-end workflow with all specific configurations and design choices.

**Step 1: Configure the extraction backbone.** Choose an off-the-shelf, pretrained LLM to serve as the neuron extraction model. The paper evaluates three backbones: Qwen3-1.7B-Base, Llama-3.2-3B, and SmolLM3-3B, with consistent results across all three (Table 1, average gains of 4.9%, 4.7%, and 5.0% respectively). The extraction model does not need to be the same as the model being trained — it can be larger, smaller, or from a different family, as demonstrated by the 0.6B extraction → 1.2B training experiment in Appendix I. The only requirement is that the extraction model uses a Transformer architecture with accessible projection weight matrices.

**Step 2: Configure the NAG hyperparameters.** Three parameters control NAG construction: which neuron type to use (default: UP projection), which layers to include (default: all layers), and the sparsity ratio `r_k` (default: 0.3%). For Qwen3-1.7B with `d_internal = 6144`, this yields `K = ceil(0.003 × 6144) ≈ 20` neurons per layer. For Llama-3.2-3B with `d_internal = 8192`, it also yields K=20 (the paper rounds to the nearest integer). For SmolLM3-3B with `d_internal = 11008`, it yields K=30. The paper reports these effective widths in Table 8. The specific value `r_k = 0.3%` was determined by sweeping across `r_k ∈ {0.01%, 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.3%, 0.5%, 1.0%, 2.1%}` on the HellaSwag target and finding that performance peaks at 0.3% across all three model scales (Figure 6). This is an empirical finding, not a theoretical optimum, but the consistency across model sizes suggests it captures a general property of how task-relevant signals are sparsely distributed in Transformer neurons.

**Step 3: Extract NAGs for target examples.** Feed each target example through the extraction model in inference mode (single forward pass, no generation, no gradient computation). For each example, at each UP projection layer, record the input activation vector `h_in` and compute impact scores `|h_in^T W_{:,k}|` for all `d_internal` neurons. Select the top-K indices. The result is one NAG per target example. The paper uses target sets of varying sizes (Table 7): ARC-C uses 1,119 examples (full train split), HellaSwag uses 10,000 (randomly sampled), TriviaQA uses 10,000, MMLU uses 1,531 (full validation split), XStoryCloze uses 360 (full English train split), XWinograd uses 2,124 (full non-English split). All target examples are drawn from training/validation splits, with 13-gram overlap decontamination against test sets to prevent leakage.

**Step 4: Build the target profile.** Aggregate the per-example NAGs into the frequency profile `{w_{\ell,k}(D_target)}` by counting, for each `(layer, neuron_index)` pair, what fraction of target examples include that neuron in their top-K. The result is a compact data structure — for Qwen3-1.7B with L=28 layers and `d_internal = 6144`, it contains at most `28 × 6144 ≈ 172,000` entries, many of which will be zero (neurons never selected by any target example). The profile is stored once and reused for all candidate comparisons.

**Step 5: Extract NAGs for all candidate documents.** The paper constructs its candidate pool by uniformly downsampling RefinedWeb from approximately 600B tokens to 150B tokens. Each document in this pool undergoes the same forward-pass and NAG extraction procedure as the target examples (Step 3). The paper notes (Appendix F.2) that this extraction takes 192 GPU-hours on H100-SXM-80GB for the full 150B-token pool using Qwen3-1.7B as the extraction model — a one-time cost that is "acceptable compared to the cost of model pretraining itself." Importantly, this extraction produces target-independent NAGs: the same candidate NAGs can be reused for any target task without re-extraction, meaning the cost is amortized across all targets.

**Step 6: Score and rank candidates.** For each candidate document, compute `s(c) = Sim(c, D_target)` using the group similarity formula. This requires looking up, for each of the candidate's `L × K` selected neurons, the target's activation frequency `w_{\ell,k}`, summing, and averaging. The paper notes (Appendix F.2) that ranking is "CPU-only and negligible compared to extraction" — instead of globally sorting all candidates, they estimate the top-`r_f` filtering threshold on a small random subset and apply it to the full pool, achieving O(N) complexity.

**Step 7: Select the top fraction.** All candidates are ranked in descending order of `s(c)`, and the top `r_f = 20%` by token count are selected. The paper explicitly fixes the filtering rate to 20% for all main experiments (Section 3.3), meaning approximately 30B tokens are selected from the 150B token pool for pretraining. The 20% rate is chosen to match the baseline comparisons: Random also samples 20% of the pool, BETR selects the top 20% by its own ranking, and FineWeb-Edu selects the top 20% by educational quality score. This ensures all methods operate under the same data budget.

**Step 8: Pretrain from scratch.** The selected 30B tokens are used to train a 1.2B-parameter Transformer model from scratch with identical architecture, optimization settings, and training budget across all experiments. The model architecture uses 24 layers, hidden dimension 2048, MLP dimension 5440, 16 attention heads, SwiGLU activation, and RoPE embeddings (detailed in Table 5 and Appendix A.1). Training uses AdamW optimizer with `β_1 = 0.9, β_2 = 0.95`, weight decay 0.1, cosine decay learning rate schedule from `5 × 10^{-4}` to `5 × 10^{-5}`, warmup ratio 0.5%, and a maximum sequence length of 2048 tokens. The only difference across experiments is which 30B tokens were selected.

**Multi-target extension.** For the multi-target setting, the paper uses a simple equal-budget mixture: for each of the six benchmarks, independently select `(r_f / 6) × 150B = 5B` tokens using the single-target pipeline with that benchmark's target examples, then concatenate all six subsets to form the 30B training corpus. There is no re-weighting, deduplication across targets, or joint optimization — the paper explicitly frames this as a "lower bound" for multi-target performance and notes that more sophisticated mixture strategies (RegMix, QuaDMix) could further improve results.

**Integration with existing quality signals.** The paper demonstrates (Section 3.6, Table 2) that NAG can be combined with the FineWeb-Edu quality classifier through joint ranking, though the paper does not specify the exact combination mechanism in the main text. The results show additive gains (+1.5% to +2.2% over FineWeb-Edu alone), indicating that NAG captures signals complementary to general educational quality — target-specific neuronal alignment and general text quality are tracking different, non-redundant aspects of data utility.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Local proxy for neuron impact (output change magnitude)** over end-to-end loss change: computationally feasible (single forward pass, no backward pass, no labels) while validated to strongly correlate with behavioral importance (Pearson +0.71).
- **Top-K discrete neuron indices** over continuous impact values: discards magnitude information to gain robustness to input-length artifacts, enables efficient set-based similarity, and produces interpretable, inspectable representations.
- **Layerwise ratio `r_k = 0.3%`** over fixed absolute K: ensures comparable sparsity across models with different internal dimensions; empirically determined to be the sweet spot where task-relevant signals are maximally concentrated without noise dilution.
- **FFN UP projection neurons** over other projection types: UP projections operate in higher-dimensional latent space that better isolates task-specific signals; empirically validated as the best-performing neuron type.
- **All-layer aggregation** over last-layer only: task-relevant signals are distributed across model depth; restricting to the final layer causes a 4.1% average drop.
- **Frequency-weighted group similarity** over exhaustive pairwise comparison: mathematically equivalent but computationally efficient (O(N) vs. O(N × |D_target|)); the proof in Appendix D guarantees no approximation error.
- **Dice coefficient for pairwise similarity** over Jaccard or cosine: fixed denominator (constant NAG size) enables clean normalization and equivalence to the group-averaged formulation.
- **Training-free, model-agnostic design** over learned classifiers: eliminates per-target training cost, avoids distribution shift between extraction model and selection model, and provides neuron-level interpretability that black-box classifiers cannot offer.
- **20% filtering rate** over variable rates: matches baseline budgets for fair comparison; the rate-sweep experiment (Figure 4) shows that NAG's ranking is utility-aligned (performance increases with more aggressive filtering), meaning the 20% choice is conservative — tighter filtering would likely yield further gains.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Target-Oriented Data Selection from Representation Matching to Computational-Signature Matching

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is not the NAG data structure or the ranking algorithm per se, but the **shift in what it means for a candidate document to be "relevant" to a target task**. Prior target-oriented methods — BETR (Mizrahi et al., 2025), perplexity-correlation approaches (SHUM et al., 2025; Thrush et al., 2025), and DAIG (Miyoshi et al., 2025) — all operate under a shared assumption: that the similarity of a candidate document to target examples, measured in some model-derived representation space (embeddings, loss values, auxiliary classifier outputs), is a good proxy for its utility in training the target capability. The paper argues this assumption is fundamentally limited because **similarity of representations is not the same as similarity of computation**.

Why does this distinction matter? Two documents can produce similar last-layer embeddings — and thus be deemed "relevant" by BETR — while engaging entirely different internal circuits in the model. A Wikipedia article that *explains* how to solve maximization problems may have high embedding similarity to a MATH training example that *is* a maximization problem, simply because they share vocabulary, topic, and surface structure. But the explanatory article exercises neural circuits for reading comprehension and fact retrieval, while the problem-solving example exercises circuits for multi-step symbolic reasoning. Training on the former does not strengthen the latter. Conversely, two documents from different domains — say, an economics word problem about profit maximization and a pure-math problem about function maxima — might have different embeddings but engage overlapping reasoning circuits (both require identifying critical points, checking boundary conditions, applying calculus operations). Embedding similarity would rank the economics problem as dissimilar to the math target and potentially discard it; neuron-activation similarity would recognize the shared computational structure and prioritize it.

This reframing is significant beyond the performance gains because it changes what practitioners should *look for* when selecting data. The dominant paradigm — "find data that looks like your target data" — is replaced with "find data that makes the model think in the same way as your target data." The paper's Figure 1 illustrates this with the economics-to-math example: a firm's profit maximization problem and a pure function maximization problem activate similar neurons despite belonging to different domains, because they share underlying reasoning structure. This is not an incremental improvement over embedding-based methods — it is a different criterion entirely, one that is causally grounded in *which computational pathways the input exercises* rather than correlated with *representational similarity*.

The paper provides direct empirical evidence for this reframing's validity. The deactivation experiment (Table 3, Section 4.1.1) demonstrates that the neurons NAG selects — only 0.12% of all parameters — are causally necessary for task performance: deactivating them causes a 23.5% accuracy collapse, while deactivating random neurons has negligible effect. This establishes that NAG is not just finding *some* neurons that happen to correlate with the target, but is identifying the specific sparse circuitry that the target capability depends on. The clustering experiment (Figure 3, Section 4.1.2) provides further support: data instances from ten diverse benchmarks form task-aligned clusters in NAG space, with related tasks (MathQA and GSM8K, both mathematical reasoning) forming adjacent clusters while remaining well-separated from linguistically different tasks (XNLI). If NAG were merely capturing surface features, tasks with similar input formats but different reasoning requirements would cluster together — but they don't. The clusters reflect the *computational demands* of the tasks, not their superficial properties.

This reframing also resolves a tension in prior work: why do embedding-based target-oriented methods work sometimes but fail in multi-target settings? The paper's Table 1 shows BETR degrading by 4.4% when moving from single-target to multi-target selection (48.8% → 44.4%), while NAG degrades by only 1.3% (49.8% → 48.5%). The likely explanation, consistent with the reframing, is that embeddings from different tasks interfere in the compressed representation space — the final layer cannot simultaneously capture the distinct computational signatures of six different tasks without them bleeding into each other. NAG's explicit, discrete neuron sets avoid this interference: each target independently selects its top-K neurons, and a candidate's similarity to each target is computed against separate target profiles. The neuron sets for different tasks may be partially overlapping (reflecting shared computational primitives) or largely disjoint (reflecting task-specific circuitry), but they do not get averaged into an ambiguous mixture in the way that embedding-space similarities do.

In sum, this innovation is **fundamental rather than incremental**. It does not propose a better similarity metric within the existing representation-matching paradigm; it proposes a different paradigm altogether. The shift from "what does the model output for this input?" to "which neurons does this input engage?" opens a different axis for data selection research — one that is more causally grounded, more interpretable, and potentially more robust to the domain-transfer scenarios that bedevil embedding-based methods.

### Innovation 2: The Sparse Functional Backbone as a Diagnostic Concept for Capability-Oriented Data Selection

The paper introduces and empirically validates a specific concept — the **sparse functional backbone** — that provides both a mechanistic explanation for why NAG works and a diagnostic tool for understanding what a target capability actually requires. The idea is that any specific downstream capability (mathematical reasoning, commonsense inference, factual retrieval) is implemented by a highly sparse subset of neurons distributed across the model's layers, and that these neurons constitute a "backbone" that can be identified by analyzing which neurons are consistently high-impact when the model processes target-relevant inputs.

This concept is not entirely novel in the mechanistic interpretability literature — Panigrahi et al. (2023) showed task-specific skill localization in fine-tuned models, and Zhao et al. (2024, 2025) identified sparse safety and multilingualism neurons — but the paper **repurposes it from a descriptive finding to an operational principle for data selection**. Prior work identified sparse functional subnetworks post-hoc, after a model was already trained, to understand its behavior. This paper inverts the direction: by identifying which neurons a pretrained reference model uses to process target examples, you can prospectively select pretraining data that will strengthen those same neurons in a new model trained from scratch. The reference model's functional backbone serves as a blueprint for what the target capability requires computationally, and candidate documents are evaluated by how well they would exercise that blueprint.

The evidence for this concept as a genuine diagnostic tool — not just a post-hoc rationalization — comes from three converging analyses:

**1. The extreme sparsity of critical neurons (Section 4.1.1, Table 3).** Deactivating just 20 neurons per layer (0.12% of all parameters) selected by NAG causes a 23.5% average accuracy collapse across six benchmarks. Deactivating an equal number of random neurons causes a 0.2% drop. The fine-grained ablation further isolates 28 neurons (0.006% of parameters) whose deactivation causes a 17.8% drop. This is not just "these neurons matter" — it is "these specific neurons matter to a degree wildly disproportionate to their numbers, and NAG identifies them purely from forward-pass behavior without any task-specific training." The concept of a functional backbone would be vacuous if the "backbone" consisted of 30% of the model's neurons — then it's just the model doing its normal work. The fact that it is 0.12% — and that NAG can find it — makes the concept both meaningful and practically discoverable.

**2. Task-discriminative clustering in NAG space (Section 4.1.2, Figure 3).** If the functional backbone concept is valid, then inputs that exercise the same capability should engage overlapping backbone neurons, and inputs requiring different capabilities should engage disjoint (or weakly overlapping) backbone neurons. The t-SNE visualization in Figure 3 confirms this: ten diverse benchmarks form distinct, well-separated clusters when embedded using NAG-based distance. Moreover, the relative positioning of clusters is semantically meaningful — MathQA and GSM8K, both mathematical reasoning tasks, form adjacent clusters, while remaining separated from purely linguistic tasks like XNLI. The clustering quality metrics in Appendix G (Table 11) quantify this: at K=20, the K-Means clustering based on NAG similarity achieves Purity 0.973, NMI 0.945, and ARI 0.946 — near-perfect alignment between the discovered clusters and the ground-truth task labels. This would not happen if NAG were capturing arbitrary or noisy neuron activations; it happens because the top-K neurons per input genuinely reflect which capability-specific circuits are being exercised.

**3. The relationship between discriminability and utility (Appendix G, Table 11).** The paper goes further by showing that the *quality* of the functional backbone representation — measured by how well it separates different tasks — predicts downstream data selection utility. NAGs with K=5 neurons per layer produce worse task clusters (Purity drops from 0.973 to 0.878) and correspondingly worse HellaSwag accuracy (58.0% vs. 60.6%). This closes the loop: better backbone representations (more discriminative neuron sets) lead to better data selection (more utility-aligned rankings), which leads to better trained models. The concept is not just descriptive; it has predictive and diagnostic value.

The practical implication of this concept extends beyond the paper's immediate method. If different capabilities have identifiable, sparse functional backbones in pretrained reference models, then:
- **Data selection can be diagnosed and debugged**: if NAG-based selection isn't working for a particular target, you can inspect which neurons are being selected and whether they plausibly relate to the target capability (rather than trusting a black-box classifier score).
- **Curriculum design becomes possible**: by analyzing which neurons are engaged by easy vs. hard examples of the same task, you could select data that progressively activates more of the backbone, building capability incrementally.
- **Capability overlap can be measured**: the overlap between functional backbones for two different tasks (e.g., mathematical reasoning and logical reasoning) could predict transfer learning success, informing multi-task training strategies.

This innovation is **fundamental in its conceptual implications but incremental in its empirical instantiation** — the sparse functional localization finding itself is not new, but the paper's contribution is showing that it can be operationalized for data selection without any training, fine-tuning, or task-specific optimization, and that the resulting representations are both causally validated (deactivation) and predictively useful (clustering-to-utility correlation).

### Innovation 3: Neuron-Level Interpretability as a Practical Alternative to Black-Box Data Selection

The paper makes a methodological argument that goes beyond the specific NAG algorithm: **interpretability is not just a nice-to-have for data selection — it enables capabilities (diagnosis, refinement, multi-target robustness) that black-box methods structurally cannot provide**. This argument is advanced not through philosophical claims but through concrete experimental demonstrations of what interpretability enables.

Consider the contrast with BETR, the strongest target-oriented baseline. BETR trains a classifier on embedding similarities and outputs a scalar relevance score per candidate. If BETR-selected data fails to improve a particular target, there is no way to diagnose why — the classifier is a black box, and its scores are uninterpretable. You can try different hyperparameters or retrain on different target examples, but you cannot look inside the selection criterion and ask: "Is this selecting data that exercises the right computational circuits, or is it selecting data that merely looks like the target at a surface level?" If BETR's performance degrades in the multi-target setting (which it does, dropping from 48.8% to 44.4% in Table 1), you cannot determine whether the classifier is confused by conflicting embedding signals from different tasks, whether it is overfitting to one task's surface features at the expense of others, or whether the embedding space simply cannot simultaneously represent six diverse capability signals.

NAG-based Ranking makes all of these questions answerable. Because NAG represents each input as an explicit set of neuron indices, you can:

**1. Inspect which neurons drive the selection.** For a given target, you can look at the target profile `{w_{\ell,k}}` and identify which neurons are consistently highly weighted. For HellaSwag, these would be neurons in specific layers that the reference model uses for commonsense reasoning. You can verify whether these neurons are plausibly related to the target capability (e.g., by checking whether deactivating them impairs HellaSwag performance but not unrelated tasks). This is exactly what the deactivation experiment in Table 3 demonstrates — and it's an experiment that is only possible because NAG makes the selected neurons explicit.

**2. Diagnose multi-target interference.** In the multi-target setting, NAG constructs separate target profiles for each benchmark and scores candidates against each profile independently. If a particular benchmark underperforms, you can examine whether its target profile overlaps heavily with other benchmarks' profiles (suggesting the tasks genuinely share computational structure and are competing for the same data) or whether the profiles are largely disjoint but the data mixture strategy is suboptimal. BETR cannot provide this diagnosis because its embedding-space similarities for different tasks collapse into a single classifier score.

**3. Refine the selection without retraining.** The paper demonstrates that NAG's performance varies with the neuron type (Figure 5) and the layer coverage (Table 4). These ablation studies are themselves a form of interpretability-driven refinement: by understanding which model components carry the most task-relevant signal, you can improve the selection criterion without any additional training — just change which neurons you extract. If a black-box classifier performs poorly, your only option is to retrain it with different data or hyperparameters, which is expensive and provides no insight into what went wrong.

**4. Validate selection quality causally.** The deactivation experiment (Table 3) is a causal validation — it proves not just that NAG-selected neurons correlate with task performance, but that they are *necessary* for it. This is a gold-standard interpretability test that no embedding-based or classifier-based method can pass, because those methods do not identify specific model components whose causal role can be tested. The paper's ability to say "deactivating NAG-selected neurons causes a 23.5% drop, therefore NAG is genuinely identifying the circuitry that implements the target capability" is a qualitatively stronger claim than "the selected data improves downstream accuracy."

The practical significance of this innovation is that it **lowers the barrier to adopting target-oriented data selection in real-world settings**. In production, data selection pipelines are not one-shot; they require iteration, debugging, and adaptation as target requirements evolve. A black-box pipeline that works on the first try but cannot be diagnosed when it fails is far less valuable than an interpretable pipeline that can be inspected, understood, and refined. The paper's demonstration that NAG can be combined with existing quality signals (Table 2, where NAG + FineWeb-Edu achieves 49.6% vs. FineWeb-Edu's 47.4%) further supports this: interpretable, neuron-level signals can be *composed* with other signals in a way that black-box signals cannot, because you can understand what each signal contributes and adjust the combination accordingly.

This innovation is **more methodological than theoretical** — it does not introduce a new concept about how neural networks work, but it makes a strong case that interpretability is not a luxury for data selection but a practical necessity for robust, debuggable, multi-target deployment. The paper's deactivation and clustering experiments are not merely validation of NAG; they are demonstrations of a *class of analyses* that become possible when data selection is based on explicit, inspectable model components rather than opaque scores.

### Innovation 4: Empirical Discovery That Task-Relevant Signals Are Distributed Across Model Depth — With Practical Consequences for Data Selection

While it is well-known in the mechanistic interpretability literature that different layers of Transformers serve different computational roles (early layers for surface features, middle layers for syntax and semantics, late layers for task-specific processing), the paper makes a specific empirical finding with direct practical consequences: **for the purpose of target-oriented data selection, restricting to final-layer representations — which is the default in embedding-based methods like BETR — discards critical task-relevant information, causing a 4.1% average performance drop**. This is not merely a confirmation of known layer-wise processing; it is a demonstration that the *distribution* of task-relevant signals across layers matters for data selection in a way that prior work did not account for.

Section 4.2.2 and Table 4 present the key result: when NAG is constructed using neurons from all 28 layers of Qwen3-1.7B, the average accuracy across six benchmarks is 49.8%. When restricted to the final layer only, accuracy drops to 45.7% — a 4.1% absolute decline, with benchmark-specific drops ranging from 2.2% (XStoryCloze) to 7.2% (TriviaQA). On TriviaQA and MMLU, where the average random-selection accuracy is only 22.9%, the final-layer variant actually *underperforms* random selection by 0.4% — meaning the final layer's signal is not just weaker than the full-layer signal, but actively misleading for these tasks.

Why is this finding significant beyond confirming existing intuitions about layer-wise processing? Because it identifies a **specific failure mode of embedding-based data selection methods and provides a concrete mechanism for why they underperform**. BETR uses last-layer embeddings to measure candidate-target similarity. If task-relevant signals were concentrated in the final layer, this would be a reasonable choice — you'd be using the layer that is closest to the task output, where task-specific features are most explicitly represented. But the paper's result shows that this assumption is empirically false for the diverse set of reasoning and knowledge tasks tested. The final layer's representations are too compressed, too task-agnostic, or too entangled with surface features to reliably distinguish data that exercises the target capability from data that merely looks similar.

The layer-wise signal distribution also explains a pattern in the benchmark-specific results: the final-layer variant performs worst on TriviaQA (22.3% → 15.1%) and best on MMLU (32.2% → 29.9%, a smaller drop). This suggests that factual knowledge retrieval (TriviaQA) relies more heavily on computational patterns distributed across early and middle layers (where factual associations are stored and retrieved), while multiple-choice academic reasoning (MMLU) may have more of its task-specific signal concentrated in later layers. This kind of benchmark-level diagnostic is only possible because NAG provides per-layer, per-neuron granularity — embedding-based methods cannot decompose their signal to understand which layers drive the selection for which tasks.

The practical consequence of this finding is a **design principle for future data selection methods**: do not assume that the final layer captures all task-relevant information. Methods that aggregate signals across layers — whether through multi-layer NAGs like this paper, or through other cross-layer aggregation schemes — are likely to be more robust across diverse targets than methods that rely on final-layer representations alone. This principle is immediately actionable: any embedding-based data selection pipeline could potentially be improved by concatenating or otherwise combining representations from multiple layers rather than using the final layer exclusively.

This innovation is **incremental in its conceptual basis but significant in its empirical implications** — the fact that different layers encode different information is not new, but the paper's quantification of *how much* data selection performance is left on the table by final-layer-only methods, and the demonstration that this varies systematically across task types, provides both a diagnostic for understanding when existing methods will fail and a prescription for building better ones.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses six benchmarks spanning reasoning and knowledge-intensive capabilities: ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018, 1,172 multiple-choice grade-school science questions requiring multi-step reasoning, evaluated with 25-shot), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019, 10,042 commonsense sentence-completion items with adversarial distractors, 10-shot), TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017, 17,944 open-domain factual questions, 5-shot exact match), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021, 14,042 multi-domain academic multiple-choice questions, 5-shot), XStoryCloze (Lin et al., 2022, 1,511 cross-lingual story completion items, 0-shot), and XWinograd (Tikhonov & Ryabinin, 2021, 2,325 coreference resolution items, 5-shot). All evaluations are conducted using the lm-eval-harness framework (Gao et al., 2024) with normalized accuracy where applicable; detailed benchmark statistics and few-shot configurations appear in Table 6 (Appendix A.2).

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses a base extraction model and a separate model for downstream pretraining evaluation. The extraction backbone is a pretrained off-the-shelf LLM (Qwen3-1.7B-Base by default, with Llama-3.2-3B and SmolLM3-3B as alternatives for backbone-dependence experiments (Grattafiori et al., 2024; Bakouch et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025)). The downstream pretraining model is a 1.2B-parameter transformer trained from scratch on the selected data, using 24 layers, hidden dimension 2048, MLP dimension 5440, 16 attention heads, SwiGLU activation, RoPE embeddings, and a 250k vocabulary tokenizer (detailed in Table 5, Appendix A.1). The 1.2B scale was chosen as a practical balance between experimental cost and representativeness; the paper additionally reports a 7B scaling experiment in Appendix I.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is benchmark accuracy — the fraction of test instances the trained model answers correctly, using normalized accuracy (`Acc_norm`) for multiple-choice benchmarks and exact match or accuracy for others as specified in Table 6. For compute efficiency, the paper reports compute multipliers (CM) — the ratio of training compute required by a baseline to achieve the same accuracy as NAG, derived by fitting scaling curves (Appendix F.1). For clustering, Purity, Normalized Mutual Information (NMI), and Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) quantify alignment between discovered clusters and ground-truth task labels (Appendix G).

- **Baselines.** Three baselines are used throughout. **Random** samples 20% of the 150B-token RefinedWeb pool uniformly — the standard baseline in the data selection literature (Mizrahi et al., 2025; Wettig et al., 2024; Sachdeva et al., 2024). **FineWeb-Edu** (Penedo et al., 2024) ranks documents by a learned classifier's estimate of educational value and selects the top 20% — representing the state of general-quality data selection. **BETR** (Mizrahi et al., 2025) scores candidates by embedding similarity to target examples using a trained classifier — representing the state of target-oriented data selection.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The unit of compute is training tokens — all methods select exactly 30B tokens from the 150B-token pool for pretraining, ensuring identical training FLOPs across experiments. The filtering rate is fixed at `r_f = 20%` for all methods. For the NAG extraction itself, the paper reports 192 GPU-hours on H100-SXM-80GB to process the full 150B-token candidate pool with Qwen3-1.7B-Base — a one-time cost that is target-independent and amortized across all experiments (Appendix F.2). The pretraining uses a cosine decay schedule from learning rate `5 × 10^{-4}` to `5 × 10^{-5}`, AdamW optimizer with `β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95`, weight decay 0.1, warmup ratio 0.5%, and max sequence length 2048 (Appendix A.1).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper conducts two types of statistical validation. For run-to-run variance, the Random baseline is repeated 5 times with different seeds, yielding standard deviations of 0.18%–0.55% across benchmarks (Table 15, Appendix J) — an order of magnitude smaller than NAG's gains. For all reported accuracies, binomial standard errors are computed as `√(p(1-p)/n)` where `n` is the benchmark test size (Tables 16–18, Appendix J), with NAG's gains consistently exceeding these standard error ranges. Target sets for NAG extraction are drawn exclusively from training/validation splits, and a 13-gram overlap decontamination check verifies no leakage against test sets (Appendix A.3). The multi-target setting uses an equal-budget mixture as a deliberately simple lower bound for that scenario.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Single-Target Data Selection: NAG Outperforms Both General-Quality and Target-Oriented Baselines

Table 1 reports the primary single-target results across six benchmarks, with NAG instantiated using three different extraction backbones. The headline finding is that **NAG-based Ranking with Qwen3-1.7B-Base achieves a 4.9% average improvement over Random sampling** (49.8% vs. 44.9% averaged across six benchmarks), with gains on every individual benchmark ranging from +2.0% on MMLU to +9.0% on HellaSwag.

**Comparison against general-quality baseline:** NAG outperforms FineWeb-Edu by 2.4% on average (49.8% vs. 47.4%). The gap is most pronounced on benchmarks where general educational quality and target-specific reasoning diverge: HellaSwag shows a 5.3% advantage for NAG (60.6% vs. 55.3%), XStoryCloze shows +4.1% (70.0% vs. 65.9%), and XWinograd shows +3.9% (80.1% vs. 76.2%). On MMLU, FineWeb-Edu slightly outperforms NAG (32.8% vs. 32.2%), consistent with MMLU's emphasis on academic knowledge that aligns well with FineWeb-Edu's educational quality classifier. On ARC-C and TriviaQA, the two methods are comparable (NAG leads by 0.3% and 2.2% respectively).

**Comparison against target-oriented baseline (BETR):** NAG outperforms BETR by 1.0% on average (49.8% vs. 48.8%), with the largest deltas on ARC-C (+1.7%, 34.0% vs. 32.3%) and HellaSwag (+3.1%, 60.6% vs. 57.5%). BETR leads on XStoryCloze (+1.0%, 71.0% vs. 70.0%) and XWinograd (+0.6%, 80.7% vs. 80.1%), while the two are approximately tied on MMLU (BETR: 31.1%, NAG: 32.2%). The paper hypothesizes that NAG's advantage stems from its aggregation of neuron-level signals across all layers rather than relying on last-layer embeddings, which BETR uses and which can conflate surface features with task-relevant structure.

**Consistency across extraction backbones:** NAG instantiated with Llama-3.2-3B achieves 49.6% average (+4.7% over Random), and with SmolLM3-3B achieves 49.9% (+5.0% over Random). The range of variation across backbones is only 0.3% on average, indicating that the method captures general properties of Transformer computation rather than model-specific artifacts. Individual benchmark variation is modest: HellaSwag ranges from 58.6% (Llama) to 60.6% (Qwen), a 2.0% spread, while ARC-C is essentially identical at 34.0%–35.0% across all three backbones.

**Benchmark-specific patterns:** The gains over Random are not uniform — they are largest on HellaSwag (+9.0% with Qwen) and smallest on MMLU (+2.0% with Qwen). The paper attributes this to the nature of the benchmarks: HellaSwag requires specific commonsense reasoning patterns that are well-captured by neuron-level activation overlap, while MMLU's broad multi-domain coverage makes it harder for any single-target selection to differentially improve all subdomains. TriviaQA shows the second-largest gain (+6.7%), consistent with factual retrieval having a distinct computational signature that NAG can isolate from general web text.

**Performance under aggressive filtering (Figure 4):** When the filtering rate `r_f` is varied from 5% to 20% on HellaSwag with Qwen3-1.7B-Base as the extraction backbone, NAG's accuracy increases from 60.5% at `r_f = 20%` to 62.3% at `r_f = 5%` — a 1.8% boost from more aggressive filtering. In contrast, Random selection degrades from 51.6% to 50.9% as the filtering rate decreases, and BETR also shows a slight degradation (−0.5%). This establishes that NAG's ranking is utility-aligned: removing lower-ranked samples improves rather than hurts performance, meaning NAG can identify not just relevant data but also *irrelevant* data within the selected pool.

#### Multi-Target Data Selection: NAG Remains Effective Where BETR Degrades

Table 1 (Multi-Target section) reports results when a single 30B-token corpus is constructed by independently selecting 5B tokens for each of the six benchmarks and mixing them without re-weighting. The key finding is that **NAG with Qwen3-1.7B-Base achieves 48.5% average accuracy in the multi-target setting (+3.6% over Random's 44.9%)**, while BETR achieves only 44.4% (−0.5% below Random). This 4.1% gap between NAG and BETR in the multi-target setting is substantially larger than the 1.0% gap in the single-target setting.

**BETR's multi-target degradation is severe on specific benchmarks:** BETR drops below Random on three benchmarks in the multi-target setting — TriviaQA falls from 15.6% (Random) to 11.6% (−4.0%), HellaSwag drops from 51.6% to 49.3% (−2.3%), and MMLU drops from 30.2% to 29.9% (−0.3%). The paper attributes this to embedding-space interference: when BETR's classifier is trained on embeddings from six diverse tasks, the compressed representation space cannot cleanly separate the distinct target signals, causing the selection to degrade. In contrast, NAG maintains positive gains over Random on all six benchmarks in the multi-target setting, with only XStoryCloze dropping from +2.9% gain (single-target) to +2.2% gain (multi-target) — a modest reduction.

**Backbone consistency in the multi-target setting is slightly reduced:** NAG with Llama-3.2-3B achieves 47.6% (+2.7% over Random) and with SmolLM3-3B achieves 47.8% (+2.9%), compared to 48.5% for Qwen3-1.7B (+3.6%). The spread across backbones in the multi-target setting is 0.9%, larger than the 0.3% spread in single-target but still modest. The paper explicitly notes that the simple equal-budget mixture strategy represents a lower bound for multi-target performance, and that more sophisticated mixture methods like RegMix (Liu et al., 2025b) or QuaDMix (Liu et al., 2025a) could further improve results.

#### NAG Combines Additively with Existing Quality Signals

Table 2 reports results of integrating NAG-based ranking with the FineWeb-Edu quality classifier. The key finding is that **combining NAG with FineWeb-Edu yields an average improvement of 1.8% over FineWeb-Edu alone** (averaging 49.0%–49.6% across the three backbones vs. FineWeb-Edu's 47.4%). The best combination — NAG with SmolLM3-3B + FineWeb-Edu — reaches 49.6%, compared to 49.9% for NAG alone with the same backbone.

**The gains are additive rather than redundant:** On ARC-C, FineWeb-Edu alone achieves 34.3%, NAG alone achieves 34.0% (with Qwen3-1.7B), but the combination achieves 35.3–35.7% — exceeding both individual methods. On HellaSwag, FineWeb-Edu achieves 55.3%, NAG alone achieves 60.6%, but the combination achieves 57.7–58.1% — still better than FineWeb-Edu alone, though below NAG alone because HellaSwag is a benchmark where NAG's target-specific signal dominates the general-quality signal. On MMLU, where FineWeb-Edu outperforms NAG alone, the combination achieves 33.1% (with SmolLM3-3B) vs. FineWeb-Edu's 32.8% and NAG alone's 31.2% — the combination recovers the best of both signals. This demonstrates that NAG captures information complementary to general-quality scores — target-specific computational alignment and general text quality are largely orthogonal dimensions of data utility.

#### Compute Efficiency: NAG Achieves Substantial Compute Multipliers

Figure 7 (Appendix F.1) reports compute multipliers — the ratio of training compute required to reach a given accuracy using baseline-selected vs. NAG-selected data. The average compute multiplier of NAG over Random is 2.42× (i.e., NAG reaches the same accuracy using 41% of the training compute), over BETR is 1.64×, and over FineWeb-Edu is 1.27×. These multipliers vary across benchmarks: on XStoryCloze, NAG achieves a 3.70× multiplier over Random, on HellaSwag 2.65×, and on ARC-C 3.26×. The multipliers are computed by comparing training curves (accuracy vs. training steps) and identifying the step count at which the baseline matches NAG's final accuracy, then computing the ratio of step counts.

#### Scaling Experiments: NAG Transfers Across Model Sizes

Appendix I reports two scaling experiments on HellaSwag. When scaling the trained model from 1.2B to 7B (with the training budget scaled from 30B to 100B tokens selected from a 500B RefinedWeb pool), NAG achieves +8.4% over Random (71.4% vs. 63.0%), comparable to the +9.0% gain at 1.2B scale (Table 13). When using a *smaller* extraction model (Qwen3-0.6B, which is smaller than the 1.2B trained model), NAG still achieves 59.9% on HellaSwag, outperforming both BETR (57.5%) and FineWeb-Edu (55.3%) (Table 14). This shows that NAG does not require the extraction model to be larger than the trained model — the neuron-activation patterns from a smaller reference model still capture task-discriminative signals that transfer to a larger model's training.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Neuron deactivation: validating that NAG captures causally critical neurons (Table 3, Appendix B).** Deactivating NAG-selected neurons — 20 per layer, approximately 0.12% of all model parameters in Qwen3-1.7B-Base — causes a 23.5% average accuracy collapse across the six benchmarks (from 60.6% to 37.1%). Deactivating an equal number of randomly selected neurons causes a 0.2% drop (from 60.6% to 60.4%). The per-benchmark drops are severe and uneven: TriviaQA collapses from 36.3% to 0.3% (−36.0%), XWinograd from 86.5% to 60.6% (−25.9%), and ARC-C from 55.7% to 30.4% (−25.3%). A fine-grained ablation deactivating only 28 neurons (0.006% of parameters) selected by the largest difference in mean impact scores between target examples and random inputs ("High-Δ") causes a 17.8% average drop, while deactivating neurons selected by High-Mean impact causes only a 0.3% drop — indicating that discriminative neurons (those with large impact differences between target and non-target inputs) are more critical than consistently high-impact neurons (Appendix B.3).

**Neuron type: UP projections dominate (Figure 5).** On HellaSwag, NAG constructed from UP projection neurons achieves 60.6% accuracy (with Qwen3-1.7B-Base, K=20), while DOWN projection yields 58.0%, O projection 59.4%, V projection 59.2%, Q projection 58.7%, and K projection 56.7%. The gap between UP (best) and K (worst) is 3.9 percentage points. The paper hypothesizes that expansion layers like UP projection operate in a higher-dimensional latent space (`d_internal = 6144` vs. `d_model = 2048`) that better isolates task-specific signals, while projection layers closer to the residual stream capture more compressed, mixed information.

**Layer coverage: all layers substantially outperform last-layer only (Table 4, Section 4.2.2).** Restricting NAG to the final layer of Qwen3-1.7B-Base causes a 4.1% average performance drop (from 49.8% to 45.7%), with per-benchmark drops ranging from 2.2% (XStoryCloze: 70.0% → 67.8%) to 7.2% (TriviaQA: 22.3% → 15.1%). On TriviaQA and MMLU, the final-layer variant achieves only 15.1% and 29.9% — actually underperforming Random selection's 15.6% and 30.2% on TriviaQA by 0.5%. This demonstrates that task-relevant signals are distributed across model depth and that final-layer-only methods like BETR's embedding similarity miss critical information, particularly for knowledge-intensive tasks.

**Neuron sparsity: performance peaks at `r_k = 0.3%` across model scales (Figure 6, Section 4.2.3).** Sweeping the layerwise neuron ratio `r_k` across the Qwen3-Base family (1.7B, 4B, 8B) on HellaSwag shows consistent behavior: accuracy rises sharply from near-random at `r_k ≈ 0` to peak at `r_k ≈ 0.3%`, then plateaus or slightly declines for larger `r_k`. At `r_k = 0.3%`, Qwen3-1.7B achieves 60.6%, 4B achieves approximately 59.8%, and 8B achieves approximately 60.0%. At `r_k = 2.1%` (7× more neurons than the optimal setting), Qwen3-1.7B achieves comparable performance to `r_k = 0.3%` — no further gain from including more neurons. This confirms that task-relevant signals are concentrated in a very sparse set of high-impact neurons and that including lower-impact neurons dilutes the discriminative signal.

**Target set size sensitivity: NAG stabilizes with as few as 200 examples (Table 12, Appendix H).** On HellaSwag, varying the target set size from 200 to 5,000 examples and measuring ranking consistency with Spearman rank correlation and Jaccard overlap of top-20% selections: Spearman ρ ≥ 0.999 across all sizes (both intra-size across 5 random draws and cross-size relative to the full 10k-example setting). Even with only 200 target samples, the top-20% selected data overlaps 94% with the full 10k setting. This demonstrates that NAG's target profile stabilizes with a very small number of examples, making the method practical for domains with limited labeled data.

**Scaling behavior: NAG transfers across training and extraction model scales (Tables 13–14, Appendix I).** At 7B training scale with 100B tokens, NAG achieves 71.4% vs. Random's 63.0% on HellaSwag (+8.4%), comparable to the +9.0% at 1.2B scale. Using Qwen3-0.6B (smaller than the 1.2B trained model) as the extraction backbone, NAG achieves 59.9% on HellaSwag, outperforming both BETR (57.5%) and FineWeb-Edu (55.3%). This establishes that NAG does not require the extraction model to match or exceed the trained model in scale — smaller, cheaper extraction models can still provide effective neuron-level signals.

**Task discriminability predicts downstream utility (Table 11, Appendix G).** When NAG width `K` is varied (5, 20, 40), clustering quality metrics (Purity, NMI, ARI) computed on ten diverse datasets correlate with downstream HellaSwag data selection performance: K=20 achieves the best clustering (Purity 0.973, NMI 0.945, ARI 0.946) and the best accuracy (60.6%), while K=5 achieves worse clustering (Purity 0.878, NMI 0.876, ARI 0.797) and worse accuracy (58.0%). This correlation provides a lightweight proxy for NAG configuration selection — clustering quality can be evaluated without training — and validates that NAG's data selection utility is mediated by its ability to form task-discriminative representations.

**Impact score validation: local proxy correlates strongly with behavioral importance (Appendix C).** On Qwen3-1.7B-Base with 500 samples, the Pearson correlation between binned neuron impact scores (grouped into 122 bins of 50 neurons each) and the resulting loss change upon deactivation is +0.71 ± 0.02. Deactivating the top 0.8% of neurons causes 159× more loss change than deactivating mid-rank neurons, validating that the computationally cheap local impact metric (`|h_in^T W_{:,k}|`) correctly identifies neurons with disproportionate effects on model behavior.

**Run-to-run variance: gains substantially exceed training noise (Table 15, Appendix J).** Over 5 independent pretraining runs with Random selection, the standard deviation ranges from 0.18% (MMLU) to 0.55% (XWinograd), with an average of approximately 0.4%. NAG's average gain across three backbones is +4.9% — more than 10× the typical run-to-run standard deviation, confirming that the reported improvements are not artifacts of training variance.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "NAG-based Ranking improves target-oriented pretraining by 4.9% on average over random sampling"

This claim is **well-supported but its generalizability beyond the specific configuration tested is unknown**. The evidence is clear within the paper's experimental setup: across six diverse benchmarks with Qwen3-1.7B as the extraction backbone, NAG achieves 49.8% vs. 44.9% for Random (Table 1), and the 4.7%–5.0% range across three extraction backbones confirms consistency. The binomial standard errors reported in Table 16 confirm that gains exceed statistical noise on every benchmark.

However, several factors qualify the strength of this claim for practitioners. First, all experiments train a 1.2B model on exactly 30B tokens with a specific learning rate schedule and architecture — the paper provides a single 7B scaling point on HellaSwag (+8.4% over Random, Table 13), but the 4.9% average figure is specific to the 1.2B/30B regime. Whether the gain holds, increases, or diminishes at larger scales (e.g., 70B models, 1T tokens) is not demonstrated. The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly and frames the 7B result as "preliminary evidence" — which it is, since it is a single benchmark, a single seed, and a single extraction backbone. Second, all experiments use RefinedWeb as the sole candidate pool — a high-quality, English-only web corpus. NAG's effectiveness on noisier corpora (raw CommonCrawl), multilingual data, or domain-specific corpora (scientific papers, code repositories) is not tested. Third, the 4.9% figure averages across benchmarks with very different gain profiles — +9.0% on HellaSwag but only +2.0% on MMLU — and a practitioner targeting MMLU specifically would see a more modest improvement.

What genuinely strengthens the claim is the filtering-rate experiment (Figure 4): that NAG accuracy *increases* with more aggressive filtering while baselines *decrease* establishes that NAG's ranking is utility-aligned, not just lucky at a single threshold. This provides internal evidence that the selection mechanism is capturing something real about data utility rather than exploiting a coincidental ordering at `r_f = 20%`.

#### Claim 2: "NAG outperforms state-of-the-art baselines by 5.3% accuracy on HellaSwag"

This claim is **valid but carefully scoped to HellaSwag, which is the benchmark where NAG shows its largest advantage**. The 5.3% figure is NAG (Qwen3-1.7B, 60.6%) minus FineWeb-Edu (55.3%) on HellaSwag (Table 1). Against BETR on HellaSwag, the advantage is 3.1% (60.6% vs. 57.5%). These are real, statistically significant gaps. However, HellaSwag is also the benchmark where all methods show their largest absolute scores and gains — it is the "sweet spot" benchmark for this setup, likely because HellaSwag's commonsense reasoning has a distinct computational signature that neuron-level analysis captures particularly well, and because the 1.2B/30B training regime is far from saturation on HellaSwag (leaving room for data selection to matter).

On other benchmarks, the advantages are smaller or sometimes absent: on MMLU, FineWeb-Edu (32.8%) slightly outperforms NAG (32.2%), and on XStoryCloze and XWinograd, BETR slightly outperforms NAG. The 5.3% figure is the *maximum* advantage on the *best* benchmark, not the typical advantage. A fairer headline would be "1.0% average improvement over BETR" (49.8% vs. 48.8%) or "2.4% average improvement over FineWeb-Edu" (49.8% vs. 47.4%). The paper does not misrepresent this — the 5.3% is correctly attributed to HellaSwag — but readers should not interpret it as the typical gain.

A missing comparison weakens the claim against BETR specifically: BETR's original paper likely tunes its classifier hyperparameters (number of training epochs, classifier architecture, pseudo-label construction). The NAG paper states it follows "the original setups of baseline papers" (Section 3.4), but BETR may have been optimized for a different candidate pool, different model scale, or different benchmarks — there may exist a BETR configuration that performs better on this specific setup than the one tested. The fair comparison would require sweeping BETR hyperparameters on the same setup, but the paper understandably uses the published defaults.

#### Claim 3: "NAG remains effective under a more applicable multi-target setting, where our best setup surpasses two baselines by 1.1% and 4.1%"

This claim is **supported with evidence but the multi-target setting is a deliberately simplified lower bound, which means the 4.1% gap over BETR is robust but the 1.1% over FineWeb-Edu may not be the best achievable**. NAG (Qwen3-1.7B) achieves 48.5% in the multi-target setting vs. FineWeb-Edu's 47.4% (+1.1%) and BETR's 44.4% (+4.1%) — Table 1. The large gap over BETR is genuinely informative: it demonstrates that embedding-based similarity degrades substantially when multiple diverse targets are mixed with a naive equal-budget strategy, while neuron-level similarity is more robust. This is a real advantage of NAG for practical multi-objective deployment.

However, the 1.1% over FineWeb-Edu should be interpreted cautiously. FineWeb-Edu is a general-quality method — it does not try to align with any specific targets, so its performance in the multi-target setting is essentially its single-target performance (since it selects the same data regardless of target). The fact that NAG only beats it by 1.1% suggests that when multiple targets are naively mixed, NAG's target-specific advantage is partially diluted. More sophisticated mixture strategies (which the paper explicitly leaves to future work) might recover larger gains. Similarly, comparing against a hypothetical "FineWeb-Edu adapted for multi-target" is not possible — FineWeb-Edu has no target-adaptation mechanism — so 1.1% over it is a conservative but narrow advantage.

The more serious limitation is that the multi-target evaluation does not test what happens when targets are partially overlapping (e.g., selecting data for both HellaSwag and XStoryCloze, which both test commonsense reasoning, vs. mathematical reasoning and factual QA, which have less overlap). The equal-budget mixture treats all six benchmarks identically, which likely leads to some benchmarks being over-represented in the data budget relative to their actual importance. A weighted mixture or a joint selection that optimizes across all targets simultaneously would be a stronger test of NAG's multi-target capability, and its absence is a genuine gap.

#### Claim 4: "Deactivating NAG-selected neurons (only 0.12% of all) causes a 23.5% performance collapse"

This claim is **strongly supported and is arguably the most convincing evidence in the paper**. The deactivation experiment (Table 3) provides causal evidence that NAG isolates genuinely critical neurons — not just correlated or epiphenomenal ones. The random-neuron control (0.2% drop vs. 23.5% drop) is a clean counterfactual, and the fine-grained High-Δ vs. High-Mean ablation (Appendix B.3, 17.8% vs. 0.3% drop) further distinguishes discriminative from merely active neurons. The number of neurons deactivated (20 per layer, 560 total out of ~172,000 UP projection neurons across 28 layers) is genuinely tiny — 0.12% — yet the effect is massive.

Two qualifications are worth noting. First, the deactivation is performed on the extraction model (Qwen3-1.7B-Base), not the trained 1.2B model. This demonstrates that the neurons NAG identifies in the extraction model are critical for that model's task performance — but it does not directly prove that these same neurons are the ones that matter during downstream pretraining. It is possible (though unlikely given the method's effectiveness) that NAG selects neurons that are critical for the extraction model's processing of target inputs but that train different circuitry in the downstream model. The transfer from extraction-model criticality to downstream-model training utility is an assumption that the deactivation experiment supports indirectly but does not test directly.

Second, the 23.5% figure is averaged across six benchmarks, but the per-benchmark drops are highly uneven: TriviaQA collapses by 36.0% while MMLU drops by 16.8%. This variance is interesting — it suggests NAG captures some tasks' computational signatures more completely than others — but it also means the "23.5%" headline should not be interpreted as uniform across all capabilities. For TriviaQA specifically, NAG-selected neurons appear to be *essentially the only neurons the model uses* for that task (a 36.0% drop to near-zero accuracy), while for MMLU, the model retains substantial capability (29.1% remaining) after deactivating NAG neurons, suggesting MMLU relies on more distributed or different circuitry.

#### What experiments would strengthen the paper

Several experiments are notably absent and would significantly strengthen the claims:

**A direct causal test that NAG-selected data improves the intended circuitry:** The deactivation experiment shows NAG identifies critical neurons in the extraction model, but there is no experiment showing that models trained on NAG-selected data actually develop stronger or more task-specific neural circuits compared to models trained on baseline-selected data. A probing or circuit-analysis experiment on the trained models would close this loop and provide mechanistic evidence for the paper's core hypothesis — that neuron-level data selection leads to better *learned representations*, not just better benchmark scores.

**A baseline that combines BETR with multi-layer embeddings:** The paper argues that BETR's limitation is its reliance on last-layer embeddings. A natural ablation would be to run BETR using concatenated embeddings from all layers (or a learned weighted combination) rather than only the final layer, to determine whether the performance gap is due to the *neuron-level* nature of NAG's signal or simply due to the *cross-layer aggregation*. If multi-layer BETR approaches NAG's performance, then the innovation is primarily in the aggregation rather than the neuron-level representation; if it does not, the neuron-level representation itself is the key driver.

**A direct evaluation on domain-transfer scenarios:** The paper's Figure 1 motivates NAG with the economics-to-math transfer example — documents from different domains activating similar neurons because they share reasoning structure. But there is no experiment that explicitly tests this: e.g., selecting data for a mathematical reasoning target from a candidate pool that includes economics text, physics text, cooking recipes, etc., and measuring whether NAG preferentially selects cross-domain documents that genuinely improve math performance. This is the most conceptually interesting claim in the paper's motivation, and it is not directly tested.

**Larger-scale experiments with statistical rigor:** The single 7B experiment on HellaSwag (Appendix I) is encouraging but insufficient to establish scaling behavior — it is one benchmark, one seed, one extraction backbone. A proper scaling study with 3–5 model scales, multiple benchmarks, and multiple seeds would be needed to determine whether the 4.9% average gain holds, grows, or shrinks at scale. The paper acknowledges this limitation, but it is a significant gap for practitioners considering adopting NAG for large-scale training.

**A computational cost comparison that includes extraction overhead in the compute multiplier:** The paper reports compute multipliers (Figure 7) that compare training cost only, not the cost of NAG extraction (192 GPU-hours on H100). For the 1.2B/30B training setup, this extraction cost is likely small relative to the training cost it enables, but for much larger training runs, the extraction cost on a full candidate pool could become non-trivial. A breakeven analysis — at what training scale does NAG's extraction cost become negligible relative to the compute savings from better data selection — would substantially strengthen the practical argument for adoption.

Despite these gaps, the core experimental evidence for the paper's central contributions is solid. NAG consistently outperforms both general-quality and target-oriented baselines across diverse benchmarks and extraction backbones, the deactivation experiment provides genuine causal evidence for the functional backbone concept, and the ablation studies (neuron type, layer coverage, sparsity) systematically validate the design choices while providing diagnostic insight into why they work. The multi-target robustness and additive combination with existing quality signals demonstrate practical deployability beyond idealized single-target settings. The main weaknesses are in external validity (single model scale, single corpus, single training regime) and in the absence of experiments that would directly test the paper's most conceptually interesting claim — that neuron-level similarity captures cross-domain computational structure that embedding similarity misses.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Overhead Is Not Accounted for in the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** NAG-based Ranking requires extracting neuron impact scores and constructing NAGs for every candidate document in the pretraining pool — a forward pass through a reference LLM (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B) for each document, recording intermediate activations at every UP projection layer, computing impact magnitudes, and selecting top-K neurons. The paper reports that this extraction takes 192 GPU-hours on H100-SXM-80GB for the 150B-token RefinedWeb pool used in the 1.2B/30B training setup (Appendix F.2). While the paper characterizes this as a "one-time cost" that is "target-independent" and therefore amortized across all target tasks, the primary comparison in Table 1 reports NAG's accuracy gains *without including this extraction cost in any compute budget or efficiency metric*. The compute multipliers in Figure 7 compare training FLOPs only — they measure how much less training compute NAG-selected data requires to reach a given accuracy, but do not account for the extraction compute that enabled the selection.

As the paper states in Appendix F.2:

> "NAG extraction requires only a single forward pass per document... and is embarrassingly parallel across documents. For our 150B token pool with Qwen3-1.7B-Base as the extraction model, NAG extraction takes 192 GPU-hours on H100-SXM-80GB, which is acceptable compared to the cost of model pretraining itself."

**The consequence.** The "acceptable" claim depends critically on the ratio of extraction cost to training cost, which the paper does not analyze. For the 1.2B/30B setup used in the main experiments, the extraction cost (192 GPU-hours) is likely small relative to training — but the paper provides no breakeven analysis to confirm this. For much larger training runs (e.g., training a 70B model on 1T selected tokens from a 5T-token candidate pool), the extraction cost scales linearly with the candidate pool size, while the training cost scales with the selected token count. If practitioners need to extract NAGs from multi-trillion-token candidate pools, the extraction cost could become a substantial fraction of total compute, potentially eroding or eliminating the reported efficiency gains.

Additionally, the extraction cost is paid *before* any target is specified, which means that for practitioners who only have a single target task, the amortization argument does not apply — the full extraction cost must be justified by gains on that one task. The paper does not provide a single-target breakeven analysis: at what training scale (model size × tokens) does the accuracy gain from NAG-based selection offset the 192 GPU-hours of extraction for a single benchmark like HellaSwag?

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Appendix F.2 provides the 192 GPU-hours figure and notes that "smaller extraction models can further reduce this cost: as shown in Sec. I, NAG extracted with Qwen3-0.6B-Base still outperforms all baselines." Appendix I shows that with Qwen3-0.6B (smaller than the 1.2B trained model), NAG achieves 59.9% on HellaSwag vs. 60.6% with Qwen3-1.7B — a 0.7% drop for a substantially cheaper extraction. However, the paper does not report the extraction cost for Qwen3-0.6B, nor does it report a compute multiplier that includes extraction overhead. The computation of compute multipliers in Appendix F.1 and Figure 7 explicitly excludes extraction cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges and partially side-steps this limitation. It notes that extraction is "a one-time cost" and "target-independent," which amortizes it across many targets — a valid argument for multi-target deployments. It demonstrates that a smaller extraction model (Qwen3-0.6B) substantially reduces cost with minimal accuracy loss. It mentions "further cost reductions are possible by (1) optimizing forward-pass throughput and GPU utilization during extraction, and (2) adopting a coarse-to-fine selection scheme, where a small extraction model performs an initial filtering pass and a larger extraction model is only applied to the shortlisted candidates" (Appendix F.2). However, none of these cost-reduction strategies are implemented or evaluated, and the headline 4.9% gain and 2.42× compute multiplier over Random do not include extraction cost. The limitation is acknowledged but not resolved.

---

### All Results Are on a Single Corpus, a Single Training Regime, and a Single Model Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** Every main experiment in the paper uses the same pretraining data pipeline: a 150B-token subset of RefinedWeb as the candidate pool, a 1.2B-parameter Transformer trained from scratch on exactly 30B selected tokens, with fixed architecture (24 layers, `d_model=2048`, 16 heads), fixed optimizer settings (AdamW with specific `β` values, weight decay 0.1), and a fixed cosine decay schedule from `5 × 10^{-4}` to `5 × 10^{-5}` (Appendix A.1, Table 5). The benchmarks are English-only reasoning and knowledge tasks (Table 6). The paper provides a single scaling experiment: 7B model, 100B tokens from a 500B RefinedWeb pool, HellaSwag only, Qwen3-1.7B extraction backbone only (Appendix I, Table 13). As the paper states in Section 6:

> "Our main experiments train a 1.2B model on 30B tokens from RefinedWeb; extending to larger models and more diverse corpora (e.g., multilingual or domain-specific data) is left for future work."

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering NAG for their own pretraining pipeline cannot determine from this paper whether the 4.9% average gain will transfer to their setting. Several specific uncertainties arise:

- **Corpus dependence:** RefinedWeb is a high-quality, English-only, heavily filtered web corpus. NAG's effectiveness on noisier corpora (raw CommonCrawl, which contains substantial non-natural-language text, boilerplate, and low-quality content) is unknown. The neuron-activation patterns that distinguish task-relevant from task-irrelevant documents in clean data may be swamped by noise in dirtier corpora, where spurious neuron activations from formatting artifacts, code snippets, or toxic content dominate the top-K selection.

- **Training scale dependence:** The 1.2B/30B setting is a relatively small-scale pretraining run. At larger scales (e.g., 70B parameters, 1T+ tokens), both the optimal filtering rate and the optimal sparsity ratio `r_k` may shift — larger models may have more distributed functional backbones (requiring higher `r_k`), and larger token budgets may benefit from less aggressive filtering (lower `r_f`) to maintain data diversity. The single 7B experiment on HellaSwag (+8.4% over Random vs. +9.0% at 1.2B) is suggestive that the gain persists but is insufficient to establish scaling behavior, since it is a single benchmark, a single scale point, and no baseline comparisons (BETR, FineWeb-Edu) are provided at 7B.

- **Multilingual and domain-specific corpora:** All benchmarks are English; the candidate pool is English web text. NAG's neuron signals are extracted from English-pretrained models (Qwen3, Llama, SmolLM3). Whether the method transfers to multilingual settings (where neuron representations for the same capability may differ across languages) or to domain-specific corpora (scientific papers, code, legal documents) is entirely untested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The explicit limitation statement in Section 6 is the primary acknowledgment. The 7B experiment (Table 13) provides one data point but is insufficient — it confirms NAG does not catastrophically break at larger scale but does not characterize how gains change with scale. The paper's backbone-dependence experiment (Table 1, three different extraction models) is a form of robustness check but operates within the same candidate pool and training regime.

**Mitigation status.** The limitation is explicitly acknowledged and deferred to future work. The 7B experiment is framed as "preliminary evidence" and "encouraging" but is explicitly not a full scaling study. The paper proposes no specific methodology for transferring NAG hyperparameters (`r_k`, `r_f`, neuron type) to new corpora or scales — a practitioner would need to replicate the hyperparameter sweeps (Figures 5, 6) on their own data, which requires training multiple models from scratch, partially defeating the purpose of a training-free selection method.

### NAG Requires a Pretrained Reference LLM with Accessible Internal Activations

**The assumption or constraint.** NAG-based Ranking fundamentally depends on having access to an off-the-shelf LLM whose internal activations at projection layers can be recorded, whose weight matrices are accessible for column-wise impact computation, and whose architecture uses standard Transformer components (the paper's neuron definition applies specifically to "projection layers in both Attention (Q, K, V) and FFN (UP, DOWN) modules," Section 2.1). The paper uses three open-weight models (Qwen3-1.7B, Llama-3.2-3B, SmolLM3-3B) as extraction backbones, and demonstrates that a smaller model (Qwen3-0.6B, Appendix I) also works.

However, this requirement excludes several deployment scenarios that are common in practice:

- **API-only models:** The most capable LLMs at any given time often exist only behind APIs (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini). Practitioners who want to select pretraining data for capabilities that are best represented in these frontier models cannot use NAG because they cannot access internal activations or weight matrices. The paper does not discuss whether NAG signals could be distilled from API-accessible outputs (e.g., using token probabilities as proxies for neuron impact) or whether the method is fundamentally tied to open-weight models.

- **Non-standard architectures:** The neuron definition (column of a projection weight matrix) is specific to standard dense Transformer layers. Architectures using mixture-of-experts (MoE), linear attention, state-space models (Mamba), or other non-standard components would require a different impact quantification method. The paper does not discuss architectural generalizability.

- **Capability mismatch:** The extraction model must be capable enough that the target capability is represented in its neurons. If the target is a capability that the extraction model genuinely lacks (e.g., a small open-weight model may not have strong mathematical reasoning), then the neurons it uses to process target inputs may not reflect the computational signature of that capability — the extraction model might process math problems using generic language circuits rather than specialized reasoning circuits, and NAG would select data that looks like math problems but does not train reasoning.

**The consequence.** For organizations whose primary LLM deployment is via API, or who are targeting capabilities that are best represented in frontier models, NAG is not directly applicable. The paper demonstrates that a 0.6B extraction model works for a 1.2B trained model (Appendix I) on HellaSwag, which suggests the extraction model can be smaller than the trained model. But HellaSwag is a commonsense reasoning task that is well-represented even in small models — it does not test the scenario where the target capability (e.g., advanced mathematical theorem proving, complex multi-step planning) is absent from the available extraction models. In such cases, the extraction model's NAGs for target inputs would capture whatever fallback processing strategy the model uses (likely shallow pattern matching), and the selected data would train that shallow strategy rather than the intended deep capability.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates robustness across extraction model families (Table 1, Qwen3, Llama, SmolLM3) and scales (Appendix I, 0.6B vs. 1.7B). The consistent performance across backbones suggests that NAG captures universal properties of Transformer computation rather than model-specific artifacts — but this consistency is demonstrated on benchmarks (HellaSwag, ARC-C, etc.) where all three backbones have non-trivial capability. The capability-mismatch case is not directly tested: there is no experiment where the extraction model performs near chance on the target but NAG-selected data still improves the trained model (which would suggest NAG can bootstrap from weak extraction models). The paper shows that NAG works when extraction models are *competent* at the target, not when they are *incompetent*.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the broader dependency on an accessible LLM indirectly (the method is described as using "any off-the-shelf LLM," Section 1), but does not explicitly discuss API-only deployment as a limitation or propose workarounds. The small-extraction-model experiment (Qwen3-0.6B, Appendix I) partially mitigates the concern by showing that very small models suffice — and small models are more likely to be open-weight and locally deployable. However, the capability-mismatch case is not addressed, and there is no guidance on how to determine whether a given extraction model is "good enough" for a target without running the full pipeline.

### The Multi-Target Setting Is Evaluated Only Under a Naive Equal-Budget Mixture, and No Joint Optimization Across Targets Is Attempted

**The assumption or constraint.** In the multi-target setting (Section 3.3, results in Table 1), the paper independently selects `r_f / 6 = ~3.3%` of the candidate pool for each of the six benchmarks (5B tokens each from the 150B pool), then concatenates all six subsets to form the 30B training corpus. There is no deduplication across subsets, no re-weighting of benchmarks, and no joint optimization that considers trade-offs between targets. The paper explicitly frames this as a "lower bound":

> "Under this naive mixture, BETR exhibits a substantial performance drop compared to the single-target setting (-4.4%), suggesting that direct mixture poses a challenging scenario and can serve as a lower bound for the multi-target setting."

The paper further acknowledges in Section 6 that "The multi-target setting uses a simple equal-budget mixture as a lower-bound scenario, and more advanced mixture strategies (e.g., RegMix, QuaDMix) could further improve multi-target performance."

**The consequence.** The multi-target results in Table 1 represent a *lower bound* on NAG's multi-target performance, not an optimized deployment. The 1.1% advantage over FineWeb-Edu and 4.1% over BETR in the multi-target setting are measured against this naive mixture — but it is not known whether these advantages would grow or shrink under a more sophisticated mixture strategy. Specifically:

- **Fairness of comparison:** BETR degrades substantially in the multi-target setting (48.8% → 44.4%, a 4.4% drop), while NAG degrades modestly (49.8% → 48.5%, a 1.3% drop). The paper interprets this as evidence that NAG's neuron-level representations are more robust to multi-target interference. However, BETR was designed and tuned for single-target selection — its multi-target degradation may reflect a lack of multi-target optimization in its original design rather than a fundamental limitation of embedding-based similarity. A fairer comparison would allow BETR to use a multi-target mixture strategy optimized for its signal type, just as NAG could benefit from one optimized for neuron-level signals.

- **Upper bound unknown:** The paper does not report what NAG could achieve with optimal multi-target mixture weights — for example, using RegMix to learn the optimal data proportions from small-scale proxy runs. The 48.5% figure in the multi-target setting is well below NAG's single-target average of 49.8%, but it's unclear how much of this gap is due to the intrinsic difficulty of multi-target selection vs. the suboptimality of the equal-budget mixture. A practitioner who needs to optimize for multiple targets simultaneously cannot determine from this paper whether to use equal-budget NAG (simple but potentially suboptimal), to invest in mixture optimization (which might close most of the gap), or to use a different approach entirely.

- **Target interaction effects:** The six benchmarks are treated independently — but some benchmarks likely have overlapping computational requirements (e.g., HellaSwag and XStoryCloze both test narrative commonsense reasoning), while others are more distinct (e.g., TriviaQA is factual retrieval, ARC-C is scientific reasoning). An optimized multi-target strategy could exploit overlaps to reduce total data requirements or allocate budget where it provides the most marginal gain. The paper's equal-budget approach ignores these interactions, potentially both under-allocating to benchmarks where NAG excels (HellaSwag, +9.0%) and over-allocating to benchmarks where NAG's advantage is small (MMLU, +2.0%).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1's multi-target section provides the raw numbers. The paper explicitly acknowledges the limitation in Section 6. There is no ablation on different mixture strategies or target weightings.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is fully transparent that the multi-target results are a lower bound and that better mixture strategies could improve them. Section 6 explicitly cites RegMix (Liu et al., 2025b) and QuaDMix (Liu et al., 2025a) as future work. However, for a practitioner reading this paper, the transparency about the limitation does not resolve it — the paper provides no guidance on how to set multi-target weights for NAG, no analysis of how NAG's single-target gains correlate with optimal multi-target allocations, and no experiment demonstrating that NAG's multi-target performance *can* approach its single-target performance with a better mixture strategy.

### The Method Has Not Been Tested on Capabilities the Extraction Model Lacks, Nor on Regimes Where the Candidate Pool Contains Few Target-Relevant Documents

**The assumption or constraint.** NAG-based Ranking operates by identifying candidate documents whose neuron-activation patterns overlap with those of target examples, and selecting those with the highest overlap. This assumes (1) that the extraction model's neurons encode the target capability in a way that NAG can detect (the functional backbone is present and discriminative), and (2) that the candidate pool contains enough documents that activate this functional backbone to fill the selection budget (e.g., 30B tokens). The paper tests the method on six benchmarks — commonsense reasoning, factual QA, academic knowledge, narrative understanding — where both assumptions plausibly hold: the extraction models (Qwen3, Llama, SmolLM3) have non-trivial performance on these tasks, and the RefinedWeb candidate pool likely contains substantial text that exercises these capabilities (stories, trivia, educational articles).

The paper does not test two important failure modes:

- **Capability not in the extraction model:** If the target is a capability that the extraction model genuinely lacks — for example, a small model may not have systematic multi-step planning ability, advanced mathematical proof construction, or domain-specific reasoning (legal argumentation, medical diagnosis). In such cases, the extraction model still produces *some* neuron-activation pattern when processing target inputs, but this pattern reflects whatever fallback strategy the model uses (guessing, pattern matching, shallow heuristics) rather than the genuine computational signature of the capability. NAG would then select data that matches this fallback pattern — training the downstream model to replicate the extraction model's limitations rather than to acquire the target capability.

- **Candidate pool lacks target-relevant content:** If the candidate pool contains very few documents that genuinely exercise the target capability (e.g., selecting mathematical reasoning data from a corpus dominated by social media text and product reviews), NAG will still select the top `r_f = 20%` ranked by similarity. These documents will be the *most similar* available but may still be poor training data for the target — NAG cannot create target-relevant data where none exists. The paper's filtering-rate experiment (Figure 4) shows NAG accuracy *increases* as `r_f` decreases (from 20% to 5%), suggesting that within the selected pool, lower-ranked documents are indeed less useful. But the experiment starts from a 150B RefinedWeb pool that already contains substantial target-relevant text — it does not test a scenario where the pool is impoverished relative to the target.

**The consequence.** For a practitioner targeting a specialized or difficult capability, NAG could silently fail: the selection pipeline would complete successfully, producing a ranked list of candidate documents, but the selected data would not train the intended capability because (a) the extraction model's NAGs did not encode the capability, or (b) the candidate pool lacked documents that genuinely instantiate it. The trained model would underperform on the target, and the practitioner would have no diagnostic signal to distinguish "NAG failed to identify relevant data" from "the target is genuinely hard and requires more training compute." The existing experiments (all on capabilities where extraction models perform well and where RefinedWeb contains relevant content) do not provide evidence that NAG would fail gracefully — i.e., produce near-random accuracy — in these scenarios, nor that NAG's ranking scores could serve as a warning flag (e.g., if all candidate documents have uniformly low similarity to the target profile, indicating a pool mismatch).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly. The deactivation experiment (Table 3) shows that NAG identifies neurons critical for the extraction model's *existing* capabilities, not that NAG can bootstrap from a model that lacks the capability. The benchmark selection (commonsense reasoning, factual QA, academic knowledge) is explicitly chosen to overlap with RefinedWeb's content distribution. The paper does not include a "hard" target where the extraction model performs near chance, nor a candidate pool ablation where target-relevant content is systematically removed.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss these failure modes, does not propose diagnostics for detecting them (e.g., thresholding on the mean similarity score of selected documents to detect pool insufficiency), and does not suggest strategies for mitigating them (e.g., using multiple extraction models, augmenting the candidate pool with domain-specific sources, or combining NAG with content-based filters to ensure minimum target relevance). This is a significant gap for practitioners targeting specialized capabilities outside the scope of general web text and general-purpose LLMs.

### The Paper Demonstrates Correlation and Causality of NAG Neurons in the Extraction Model, but Does Not Establish That Training on NAG-Selected Data Produces Better Learned Representations

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's core mechanistic claim is that NAG identifies a sparse "functional backbone" — neurons that are causally critical for the extraction model's target task performance — and that selecting pretraining data which activates this same backbone will train a new model to develop stronger target capabilities. The paper provides strong evidence for the first half of this claim: the deactivation experiment (Table 3, Section 4.1.1) demonstrates that NAG-selected neurons (0.12% of parameters) are causally necessary for the extraction model's task performance, with deactivation causing a 23.5% accuracy collapse. The clustering experiment (Figure 3, Section 4.1.2) demonstrates that NAG representations are task-discriminative. The filtering-rate experiment (Figure 4, Section 4.1.3) demonstrates that NAG's rankings are utility-aligned — removing lower-ranked data improves downstream performance.

However, the paper does not provide direct evidence for the second half of the claim: that models trained on NAG-selected data actually develop neural circuits that more closely resemble the extraction model's functional backbone, compared to models trained on baseline-selected data. The only evidence is indirect: models trained on NAG-selected data achieve higher downstream accuracy (Table 1), from which the paper infers that the learned representations are better. But this inference could be explained by alternative mechanisms — for example, NAG might simply select data that is in-distribution for the target task (avoiding distribution shift), or that has higher signal-to-noise ratio (avoiding noisy or contradictory training examples), without specifically strengthening the identified functional backbone. The accuracy improvement would be real in either case, but the claim that NAG works *because it identifies computation-specific training data* would be unsupported.

**The consequence.** The paper's central conceptual contribution — reframing data selection from representation-matching to computational-signature-matching — rests on the premise that training on inputs which activate the same neurons as target inputs *causes* the model to develop those neurons' functionality more effectively. If this premise is false, and NAG's effectiveness is instead due to a simpler mechanism (e.g., NAG indirectly filters for in-domain data, and any reasonable in-domain filter would work equally well), then the conceptual reframing is not supported by the evidence. The paper would still have demonstrated a useful data selection method — but the *reason* it works would be different from the claimed reason, and the claimed advantages (cross-domain transfer, interpretability, multi-target robustness) would rest on weaker foundations.

This matters practically because if NAG's mechanism is primarily in-domain filtering, then its advantage over BETR (which also does in-domain filtering, via embedding similarity) should shrink or disappear in settings where embedding-based domain detection works well. The paper's results show NAG outperforming BETR consistently, which suggests the mechanism is not *just* in-domain filtering — but the paper does not explicitly rule out alternative explanations.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The strongest evidence for the computational-signature-matching mechanism is the backbone-consistency result (Table 1): NAG works with three different extraction backbones (Qwen3, Llama, SmolLM3) despite these models having different architectures, training data, and embedding spaces, suggesting that the selected data is aligned with something deeper than model-specific embeddings — i.e., computational patterns that are shared across Transformers trained on similar data. The cross-domain transfer example in Figure 1 (economics profit maximization → math function maximization) motivates the mechanism but is an illustrative example, not an experimental result — there is no experiment showing NAG preferentially selects cross-domain documents that genuinely transfer. The task-discriminative clustering (Figure 3) shows NAG separates benchmark data by task, which is consistent with computational-signature-matching but is also consistent with simpler mechanisms (e.g., vocabulary differences between benchmarks driving different neuron activations).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not directly address this limitation. It does not conduct probing experiments on trained models to determine whether NAG-selected data leads to more task-specific internal representations, does not compare the neuron-activation patterns of models trained on NAG-selected vs. baseline-selected data, and does not explicitly rule out alternative explanations for NAG's effectiveness. The interpretability analysis (Section 4) focuses on the extraction model (why NAG works *as a representation*) rather than on the trained model (why training on NAG-selected data works *as a training intervention*). This is a significant gap between the paper's mechanistic claims and its empirical validation.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a genuine **methodological pivot** in how the field approaches pretraining data selection — from representation-space similarity (what the model outputs) to computational-signature similarity (which neurons fire). This is not merely a better metric within the existing paradigm; it is a different paradigm altogether. The shift can be stated precisely: prior work asks "does this candidate document produce embeddings or losses that resemble those of my target examples?" while NAG asks "does this candidate document engage the same sparse neural circuitry that my target examples engage?" These are fundamentally different questions with different failure modes, different interpretability properties, and different robustness characteristics.

The magnitude of this shift is substantive but bounded. The paper does not claim that neuron-level signals replace all other forms of data selection — Section 3.6 explicitly demonstrates that NAG signals combine additively with general-quality classifiers (FineWeb-Edu), yielding 49.6% average vs. 47.4% for FineWeb-Edu alone, a +2.2% gain. This positions neuron-level signals as a **complementary axis** rather than a wholesale replacement: general quality filters establish a baseline, and NAG re-ranks within that baseline to align data with specific downstream capabilities. The practical implication is that data selection pipelines should be multi-signal — quality, domain relevance, *and* computational alignment each contribute non-redundant information about data utility.

What makes this pivot consequential is that it **resolves a tension in the prior literature that was invisible from within the representation-matching paradigm**. The paper's multi-target results (Table 1) show BETR degrading from 48.8% (single-target) to 44.4% (multi-target, a 4.4% drop), while NAG degrades only from 49.8% to 48.5% (a 1.3% drop). Under the representation-matching paradigm, this is puzzling — why would a similarity metric that works well for individual targets break when multiple targets are combined? The answer only becomes visible from the neuron-level perspective: embedding spaces are continuous and compressed, so signals from different tasks interfere destructively when mixed. Neuron sets are discrete and sparse — the top-K neurons for HellaSwag and the top-K neurons for TriviaQA may be largely disjoint, so their target profiles can coexist without interference. This explains why prior target-oriented methods showed inconsistent results across settings — they were tested on different implicit task mixtures, and their embedding-based signals handled multi-target interference with varying success.

The paper also **shifts the interpretability standard for data selection**. Prior methods produce scalar scores from black-box classifiers — if selection fails, there is no diagnostic pathway beyond retraining with different hyperparameters. NAG makes the selection criterion inspectable: you can look at *which specific neurons* drive the selection for a given target, verify their relevance via deactivation (Table 3), and refine the selection by changing which neurons you extract (Figures 5, 6). This transforms data selection from a trust-the-classifier exercise into a hypothesis-driven process — you can ask "is my selection capturing surface features or actual computational structure?" and get an answer by examining the selected neurons. The paper's clustering experiment (Figure 3) and the correlation between clustering quality and downstream utility (Table 11) provide a lightweight, training-free method for validating NAG configurations before committing to expensive pretraining runs, further lowering the barrier to adoption.

Certain research directions become **more attractive** in light of this work. The concept of a sparse functional backbone — 0.12% of neurons causally responsible for up to 36% of task performance (Table 3) — provides a concrete target for interpretability research: rather than trying to understand all neurons, focus on characterizing these high-impact, task-specific subsets. The cross-domain transfer hypothesis (Figure 1: economics profit maximization activating similar neurons to pure math problems) suggests that neuron-level similarity could identify training data that transfers across surface-form boundaries, a capability that embedding-based methods struggle with. The backbone-consistency result across three model families (Table 1, 49.6%–49.9% average, a 0.3% range) suggests that the functional organization of Transformer neurons has universal properties that can be exploited without model-specific tuning.

Conversely, certain directions become **less attractive**. The paper's finding that last-layer-only NAG underperforms all-layer NAG by 4.1% on average (Table 4), and actually underperforms random selection on TriviaQA, suggests that research on embedding-based data selection that uses exclusively final-layer representations is hitting a fundamental ceiling — the last layer simply does not contain enough task-discriminative information. The pattern of verifier over-optimization that the paper documents for aggressive search methods does not apply directly here (NAG is training-free), but the broader lesson — that black-box optimization of a proxy signal eventually exploits that signal rather than improving the true objective — applies to classifier-based data selection methods. BETR's multi-target degradation (44.4% vs. 48.8% single-target) is plausibly a form of proxy exploitation: the classifier learns to exploit embedding-space correlations that work for single targets but fail when multiple, conflicting correlations must be satisfied simultaneously.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct causal validation that NAG-selected data produces better learned representations.** The paper demonstrates that deactivating NAG-selected neurons in the *extraction model* causes a 23.5% collapse (Table 3), and that training on NAG-selected data improves downstream *accuracy* (Table 1). But the causal chain — NAG identifies computation-relevant data → training on that data strengthens those computations → the trained model develops better internal representations — has a missing link: the paper never examines the internal representations of models trained on NAG-selected vs. baseline-selected data. A strong follow-up would train two models (NAG-selected, Random-selected), extract their UP-projection neuron activations on target test examples, and measure whether the NAG-trained model shows (a) higher activation of the functional backbone neurons identified by the extraction model, (b) more strongly task-discriminative clustering in its own NAG space, and (c) greater overlap between its high-impact neurons and the extraction model's target profile. If NAG-selected data genuinely strengthens the intended circuitry, these measures should show systematic differences. If they do not — if NAG-trained models achieve higher accuracy without measurably different internal representations — that would force a revision of the paper's mechanistic claims and suggest that NAG works through a simpler mechanism (e.g., better data diversity, reduced noise) that is still valuable but conceptually different from the computational-signature-matching hypothesis.

**Cross-domain transfer: does NAG select data from different domains that genuinely transfers?** The paper's motivating Figure 1 shows an economics profit-maximization problem activating neurons similar to a pure-math maximization problem, implying NAG would select the economics text as training data for a math target. This is the most conceptually interesting claim in the paper, and **it is not directly tested anywhere in the experiments**. A strong follow-up would construct a controlled experiment: define a target task (e.g., function maximization problems from a math benchmark), create a candidate pool with documents from three categories — (a) in-domain math problems, (b) cross-domain problems with similar reasoning structure (economics optimization, physics energy minimization, engineering design constraints), and (c) unrelated text (fiction, sports, recipes) — and measure whether NAG preferentially ranks category (b) over category (c) and whether training on category (b) documents improves math performance beyond what would be expected from general language exposure. The comparison against BETR on this task would be particularly informative: if NAG selects category (b) documents that BETR ranks as low-similarity (because their embeddings differ from math text), it would directly validate the claim that neuron-level similarity captures computational structure that embedding similarity misses. If NAG does *not* show cross-domain transfer, that would not invalidate the method (it still works for in-domain selection) but would significantly narrow its claimed advantages.

**Characterizing how the functional backbone varies with extraction model capability across a difficulty gradient.** The paper shows NAG works with extraction models spanning 0.6B to 3B parameters (Appendix I) and across three model families (Table 1), but all extraction models tested have non-trivial accuracy on all target benchmarks. The critical unexamined case is: what happens when the extraction model is *not capable* at the target task? If NAG is applied using a model that solves math problems at near-chance accuracy, do the selected neurons still provide a useful signal for data selection, or does the signal degrade to random? A strong follow-up would systematically vary extraction model capability — e.g., by using a family of models at different training checkpoints (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% of full pretraining) — and measure how NAG's data selection quality (downstream accuracy of the trained model) varies with extraction model accuracy on the target. If NAG's signal quality degrades gracefully (roughly linearly with extraction model accuracy), it provides a practical guideline: use the best available extraction model, but even a weak one helps. If there is a cliff — NAG selection provides no benefit until the extraction model exceeds some minimum accuracy threshold — that defines a hard boundary condition for the method's applicability. This experiment would also test whether NAG can *bootstrap*: can a weak extraction model select data that trains a stronger model on the target, which could then serve as a better extraction model for the next iteration?

**Optimizing multi-target mixture weights for NAG to close the gap with single-target performance.** The paper's multi-target results (Table 1) use a naive equal-budget mixture and achieve 48.5% with Qwen3-1.7B, a 1.3% drop from the 49.8% single-target average. The paper explicitly cites RegMix (Liu et al., 2025b) and QuaDMix (Liu et al., 2025a) as methods that could improve this. A strong follow-up would apply RegMix to NAG's multi-target setting: run small-scale proxy training runs with varying target weights on NAG-selected subsets, fit a regression model predicting benchmark accuracy from mixture proportions, and deploy the optimized weights at the full 30B token scale. The key question is whether optimized mixture weights can recover most of the single-target performance — e.g., achieving 49.0%+ in the multi-target setting vs. 49.8% single-target average, indicating that multi-target NAG is not fundamentally limited by neuron-set interference but only by allocation suboptimality. If the optimized mixture substantially closes the gap, it validates NAG's claim to multi-target robustness (neuron sets for different tasks do not interfere, so the only challenge is allocation). If the gap persists even with optimal mixing, it suggests some inherent capacity limitation — training on six target-aligned data distributions simultaneously may dilute each one's effect regardless of mixture proportions.

**Extending NAG beyond Transformer projection neurons to other architectures and modalities.** The paper's neuron definition (column of a projection weight matrix) is specific to dense Transformer layers. As architectures diversify — mixture-of-experts, linear attention, state-space models, and eventually entirely different paradigms — the question of whether the neuron-level data selection principle generalizes becomes urgent. A concrete follow-up would implement NAG for a mixture-of-experts Transformer (e.g., Mixtral): define a neuron as a column in an expert's FFN UP projection, extract top-K neurons per expert per layer (with the additional dimension of *which expert* was activated for each token), and test whether the resulting NAGs (now including expert indices) maintain the task-discriminative and utility-aligned properties demonstrated in this paper. For modalities beyond text — e.g., vision Transformers where "neurons" are columns in patch-projection or attention layers — the same principle could be applied: characterize an image by which neurons in a pretrained vision model fire when processing it, and select pretraining images that activate similar neurons to target task images (e.g., medical images, satellite imagery). A negative result — NAG's principles fail to transfer to a specific architecture or modality — would be equally informative, defining the boundary conditions of the computational-signature-matching paradigm.

**Comparative analysis: NAG neurons vs. probing-based feature importance vs. gradient-based attribution for data selection.** NAG selects neurons based on forward-pass impact magnitude (`|h_in^T W_{:,k}|`), validated against loss change (Appendix C, Pearson +0.71). But this is one of many possible ways to identify "important" neurons — gradient-based attribution (integrated gradients, input × gradient) or probing-based methods (training linear classifiers to predict task labels from neuron activations) could produce different neuron importance rankings. A strong follow-up would systematically compare data selection quality when NAGs are constructed from (a) the paper's impact-magnitude ranking, (b) gradient-magnitude ranking (absolute value of the gradient of the target-task loss with respect to each neuron's output), and (c) probing-based ranking (weights of a linear classifier trained to predict target-vs-random from neuron activations). This comparison serves two purposes: it stress-tests whether NAG's effectiveness is robust to the specific importance metric (if all three produce similar data selection quality, the method is metric-agnostic and the core contribution is the top-K, all-layer framework rather than the impact formula); and it reveals whether gradient-based selection — which requires a backward pass and labels, breaking the "training-free" property — provides enough additional signal to justify the extra cost. If gradient-based NAG substantially outperforms impact-based NAG, it suggests a cost-accuracy tradeoff that practitioners can tune.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient domain-specific pretraining for small and medium-scale deployments.** The paper's headline result — 4.9% average improvement over random selection at 1.2B/30B scale with a 2.42× compute multiplier (Figure 7) — translates directly to cost savings for organizations that pretrain models for specific domains. Consider a legal-tech company that needs a model specialized for contract analysis. Instead of pretraining on a general web crawl (where contract-relevant text is diluted by billions of tokens of unrelated content) or manually curating a legal corpus (expensive, slow, hard to update), they can: (1) collect a small set of ~1,000 representative contract-analysis examples, (2) use an off-the-shelf LLM (Qwen3-1.7B or smaller, 0.6B) to extract NAGs from their candidate web corpus, (3) select the top 20% ranked by NAG similarity to their contract examples, and (4) pretrain their model on this subset. The extraction cost for a 150B-token pool is 192 H100-GPU-hours with Qwen3-1.7B (Appendix F.2), and substantially less with Qwen3-0.6B. For a single target, this one-time cost must be weighed against the training compute savings — with a 2.42× compute multiplier, if training on the full pool would cost X GPU-hours, training on the NAG-selected 20% subset costs 0.41X, saving 0.59X. The breakeven depends on X, but for any training run where X substantially exceeds 192 GPU-hours (which begins around 1.2B/30B scale), NAG extraction is a net compute saver. For multi-target deployments, the extraction cost is amortized across all targets, making the economics even more favorable.

**Plug-in component for existing data curation pipelines, complementary to quality filters.** The paper's Section 3.6 (Table 2) demonstrates that NAG signals combine with the FineWeb-Edu quality classifier to achieve 49.6% average accuracy, exceeding either method alone (NAG: 49.9%, FineWeb-Edu: 47.4%). This positions NAG as a **composable signal** that can be integrated into existing data pipelines without replacing them. A production data pipeline typically involves multiple filtering stages: language detection, deduplication, quality scoring, toxicity filtering, and domain-relevance heuristics. NAG adds a new stage — target alignment scoring — that is orthogonal to all of these. Concretely, a pipeline could: (1) apply standard quality and safety filters to remove low-quality or harmful content, (2) score remaining documents with a general-quality classifier (e.g., FineWeb-Edu), (3) score remaining documents with NAG-based target similarity, (4) combine the two scores (e.g., via a weighted sum or a threshold on each) to produce the final selection. The paper does not prescribe a specific combination method, but the additive gains in Table 2 suggest that even simple combination (e.g., taking the intersection of top-ranked documents under both criteria, or using NAG similarity as a re-ranking within the FineWeb-Edu top-k) yields improvements. This has immediate practical value: organizations already invested in quality-filtering pipelines can add NAG as a post-processing step without retooling their existing infrastructure.

**Data selection for self-improvement and distillation pipelines.** The paper's conceptual framework — select data that exercises the same neural circuits as the target capability — is directly applicable to self-improvement loops where a model generates its own training data. Consider the STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) or ReST^EM (Singh et al., 2024) paradigm: a model generates many candidate solutions to problems, filters for correct solutions (via a verifier or ground-truth check), and fine-tunes on the successful trajectories. The generation step typically uses random sampling or simple heuristics to decide which problems to attempt many generations on. NAG offers a principled alternative: characterize the target capability (e.g., "generating correct step-by-step math solutions") by extracting NAGs from examples of successful solutions, then use NAG similarity to select *which unlabeled problems* to attempt generating solutions for — prioritizing problems whose neuron-activation patterns overlap with known successful-solution patterns. This could make self-improvement more data-efficient by focusing generation compute on problems that are in the model's zone of proximal development (neuron-activation patterns exist but are weak or inconsistent) rather than on problems that are either trivially easy (strong, consistent activation) or fundamentally out of reach (no relevant activation). The paper's Appendix K negative result on ReST^EM — where RL-trained revision models degraded — highlights that naive self-improvement can backfire, making principled data selection within self-improvement loops especially valuable.
