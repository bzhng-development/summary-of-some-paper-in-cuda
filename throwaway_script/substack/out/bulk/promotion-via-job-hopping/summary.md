Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman post on promotion via job hopping:

**When job hopping makes sense**

Job hopping can get you promoted years faster than you would've been otherwise. The key is understanding how companies pick what level they interview you for. Some companies—Meta is the named example—don't hesitate to promote you if there's enough signal showing you can perform at the next level. But most companies, including Google and Amazon, are "much slower to promote even if you have strong next-level performance." Job hopping becomes a great way to get promoted faster to the level you're already performing at. The benefit doesn't come for free, though.

**The trade-offs of switching companies**

Each time you start a new job, you need to ramp up before you're effective. You need to build trust with people and learn a new tech stack. That ramp-up time takes away from the impactful work you could be doing toward subsequent promotions. On top of that, switching companies resets your track record, which future promotions rely on. Ryan offers a personal example: he got promoted from Senior to Staff in one year because he was "already doing Staff-level work when I was a mid-level engineer"—the accumulated proof was there. When you switch companies, you start from scratch.

The upside is clear when your current situation isn't great. Switching companies is a great opportunity to change your manager and team. If you think your team situation isn't working, trying a new team and getting a promotion at the same time is "a win-win."

**How companies decide your interview level**

The level you're interviewed for is a combination of three factors. First is your **current level**—major tech companies use internal lookup tables to map other companies' levels to their own (L4 at Google is roughly E4 at Meta; Levels.fyi gives a decent approximation). Second is **years of experience**, with rough guardrails: Junior is 0–2 years, Mid-Level is 2–5+ years, Senior is 5+ years, and Staff is 10+ years. Third is **scope**—how large and complex were the workstreams you drove, and what level's expectations does your work actually fit? Based on these criteria, recruiters will add you to an interview loop that borders on two levels. Depending on how you perform in the interview, they may downlevel you or keep your level as is.

**Strategizing your jump for different career stages**

Job hopping for a promotion from Junior to Mid-level is easy because of the low YoE and scope requirements. Ryan was able to get Mid-level offers from Blend and Affirm with only 8 months of experience at Amazon as a Junior engineer.

For Senior+ levels, it's harder to get an interview at the next level if you're coming from a company with a well-defined ladder. Recruiters can easily figure out where you fit, so there isn't any reason to interview you for a higher level. That doesn't make it impossible, though. Zach Wilson's trajectory is offered as proof: Junior → Mid-level promo at Meta at 4 years of experience, then a Mid-level → Senior promo jump to Netflix at 6 YoE, followed by a Senior → Staff promo jump to Airbnb at 8 YoE.

**Leaving the well-defined ladder to reset your placement**

If you work outside of the well-defined tech ladder, you have a much better opportunity to hop to higher levels. Ryan knows a few people who left the industry ladder to do their own thing, and when they came back, they all got interviews for levels higher than the one they left. One was a failed founder who left as a Mid-level engineer and got Senior interviews after a year. Another was an open-source contributor who left as a Junior engineer and got Senior/Staff interviews after a year or two. A third was a failed founder and writer who left as a Senior engineer and got Senior Staff interviews in just two years. The unifying pattern: when you aren't coming from a well-defined ladder, there's uncertainty about where you might fit. If you "sell your experience and interview well, you can place on the high side of that range of uncertainty."

---

Ryan's framework reveals a asymmetry in how the industry maps external candidates onto internal ladders: the **well-defined ladder** creates a ceiling for internal employees at other recognizable companies, while stepping outside that system introduces enough uncertainty to reset your placement entirely. His advice to "sell your experience and interview well" to capture the high side of that uncertainty complements his earlier point that the reset cuts both ways—you lose the accumulated track record that powered his own Senior-to-Staff promo in one year. The whole calculus of job hopping, then, is a bet on whether the promo acceleration from escaping a slow-to-promote company outweighs the trust and proof you forfeit by starting from scratch.
