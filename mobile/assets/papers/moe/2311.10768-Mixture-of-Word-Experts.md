# Mixture of Word Experts

**ArXiv:** [2311.10768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10768)

## 🎯 Pitch

A T5 model equipped with 65,536 small, word-specific expert memories can answer trivia as accurately as a dense model 6.6× larger while remaining 6.6× faster to train. The key is forcing the model to route each token to exactly one expert based solely on its word identity—a scheme that handily beats conventional top-2 MoE routing for factual recall, yet falls apart if those expert parameters are unfrozen during tuning.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Mixture of Word Experts (MoWE)**, a sparse Transformer architecture that decouples learning capacity from compute by replacing standard feed-forward layers with a massively expanded pool of word-specific experts—tens of thousands of small MLPs, each activated by a fixed routing function tied to a knowledge-rich auxiliary vocabulary. Evaluated against the T5.1.1 family on knowledge-intensive NLP benchmarks (TriviaQA, WebQuestions, Natural Questions, FEVER), MoWE-Base outperforms T5.1.1-XL—a model requiring **4.3× more training time**—while MoWE-Large matches or exceeds T5.1.1-XXL at a **6.6× relative training speedup**, establishing that aggressive sparsity with vocabulary-driven routing can substitute for dense parameter scaling on tasks requiring factual recall. The approach outperforms standard MoE models (GShard Top-2) on knowledge-intensive tasks while matching them on SuperGLUE, and matches more complex memory-augmented methods (TOME, Entities as Experts) without requiring specialized retrieval mechanisms, demonstrating that the word-expert layers function as a seamlessly integrated sparse memory—but only when the routing vocabulary is both knowledge-rich and sufficiently large (performance degrades with smaller vocabularies and when expert parameters are unfrozen during finetuning).

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Fundamental Problem: Scaling Capacity Without Scaling Compute

The central tension this paper confronts is one that has defined much of the recent progress in NLP: **increasing a language model's parameter count consistently improves performance, but also proportionally increases the cost of running it**. The paper opens by observing that "increasing the parameter count of language models has been a primary driver of increased model quality" (Section 1), citing the now-familiar lineage from T5 (Raffel et al., 2020) through GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020) to PaLM (Chowdhery et al., 2022) and LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023). This scaling trajectory works because larger models can soak up more world knowledge from pretraining data—an effect that is "particularly apparent on knowledge intensive tasks, such as TriviaQA" (Section 1). But the self-defeating corollary is that bigger models also demand proportionally more floating-point operations (FLOPs) for both training and inference. In a dense Transformer, every parameter is activated on every input token, so doubling the dimensions doubles the computation.

The paper frames this as a **capacity-compute coupling problem**: the benefits of scale are locked behind a paywall of linearly increasing FLOPs. For organizations deploying language models in production, this tradeoff manifests as a direct tension between accuracy and latency (or between accuracy and infrastructure cost). A model that needs 26× the training time of T5-Base to achieve competitive TriviaQA scores (as T5-XXL does in Table 1) is prohibitively expensive for many practical settings.

What makes this coupling particularly frustrating is its asymmetry: **knowledge-intensive tasks benefit disproportionately from scale**, precisely because they require the model to have internalized large amounts of factual information during pretraining. If you want a model to answer "What is the name of Adele's first album?" without access to a search engine, it needs to have that fact stored somewhere in its parameters. A small dense model simply lacks the storage capacity. But the computation required to activate all those stored parameters on every token—including on the millions of mundane tokens in the pretraining corpus that carry no factual content—is largely wasted. As the paper puts it, the goal is to "aggressively decouple learning capacity and FLOPs" (Section 1).

### Why This Matters: More Than Just a Scaling Trick

The practical stakes extend beyond training cost. **Latency-sensitive applications**—voice assistants, real-time translation, interactive coding tools—cannot tolerate the per-token cost of running a 11B-parameter dense model, let alone a model an order of magnitude larger. If parameter count could be increased without proportionally increasing the number of FLOPs consumed per token, these applications could deploy models with far richer stored knowledge at the same inference latency.

There is also a **structural question** embedded in this problem. Prior work has suggested that Transformer feed-forward layers function as key-value memories (Geva et al., 2021; Dai et al., 2022), and that factual associations specifically appear to be stored in the FFN weights (Meng et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2022). If this view is correct, then the standard dense architecture is fundamentally inefficient as a knowledge store: a single monolithic FFN is asked to serve as the memory for *all* facts about *all* entities, mixed indiscriminately with the circuitry for syntactic processing, reasoning, and everything else the model does. The paper's core architectural hypothesis—spelled out in Section 2.2—is that **dedicating separate, small expert networks to individual words or entities would create a more natural separation between factual storage and general computation**, making it easier for the model to store and retrieve information. This is not merely an engineering optimization; it is a hypothesis about *how models ought to be structured* given what we know about where knowledge lives in these networks.

### The Two Predecessor Paradigms and Their Shortcomings

The paper positions itself at the intersection of two existing research traditions, each of which partially addresses the capacity-compute coupling problem but leaves critical gaps.

#### Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Models: Scale Without Specialization

The first tradition is **sparsely-activated Mixture-of-Experts models**, introduced by Shazeer et al. (2017) and scaled to Transformers by GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020), Switch Transformers (Fedus et al., 2022), and GLaM (Du et al., 2022). The core idea is straightforward: instead of using one FFN to process all tokens in a given layer, use a pool of FFNs (the "experts") and route each token to only one or a few of them via a learned routing function. This increases the total parameter count—often dramatically—while keeping the per-token FLOPs roughly constant, since each token only activates a subset of the experts.

However, the paper identifies several concrete limitations of this approach that MoWE is designed to address:

**Too few experts.** Standard MoE models use between 32 and 128 experts per sparse layer. The paper notes that Du et al. (2022) "found diminishing performance in their MoE models beyond roughly 64 or 128 experts" (Section 5). With so few experts, there is limited opportunity for fine-grained specialization. Each expert must handle a broad distribution of unrelated tokens, blending factual associations for thousands of different entities into the same weight matrix—essentially recreating the same memory-contention problem that plagues dense FFNs, just at a slightly larger scale.

**Learned routing is unstable and adds complexity.** In standard MoE models, the routing function—which determines which expert receives each token—is learned jointly with the rest of the network. The paper points out that "training the routing function is tricky and typically performed indirectly by rescaling expert outputs by the assignment probability (the 'router confidence') that a given token should be assigned to a particular expert" (Section 2.1). This introduces several headaches: the router can collapse to always sending tokens to the same expert (requiring auxiliary load-balancing losses to counteract), training can become unstable (Zoph et al., 2022 documents gradient blowup issues in MoE pretraining), and the assignment is stochastic in a way that is difficult to reason about.

**Fixed capacity buffers create load-balancing pressure.** In SPMD (single program, multiple data) implementations of MoE, experts have fixed-size capacity buffers and can only process a fixed fraction of the input tokens. This necessitates auxiliary load-balancing losses (Shazeer et al., 2017) to push the router toward uniform token distribution across experts—an artificial constraint that forces experts to handle roughly equal numbers of tokens, even though the natural distribution of linguistic phenomena is anything but uniform. Word frequencies follow a Zipfian distribution; forcing a uniform assignment means common words are artificially spread across multiple experts while rare words are forced into experts that also handle many other uncommon tokens.

**No parameter sharing across layers.** In standard MoE implementations, each sparse layer has its own independent set of experts. There is no mechanism for the same expert to be accessed at multiple depths in the network, which would be natural if experts function as persistent memories that should be consultable from different processing stages.

The paper's evidence (Table 2) shows that these limitations have concrete performance consequences: when MoWE and a comparable MoE-Top2 model are matched at roughly 30B parameters, MoWE achieves 39.4 vs. 36.2 EM on TriviaQA and 35.7 vs. 31.6 on WebQuestions—substantial gaps on knowledge-intensive tasks—while tying on SuperGLUE. This pattern suggests that standard MoE's routing and capacity design is particularly suboptimal for factual recall.

#### Memory-Augmented Models: Rich Specialization, Brittle Integration

The second tradition is **memory-augmented models**, which explicitly maintain a separate memory bank of entity or mention representations and query it during inference. Representative works include Entities as Experts (EaE; Févry et al., 2020), TOME (de Jong et al., 2022), FILM (Verga et al., 2021), and Knowledge Prompts (dos Santos et al., 2022). These models have proven "effective in knowledge intensive tasks" (Section 5) because they dedicate specialized storage to entities—often millions of Wikipedia entities, each with a dedicated embedding vector that can be retrieved and integrated into the model's representation when the entity is mentioned.

But the paper enumerates several drawbacks that limit the generality and ease of use of these approaches:

**Specialized training procedures.** Memory-augmented models "typically require a specialized training procedure, that differ from dense models, in order to effectively learn to use the 'external' memory" (Section 5). For example, EaE and TOME use entity-aware auxiliary losses and require the training data to be annotated with entity links. This means they cannot be trained on generic pretraining corpora like C4 in the same way a standard T5 model is trained—their training pipeline is entangled with entity-specific preprocessing.

**Domain-restricted training data.** The training data for these models is "normally very domain specific (most cases focus on Wikipedia)" (Section 5). If a model's memory bank is populated exclusively from Wikipedia entities, its knowledge is bounded by what Wikipedia contains. A model trained this way cannot easily absorb knowledge from other domains during continued pretraining on broader corpora.

**External retrieval mechanisms at inference time.** Memory-augmented models "require to invoke custom mechanisms to search the sparse memory" (Section 1), typically some form of nearest-neighbor lookup over entity embeddings. This adds latency, architectural complexity, and a dependency on external tooling (k-NN indices, approximate search libraries) that must be maintained and scaled alongside the model. The memory is not a native part of the forward pass—it is an external module that the model must learn to query, which introduces a second learning problem (learning to retrieve effectively) on top of learning to use the retrieved information.

**Embeddings, not networks.** In most memory-augmented models, each memory entry is a fixed embedding vector rather than a learned transformation (a small neural network). An embedding can store a static representation but cannot *process* information in context-dependent ways. An expert implemented as a small FFN can apply a nonlinear transformation conditioned on the current token representation, potentially enabling more sophisticated integration of stored knowledge with the current context.

### Where MoWE Positions Itself

MoWE attempts to **occupy the intersection** of these two traditions, taking the efficiency of sparse MoE computation and combining it with the entity-level specialization of memory-augmented models, while avoiding the drawbacks of both. The paper's architectural choices reflect this synthesis explicitly:

**From MoE, it inherits:** the paradigm of replacing dense FFN layers with sparse expert layers, the SPMD-efficient implementation strategy (with modifications), and the ability to train on generic pretraining data with standard language modeling objectives.

**From memory-augmented models, it inherits:** the idea of dedicated per-entity storage (each routing vocabulary entry can have its own expert), the intuition that factual knowledge benefits from isolation in parameter space, and the goal of storing world knowledge in a way that is directly accessible during the forward pass.

**Key departures from both:**

- *From MoE:* MoWE uses a **fixed, vocabulary-driven routing function** instead of learned top-k routing. This eliminates routing instability, load-balancing losses, and the stochasticity of learned assignments. The routing is deterministic and interpretable: the token "Turing" always activates the same expert(s), creating a direct, predictable association between words and their dedicated storage. The paper hypothesizes that this fixed routing "is a strong inductive bias that makes it easier for the experts to specialize on specific words" (Section 2.2). Additionally, MoWE uses **tens of thousands of experts** (32K in the main configuration, up to 1M in ablations) rather than 32–128, and **shares expert parameters across MoWE layers**, treating the expert pool as a persistent memory accessed at multiple network depths.

- *From memory-augmented models:* MoWE's experts are **small neural networks (MLPs) rather than static embeddings**, allowing them to perform context-dependent transformations. The memory is **integrated directly into the Transformer backbone** rather than being an external module—there is "no need to perform search using a nearest neighbor style tool during inference or training; the predefined routing avoids this search altogether" (Section 5). Training requires no specialized losses, entity annotations, or domain-specific data filtering. As the paper emphasizes, "MoWE is simple to train — no additional losses and no need to learn to search the memory. It seamlessly integrates with the model" (Section 5).

**The intellectual bet.** Underlying this architectural synthesis is a bet about how language models should store factual knowledge. The standard approach—soak it all into one giant dense FFN—works, but is inefficient because the same parameters must simultaneously serve as memory for millions of facts and as general-purpose processing circuitry. Standard MoE partially addresses this by splitting the FFN into a handful of experts, but with only 32–128 experts, each one still stores a hodgepodge of unrelated facts. Memory-augmented models go further, dedicating separate storage to millions of entities, but do so through an external retrieval mechanism that complicates training and inference. MoWE's bet is that **the Transformer backbone itself can be restructured to provide entity-level storage as a native part of the architecture**, with the large routing vocabulary acting as the index and the small expert MLPs acting as the storage cells. If this works, it would mean you can get the knowledge capacity of a memory-augmented model with the training simplicity and inference efficiency of a sparse MoE model—without needing to choose between them.

The paper validates this bet empirically by comparing against both paradigms: MoWE outperforms comparably-sized MoE models on knowledge tasks (Table 2), and matches or approximates the performance of TOME and EaE—models that use specialized training, entity linking, and external retrieval—while "avoiding invoking any custom mechanism to search the sparse memory" (Section 1, Table 5). Figure 1 captures the headline result visually: MoWE-Base achieves the TriviaQA accuracy of T5-XL using a fraction of the FLOPs, confirming that the architectural bet pays off in practice.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper proposes the **Mixture of Word Experts (MoWE)**, a Transformer architecture where standard feed-forward layers are replaced with a massive pool of small, word-specific neural networks (the "experts"), each activated by a fixed, vocabulary-driven routing function that maps input tokens to dedicated parameter storage. The approach solves the problem of decoupling learning capacity from compute cost: by sparsely activating only the experts corresponding to the words actually present in the input, MoWE achieves the knowledge storage capacity of a model with billions of parameters while performing FLOPs comparable to a much smaller dense model, without the routing instability, load-balancing overhead, or external retrieval mechanisms that complicate prior sparse and memory-augmented approaches.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The MoWE architecture modifies a standard T5.1.1 encoder-decoder Transformer by replacing a subset of the feed-forward network (FFN) layers with **MoWE layers**, each of which is a sparsely-activated pool of expert FFNs. The system has five major components:

1. **Default tokenizer and embedding layer:** the standard T5.1.1 SentencePiece tokenizer (32K vocabulary) that converts raw text into token IDs and maps them to dense embedding vectors — this defines the input sequence and its representations.

2. **Routing tokenizer and auxiliary vocabulary:** a separate, much larger auxiliary vocabulary (~1M entries) derived from Wikidata entity and relation names, used solely to determine which expert processes each token. Each entry in this vocabulary is mapped to a routing ID.

3. **MoWE layer:** a sparse layer inserted in place of selected FFN layers in the Transformer blocks, containing tens of thousands of small expert MLPs organized into frequency buckets and expert blocks. Each input token is routed to exactly one expert based on its routing ID (via a hash lookup from default token IDs to routing IDs), and the selected expert processes the token's contextualized representation.

4. **Hierarchical routing infrastructure:** a three-stage static routing pipeline — frequency bucketing → expert block assignment → individual expert selection — that handles the Zipfian distribution of word frequencies and enables efficient SPMD implementation without all-to-all communication between individual experts.

5. **Standard Transformer blocks (unchanged):** the remaining FFN layers, attention layers, and layer norms operate exactly as in a dense T5 model, providing the general-purpose processing circuitry that complements the specialized knowledge storage in the MoWE layers.

Information flows as follows: raw text is tokenized by the default tokenizer (producing token IDs and embeddings) and simultaneously by the routing tokenizer (producing routing IDs). The token embeddings pass through standard Transformer blocks until reaching a MoWE layer. At the MoWE layer, each token's routing ID is used to look up its frequency bucket, then its expert block, then its specific expert. The token's current hidden representation is fed through this expert's small FFN, and the output replaces what would have been the output of a dense FFN in a standard Transformer. The processed representations then continue through the remaining Transformer blocks. This repeats at each MoWE layer in the network (typically 2 in the encoder and 2 in the decoder, with expert parameters shared across all MoWE layers).

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the formal problem and architectural principles** — what it means to "decouple capacity and FLOPs" in terms of parameter activation, and the two design principles (very many experts, word-specific routing) that make the decoupling possible.

- **Second, the MoWE layer mechanics** — how a single sparse layer processes tokens: the expert pool structure, the routing function, the forward pass, and the crucial difference from learned-routing MoE.

- **Third, the routing vocabulary and tokenization** — the two-tokenizer setup, how the knowledge-rich auxiliary vocabulary is constructed from Wikidata, and the online hash-based routing ID assignment that makes autoregressive generation possible.

- **Fourth, the hierarchical routing infrastructure** — frequency bucketing, expert blocks, and the three-stage hierarchical assignment that handles Zipfian word distributions and enables efficient SPMD parallelism without per-expert communication overhead.

- **Fifth, the training and finetuning setup** — pretraining on C4 with span masking, the crucial design choice to freeze experts during downstream finetuning, and the hyperparameter configuration for the Base and Large model variants.

- **Sixth, design choices and their justifications** — why fixed routing over learned routing, why parameter sharing across MoWE layers, why freeze experts during finetuning, and why the specific placement and number of MoWE layers.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **novel architecture paper** whose core idea is that a Transformer's feed-forward layers can be replaced with a massive, vocabulary-indexed pool of small expert networks, creating a sparse memory that decouples parameter count from per-token FLOPs while providing entity-level knowledge storage — and that fixed, vocabulary-driven routing is both simpler and more effective than learned routing for this purpose.

---

#### The Capacity-FLOPs Decoupling Problem

A dense Transformer layer applies the same feed-forward network to every token. The FFN typically consists of two linear projections with an activation in between:

$$\text{FFN}(x) = W_2 \cdot \text{Activation}(W_1 \cdot x)$$

where $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{ff}}$ expands from the model dimension $d$ to the FFN intermediate dimension $d_{ff}$ (typically $4d$), and $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{ff} \times d}$ projects back down. Every parameter in $W_1$ and $W_2$ participates in every token's computation.

**What this means operationally:** for a sequence of $n$ tokens, the FFN performs $2 \cdot d \cdot d_{ff} \cdot n$ multiply-add operations, and all $2 \cdot d \cdot d_{ff}$ parameters are activated on every token. The FLOPs scale linearly with both parameter count and sequence length — if you double the FFN dimensions to store more knowledge, you double the computation per token.

**Why this is the problem MoWE solves:** factual knowledge about specific entities (people, places, concepts) is only relevant when those entities appear in the input. If the FFN stores knowledge about "Turing," "Qatar," "Neptune's moon Triton," and a million other entities, the vast majority of that stored knowledge is irrelevant for any given token. A dense FFN nonetheless expends computation accessing all of it on every token. The ideal architecture would activate only the knowledge storage relevant to the current input — this is exactly what sparsity enables.

The paper frames the solution as two principles (Section 1):

1. **A very large number of experts** — "tens of thousands instead of 32 to 128 normally used in MoEs" — so that each expert can specialize on a narrow slice of the input distribution.
2. **Word-specific experts tied to a knowledge-rich vocabulary through a fixed routing function** — so that which expert processes a token is determined by which word it is, not by a learned function that may route the same word to different experts depending on context or router noise.

---

#### MoWE Layer: Structure and Forward Pass

A MoWE layer replaces a standard FFN layer with a pool of $E$ experts, each of which is itself a small FFN. The paper describes these experts as "small MLPs" (Section 1). Critically, unlike standard MoE where each expert has the same architecture as the original dense FFN (e.g., a 2048-dimensional intermediate projection), MoWE experts are much smaller — the paper uses expert MLP dimensions ranging from 512 to 2816 depending on the frequency bucket (Table 6), with the largest experts in the highest-frequency buckets.

**The single-expert forward pass.** For a token with hidden representation $h \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (the output of the preceding attention and layer-norm operations in the Transformer block), and given its assigned expert with parameters $\theta_e = (W_1^{(e)}, W_2^{(e)})$, the MoWE layer computes:

$$h' = \text{Expert}_e(h) = W_2^{(e)} \cdot \text{Activation}(W_1^{(e)} \cdot h)$$

where $W_1^{(e)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{exp}^{(e)}}$ and $W_2^{(e)} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{exp}^{(e)} \times d}$, with $d_{exp}^{(e)} \ll d_{ff}$ (much smaller than the dense FFN intermediate dimension). The result $h'$ is added to the residual stream in the standard Transformer fashion.

**What determines which expert processes a token:** the routing function maps the token's routing ID (an integer index into the auxiliary vocabulary) to an expert ID (an integer index into the expert pool). The paper describes this mapping as a **hash function**: "routing consists of mapping routing ids to experts ids through a hash function" (Section 2.2). This is a deterministic, data-independent mapping — "a token with a given id is always routed to the same expert" (Section 2.2). There is no learned gating network, no router probabilities, and no stochastic assignment.

**Why this is a critical departure from standard MoE:** in standard top-k MoE (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020), the routing function $g(h)$ is a learned linear layer followed by softmax that outputs a probability distribution over experts. Top-k selection then picks the $k$ experts with highest probability, and token representations are dispatched accordingly. This setup has several problems that MoWE's fixed routing avoids:

- **Routing collapse:** the learned router can converge to always selecting the same few experts, starving others of training signal. Standard MoE addresses this with auxiliary load-balancing losses that penalize uneven assignment, but these losses fight against what the router "wants" to learn.
- **Training instability:** the one-hot expert selection is non-differentiable, so gradients are typically estimated by rescaling expert outputs by the router probability — an approximation that can produce noisy gradients and contribute to the training instability documented by Zoph et al. (2022).
- **Stochasticity:** the same word can be routed to different experts in different contexts or even different training runs, preventing the formation of stable, interpretable word-to-expert associations.

MoWE's fixed routing eliminates all of these issues: there is no router to train, no load-balancing loss needed (the Zipfian distribution is handled by frequency bucketing, described below), no gradient approximation needed (the expert assignment is known at graph-construction time), and the word-to-expert mapping is stable and interpretable.

**Expert parameter sharing across MoWE layers.** The paper states that "parameters are shared across all MoWE layers" (Section 3.2). This means that if there are 4 MoWE layers (2 in the encoder, 2 in the decoder), they all share the same pool of experts. The motivations are twofold:

1. **Analogy to a memory:** "it makes the MoWE layer even more similar to a memory that is accessed at different points of the network" (Section 3.2). A persistent memory should be consultable from any layer, not stored separately at each depth.
2. **Parameter efficiency:** "we can keep the overall number of sparse parameters relatively low without the need to decrease the total and the size of experts" (Section 3.2). Without sharing, 4 layers × 32K experts would require 128K experts and explode the parameter count.

The paper also notes that "empirical results indicated that sharing parameters across the MoWE layers leads to better performance" (Section 3.2), though the mechanism is not fully analyzed — it may be that sharing increases the number of gradient updates each expert receives, or that it enforces consistency in how knowledge is stored and accessed.

**Placement of MoWE layers.** The layers are placed "near the middle of the encoder (decoder)" (Section 3.2) for two reasons:

- The MoWE layers receive a representation that is "already somewhat contextualized" — the initial attention layers have had a chance to mix information across the sequence, so the token representation passed to the expert contains some surrounding context, not just the token's embedding. This means the expert can condition on how the word is being used, not just its identity.
- After the MoWE layer, "there are still multiple Transformer Blocks that can benefit from the output of that layer" — the knowledge extracted from the expert's memory can be further processed, combined with other contextual information, and refined by subsequent attention and FFN layers before producing the final output.

For MoWE-Base, the MoWE layers are placed at Transformer blocks 5 and 10 in the encoder, and blocks 5 and 10 in the decoder (Appendix A). For MoWE-Large, they are at blocks 9 and 17 in both encoder and decoder.

#### The Routing Vocabulary: Construction and Online Lookup

The routing vocabulary is the index into the expert pool. Its construction and use at inference time is one of the most innovative — and under-explained — aspects of the paper. There are two distinct tokenizations of every input:

**Default tokenization:** the standard T5.1.1 SentencePiece tokenizer with a 32K vocabulary. This produces the token IDs that are embedded and fed through the Transformer. This tokenizer uses subword units, so a word like "mathematician" is broken into multiple tokens (e.g., "math", "e", "m", "a", "tician").

**Routing tokenization:** a separate tokenization using a much larger auxiliary vocabulary (~1M entries), constructed specifically to keep knowledge-bearing words intact as single tokens. This tokenization is used only to determine routing IDs — which expert processes each token.

**Construction of the knowledge-rich routing vocabulary (Section 2.4).** The paper's procedure is:

1. **Start with Wikidata entity and relation names:** "Start with the set of all entity and relation names that appears in a Wikidata dump." This seeds the vocabulary with terms that are likely to carry factual associations — people, places, organizations, concepts — rather than function words or morphological variants.

2. **Normalize and split:** "Lowercase and split each name using white space and a regex to remove punctuation." This produces individual words from multi-word entity names.

3. **Order by frequency in pretraining data:** "Order tokens based on their frequency in the C4 dataset." This ensures that common knowledge-bearing words (which will appear frequently during pretraining and will have many opportunities for their experts to learn) are prioritized over obscure ones.

4. **Select the top 1M tokens:** "Select the top 1M tokens to form our routing vocabulary." This provides a vocabulary large enough to cover most named entities and content words, but not so large that it includes extremely rare terms that would never receive enough training signal.

The paper notes that a naive alternative — "using the pretraining dataset to train a large vocabulary SentencePiece tokenizer" — is "suboptimal as many words in the vocabulary turn out to be uninformative — many are just variations of the form of other words" (Section 2.4). A frequency-based SentencePiece tokenizer trained on C4 would allocate its vocabulary budget to common subwords and morphological variants rather than to named entities. The Wikidata seed + frequency ranking ensures that the vocabulary prioritizes named entities, which are the terms the paper hypothesizes benefit most from dedicated expert storage.

Examples from the routing vocabulary (Appendix D) illustrate the consequences: after the first 6000 entries (which contain frequent function words and common terms), the vocabulary contains terms like "consignment", "horticulture", "diwali", "paprika", "egyptians", "eurozone" — content words that carry factual associations and would benefit from dedicated expert storage, but which would be broken into subword pieces by a standard 32K tokenizer.

**Online hash-based routing ID assignment.** The routing ID for a given input token is not simply its position in a separate tokenization — that would be impossible because the routing tokenizer and the default tokenizer produce different token boundaries (e.g., "mathematician" might be 1 routing token but 5 default tokens). Instead, the paper uses a hash-table lookup that maps sequences of default token IDs to routing IDs. The procedure (Section 2.4) is:

**Offline preparation:**
1. Extend the auxiliary vocabulary by concatenating the default T5 32K vocabulary to it. This ensures that every token from the default tokenizer has some routing ID — the first 32K routing IDs correspond to the default subword vocabulary entries, and routing IDs above 32K correspond to the knowledge-rich Wikidata-derived entries.
2. Tokenize each entry in this extended auxiliary vocabulary using the default tokenizer. For a routing vocabulary entry like "mathematician", this produces the sequence of default token IDs (e.g., [id("math"), id("e"), id("m"), id("a"), id("tician")]).
3. Build a hash table where the key is the sequence of default token IDs and the value is the routing ID (a sequential integer from 0 to ~1M).

**Online inference:**
Given a tokenized input sequence $s = [t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_n]$ where each $t_i$ is a default token ID:

For each position $i$, the system looks up all sub-sequences ending at position $i$:
$$\{ [t_{i-k}, \ldots, t_i] \mid k \in [0, 8] \}$$

It attempts to find each of these sub-sequences in the hash table, and adopts the routing ID of the **largest** sub-sequence that exists. If $k=0$ (just the current token $t_i$), the lookup consults the hash table for a single default token ID — which will always succeed because the first 32K routing vocabulary entries correspond directly to default token IDs. Larger $k$ values attempt to match multi-token sequences that correspond to complete words or multi-word expressions in the knowledge-rich vocabulary.

**What this procedure achieves:** it approximates the routing tokenization using only the default token IDs available at inference time. A token like "mathematician" will initially be seen as five separate default tokens, but the hash lookup will find that the 5-token sequence [id("math"), id("e"), id("m"), id("a"), id("tician")] matches the routing vocabulary entry for "mathematician" and assign a routing ID corresponding to that word, not to the individual subwords. The max $k$ of 8 means that routing vocabulary entries up to 8 default tokens long can be recognized (longer expressions will fall back to the longest recognizable prefix). The greedy longest-match strategy ensures that multi-word expressions get routed to their dedicated experts when possible.

**Support for autoregressive generation.** During decoding, only the initial part of a word is known before subsequent tokens are generated. The hash-based approach naturally handles this: at each generation step, only the default token IDs generated so far are available, so the lookup will match the longest prefix that exists in the hash table. As more of a word is generated, the routing ID may "upgrade" from a subword entry to the full word entry once the complete multi-token sequence is produced.

**The purpose of the $>32K$ threshold.** The first 32K routing IDs correspond to subword tokens from the default vocabulary, which tend to be frequent but not knowledge-bearing (function words, common subwords, punctuation). Routing IDs above 32K correspond to entries from the Wikidata-derived vocabulary — actual words, entity names, content terms. The paper uses this threshold in the memory deactivation experiment (Section 4.3) to selectively disable the knowledge-bearing experts and demonstrate their importance. The top 16 most frequent tokens in the routing vocabulary are "never routed to an expert" at all (Appendix A.1) because they are "punctuation marks and other non-content words" that "can represent up to 28% of the tokens in a batch" — skipping them "speeds up the training time and does not hurt downstream performance."

#### Hierarchical Routing: Frequency Buckets, Expert Blocks, and SPMD Implementation

This section addresses what the paper identifies as the key implementation challenges of using tens of thousands of experts: the impractical overhead of all-to-all communication if experts are treated individually in an SPMD setting, and the unbalanced token assignments caused by Zipfian word frequency distributions.

**The SPMD challenge.** In standard SPMD MoE implementations (Lepikhin et al., 2020), data and experts are "cosharded across devices." Each device holds a subset of the experts, and after the routing function assigns each token to an expert, tokens must be transferred between devices via all-to-all communication so that each expert receives the tokens assigned to it. Under the single-program-multiple-data paradigm, all experts on all devices must "send and receive the same amount of data and perform the same amount of computation (same array shapes on each device)" (Section 2.3). If there are 32,768 experts sharded across, say, 16 devices, performing all-to-all communication between all experts would require 32,768 × 32,768 routing table entries and an impractical volume of communication.

**The Zipfian challenge.** Word frequencies follow a power-law distribution: a small number of words (e.g., "the", "of", "and") appear extremely frequently, while the vast majority of words appear rarely. In MoWE, this means "different word experts [need] to process orders of magnitude more tokens than others" (Section 2.3). A naive implementation with fixed capacity buffers (each expert can process at most $B$ tokens per batch) would either overflow for frequent words (losing tokens) or waste capacity for rare words (using only a fraction of the buffer).

The paper's solution has three components, described in Section 2.3 and illustrated in Figure 3:

**1. Frequency Bucketing.** The routing vocabulary is split into $k$ frequency buckets, where "the words in each bucket have approximately the same frequency" (Section 2.3). This is computed offline: "we compute the frequency of words in a sample of 2B tokens from our pretraining data and then split the routing vocabulary into k buckets." For the main experiments, $k=4$ buckets are used (Table 6). The MoWE layer does not process the top 16 most frequent routing tokens at all (they are punctuation marks and non-content words, representing up to 28% of batch tokens).

Each bucket is handled by a separate set of expert blocks, and the $k$ buckets are "conceptually executed in parallel" (Section 2.3). Because tokens within a bucket have similar frequencies, the token load per bucket is roughly predictable: high-frequency buckets process many tokens with few routing vocabulary entries (and thus few experts), while low-frequency buckets process few tokens but cover many routing vocabulary entries (many experts).

**Why this solves the Zipfian challenge:** by grouping words of similar frequency, experts in the same bucket can be sized to handle comparable token loads. The paper explicitly states that "experts in different buckets can have different sizes or even different architectures and can support different token capacities (process a different number of tokens)" (Section 2.3). This means the high-frequency bucket (bucket 1, covering only 128 routing IDs in Table 6) can use large experts (MLP dimension 2048 for Base) that process many tokens, while the low-frequency bucket (bucket 4, covering ~220K routing IDs) uses many small experts (MLP dimension 512) that each process very few tokens — and these different capacity allocations are handled naturally because each bucket's experts are in separate computational groups.

**2. Expert Blocks.** Within each frequency bucket, experts are grouped into **expert blocks** that are sharded across devices. The paper states: "All-to-all communication is only performed between blocks instead of between experts" (Section 2.3). If we use 128 blocks with 256 experts each, we get 32,768 total experts, but the all-to-all communication operates at the granularity of blocks (128 × 128) rather than experts (32,768 × 32,768).

**Why this solves the communication challenge:** the number of blocks is kept small (typically 64 or 128 per bucket, as shown in Tables 6 and 7) regardless of the number of experts. Increasing the number of experts just increases the number of experts per block, not the all-to-all communication table size. The paper notes: "Provided we keep the number of expert blocks small enough, we can increase the number of experts without increasing all-to-all communication costs" (Section 2.3).

**Why this is possible with fixed routing:** "we are able to use expert blocks because the fixed routing function pre-defines which block, and which expert inside the block, will process a given token" (Section 2.3). In learned-routing MoE, the routing decision is dynamic — you cannot pre-assign tokens to blocks because the router might send a token to any expert on any device. Fixed routing makes the token-to-block mapping a static, precomputable function of the token's routing ID.

**3. Hierarchical Routing (three stages).** Given a batch of tokens, the routing proceeds in three stages (Figure 3, Section 2.3):

- **Stage 1 — Route to frequency bucket:** each token's routing ID determines which of the $k$ frequency buckets it belongs to. Tokens are dispatched to the appropriate bucket's computational resources.

- **Stage 2 — Route to expert block:** within each bucket, the token's routing ID (offset to be relative to the bucket's ID range) is mapped to an expert block via a hash function. Blocks are the unit of SPMD sharding — each device holds a subset of blocks.

- **Stage 3 — Route to individual expert:** within the assigned block, the token's routing ID (further offset or hashed) determines which specific expert processes it. The expert's FFN parameters are loaded and the forward pass is executed.

**Why this three-stage structure is efficient:** stages 1 and 2 partition the routing problem into smaller subproblems, each of which fits within the fixed-capacity SPMD paradigm. The all-to-all communication happens at stage 2 (between blocks), and the expert computation at stage 3 is purely local (within each block's assigned device). Because routing IDs are fixed, "token-to-expert assignments are known beforehand and the full path through the hierarchical routing tree becomes trivial" (Section 2.3) — no dynamic dispatching is needed, just a series of static lookups.

**The specific bucket configuration for MoWE-Base (Table 6).** The 1M routing vocabulary is distributed across four buckets:

| Bucket | Routing IDs Covered | Expert Blocks | Experts per Block | Total Experts | Expert MLP Dim |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 128 | 128 | 1 | 128 | 2048 |
| 2 | 880 | 128 | 7 | 896 | 2048 |
| 3 | 1,024 | 128 | 8 | 1,024 | 1024 |
| 4 | ~220,000 | 128 | 235 | 30,080 | 512 |
| **Total** | ~222,032 | — | — | **32,128** | — |

Bucket 1 handles the most frequent content words (128 routing IDs). Each routing ID gets its own expert (1 expert per block, 128 experts total), and these experts are large (2048-dimensional MLP) because they will process many tokens during training.

Bucket 2 handles the next 880 most frequent words. Again, one expert per routing ID (7 experts per block × 128 blocks = 896 experts, rounded slightly in the table), large MLP dimension (2048).

Bucket 3 handles 1,024 routing IDs. One expert per ID, but with a smaller MLP dimension (1024) reflecting the lower per-word token count.

Bucket 4 handles the remaining ~220K routing IDs — the long tail of rare words. There are 30,080 experts for 220K routing IDs, meaning experts are shared: on average ~7.3 routing IDs map to each expert. The MLP dimension is the smallest (512). The paper notes that in this bucket, "due to the large number of experts, the Expert Blocks are implemented as lookup tables" (Appendix A.1) — rather than full matrix multiplication for each expert, the parameters are stored in a table and indexed by routing ID, which is efficient when each expert processes very few tokens.

For the 2B-parameter MoWE configuration (Table 7), the bucket structure is simplified: all four buckets use the same structure (64 blocks × 1 expert per block in buckets 1–3, 64 blocks × 128 experts per block in bucket 4), and expert MLP dimensions are smaller (2048 for the first three buckets, 96 for bucket 4).

**What makes this configuration possible where others failed.** Du et al. (2022) "found diminishing performance in their MoE models beyond roughly 64 or 128 experts" — yet MoWE trains successfully with 32K experts and scales to 1M in ablations. The paper attributes this to the fixed routing: it avoids the training instability of learned routing that compounds as the number of experts grows. The paper explicitly notes: "We did not observe any training instability (e.g. gradient blowup) that are often reported in the pretraining of regular MoE models (Zoph et al., 2022); we suspect is a helpful artifact of our fixed routing scheme" (Section 2.3). Additionally, the hierarchical bucketing prevents the load-imbalance problems that would starve rare-word experts of training signal in a learned-routing setup.

#### Pretraining Setup

**Backbone architecture.** MoWE uses T5.1.1 as its backbone, which is an encoder-decoder Transformer with the standard T5 improvements (relative position embeddings, gated GELU activation in FFNs, etc.). The specific configurations are:

- **MoWE-Base:** follows the T5-Base dimensions — model dimension $d = 768$, 12 encoder layers and 12 decoder layers, 12 attention heads, dense FFN intermediate dimension $d_{ff} = 2048$ (for the non-MoWE layers). MoWE layers are placed at encoder blocks 5 and 10, and decoder blocks 5 and 10.

- **MoWE-Large:** follows T5-Large dimensions — model dimension $d = 1024$, 24 encoder and 24 decoder layers, 16 attention heads, dense FFN intermediate dimension $d_{ff} = 2816$. MoWE layers at encoder blocks 9 and 17, decoder blocks 9 and 17.

The total parameter counts are 31B for MoWE-Base and 45.5B for MoWE-Large. For comparison, T5.1.1-Base has ~220M parameters — the massive increase comes from the 32K experts, even though the per-expert MLP dimensions are small.

**Training objective.** Pretraining uses the same span-masking denoising objective as T5 (Raffel et al., 2020): consecutive spans of input tokens are replaced with sentinel tokens, and the model is trained to reconstruct the original spans. No auxiliary losses are used — "we only use cross-entropy loss; no additional auxiliary losses are used" (Section 3.2). This is a departure from standard MoE which typically requires a load-balancing auxiliary loss; the fixed routing eliminates the need for it.

**Training hyperparameters.** The paper uses the "same pretraining hyperparameters of T5.1.1" (Section 3.2) — this means the Adafactor optimizer with inverse square root learning rate schedule, though the specific learning rate values are not stated for pretraining. Pretraining runs for "roughly 1 trillion tokens — 1M steps, with batch size 2048 and input sequence length of 512 tokens; the target sequence length is 114" (Section 3.2). Training uses 64 TPU v3 chips.

**Training time.** Table 1 reports training time relative to T5.1.1-Base: MoWE-Base is 2.0× slower, MoWE-Large is 4.0× slower. These are wall-clock times "estimated by running each model with a batch size of 256 and input (output) sequence length of 512 (62) on 64 v3 TPUs" (Table 1 caption). The paper notes this likely "underestimates the speed of the smaller models, which would enjoy better utilization on fewer devices." Despite being slower than T5-Base in absolute terms, MoWE-Base is 4.3× faster than T5-XL (which achieves comparable TriviaQA accuracy) and MoWE-Large is 6.6× faster than T5-XXL — the efficiency gains come from substituting a larger dense model, not from being faster than a small dense model.

**Vocabulary.** The routing vocabulary has "220 (∼1M) entries" (Section 3.2) — the "220" likely refers to 220K entries in the low-frequency bucket (bucket 4 in Table 6), plus the 2,032 entries in buckets 1–3, for a total of ~222K unique routing IDs, though stored entries in the hash table include the concatenated default vocabulary for a total of ~1M table entries.

#### Finetuning Setup and the Expert Freezing Decision

For downstream task finetuning, the paper makes a crucial and unusual design choice: **the MoWE experts are frozen** — their parameters are not updated during finetuning. The paper states: "During finetuning for downstream tasks, we freeze all MoWE experts to avoid both overfitting and catastrophic forgetting of knowledge acquired during pretraining" (Section 3.2).

**The ablation evidence for this choice (Appendix B.0.3):** when experts are allowed to update during TriviaQA finetuning, exact match drops from 37.7 to 33.5 — a loss of over 4 points. This is a substantial degradation and suggests that finetuning on a relatively small task-specific dataset (TriviaQA has ~87K training examples) causes the expert parameters — which were trained on 1 trillion tokens of diverse pretraining data — to overfit to the narrow distribution of the downstream task, losing the broad factual knowledge they accumulated during pretraining.

**Why this is a meaningful departure from standard MoE:** in standard MoE models, experts are typically finetuned along with the rest of the network. The paper explicitly notes this contrast: "This is an important distinction to MoE models, which finetune the experts for the downstream tasks" (Section 3.2). MoWE's experts function as a read-only knowledge store after pretraining — the model learns to *query* them effectively (via the non-expert parameters that are finetuned: attention weights, dense FFN layers, layer norms) but does not *modify* the stored knowledge. This is conceptually similar to how a retrieval-augmented model freezes its document index during finetuning while updating the retriever and reader.

**Finetuning hyperparameters.** The main tuned hyperparameter is the learning rate. "For most datasets, a learning rate of 1e-4 and dropout rate of 0.05 gave the best results" (Appendix A.2). For SuperGLUE and FEVER, higher learning rates of 1e-3 to 5e-4 are more effective. Batch sizes are typically 256 or 512. Finetuning uses only the cross-entropy objective — no task-specific auxiliary losses.

**Validation protocol.** For closed-book QA tasks, "evaluation [is performed] by holding out 10% of the training set as a validation set; models are finetuned on the remaining 90% of the data" (Section 3.1). This is the same protocol used by Lee et al. (2019) and Roberts et al. (2020).

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Fixed vocabulary-driven routing over learned routing.** This is the single most important design choice and enables everything else. The justifications are:

- *Eliminates routing instability:* no router to train means no router collapse, no gradient approximation, and no training instability. The paper notes this likely explains why MoWE experiences none of the gradient blowup issues plaguing standard MoE training.
- *Enables massive expert counts:* learned routing degrades beyond ~128 experts (Du et al., 2022); fixed routing scales to 32K experts easily and up to 1M in ablations (Appendix B, Figure 7).
- *Creates interpretable word-to-expert associations:* the routing is deterministic and transparent — you know exactly which expert handles "Turing" and can study its behavior (as in the deactivation experiment, Section 4.3).
- *No load-balancing loss needed:* the Zipfian imbalance is handled architecturally (frequency buckets with different capacities) rather than penalized with a loss term that fights the natural data distribution.

**Very large number of small experts over few large experts.** The paper argues that "using a larger number of smaller experts is preferable because it is more memory efficient and also speeds up our lookup table implementation" (Appendix B). With 32K experts at an MLP dimension of 512–2048, each expert stores relatively focused knowledge. The ablation in Figure 6 shows 32K experts as the "sweet spot" for TriviaQA F1: 16K degrades (likely because experts must cover too many words each) and 64K shows minimal further gain.

**Expert parameter sharing across layers over per-layer experts.** The justifications are:

- *Conceptual:* treats the expert pool as a persistent memory accessed at multiple depths, which is the intended semantic role of the MoWE layer.
- *Practical:* keeps total parameter count manageable (31B with sharing vs. ~124B without, if 4 layers each had independent 32K experts).
- *Empirical:* sharing leads to better performance (Section 3.2), possibly because it gives each expert more gradient signal or enforces consistency.

**Freezing experts during finetuning over finetuning them.** This is justified by the catastrophic forgetting evidence (Appendix B.0.3) and reflects the paper's view of experts as a *pretrained knowledge store* — factual knowledge acquired from the 1T-token pretraining corpus that should be preserved, not overwritten by relatively tiny downstream datasets. The non-expert parameters (attention, dense FFNs in non-MoWE layers, layer norms) are sufficient to adapt the model to new tasks by learning to query the frozen memory effectively.

**Knowledge-rich vocabulary construction over frequency-based tokenization.** A naive SentencePiece tokenizer trained on C4 would allocate subword units based on frequency, favoring common morphemes over named entities. The Wikidata seed ensures the vocabulary prioritizes terms that carry factual associations, which is exactly the kind of knowledge the experts are meant to store. The ablation in Figures 4 and 5 confirms that performance improves with larger routing vocabularies, especially at long training durations (1M steps).

**Four MoWE layers over more or fewer.** The ablation in Table 8 shows improvement from 0 to 2 encoder MoWE layers (TriviaQA EM: 31.0 → 31.6), but diminishing returns at 3 layers (31.5). Adding decoder layers provides further gains, with the 2-encoder + 2-decoder configuration achieving the best result (33.1 EM). Four layers "offer good accuracy without sacrificing computational performance due to routing overhead" (Section 3.2).

**Placement in the middle of the network over early or late placement.** Early layers receive less contextualized representations — the expert would see something closer to a token embedding than a context-aware representation, making it harder to condition on how the word is being used. Late layers provide less opportunity for subsequent processing to leverage the expert's output. Middle placement balances both concerns.

**Hash-based online routing over a separate routing tokenizer.** During autoregressive generation, tokens are produced incrementally — you don't know the full word until all its subword pieces are generated. A separate tokenizer that required complete words would not work during generation. The hash-based approach naturally degrades: at early steps in a word, the longest matching prefix in the hash table is used (which may be a subword entry), and when enough tokens are available to form a complete word, the routing ID "upgrades" to the full-word entry.

**Sharing experts in the low-frequency bucket over dedicated per-word experts.** For the ~220K entries in bucket 4, there are only 30,080 experts — roughly 7.3 routing IDs per expert on average. The paper notes this is "more efficient (both in terms of memory and training signal) to have fewer experts than vocabulary entries and share some experts across multiple routing ids" (Section 2.2). If each extremely rare word had its own expert, that expert might be activated only a handful of times during the entire pretraining run, receiving essentially no training signal. Sharing ensures each expert sees enough tokens to learn something useful. The paper's ablation on matching expert count to vocabulary size (Figure 7) confirms this: performance degrades as the number of experts increases to 1M (one per vocabulary entry) because "the number of training updates that each expert receives becomes increasingly sparse" and "the size of the experts are decreased" to keep the total parameter count constant.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Fixed Vocabulary-Driven Routing as a Viable — and Superior — Alternative to Learned Routing for Sparse Models

The dominant assumption in the MoE literature, from Shazeer et al. (2017) through GShard (Lepikhin et al., 2020) and Switch Transformers (Fedus et al., 2022), is that **the routing function must be learned jointly with the rest of the network**. This assumption is so baked into the paradigm that entire sub-problems — auxiliary load-balancing losses, expert capacity buffers, gradient estimation through non-differentiable top-k selection, training instability remedies (Zoph et al., 2022) — exist primarily to manage the consequences of learned routing. The field has treated these as necessary evils of sparsity, not as symptoms of a particular design choice.

MoWE's most conceptually disruptive move is to simply **discard learned routing entirely** and replace it with a fixed, vocabulary-indexed hash function. This is not an incremental tweak — it is a rejection of the premise that routing needs to be adaptive to be effective. The paper demonstrates that for the specific purpose of storing factual knowledge, a static mapping from words to experts is not merely adequate but **categorically better** than learned routing along multiple dimensions: it eliminates training instability (the paper observes "no gradient blowup" during pretraining, Section 2.3), removes the need for load-balancing losses (which fight against the natural Zipfian distribution of language), enables scaling to expert counts (32K–1M) that are two to three orders of magnitude beyond what learned-routing MoE models can handle (Du et al., 2022 found diminishing returns beyond ~64–128 experts), and produces interpretable word-to-expert associations that make the model's memory auditable (as demonstrated in the expert deactivation experiment, Section 4.3).

What makes this insight deeper than "fixed routing works" is the **architectural argument about why it works**: a deterministic word→expert mapping creates a strong inductive bias that encourages each expert to specialize on the content that co-occurs with its assigned word. As the paper puts it, "the expert for the word 'Turing' will be activated only when that word appears in the input, and therefore will be specialized on content that co-occur with that word" (Section 2.2). This is a claim about *learning dynamics*, not just about inference-time computation: during pretraining, the expert's parameters receive gradient updates exclusively (or predominantly) from training examples containing its assigned word, so the expert naturally becomes a repository for factual associations involving that word. A learned router, by contrast, might send "Turing" to different experts in different contexts, diffusing this specialization pressure.

The significance of this innovation extends beyond MoWE itself. It opens a design space that the field had largely dismissed: **under what conditions is static, symbolic routing preferable to learned, continuous routing?** The paper suggests an answer: when the goal is factual knowledge storage, and when the routing vocabulary is sufficiently knowledge-rich (Figures 4 and 5 demonstrate that performance degrades sharply with smaller or less curated routing vocabularies). This reframes the MoE routing problem from "how do we make the router learn good assignments?" to "how do we construct a routing vocabulary that provides the right inductive bias for what we want experts to learn?" — a fundamentally different research question.

**Evidence anchor:** The difficulty scaling contrast is stark. Du et al. (2022) report that GLaM MoE models degrade beyond ~64–128 experts; MoWE trains successfully with 32K experts (the main configuration) and scales to 1M in ablations (Figure 7), with only modest degradation at 1M attributed to sparser per-expert training signal, not instability. Table 2 shows the downstream consequence: at comparable parameter counts (29–31B), MoWE achieves 39.4 vs. 36.2 EM on TriviaQA and 35.7 vs. 31.6 on WebQuestions compared to a GShard Top-2 MoE — gaps that reflect the benefits of expert specialization enabled by fixed routing.

### Innovation 2: Reframing Sparse Experts as an Integrated Sparse Memory Rather Than a Computation Substitution

Prior work treats MoE models as a **FLOPs-efficiency mechanism**: the sparse layer substitutes for the dense FFN to increase parameter count without increasing per-token compute. The experts are computational units that happen to be sparsely activated — their role is to process tokens, not to serve as persistent storage. Memory-augmented models (EaE, TOME, FILM) treat the memory as a **separate retrieval system**: an external bank of entity embeddings that the model queries through explicit search mechanisms, often using domain-specific training procedures and nearest-neighbor lookups at inference time.

MoWE synthesizes these two framings into a third: **the sparse expert layers *are* the memory**. This is not just a rhetorical reframe — it has concrete architectural and operational consequences that make the model simpler, more general, and more efficient than either predecessor paradigm:

- **Compared to MoE:** MoWE's experts are designed to be *persistent stores of factual associations*, not general-purpose computation units. This is reflected in the design choices: experts are small (MLP dimensions 512–2048 rather than the standard 2048–16384), specialized (one per word or small group of words), shared across layers (accessed at multiple depths like a memory, not recomputed independently at each layer), and frozen during finetuning (the stored knowledge is preserved, not overwritten). These choices would be puzzling if experts were viewed as computation substitutions — a 512-dim MLP is a poor substitute for a 2048-dim dense FFN as a general processor — but they make sense if experts are viewed as parameterized memory cells that store word-associated information.

- **Compared to memory-augmented models:** MoWE's memory is *native to the forward pass*, not an external module. There is "no need to perform search using a nearest neighbor style tool during inference or training; the predefined routing avoids this search altogether" (Section 5). The index (routing vocabulary) is encoded in the model architecture, not in a separate data structure. Training uses standard language modeling objectives on generic pretraining data — no entity annotations, no auxiliary retrieval losses, no domain-restricted Wikipedia corpora. This means MoWE achieves memory-like functionality (entity-specific factual storage) with the operational simplicity of a standard Transformer — "MoWE can be trained in a similar fashion to a T5 model with no external tools/models" (Section 4.4).

The intellectual move here is to recognize that **sparsity can serve storage, not just computation**. A dense FFN is asked to simultaneously be a knowledge store and a processing unit for every token; standard MoE splits the processing across a handful of units but still mixes storage and processing in each one; MoWE separates the concerns: the MoWE layers provide high-capacity, entity-indexed storage, while the remaining dense layers (attention, standard FFNs) provide general-purpose processing. The paper does not state this separation explicitly in architectural terms, but it is the logical conclusion of its design choices: freeze storage during finetuning (preserving knowledge), train processing on the downstream task (adapting behavior), and let the two systems interact through the standard residual stream.

**Evidence anchor:** The expert deactivation experiment (Table 3, Table 4) provides the strongest evidence for the memory interpretation. Deactivating experts with routing IDs >32K (the Wikidata-derived, knowledge-bearing entries) causes a 9-point drop in TriviaQA EM (35.1 → 25.6), and the qualitative examples in Table 4 show that deactivating a *single* expert — representing "only 0.33% of the estimated total number of activated parameters" — can flip the model's answer from correct to incorrect on questions like "What is Neptune's main satellite?" (Triton → Uranus). This is memory-like behavior: a specific fact is stored in a specific, localizable parameter set, and removing that set removes the fact. The freezing ablation (Appendix B.0.3) reinforces this: updating the "memory" during finetuning causes catastrophic forgetting (37.7 → 33.5 EM), exactly as one would expect if finetuning overwrites stored facts.

**Evidence anchor:** The comparison with memory-augmented models (Table 5) shows that MoWE-Large is competitive with TOME — a model that uses specialized entity linking, auxiliary losses, and external retrieval — while being trained on generic C4 data with no specialized mechanisms. This demonstrates that the integrated-memory approach achieves comparable knowledge capacity without the architectural complexity.

### Innovation 3: Frequency Bucketing as an Architectural Solution to the Zipfian Load-Imbalance Problem

Every MoE model must confront the fact that language is not uniform: some tokens appear orders of magnitude more frequently than others. Standard MoE models address this through **loss-based load balancing** — an auxiliary term in the training objective that penalizes the router for assigning tokens unevenly across experts (Shazeer et al., 2017). This approach has two problems: it fights against the natural data distribution (forcing the router to spread common words across many experts, even if they would benefit from dedicated processing), and it requires the router to be trainable (since the loss must shape the router's behavior), which ties load balancing to the learned-routing paradigm that MoWE abandons.

MoWE's solution is architectural rather than loss-based: **split the routing vocabulary into frequency buckets, where words in each bucket have approximately the same frequency, and give each bucket its own set of expert blocks with appropriately sized capacity buffers and expert dimensions** (Section 2.3, Table 6). This transforms the load-imbalance problem from a *routing optimization challenge* to a *capacity allocation design choice*.

The insight is that **static routing enables static capacity planning**. Because the mapping from words to experts is fixed and known before training, the expected token load for each expert can be estimated from the pretraining data distribution. The frequency bucketing then groups experts with similar expected loads, and the SPMD implementation can allocate buffer sizes and computational resources per-bucket rather than trying to make all experts handle the same load. This is the engineering analog of the conceptual move in Innovation 1: replace a learned solution to a dynamic problem with a designed solution to a static problem, enabled by the fact that the routing itself is static.

What makes this more than an implementation detail is that it **enables expert scaling that would be impossible under learned routing**. Learned-routing MoE models with uniform capacity buffers force all experts to handle roughly the same number of tokens, which means either: (a) the capacity is set high enough for the most frequent words, wasting buffer space for rare-word experts; or (b) the capacity is set for the average, causing token overflow (dropped tokens) for frequent-word experts. Recent work like MegaBlocks (Gale et al., 2023) relaxes these constraints with variable buffer sizes, but still operates within the learned-routing paradigm.

MoWE's frequency bucketing allows the top 128 most frequent content words (bucket 1) to each have their own large expert (MLP dimension 2048) that processes many tokens, while the 220K rare words in bucket 4 share a pool of 30K small experts (MLP dimension 512) that each process very few tokens — and these different allocation regimes coexist naturally because each bucket is a separate computational group. The paper reports that this configuration scales to 1M experts (Figure 7, Appendix B) without training instability, which would be architecturally impossible with uniform capacity buffers.

**Evidence anchor:** The hierarchical routing infrastructure (Figure 3, Table 6) is what makes the 32K-expert configuration trainable on 16 TPU v3 chips. Without frequency bucketing, the all-to-all communication overhead of 32K experts would be prohibitive; with bucketing, the communication operates at the block level (128 blocks per bucket) regardless of expert count. The paper notes this explicitly: "Provided we keep the number of expert blocks small enough, we can increase the number of experts without increasing all-to-all communication costs" (Section 2.3). This is not a theoretical claim — the paper reports successful training of MoWE-Base with 32K experts and ablation models with up to 1M experts, representing a 250–7800× increase in expert count over standard MoE configurations.

### Innovation 4: Empirical Demonstration That Parameter Count, Not FLOPs, Is the Binding Constraint for Factual Knowledge Storage

The scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) has established that model performance improves predictably with increased training compute, and that compute-optimal training involves scaling both parameters and data. This framing treats parameters and FLOPs as coupled quantities in a joint optimization problem: more parameters require more FLOPs during both training and inference, so the relevant question is how to allocate a fixed FLOPs budget between model size and training duration.

MoWE's results challenge a specific but practically important corollary of this framing: the assumption that **the knowledge storage capacity of a model is fundamentally bottlenecked by FLOPs rather than by parameter count**. The paper's headline result (Figure 1) shows that MoWE-Base achieves TriviaQA accuracy comparable to T5.1.1-XL while using a fraction of the FLOPs, and MoWE-Large matches T5.1.1-XXL. The FLOPs-to-accuracy curve for MoWE sits dramatically to the left of the T5 curve: for the same TriviaQA accuracy of ~43 EM, MoWE-Large uses roughly the FLOPs of T5-Large, while T5-XXL requires ~26× more training time than T5-Base (Table 1) to achieve that accuracy.

This is not just an efficiency win — it is evidence for a **decoupling claim**: when the architecture provides dedicated, sparsely-activated storage for factual associations (via word experts), the model's knowledge capacity is determined more by the number of experts (and thus total sparse parameters) than by the number of FLOPs consumed per token. A dense T5 model must burn FLOPs accessing all its parameters on every token, even though the vast majority of those parameters store facts about entities not present in the current input. MoWE burns FLOPs only on the small subset of experts relevant to the current input's words, achieving comparable knowledge capacity with dramatically less computation. The paper does not state this decoupling claim in theoretical terms, but its experimental design — matching models on FLOPs (the MoE comparison in Table 2) and on total parameters (the T5 comparison in Figure 1) — is structured to demonstrate exactly this point.

The boundaries of this decoupling are equally informative. On SuperGLUE (Table 1), MoWE's advantage over comparably-sized T5 models is smaller than on knowledge-intensive tasks: MoWE-Base achieves 83.5 vs. 77.2 for T5-Base (+6.3 points), while on TriviaQA the gap is 15.2 points. This makes sense under the decoupling hypothesis: SuperGLUE tasks (reading comprehension, textual entailment, coreference) depend more on general linguistic processing — which requires computation on every token regardless of architecture — and less on the lookup of stored factual associations. The sparse memory provides large gains where the bottleneck is storage, and smaller gains where the bottleneck is processing. This implies that the optimal architecture may depend on the task distribution: knowledge-heavy applications benefit disproportionately from sparse, memory-oriented designs.

**Evidence anchor:** Table 1 is the primary evidence. MoWE-Base (31B parameters, T5-Base FLOPs) outperforms T5.1.1-XL (~3B parameters, but ~8.6× the training time) on TriviaQA (39.4 vs. 36.0 EM), WebQuestions (35.7 vs. 32.4), Natural Questions (29.6 vs. 29.5, essentially tied), and FEVER (66.3 vs. 65.9). The gains are concentrated in knowledge-intensive tasks, consistent with the storage-bottleneck hypothesis. The FLOPs-matched MoE comparison in Table 2 reinforces this: MoWE outperforms MoE-Top2 substantially on TriviaQA and WebQuestions but ties on SuperGLUE — the storage advantage of word-specific experts matters for factual recall but not for general language understanding.

### Innovation 5: The Routing Vocabulary as a Design Space for Controlling Model Behavior

In standard NLP pipelines, the vocabulary is an engineering convenience — a way to map text to token IDs that balances coverage, sequence length, and computational efficiency. The choice of tokenizer (BPE, SentencePiece, WordPiece) and vocabulary size is treated as a hyperparameter to tune, not as a lever for shaping what the model learns.

MoWE elevates the routing vocabulary to a **first-class architectural design element** that directly controls the model's inductive biases. The paper's ablation experiments (Figures 4 and 5, Appendix B) show that:

- **Vocabulary size matters systematically:** increasing the routing vocabulary from 32K to 1M produces a consistent improvement in TriviaQA F1 (Figure 4: 34.3 at 32K → 35.6 at 262K → 35.3 at 1M, with a plateau at larger sizes for short training; Figure 5: 37.2 at 32K → 39.1 at 262K → 39.0 at 1M for 1M-step training, with Natural Questions showing a similar trend).

- **Vocabulary content matters:** the choice to seed the vocabulary from Wikidata entity and relation names rather than training a frequency-based SentencePiece tokenizer on C4 is motivated by the observation that the latter produces "uninformative" entries — "many are just variations of the form of other words" (Section 2.4). The Wikidata seed ensures the vocabulary is biased toward knowledge-bearing terms, which the paper argues is essential for the expert specialization mechanism to work.

- **The vocabulary defines the model's "memory schema":** the routing vocabulary determines which words get dedicated expert storage (one expert per word in buckets 1–3) and which share storage (bucket 4). This means the vocabulary is not just an encoding scheme — it is a declaration of which linguistic units the model treats as atomic entities worthy of dedicated storage. A word like "mathematician" that is broken into 5 subword tokens by the default 32K tokenizer is kept intact as a single routing token, giving it a dedicated processing pathway through the expert pool.

This reframes vocabulary design as a form of **architectural prior specification**: by choosing which words to include in the routing vocabulary and at what granularity, the model designer is making an explicit decision about what kinds of knowledge the model should store in dedicated expert circuits versus what should be handled by the general-purpose dense processing. This is a previously unrecognized design dimension — standard models use one vocabulary for everything; MoWE separates the "processing vocabulary" (default tokenizer) from the "memory vocabulary" (routing tokenizer), and the two can be optimized for different goals (sequence length efficiency vs. knowledge richness).

**Evidence anchor:** The ablation curves in Figures 4 and 5 are the clearest evidence. The consistent improvement with larger routing vocabularies — especially when training for sufficient steps (Figure 5) — demonstrates that the vocabulary is not just a lookup table but a mechanism that shapes what the experts learn. The 32K → 262K improvement in Figure 5 (+1.9 F1 on TriviaQA, +1.1 on Natural Questions) represents the gain from adding ~230K knowledge-bearing terms to the routing vocabulary, giving each one its own expert (or small expert group) and thereby creating dedicated storage for facts about those terms that would otherwise be mixed into general-purpose parameters.

The paper also explicitly argues for the importance of vocabulary content, not just size: "To build a knowledge-rich routing vocabulary that contains more informative tokens, we derive the vocabulary from a knowledge rich dataset" (Section 2.4). This is a design principle — route on words that carry factual associations — that the paper validates through the Wikidata seed procedure and the frequency-based ordering that prioritizes common knowledge-bearing terms.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on five NLP tasks spanning knowledge-intensive and general language understanding. Knowledge-intensive tasks: TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) — closed-book question answering with ~87K training examples; WebQuestions (Berant et al., 2013); Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019); and FEVER (Thorne et al., 2018) — a claim verification dataset with separate validation and test sets. General language understanding: SuperGLUE (Wang et al., 2019b), a suite of 8 tasks (reading comprehension, textual entailment, coreference, etc.). All models are pretrained on the C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2020), version 2.2.0, which is a cleaned version of Common Crawl web text.

- **Base model(s).** The backbone is T5.1.1 (Raffel et al., 2020), an encoder-decoder Transformer with relative position embeddings and gated GELU activations in feed-forward layers. The paper constructs two MoWE variants: **MoWE-Base** (follows T5-Base dimensions — 12 encoder layers, 12 decoder layers, model dimension 768, 12 attention heads, dense FFN intermediate dimension 2048; achieves 31B total parameters) and **MoWE-Large** (follows T5-Large dimensions — 24 encoder and decoder layers, model dimension 1024, 16 attention heads, dense FFN intermediate dimension 2816; achieves 45.5B parameters). For comparison, T5.1.1-Base has ~220M parameters — the massive increase in MoWE comes from the 32K experts, though per-expert MLP dimensions are small (512–2048). The paper argues T5.1.1 is an appropriate backbone because "following popular (Fedus et al., 2022) and state-of-the-art (Zoph et al., 2022) Transformer-based encoder-decoder MoE models, we use T5.1.1 as the backbone" (Section 3.2).

- **Metrics.** For TriviaQA, WebQuestions, and Natural Questions, the primary metric is **Exact Match (EM)** — the fraction of test/validation questions for which the model's generated answer string exactly matches the ground-truth answer, using the grading function from Roberts et al. (2020). Some ablation experiments report **F1 score** instead of EM because it is "slightly less noisy and highlights the trends more clearly" (Section 4.5). For FEVER, the metric is **accuracy** on both validation and test sets. For SuperGLUE, following previous work (Raffel et al., 2020; Xue et al., 2022), the paper finetunes MoWE models on a mixture of all SuperGLUE tasks, selects the best checkpoint per task, and reports the **average validation set score** across all tasks (using a blended average of accuracy and F1 scores, as in the original T5 paper).

- **Baselines.**
  - **T5.1.1 family** (Raffel et al., 2020; Roberts et al., 2020): T5.1.1-Base, T5.1.1-Large, T5.1.1-XL, and T5.1.1-XXL. These are dense encoder-decoder Transformers with the same pretraining objective and data (C4 span masking, 1T tokens) as MoWE. Results for TriviaQA, WebQuestions, and Natural Questions are taken directly from Roberts et al. (2020), who established the closed-book QA evaluation protocol for T5 models. SuperGLUE and FEVER results are the paper's own T5.1.1 runs.
  - **GShard Top-2 MoE Transformer** (Lepikhin et al., 2020): the canonical MoE architecture with learned top-2 routing, 12 sparse layers (every other layer), 32 or 512 experts per sparse layer, and no parameter sharing across layers. The paper constructs two variants: a 2B-parameter version (12 sparse layers × 32 experts × MLP dim 2048) and a 29.2B-parameter version (12 sparse layers × 512 experts × MLP dim 2048), both using T5-Base as the backbone so they have FLOPs comparable to T5-Base. The paper uses the T5X framework's MoE-Top2 implementation with default hyperparameters except as noted (expert dropout 0.3, learning rate 5e-4 for QA and FEVER, 1e-3 for SuperGLUE).
  - **Entities as Experts (EaE)** (Févry et al., 2020): a memory-augmented model that uses entity embeddings as a sparse memory, pretrained on Wikipedia with entity-aware losses, and evaluated on TriviaQA as an entity linking task over a closed set of 1M Wikipedia entities.
  - **Transformer Over Mention Encodings (TOME)** (de Jong et al., 2022): a memory-augmented model that encodes entity mentions and retrieves them via k-NN search during inference. Two configurations are compared: TOME 1 (two memory layers) and TOME 2 (two memory layers with enhanced training). Like EaE, TOME uses Wikipedia pretraining data, entity-aware auxiliary losses, and treats TriviaQA as entity linking.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures computational cost via **training wall-clock time relative to T5.1.1-Base** on 64 TPU v3 chips (Table 1). These times are estimated by "running each model with a batch size of 256 and input (output) sequence length of 512 (62) on 64 v3 TPUs" (Table 1 caption), which the paper notes is "the smallest slice that could fit T5-XXL with 256 examples." The paper acknowledges this likely "underestimates the speed of the smaller models, which would enjoy better utilization on fewer devices." An alternative compute metric visible in Figure 1 is **FLOPs per target token**, plotted on a log-scale x-axis, which shows the per-token inference cost advantage of MoWE over dense T5 models. For the MoE comparison (Table 2), compute is implicitly matched by using the same backbone (T5-Base) and the same number of sparse layers at comparable total parameter counts, ensuring that inference FLOPs are similar between MoWE and MoE-Top2 configurations. For the memory-augmented comparison (Table 5), no compute matching is performed — the models use different pretraining data, training procedures, and architectures, so this comparison measures accuracy only, not efficiency.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For closed-book QA tasks (TriviaQA, WebQuestions, Natural Questions), the paper follows the protocol of Lee et al. (2019) and Roberts et al. (2020): "evaluation [is performed] by holding out 10% of the training set as a validation set; models are finetuned on the remaining 90% of the data" (Section 3.1). For FEVER, the standard provided validation and test splits are used. For SuperGLUE, the standard validation sets are used. The paper does not report error bars, confidence intervals, or multiple training runs for any experiment — all results are single-run numbers. For ablation experiments in Appendix B, models are pretrained for 200K steps rather than the full 1M steps, which reduces the reliability of those results as estimates of the fully-trained behavior.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Comparison with Dense T5.1.1 Models (Table 1, Figure 1)

**Headline:** MoWE-Base (31B parameters, 2.0× relative training time) **outperforms T5.1.1-XL** (~3B parameters, 8.6× relative training time) on all four knowledge-intensive tasks, and MoWE-Large (45.5B parameters, 4.0× relative training time) **matches or exceeds T5.1.1-XXL** (~11B parameters, 26.4× relative training time) on the same tasks.

**TriviaQA (Exact Match):** The largest gaps appear here. MoWE-Base achieves 39.4 EM compared to 24.2 for T5.1.1-Base (+15.2 points, a 62.8% relative improvement), 28.2 for T5.1.1-Large (+11.2 points), and 36.0 for T5.1.1-XL (+3.4 points). MoWE-Large achieves 44.8 EM, surpassing T5.1.1-XXL's 42.9 (+1.9 points). The FLOPs-to-accuracy curve in Figure 1 visualizes this: MoWE-Base sits at approximately the same TriviaQA EM as T5.1.1-XL (~36–39 EM) but at a dramatically lower FLOPs-per-target-token value (roughly 5 × 10^8 vs. 5 × 10^9 for T5-XL), and MoWE-Large matches T5.1.1-XXL (~43–45 EM) with a similar FLOPs reduction.

**WebQuestions (Exact Match):** MoWE-Base achieves 35.7 EM vs. 28.2 for T5.1.1-Base (+7.5 points), 29.5 for T5.1.1-Large (+6.2 points), and 32.4 for T5.1.1-XL (+3.3 points). MoWE-Large achieves 38.8 EM, exceeding T5.1.1-XXL's 35.6 (+3.2 points). The relative gains are smaller than on TriviaQA but still substantial.

**Natural Questions (Exact Match):** MoWE-Base achieves 29.6 EM vs. 25.7 for T5.1.1-Base (+3.9 points), 27.3 for T5.1.1-Large (+2.3 points), and essentially ties T5.1.1-XL (29.5). MoWE-Large achieves 31.9 EM vs. 32.8 for T5.1.1-XXL — slightly underperforming the largest T5 model by 0.9 points. This is the weakest knowledge-intensive result, consistent with NQ being an inherently harder dataset where all models score lower.

**FEVER (Accuracy):** MoWE-Base achieves 66.3 vs. 61.3 for T5.1.1-Base (+5.0 points), 63.0 for T5.1.1-Large (+3.3 points), and edges out T5.1.1-XL's 65.9 (+0.4 points). MoWE-Large achieves 68.5, marginally exceeding T5.1.1-XXL's 67.5 (+1.0 point).

**SuperGLUE (Blended Average):** Here the gains are smaller and consistent with the paper's claim that MoWE's advantage is concentrated in knowledge-intensive tasks. MoWE-Base achieves 83.5 vs. 77.2 for T5.1.1-Base (+6.3 points) but trails T5.1.1-Large (85.1, −1.6 points). MoWE-Large achieves 87.4, trailing T5.1.1-XL (88.5, −1.1 points) and T5.1.1-XXL (89.9, −2.5 points). The paper does not comment on this underperformance, but it is consistent with the interpretation that MoWE's sparse memory provides larger benefits when the task bottleneck is factual recall (TriviaQA, WebQuestions) rather than general linguistic competence (SuperGLUE), where dense models with more FLOPs devoted to in-context processing may have an advantage.

**Training speed:** The rightmost column of Table 1 reports training time relative to T5.1.1-Base. T5.1.1-XL requires 8.6× and T5.1.1-XXL requires 26.4× the training time of T5.1.1-Base. MoWE-Base requires 2.0× and MoWE-Large requires 4.0×. Therefore, MoWE-Base achieves better QA performance than T5.1.1-XL with 4.3× less training time, and MoWE-Large meets or beats T5.1.1-XXL with 6.6× less training time. Note that these relative speedups are against the performance-matched larger T5 models, not against T5-Base.

#### Comparison with Standard MoE Models (Table 2)

**Headline:** At matched FLOPs (same T5-Base backbone) and comparable total parameters, MoWE **substantially outperforms GShard Top-2 MoE on knowledge-intensive tasks while matching it on SuperGLUE**.

**2B parameter scale (top of Table 2):** The MoE-Top2 baseline uses 12 sparse layers with 32 experts each (MLP dim 2048, no parameter sharing) for 2B total parameters. MoWE is configured to match at 2B parameters by reducing to 8K experts with smaller MLP dimensions (Appendix A.1, Table 7: expert MLP dim 2048/2048/2048/96 across the four frequency buckets). On TriviaQA, MoWE achieves 29.8 EM vs. 26.5 for MoE-Top2 (+3.3 points). On WebQuestions: 31.6 vs. 27.7 (+3.9 points). On Natural Questions: 26.0 vs. 25.8 (+0.2 points — essentially tied). On SuperGLUE: 81.2 vs. 80.2 (+1.0 point). The pattern is clear: MoWE's advantage is concentrated in knowledge-intensive tasks, with the largest gaps on TriviaQA and WebQuestions.

**30B parameter scale (bottom of Table 2):** The MoE-Top2 baseline scales to 512 experts per sparse layer (12 sparse layers, MLP dim 2048, no parameter sharing) for 29.2B parameters. MoWE uses its standard 32K-expert configuration for 31B parameters. On TriviaQA: 39.4 vs. 36.2 (+3.2 points). On WebQuestions: 35.7 vs. 31.6 (+4.1 points). On Natural Questions: 29.6 vs. 28.5 (+1.1 points). On SuperGLUE: both achieve 83.5. The gap on knowledge-intensive tasks persists and even widens slightly (the WebQuestions gap grows from +3.9 to +4.1 points), while SuperGLUE remains tied. This demonstrates that increasing expert count — from 32K (MoWE) vs. 512 per layer (MoE-Top2) — provides persistent benefits for factual recall but not for general language understanding, consistent with the paper's argument that word-specific experts function as specialized knowledge storage.

**Architectural differences to note:** The MoE-Top2 models use 12 sparse layers (every other layer) with no parameter sharing, while MoWE uses only 4 MoWE layers (2 encoder, 2 decoder) with full parameter sharing across them. The expert counts differ dramatically: 32–512 per layer for MoE-Top2 vs. 32K total (shared) for MoWE. Expert MLP dimensions: 2048 uniformly for MoE-Top2 vs. 512–2048 varying by frequency bucket for MoWE. The paper attributes MoWE's superior knowledge-task performance to "our strategy of using large knowledge-rich vocabulary to perform routing" (Section 4.2), though the ablation experiments that would isolate this factor (matching expert count while varying routing strategy) are not performed.

#### Evidence That the MoWE Layer Functions as a Sparse Memory (Table 3, Table 4)

**Headline:** Deactivating the experts associated with knowledge-bearing words (>32K routing IDs) causes a **9-point drop in TriviaQA EM**, and qualitative examples show that **deactivating a single expert can flip correct answers to incorrect ones**.

Table 3 reports the core finding: for a MoWE model finetuned on TriviaQA, keeping all experts activated yields 35.1 EM. Deactivating experts of tokens with routing IDs >32K (the Wikidata-derived, knowledge-bearing entries) during both finetuning and inference reduces EM to 25.6 — a drop of 9.5 points. The >32K threshold is chosen because "the first 32K routing ids roughly correspond to frequent and less knowledge-driven tokens that resulted from concatenating the default vocabulary to the auxiliary one" (Section 4.3). This means the deactivation specifically targets the experts that were created from the Wikidata-seeded knowledge-rich vocabulary, leaving only the experts corresponding to the default T5 subword vocabulary active.

Table 4 provides qualitative examples that make the memory interpretation concrete. For the experiment described, the MoWE model has a single MoWE layer in the encoder with 32K experts. For six example TriviaQA questions, the table shows the model's answer when the expert corresponding to the highlighted word is active (column "No") vs. deactivated (column "Yes"):

- "What is the name of Adele's first album?": "19" → "Addiction" (deactivating "Adele"'s expert changes the answer from correct to incorrect)
- "Who followed William Taft as US President?": "Woodrow Wilson" → "James Garfield"
- "Quinsy affects which part of the human body?": "Tonsils" → "Feet"
- "What country will host the 2022 FIFA World Cup competition?": "Qatar" → "Brazil"
- "What is Neptune's main satellite?": "Triton" → "Uranus" (deactivating "Neptune"'s expert)
- "What was the first name of Italian statesman and writer Machiavelli?": "Niccolo" → "Francois"

In each case, deactivating a single word's expert causes a complete change in the model's answer, from correct to incorrect. The paper emphasizes: "a single expert represents only 0.33% of the estimated total number of activated parameters" and "because the MoWE layer is frozen during finetuning, all the knowledge that is being leveraged in the downstream task comes from the pretraining corpus" (Section 4.3). This is strong evidence that factual knowledge acquired during pretraining is stored in specific, localizable expert parameters, and that removing those parameters removes the associated knowledge.

#### Comparison with Memory-Augmented Models (Table 5)

**Headline:** MoWE-Large **matches or outperforms TOME and EaE** on FEVER and TriviaQA, while requiring no specialized training data, auxiliary losses, entity annotations, or external retrieval mechanisms.

Table 5 compares MoWE with two recently proposed memory-augmented models on TriviaQA and FEVER. For this comparison, MoWE models are given an additional 40K pretraining steps on the Salient Span Masking (SSM) data from Guu et al. (2020), following Roberts et al. (2020), to make them "a little more specialized on Wikipedia domain, which is known to benefit tasks such as TriviaQA" (Section 4.4). Results are:

**TriviaQA (Exact Match, validation set):** EaE achieves 43.2, TOME 1 achieves 50.8, TOME 2 achieves 54.6. MoWE-Base + SSM achieves 44.9, MoWE-Large + SSM achieves 50.2. So MoWE-Large outperforms EaE by 7.0 points, matches TOME 1 (50.2 vs. 50.8 — a 0.6 point deficit), but trails TOME 2 by 4.4 points (50.2 vs. 54.6). The paper acknowledges that its results are on the validation set only because "the server used to score the test set is no longer active" (footnote 5) — the EaE and TOME numbers are presumably test set results from de Jong et al. (2022), which makes this comparison slightly apples-to-oranges.

**FEVER (Accuracy, dev/test):** EaE achieves 66.1/63.6, TOME 1 achieves 70.5/67.8, TOME 2 achieves 71.1/68.1. MoWE-Base + SSM achieves 69.1/66.9, and MoWE-Large + SSM achieves 70.5/68.7. MoWE-Large matches TOME 1 on dev and edges out TOME 1 on test (68.7 vs. 67.8) and TOME 2 on test (68.7 vs. 68.1 by 0.6 points), while trailing TOME 2 on dev (70.5 vs. 71.1).

The paper emphasizes that these comparisons are favorable to MoWE on a **simplicity** dimension: "EaE and TOME models are arguably more customized solutions to these tasks" (Section 4.4). Specifically:
- EaE and TOME treat TriviaQA as an entity linking task over a closed set of 1M Wikipedia entities; MoWE performs open-ended answer generation.
- EaE and TOME "use specialized training procedures, including adding additional loss functions and entity or noun phrase chunking, and require k-nn tools to search relevant embeddings in their memory" (Section 4.4).
- MoWE uses standard T5 span-masking pretraining on generic C4 data (plus optional SSM for this comparison), requires no entity annotations, and integrates memory access seamlessly into the model backbone without external retrieval tools.

The paper does not claim MoWE dominates these baselines on accuracy — it claims competitive accuracy with a simpler, more general approach. This is a meaningful distinction: MoWE's integrated memory architecture achieves most of the benefit of specialized memory-augmented models without the domain restriction and architectural complexity those models require.

#### Effectiveness of Knowledge-Driven Routing Vocabularies (Figures 4, 5, 6, 7, Appendix B)

This is the paper's primary ablation axis, demonstrating that the routing vocabulary is not merely a lookup table but a mechanism that shapes model performance. The experiments use a fixed MoWE baseline configuration (T5.1.1-Base backbone, 32K experts, 15.5B sparse parameters) and vary routing vocabulary size, number of experts, and number of MoWE layers.

**Routing vocabulary size at short training (Figure 4):** Models pretrained for 200K steps with routing vocabulary sizes of 32K, 65K, 131K, 262K, 524K, and 1M (top-K words from the 1M knowledge-rich vocabulary by C4 frequency). TriviaQA F1: 34.3 → 34.7 → 34.9 → **35.6** → 35.2 → 35.3. Performance improves up to 262K (+1.3 F1 over 32K) and plateaus or slightly declines thereafter. The paper interprets this as evidence that "routing with large knowledge-rich vocabularies leads to better performance" (Section 4.5), with the plateau at larger sizes suggesting diminishing returns from extremely rare vocabulary entries.

**Routing vocabulary size at full training (Figure 5):** Models pretrained for 1M steps (the full pretraining run) with vocabulary sizes of 32K, 262K, and 1M. TriviaQA F1: 37.2 → 39.1 → 39.0. Natural Questions F1: 32.8 → 33.9 → 34.3. The improvements are larger and more consistent at full training (+1.9 F1 from 32K to 1M on TriviaQA, +1.5 on Natural Questions), and the plateau at 1M is nearly flat (39.1 → 39.0 on TriviaQA). This suggests that with sufficient training, larger routing vocabularies continue to provide benefits, and the optimal vocabulary size for the 1M-step regime is around 262K–1M.

**Number of experts at fixed routing vocabulary (Figure 6):** With the routing vocabulary fixed at 1M and total sparse parameters fixed at 15.5B, varying the number of experts between 16K, 32K, and 64K. TriviaQA F1: 35.2 (16K) → 35.3 (32K) → 34.7 (64K). The paper identifies 32K as "a sweet spot in terms of number of experts for MoWE" (Appendix B.0.1). The degradation at 64K is attributed to the experts becoming smaller (since total parameters are fixed), with the paper noting that "using a larger number of smaller experts is preferable because it is more memory efficient and also speeds up our lookup table implementation of Expert Blocks in frequency bucket 4" (Appendix B.0.1) — though this argues *against* the drop at 64K. A possible reconciliation: at 64K experts with fixed total parameters, each expert is so small that its capacity to store useful knowledge is degraded, outweighing the specialization benefit.

**Matching expert count to routing vocabulary size (Figure 7):** A more extreme test: increasing the number of experts to match the routing vocabulary size (32K, 65K, 131K, 262K, 524K, 1M experts), while keeping total sparse parameters fixed by decreasing per-expert MLP dimensions. At 1M experts, each expert has an MLP dimension of only 8. TriviaQA F1: 34.5 (32K experts) → 34.2 (65K) → 34.1 (131K) → 34.0 (262K) → 33.7 (524K) → 32.6 (1M). There is a "progressive degradation in performance when matching the number of experts to the size of the vocabulary" (Appendix B.0.1). The paper attributes this to two factors: "the number of training updates that each expert receives becomes increasingly sparse" and "the size of the experts are decreased." The first factor is arguably the more fundamental: if an expert is activated only when its specific rare word appears, it might receive only a handful of gradient updates during the entire pretraining run, making it effectively untrained. The paper's architectural choice to share experts in bucket 4 (30,080 experts for ~220K vocabulary entries, ~7.3:1 sharing ratio) is a practical compromise between specialization and training signal.

**Number of MoWE layers (Table 8):** Ablating the number of MoWE layers in the encoder and decoder (all models trained for 200K steps): with 0 MoWE layers in decoder, encoder layers improve TriviaQA EM from 31.0 (1 layer) → 31.6 (2 layers) → 31.5 (3 layers, slight regression). Adding decoder layers helps: 2 encoder + 1 decoder achieves 32.4, and 2 encoder + 2 decoder achieves 33.1 — the best configuration tested. The paper's main architecture uses 2 encoder + 2 decoder MoWE layers, consistent with this ablation. The modest gain from 1 to 2 encoder layers and the regression at 3 suggests diminishing returns to routing-specific computation: two layers provide sufficient memory access bandwidth, and additional layers add routing overhead without proportionate benefit.

**Expert sizes per frequency bucket (Table 9):** For a single MoWE layer in the encoder, the paper scales expert MLP dimensions in each frequency bucket. Starting from [512, 256, 128, 64] (3.9B sparse parameters), doubling to [1024, 512, 256, 128] (7.8B) improves TriviaQA EM from 28.5 → 29.6, doubling again to [2048, 1024, 512, 256] (15.5B) yields 30.0, and the final doubling to [2048, 2048, 1024, 512] (31.0B) yields 31.0. The paper notes a "consistent improvement of 1 point in EM when doubling the model size" and speculates that "the increase would be larger if we pretrained the model for 1M steps instead of 200K steps" (Appendix B.0.1). This suggests that expert capacity — both the number of experts and their individual sizes — is a meaningful scaling axis, and that the 200K-step ablation regime may underestimate the benefits of larger experts.

**Freezing vs. unfreezing experts during finetuning (Appendix B.0.3):** The paper reports a single ablative comparison: "MoWE-Base on TriviaQA gets EM of 37.7 when freezing the experts during finetuning. When we allow the update of experts during finetuning, EM drops by 5 points to 33.5." This is a substantial degradation — over 11% relative drop in accuracy — and is the primary evidence for the paper's claim that experts function as a pretrained knowledge store that should not be overwritten by downstream task data. The paper does not explore intermediate regimes (e.g., finetuning with a very low learning rate for experts, or unfreezing only certain frequency buckets), which might have revealed nuances in this tradeoff.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper collectively support its central claims, but each claim comes with important scope limitations, and several gaps in the experimental design make some conclusions weaker than the paper suggests.

**Claim: MoWE outperforms T5 models with significantly fewer FLOPs on knowledge-intensive tasks.** The evidence in Table 1 and Figure 1 is strong and consistent across four knowledge-intensive datasets. MoWE-Base, which uses T5-Base's per-token FLOPs but has 31B parameters, achieves TriviaQA EM (39.4) comparable to T5.1.1-XL (36.0) and T5.1.1-XXL (42.9), and MoWE-Large (44.8) exceeds T5-XXL. The training speed ratios (4.3× and 6.6× relative to T5-Base) are substantial and well-documented. However, several qualifications are necessary:

- **Only encoder-decoder models are compared.** The T5.1.1 baseline is appropriate for MoWE since MoWE uses T5.1.1 as its backbone, but the field has largely moved to decoder-only architectures (GPT-style) for large-scale language modeling. Whether MoWE's advantages transfer to decoder-only architectures is untested. The encoder-decoder design may be particularly synergistic with MoWE because the encoder can use word experts to enrich token representations before the decoder generates answers — in a decoder-only model, the experts would be activated only on prefixes, which might be less informative.

- **The compute baseline is narrowly defined.** The paper measures "training time relative to T5.1.1-Base" on 64 TPU v3 chips with a specific batch size and sequence length configuration. This is a reasonable practical metric, but it conflates several factors: FLOPs, memory access patterns, communication overhead, and hardware utilization. The FLOPs-per-target-token metric in Figure 1 is more fundamental, but the paper does not report total pretraining FLOPs, making it difficult to compare MoWE's efficiency against FLOPs-matched dense models from other work. A reader wanting to reproduce the FLOPs-matched comparison would need to estimate T5 model FLOPs independently.

- **The T5-XL and T5-XXL baselines may not be optimally trained for their size.** The paper uses the same 1M-step pretraining budget for all T5 models (T5-Base through T5-XXL). Scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) suggest that larger models benefit from more training tokens. If T5-XXL were trained for longer (proportional to its parameter count), its performance might improve relative to MoWE-Large, narrowing the reported advantage. This is a well-known challenge in model comparison: fixing training tokens makes the larger model look worse because it is undertrained relative to its capacity, while scaling tokens optimally makes the comparison confounded (you're now comparing different total FLOPs budgets).

- **The routing cost is not fully isolated.** The MoWE architecture involves a hash-table lookup, frequency bucketing, and expert block routing that add computational overhead beyond the FLOPs of the expert FFNs themselves. The paper states that the top 16 most frequent routing tokens are not processed by any expert ("they can represent up to 28% of the tokens in a batch. This speeds up the training time and does not hurt downstream performance" — Appendix A.1), which partially offsets this overhead, but a detailed FLOPs breakdown (dense FFN vs. routing logic vs. expert computation) is not provided. The training time ratios in Table 1 include this overhead implicitly, but a reader trying to understand where the FLOPs go cannot reconstruct it from the reported data.

**Claim: MoWE outperforms standard MoE on knowledge-intensive tasks while matching on general NLU.** The evidence in Table 2 supports this for the specific configurations tested: MoWE with 32K shared experts beats MoE-Top2 with 32–512 unshared experts per layer on TriviaQA and WebQuestions at both 2B and 30B scales. However:

- **The comparison is not a controlled ablation of routing strategy alone.** The two architectures differ along multiple axes simultaneously: number of experts (32K shared vs. 32–512 per layer unshared), number of sparse layers (4 vs. 12), expert MLP dimensions (varying by bucket vs. uniform 2048), routing mechanism (fixed vocabulary-driven vs. learned top-2), presence of load-balancing loss (absent vs. present), and finetuning protocol (frozen experts vs. finetuned experts). The paper attributes MoWE's superiority to "our strategy of using large knowledge-rich vocabulary to perform routing" (Section 4.2), but this is an inference, not a demonstrated fact — any of the other differences could contribute to or cause the performance gap. A cleaner ablation would hold expert count, expert size, and number of sparse layers constant while varying only the routing mechanism (fixed vocabulary-driven vs. learned top-2).

- **The MoE-Top2 baselines use a specific configuration (GShard-style) that may not be optimal.** The paper uses 12 sparse layers (every other layer) for MoE-Top2, following the GShard convention, but more recent MoE work (e.g., ST-MoE, Zoph et al., 2022) uses different configurations and training stabilization techniques. The MoE-Top2 models use expert dropout 0.3 (stated in Appendix C), but other hyperparameters that affect MoE performance (capacity factor, auxiliary loss weight, router z-loss weight) are not reported. If the MoE-Top2 baselines are undertuned relative to MoWE, the comparison overstates MoWE's advantage.

- **The expert count scaling direction is not tested for learned routing.** The paper shows that MoWE can use 32K experts successfully, but does not test how far the learned-routing baseline can scale. The MoE-Top2 models use 32 or 512 experts per layer, but could learned routing handle 8K or 16K experts per layer with appropriate capacity and stability interventions? The paper cites Du et al. (2022)'s finding of diminishing returns beyond 64–128 experts, but this was in a specific architecture and training regime — it would strengthen the paper's claim to demonstrate the failure of learned routing at large expert counts rather than assume it.

**Claim: The MoWE layer functions as a sparse memory where factual knowledge is stored in specific experts.** The deactivation experiments (Tables 3, 4) provide compelling qualitative and quantitative evidence: a 9-point EM drop when deactivating knowledge-bearing experts, and individual expert deactivation flipping correct answers to incorrect ones. This is a genuinely informative experiment that directly supports the memory interpretation. The freezing ablation (37.7 → 33.5 EM when unfreezing experts during finetuning) is consistent with catastrophic forgetting of stored knowledge. However:

- **The deactivation experiment is performed on only one model configuration** (single MoWE layer in encoder, 32K experts, Table 4). It is unclear whether the same strong dependence on individual experts holds for models with multiple MoWE layers (where knowledge might be distributed across layers, reducing the impact of any single expert deactivation) or for models with different expert counts and sizes.

- **The mechanism of knowledge storage is not investigated.** The paper shows that deactivating an expert removes knowledge, but does not analyze *how* the knowledge is stored in that expert — whether it functions as a key-value memory (Geva et al., 2021), stores a compact representation of co-occurrence statistics, or something else. Experiments that probe the expert's internal representations (e.g., analyzing its weight matrix for factual associations, testing whether editing the expert's weights can update stored knowledge, or measuring whether the same expert stores facts about multiple entities) would strengthen the memory interpretation and connect it to the existing literature on knowledge localization in Transformers.

- **The >32K threshold for "knowledge-bearing" experts is approximate.** The first 32K routing IDs "roughly correspond to frequent and less knowledge-driven tokens" that come from concatenating the default vocabulary. But some default vocabulary tokens (e.g., "Paris", "London") likely carry factual associations, and their experts would remain active in the deactivation condition, diluting the measured effect. The 9-point drop is therefore a lower bound on the importance of the knowledge-bearing experts — the true effect of removing *all* factual knowledge storage might be even larger.

**Claim: MoWE matches more complex memory-augmented models without specialized mechanisms.** The comparison in Table 5 shows that MoWE-Large + SSM is competitive with TOME on FEVER (matching or slightly exceeding) and approaches TOME on TriviaQA (within 4.4 points of TOME 2). This supports the claim that integrated sparse memory can achieve comparable knowledge capacity to external retrieval-based memory. However:

- **The comparison is confounded by training data.** MoWE + SSM receives 40K additional pretraining steps on Wikipedia-derived span masking data, while EaE and TOME are pretrained entirely on Wikipedia with entity-aware objectives. It is unclear how much of MoWE's performance is attributable to the SSM fine-tuning versus the base architecture. A MoWE model without SSM compared against EaE/TOME would be a fairer test of the architectural claim.

- **The evaluation domain is narrow.** All comparisons are on TriviaQA and FEVER — both are Wikipedia-centric, knowledge-intensive tasks where the entities being queried are well-covered by Wikipedia and Wikidata (the source of MoWE's routing vocabulary). MoWE's routing vocabulary was explicitly constructed from Wikidata entity and relation names, so it is effectively *designed* for exactly this domain. Performance on knowledge-intensive tasks from other domains (scientific, medical, legal, financial) where the routing vocabulary might have poorer coverage is not evaluated, and the memory-augmented baselines (which also use Wikipedia entities) share this limitation.

- **TOME 2 substantially outperforms MoWE on TriviaQA** (54.6 vs. 50.2), and the paper does not discuss what accounts for this gap or whether it could be closed by further scaling MoWE. The claim of "similar performance to more complex memory augmented approaches" (Section 1) is reasonable for FEVER and TOME 1, but overstates the case for TOME 2 on TriviaQA.

**General experimental design concerns:**

- **No error bars or statistical significance testing.** All results are single-run numbers with no reported variance. Given that the test sets are modest in size (TriviaQA validation: ~10% of 87K ≈ 8.7K examples; WebQuestions: the standard split is ~2K test examples; Natural Questions: ~3.6K test examples according to the original paper), differences of 1–3 EM points could plausibly fall within run-to-run variance. This is particularly concerning for the ablation experiments, which use 200K-step pretraining (1/5 of the full run) and may have higher variance.

- **The ablation models are undertrained.** All ablation experiments in Appendix B use 200K pretraining steps rather than the full 1M steps. The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly when speculating that "the increase would be larger if we pretrained the model for 1M steps" (Table 9 commentary). Trends observed at 200K steps may not hold at 1M steps — for example, the routing vocabulary size ablation (Figures 4 vs. 5) shows that the plateau at larger vocabularies is more pronounced at 200K steps, and the 1M model continues to benefit from 262K and 1M vocabularies. This means the ablation results should be interpreted as *directional indicators* rather than precise estimates of the fully-trained behavior.

- **The SuperGLUE result for MoWE-Large underperforms T5-Large** (87.4 vs. 85.1 — wait, MoWE-Large 87.4 vs. T5.1.1-Large 85.1 actually outperforms by 2.3 points — but underperforms T5.1.1-XL 88.5 and T5.1.1-XXL 89.9). This is consistent with MoWE's design (specialized for knowledge storage, not general language processing), but the paper does not analyze whether this gap is an inherent limitation of the sparse architecture or could be closed by different expert allocation strategies. An experiment that varies the number of MoWE layers on SuperGLUE (to test whether the sparse layers interfere with or help with general NLU) would be informative.

- **No analysis of expert utilization or specialization.** The paper claims that experts specialize on content co-occurring with their assigned words, but provides no direct evidence for this claim beyond the deactivation experiments. Analyses that could support the specialization hypothesis but are absent include: measuring the variance of expert activations across different inputs, analyzing whether experts for semantically related words develop similar representations, testing whether knowledge about an entity is localized to its word expert or distributed across the expert pool, and probing expert representations for specific factual associations.

- **The computational cost of the routing infrastructure is not benchmarked.** The paper states that MoWE uses 64 TPU v3 for pretraining and reports training speed ratios, but does not decompose the training time into components: dense layer computation, expert FFN computation, routing table lookups, all-to-all communication, and frequency bucket orchestration. A reader attempting to implement MoWE would need to know where the computational bottlenecks lie — particularly whether the hash-table lookup on CPU or the all-to-all communication between expert blocks dominates the overhead at scale.

Despite these limitations, the experiments collectively establish MoWE as a novel and effective architecture for knowledge-intensive NLP tasks. The headline result — that vocabulary-driven sparse experts can match or exceed the knowledge capacity of much larger dense models at a fraction of the training cost — is robustly supported by the T5 comparison (Table 1). The ablation experiments, while undertrained, provide credible evidence that larger routing vocabularies and sufficient (but not excessive) numbers of experts improve performance. The deactivation experiments (Tables 3, 4) provide genuinely novel qualitative evidence that specific pieces of knowledge are localized to specific expert parameters — a finding that connects MoWE to the broader literature on knowledge localization in Transformers and opens avenues for future investigation into interpretable, auditable language models where facts can be inspected and modified at the level of individual experts.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in the Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The core efficiency claim — MoWE-Base is 4.3× faster to train than T5.1.1-XL while matching its accuracy — is calculated as the ratio of training wall-clock times (2.0× vs. 8.6× relative to T5-Base in Table 1). This comparison counts only the forward and backward passes of the Transformer, but MoWE introduces additional computational work that is not isolated in the cost model: the routing tokenization (online hash-table lookups across sub-sequences up to length 8 for every token position), the frequency bucketing dispatch, the all-to-all communication between expert blocks, and the expert block table lookups. The paper acknowledges that the top 16 most frequent routing tokens are excluded from MoWE processing to "speed up the training time" (Appendix A.1), which confirms that routing overhead is practically meaningful — if it were negligible, there would be no reason to exclude tokens to save time. However, the paper never quantifies what fraction of total training FLOPs or wall-clock time is consumed by routing logic versus dense computation versus expert computation.

**The consequence.** A practitioner attempting to estimate the total cost of deploying MoWE cannot determine how much of the reported 4.3× speedup comes from genuine FLOPs reduction (fewer multiply-adds per token because only one small expert is activated instead of a large dense FFN) versus how much is architecture-dependent (TPU v3's efficiency at SPMD all-to-all operations at the specific block count and batch size used). If the routing infrastructure adds, say, 30% overhead on a different hardware platform (GPUs, which have different communication topologies), the effective advantage over dense models shrinks. More importantly, the cost model is **inference-only in its machinery** (the MoWE layer substitutes a small expert FFN for a large dense FFN) but the speedup is reported as a **training** metric. Training involves backward passes where gradients flow through the routing infrastructure, and it's unclear whether the backward pass of the hash lookup and frequency dispatch is as efficient as the forward pass suggests. The paper reports that MoWE-Base is 2.0× slower than T5-Base at training (Table 1) — meaning the sparse architecture, despite activating fewer parameters per token, is actually *slower* than the dense model it's based on. The efficiency claim is only relative to the larger T5 models, not absolute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The training time ratios in Table 1 ("estimated by running each model with a batch size of 256 and input (output) sequence length of 512 (62) on 64 v3 TPUs") are the only cost data provided. The paper notes that this likely "underestimates the speed of the smaller models, which would enjoy better utilization on fewer devices" (Table 1 caption), meaning the 2.0× and 4.0× numbers for MoWE might be pessimistic — but the magnitude of the routing-specific overhead is not decomposed anywhere. Figure 1's x-axis uses "Float Operations per Target Token" but the calculation methodology for MoWE's FLOPs is not described, making it unclear whether routing operations (hash lookups, dispatch, communication) are included in the FLOPs count or excluded as non-arithmetic operations.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not provide a FLOPs breakdown, does not benchmark routing overhead in isolation, and does not test on hardware other than TPU v3. The choice to exclude the top 16 routing tokens (28% of batch tokens) to improve speed (Appendix A.1) is a practical acknowledgment of overhead but also a workaround rather than a solution — it means 28% of tokens receive *no* expert processing, which might harm performance on tasks where function words carry important syntactic or pragmatic information.

### The Routing Vocabulary Is Wikipedia/Wikidata-Centric, Limiting Knowledge Coverage to Those Domains

**The assumption or constraint.** The knowledge-rich routing vocabulary is constructed entirely from Wikidata entity and relation names (Section 2.4): "Start with the set of all entity and relation names that appears in a Wikidata dump," normalize and split, order by C4 frequency, and select the top 1M. This means the vocabulary — and therefore the entire expert index that determines which words get dedicated storage — is biased toward the entity coverage of Wikidata. Knowledge domains that are poorly represented in Wikidata (specialized scientific terminology, legal concepts, financial instruments, cultural knowledge from underrepresented regions, rapidly emerging entities) will have few or no dedicated experts.

The paper does not evaluate MoWE on any knowledge-intensive task outside the Wikipedia/Wikidata distribution. TriviaQA, WebQuestions, and Natural Questions are all general-knowledge QA datasets whose answers are predominantly Wikipedia entities. FEVER is a Wikipedia claim verification task. The memory-augmented baselines (EaE, TOME) share this Wikipedia focus. The pretraining data (C4) is a cleaned Common Crawl snapshot — broader than Wikipedia, but the routing vocabulary does not adapt to C4's coverage gaps. A term like "mRNA vaccine" or "zero-day exploit" that appears frequently in C4 but is absent from a static Wikidata dump would be broken into subword tokens lacking dedicated expert storage.

**The consequence.** MoWE's performance advantage on knowledge-intensive tasks may not transfer to domains where the routing vocabulary has poor coverage. If a downstream task involves querying facts about entities outside Wikidata's coverage, those entities get no dedicated expert, and their factual associations are stored in the general-purpose dense FFN layers (exactly as in a standard T5 model). In such domains, MoWE would likely perform similarly to a comparably-sized dense T5 model — still beneficial from the parameter scaling enabled by sparsity, but without the word-expert specialization that drives the large gains on TriviaQA. A user deploying MoWE for a domain-specific knowledge task (medical QA, legal document understanding, financial entity linking) would need to reconstruct the routing vocabulary from a domain-specific knowledge base, which the paper provides no procedure for.

More subtly, the static vocabulary creates a **knowledge cutoff at the time the Wikidata dump was produced**. Entities that came into existence or prominence after the dump date (new companies, products, political figures, scientific discoveries) have no dedicated experts, while pre-existing entities do. Over time, the model's effective knowledge storage becomes increasingly skewed toward older entities, and the sparse memory allocation becomes progressively misaligned with the distribution of entities in the world. Standard dense models also suffer from knowledge staleness, but the degradation is diffuse across all parameters; MoWE's architecture makes the staleness *structured* — precisely the words you'd most want dedicated storage for (emerging entities) are the ones least likely to have it.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no evaluation on non-Wikipedia/Wikidata knowledge tasks. All five evaluation datasets are either directly Wikipedia-derived (TriviaQA, FEVER) or general-knowledge QA whose answers heavily overlap with Wikipedia entities. The ablation on routing vocabulary size (Figures 4 and 5) shows improved performance with more vocabulary entries, which is consistent with better entity coverage — but only within the Wikidata-seeded vocabulary. No ablation tests what happens when the vocabulary is seeded from a different knowledge base, or when it deliberately excludes domain-specific terms to measure the performance impact. Appendix D shows examples of the routing vocabulary after position 6000, which are all general-knowledge content words ("horticulture," "diwali," "paprika") — no domain-specific terminology appears, confirming the vocabulary's generalist, Wikidata-centric character.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper states in Section 6 that the work "hopefully invites future research on word experts," implicitly acknowledging the vocabulary design space as open. But no procedure is suggested for extending the routing vocabulary to new domains, and no experiment tests robustness to vocabulary coverage gaps. A practitioner would need to independently develop a methodology for constructing domain-specific routing vocabularies and re-running pretraining.

### The Architecture Is Validated on a Single Model Family (Encoder-Decoder T5) with No Evidence of Transfer to Decoder-Only Architectures

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use T5.1.1 as the backbone — an encoder-decoder Transformer with span-masking pretraining. MoWE layers are inserted in both the encoder and the decoder (two layers each in the main configuration). This design leverages the encoder to process the full input context (including the question and any additional information), activate word experts for knowledge-bearing terms in the input, and produce enriched representations that the decoder can then attend to while generating answers. The paper motivates encoder placement as ensuring MoWE layers "receive a representation of the token that is already somewhat contextualized" (Section 3.2).

**The consequence.** It is unclear whether MoWE's benefits transfer to decoder-only architectures (GPT-style), which now dominate large-scale language modeling. In a decoder-only model, there is no separate encoder to process the full input before generation begins. Word experts would be activated on the prefix tokens as they are generated autoregressively, meaning the expert for a key entity in the question might be activated only after the question has been partially or fully processed — a fundamentally different information flow. The encoder-decoder design allows the model to use word-expert knowledge to *inform the entire decoding process*; a decoder-only design would need to integrate expert outputs into the autoregressive causal attention mask, which might limit their effectiveness.

This limitation matters because the paper's motivation is broadly about "decoupling learning capacity and FLOPs" (Section 1) for language models generally, not specifically for encoder-decoder architectures. If the approach requires an encoder-decoder backbone to be effective, its applicability is substantially narrower — it would not apply to GPT-style models used for few-shot prompting, open-ended generation, or chatbot applications. The paper does not discuss whether the MoWE layer could be adapted to decoder-only Transformers or what modifications would be needed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. All models are T5.1.1 encoder-decoder variants. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation or discuss decoder-only applicability. The related work (Section 5) discusses MoE models applied to both encoder-decoder (GShard) and decoder-only (GLaM, Switch Transformers) architectures, suggesting the authors are aware of the distinction, but no analysis of MoWE's architecture-specific dependencies is provided.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper positions MoWE as a general architecture for language models but tests it exclusively in one architectural paradigm. Future work would need to design a decoder-only MoWE variant and evaluate whether the word-expert mechanism provides comparable benefits when integrated into causal attention rather than bidirectional encoder processing.

---

### The Expert Freezing Strategy Is Validated on Only One Downstream Task, with No Exploration of Partial or Scheduled Unfreezing

**The assumption or constraint.** During downstream finetuning, all MoWE expert parameters are completely frozen. The paper justifies this with a single ablative comparison on TriviaQA (Appendix B.0.3): freezing yields 37.7 EM, unfreezing yields 33.5 EM — a 4.2-point drop. Based on this, the paper concludes that freezing is necessary "to avoid both overfitting and catastrophic forgetting of knowledge acquired during pretraining" (Section 3.2) and applies this policy uniformly to all downstream tasks.

**The consequence.** The freezing policy treats all experts identically regardless of their frequency bucket, their relevance to the downstream task, or the size of the finetuning dataset. This is a blunt instrument that may be suboptimal for several realistic scenarios:

- **Large downstream datasets.** On tasks with substantial finetuning data (hundreds of thousands of examples, unlike TriviaQA's ~78K training examples after the 10% validation holdout), unfreezing experts with careful regularization (low learning rate, early stopping, gradual unfreezing) might improve performance by allowing experts to specialize further on task-relevant knowledge without catastrophic forgetting. The paper does not test this.

- **Domain-specific tasks where pretraining knowledge is incomplete.** If a downstream task requires knowledge that was poorly covered in pretraining (e.g., domain-specific entities not in the routing vocabulary), allowing experts to update could incorporate new knowledge. Freezing prevents this entirely.

- **Different frequency buckets may have different forgetting profiles.** High-frequency experts (bucket 1, 2048-dim MLPs) receive many gradient updates during pretraining and may have robust, well-learned knowledge representations that resist catastrophic forgetting even with moderate finetuning. Low-frequency experts (bucket 4, 512-dim MLPs) receive fewer updates and may be more fragile — freezing might be more important for them specifically. The uniform freezing policy does not distinguish.

- **Negative transfer on SuperGLUE.** MoWE underperforms comparably-sized T5 models on SuperGLUE (Table 1: MoWE-Base 83.5 vs. T5.1.1-Large 85.1, MoWE-Large 87.4 vs. T5.1.1-XL 88.5). It's possible that frozen experts actually *harm* general NLU performance by forcing the model to route through a static memory that is optimized for factual recall, not for the syntactic and semantic processing SuperGLUE demands. Allowing experts to adapt during finetuning might close this gap, but the paper never tests this hypothesis.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only the single TriviaQA ablation (37.7 vs. 33.5). The paper does not report freezing vs. unfreezing results for any other task, for any MoWE layer configuration besides the standard one, or for any intermediate regime (e.g., unfreezing only bucket 4 experts, using a 10× smaller learning rate for experts, or gradually unfreezing experts after an initial freezing phase). The 4.2-point drop on TriviaQA is substantial but may not generalize — TriviaQA, with its relatively small finetuning set and high overlap with pretraining knowledge, may be particularly susceptible to catastrophic forgetting.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed beyond the single ablation. The paper presents freezing as a binary design choice validated by one data point, without exploring the broader design space of expert adaptation strategies. This limits the practical guidance for practitioners who might want to fine-tune MoWE on tasks with different dataset sizes or domain characteristics.

### Model Scaling, Training, and Evaluation Are Performed at a Modest Absolute Scale That May Obscure Scaling Trends

**The assumption or constraint.** The largest MoWE model (MoWE-Large) has 45.5B total parameters but only ~220M dense backbone parameters (T5-Large scale) — the remaining ~45.3B parameters are in the 32K sparse experts. Pretraining uses 1 trillion tokens (1M steps at batch size 2048 and sequence length 512). The largest T5 baseline is T5-XXL at ~11B parameters. While this is a substantial experimental scale, it is small relative to the frontier models that have driven recent progress (models at the 100B–500B parameter scale trained on multiple trillions of tokens). The paper's scaling conclusions — that word experts can substitute for dense parameters, that 32K is the sweet spot for expert count, that performance plateaus at ~262K routing vocabulary entries — are derived from experiments at this specific scale.

**The consequence.** Several of the paper's architectural conclusions may not hold at larger scales:

- **The optimal number of experts (32K, per Figure 6) might shift with model size.** A larger backbone (e.g., T5-XXL scale with 1024-dim hidden states) might benefit from more or larger experts because the backbone can extract richer contextual information for each token, making higher-capacity expert storage more useful. Alternatively, the optimal expert count might decrease if the backbone itself becomes better at storing knowledge.

- **The routing vocabulary size plateau (Figures 4, 5) might not be real.** At 1M training steps, the gap between 262K and 1M vocabularies is essentially flat (-0.1 TriviaQA F1, +0.4 Natural Questions F1). But with 10M or 100M training steps, larger vocabularies might show continued improvement as rare-term experts receive enough gradient updates to become useful. The paper's own observation that improvements are "more pronounced when training for longer" (Figure 5 vs. Figure 4) suggests the trend may continue.

- **The training speed ratios (4.3×, 6.6×) might compress at larger scales** if routing overhead scales poorly with expert count or if the SPMD communication pattern becomes less efficient when sharded across more devices.

- **The FLOPs-matched comparison with T5 might change if T5 were trained with compute-optimal scaling** (matching Hoffmann et al., 2022). The paper uses the same 1T token budget for all T5 sizes, which likely undertrains T5-XXL relative to its capacity. At larger scales with compute-optimal training, the dense baselines would be stronger, potentially reducing MoWE's relative advantage.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The scaling experiments are limited to two sizes (Base and Large) separated by approximately 1.5× in backbone parameters and 1.5× in sparse parameters. No experiments vary the backbone size independently of the expert configuration to test whether the optimal number of experts, expert dimensions, or routing vocabulary size scale with backbone capacity. The MoE comparison (Table 2) uses only the Base backbone. The ablation on expert sizes per frequency bucket (Table 9) shows consistent improvement when doubling expert capacity, suggesting that larger experts might continue to help — but this is tested only at the 200K-step scale. Figure 7 shows degradation at 1M experts, but this experiment fixes total sparse parameters (decreasing per-expert size), so it tests a different tradeoff than simply scaling up both expert count and backbone size together.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss how its findings might change at larger scales or acknowledge the modest absolute scale as a limitation. The scaling from Base to Large is used to demonstrate that MoWE's benefits persist across model sizes, but two data points do not establish a scaling trend, and the range covered (220M Base backbone to ~350M Large backbone) is narrow relative to the 3+ orders of magnitude across which scaling laws are typically studied.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

MoWE introduces a conceptual shift in how the field thinks about the relationship between sparsity, routing, and knowledge storage in language models. Before this work, the dominant paradigm treated mixture-of-experts models primarily as a **computational efficiency mechanism**: sparsity lets you increase parameter count without proportionally increasing FLOPs, and learned routing is the necessary (if troublesome) means to achieve that sparsity. Memory-augmented models occupied a separate design space entirely, treating memory as an external module requiring specialized training and retrieval.

MoWE collapses this distinction by demonstrating that **the sparse expert layers can themselves serve as the memory** — and that fixed, vocabulary-driven routing is not merely adequate but potentially superior to learned routing for this purpose. This is not an incremental efficiency improvement on standard MoE; it is a reframing of what sparse experts *are for*. The paper's evidence that individual experts store specific, localizable facts (Table 4: deactivating the expert for "Neptune" flips the answer for "What is Neptune's main satellite?" from "Triton" to "Uranus") makes this reframing concrete: experts are not just computation units that happen to be sparsely activated; they are parameterized memory cells indexed by the words they store knowledge about.

This reframing has several cascading effects on the research landscape:

**It challenges the necessity of learned routing in sparse models.** The field has invested heavily in solving the problems that learned routing creates — load-balancing losses (Shazeer et al., 2017), capacity factors (Lepikhin et al., 2020), router z-loss (Zoph et al., 2022), expert choice routing (Zhou et al., 2022) — under the implicit assumption that routing *must* be adaptive to be effective. MoWE's strong results with a deterministic hash function suggest that for the specific goal of factual knowledge storage, the problems that learned routing solves (flexibility, context-sensitivity) may be less important than the problems it creates (instability, load imbalance, poor specialization). This opens a design space the field had largely dismissed: **under what conditions is static, symbolic routing preferable to learned, continuous routing?** The paper's answer — when the goal is entity-indexed knowledge storage and the routing vocabulary is sufficiently knowledge-rich — provides a concrete hypothesis to test and refine.

**It provides a unified explanation for conflicting results in the MoE scaling literature.** Du et al. (2022) found diminishing returns beyond 64–128 experts in GLaM, while MoWE scales successfully to 32K experts (and up to 1M in ablations, Figure 7). The resolution is not that one architecture is "better" in absolute terms, but that **expert count interacts with routing strategy**: learned routing fails to provide useful specialization pressure at scale because the router has no incentive to assign words consistently to the same experts, while fixed routing naturally creates word-expert associations that lead to meaningful specialization. This reframes the expert scaling question from "how many experts can we train?" to "what routing strategy produces useful specialization at the expert count we want?"

**It reconnects the MoE literature to the knowledge localization literature.** Work by Geva et al. (2021), Dai et al. (2022), and Meng et al. (2022) has established that factual knowledge in Transformers is stored in FFN layers and can be localized to specific parameters. MoWE takes this finding and builds it into the architecture itself: by design, facts about "Turing" are stored in the expert indexed by "Turing." This makes MoWE a natural platform for knowledge editing and interpretability research — rather than searching for which parameters store a fact (as in Meng et al., 2022), you know exactly which expert to inspect or modify. The paper does not explore this direction, but the architecture makes it newly tractable.

**It suggests a path toward models where storage and processing are architecturally separated.** Standard dense Transformers mix factual storage, syntactic processing, and reasoning into the same weight matrices. Standard MoE partially separates these by splitting the FFN into experts, but with only 32–128 experts, each one still stores a heterogeneous mix of facts. MoWE's large number of small, word-specific experts represents a step toward **disentangled architectures** where factual associations are stored in dedicated, interpretable units (word experts) while general linguistic processing happens in the dense backbone. The paper does not make this claim explicitly, but the architecture — frozen experts providing knowledge to a trainable dense backbone — instantiates this separation. Future architectures might push this further, with separate expert pools for different knowledge types (factual, linguistic, procedural) accessed through different routing mechanisms.

**Where this work sits on the paradigm-shift spectrum.** MoWE is best understood as a **conceptual reframing with strong empirical backing**, not a paradigm shift. It does not introduce fundamentally new mathematical machinery (experts are standard FFNs, routing is hash-based lookup, training is standard cross-entropy) nor does it establish new theoretical principles. Rather, it takes existing components — MoE sparsity, fixed routing, knowledge-rich vocabularies — and combines them in a way that changes how we think about what these components are doing. The paper's most durable contribution may be less the specific MoWE architecture and more the **demonstration that vocabulary design is a first-class architectural lever** for controlling what sparse models learn and store. This is a concept that generalizes beyond MoWE: any model with discrete, indexable storage could benefit from careful construction of the index.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Knowledge editing via direct expert weight modification.** The deactivation experiments (Tables 3, 4) demonstrate that specific facts are stored in specific, localizable experts. This makes MoWE a uniquely tractable platform for knowledge editing: if the model's belief that Neptune's main satellite is Triton is stored in the expert for "Neptune," updating that belief should require modifying only that expert's parameters, with minimal impact on other knowledge. A concrete experiment: take the MoWE-Base model, identify the expert(s) responsible for a set of facts (e.g., via the deactivation methodology from Table 4), apply a targeted weight update (using an approach analogous to ROME (Meng et al., 2022) but operating on a specific expert rather than the entire FFN), and measure (a) whether the target fact is successfully updated, (b) whether other facts about the same entity are preserved, and (c) whether facts about unrelated entities are unaffected. The key metric is **specificity**: does editing one expert affect only the facts stored in that expert, or does knowledge bleed across the expert pool? A null result — finding that knowledge is distributed across many experts despite the architectural design — would be equally informative, suggesting that word-expert routing does not prevent the model from developing distributed representations.

**Domain-adaptive routing vocabularies and continual pretraining.** The paper constructs its routing vocabulary from Wikidata and evaluates only on Wikipedia-centric tasks. A natural stress test is to construct **domain-specific routing vocabularies** (e.g., from the UMLS medical ontology, from SEC EDGAR financial entities, from the USPTO patent database) and measure whether MoWE pretrained with these vocabularies shows disproportionate improvement on domain-specific QA compared to a Wikidata-vocabulary MoWE or a dense T5 model. The hypothesis is that the routing vocabulary acts as a prior over what knowledge gets dedicated storage — a medical vocabulary should produce experts that specialize on drug interactions and disease phenotypes, while a financial vocabulary should produce experts that specialize on company metrics and market terminology. This experiment would also test the paper's implicit claim that the vocabulary construction procedure (Section 2.4) is general: does the Wikidata seed + C4 frequency ranking recipe work for domains where WikiData coverage is sparse? A negative result might show that the routing vocabulary's quality depends heavily on the seed knowledge base's coverage of the target domain, motivating research into vocabulary construction from raw corpora without a structured knowledge base.

**Mechanistic analysis of expert specialization during pretraining.** The paper *claims* that fixed routing encourages experts to specialize on content co-occurring with their assigned words, but provides no direct evidence beyond the deactivation experiments. A mechanistic study would track expert representations over the course of pretraining to answer: (1) At what point in training do experts begin to develop word-specific specializations? (2) Do experts for semantically related words (e.g., "mathematician" and "physicist") develop similar internal representations, or are they independently specialized? (3) What is stored in the experts for high-frequency function words (bucket 1, 2048-dim MLPs) versus rare content words (bucket 4, 512-dim MLPs) — do the former serve as general linguistic processors while the latter are purely factual stores? (4) When an expert is shared across multiple routing IDs in bucket 4 (~7.3 routing IDs per expert), does it develop a shared representation that captures commonalities among those words, or does it behave as a superposition of independent sub-expert circuits? This would use techniques from the mechanistic interpretability toolkit — probing classifiers, activation patching, sparse autoencoders — applied to expert representations at different layers and training checkpoints. The key insight sought is whether MoWE's architectural inductive bias (one expert per word) actually produces the intended specialization, or whether the model finds ways to circumvent the bias (e.g., by offloading factual storage to dense layers to compensate for insufficient expert capacity).

**Scaling expert count with backbone size to identify the storage-computation Pareto frontier.** The paper tests two backbone sizes (Base and Large) but keeps the expert configuration (32K experts, same bucket dimensions, same routing vocabulary) constant across both. A systematic scaling study would jointly vary backbone size (T5-Small through T5-XXL) and expert configuration (expert count, expert dimensions per bucket, routing vocabulary size) and map the resulting **Pareto frontier of TriviaQA accuracy vs. total parameters and FLOPs**. The key question: does the optimal expert count scale with backbone capacity, or is there an absolute ceiling on useful specialization? The paper's Figure 6 shows 32K as a sweet spot for the Base backbone, but a larger backbone with higher-dimensional hidden states might benefit from more experts (to store more knowledge) or larger experts (to integrate stored knowledge more effectively with richer token representations). This experiment would produce **scaling laws for sparse memory** analogous to the dense scaling laws of Kaplan et al. (2020) and Hoffmann et al. (2022), providing practical guidance for allocating parameters between the dense backbone and the sparse expert pool. A negative result — finding that expert count optimum is independent of backbone size — would suggest that the expert pool saturates as a knowledge store at a certain capacity, and further gains require better backbone processing rather than more memory.

**Decoder-only MoWE for autoregressive language modeling.** The paper exclusively uses an encoder-decoder T5 backbone, but most large language models today are decoder-only (GPT-style). A decoder-only MoWE variant would need to address: (1) where to insert MoWE layers (middle layers, similar to the encoder-decoder design?); (2) how causal attention interacts with expert processing (since the expert for a question entity is activated only when the entity is generated or encountered in the prefix, not in a separate encoding pass); (3) whether the hash-based routing ID assignment works with autoregressive token generation (the paper claims it does, Section 2.4, but no empirical validation is provided). A concrete experiment: implement a decoder-only MoWE based on a GPT-2 or LLaMA-scale backbone, pretrain on C4 or The Pile, and evaluate on closed-book QA (TriviaQA, Natural Questions) and general language modeling (perplexity on held-out text). The key comparison is whether the word-expert mechanism provides the same disproportionate benefit on knowledge-intensive tasks in a decoder-only setting, or whether it requires bidirectional encoder context to be effective. A negative result — decoder-only MoWE showing minimal gains over a dense baseline — would bound the architectural generality of the approach and suggest that the encoder plays a critical role in integrating expert knowledge that the paper does not identify.

**Adaptive expert freezing and continual learning.** The paper freezes all experts during all downstream finetuning based on a single TriviaQA ablation (Appendix B.0.3). This is a blunt policy with unexplored alternatives. A systematic study would evaluate a spectrum of adaptation strategies: (1) fully frozen (the paper's default), (2) fully unfrozen, (3) unfrozen with per-bucket learning rates (lower for high-frequency buckets where pretraining knowledge is most concentrated, higher for low-frequency buckets where experts may be undertrained), (4) gradual unfreezing (start frozen, unfreeze later in finetuning once task-specific adaptation has stabilized), (5) selective unfreezing based on expert relevance to the downstream task (unfreeze only experts for words that appear frequently in the finetuning data). The experiment would test these across tasks with varying finetuning dataset sizes (1K, 10K, 100K, 1M examples) and domain overlap with pretraining (high overlap: TriviaQA; low overlap: BioASQ biomedical QA). The hypothesis is that freezing is most important when the finetuning dataset is small and the domain overlaps with pretraining (the catastrophic forgetting regime), but that unfreezing becomes beneficial with large finetuning datasets or domain shift (where pretraining knowledge needs to be supplemented). This would provide practical guidance for MoWE finetuning and also test a deeper question: to what extent is the pretrained knowledge in experts *consolidated* (requiring preservation) versus merely *initialized* (benefiting from further training)?

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient deployment for knowledge-intensive enterprise search and QA.** Organizations running internal question-answering systems over domain-specific knowledge bases (corporate wikis, product documentation, regulatory filings) face a direct tradeoff between model accuracy and inference cost. Currently, they must choose between a small, fast model with poor factual recall and a large, slow model that can store enterprise knowledge. MoWE offers a third option: pretrain a MoWE model with a routing vocabulary constructed from the organization's entity catalog (product names, internal jargon, regulatory terms) and the general Wikidata vocabulary, yielding a model with dedicated expert storage for enterprise-specific entities alongside broad world knowledge. The paper's results suggest this model would achieve the accuracy of a much larger dense model (e.g., MoWE-Base matching T5-XXL on TriviaQA, 4.3× faster training, Figure 1) at a fraction of the inference latency. The practical benefit is **reduced serving costs for latency-bounded applications**: a customer support chatbot that must respond within 200ms can deploy MoWE on a single accelerator rather than requiring a cluster to run a larger dense model within the latency budget.

**Retrieval-free knowledge grounding for resource-constrained edge devices.** Voice assistants on phones, smart speakers, and wearable devices have strict compute and memory constraints that preclude running large dense models or making network calls to retrieval-augmented systems. MoWE's sparse activation pattern — only a handful of small experts are active per token — is naturally suited to this regime: the total active parameter count per token is comparable to a small dense model, but the stored knowledge capacity is orders of magnitude larger because the inactive experts sit in device memory (or can be streamed from flash storage on demand) without being computed. A MoWE-Base configured with 31B total parameters but T5-Base FLOPs (Section 3.2, Table 1) could potentially run on-device for tasks like "set a timer," "what's the capital of Burkina Faso," or "who wrote The Great Gatsby" — tasks that mix procedural command execution with factual recall. The key enabler is that factual knowledge (stored in experts) is accessed sparsely and on-demand, while the small dense backbone handles routine processing. The paper's result that MoWE-Base achieves 39.4 TriviaQA EM versus 24.2 for T5-Base (Table 1) suggests this device would answer general-knowledge questions far more accurately than a small dense model, without the latency penalty of cloud-based retrieval.

**Interpretable factual auditing and selective knowledge removal.** Regulatory requirements (GDPR "right to be forgotten," content moderation policies, enterprise data retention policies) increasingly demand that deployed models be auditable for what facts they have memorized and capable of forgetting specific pieces of information. MoWE's architecture makes this tractable in a way that dense models are not: each expert is associated with a specific word or small group of words, and the deactivation experiments (Tables 3, 4) demonstrate that removing an expert removes the associated factual knowledge. An auditor could query the model about a specific entity (e.g., "what do you know about [REDACTED_PERSON]?") and, if the model produces sensitive information, identify the responsible expert (the one indexed by the entity's name in the routing vocabulary) and remove or overwrite it. The paper shows that deactivating knowledge-bearing experts causes a 9-point TriviaQA EM drop (Table 3), confirming that knowledge is substantially localized. This is a practical capability that dense models cannot offer — in a dense model, knowledge about any given entity is diffused across millions of parameters with no known indexing scheme, making surgical knowledge removal essentially impossible without degrading the entire model. MoWE's word-expert mapping provides a natural deletion interface: to forget facts about entity X, delete or retrain expert X.

**Rapid domain adaptation via vocabulary expansion and expert initialization.** When deploying a language model in a new domain (e.g., a pharmaceutical company releasing an internal LLM for drug discovery literature), the vocabulary of important entities is known in advance from domain ontologies. MoWE allows a targeted adaptation procedure: (1) expand the routing vocabulary with domain-specific entity names (drug names, gene symbols, disease phenotypes), (2) initialize new experts for these vocabulary entries (randomly or via a domain-specific pretraining corpus), (3) continue pretraining on the domain corpus with the standard MoWE objective, without modifying the general-domain experts if they are frozen. This is more parameter-efficient than continued pretraining of a dense model (which would update all parameters and risk catastrophic forgetting of general knowledge) and simpler than training a separate retrieval index (which would require maintaining and querying an external knowledge base). The paper's finding that expert freezing during finetuning preserves pretraining knowledge (Appendix B.0.3: 37.7 → 33.5 when unfrozen on TriviaQA) suggests that this kind of additive adaptation — adding new experts without modifying existing ones — could expand the model's knowledge coverage without degrading existing capabilities. A practical deployment would precompute routing vocabularies for common domains (legal, medical, scientific) and ship them as "expert expansion packs" that a base MoWE model loads on demand.
