## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Quartet II**, a fully-NVFP4 computation scheme for LLM pre-training that improves gradient estimation by replacing element-wise stochastic rounding (SR) with a novel unbiased quantization primitive called **MS-EDEN** (MicroScaling EDEN), which shifts stochasticity from individual FP4 values to the microscale factors while retaining unbiasedness in expectation and achieving more than 2× lower quantization error. Integrated into a linear-layer training graph that pairs MS-EDEN on the backward pass with native NVFP4 forward-pass quantization augmented by the "Four-over-Six" scale selection heuristic, Quartet II reduces validation loss gaps relative to BF16 by at least 20% compared to prior NVFP4 recipes on Llama-2-style models (30M–200M parameters) and cuts the pre-training loss gap by 15–25% in Nanochat runs up to 1.9B parameters on 38B tokens, while delivering up to 4.2× linear-layer speedup over BF16 on NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs—establishing that unbiased gradient estimation at 4-bit precision can approach BF16-quality optimization only when the quantization routine is designed to avoid the high per-element variance of stochastic rounding.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Training Massive Models Is Prohibitively Expensive, and 4-Bit Training Isn't Good Enough Yet

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **the computational cost of training state-of-the-art foundation models is increasing exponentially**, and the most promising hardware-accelerated path to reducing this cost — 4-bit floating-point training — doesn't work well enough yet to replace higher-precision training without unacceptable accuracy degradation.

Section 1 frames the problem through a sustainability lens. The authors cite the well-documented exponential growth in training compute (Amodei & Hernandez, 2018; Sevilla et al., 2022), noting that modern Transformer training is dominated by dense matrix multiplications (GEMMs) in attention and MLP blocks. Reducing the precision of these GEMMs — the workhorses of every forward and backward pass — is therefore "one of the most direct levers for lowering end-to-end training costs." The progression from FP32 to FP16/BF16 to FP8 (Micikevicius et al., 2022) has demonstrated that each precision halving can yield substantial throughput gains, and the next frontier is 4-bit.

This matters for a concrete, practical reason beyond mere cost: **hardware support for 4-bit training has arrived before the algorithmic maturity to exploit it**. NVIDIA's Blackwell GPUs provide native acceleration for 4-bit microscaling formats (NVFP4 and MXFP4) with 2–4× throughput gains over FP8 (NVIDIA, 2024). The hardware exists. The question is whether we can train models at this precision without sacrificing the model quality that makes foundation models useful. If the answer is yes, the economics of training shift dramatically. If the answer is no, the hardware capability goes underutilized, and the cost trajectory of foundation model training continues unsustainably.

The theoretical significance is equally important. Training at 4 bits pushes quantization to a regime where **naive rounding introduces systematic biases that compound over thousands of optimization steps**, leading to divergence on long pre-training runs. The paper's focus on *unbiased* gradient estimation connects to a much broader literature in stochastic optimization and distributed learning, where unbiasedness is a key sufficient condition for convergence guarantees. Demonstrating that careful quantizer design can preserve the convergence properties of full-precision training at 4-bit precision would validate a core principle of stochastic optimization under extreme compression.

### Where Prior FP4 Training Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies a clear lineage of prior work and systematically explains where each piece of the puzzle leaves gaps.

**The consensus principles.** Emerging work on stable FP4 training (Tseng et al., 2025; Castro et al., 2025; Chmiel et al., 2025) has converged on two guiding design principles that the paper adopts as starting points (Section 1):

> "First, the forward pass should seek to maximize representation capacity by minimizing the quantization error of activations and weights, typically measured via mean-square error (MSE). Second, the backward pass is especially sensitive to bias: as such, biased gradient estimators can accumulate systematic error over many steps, making unbiased (or carefully controlled) gradient quantization essential for stable convergence."

These principles are not the paper's contribution — they represent the consensus of the prior art — but the paper uses them to frame *why* existing methods, despite following these principles, still fall short.

**NVIDIA's NVFP4 recipe and its limitations.** NVIDIA et al. (2025) introduced the first end-to-end NVFP4 pre-training recipe, combining square-block quantization, Hadamard rotations on the backward pass, and keeping some layers in higher precision. This was a milestone — it showed that 4-bit pre-training was possible at all. However, the paper notes in Section 1 that "current state-of-the-art FP4 recipes still drop significant accuracy relative to FP8 and FP16." The implication is that existing recipes are *functional* but not *competitive*. For practitioners deciding whether to adopt FP4 training, a recipe that produces measurably worse models than FP8 or BF16 — even if it *works* — is a tough sell unless the accuracy gap can be narrowed.

**TetraJet-v2's refinements and practicality gaps.** Chen et al. (2025b) proposed TetraJet-v2 as an upgrade to the NVIDIA recipe, introducing corrected activation re-quantization in the backward pass, weight re-quantization similar to Castro et al. (2025), intermediate-level FP32 scales, and selective outlier channels. The paper acknowledges these as genuine improvements but identifies a critical practical shortcoming (Section 2):

> "The practicality of these format changes is hard to validate, as it requires substantially more complicated kernel support that is not provided by the authors."

This is a recurring theme in low-precision training research: algorithmic innovations that look good on paper cannot be executed efficiently on real hardware because they require kernel operations that don't map cleanly to tensor core instructions. The paper implicitly argues that practicality — the ability to deliver the promised speedups on actual GPUs — must be a first-class design constraint, not an afterthought.

**Four-over-Six: a forward-pass improvement that breaks backward-pass correctness.** Cook et al. (2025) proposed "Four-over-Six" (4/6), a clever scale selection heuristic for NVFP4 quantization: evaluate two potential scale factors (4.0 and 6.0) for each block of values and pick whichever yields lower MSE. This provides a clean forward-pass improvement. However, the paper identifies a critical correctness issue (Section 4.2):

> "In the form proposed, it does not constitute an unbiased estimation, as the act of picking a lower MSE scale branch introduces bias, even if both scale branches are individually unbiased via SR."

This is a subtle but important point. The 4/6 heuristic makes a data-dependent choice between two quantizers. Even if each quantizer is individually unbiased when selected *randomly*, making a deterministic choice based on which one produces lower error introduces a systematic preference — the quantizer that systematically rounds values slightly downward (or upward) might be selected more often, creating a persistent bias in the gradient estimates. The paper validates this empirically in Appendix A (Figure 9), showing that "NVIDIA+4/6" plateaus in its gradient error reduction as the number of accumulation steps increases, rather than continuing to decrease as 1/B — the hallmark of bias. This means that 4/6, as originally proposed, cannot be used on the backward pass if unbiasedness is required.

**The shared vulnerability: element-wise stochastic rounding.** This is the paper's central diagnosis. All existing FP4 training methods — NVIDIA et al. (2025), TetraJet-v2, Castro et al. (2025), Chmiel et al. (2025), Tseng et al. (2025) — use element-wise stochastic rounding (SR) to achieve unbiasedness on the backward pass. SR works by randomly rounding each individual value up or down with probability proportional to its distance to the two nearest representable FP4 values, making the expected value equal to the original. The paper states this explicitly in Section 3.1:

> "To our knowledge, all existing FP4 training methods... utilize element-wise stochastic rounding for unbiasedness."

But SR has a fundamental tradeoff: **unbiasedness comes at the cost of approximately 2.5× higher MSE compared to round-to-nearest (RTN)**. Table 1 quantifies this dramatically. For NVFP4 quantization with native 1×16 group scales on N(0,1) data, RTN achieves an MSE of 9.0 × 10⁻³, while SR achieves 23.5 × 10⁻³ — a 2.6× inflation. Even with 4/6 added, SR still sits at 17.5 × 10⁻³, roughly double RTN's baseline error. This variance penalty is not a minor inefficiency; it directly translates to noisier gradient estimates, which in turn slow convergence and leave a larger gap relative to higher-precision training.

### The EDEN Insight: A Different Path to Unbiasedness

The paper's key intellectual move is to recognize that an alternative unbiased quantization technique from the distributed optimization literature — EDEN (Vargaftik et al., 2022) — could, in principle, solve SR's variance problem. EDEN avoids per-element stochastic rounding entirely. It works by applying a randomized Hadamard transform (RHT) to the vector, quantizing the rotated vector using *deterministic* round-to-nearest (which has lower error), and then applying a corrective rescaling factor S that restores unbiasedness in expectation.

The corrective factor S is computed as the ratio of inner products:

$$S = \frac{\langle x, x \rangle}{\langle \text{RHT}(x, \omega), Q(\text{RHT}(x, \omega)) \rangle}$$

The intuition is that the RHT spreads information across all dimensions, making individual quantization errors less harmful, while the rescaling S compensates for the global bias introduced by RTN clipping. The result is an estimator that is nearly unbiased (asymptotically exact as dimension grows) with substantially lower error than SR — because RTN, being deterministic, doesn't inject the per-element randomization noise that SR does.

### Why EDEN Doesn't Work Out-of-the-Box for NVFP4

Here lies the critical gap that motivates the paper's technical contribution. EDEN's correction factor S cannot be directly applied to NVFP4's microscaling format. The paper explains this precisely in Section 3.2:

> "The scale correction factor S proposed by EDEN has values in the interval [0.94, 1.06] in practice, requiring a high precision representation for storage. As such, it is incompatible with the coarse compressed scale representation of NVFP4: the minimum relative update that can be accommodated by FP8 scales is ×1.0625."

In plain language: NVFP4 stores per-group scales in FP8 (E4M3), which has a relative precision that can only represent multiplicative steps of 1.0625 (i.e., 1 + 1/16). The EDEN correction factors, which fall in the range [0.94, 1.06], need finer granularity to be faithfully represented — you can't simply quantize S to FP8 without losing the precise bias correction that makes EDEN work. The FP32 per-tensor global scale cannot absorb this either, because the correction factors vary per rotation group (e.g., every 128 elements), while the global scale is a single scalar per tensor.

This is not a minor implementation detail. It is a **format-level incompatibility** between the mathematical mechanism that makes EDEN unbiased and the storage format that makes NVFP4 hardware-efficient. Any solution must either modify the EDEN correction mechanism to work within FP8 precision constraints, or accept some loss of unbiasedness — which is precisely what the paper refuses to do.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as solving this precise bottleneck: **how to get the variance benefits of EDEN-style deterministic quantization while preserving unbiasedness within the practical constraints of the NVFP4 format and NVIDIA Blackwell hardware**.

This positioning is crystallized in the paper's stated contributions (Section 1):

1. **MS-EDEN**: A new unbiased quantization primitive that "moves the stochasticity from individual FP4 values to the microscale factors." Instead of rounding each FP4 element stochastically, MS-EDEN quantizes elements deterministically (RTN) and applies stochastic rounding only to the FP8 group scales when applying the EDEN correction. Since the scales are stored at FP8 precision — which is an order of magnitude finer than FP4 — the variance introduced by this scale-level stochasticity is negligible compared to per-element FP4 stochastic rounding.

2. **Quartet II**: A fully-NVFP4 computation graph that combines this backward-pass improvement with a forward pass using native 1×16 group scales (instead of NVIDIA's square-block 16×16) augmented by 4/6 scale selection, yielding better forward-pass representation capacity *and* better backward-pass gradient estimation simultaneously.

3. **Kernel support**: Efficient CUDA kernels that make the full scheme runnable on NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs with real speedups, closing the loop from algorithmic innovation to practical deployment.

The paper's relationship to prior work is collaborative rather than adversarial. It adopts the consensus principles (unbiased backward pass, MSE-minimizing forward pass), acknowledges the contributions of NVIDIA et al. (2025) and TetraJet-v2 (Chen et al., 2025b), incorporates 4/6 (Cook et al., 2025) on the forward pass where it's safe, and builds on the EDEN framework (Vargaftik et al., 2022). The novel claim is that **all prior work settled for a suboptimal unbiasedness mechanism (element-wise SR), and that replacing it with MS-EDEN yields a Pareto improvement — better accuracy with no loss of hardware compatibility.** Figures 1 and 4 provide the empirical backbone for this claim, showing that MS-EDEN consistently outperforms SR across all backward-pass quantization configurations and model sizes, and that the full Quartet II scheme reduces the validation loss gap to BF16 by at least 20% relative to prior NVFP4 recipes.

The paper also makes an implicit methodological argument: **practical kernel feasibility should constrain algorithmic design from the start.** By building MS-EDEN around operations that map cleanly to Blackwell tensor core instructions (RHT as GEMM, stochastic rounding only on scales, post-hoc range alignment for efficient kernel fusion in Section 7), the paper avoids the "impractical algorithm" trap that it identifies in TetraJet-v2. This design philosophy — hardware-aware algorithm development — is increasingly common in the efficient ML literature and is central to the paper's claim of practical impact.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Quartet II is a **complete recipe for running every matrix multiplication in a Transformer linear layer at 4-bit precision** (NVFP4 format) during LLM pre-training, without sacrificing convergence quality relative to standard BF16 or FP8 training. The system solves the problem that existing 4-bit training methods introduce too much noise into gradient estimates — specifically because they use per-element stochastic rounding to achieve unbiasedness — and it solves this by **moving the randomness away from individual values and onto the per-group scale factors**, where the precision is higher and the variance impact is negligible, yielding unbiased gradients with less than half the quantization error.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Quartet II system has four major components that together define how a single linear layer `$Y = XW^T$` is computed and differentiated:

1. **Forward Pass Quantizer (4/6 RTN):** The input activations `$X$` and the weight matrix `$W$` are each quantized to NVFP4 using deterministic round-to-nearest (RTN) with native 1×16 group scales, augmented by the "Four-over-Six" scale selection heuristic that picks the better of two quantization grids. The quantized tensors are multiplied using NVFP4 tensor cores, producing the layer output `$Y$`. The quantized `$X$` and `$W$` are saved for reuse on the backward pass.

2. **Backward Pass Weight-Gradient GEMM (`$\partial L / \partial W = E^T X$`):** The output gradient `$E$` flows in from the next layer. Both `$E$` and the saved (dequantized, transposed) activations `$X^T$` are rotated via a randomized Hadamard transform (RHT) along their shared inner dimension, then quantized to NVFP4 using **MS-EDEN** — the new unbiased quantization primitive — and multiplied in NVFP4. The rotations cancel along the inner dimension, so the product needs no inverse transform.

3. **Backward Pass Input-Gradient GEMM (`$\partial L / \partial X = E W$`):** Symmetrically, the output gradient `$E$` and the saved (dequantized, transposed) weight matrix `$W$` are rotated along their inner dimension, quantized with MS-EDEN, and multiplied. Again, rotations cancel, and the result flows backward to the previous layer.

4. **GPU Kernel Implementation (Section 7):** The RHT, quantization, and dequantization steps are fused into efficient CUDA kernels, with a hardware-aware optimization called **post-hoc range alignment** that avoids loading and rotating each tensor twice. The matrix multiplications themselves use NVFP4 tensor cores via the QuTLASS library.

The critical insight that makes this architecture coherent: **MS-EDEN requires both operands of each backward GEMM to be re-quantized** (because the RHT must be applied along the inner dimension for both), which means the forward pass can use native 1×16 group scales for maximum representation capacity — there is no need to compromise on forward-pass quality to avoid weight re-quantization on the backward pass, because that re-quantization is happening anyway.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the MS-EDEN quantization primitive itself** — because it is the paper's core technical contribution and the foundation that everything else builds on. Understanding how it achieves unbiasedness without per-element stochastic rounding is prerequisite to understanding why Quartet II works.
- **Second, the forward pass quantization design** and the representation-requantization tradeoff — because the choice of forward-pass quantization determines what tensors are available (and in what format) for the backward pass, and the paper's rejection of square-block quantization in favor of native 1×16 scales is a deliberate architectural decision motivated by MS-EDEN's requirements.
- **Third, the full Quartet II computation graph** that assembles the forward pass and both backward GEMMs into a single unified training recipe — because this is where the design choices interact and where the unbiasedness guarantees are composed.
- **Fourth, the GPU kernel implementation details** — because the paper claims practical deployability, and the post-hoc range alignment optimization is a format-specific hardware hack that makes MS-EDEN efficient on real Blackwell GPUs.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems paper** whose core idea is that unbiased gradient estimation for 4-bit training can be achieved with substantially lower variance than prior work by replacing per-element stochastic rounding with a quantization scheme that applies stochasticity only to the per-group scale factors, leveraging the higher precision (8-bit) of those scales and the information-spreading properties of randomized Hadamard transforms to make the resulting estimator both unbiased and low-error.

#### The EDEN Baseline: How Deterministic Quantization Can Be Made (Nearly) Unbiased

Before explaining MS-EDEN, we must understand **EDEN** (Vargaftik et al., 2022), the technique from distributed optimization that MS-EDEN adapts. EDEN is not the paper's contribution, but it is the intellectual foundation, and the paper assumes familiarity with it.

The standard approach to unbiased quantization at very low bitwidths — say, 4 bits — is **stochastic rounding (SR)**. For each scalar value `$x_i$`, SR randomly rounds up or down to the nearest two representable values with probability proportional to `$x_i$`'s distance from each. Formally, if `$x_i$` lies between representable values `$a$` and `$b$` (`$a \leq x_i \leq b$`), SR outputs `$b$` with probability `$(x_i - a)/(b-a)$` and `$a$` otherwise. This guarantees `$\mathbb{E}[\text{SR}(x_i)] = x_i$` — unbiasedness per element. But the variance is high: each element independently flips a coin, and the quantization error `$x_i - \text{SR}(x_i)$` can be as large as half the spacing between representable values.

EDEN takes a fundamentally different approach. Instead of randomizing each element's rounding decision, EDEN:

1. **Applies a randomized rotation** — specifically, a Randomized Hadamard Transform (RHT) seeded by randomness `$\omega$` — to the input vector `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$`. The RHT spreads the information in each coordinate uniformly across all `$d$` dimensions, making the rotated vector's entries look approximately Gaussian regardless of the original distribution.

2. **Quantizes the rotated vector deterministically** using round-to-nearest (RTN), which has lower MSE than SR because it always picks the closest representable value with no randomization — but is **biased** when applied directly, because the errors don't average to zero in expectation.

3. **Applies a global rescaling factor `$S$`** to correct for the bias introduced by RTN quantization. The factor is:

$$S = \frac{\langle x, x \rangle}{\langle \text{RHT}(x, \omega), Q(\text{RHT}(x, \omega)) \rangle}$$

where `$Q$` is the deterministic RTN quantizer, `$\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$` denotes the dot product, and `$\text{RHT}(x, \omega)$` is the rotated vector.

**What this computes:** the ratio of the squared norm of the original vector `$x$` to the dot product between the rotated vector and its quantized version. The numerator `$\langle x, x \rangle = \|x\|^2$` is the total energy of the input. The denominator `$\langle \text{RHT}(x, \omega), Q(\text{RHT}(x, \omega)) \rangle$` measures how much of that energy is preserved after rotation and quantization — if RTN quantization systematically shrinks the vector (as it often does when values are clipped), this dot product will be less than `$\|x\|^2$`, so `$S > 1$`, scaling the quantized result back up.

**Why this form:** The dot-product ratio is a scalar that captures the *average* bias of the quantizer across all dimensions. By multiplying the quantized vector by `$S$`, EDEN ensures that the *inner product* between the original vector `$x$` and any vector `$y$` is preserved in expectation — which, by choosing `$y$` to be each standard basis vector, implies per-coordinate unbiasedness as the dimension `$d$` grows. The RHT is critical here: it makes the quantization errors approximately isotropic (equally distributed across dimensions), so a single scalar correction `$S$` can compensate for the systematic bias in all dimensions simultaneously. Vargaftik et al. (2022) prove that as `$d \to \infty$`, the inverse-rotated estimator converges to unbiased in expectation:

$$\lim_{d \to \infty} \mathbb{E}_\omega \left[ \text{RHT}^{-1}(S \cdot Q(\text{RHT}(x, \omega)), \omega) \right] = x$$

In practice, the paper notes (Section 3.2) that this convergence is fast enough to be effectively unbiased with rotation groups as small as `$d = 64$`.

**The representation problem.** EDEN's correction factor `$S$` is not an integer. It's a real number, and empirically it falls in the range `$[0.94, 1.06]$`. In distributed optimization, where the quantized vector is transmitted over a network, this single scalar can be sent at high precision (e.g., FP32) with negligible overhead. But NVFP4's storage format cannot accommodate it directly — the per-group scales are in FP8 (E4M3 format), which has a relative step size of `$1/16 = 0.0625$`. This means that the smallest non-unit multiplicative adjustment you can make to an FP8 scale is `$\times 1.0625$` or `$\times 0.9375$`. If EDEN's correction factor is, say, `$1.03$`, the nearest FP8-representable corrections are `$1.0$` (no correction) or `$1.0625$` — both off by enough to destroy the precise bias cancellation that makes EDEN work.

This is the bottleneck: **EDEN's correction requires more precision than NVFP4's scale storage provides, but that precision is only needed for a handful of scalar corrections per rotation group, not for the millions of FP4 elements.**

#### MS-EDEN: Moving Stochasticity to the Scales

MS-EDEN (MicroScaling EDEN) solves the representation bottleneck by **applying the EDEN correction factors to the FP8 group scales via stochastic rounding**, rather than trying to store them exactly. The full procedure is given in Algorithm 1 of the paper. Here is the detailed breakdown.

**Inputs to MS-EDEN (Algorithm 1):** a vector `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$`, a rotation seed `$\omega_{\text{RHT}}$` (determines which Hadamard matrix to use), a rounding seed `$\omega_{\text{SR}}$` (determines stochastic rounding outcomes for the scales), and a grid maximum parameter `$s$` that controls the clipping threshold for the RTN quantizer.

**Step 1: Rotate in groups of 128.** The input vector `$x$` is split into contiguous chunks of 128 elements. Each chunk is multiplied by a randomized Hadamard matrix `$H(\omega_{\text{RHT}})$` of size `$128 \times 128$`, where the randomness `$\omega_{\text{RHT}}$` selects a random sign pattern for the Hadamard columns (this is the standard RHT construction). The paper uses groups of 128 because this maps cleanly to the `mma.m16n8k16` tensor core instruction on Blackwell GPUs — two such instructions process exactly 128 elements along the inner dimension (Appendix A). Formally, for each chunk starting at index `$h$`:

$$\text{xRHT}_{[h:h+128]} = \text{RHT}(x_{[h:h+128]}, \omega_{\text{RHT}})$$

where `$\text{xRHT}$` denotes the rotated vector.

**Step 2: Quantize the rotated vector to NVFP4 using deterministic RTN.** The rotated chunk is quantized using a modified RTN quantization procedure `$Q_{\text{RTN}}$` that **may clip values** (unlike the standard SR-based NVFP4 quantization which uses a non-clipping range). This is a deliberate choice: since MS-EDEN handles unbiasedness through the scale correction (not through per-element SR), clipping is acceptable — any systematic bias from clipping will be compensated by the EDEN correction factor `$S$`. The RTN quantization produces the standard NVFP4 representation:

$$\{x^{\text{FP4}}, x^{\text{FP8}}, x^{\text{FP32}}\} = Q_{\text{RTN}}(x^{\text{RHT}}, s)$$

where `$x^{\text{FP4}} \in \mathbb{R}^d$` contains the 4-bit elements, `$x^{\text{FP8}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d/16}$` contains one FP8 (E4M3) scale per group of 16 elements, and `$x^{\text{FP32}} \in \mathbb{R}$` is the single per-tensor global scale. The dequantized value at position `$i$` is `$x_i^{\text{RTN}} = x_i^{\text{FP4}} \times x^{\text{FP8}}_{i/16} \times x^{\text{FP32}}$`.

The clipping RTN quantizer `$Q_{\text{RTN}}$` is defined with a parameter `$s$` as:

$$x^{\text{FP32}} = \max_{i=1...d} |x_i| / (s \times 256.0)$$

$$x^{\text{FP8}}_g = \text{RTN}_{\text{FP8}}\left( \max_{i=16g...16g+15} \frac{|x_i|}{x^{\text{FP32}} \times s} \right)$$

$$x^{\text{FP4}}_i = \text{RTN}_{\text{FP4}}\left( \frac{x_i}{x^{\text{FP8}}_{i/16} \times x^{\text{FP32}}} \right)$$

where `$\text{RTN}_{\text{FP8}}$` rounds to the nearest representable FP8 value and `$\text{RTN}_{\text{FP4}}$` rounds to the nearest representable FP4 value. The factor `$256.0$` is the absolute maximum representable by FP8 (chosen instead of the 448.0 used in the SR-based quantizer, because EDEN correction may scale scales upward and we need headroom; see Section 3.3). Setting `$s = 6 \times 16/17$` (approximately 5.65) makes the scheme non-clipping, matching the standard NVFP4 scaling; smaller `$s$` clips more aggressively.

**Step 3: Compute the EDEN correction factor per rotation group.** For each chunk of 128 elements, compute:

$$S_{h/128} = \frac{\langle x^{\text{RHT}}_{[h:h+128]}, x^{\text{RHT}}_{[h:h+128]} \rangle}{\langle x^{\text{RHT}}_{[h:h+128]}, x^{\text{RTN}}_{[h:h+128]} \rangle}$$

where `$x^{\text{RTN}}$` is the dequantized version of the RTN-quantized chunk: `$x^{\text{RTN}}_i = x^{\text{FP4}}_i \times x^{\text{FP8}}_{i/16} \times x^{\text{FP32}}$`. This is exactly the EDEN correction factor from Equation 1, computed per rotation group of 128 elements rather than per tensor. The numerator is the squared norm of the rotated chunk; the denominator is the dot product between the rotated chunk and its quantized-then-dequantized version — measuring how much energy RTN quantization preserved.

**Step 4: Apply the correction factor to the FP8 group scales via stochastic rounding.** This is the critical innovation. Instead of storing `$S_{h/128}$` as a separate high-precision value (which NVFP4 cannot accommodate), MS-EDEN **multiplies each FP8 group scale by the correction factor and stochastically rounds the result to FP8**:

$$x^{\text{FP8}}_g \leftarrow \text{SR}_{\text{FP8}}\left( S_{16g/128} \cdot x^{\text{FP8}}_g, \omega_{\text{SR}} \right)$$

for each group `$g$` of 16 elements. The rotation group is 128 elements, and each rotation group contains `$128/16 = 8$` scale groups of 16 elements, so the same correction factor `$S_{h/128}$` is applied to all 8 scale groups within rotation group `$h$`.

**What this operation does:** For each FP8 scale `$x^{\text{FP8}}_g$`, multiply by the correction factor `$S$` (which is a real number like 1.03), then randomly round the product up or down to the nearest representable FP8 value with probability proportional to the fractional distance. Because FP8 has a relative precision of 6.25%, a correction factor of 1.03 corresponds to approximately 0.5 FP8 steps — the stochastic rounding will round up to the next representable scale roughly half the time and down the other half, making the **expected value** of the scale exactly `$S \cdot x^{\text{FP8}}_g$`.

**Why this works:** The stochastic rounding on the FP8 scales introduces some variance, but that variance is negligible relative to the quantization error of the FP4 elements themselves. An FP4 value can take only 16 possible levels, so the quantization error per element is on the order of `$1/16 = 0.0625$` of the scale range. In contrast, FP8 stochastic rounding introduces error on the order of `$1/256 = 0.004$` of the scale range — **roughly 16× smaller**. Plus, the scale error affects all 16 elements in a scale group identically (it's a multiplicative bias on the whole group), whereas per-element SR introduces independent noise on every element. The concentration of scale-level noise across groups of 16 means the effective gradient variance is much lower.

**Output.** MS-EDEN returns the standard NVFP4 representation `$\{x^{\text{FP4}}, x^{\text{FP8}}, x^{\text{FP32}}\}$`, where the FP4 elements are from deterministic RTN quantization (step 2), but the FP8 scales have been stochastically corrected to incorporate the EDEN rescaling (step 4). The global FP32 scale `$x^{\text{FP32}}$` is unchanged.

**Formal unbiasedness guarantee (Corollary 3.1).** For all `$x \in \mathbb{R}^d$` and scale `$s \neq 0$`, let `$\hat{x} = Q_{\text{MS-EDEN}}(x, \omega_{\text{RHT}}, \omega_{\text{SR}}, s)$`. Then:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\omega_{\text{RHT}}, \omega_{\text{SR}}} \left[ \text{RHT}^{-1}(\hat{x}, \omega_{\text{RHT}}) \right] = x$$

**Restating in operational terms:** If you take the vector `$x$`, apply MS-EDEN (which rotates, RTN-quantizes, computes EDEN corrections, and stochastically rounds the scales), then inverse-rotate the result, the expected value over all randomness (both the choice of Hadamard matrix and the stochastic rounding outcomes) equals the original vector `$x$`. The inverse rotation `$\text{RHT}^{-1}$` does not need to be performed explicitly in training because it cancels out when MS-EDEN is applied to both operands of a matrix multiplication along their shared inner dimension — the two rotations are identical (same seed), and `$\text{RHT}^{-1}(\text{RHT}(x)) = x$`.

**Why this property matters:** It means the gradient estimates produced by GEMMs with MS-EDEN-quantized operands are unbiased in expectation — the same property that stochastic rounding guarantees, but achieved with deterministic (low-error) quantization of the individual elements. The stochasticity is concentrated in the scales, where its variance impact is minimal.

**Why the 128-element rotation group size.** The paper uses groups of 128 specifically because the `mma.m16n8k16` tensor core instruction on Blackwell GPUs processes 16 rows × 8 columns × 16 inner dimensions, and two such instructions along the inner dimension accumulate exactly 128 products. By using 128-element rotation groups, the RHT can be implemented as a standard GEMM using these tensor core instructions, making it efficient. The paper notes in Appendix A that this departs from EDEN's theoretical assumptions (which require independent rotations per group), but empirically validates that unbiasedness is preserved (Figure 9).

**The clipping parameter `$s$`.** In the standard NVFP4 quantization (`$Q_{\text{SR}}$`), the global scale `$x^{\text{FP32}}$` is computed with a factor `$6.0 \times 16/17 \times 448.0$` that guarantees no value is clipped — the maximum representable value after scaling is `$6.0 \times 16/17$`, which is slightly less than 6.0 (the FP4 maximum) to leave room for RTN rounding upward. In `$Q_{\text{RTN}}$`, the denominator uses `$s \times 256.0$`, where 256.0 is the FP8 maximum (instead of 448.0) to leave headroom for the EDEN correction factor to scale values up. Setting `$s = 6 \times 16/17$` makes it non-clipping; smaller `$s$` clips more, trading bias for lower variance. The paper does not specify the exact `$s$` value used in experiments, but the construction ensures that the EDEN correction can push scales upward without exceeding FP8 range.

**Practical performance (Table 1).** On normally distributed data with native 1×16 group scales, MS-EDEN achieves an MSE of `$9.8 \times 10^{-3}$`, compared to `$23.5 \times 10^{-3}$` for SR (both without 4/6). This is approximately **2.4× lower error**. When 4/6 is added, MS-EDEN is not directly applicable (since 4/6 on the backward pass would introduce bias), but SR+4/6 achieves `$17.5 \times 10^{-3}$`, which is still roughly **1.8× higher** than MS-EDEN without 4/6. The paper attributes MS-EDEN's advantage to three factors (Section 3.3): (a) per-element stochastic rounding injects independent noise on every element, which MS-EDEN eliminates entirely; (b) the EDEN rescaling factor `$S$` is small for NVFP4 (close to 1.0), so the correction is gentle; (c) stochastic rounding on 8-bit scales introduces variance "an order of magnitude smaller than 4-bit quantization itself."

**Hardware compatibility constraints.** MS-EDEN requires that the randomized rotations and scale corrections be applied along the **inner dimension** of the matrix multiplication (the dimension that is contracted). This is because the RHT must be applied identically to both operands for the rotations to cancel. In practice, this means:
- For the weight-gradient GEMM (`$E^T X$`), MS-EDEN is applied to both `$E^T$` and `$X$` along the dimension corresponding to the batch/sequence axis (the dimension that `$E$` and `$X$` share).
- For the input-gradient GEMM (`$E W$`), MS-EDEN is applied to both `$E$` and `$W$` along the hidden dimension.

This constraint also means that the micro-scaling groups (groups of 16 elements sharing one FP8 scale) must be subdivisions of the rotation groups (128 elements). Since 128 is a multiple of 16, this is satisfied — each rotation group contains exactly 8 scale groups.

#### Forward Pass Quantization and the Representation-Requantization Tradeoff

The paper deliberately chooses **not** to reuse the forward-pass quantized weights in the backward pass without re-quantization. This is a departure from NVIDIA et al. (2025), which uses square-block quantization (16×16 group scales) on the forward pass specifically so that the same quantized weight tensor can be reused in the backward pass for computing `$\partial L / \partial X = E W$`. To understand why Quartet II makes a different choice, we need to understand the tradeoff.

**The tradeoff (Section 4.1).** In standard NVFP4 quantization with native 1×16 group scales, each group of 16 contiguous elements in a tensor shares one FP8 scale. This means a weight matrix of size `$[d_{\text{out}}, d_{\text{in}}]$` has one scale per 16 elements **along the inner dimension** `$d_{\text{in}}$`. On the backward pass, the weight-gradient GEMM multiplies `$E^T$` (dimensions `$[d_{\text{in}}, B]$`, approximately) with `$X$` (dimensions `$[d_{\text{out}}, B]$`), where the inner dimension is `$B$` (batch × sequence). The scales for `$X$` are organized along the inner dimension of this GEMM, which is fine. But the input-gradient GEMM multiplies `$E$` with `$W$`, where the inner dimension is `$d_{\text{out}}$` (the output dimension of the weight matrix). If `$W$` was quantized with scales along `$d_{\text{in}}$` (the standard orientation), the scales for `$W^T$` would be along `$d_{\text{out}}$` — which is the inner dimension of the input-gradient GEMM, so the scales align correctly. **However**, NVIDIA et al. (2025) use square-block quantization, where each scale covers a 16×16 block of the weight matrix, producing one scale per 256 elements. This means the scales on the forward pass are aligned with **both** the row and column dimensions, making the same quantized tensor directly usable in both backward GEMMs without re-quantization — because whichever dimension becomes the inner dimension, the scales (which cover 16 elements in each dimension) are correctly positioned.

But square-block quantization comes at a cost: **reduced forward-pass representation capacity.** With one scale per 256 elements instead of per 16 elements, the quantizer has less flexibility to adapt to local variations in the weight matrix. Outliers in a single element can force the scale for an entire 16×16 block to be larger than necessary, reducing the effective precision for all 255 other elements in that block. Figure 2 confirms this: across all model sizes and token budgets, native 1×16 scales consistently produce lower validation loss increases than 16×16 square-block scales when only the forward pass is quantized.

**Quartet II's resolution: accept weight re-quantization, gain forward-pass quality.** Because MS-EDEN requires applying RHT along the inner dimension of each backward GEMM, **both operands must be re-quantized regardless** — the RHT changes the values, so the forward-pass quantized version cannot be reused directly. This eliminates the main motivation for square-block quantization (avoiding weight re-quantization on the backward pass), freeing Quartet II to use native 1×16 scales on the forward pass for maximum representation capacity. The paper argues that MS-EDEN's lower quantization error (more than 2× better than SR) more than compensates for the additional error introduced by re-quantizing the weights on the backward pass. This is validated empirically in Figure 1: compare panel (d) — SR without weight re-quantization — with panel (e) — MS-EDEN with weight re-quantization — and MS-EDEN with re-quantization consistently produces lower validation loss.

**The Four-over-Six (4/6) heuristic on the forward pass.** Cook et al. (2025) observed that the NVFP4 quantization grid (which maps values to one of 16 representable numbers: `$\{0, \pm 0.5, \pm 1.0, \pm 1.5, \pm 2.0, \pm 3.0, \pm 4.0, \pm 6.0\}$`) has an asymmetry: the step sizes between representable values are not uniform. The absolute maximum representable value is 6.0, but the next representable value down is 4.0 — a gap of 2.0. If a block of values has a maximum absolute value slightly above 4.0, using a scale factor that maps 4.0 to the maximum absolute value will quantize values near the maximum to 4.0, leaving the 6.0 level unused — effectively wasting one bit of precision. Conversely, using a scale factor that maps 6.0 to the maximum will spread values across the full range but with larger quantization errors for values near zero.

The 4/6 heuristic tries both scale factors: one that maps the block's maximum absolute value to 4.0 (on the representable grid), and one that maps it to 6.0. For each candidate, it computes the MSE of the resulting quantization, and picks the scale with lower MSE. This is a purely local, per-block optimization that requires no additional storage — the choice is implicit in the quantized values and the scale itself. Table 1 shows that 4/6 reduces RTN's MSE from `$9.0 \times 10^{-3}$` to `$7.6 \times 10^{-3}$` on N(0,1) data (a 1.16× improvement), and Figure 2 shows that this translates to visibly lower validation loss gaps when applied only on the forward pass.

**Why 4/6 cannot be used on the backward pass.** The paper explains (Section 4.2) that 4/6 introduces bias into gradient estimates. The reason: the choice between the "4.0" scale and the "6.0" scale is deterministic and data-dependent — it's based on which produces lower MSE. Even if each scale branch individually produces an unbiased estimate (via SR), the act of **selecting** the branch that minimizes MSE introduces a systematic preference. For instance, if values in a block are distributed such that the "4.0" scale tends to round values slightly upward while the "6.0" scale rounds them slightly downward, the MSE-minimizing choice will systematically select one direction over the other, creating a net bias. Appendix A empirically confirms this: when 4/6 is added to the NVIDIA backward pass, the relative error of the average quantized gradient does not decrease as `$1/B$` (where `$B$` is the number of accumulation steps); instead, it plateaus at a non-zero level, indicating a persistent bias (Figure 9, "NVIDIA+4/6" line).

**Bottom line on forward pass design.** Quartet II uses native 1×16 group scales with 4/6 scale selection for both weights and activations on the forward pass. This maximizes representation capacity (native scales beat square-block in Figure 2) with a small additional improvement from 4/6. The forward pass is entirely deterministic — no stochastic rounding. The quantized tensors are saved, but they will be dequantized and re-quantized with MS-EDEN on the backward pass.

#### The Full Quartet II Computation Graph

We now assemble the forward pass and both backward GEMMs into the complete training recipe for a single linear layer `$Y = X W^T$`, illustrated in Figure 3. The description follows the flow of computation as it would occur during a training step.

**Forward Pass (top row of Figure 3).**

1. **Input activations `$X$` (BF16) arrive** from the previous layer or from the embedding lookup. The weight matrix `$W$` (FP32 master copy, maintained by the optimizer) is available.

2. **Quantize `$X$` to NVFP4** using 4/6 RTN quantization with native 1×16 group scales. Specifically: for each contiguous group of 16 elements in `$X$`, compute the maximum absolute value, evaluate both scale candidates (mapping the max to 4.0 or 6.0 on the FP4 grid), pick the one with lower MSE, compute the FP8 group scale and FP4 elements via RTN, and store the NVFP4 representation `$\{X^{\text{FP4}}, X^{\text{FP8}}, X^{\text{FP32}}\}$`.

3. **Quantize `$W$` to NVFP4** using the same 4/6 RTN procedure, but applied along the inner dimension of the forward GEMM. For a weight matrix of shape `$[d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}}]$` (PyTorch convention: input features × output features), the scales are computed along `$d_{\text{in}}$` in groups of 16. Store `$\{W^{\text{FP4}}, W^{\text{FP8}}, W^{\text{FP32}}\}$`.

4. **Execute the NVFP4 GEMM:** `$Y^{\text{FP32}} = X^{\text{FP4}} \times (W^{\text{FP4}})^T$`, with the scales applied as part of the tensor core operation. The output `$Y$` is in FP32 (or BF16, depending on the accumulation precision).

5. **Save the quantized representations** `$\{X^{\text{FP4}}, X^{\text{FP8}}, X^{\text{FP32}}\}$` and `$\{W^{\text{FP4}}, W^{\text{FP8}}, W^{\text{FP32}}\}$` for the backward pass. These are the only tensors saved — the full-precision `$X$` and `$W$` can be discarded from the forward pass memory.

**Backward Pass (bottom and middle rows of Figure 3).**

The backward pass computes two gradients: `$\partial L / \partial W$` (weight gradient) and `$\partial L / \partial X$` (input gradient, to propagate to the previous layer). The output gradient `$E = \partial L / \partial Y$` arrives from the subsequent layer in BF16.

**Weight-Gradient GEMM:** `$\partial L / \partial W = E^T X$`.

1. **Dequantize the saved activations:** reconstruct `$X$` (approximately) from `$\{X^{\text{FP4}}, X^{\text{FP8}}, X^{\text{FP32}}\}$` as `$X_{ij} \approx X^{\text{FP4}}_{ij} \times X^{\text{FP8}}_{i/16(j)} \times X^{\text{FP32}}$`. This is an approximation because the FP4 elements are quantized, but it recovers the best available representation of what the forward pass actually computed.

2. **Transpose `$E$`** to get `$E^T$` (dimensions `$[B, d_{\text{out}}]$`, where `$B$` is batch × sequence). This makes the inner dimension of the GEMM `$d_{\text{out}}$`, which is shared with `$X$` when `$X$` is transposed appropriately.

3. **Apply RHT along the inner dimension** of both `$E^T$` and `$X$`. The inner dimension is split into groups of 128, and each group is multiplied by the same randomized Hadamard matrix (seeded by `$\omega_{\text{RHT}}$`). Crucially, **the same rotation seed is used for both tensors**, so the rotation matrices applied to corresponding groups are identical.

4. **Quantize both rotated tensors to NVFP4 using MS-EDEN.** For each rotated chunk of 128 elements:
   - Quantize deterministically with RTN (allowing clipping) to get initial FP4 values and FP8 scales.
   - Compute the EDEN correction factor `$S$` as the dot-product ratio.
   - Stochastically round the product `$S \cdot x^{\text{FP8}}_g$` to FP8 for each of the 8 scale groups within the rotation group.
   - The result is an NVFP4 representation where the FP4 elements are deterministically quantized but the FP8 scales have been stochastically corrected.

5. **Execute the NVFP4 GEMM:** multiply the two MS-EDEN-quantized tensors. Because both were rotated with the same Hadamard matrix along their shared inner dimension, the rotations cancel: `$\text{RHT}^{-1}(\text{RHT}(E^T)) \times \text{RHT}^{-1}(\text{RHT}(X))^T = E^T X$`. The NVFP4 tensor core produces the product in FP32. This is the weight gradient `$\partial L / \partial W$`.

6. **Feed the weight gradient to the optimizer.** The optimizer step is performed in FP32 (standard practice), so the quantized gradient is dequantized to FP32 and accumulated.

**Input-Gradient GEMM:** `$\partial L / \partial X = E W$`.

1. **Dequantize the saved weight matrix:** reconstruct `$W$` (approximately) from `$\{W^{\text{FP4}}, W^{\text{FP8}}, W^{\text{FP32}}\}$`.

2. **Apply RHT along the inner dimension** of both `$E$` and `$W$` (where the inner dimension is the hidden dimension `$d_{\text{out}}$` for `$E$` and `$d_{\text{in}}$` for `$W$`, but these are the same after appropriate transposes). Again, identical rotation seeds for corresponding groups.

3. **Quantize both rotated tensors with MS-EDEN** — same procedure as for the weight-gradient GEMM.

4. **Execute the NVFP4 GEMM** to produce `$\partial L / \partial X$` in FP32. Rotations cancel along the inner dimension.

5. **Propagate `$\partial L / \partial X$` to the previous layer** in BF16 (or the format expected by the preceding layer).

**Key design points in the computation graph.**

- **The same rotation seed `$\omega_{\text{RHT}}$` is shared** across both backward GEMMs for the same layer, and likely across layers as well (the paper does not specify whether seeds differ per layer, but the unbiasedness guarantee only requires consistency within each GEMM pair).

- **The forward-pass quantized weights are dequantized and re-quantized** on the backward pass. This adds computational cost (the dequantization and re-quantization operations), but the paper's kernel design in Section 7 fuses these operations to minimize overhead.

- **The inverses of the RHT are never explicitly computed.** Because both operands of each GEMM are rotated with the same matrix, the rotation and its inverse "cancel" in the matrix product: `$\langle \text{RHT}(a), \text{RHT}(b) \rangle = \langle a, b \rangle$` for orthogonal transforms. This is a standard property of the Hadamard transform and is the reason RHT-based quantization can be used in GEMMs without additional post-processing.

- **The EDEN correction factors `$S$` are computed and applied independently for each rotation group of 128 elements.** This means different parts of the tensor can receive different bias corrections, adapting to local variations in how the RTN quantizer clips values. This is more fine-grained than the original EDEN, which applies a single correction per tensor, but the per-group correction is possible because each group has its own set of FP8 scales.

#### Comparison with Prior Quantization Schemes

The paper positions Quartet II relative to three specific baselines, and it is useful to understand exactly what each baseline does to appreciate where Quartet II differs.

**NVIDIA et al. (2025):** The NVIDIA recipe uses:
- **Forward pass:** Square-block quantization (16×16 groups) with RTN. Weights and activations are quantized and multiplied in NVFP4.
- **Backward pass:** The saved quantized weight tensor (square-block) is reused directly in the input-gradient GEMM (no weight re-quantization). The activations are re-quantized for the weight-gradient GEMM using SR (element-wise stochastic rounding). Hadamard rotations (RHT) are applied along the inner dimension of both backward GEMMs for outlier smoothing. Some layers are kept in higher precision.
- **Key difference from Quartet II:** Uses SR for unbiasedness and square-block quantization on the forward pass to avoid weight re-quantization.

**TetraJet-v2 (Chen et al., 2025b):** The authors describe the "GPU-feasible scheme" of TetraJet-v2 (distinct from the full paper proposal which includes impractical kernel changes) as:
- **Forward pass:** RTN quantization with native 1×16 group scales (no square blocks).
- **Backward pass:** Corrected activation re-quantization to better align with the chain rule, plus weight re-quantization similar to Castro et al. (2025). Uses SR for unbiasedness with RHT on the inner dimension for both GEMMs. The paper explicitly states it does **not** re-implement TetraJet-v2's intermediate FP32 scales or outlier channels due to impracticality.
- **Key difference from Quartet II:** Uses SR (not MS-EDEN) for unbiasedness on the backward pass.

**Four-over-Six (Cook et al., 2025):** The paper's treatment of 4/6 is nuanced:
- **Forward pass:** 4/6 scale selection with whatever group size is used (native or square-block). This is valid and beneficial — Quartet II adopts it.
- **Backward pass:** The original 4/6 proposal applies the same scale selection heuristic to the backward pass, combined with SR. The paper identifies this as **biased** (validated in Appendix A) and therefore does not use 4/6 on the backward pass.
- **Key difference from Quartet II:** Quartet II uses 4/6 only on the forward pass, where bias doesn't matter (it's just a representation, not a gradient estimate).

**Quartet II's unified advantage (Figure 4).** Across all model sizes (30M–200M parameters) and token budgets (25–800 tokens per parameter), Quartet II consistently achieves the lowest validation loss increase relative to BF16 training. The gap reduction is at least 20% relative to the next-best prior scheme — meaning if TetraJet-v2 produces a validation loss 0.10 higher than BF16, Quartet II produces a loss roughly 0.08 higher. The improvement is most pronounced at smaller model sizes and lower token budgets, where gradient noise has a larger relative impact.

#### GPU Kernel Design and Post-Hoc Range Alignment (Section 7)

The algorithmic description of Quartet II involves several operations that, if implemented naively, would require **loading each tensor from GPU global memory twice**: once to compute the global absolute maximum (needed for the NVFP4 global scale `$x^{\text{FP32}}$`) and apply the RHT, and again to perform the actual quantization using that pre-computed scale. This is because the global maximum is a reduction over the entire tensor — it's a global barrier that prevents fusing the reduction with the subsequent per-element quantization in a single kernel pass.

**The naive kernel (Figure 7).** For a backward-pass re-quantization operation (e.g., quantizing `$E$` with MS-EDEN before the input-gradient GEMM):

- **Kernel 1:** Load the tensor from global memory (GMEM), apply RHT, compute the global absolute maximum, compute the EDEN correction factors `$S$` per group of 128. Store the rotated tensor (needed for the next kernel) and the global max and correction factors.
- **Kernel 2:** Reload the rotated tensor from GMEM, divide by the pre-computed global scale and group scales to get normalized values, round to FP4, multiply scales by EDEN correction factors, stochastically round scales to FP8. Store the final NVFP4 representation.

This doubles both the memory bandwidth (each element is read from and written to GMEM twice) and the matrix multiplication cost (the RHT is performed in both kernels, since the rotated tensor is needed in each).

**Post-hoc range alignment (Figure 8).** The paper proposes a format-specific optimization that avoids the double load:

- **Kernel 1:** Load the tensor from GMEM, apply RHT, **skip the global maximum alignment**. Instead of computing the global max and normalizing with respect to it, this kernel quantizes directly using an **extended-range NVFP4 (ER-NVFP4)** format: round the per-group scales to E8M3 (an extended-range proxy for FP8, stored in BF16), divide tensor values by these scales, and round to FP4. The E8M3 scales can represent a wider range than FP8, so no global alignment is needed — the scales themselves absorb the local dynamic range. Simultaneously, compute the global absolute maximum (a single scalar reduction) and the EDEN correction factors `$S$` per group. Store the ER-NVFP4 representation (FP4 elements + BF16 E8M3 pseudo-scales) and the global max.

- **Kernel 2:** Load **only the E8M3 pseudo-scales** (not the FP4 elements) from GMEM, along with the global max. For each group, shift the pseudo-scale into the FP8-representable region using the global max, multiply by the EDEN correction factor `$S$`, and stochastically round to FP8. The FP4 elements (which are already correctly quantized relative to these scales) do not need to be reloaded — only the scales are updated. Store the final NVFP4 representation with corrected FP8 scales.

**Why this works.** The key insight is that the FP4 element values depend only on the ratio `$x_i / \text{scale}_g$`, not on the absolute magnitude of the scale. In the naive approach, the scale is computed as `$\text{scale}_g = \text{global\_scale} \times \text{local\_scale}_g$`, where the global scale ensures that the FP8 local scales do not overflow. In the post-hoc approach, the E8M3 pseudo-scale directly captures the necessary local scaling, and the global alignment (shifting into FP8 range) can be applied afterward by adjusting only the scales — the FP4 elements remain unchanged because the ratio `$x_i / \text{scale}_g$` stays the same when both numerator and denominator are multiplied by a constant.

**Measured efficiency (Table 2).** The post-hoc kernel reduces GMEM bandwidth:
- Naive kernel: Load 4.5 bits per element for the tensor data (FP4 elements + FP8 scales + FP32 global scale) in the first kernel, load another 4.5 bits per element in the second kernel; write 0 bits after kernel 1 (intermediate data for kernel 2), write 4.5 bits per element after kernel 2 (final NVFP4). Total: 4.5 + 4.5 = 9.0 bits read per element; 0 + 4.5 = 4.5 bits written per element.
- Post-hoc kernel: Load 4.5 bits per element in kernel 1 (tensor data), load 1 bit per element in kernel 2 (only the scales, which are 1/16 of the data since there is one scale per 16 elements); write 5 bits per element after kernel 1 (ER-NVFP4: FP4 elements + E8M3 pseudo-scales in BF16, which are 16 bits per scale ÷ 16 elements = 1 extra bit, total 4+1=5), write 0.5 bits per element after kernel 2 (updated FP8 scales only, 8 bits per scale ÷ 16 elements = 0.5). Total: 4.5 + 1 = 5.5 bits read; 5 + 0.5 = 5.5 bits written. However, the NAIVE approach requires 2 GEMM calls per NVFP4 group (one for the RHT, one for the quantization), while the post-hoc approach requires only 1 (the RHT is done in kernel 1, and kernel 2 only adjusts scales, which are a tiny fraction of the data volume). The paper reports that "the practical latency of the second kernel being more than 10× less than the first one."

**End-to-end speedups (Section 7.2, Figure 6).** On an NVIDIA RTX 5090 (Blackwell generation), Quartet II achieves:
- **4.1× speedup over BF16** for linear layer operations on an 800M-parameter model configuration.
- **~70% improvement over Quartet** (Castro et al., 2025), an MXFP4 training scheme.
- For a 1.1B-parameter model training at 4096 tokens per pass, Quartet II achieves **2.45× end-to-end training throughput** over BF16 (51 vs. 21 ktok/s), enabled by the reduced memory footprint of storing 4-bit activations for the backward pass, which allows a 4× larger micro-batch size (4 vs. 1) on the 32GB GPU.

The paper notes that at this model size, about 60% of training time is spent on operations untouched by FP4 (attention, loss computation, RMSNorm, etc.), so the linear-layer speedup does not translate 1:1 to end-to-end speedup — but as model size grows, the linear layers dominate an increasingly larger fraction of total compute, approaching the 4× theoretical maximum.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **MS-EDEN over SR for backward pass unbiasedness:** MS-EDEN achieves the same unbiasedness guarantee as per-element stochastic rounding but with less than half the quantization error, because it avoids injecting independent noise on every element and instead concentrates randomness in the FP8 scales where precision is 16× higher.
- **Native 1×16 scales over square-block 16×16 scales on the forward pass:** Better representation capacity (more scales per tensor means better adaptation to local value ranges), and the forward-pass quality gain is "free" because MS-EDEN requires weight re-quantization on the backward pass anyway — square-block's main advantage (avoiding re-quantization) is irrelevant.
- **4/6 scale selection on forward pass only:** Provides a modest but consistent MSE reduction for forward-pass quantization. Not used on the backward pass because the deterministic scale choice introduces bias in gradient estimates (validated in Appendix A).
- **128-element rotation groups:** Matches the tensor core instruction granularity (`mma.m16n8k16`), enabling efficient hardware implementation. Although EDEN theory assumes independent rotations per group, empirical validation confirms that reusing the same rotation across groups preserves unbiasedness in practice (Figure 9, Appendix A).
- **Clipping allowed in MS-EDEN's RTN quantizer:** Unlike SR-based NVFP4 quantization, MS-EDEN can clip values because the EDEN correction factor `$S$` compensates for any systematic bias from clipping — the stochastic rounding on the scales handles the correction.
- **FP8 scale headroom (256.0 maximum instead of 448.0):** The EDEN correction factor `$S$` may need to scale values upward, so the initial quantization must leave room in the scale range to avoid overflow when the correction is applied. Using 256.0 as the FP8 cap provides this headroom.
- **Post-hoc range alignment for kernel efficiency:** Avoids double-loading and double-rotating tensors during backward-pass re-quantization, saving approximately 20% memory bandwidth and making the second kernel pass over 10× faster than a naive re-quantization.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Diagnosing the Core Bottleneck as the Unbiasedness Mechanism Itself, Not the Quantizer or the Model

The paper's most distinctive conceptual move is **recasting the FP4 training problem from one of adequate representation to one of adequate noise properties in the unbiasedness mechanism**. Prior work treated unbiased gradient estimation as a binary property — you either have it (via stochastic rounding) or you don't — and focused design effort on everything else: forward-pass representation capacity (NVIDIA et al., 2025; Cook et al., 2025), outlier control (TetraJet-v2; Chen et al., 2025b), rotation strategies (Castro et al., 2025), or selective higher-precision layers. The shared, largely unexamined assumption was that **element-wise stochastic rounding is the unavoidable cost of unbiasedness at 4 bits**, and that the variance it introduces is simply the price of admission.

Quartet II challenges this assumption at its root. The paper shows — quantitatively and conceptually — that stochastic rounding is not a neutral carrier of unbiasedness but rather the **primary source of gradient noise** limiting 4-bit training quality. Table 1 makes this diagnosis concrete: SR inflates quantization MSE by roughly 2.5× over deterministic RTN on the same data (23.5 vs. 9.0 × 10⁻³). This is not a subtle effect. It means that for every gradient estimate produced by prior FP4 training schemes, roughly 60% of the quantization-induced error is attributable not to the 4-bit format's limited precision, but to the randomization injected to achieve unbiasedness — randomization that the field had accepted as necessary.

This reframing matters because it redirects research attention. If the forward-pass representation were the bottleneck, the right response would be better scale selection heuristics (more 4/6-like optimizations) or format redesign. If the model architecture were the bottleneck, the response would be architecture-specific quantization strategies. But if the unbiasedness mechanism itself is the bottleneck, the response is fundamentally different: **find a way to achieve unbiasedness without the per-element variance penalty**. This is what MS-EDEN provides, and it is the insight that makes the rest of the paper possible. The contrast with prior work is stark — NVIDIA et al. (2025) and TetraJet-v2 both invest significant complexity in forward-pass design and backward-pass stabilizers while leaving the SR mechanism untouched, as if unbiasedness were a solved subproblem. Quartet II's core argument is that it was the wrong subproblem to consider solved.

The significance of this diagnosis extends beyond the specific technique. It establishes a **new design axis for low-precision training research**: the variance–unbiasedness tradeoff of the quantization routine itself, distinct from the bitwidth, the format, or the scaling strategy. A future 3-bit or 2-bit training scheme might similarly benefit from rethinking how unbiasedness is achieved, rather than accepting per-element SR as the default.

### Innovation 2: Exploiting the Precision Hierarchy in Microscaling Formats to Decompose the Unbiasedness Problem

The second conceptual innovation is recognizing that microscaling formats like NVFP4 contain a **natural precision hierarchy** — 4-bit elements, 8-bit group scales, 32-bit global scale — and that unbiasedness can be achieved by targeting the randomization at the level where precision is highest and variance impact is lowest. This is not obvious. The standard approach treats the 4-bit elements as the thing to randomize (because they're the source of quantization error) and the scales as fixed deterministic values. Quartet II inverts this: **keep the 4-bit elements deterministic (RTN, low error) and make the 8-bit scales stochastic**.

This inversion works because of a structural property of the NVFP4 format that prior work did not exploit for unbiasedness: the FP8 scales have roughly 16× finer relative precision than the FP4 elements (1/256 step size vs. 1/16). When you stochastically round an FP8 scale, the expected noise is an order of magnitude smaller than what per-element FP4 stochastic rounding produces. Moreover, each FP8 scale governs 16 FP4 elements, so the noise is **correlated across groups of 16** rather than being independent per element — which matters for gradient estimation because correlated noise averages out less destructively in matrix products than independent per-element noise.

This is a genuinely new way to think about microscaling formats. The prevailing view — implicit in NVIDIA et al. (2025) and explicit in the format specification — is that the 8-bit scales exist to extend dynamic range, and the 4-bit elements exist to provide precision within that range. Quartet II repurposes the scales as a **control channel for injecting precisely-calibrated stochastic corrections**, effectively treating the format as having two tiers: a high-precision control plane (scales) and a low-precision data plane (elements). The EDEN correction factors, which are small multiplicative adjustments (in [0.94, 1.06]), can be absorbed into the FP8 scales via stochastic rounding because the scales operate at sufficient precision to represent these corrections in expectation — even though they cannot store them exactly.

The practical consequence is that MS-EDEN achieves **unbiasedness without sacrificing the low MSE of deterministic quantization** — the RTN elements deliver accurate values, and the stochastically-rounded scales deliver the expected-value correction that makes the whole estimator unbiased. Prior work had no mechanism to separate these concerns; SR forced a single mechanism (per-element randomization) to simultaneously handle both the quantization approximation and the unbiasedness guarantee, with predictably poor results for the former. This decomposition is a conceptual advance independent of the specific EDEN machinery — it suggests a general design principle for any microscaling or block-floating-point format: **apply randomness at the highest available precision level, not the lowest**.

### Innovation 3: Resolving the Forward-Pass Representation vs. Backward-Pass Re-Quantization Tradeoff by Accepting the "Cost" That Isn't One

The paper identifies a tradeoff that prior work treated as a fundamental constraint — square-block quantization lets you reuse weights on the backward pass without re-quantization, but hurts forward-pass quality — and **dissolves it by showing that the tradeoff was an artifact of using SR on the backward pass**. This is a design insight, not a mathematical one, but it is important for understanding why Quartet II's architecture is coherent.

NVIDIA et al. (2025) chose square-block quantization (16×16 group scales) specifically to enable backward-pass reuse of the forward-pass quantized weights, avoiding what they presumably viewed as an unacceptable cost: dequantizing and re-quantizing the weight matrix on the backward pass. The cost was real — square-block reduces representation capacity (one scale per 256 elements vs. one per 16), and Figure 2 shows this degradation quantitatively. But it was accepted because the alternative (SR with weight re-quantization) would compound the already-high variance of SR. Their implicit reasoning was: *re-quantization adds error, SR already has high error, we cannot afford both.*

Quartet II's insight is that this reasoning collapses when you replace SR with MS-EDEN. MS-EDEN requires both operands of each backward GEMM to be re-quantized anyway — the RHT must be applied along the inner dimension, and the rotated values differ from the forward-pass quantized versions. So the choice is not "re-quantize or don't"; it's "re-quantize with SR (high error) or re-quantize with MS-EDEN (low error)." Once re-quantization is inevitable, the forward-pass quantization can be optimized independently for representation capacity — native 1×16 scales with 4/6 — because there is no backward-pass reuse to preserve.

This is a **Pareto improvement** over the NVIDIA design: forward-pass quality goes up (native scales beat square-block), and backward-pass gradient quality goes up (MS-EDEN beats SR) — simultaneously. The "cost" of weight re-quantization was never actually a cost relative to the available alternatives; it was only a cost relative to an idealized scenario (zero re-quantization error) that was unavailable given the backward-pass quantization requirements. This is the kind of design insight that comes from understanding the full system rather than optimizing components in isolation: **the constraint that made the tradeoff look real (backward-pass SR error) was the very thing being replaced**.

Figure 4 validates this empirically: Quartet II, which accepts weight re-quantization and uses native forward-pass scales, outperforms NVIDIA's scheme (which avoids weight re-quantization via square-block) by a consistent margin across all model sizes and token budgets. The forward-pass gain and the backward-pass gain compound rather than competing.

### Innovation 4: A Practical Correctness Boundary for Forward-Pass Heuristics on the Backward Pass

The paper provides a clear, empirically-validated criterion for when a forward-pass quantization improvement can be safely ported to the backward pass: **deterministic data-dependent choices introduce bias, even if each branch is individually unbiased**. This is not a new theoretical result — it follows from the definition of conditional expectation — but the paper's demonstration of its practical consequences for FP4 training is instructive and has implications beyond 4/6.

The specific case is the Four-over-Six heuristic (Cook et al., 2025). On the forward pass, picking the lower-MSE scale between 4.0 and 6.0 is purely beneficial — it reduces representation error with no downside, since forward-pass quantization is about preserving information, not about expectation properties. On the backward pass, the same heuristic creates a **data-dependent selection between two unbiased estimators**. The paper shows (Appendix A, Figure 9) that combining 4/6 with SR on the backward pass produces gradient estimates whose error does not decrease as 1/B with more accumulation steps — the hallmark of bias. The deterministic selection between scale branches, each individually unbiased, introduces a net preference that accumulates rather than averaging out.

This is a practically important boundary because the temptation to port forward-pass improvements to the backward pass is natural — if 4/6 improves forward-pass MSE, why wouldn't it help gradient estimation? The paper's answer is crisp: **MSE is the wrong metric for gradient estimators; unbiasedness is the right metric, and unbiasedness is not preserved under deterministic selection.** This cleanly separates the design space: forward pass → optimize for MSE (use 4/6, RTN, whatever deterministic tricks improve fidelity); backward pass → optimize for unbiasedness with controlled variance (use MS-EDEN, avoid data-dependent branch selection).

The broader implication is that the backward pass is a **more constrained optimization problem** than the forward pass — not just "harder" but subject to different correctness criteria. Prior work (NVIDIA et al., 2025; TetraJet-v2) implicitly recognized this by treating SR as the backward-pass default, but didn't articulate the principle that separates acceptable from unacceptable backward-pass optimizations. Quartet II's explicit treatment of this boundary — adopting 4/6 on the forward pass while rejecting it on the backward pass, with empirical justification — provides a template for reasoning about future quantization heuristics.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary experiments (Sections 6.1–6.2) use the C4 dataset (Dodge et al., 2021) for language modeling pre-training. Nanochat experiments (Section 6.2) additionally use FineWeb-Edu (Lozhkov et al., 2024) for pre-training and downstream benchmarks (ARC, GSM8K, HumanEval, MMLU) for post-training evaluation. Appendix A uses a pre-trained Llama-3.2-1B model (et al., 2024) for unbiasedness verification, not the C4-trained models.

- **Base model(s).** All main experiments train Llama-2-style Transformer models (Vaswani et al., 2023; Touvron et al., 2023) from scratch. Four model sizes are swept: 30M, 50M, 100M, and 200M parameters (Section 6.1, Table 3). Nanochat experiments (Section 6.2) use 560M and 1.9B parameter models following the Nanochat architecture (Karpathy, 2025) with Muon optimizer, QK-normalization, and ReLU² activations. The backward-pass quantization validation (Section 6.1, Figure 1) selectively enables quantization on these same Llama-2-style models. No pre-trained base models are used as starting points — all training runs are from random initialization.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **C4 validation loss increase relative to BF16 training** (measured as absolute loss difference or percentage increase). For Nanochat, the primary pre-training metric is **increase in bits-per-byte (BPB)** relative to BF16 (Figure 5). Post-training evaluation uses zero-shot accuracy on ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, GSM8K, HumanEval, and MMLU (Table 5). For the unbiasedness verification (Appendix A, Figure 9), the metric is the **relative L2 error** between the average of B independently quantized backward passes and the unquantized backward pass: `$||\frac{1}{B} \sum \hat{G}(\omega) - G||_2^2 / ||G||_2^2$`, which should decrease as ~1/B if the estimator is unbiased.

- **Baselines.** Four prior NVFP4 schemes are compared:
  - **NVIDIA** (NVIDIA et al., 2025): Forward pass with square-block quantization (16×16 group scales) and RTN; backward pass with SR and RHT on the inner dimension, without weight re-quantization on the input-gradient GEMM.
  - **4/6** (Cook et al., 2025): The forward pass from NVIDIA augmented with Four-over-Six scale selection. The paper does not apply 4/6 to the backward pass due to bias concerns (validated in Appendix A, Figure 9). Section 6.1 does not explicitly specify which backward-pass quantization 4/6 uses — the baseline name "4/6" appears to refer to forward-pass improvement over the standard NVIDIA backward pass, but the exact backward-pass configuration is ambiguous.
  - **TetraJet-v2** (Chen et al., 2025b): The "GPU-feasible scheme" as defined in Section 2: forward pass with native 1×16 group scales and RTN (no square blocks); backward pass with SR and RHT on the inner dimension for both GEMMs, including weight re-quantization. The paper explicitly does not implement TetraJet-v2's intermediate FP32 scales or outlier channels.
  - **Quartet II** (this paper): Forward pass with native 1×16 group scales, RTN, and 4/6 scale selection; backward pass with MS-EDEN (RHT + deterministic RTN quantization + stochastic rounding of EDEN-corrected FP8 scales) on both GEMMs.
  The unquantized **BF16** baseline uses standard BF16 matrix multiplications and serves as the reference for all loss gap measurements.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** "Compute" is measured in **tokens consumed during pre-training** (not inference generation budget, since this is a training paper). The data-to-parameter ratio D/N is swept from 25 to 800 tokens per parameter (Section 6.1), covering from approximately compute-optimal (Hoffmann et al., 2022) to heavily over-trained regimes. All schemes within a given D/N × model size configuration process exactly the same amount of data with the same optimizer hyperparameters (learning rate, weight decay, batch size) as the BF16 baseline — the only difference is the quantization of linear-layer operations. Speedup measurements (Section 7) use per-operation latency for linear layers (Figure 6) and end-to-end training throughput in ktok/s (Section 7.2) on an NVIDIA RTX 5090.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is used in the standard sense — all results are from single training runs (the paper does not report error bars or multiple seeds). The unbiasedness experiment (Appendix A) uses `$B = 2^0, 2^1, ..., 2^13$` independent quantized backward passes over the same batch of sample data to verify that relative error decreases as ~1/B. Hyperparameters for all QAT runs are **identical** to the BF16 baseline (Section 6.1: "We reuse all hyper-parameters (including LR and weight decay) between BF16 baseline and QAT runs"), eliminating hyperparameter tuning as a confounding variable. For Nanochat (Section 6.2), the same policy applies — all training hyperparameters from the unquantized baseline scripts are preserved.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Backward Pass Quantization in Isolation (Figure 1)

The paper first validates MS-EDEN's gradient estimation quality by selectively quantizing only the backward pass while keeping the forward pass in BF16. This isolates the effect of gradient quantization from forward-pass representation effects.

**Five quantization configurations are tested** (panel labels correspond to Figure 1 subplots):

- **(a) `$E \cdot W^T, Q(E^T) \cdot Q(X^T)^T$`:** Only the weight-gradient GEMM is quantized (both operands). The input-gradient GEMM remains in BF16.
- **(b) `$Q(E) \cdot W, E^T \cdot X$`:** Only the input-gradient GEMM is quantized **without** weight re-quantization — `$W$` is used in its original BF16 form, and only `$E$` is quantized.
- **(c) `$Q(E) \cdot Q(W^T)^T, E^T \cdot X$`:** Only the input-gradient GEMM is quantized **with** weight re-quantization — both `$E$` and `$W$` are quantized.
- **(d) `$Q(E) \cdot W, Q(E^T) \cdot Q(X^T)^T$`:** Both backward GEMMs are quantized **without** weight re-quantization on the input-gradient side.
- **(e) `$Q(E) \cdot Q(W^T)^T, Q(E^T) \cdot Q(X^T)^T$`:** Both backward GEMMs are quantized **with** weight re-quantization on the input-gradient side. This is the full backward-pass configuration corresponding to Quartet II.

For each configuration, the paper compares **SR** (the prior state-of-the-art) and **MS-EDEN** (where applicable — MS-EDEN is incompatible with configurations (b) and (d) because it requires weight re-quantization). RHT is applied on the inner dimension whenever both operands in a GEMM are quantized.

**Headline results from Figure 1:**

- **In every configuration where both are applicable, MS-EDEN consistently produces lower validation loss increase than SR.** For configuration (a) (weight-gradient GEMM only), MS-EDEN shows roughly 0.003–0.005 loss increase over BF16 at D/N = 800 across model sizes 30M–100M, while SR shows roughly 0.004–0.007 — a relative improvement of approximately 25–40%. For configuration (c) (input-gradient with re-quantization), the gap is similar. For configuration (e) (full backward pass with re-quantization), MS-EDEN achieves roughly 0.005–0.010 loss increase vs. SR's roughly 0.008–0.014 across the D/N range.

- **The most important comparison is (d) vs. (e):** SR without weight re-quantization (d) — the NVIDIA-style approach that avoids backward-pass weight re-quantization by using square-block quantization on the forward pass — is **directly outperformed by MS-EDEN with weight re-quantization** (e). For the 100M model at D/N = 200, panel (d) with SR shows approximately 0.010–0.012 loss increase, while panel (e) with MS-EDEN shows approximately 0.007–0.008. This validates the paper's central claim: **accepting weight re-quantization and using MS-EDEN is better than avoiding re-quantization and using SR**, even though the former performs an extra quantization operation.

- **Comparing (c) vs. (e):** Adding the weight-gradient GEMM quantization (moving from only input-gradient quantized to both GEMMs quantized) increases the loss gap, but MS-EDEN degrades more gracefully than SR. For SR, the gap between (c) and (e) is roughly 0.004–0.005 absolute loss increase; for MS-EDEN, it's roughly 0.002–0.003.

- **The advantage holds across model sizes and token budgets.** The paper tests 30M, 50M, and 100M models (N=30M, 50M, 100M in the figure) with D/N from roughly 25 to 800. MS-EDEN's advantage is most pronounced at smaller models and lower D/N (where gradient noise matters more), but the ordering is consistent throughout.

- **No results are shown for 200M models in Figure 1.** The backward-pass ablation stops at 100M parameters; the 200M model appears only in the full quantization comparison (Figure 4).

#### Forward Pass Quantization in Isolation (Figure 2)

The paper ablates forward-pass quantization by quantizing only the forward pass (all GEMMs) while keeping the backward pass in BF16. Four configurations are compared, varying group scale type (square-block 16×16 vs. native 1×16) and 4/6 usage.

**Headline results from Figure 2:**

- **Native 1×16 group scales consistently outperform square-block 16×16 scales.** Across all model sizes (30M–200M) and D/N ratios, the curves for "1x16gs" are below those for "16x16gs," representing lower validation loss increase over BF16. For the 200M model at D/N = 800, native scales show roughly 0.04–0.05 loss increase while square-block shows roughly 0.07–0.08 — approximately a 40% reduction in the gap.

- **4/6 provides a larger relative improvement for native scales than for square-block scales.** For native scales, adding 4/6 reduces the loss gap by roughly 30–50% (e.g., 200M at D/N = 400: "1x16gs" ~0.05 loss increase, "1x16gs+4/6" ~0.03–0.04). For square-block scales, the improvement is more modest (~10–20%), because 4/6 effectively only improves activation quantization in the square-block case — the weights are already constrained by the coarse 16×16 scales. The paper notes this explicitly (Section 6.1): "'4/6' synergizes with native group scales — a novel result we incorporate into Quartet II."

- **The native 1×16 + 4/6 combination ("1x16gs+4/6") is the best forward-pass-only configuration** at nearly all model sizes and D/N ratios. This motivates Quartet II's forward-pass design choice.

- **No results are shown for SR on the forward pass** — SR is only used on the backward pass, where unbiasedness matters. The forward pass uses deterministic RTN throughout, since representation fidelity (not expectation properties) is the objective.

#### Full Quantization: Quartet II vs. Prior Schemes (Figure 4)

This is the paper's central comparison: both forward and backward passes quantized to NVFP4, comparing Quartet II against three prior schemes.

**Headline results from Figure 4:**

- **Quartet II achieves the lowest validation loss increase relative to BF16 across all model sizes (30M, 50M, 100M, 200M) and all D/N ratios (25–800).** This is the paper's primary empirical claim.

- **The gap reduction relative to prior schemes is at least 20%.** For example, at 100M parameters and D/N = 200, the approximate loss increases are: NVIDIA ~0.10, 4/6 ~0.09, TetraJet-v2 ~0.08, Quartet II ~0.06. The improvement from TetraJet-v2 to Quartet II (~0.02 absolute) represents roughly 25% of the TetraJet-v2 gap to BF16 (~0.08). At 200M and D/N = 400: NVIDIA ~0.10, 4/6 ~0.08, TetraJet-v2 ~0.07, Quartet II ~0.05 — the Quartet II gap is roughly 50% of the BF16 gap that TetraJet-v2 still exhibits.

- **NVIDIA is consistently the worst performer**, with its square-block forward pass and SR backward pass. This validates the paper's diagnosis that both the forward-pass representation compromise (square-block) and the backward-pass variance penalty (SR) are costly.

- **4/6 improves over NVIDIA** but trails TetraJet-v2 and Quartet II, consistent with 4/6 being a forward-pass-only improvement that doesn't address the SR variance issue on the backward pass.

- **TetraJet-v2 is the strongest prior baseline**, benefiting from native forward-pass scales (no square-block penalty) and corrected backward-pass re-quantization. This makes Quartet II's improvement over TetraJet-v2 the most informative comparison: both use native forward-pass scales and weight re-quantization on the backward pass, so the difference isolates the effect of replacing SR with MS-EDEN on the backward pass.

- **The improvement is most pronounced at moderate model sizes and token budgets**, where training is far enough from the BF16 optimum that differences in gradient quality matter, but not so data-starved that all methods degrade similarly. At very low D/N (25), all methods show larger gaps and the relative differences compress somewhat.

#### Nanochat Pre-Training at Larger Scale (Figure 5, Table 5)

The paper validates Quartet II on two larger-scale training runs using the Nanochat pipeline (Karpathy, 2025), which differs from the Llama-2-style ablations in architecture (Muon optimizer, QK-normalization, ReLU² activations), data (FineWeb-Edu), and training recipe (WSD learning rate schedule with mid-training and SFT phases).

**Headline results from Figure 5 (pre-training validation BPB increase over BF16):**

- **560M parameters, 11B tokens ("Speedrun"):** Quartet II achieves approximately 1.22% increase in bits-per-byte over BF16, compared to NVIDIA (~1.57%), 4/6 (~1.52%), and TetraJet-v2 (~1.56%). This represents roughly a 22% relative reduction in the BPB gap vs. TetraJet-v2.

- **1.9B parameters, 38B tokens ("$1000 run"):** Quartet II achieves approximately 1.44% BPB increase over BF16, compared to NVIDIA (~1.92%), 4/6 (~1.76%), and TetraJet-v2 (~1.72%). This represents roughly a 16% relative reduction vs. TetraJet-v2.

- **The loss curves are stable for all methods**, with no divergence or NaN issues. The paper notes: "Loss spikes are observed for both BF16 and QAT around 6T tokens but training stabilizes later" (Figure 5 caption), indicating that the spikes are not quantization-specific.

- **The absolute BPB values (Table 5)** show: for the 1.9B model, BF16 reaches 0.6925 BPB, Quartet II reaches 0.7025, TetraJet-v2 reaches 0.7044, NVIDIA reaches 0.7058. The differences between QAT methods are small in absolute terms (~0.002–0.003 BPB) but consistent in ordering.

**Headline results from Table 5 (post-training zero-shot benchmarks):**

- **The differences between QAT methods on downstream benchmarks are largely not statistically significant.** Table 5 reports trust intervals (2 standard deviations) for the 560M BF16 baseline, showing e.g., ArcC 38.8±2.8%, GSM8K 11.4±1.8%. The FP4 methods all fall within roughly 1–1.5 standard deviations of each other on most benchmarks.

- **Quartet II does not consistently outperform other QAT methods on downstream tasks.** For the 1.9B model: ArcC – Quartet II 52.1% vs. NVIDIA 56.8% (Quartet II is worse); GSM8K – Quartet II 16.9% vs. NVIDIA 17.1% (essentially tied); HumanEval – Quartet II 5.5% vs. TetraJet-v2 8.5% (Quartet II is worse); MMLU – Quartet II 41.6% vs. NVIDIA 43.0% (Quartet II is worse). The paper acknowledges this: "insignificant differences between the QAT methods, probably due to short instruction tuning and small test datasets."

- **The pre-training BPB advantage does not reliably translate to downstream task improvement** in this experimental setup. This is a notable limitation — the paper's main claim of improved training quality is validated on pre-training loss, but the downstream benefit is not demonstrated.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Unbiasedness verification of MS-EDEN (Appendix A, Figure 9):** The paper verifies that Quartet II produces effectively unbiased gradients by measuring how the relative L2 error between the average quantized gradient and the unquantized gradient decreases with accumulation steps B. For an unbiased estimator, this error should decrease as ~1/B (from the Central Limit Theorem). Quartet II's error concentration curve closely follows the 1/B reference line for three weight matrices (q_proj, k_proj, v_proj) in Layer 0 of a Llama-3.2-1B model, confirming unbiasedness despite practical compromises (128-element rotation groups instead of infinite dimension, identical rotations across groups, and no stochastic rounding on underflowing FP8 values). NVIDIA and TetraJet-v2 also show ~1/B behavior, confirming their SR-based estimators are unbiased. **The NVIDIA+4/6 combination plateaus** at a non-zero error level as B increases (roughly 10⁻¹ to 10⁻²), providing direct empirical evidence that 4/6 introduces bias when applied to the backward pass. This is the paper's justification for restricting 4/6 to the forward pass only.

- **Impact of weight re-quantization on MS-EDEN vs. SR (Figure 1, panels c–e):** The comparison of panel (c) (input-gradient only, with weight re-quantization) vs. panel (b) (input-gradient only, without weight re-quantization) isolates the cost of weight re-quantization. For SR, the gap between (b) and (c) is substantial (roughly 0.003–0.005 additional loss increase), confirming that weight re-quantization with SR is costly. For MS-EDEN, the gap between panel (c) and the hypothetical (no-re-quantization) cannot be measured because MS-EDEN requires re-quantization — but MS-EDEN in panel (c) already outperforms SR in panel (b) (without re-quantization), demonstrating that MS-EDEN's intrinsic advantage outweighs the re-quantization penalty. Panel (e) vs. panel (d) shows the same pattern for the full backward pass: MS-EDEN with re-quantization beats SR without re-quantization.

- **Effect of rotation group size on unbiasedness (Appendix A):** The paper uses 128-element rotation groups (to match `mma.m16n8k16` tensor core granularity) rather than EDEN's theoretical assumption of independent rotations. Figure 9 validates that this practical compromise preserves unbiasedness — the error decreases as ~1/B — across multiple weight matrices in a pre-trained Llama-3.2-1B model. The paper also uses identical rotations for all groups within a tensor to enable implementing the RHT as a single GEMM. Again, Figure 9 confirms this does not break unbiasedness in practice.

- **4/6 on forward pass with different group scale types (Figure 2):** The ablation shows that 4/6 provides roughly double the relative improvement for native 1×16 scales compared to square-block 16×16 scales. This is explained by the fact that 4/6 improves both weight and activation quantization when native scales are used (since each has independent per-16-element scales), but effectively only improves activation quantization with square-block (since weight scales are fixed at 16×16 blocks). The paper presents this as "a novel result" incorporated into Quartet II's design.

- **FP8 scale precision for EDEN correction (Section 3.2):** The paper argues that FP8 scales cannot directly store EDEN correction factors because the minimum relative update (×1.0625) is too coarse for values in [0.94, 1.06]. Stochastic rounding on the scales resolves this — the expected value is exact even though individual realizations are quantized. This is not experimentally ablated (e.g., comparing FP16 vs. FP8 scale storage for the correction), but the mathematical reasoning is clear.

- **Post-hoc range alignment for kernel efficiency (Section 7, Table 2):** The paper compares the naive MS-EDEN re-quantization kernel (two full tensor loads, two RHT operations) with the post-hoc range alignment kernel (one full tensor load + one cheap scale-only update). The optimization reduces GMEM bandwidth by approximately 20% (9.0 → 5.5 bits read per element, 4.5 → 5.5 bits written per element) and eliminates one GEMM call per NVFP4 group (2 → 1 `mma.m16n8k16` instructions per group). The practical latency reduction of the second kernel is reported as "more than 10× less than the first one." This is validated through the linear-layer speedup measurements in Figure 6 and end-to-end throughput in Section 7.2.

- **Robustness to model scale and architecture:** The paper validates Quartet II on two distinct training pipelines — Llama-2-style models trained on C4 (Section 6.1) and Nanochat models trained on FineWeb-Edu (Section 6.2) — with different optimizers (AdamW vs. Muon), learning rate schedules (cosine vs. WSD), and architectural details (standard MLP vs. ReLU² activations, standard attention vs. QK-normalization). Quartet II's advantage over prior schemes is consistent across both pipelines, suggesting that MS-EDEN's gradient estimation improvement is not architecture-specific.

- **Negative result: 4/6 on backward pass (Appendix A, Figure 9):** The paper demonstrates that adding 4/6 to the NVIDIA backward pass breaks unbiasedness. The error concentration curve for "NVIDIA+4/6" plateaus rather than continuing to decrease as 1/B. This is a clean negative result that establishes a boundary for backward-pass quantization: deterministic data-dependent scale selection introduces bias even when the underlying quantizer is unbiased.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "MS-EDEN has more than 2× lower quantization error than SR" (from the Abstract and Table 1)

**What was tested:** Table 1 reports MSE on N(0, 1) synthetic data for SR (23.5 × 10⁻³) vs. MS-EDEN (9.8 × 10⁻³), a factor of 2.4×.

**Strengths:** The comparison is clean — same data distribution, same NVFP4 format, both unbiased. The 2.4× factor is unambiguous for this synthetic test.

**Weaknesses:** This is measured on synthetic Gaussian data, not on actual gradient tensors from LLM training. The relationship between synthetic-data MSE and actual training loss is not direct — gradient tensors may have different distributions (sparsity, outliers, structured correlations) that could affect the relative performance of MS-EDEN vs. SR. The paper does not report per-tensor MSE measurements on real gradient tensors from a training run. The "more than 2×" claim in the abstract should therefore be qualified as applying to the synthetic benchmark; the real training advantage may differ in magnitude, though the consistent improvement in Figures 1, 4, and 5 suggests the advantage is real and substantial.

#### Claim 2: "Quartet II achieves consistently better gradient estimation across all major matrix multiplications" (from the Abstract)

**What was tested:** Figure 1 ablates backward-pass quantization configurations (a)–(e) and shows that MS-EDEN outperforms SR in all configurations where both are applicable — (a), (c), and (e). The conclusion that MS-EDEN produces better gradient estimates is supported.

**What was not tested:** The ablation does not measure "gradient estimation quality" directly (e.g., cosine similarity to the BF16 gradient, or gradient variance). It measures final validation loss after full training runs, which is the downstream consequence of better gradient estimation over many steps. This is the right metric for practical impact, but it conflates gradient estimation quality with other factors that affect training dynamics. A direct measurement of per-step gradient error (e.g., Figure 9-style concentration experiments at different points during training, not just on a pre-trained model) would strengthen the mechanistic claim. Additionally, the ablation in Figure 1 only goes up to 100M parameters — the 200M model is absent from the backward-pass-only analysis, which is the regime where the paper's central theoretical contribution (gradient estimation) is most directly tested.

#### Claim 3: "Quartet II yields consistently improved gradient estimation... we validate these improvements in end-to-end LLM pre-training" and "Quartet II improves consistently... by at least 20% in terms of loss" (from the Abstract and Section 6.1)

**What was tested:** Figure 4 compares Quartet II against three baselines across 30M–200M models at D/N from 25 to 800, showing Quartet II achieves the lowest validation loss increase relative to BF16 at every point. Figure 5 extends this to 560M and 1.9B models in a different training pipeline.

**Strengths:** The consistency is striking — Quartet II wins at every model size, every token budget, and in both training pipelines. There are no crossover points where another method pulls ahead. The 20% improvement figure is a reasonable summary: at most data points in Figure 4, the absolute loss gap reduction from TetraJet-v2 to Quartet II is roughly 20–30% of the TetraJet-v2-to-BF16 gap.

**Weaknesses:**

1. **Single training run per configuration.** The paper reports no error bars, standard deviations, or multiple seeds. The loss curves in Figure 5 appear smooth and well-separated, suggesting the differences are real, but without any measure of variability, the statistical reliability of the 20% figure cannot be assessed. At the larger model sizes (1.9B), the absolute BPB differences between methods are small (0.0019 BPB between Quartet II and TetraJet-v2 in Table 5), and it's unclear whether this difference would replicate across random seeds.

2. **The "20% improvement" figure is relative to the already-small gap between prior methods and BF16.** At 100M parameters and D/N = 200, the TetraJet-v2 gap to BF16 is approximately 0.08 loss, and Quartet II reduces this to approximately 0.06 — an absolute improvement of 0.02 loss. Whether a 0.02 improvement in C4 validation loss at 100M scale translates to meaningful downstream differences is not demonstrated. The Nanochat benchmark results (Table 5) show no systematic downstream advantage for Quartet II over other QAT methods, which raises the question: **is the pre-training loss improvement large enough to matter for practical applications?**

3. **The 4/6 baseline configuration is ambiguous.** Figure 4 includes "4/6" as a baseline, but Section 6.1 does not specify what backward-pass quantization this baseline uses. If "4/6" means "NVIDIA forward pass with 4/6 + NVIDIA backward pass with SR," that's a specific (and apparently worse) combination. If it means something else, the comparison is unclear. This ambiguity matters because the "20% improvement" claim is partly benchmarked against this baseline.

4. **The 1.9B parameter run (38B tokens) is relatively small scale for modern LLM pre-training.** The paper claims relevance to "massive models" and "LLM pre-training," but the largest model trained is 1.9B parameters — roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than state-of-the-art foundation models (Llama 3 is 8B–405B). The paper does not demonstrate that MS-EDEN's advantage scales to the regime where 4-bit training would be most economically impactful (10B+ parameters, 1T+ tokens).

#### Claim 4: "Up to 4.2× speedup over BF16" (from the Abstract and Figure 6)

**What was tested:** Figure 6 reports linear-layer-only speedup over BF16 for characteristic layer shapes from 800M–22B models on an RTX 5090. The 4.2× figure corresponds to the 800M model configuration (or possibly a specific layer within it — the paper does not specify which model size corresponds to the peak 4.2×). End-to-end training throughput on a 1.1B model achieves 2.45× over BF16 (51 vs. 21 ktok/s).

**Strengths:** The linear-layer measurement isolates the GEMMs that Quartet II accelerates, controlling for attention and other operations. The end-to-end measurement includes all overhead. The comparison against Quartet (Castro et al., 2025) for MXFP4 (~70% improvement) provides a relevant prior-art baseline.

**Weaknesses:**

1. **The 4.2× figure is for linear layers only, not end-to-end training.** The abstract says "up to 4.2× speedup over BF16" without qualification. The main text clarifies this is linear-layer speedup, but the abstract could be misread as claiming end-to-end speedup. The end-to-end speedup is 2.45× (Section 7.2), and only at a small model size (1.1B) where memory savings from FP4 permit a larger micro-batch size — the speedup is partly due to better memory utilization, not purely compute throughput.

2. **Single GPU, single model size for end-to-end measurements.** The end-to-end throughput is measured only for a single 1.1B parameter model on a single RTX 5090. As model size grows, the fraction of time spent in linear layers increases (the paper acknowledges that at 1.1B, ~60% of time is in non-GEMM operations), so end-to-end speedup should approach the 4× linear-layer figure. But this extrapolation is not validated with measurements at larger model sizes or in distributed settings.

3. **Kernel support is specific to Blackwell GPUs.** The CUDA kernels use `mma.m16n8k16` tensor core instructions that are Blackwell-specific. The paper does not discuss portability to other hardware (AMD, previous NVIDIA generations) or to MXFP4 format, which limits the practical audience.

4. **The paper does not measure speedup for the full Quartet II scheme at the largest validated model size (1.9B).** Speedup measurements are on 800M–22B characteristic layer shapes (Figure 6), not on actual end-to-end training of the 1.9B Nanochat model. The 2.45× end-to-end figure is for a 1.1B model with a custom configuration (fused QKV GEMMs) that may differ from the 1.9B training setup.

#### Claim 5: "Our proposal synergizes well with recent training improvements aimed specifically at NVFP4" (from the Abstract)

**What was tested:** The paper incorporates 4/6 (Cook et al., 2025) on the forward pass, and shows that the combination of native 1×16 scales + 4/6 + MS-EDEN outperforms either improvement alone. Figure 4 shows Quartet II (which includes 4/6 on forward) outperforming TetraJet-v2 (which does not include 4/6), and Figure 1 shows MS-EDEN (which does not include 4/6) outperforming SR alone. The claim of "synergy" is that the forward-pass gain from 4/6 and the backward-pass gain from MS-EDEN are complementary rather than redundant.

**Strengths:** The separate ablations (Figure 1 for backward pass, Figure 2 for forward pass) combined with the full comparison (Figure 4) support the claim that the improvements are additive — each component independently closes part of the gap to BF16.

**Weaknesses:** The paper does not include a Quartet II variant without 4/6 on the forward pass, which would directly measure how much of the gain in Figure 4 comes from 4/6 vs. MS-EDEN. Without this ablation, the quantitative contribution of "synergy" vs. MS-EDEN on the backward pass alone cannot be isolated. The comparison of "TetraJet-v2" vs. "Quartet II" in Figure 4 confounds two changes: the backward-pass quantizer (SR → MS-EDEN) and the forward-pass scale selection (no 4/6 → 4/6). The improvement from TetraJet-v2 to Quartet II is therefore an upper bound on the contribution of MS-EDEN alone.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

1. **A direct gradient-quality comparison at scale:** Measure per-step gradient cosine similarity or gradient variance between Quartet II and BF16 (or Quartet II vs. SR-based schemes) during training, not just final validation loss. This would provide mechanistic evidence for the paper's core claim that MS-EDEN improves gradient estimation, rather than relying solely on downstream loss as a proxy.

2. **MS-EDEN without 4/6 on the forward pass (full Quartet II ablation):** A Quartet II configuration with native 1×16 scales, RTN forward pass, MS-EDEN backward pass, but *no* 4/6, would isolate the contribution of MS-EDEN from the contribution of 4/6 in the full-system comparison.

3. **Training runs at larger scale (7B+ parameters):** The paper's largest pre-training run is 1.9B parameters on 38B tokens, which is 20 tokens-per-parameter — well below the 200–800 D/N ratios used in the smaller-scale ablations. A 7B model trained to compute-optimal or overtrained ratios would substantially strengthen the claim that Quartet II is practical for "massive models."

4. **Multiple random seeds:** The paper reports no measure of variability. Two or three seeds per configuration at the 100M scale would provide confidence intervals and help assess whether the observed differences are statistically significant — particularly important given that the absolute improvements over TetraJet-v2 at larger scales are small.

5. **Downstream evaluation on the Llama-2-style models:** The paper evaluates downstream performance only on Nanochat (Table 5), where pre-training improvements did not translate to downstream gains. Evaluating the C4-trained Llama-2-style models on standard benchmarks (HellaSwag, PIQA, etc.) would show whether the consistent pre-training loss improvements in Figure 4 translate to better models.

6. **Comparison against a BF16 baseline with matched training time:** The paper compares QAT methods against BF16 at the same number of training tokens, but FP4 training is faster. A comparison where BF16 is given proportionally more training time (to match wall-clock time) would address the practical question: "Should I use Quartet II with 2× more tokens or BF16 with fewer tokens for the same training budget?" This is analogous to the FLOPs-matched comparison in the reference paper and is notably absent here.

7. **Ablation of the EDEN correction factor itself:** What happens if MS-EDEN is used without the EDEN correction — i.e., deterministic RTN with RHT but no scale correction? This would measure how much of MS-EDEN's advantage comes from the RHT normalization vs. the bias correction. If RHT alone provides most of the benefit (by making the quantization error more Gaussian and less structured), the EDEN correction might be less critical than the paper implies.

8. **GRADIENT VARIANCE MEASUREMENTS DURING TRAINING:** The paper measures quantization MSE on synthetic data (Table 1) and final validation loss after training, but never directly measures the variance of gradient estimates during training. Since MS-EDEN's claimed mechanism is reduced gradient variance relative to SR, a direct measurement (e.g., computing the variance of quantized gradients across multiple forward/backward passes on the same data, at different points during training) would provide the strongest causal evidence for the paper's central thesis.

#### Summary Assessment

The experiments **genuinely support** the paper's core claims — Quartet II reduces validation loss relative to prior NVFP4 recipes across multiple model sizes and training configurations. The consistency of the improvement (no crossover points, replicated across two training pipelines) is the strongest evidence. However, the experiments demonstrate something **narrower** than what the abstract implies: the improvement is validated on relatively small-scale pre-training (up to 1.9B parameters, 38B tokens), on a single benchmark (C4 language modeling loss, plus BPB for Nanochat), without demonstrated downstream task benefits, and without the statistical rigor (multiple seeds) or ablation depth (MS-EDEN without 4/6 on forward pass) that would make the mechanistic claims fully airtight. The speedup claims are real but specific to Blackwell hardware, linear layers measured in isolation, and small-batch configurations where memory savings permit larger micro-batches — the generalizability to distributed large-scale training is plausible but not demonstrated.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Validation Scale: Largest Training Run Is 1.9B Parameters on 38B Tokens — Two Orders of Magnitude Below Production LLM Pre-Training

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper positions Quartet II as a solution for "massive models" and "LLM pre-training" (Abstract, Section 1), and the motivating framing in Section 1 invokes the exponential cost growth of "state-of-the-art foundation models." Yet the largest end-to-end training run is 1.9B parameters trained on 38B tokens (Section 6.2, Figure 5) — roughly the scale of GPT-2, not GPT-4 or Llama 3. At this scale, the training budget is 20 tokens per parameter, which is substantially below the 200–800 D/N ratios used in the smaller-scale Llama-2-style ablations (Section 6.1, Figure 4) and far below the ~200+ D/N typical of production LLM training. The paper does not validate Quartet II at 7B, 13B, 70B, or larger scales where 4-bit training would deliver the most economically meaningful FLOPs savings and where emergent training dynamics (loss spikes, gradient variance accumulation over trillions of tokens) might manifest differently.

**The consequence.** It is unknown whether the 15–25% relative reduction in BPB gap compared to prior NVFP4 recipes (Figure 5) persists, grows, or shrinks at production scale. Several mechanisms could cause degradation at larger scales:

- **Gradient noise accumulation over many more steps.** MS-EDEN's stochastic rounding on FP8 scales introduces a small but non-zero variance (mitigated but not eliminated compared to per-element SR). Over 1T+ tokens, this variance might accumulate into a measurable residual gap relative to BF16 that is not visible in 38B-token runs. The paper's own unbiasedness verification (Appendix A, Figure 9) only checks a single backward pass on a pre-trained model, not the long-term stochastic properties of training trajectories.

- **Interaction with distributed training.** The paper's throughput measurements (Section 7.2) are on a single RTX 5090. At production scale, quantization error interacts with data-parallel gradient aggregation, pipeline parallelism, and tensor parallelism. The EDEN correction factors are computed per-rotation-group (128 elements) and per-GPU, but the paper does not analyze whether the correction factors are statistically consistent across devices or whether cross-device gradient variance is affected.

- **Emergent instabilities.** The paper notes loss spikes "observed for both BF16 and QAT around 6T tokens but training stabilizes later" (Figure 5 caption), but this is at 11B-token scale. Larger models are known to exhibit loss spikes that are sensitive to numerical precision (e.g., the FP8 training literature documents this). Whether MS-EDEN's variance properties make Quartet II more or less susceptible to such instabilities at scale is unexamined.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly by not claiming validation beyond 1.9B parameters, but it does not discuss the scale gap as a limitation. The Llama-2-style ablations (Figure 4) sweep 30M–200M parameters across a wide range of D/N ratios (25–800) to test robustness to overtraining, which is a strength — the improvements hold consistently. However, the 200M model at D/N = 800 processes 160B tokens, which is actually more data than the 1.9B Nanochat run (38B). So the paper does test token-scale robustness in the smaller-model regime. The gap is in model-scale robustness: no experiment bridges the gap between 200M and production-scale 7B+ models.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss future work at larger scale, acknowledge the gap, or argue why the 1.9B result should extrapolate. A practitioner considering Quartet II for a 70B-parameter training run has no direct evidence that the advantage over prior NVFP4 recipes withstands the scaling transition.

### 6.2 The Pre-Training Loss Improvements Do Not Translate to Downstream Task Improvements in the Paper's Own Evaluation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central empirical claim is that Quartet II reduces the pre-training validation loss gap relative to BF16 by 15–25% compared to prior NVFP4 recipes (Section 6.1, Figure 4; Section 6.2, Figure 5). The implicit assumption — standard in the pre-training literature — is that lower pre-training loss implies better downstream model quality after fine-tuning. The Nanochat experiments (Section 6.2, Table 5) test this assumption by evaluating downstream benchmarks (ARC, GSM8K, HumanEval, MMLU) after mid-training and supervised fine-tuning.

**The consequence.** The downstream results in Table 5 contradict the assumption. Quartet II does not consistently outperform other QAT methods on downstream tasks, and in several cases it performs **worse** than the NVIDIA baseline despite having measurably better pre-training BPB:

- **1.9B model, ArcC:** Quartet II 52.1% vs. NVIDIA 56.8% (Quartet II is 4.7 points worse).
- **1.9B model, HumanEval:** Quartet II 5.5% vs. TetraJet-v2 8.5% (Quartet II is 3.0 points worse).
- **1.9B model, MMLU:** Quartet II 41.6% vs. NVIDIA 43.0% (Quartet II is 1.4 points worse).

The statistical significance is limited — the 560M baseline confidence intervals (Table 5) show that ArcC at 38.8±2.8% has enough noise that many pairwise comparisons are inconclusive — but the pattern is inconsistent with a method that "consistently improves" over baselines. If a practitioner chooses Quartet II based on its pre-training loss advantage, they cannot expect the downstream model to be better, and in some cases it may be worse.

This matters because practitioners care about downstream task performance, not pre-training loss per se. A method that improves pre-training metrics without downstream improvement may be a false positive — or at minimum, the pre-training gain is small enough relative to the inherent variance of downstream evaluation that it is overwhelmed by other factors (fine-tuning randomness, benchmark noise).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper presents these results transparently in Table 5 and acknowledges the issue: "insignificant differences between the QAT methods, probably due to short instruction tuning and small test datasets" (Section 6.2, discussion of Table 5). This is a fair caveat — the Nanochat post-training phase includes mid-training and SFT on relatively small datasets, which might not be sufficient to surface pre-training quality differences. However, this caveat also undermines the paper's practical narrative: if the downstream evaluation pipeline used in the paper (Nanochat) is too noisy to detect the claimed improvement, then the paper has not demonstrated that Quartet II produces better models by any metric that end-users care about.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged ("insignificant differences... probably due to short instruction tuning"), but not explored. The paper does not:
- Evaluate downstream tasks on the C4-trained Llama-2-style models (which have more controlled training conditions and might better isolate pre-training quality).
- Run longer SFT or multiple SFT seeds to increase statistical power.
- Discuss whether the pre-training BPB differences (0.0019–0.0033 BPB in Table 5) are large enough to expect downstream effects — it is possible that a 0.3% BPB improvement is genuinely too small to matter for downstream tasks, and the paper does not engage with this possibility.

### 6.3 MS-EDEN Requires Rotations on the Inner GEMM Dimension, Forcing Weight Re-Quantization — Which Adds Memory Bandwidth and Compute Overhead Not Accounted for in the "More Than 2× Lower Error" Narrative

**The assumption or constraint.** MS-EDEN's unbiasedness mechanism relies on applying identical randomized Hadamard transforms (RHT) to both operands of each backward-pass GEMM along their shared inner dimension (Section 3.3, Section 5). This means that the forward-pass quantized weights cannot be reused directly on the backward pass — they must be dequantized, transposed, re-rotated, and re-quantized with MS-EDEN (Section 4.1, Figure 3). This is not a bug — it's a feature of the design — but it introduces a **runtime cost** that the paper does not quantify relative to schemes that avoid weight re-quantization (e.g., NVIDIA et al., 2025, which uses square-block quantization to enable backward-pass reuse).

**The consequence.** The paper's headline efficiency narrative — "MS-EDEN has more than 2× lower quantization error than SR" (Abstract, Table 1) — implicitly suggests that MS-EDEN is a drop-in improvement over SR with no additional costs. But the MS-EDEN backward pass performs extra operations that an SR-based scheme without weight re-quantization does not:

1. **Dequantization of the forward-pass weight tensor** (NVFP4 → BF16/FP32).
2. **Transposition of the weight tensor** (to align the inner dimension for the input-gradient GEMM).
3. **RHT along the inner dimension** (a GEMM operation in itself).
4. **MS-EDEN re-quantization** (RTN quantization + EDEN correction computation + stochastic rounding of scales).

These operations consume memory bandwidth and compute. The paper's kernel design (Section 7, post-hoc range alignment) optimizes the re-quantization step, but it does not eliminate the fundamental overhead: an SR-based scheme that reuses forward-pass quantized weights (NVIDIA-style) avoids steps 1–4 entirely for the weight tensor on the input-gradient GEMM.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges the re-quantization requirement implicitly (Section 4.1: "MS-EDEN requires the application of randomized rotations along the micro-scaling group dimension... This creates the need to re-quantize the weight tensor W") and argues that this is acceptable because MS-EDEN's error is low enough to compensate. Figure 1 empirically validates this: MS-EDEN with weight re-quantization (panel e) outperforms SR without weight re-quantization (panel d). So the **accuracy cost** of re-quantization is negative — MS-EDEN + re-quantization is more accurate than SR without it. But the **runtime cost** is never measured in isolation: the paper reports speedups for Quartet II's full backward pass (Figure 6), which includes the re-quantization overhead, but does not compare against a hypothetical SR-based scheme with weight reuse to show how much overhead the re-quantization adds relative to a simpler alternative.

Table 2 reports GMEM bandwidth for the naive vs. post-hoc MS-EDEN re-quantization kernel, showing a 20% bandwidth reduction from the optimization. But this is comparing two MS-EDEN implementations against each other, not MS-EDEN against no re-quantization at all. An SR-based scheme that reuses forward-pass quantized weights would have zero re-quantization bandwidth for the weight tensor.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed by the speedup measurements (Figure 6, Section 7.2), which show that the full Quartet II backward pass achieves 4.1× speedup over BF16 for linear layers — meaning the overhead, whatever it is, does not eliminate the throughput advantage. However, the paper does not analytically decompose where the 4.1× speedup comes from vs. the theoretical maximum of NVFP4 tensor cores (which is ~4× over BF16 for pure GEMMs). It is plausible that the re-quantization overhead eats into the speedup and that an SR-based scheme with weight reuse would be modestly faster (since it avoids the re-quantization), but the paper provides no data to assess this tradeoff. A practitioner choosing between Quartet II and a simpler scheme might want to know: is the accuracy gain worth the throughput cost of re-quantization? The paper does not provide the data to answer this.

### 6.4 The Four-over-Six Heuristic Cannot Be Used on the Backward Pass — Closing Off a Natural Avenue for Further Gradient Quality Improvement

**The assumption or constraint.** Quartet II uses 4/6 scale selection (Cook et al., 2025) only on the forward pass, where MSE minimization is the objective and bias is irrelevant. On the backward pass, 4/6 is explicitly rejected because "the act of picking a lower MSE scale branch introduces bias, even if both scale branches are individually unbiased via SR" (Section 4.2). Appendix A empirically validates this: when 4/6 is combined with SR on the backward pass ("NVIDIA+4/6" in Figure 9), the gradient estimation error plateaus rather than decreasing as 1/B with more accumulation steps — the hallmark of persistent bias.

**The consequence.** MS-EDEN already provides better backward-pass quantization than SR (Table 1: 9.8 vs. 23.5 × 10⁻³ MSE), but the 4/6 heuristic would provide an additional MSE reduction. On the forward pass, 4/6 reduces RTN's MSE from 9.0 to 7.6 × 10⁻³ (Table 1), a 15% improvement. If a bias-free version of 4/6 could be developed for the backward pass, MS-EDEN + 4/6 might achieve an MSE of approximately 8.3 × 10⁻³ (assuming a similar 15% relative reduction), further narrowing the gap to BF16. But Quartet II's design rules this out — the backward pass is limited to the standard NVFP4 quantization grid (no adaptive scale selection per block), leaving some representation capacity on the table.

This is a **fundamental tradeoff** between bias and variance in gradient estimation that the paper does not resolve. The backward pass is simultaneously where unbiasedness matters most (Section 3: "the backward pass is especially sensitive to bias") and where quantization errors have the largest impact on optimization (gradient errors compound across layers through backpropagation). The inability to use a simple, effective heuristic like 4/6 on the backward pass means that gradient quantization quality is **capped** by the coarseness of the fixed NVFP4 grid — and this cap is independent of MS-EDEN's improvement over SR. Even with perfect unbiasedness, the gradient estimates are limited by the representable values in the FP4 format without adaptive scale selection.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 9 provides unambiguous evidence that 4/6 + SR on the backward pass introduces bias (the error plateau). Table 1 quantifies what is lost: SR+4/6 achieves 17.5 × 10⁻³ MSE on N(0,1) data, while SR alone is 23.5, and MS-EDEN (without 4/6) is 9.8. The gap between 17.5 (biased) and 23.5 (unbiased) represents the MSE reduction that 4/6 could theoretically provide if bias could be eliminated. MS-EDEN achieves 9.8 without 4/6 at all, so it already beats the biased 4/6+SR combination — but it cannot incorporate 4/6's additional 15% improvement.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper identifies the bias problem and chooses to exclude 4/6 from the backward pass — a correct conservative decision. But it does not discuss whether a bias-corrected version of 4/6 is possible (e.g., by making the scale selection itself stochastic, or by incorporating the selection into the EDEN correction framework), nor does it quantify how much additional improvement a hypothetical bias-free 4/6 backward pass would provide over current Quartet II. This leaves open the question: is MS-EDEN close to the best achievable backward-pass quantization for NVFP4, or is there substantial headroom remaining?

### 6.5 Quartet II's Gradient Estimation Advantage Is Validated Only on Language Modeling Pre-Training — No Evidence for Other Modalities, Architectures, or Training Objectives

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper train Transformer language models on text data: Llama-2-style models on C4 (Section 6.1) and Nanochat models on FineWeb-Edu (Section 6.2). The training objective is standard next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss. The paper claims Quartet II is a "fully-NVFP4 linear-layer computation graph" (Section 5) and a "computation scheme for NVFP4 LLM pre-training" (Section 8), which correctly scopes the empirical validation to language models. However, the abstract and introduction frame the contribution more broadly as improving "quantized training" and "gradient estimation" without modality qualification.

**The consequence.** Several properties of language modeling pre-training make it a favorable setting for MS-EDEN that may not generalize:

- **Gradient distributions.** Language model gradients have specific statistical properties — notably, activation outliers in certain feature dimensions (which motivate the use of RHT in the first place). MS-EDEN's RHT-based approach assumes that rotating gradients makes them approximately Gaussian, enabling the scalar EDEN correction to work. This assumption may hold differently for vision transformers, graph neural networks, or multi-modal models where gradient distributions are structured differently (e.g., vision models have spatial correlations that might survive RHT).

- **Training stability.** Language model pre-training with Adam/AdamW/Muon is relatively robust to gradient noise — it tolerates the variance introduced by stochastic gradient descent with small batch sizes. Other training regimes (e.g., RLHF with PPO, meta-learning, adversarial training) may be more sensitive to the specific variance structure of MS-EDEN (correlated noise across groups of 16 elements due to shared scale stochasticity vs. independent noise per element from SR).

- **Architecture specificity.** The paper's backward-pass quantization is validated on standard Transformer linear layers (QKV projections, MLP up/gate/down projections). It is not tested on other matrix multiplication patterns common in architectures like mixture-of-experts (routed GEMMs with dynamic sparsity), differentiable attention mechanisms with higher-order tensor contractions, or sequence-parallel training where the inner dimension of GEMMs may be partitioned.

- **Loss landscape.** Cross-entropy loss for language modeling produces gradients that are averages over many token predictions, providing natural variance reduction. Objectives with sparser supervision signals (e.g., contrastive loss, reinforcement learning from sparse rewards, or molecular property prediction with single scalar targets) might expose gradient variance issues that are masked in the language modeling setting.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None beyond text pre-training. The paper validates on two training pipelines (Llama-2-style and Nanochat) that both train language models, so intra-domain robustness is partially tested (different data, optimizers, architectures within the LM family). But there is no cross-domain or cross-objective validation. The paper does not claim broader applicability, but it also does not discuss the limitation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's title and framing ("Accurate LLM Pre-Training in NVFP4") appropriately scope the contribution to LLMs, but the absence of any discussion about generalization boundaries means a practitioner considering Quartet II for vision or multi-modal training has no guidance on whether the benefits would transfer.

### 6.6 Kernel Support Is Tied to NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs and the QuTLASS Library — Portability to Other Hardware Generations or Vendors Is Not Addressed

**The assumption or constraint.** Quartet II's CUDA kernels (Section 7, Appendix D) use Blackwell-specific tensor core instructions (`mma.m16n8k16`) and the QuTLASS library (Castro & Alistarh, 2025) for NVFP4 matrix multiplications. The RHT is implemented as a GEMM to leverage tensor core throughput. The post-hoc range alignment optimization (Section 7) exploits the specific format of NVFP4's two-level scale representation. These design choices are justified for achieving the paper's speedup targets, but they tie the implementation to a specific hardware generation.

**The consequence.** Quartet II cannot run on:

- **Previous NVIDIA GPU generations** (Hopper, Ampere) that lack native NVFP4 tensor core support. While these GPUs could emulate NVFP4 operations in software or compute them at higher precision, the throughput advantages would vanish — possibly even becoming slower than BF16 due to the quantization overhead.
- **AMD GPUs** (MI300X, etc.), which use the MXFP4 format rather than NVFP4. MS-EDEN's algorithm is format-agnostic in principle (it only requires a microscaling format with FP8 group scales and FP4 elements), but the kernel implementations, tensor core instruction mappings, and scale manipulation logic would need to be rewritten for AMD's ROCm ecosystem and MXFP4 format specifics.
- **Future NVIDIA architectures** if they change the tensor core instruction granularity from `m16n8k16` — the 128-element rotation group size is chosen specifically to match two of these instructions, and a different tile size would require changing the rotation group size and re-validating the unbiasedness properties (Appendix A validates 128-element groups specifically).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides speedup measurements exclusively on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 (Blackwell, Section 7.2, Appendix D). The QuTLASS dependency is stated. No discussion of portability, hardware requirements, or software dependencies is provided beyond the implementation details.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the hardware specificity as a limitation, propose abstractions that would facilitate porting, or provide non-Blackwell reference implementations. For a paper whose contribution includes "Efficient kernels: we show that our scheme is efficiently implementable on the NVIDIA Blackwell generation of GPUs" (Section 1), the hardware specificity is a feature, not a bug — but practitioners without Blackwell hardware (which is new and expensive at the time of writing) have no path to reproducing or deploying the method. The algorithmic contribution (MS-EDEN, the Quartet II computation graph) is hardware-agnostic in theory, but the paper provides no guidance for implementing it efficiently on other platforms.
