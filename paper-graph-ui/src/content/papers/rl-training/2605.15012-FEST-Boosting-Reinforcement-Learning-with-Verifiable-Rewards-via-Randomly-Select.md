# FEST: Boosting Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards via Randomly Selected Few-Shot Guidance

**ArXiv:** [2605.15012](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15012)

## 🎯 Pitch

You can slash the demonstration data needed for math RL training by 99.7%—just 128 random examples suffice—by jointly training with a semi-online DPO loss, but only if you add a specific decaying weight scheme to stop the tiny dataset from causing catastrophic overfitting and model collapse.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **FEST**, a **few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR** algorithm that boosts reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards using only 128 randomly selected SFT demonstrations. Evaluated on six mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME25, AMC23, AIME24, MATH-500, OlympiadBench, Minerva) using Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, FEST integrates three vital components — supervised learning (SFT data providing expert guidance), on-policy learning (agent-generated rollouts as negative examples in a semi-online DPO loss), and decaying weights (preventing overfitting from multiple-epoch training on the tiny few-shot dataset) — to consistently outperform all baselines, achieving an average accuracy of 42.36% with the GRPO variant versus 39.79% for pure RL. The paper demonstrates that FEST matches the performance of methods trained on the full 46K SFT dataset while requiring only 128 demonstrations, and critically establishes that this few-shot paradigm succeeds only when all three components work in concert — ablating any single component causes performance to drop below the vanilla RL baseline or triggers model collapse.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLVR Is Sample-Inefficient on Hard Problems Despite Its Promise

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) represents a significant advance in post-training large language models. Unlike Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which relies on subjective human preferences to assign rewards, RLVR uses objective, deterministic reward signals — unit tests for code generation, exact string matching for mathematical answers — that are free from the noise, ambiguity, and expense of human annotation. This makes RLVR exceptionally well-suited for reasoning-heavy tasks where correctness is well-defined, and it has driven state-of-the-art results including gold-medal performance in international mathematical competitions and preliminary attacks on open research problems.

However, RLVR inherits a fundamental challenge from the broader reinforcement learning literature: **low sample efficiency on complex tasks**. The paper identifies this as the central bottleneck. In mathematical reasoning tasks with binary rewards — where a model either produces the correct final answer or it does not — an entire batch of generated solutions may yield zero correct rollouts. When this happens, every roll-out receives an identical reward, the computed advantage collapses to zero, and the policy gradient update provides **no useful learning signal whatsoever**. The problem is particularly acute on hard questions that push beyond the boundaries of the base model's existing capabilities: the model cannot improve on what it cannot even occasionally get right.

The paper quantifies the practical cost of this inefficiency. State-of-the-art algorithms such as DAPO and CISPO address the zero-signal problem by **repeatedly sampling until at least one successful roll-out is found** — a strategy that can triple the average computational overhead. This is not a minor inefficiency; it represents a fundamental scaling problem. If RLVR requires exponentially more sampling to make progress on incrementally harder problems, the approach becomes economically and environmentally intractable for the hardest reasoning tasks that matter most.

The theoretical significance of this gap is equally important. RLVR is fundamentally an **exploration problem**: the model must discover correct reasoning chains in a vast combinatorial space using only sparse binary feedback. Prior knowledge from pre-training and supervised fine-tuning provides an initial bias — analogous to imitation learning in robotics — but the paper argues that RL-trained LLMs still struggle to **explore beyond the capabilities of their base models**. They can sharpen the output distribution toward correct answers they already produce with some frequency, but they cannot reliably discover qualitatively new solution strategies that the base model would never generate by chance. This is the exploration-exploitation tension recast for language model post-training: exploitation via RL sharpens existing capabilities, but exploration — genuine discovery of new reasoning patterns — needs something more.

---

### Why This Problem Matters: The SFT Data Bottleneck

The naive solution to RLVR's sample-inefficiency problem is to provide more explicit guidance: conduct Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on expert demonstrations when RL fails to produce positive roll-outs. This approach — which the paper terms **demonstration-guided RLVR**, or **unified post-training** — has emerged as the dominant paradigm in recent work. The logic is straightforward: when the model cannot solve a problem on its own, show it a correct reasoning trace and train it to imitate that trace. This gives the model a learning signal even when its own samples are uniformly incorrect.

The paper highlights a critical issue with this solution: **SFT data is extraordinarily expensive to acquire at scale**. The authors provide a concrete example that lands with force: curating just 2,500 questions for the "Humanity's Last Exam" benchmark required contributions from 1,000 graduate-degree holders, and even then they provided only brief rationales — not the long reasoning traces that modern chain-of-thought training demands. This level of human effort scales catastrophically. For mathematical reasoning, expert annotators must not only possess domain expertise but must also produce detailed, step-by-step solutions that expose useful reasoning patterns to the model. The cost per annotated example is high, and the demand for such data is growing faster than the supply of qualified annotators.

The paper also flags several second-order costs. **Bootstrapping and distilling** from existing proprietary models (using GPT-4 or similar systems to generate SFT demonstrations) is an alternative route taken by many practitioners, but this introduces its own set of problems: legal concerns around copyright and terms-of-service violations, financial costs from proprietary API access, and the risk of **model collapse** when models are trained recursively on model-generated data without sufficient fresh human input. Meanwhile, although answer-only RL data — question-answer pairs without reasoning traces — is relatively abundant (large-scale mathematical Q&A can be mined from online forums), raw community answers typically require extensive filtering and rewriting to serve as high-quality SFT demonstrations. The reasoning trace itself — the chain-of-thought connecting question to answer — is the scarce resource.

This sets up the paper's motivating tension: SFT demonstrations improve RLVR, but SFT demonstrations are expensive and scarce; answer-only RL data is cheap and abundant, but it provides only binary feedback that can fail to produce any learning signal on hard problems. The community needs a method that can **extract maximal benefit from minimal SFT data**, using abundant answer-only data as the backbone of training and sparse expert demonstrations as strategic guidance.

---

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper provides a detailed taxonomy of existing demonstration-guided RLVR methods in Table 1, making their limitations explicit. These methods span several design families:

**Reward-based switching methods** (HPT, ReLIFT, SuperRL, MIFO, DyME) identify problems where the model's RL roll-outs fail to achieve positive reward and apply SFT on those specific problems. The SFT demonstrations are pulled from a prepared dataset. The key limitation: these methods require substantial SFT data. HPT uses approximately 10,000 demonstrations; ReLIFT uses 8,600; MIFO uses 6,400. Even the most data-efficient reported method, CHORD, still requires 5,000 demonstrations. For tasks where even 5,000 expert traces would be prohibitively expensive, these methods are non-starters.

**Fixed-ratio blending methods** (LUFFY, SRFT) interleave SFT and RL gradients during every update by treating SFT demonstrations alongside RL roll-outs in a joint optimization. These methods consume even more SFT data — upwards of 46,000 demonstrations — and show no practical path to the extreme few-shot regime.

**Gradient-balancing and dynamic-weighting methods** (CHORD, SASR) attempt to match gradient norms between SFT and RL objectives or apply per-token probability weighting to harmonize the two signals. Even here, CHORD's 5,000-demonstration requirement is the lower bound reported in prior work. SASR operates on simpler tasks and lacks a usable codebase.

**Critically, all these methods share two common weaknesses when pushed to the few-shot limit.** First, they implicitly assume access to demonstrations **on demand** — that is, when the model fails on a particular problem, an SFT trace for that specific problem exists in the prepared dataset. In the few-shot setting, this assumption collapses: with only 128 randomly selected demonstrations covering a tiny fraction of the problem space, there is virtually no chance that a given failure case will have a corresponding SFT trace to provide guidance. Second, they lack the machinery to prevent **severe overfitting** when trained repeatedly over a minuscule SFT dataset across multiple epochs. The paper provides direct evidence of this failure mode: HPT-G and ReLIFT-G — baseline variants that extend RL training to the gold few-shot dataset — suffer abrupt performance collapse mid-training, dropping on both the SFT and RL training sets simultaneously.

The paper also notes that pure few-shot SFT (without RL on the answer-only data) and Self-Play Fine-Tuning (SPIN) fail entirely in the 128-shot regime. Both methods overfit rapidly from the earliest optimization steps, producing degenerate models that lose general reasoning capability. This establishes that the few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR problem is **not solvable by simply training harder on the few-shot data** — the overfitting problem is fundamental and requires architectural solutions.

---

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper introduces a **new problem setting: few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR**, defined by three concrete constraints that distinguish it from all prior work:

1. **No on-demand data:** Demonstrations cannot be generated on-demand for arbitrary failing problems. The expert traces are fixed, limited, and randomly sampled — they may not cover the specific questions the model fails on.

2. **Limited semantic coverage:** With only 128 demonstrations, the SFT dataset cannot cover the broad reasoning paradigms required for competition-level mathematics. Unlike specialized few-shot curation methods (e.g., LIMOv2 with its careful difficulty-diversity selection), FEST does not assume a curated pipeline; the demonstrations may be randomly selected and potentially redundant.

3. **Overfitting risk:** Training repeatedly over a 128-example dataset will cause the model to memorize surface patterns rather than learn generalizable reasoning skills, degrading performance on unseen problems.

The paper positions FEST as a **structural solution, not a data-engineering solution**. Rather than trying to select or augment the few-shot data to make it more representative — the strategy pursued by prior few-shot SFT methods like s1K and LIMO — FEST designs an optimization algorithm that can extract useful guidance from whatever few-shot data happens to be available, by **integrating three vital components**: a supervised learning signal from the SFT traces (to provide expert guidance), an on-policy learning signal from the model's own roll-outs evaluated against those traces (to mitigate exposure bias and expand the effective learning basis beyond the fixed SFT questions), and a decaying weight on the SFT objective (to prevent overfitting as RLVR training progresses).

The theoretical framing is notable: FEST casts the semi-online DPO loss — where SFT demonstrations serve as preferred responses and model-generated roll-outs as non-preferred — as fulfilling all three requirements simultaneously. The gradient of this loss decomposes naturally into a weighted SFT term (supervised learning) and a REINFORCE-like term with negative reward (on-policy learning), with the decaying weight emerging from the adaptive β scheduling. The paper further establishes a formal equivalence between this semi-online DPO paradigm and adversarial training under an Integral Probability Metric, providing theoretical grounding for why the framework should expand the model's reasoning capabilities beyond its base distribution — it is, in effect, training a discriminator to distinguish expert from model-generated reasoning and using that signal to push the model toward expert-like behavior.

By introducing the few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR setting and demonstrating that FEST can match the performance of full-dataset methods with only 128 random demonstrations, the paper argues for a **fundamental reassessment of the SFT data requirements** in unified post-training. The implication is that the community has been using far more expert data than necessary — not because the data isn't helpful, but because existing algorithms were not designed to extract its full value under sparsity constraints. FEST provides both the algorithmic template and the theoretical justification for doing more with less.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

FEST is a training algorithm that jointly optimizes a language model using two fundamentally different data sources: a tiny set of expert-written reasoning traces (as few as 128 examples) and a large set of question-answer pairs with verifiable rewards. It solves the problem of extracting substantial performance gains from minimal SFT data by structuring the loss on the few-shot data as a **semi-online DPO objective** whose gradient naturally decomposes into three essential components — supervised learning from the expert traces, on-policy learning where the model evaluates its own outputs against those traces, and an adaptive weight that automatically weakens the SFT signal as RL training progresses to prevent overfitting.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture

The system operates in a unified training loop with five major components:

- **Two datasets:** `$D_E$` (few-shot SFT dataset, 128 randomly selected expert reasoning traces) and `$D_I$` (answer-only RL dataset, the remaining 99.7% of the 46K-question corpus stripped of their reasoning traces, providing only ground-truth answers for reward computation).
- **A policy model `$\pi_\theta$`** (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) that generates chain-of-thought rollouts for all questions in both datasets.
- **A verifier** (math-verify library with exact string matching) that computes binary rewards `$r(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$` for rollouts against ground-truth answers in `$D_I$`.
- **A GRPO loss `$\mathcal{L}_I$`** applied to rollouts from `$D_I$`, providing token-level policy optimization using group-relative advantages.
- **A semi-online DPO loss `$\mathcal{L}_E$`** applied to `$D_E$`, where expert traces serve as preferred responses and the model's own rollouts (generated on-the-fly) serve as non-preferred responses, with adaptive `$\beta$` coefficients that modulate learning strength based on whether the model can already solve the question.

Information flows as follows: each training step samples a batch of 128 questions from each dataset → the current policy generates 8 rollouts per question from both `$D_E$` and `$D_I$` → the verifier computes binary rewards for all rollouts → GRPO advantages are computed for `$D_I$` rollouts and used to form `$\mathcal{L}_I$` → the model's own rollouts on `$D_E$` become negatives in a DPO framework where expert traces are positives, with `$\beta$` values assigned per negative based on a solvability heuristic → the two losses are combined as `$L = c \cdot L_E + L_I$` and backpropagated. Training proceeds for 600 steps with a cosine learning rate schedule from `$1\times 10^{-5}$` to `$5\times 10^{-6}$`.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the three challenges and three components** (Section 3.1 of the paper): what makes few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR uniquely hard, and what properties the loss on `$D_E$` must have to address them. This is the design rationale that motivates everything else.
- **Second, the core FEST objective** (Section 3.2): the GRPO loss on `$D_I$`, the semi-online DPO loss on `$D_E$`, the gradient decomposition that reveals how the three components are realized, and the adaptive `$\beta$` scheduling strategy.
- **Third, FEST-GRPO** (Section 3.3): the variant that addresses a structural gradient mismatch between sequence-level DPO and token-level GRPO by decomposing the DPO loss and substituting REINFORCE with GRPO for the on-policy component.
- **Fourth, theoretical grounding** (Appendix B.2): the equivalence between semi-online DPO and adversarial training under an Integral Probability Metric, explaining why the framework should expand the model's capabilities beyond its base distribution.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **training algorithm paper** whose core idea is that a few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR objective must simultaneously provide expert guidance (supervised learning), enable the model to critically evaluate its own outputs (on-policy learning), and progressively reduce dependence on the sparse SFT data to prevent overfitting (decaying weight). The semi-online DPO framework — where expert traces are fixed positives and model rollouts are on-the-fly negatives — satisfies all three requirements in a single, decomposable loss.

---

#### The Three Challenges and the Three Required Components

The paper explicitly frames the few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR problem as introducing three challenges absent from prior unified post-training work, and argues that these challenges collectively define the necessary properties of any successful loss function on `$D_E$`.

**Challenge 1: No on-demand data.** In prior work like HPT or ReLIFT, when the model fails on a question — producing zero correct rollouts in a batch — the algorithm can pull an SFT demonstration for that specific question from a large prepared dataset and train on it. With only 128 randomly selected demonstrations covering a tiny fraction of the total problem space, this is impossible. The probability that any particular failing question has a corresponding SFT trace in `$D_E$` is approximately `$128/46000 \approx 0.28\%$`. The loss on `$D_E$` must therefore provide useful guidance even when the SFT traces do not correspond to the specific questions the model is currently failing on — it must transfer general reasoning patterns rather than provide question-specific corrections.

**Challenge 2: Limited semantic coverage.** The paper notes that 128 examples cannot cover the broad range of reasoning paradigms required for competition-level mathematics. Unlike specialized few-shot curation methods like LIMOv2 — which explicitly selects examples for difficulty, diversity, and reasoning quality — FEST does not assume a curated pipeline. `$D_E$` may be a random sample that is redundant, unrepresentative, or missing entire categories of problems. The loss on `$D_E$` must therefore extract useful signal from whatever few-shot data is available, without relying on it being comprehensive or carefully selected.

**Challenge 3: Overfitting risk.** Training repeatedly over 128 examples across multiple epochs will cause the model to memorize surface patterns — specific phrasings, numerical values, problem structures — rather than learning generalizable reasoning skills. The loss on `$D_E$` must incorporate a mechanism that progressively weakens the SFT signal as training advances, preventing the model from over-optimizing on the sparse expert data at the expense of the broader RL objective.

From these challenges, the paper derives three **vital components** that any loss on `$D_E$` must satisfy:

- **Supervised learning:** The loss must include a term that pulls the model toward the expert demonstrations, providing the only source of external knowledge beyond binary reward signals. This directly addresses the limited semantic coverage challenge by extracting maximal information from whatever expert traces are available.

- **On-policy learning:** The loss must enable the model to evaluate its own rollouts against the SFT traces, expanding the effective learning basis beyond the fixed 128 questions. Since the model-generated negatives are sampled on-the-fly for each SFT question, the training signal adapts as the model's policy changes — this is the "on-policy" property. The paper argues this addresses both Challenge 1 (by creating a learning signal on `$D_E$` questions that doesn't require the model to fail on those specific questions — it's always comparing its current output to the expert trace) and Challenge 2 (by using the model's own diverse rollouts as contrastive negatives, effectively expanding the coverage of what the SFT signals teach beyond the traces themselves).

- **Decaying/adaptive weight:** The loss must incorporate a mechanism that controls the strength of the SFT signal over time, preventing overfitting. The paper argues this directly addresses Challenge 3. It supports this argument with empirical evidence from HPT full-dataset training (Appendix D.1), where the proportion of SFT trajectories naturally decays to approximately 2% in late training while test performance continues to improve — demonstrating that even in the large-data regime, SFT guidance should diminish as RLVR takes over.

**Why semi-online DPO satisfies all three.** The paper's key design insight is that a **semi-online DPO loss** — where SFT demonstrations are the preferred responses `$y^+$` and the model's own rollouts (generated in the current iteration) are the non-preferred responses `$y^-$` — provides all three components in a single framework. The gradient of this loss decomposes into a weighted SFT term (supervised learning) and a REINFORCE-like term with negative reward (on-policy learning), while the `$\beta$` coefficient can be scheduled adaptively to implement decaying weights. The paper explicitly notes that semi-online DPO is "an ideal framework to satisfy these requirements."

#### The Core FEST Objective

The training objective for FEST combines two losses applied to two separate datasets, optimized jointly over the policy parameters `$\theta$`:

$$L = c \cdot L_E + L_I$$

where `$c > 0$` is a constant balancing coefficient, `$L_E$` is the semi-online DPO loss applied to the few-shot dataset `$D_E$`, and `$L_I$` is the GRPO loss applied to the answer-only dataset `$D_I$`. The paper uses `$c = 0.01$` for FEST-DPO and `$c = 1$` for FEST-GRPO (due to the different gradient magnitudes — addressed in Section 3.3).

**GRPO loss on `$D_I$`.** For prompts sampled from `$D_I$`, FEST uses the standard Group Relative Policy Optimization objective with DAPO-style asymmetric clipping:

$$L_I = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D_I, y \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)} \left[ -\frac{1}{nM} \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^{|y_i|} \min\left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,j}|x, y_{i,<j})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,j}|x, y_{i,<j})} A_i, \text{clip}\left( \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,j}|x, y_{i,<j})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,j}|x, y_{i,<j})}, 1-\epsilon_1, 1+\epsilon_2 \right) A_i \right) \right]$$

where `$n = 8$` is the number of rollouts per prompt (a "group"), `$y_i$` is the `$i$`-th rollout with `$|y_i|$` tokens, `$M$` is an upper limit on rollout length (8192 tokens), `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` is the policy frozen at the start of the current sampling-training iteration, and `$\epsilon_1 = 0.2$`, `$\epsilon_2 = 0.3$` are the asymmetric clipping parameters (following DAPO, where `$\epsilon_2 > \epsilon_1$`, allowing more room for probability increases than decreases).

The **advantage** `$A_i$` for rollout `$i$` is computed as:

$$A_i = r(x, y_i) - \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n r(x, y_k)$$

where `$r(x, y_i) \in \{0, 1\}$` is the binary reward for rollout `$i$` (1 if the final answer matches the ground truth, 0 otherwise). This is a **group-relative advantage**: each rollout is scored relative to the average reward within its group of n siblings, all answering the same question. Critically, the paper follows HPT and Dr. GRPO in **omitting** both the standard deviation normalization in the advantage and the KL regularizer term, noting that this formulation "without token mean" is used.

**What GRPO computes:** For each token in each rollout, it compares the current policy's probability of that token against the old policy's probability (from before the current update), multiplies this ratio by the advantage (how much better or worse this rollout was than average), clips the ratio to prevent destructive updates, and averages over all tokens and rollouts. The objective encourages the policy to increase probability on tokens in above-average rollouts and decrease probability on tokens in below-average rollouts, with the clip preventing any single update from being too large. The `$n=8$` group size means each question gets eight attempts per step, and the relative advantage captures whether a particular rollout was better or worse than the model's typical performance on that question.

**Semi-online DPO loss on `$D_E$`.** For prompts sampled from `$D_E$`, FEST uses a DPO loss where the expert reasoning trace `$y^+$` (from the SFT dataset) is the preferred response and a model-generated rollout `$y^-$` (sampled from `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$`) is the non-preferred response:

$$L_E = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+) \sim D_E, \, y^- \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)} \left[ \log \sigma\left( \beta \cdot r^+ - \beta \cdot r^- \right) \right]$$

where:

$$r^+ = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^+|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+|x)}, \quad r^- = \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y^-|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^-|x)}$$

and `$\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$` is the sigmoid function, and `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is the reference policy (the model at initialization, frozen throughout training). The `$\beta > 0$` hyperparameter controls the strength of the preference signal — higher `$\beta$` means the model is penalized more heavily for deviating from expert-like behavior.

**What the semi-online DPO loss computes:** For each few-shot SFT example, the model generates one rollout `$y^-$` using its current policy (this is what makes it "semi-online" — only the negative is on-policy; the positive `$y^+$` is fixed from `$D_E$`). Both the expert trace and the model's rollout are scored using their **implicit rewards** `$r^+$` and `$r^-$` — the log-ratio of the current policy's probability versus the reference policy's probability for each sequence. The loss computes the log-sigmoid of the scaled difference between these implicit rewards, effectively asking: "Is the expert trace more probable under the current policy (relative to the reference) than the model's own generation?" Training minimizes the negative log-sigmoid, which pushes the implicit reward gap `$\beta(r^+ - r^-)$` to be large and positive — meaning the model should strongly prefer the expert trace over its own generation.

**Why this form matters — the gradient decomposition.** The paper's crucial insight lies in the gradient of `$L_E$` with respect to the policy parameters:

$$\nabla_\theta L_E = -\beta \, \mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+) \sim D_E, \, y^- \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \left[ \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+)) \cdot \left( \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y^+|x) - \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y^-|x) \right) \right]$$

where `$\sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+))$` is a positive scalar weight (the probability, under the current implicit reward model, that the model's `$y^-$` was actually preferred — which serves as the weighting for how much the model should learn from this particular contrastive pair).

**What this gradient does:** The term `$\nabla \log \pi_\theta(y^+|x)$` is the standard SFT gradient — it increases the probability of the expert trace tokens. The term `$-\nabla \log \pi_\theta(y^-|x)$` is a **negative REINFORCE-style gradient** — it decreases the probability of the model-generated tokens. The overall gradient is a weighted combination: when `$\beta(r^- - r^+)$` is large and positive (the model already strongly prefers its own output over the expert), the sigmoid weight `$\sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+))$` approaches 1, so the gradient applies strong SFT and strong negative RL — the model needs substantial correction. When the model already prefers the expert (`$r^- - r^+$` is large and negative), the sigmoid approaches 0, and very little gradient is applied — the model is already on track.

**The three components in the gradient.** The paper explicitly maps the three vital components to the gradient structure:

- **Supervised learning:** The `$+\nabla \log \pi_\theta(y^+|x)$` term directly increases the probability of expert tokens — this is the supervised signal providing external knowledge.
- **On-policy learning:** The `$-\nabla \log \pi_\theta(y^-|x)$` term decreases the probability of model-generated tokens — this is the on-policy component, equivalent to REINFORCE with a negative reward of `$-\beta \cdot \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+))$`. Because `$y^-$` is sampled from the current policy, this gradient adapts as the model changes — it is truly on-policy, unlike fixed offline DPO where both `$y^+$` and `$y^-$` are pre-computed.
- **Decaying weight:** The factor `$\beta \cdot \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+))$` serves as the adaptive weight. While the paper discusses decaying weights primarily in terms of the `$\beta$` scheduling (see below), the sigmoid factor also naturally decays: as the model improves and starts generating better `$y^-$` (closer to the expert trace), the implicit reward gap shrinks, `$\sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+))$` decreases, and the effective learning rate on `$D_E$` drops — the model naturally transitions from heavy reliance on SFT to lighter regularization.

---

#### Adaptive `$\beta$` Scheduling Based on Model Solvability

The paper identifies a limitation with the standard DPO framework: it applies uniform learning strength across all data in `$D_E$`, regardless of problem difficulty. For simple questions where the model can already produce correct answers, deviations from the SFT demonstration should be tolerated — the model shouldn't be forced to exactly mimic the expert trace when its own approach is valid. For questions the model cannot solve at all, the model should prioritize learning from the expert trace.

To implement this, FEST introduces an **adaptive `$\beta$` strategy based on model solvability**. For a question `$x$` with a group of `$n = 8$` generated rollouts `$\{y^-_1, \ldots, y^-_n\}$` and their corresponding binary rewards `$r \in \{0, 1\}$`, the `$\beta$` value for a specific negative rollout `$y^-_i$` is assigned as:

$$\beta(x, y^-_i) = \begin{cases} \beta_1, & \text{if } \forall j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}, r(x, y^-_j) = 0 \\ \beta_2, & \text{if } r(x, y^-_i) = 0 \text{ and } \exists j \in \{1, \ldots, n\}, r(x, y^-_j) = 1 \\ \beta_3, & \text{if } r(x, y^-_i) = 1 \end{cases}$$

where `$\beta_1$`, `$\beta_2$`, and `$\beta_3$` are constant hyperparameters.

**What this heuristic computes:** For each question in `$D_E$`, the model generates 8 rollouts. If **none** of the 8 rollouts are correct (`$\forall j, r(x, y^-_j) = 0$`), the question is effectively unsolvable by the current policy — every negative rollout `$y^-_i$` gets `$\beta_1$`. This should be the highest `$\beta$` value, providing the strongest expert guidance on hardest questions. If **some but not all** rollouts are correct, the question is borderline-solvable — incorrect rollouts get `$\beta_2$` (moderate guidance; the model can sometimes solve this, so it needs less correction) while correct rollouts get `$\beta_3$` (minimal guidance; the model already produces correct answers, so forcing it toward the expert trace could be counterproductive). For unsolvable questions, the model needs strong expert guidance (`$\beta_1$` high); for solvable questions with occasional mistakes, moderate correction (`$\beta_2$` intermediate); for correct rollouts, weak to zero correction (`$\beta_3$` low or zero).

The paper's chosen values are `$(\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3) = (0.1, 0.01, 0.01)$` for FEST-DPO and `$(0.005, 0.01, 0.05)$` for FEST-GRPO. The difference reflects the different gradient magnitude characteristics of the two variants (discussed below).

**Why this adaptive scheme is necessary.** In few-shot settings, every piece of data in `$D_E$` is precious, but not all learning signals from that data are equally useful. If the model already solves a question correctly, pushing it to exactly match the expert trace could cause it to unlearn valid solution strategies — the expert trace is **a** correct solution, not **the only** correct solution. Conversely, if the model consistently fails on a question, the expert trace is the only available guidance and should be weighted heavily. This adaptive `$\beta$` schedule implements a form of **automatic curriculum learning**: as training progresses and more questions transition from unsolvable to solvable, the effective SFT signal naturally weakens — this is the decaying weight in action.

**The `$\beta$` magnitude is unusually small compared to standard DPO.** The paper devotes Appendix D.3 to explaining why FEST requires `$\beta \in [0.001, 0.1]$` rather than the standard DPO range of `$[0.1, 0.2]$`. The reason is two-fold. First, chain-of-thought reasoning traces in FEST can extend up to 8192 tokens — far longer than typical RLHF preference pairs (which might be a few hundred tokens). This means the log-probability ratios `$r^+$` and `$r^-$` can have much larger magnitudes, and the difference `$r^+ - r^-$` grows proportionally. Second, repeated training over a tiny 128-example dataset further amplifies the log-ratio differences as the model memorizes the expert traces. The paper derives that the effective gradient weight is approximately `$\beta e^{-\beta\Delta}$` where `$\Delta = r^+ - r^-$`, and shows that for the magnitudes encountered in FEST training, this quantity actually **increases** as `$\beta$` decreases below a threshold of approximately `$z \approx 2.56$` (where `$z = \beta\Delta$` is the implicit advantage). Counterintuitively, smaller `$\beta$` values provide **stronger** and **more persistent** learning signals in this regime.

The paper also distinguishes two regimes of `$\beta$` behavior. With larger `$\beta$` (close to 0.1), the learning signal is "switch-like": very strong initially but rapidly dropping to near zero as the model begins to prefer the expert trace. With smaller `$\beta$` (close to 0.001), the signal is "constant": strong and steady throughout training because the sigmoid never saturates fully. The paper finds that values in the intermediate range `$[0.001, 0.1]$` work well, with degradation at both extremes.

#### FEST-GRPO: Addressing the Gradient Mismatch

The paper identifies a structural problem with FEST-DPO: the DPO loss `$L_E$` operates at the **sequence level** — the probabilities inside the log-sigmoid are joint probabilities over entire responses — while the GRPO loss `$L_I$` operates at the **token level** — applying clipped importance sampling independently to each token. This mismatch causes the gradient norms of `$L_E$` and `$L_I$` to differ by orders of magnitude (DPO gradients are typically 10–100× larger than GRPO gradients, as shown in Figure 4 of Appendix D.1). This necessitates "exhaustive tuning of the coefficient `$c$`" to balance the two contributions.

**The decomposition insight.** The paper observes that by examining the DPO gradient from a REINFORCE perspective, the on-policy component of the gradient:

$$\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+) \sim D_E, \, y^- \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \left[ \beta \cdot \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+)) \cdot \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y^-|x) \right]$$

is functionally equivalent to **REINFORCE with a negative reward** of `$-\beta \cdot \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+)) < 0$`. Similarly, the supervised component:

$$\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+) \sim D_E, \, y^- \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}} \left[ \beta \cdot \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+)) \cdot \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y^+|x) \right]$$

is equivalent to **weighted SFT** with a positive weight of `$\beta \cdot \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+)) > 0$`.

This yields the key conceptual reframing: **Semi-online DPO ≈ REINFORCE with negative reward + weighted SFT.**

**The FEST-GRPO construction.** FEST-GRPO exploits this equivalence by replacing the REINFORCE-like component with a full GRPO loss applied to `$D_E$`. Specifically, FEST-GRPO retains the original `$L_I$` (GRPO on `$D_I$`) unchanged, while replacing the DPO-based `$L_E$` with a hybrid:

- **Weighted SFT on the expert traces:** a standard supervised fine-tuning loss on `$(x, y^+) \sim D_E$` where each expert trace is weighted by `$\beta \cdot \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+))$` (computed using the model's current rollouts `$y^-$` as the negative).
- **GRPO on `$D_E$` with negative advantages:** the model's generated rollouts `$y^-$` on `$D_E$` questions are treated as GRPO rollouts but with **negative advantages** `$A_i < 0$` for all rollouts (regardless of their actual rewards), pushing the model away from its own generated outputs on these few-shot questions.

**Why this resolves the gradient mismatch.** Both the SFT term and the GRPO term in FEST-GRPO operate at the token level: SFT is intrinsically token-level (maximizing `$\log \pi_\theta(y^+_{i,j}|x, y^+_{i,<j})$` for each token), and GRPO is token-level with per-token clipping. This unifies the gradient scales with `$L_I$`, eliminating the magnitude mismatch. The paper sets `$c = 1$` for FEST-GRPO (versus `$c = 0.01$` for FEST-DPO), consistent with this interpretation — the coefficients no longer need to compensate for drastically different gradient norms.

**Why negative reinforcement learning is theoretically justified.** The paper cites Zhu et al. (2025), who demonstrated that RL with purely negative rewards redistributes probability mass toward other feasible solutions, thereby **preventing overfitting** and facilitating more robust exploration. In the FEST context, applying negative GRPO on `$D_E$` and positive GRPO on `$D_I$` creates a push-pull dynamic: the model is pushed away from its own suboptimal outputs on the few-shot questions (where it has expert reference) while being pulled toward high-reward outputs on the abundant answer-only questions. The negative advantage on `$D_E$` does not specify what the model **should** produce — only what it should **not** produce — which preserves exploration diversity on those questions while still providing a useful learning signal.

**Remark 3.4 in the paper** reinforces this: "The efficacy of RL with purely negative rewards is supported by prior theoretical work. Zhu et al. demonstrated that negative RL redistributes probability mass toward other feasible solutions, thereby preventing overfitting and facilitating more robust exploration." This contrasts with approaches like HPT-G or ReLIFT-G that naively apply standard (positive-advantage) RL on `$D_E$` — those methods can overfit rapidly because positive RL on a tiny dataset reinforces existing patterns rather than encouraging exploration of alternatives.

#### Theoretical Grounding: Adversarial Training Under an Integral Probability Metric

The paper provides theoretical justification for the semi-online DPO framework in Appendix B.2, casting it as an adversarial training process that minimizes the discrepancy between the model's policy and the expert policy from `$D_E$`.

**The adversarial formulation.** The training process is framed as a minimax game between a **generator** (the policy `$\pi$`) and a **discriminator** (a reward function `$f$`):

$$\max_f \min_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D_E} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_E(\cdot|x)}[f(x, y)] - \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[f(x, y')] \right]$$

where `$\pi_E$` is the expert behavioral policy (approximated by the empirical distribution of `$D_E$`). This objective measures the **Integral Probability Metric (IPM)** between the expert and agent distributions — the discriminator tries to find the function `$f$` that maximally separates the two distributions, while the generator tries to minimize that separation by producing outputs indistinguishable from expert traces.

**Introducing the logistic loss.** The linear objective is unbounded for unconstrained `$f$`, causing training instability. The paper replaces it with a **log-sigmoid loss** (equivalent to the "logistic loss" in SPIN), following the principle of Wasserstein inverse RL:

$$\max_f \min_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D_E} \left[ g\left( \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_E}[f(x, y)] - \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \pi}[f(x, y')] \right) \right]$$

where `$g(x) = -\log(1 + e^{-x})$` is the log-sigmoid function. This provides a smooth, bounded surrogate that penalizes the discriminator when the expert-agent gap is large but saturates gracefully.

**The inner optimization: KL-regularized policy improvement.** To make the inner minimization over `$\pi$` tractable, the paper adds a KL-regularization term anchored at the reference policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`:

$$\min_\pi \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D_E} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[f(x, y')] - \beta \, \text{KL}(\pi \parallel \pi_{\text{ref}}) \right]$$

The analytical solution for the optimal policy under this objective is the Gibbs distribution:

$$\pi(y'|x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x) \exp\left( \frac{f(x, y')}{\beta} \right)$$

which can be rearranged to solve for the discriminator: `$f(x, y') = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y'|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'|x)} + c(x)$`. Substituting this back into the minimax objective yields the final form, which after applying Jensen's inequality (exploiting the concavity of the log-sigmoid) becomes exactly the semi-online DPO loss:

$$\min_\pi -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+) \sim D_E, \, y^- \sim \pi(\cdot|x)} \log \sigma\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi(y^+|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y^-|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^-|x)} \right)$$

**What this theoretical grounding explains.** By showing that semi-online DPO is equivalent to adversarial IPM minimization, the paper provides a theoretical reason why the framework should **expand the model's capabilities beyond its base distribution**. The discriminator is actively trained (implicitly, through the policy gradient) to distinguish expert traces from model-generated traces, and the policy is trained to fool this discriminator. Over multiple training steps, this adversarial dynamic pushes the policy toward the expert distribution — not just at the level of individual tokens, but at the level of the **distribution** of reasoning patterns. This is fundamentally different from pure SFT (which simply imitates specific traces) or pure RLVR (which sharpens the existing distribution but doesn't push it toward new modes). The semi-online DPO framework combines both: the SFT term provides mode-seeking toward known correct solutions, while the adversarial dynamic provides distribution-matching toward expert-like reasoning patterns.

**Connection to HPT's unified framework.** In Appendix B.3, the paper extends HPT's unified post-training framework — which originally synthesized gradients of various algorithms into a generalized policy gradient estimator of the form `$\nabla \pi_\theta \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\text{stable}} \cdot \frac{1}{\pi_0} \cdot A$` — to include DPO. By decomposing DPO's gradient into the weighted SFT + negative RL form established in Section 3.3, the paper shows that DPO fits into the same template with `$\pi_0 = \pi_\theta$` (for the SFT term) and `$\pi_0 = \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` (for the negative RL term), with advantage `$A = \pm \beta \cdot \sigma(\beta(r^- - r^+))$`. FEST-GRPO extends this further by replacing the REINFORCE advantage with token-level clipped GRPO advantages. This places FEST in a broader taxonomy of post-training algorithms and demonstrates that the few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR setting requires a specific combination of components — supervised signal, on-policy signal, and adaptive weighting — that can be realized within this unified framework through careful choice of `$\pi_0$`, `$A$`, and stability masks.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Semi-online DPO over fully offline DPO:** only the negative rollouts are generated online while positives are fixed from `$D_E$`. This provides the on-policy learning component (the model's own outputs serve as negatives, adapting as the model improves) while keeping the expert signal stable and interpretable. Fully offline DPO (both positives and negatives pre-computed) would lack the on-policy adaptation; fully online DPO (both positives and negatives generated online) would require on-demand expert traces, which is precisely what's unavailable in the few-shot setting.

- **Adaptive `$\beta$` based on solvability over uniform `$\beta$`:** prevents over-correction on questions the model already gets right (where the expert trace may represent only one of many valid approaches) while providing strong correction on consistently failed questions. This implements a form of automatic curriculum — the effective SFT signal weakens as the model improves, transitioning from heavy expert guidance to lighter regularization.

- **FEST-GRPO over FEST-DPO:** resolves the token-level vs. sequence-level gradient magnitude mismatch, unifying both losses at the token level and eliminating the need for exhaustive `$c$` tuning. The negative GRPO on `$D_E$` also has better theoretical properties (redistribution of probability mass rather than collapse to a single mode, as established by Zhu et al.).

- **Very small `$\beta$` (0.001–0.1) over standard DPO range (0.1–0.2):** necessary due to the extreme length of chain-of-thought reasoning traces (up to 8192 tokens) and repeated training over tiny datasets, which cause log-ratio differences orders of magnitude larger than typical RLHF sequences. In this regime, decreasing `$\beta$` counterintuitively **increases** the effective learning signal strength.

- **Asymmetric clipping (`$\epsilon_1 = 0.2$, `$\epsilon_2 = 0.3$`) following DAPO:** allows more room for probability increases (helpful for exploration) than decreases (helpful for stability), consistent with the observation in RLVR that policies benefit from asymmetric update constraints.

- **Omission of KL regularizer and advantage standard deviation:** following best practices from HPT and Dr. GRPO, simplifying the loss without degrading performance in practice. The reference policy in the DPO loss (`$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`) provides implicit regularization.

- **Random 128-shot selection over curated few-shot selection:** designed to show that FEST works with uncurated, potentially non-representative data, removing the data-engineering burden assumed by prior few-shot methods like LIMO and s1K. This is a deliberate design choice to stress-test the algorithm's robustness.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Framing Few-Shot Demonstration-Guided RLVR as a Distinct Problem with Its Own Structural Requirements

The paper’s most important conceptual contribution is not the FEST algorithm itself, but the act of **defining a new problem setting** and deriving the necessary conditions that any solution must satisfy. Prior work on demonstration-guided RLVR — HPT, ReLIFT, LUFFY, MIFO, CHORD — operated under an implicit assumption that SFT data could be treated as an abundant resource to be deployed strategically: pull demonstrations when RL fails, blend them with RL gradients at fixed ratios, or weight them dynamically based on reward signals. The common thread across all these methods is the assumption that, when the model needs expert guidance on a particular problem, a corresponding expert trace is available somewhere in a prepared dataset.

FEST breaks this assumption explicitly and names the consequences. The paper identifies three constraints that collectively define what it means to operate in a **few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR regime**: (1) no on-demand data — you cannot pull an SFT trace for the specific question the model is failing on because your 128 examples almost certainly don’t include it; (2) limited semantic coverage — 128 randomly selected traces cannot span the full diversity of reasoning paradigms needed for competition-level mathematics; and (3) overfitting risk — training repeatedly over 128 examples will cause memorization rather than generalization unless explicitly counteracted.

This framing matters because it **explains why existing methods break catastrophically** when pushed to the few-shot limit. The paper provides direct evidence: HPT-G and ReLIFT-G — variants that naively extend RL training to the few-shot gold dataset — suffer abrupt performance collapse mid-training (Figure 5 in Appendix D.2), with accuracy plummeting on both the SFT and RL training sets simultaneously. LUFFY and CHORD-ϕ, designed for large-data regimes, stagnate and overfit rather than continuing to improve (Figures 8 and 9 in Appendix D.4). Pure few-shot SFT and SPIN fail within the first few hundred steps, producing degenerate models that lose general reasoning capability (Figure 11 in Appendix D.5). The paper’s diagnostic contribution is showing that these are not hyperparameter-tuning failures — they are **structural failures** arising from the mismatch between what existing algorithms assume about data availability and what the few-shot regime actually provides.

By deriving three necessary components — supervised learning, on-policy learning, and decaying weights — directly from the three identified challenges, the paper provides a **design template** for future work in this regime. This is conceptually distinct from simply proposing a new algorithm: it establishes a set of criteria against which any few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR method can be evaluated, and shows that methods lacking any one component will either fail to improve over pure RL or collapse entirely (Table 5). This is a **fundamental reframing** of the problem, not an incremental improvement to existing blending techniques.

---

### Innovation 2: Semi-Online DPO as a Unified Mechanism Satisfying Three Orthogonal Requirements

The paper’s central design insight is that a **semi-online DPO loss** — expert traces as fixed positives, model-generated rollouts as on-the-fly negatives — simultaneously satisfies all three structural requirements derived from the few-shot problem definition. This is elegant not because DPO is novel, but because **the mapping from requirements to mechanism is exact and non-obvious**.

Prior work had used DPO in post-training pipelines (SPIN for self-play, various online DPO variants for iterative preference learning), but always with large SFT datasets and always as a preference-learning tool — the goal was to align the model with human preferences expressed through pairwise comparisons. FEST repurposes DPO for a fundamentally different function: not learning preferences, but **providing structured regularization** in a data-sparse regime. The expert traces are not “preferred” because a human judged them better — they are treated as preferred because they represent correct reasoning, which is definitionally better than anything the model generates on its own for that question. The negative rollouts are on-policy not because the model needs to learn what humans dislike, but because on-policy sampling is what creates the three required components.

This repurposing is made precise through the gradient decomposition. The paper shows that the DPO gradient naturally splits into a weighted SFT term (supervised learning) and a REINFORCE term with negative reward (on-policy learning), with the sigmoid weight providing automatic adaptive scaling (the decaying weight). No component had to be engineered separately; they all emerge from a single loss function chosen for its structural properties.

The comparison to prior work sharpens what makes this distinctive. SPIN used semi-online DPO (identical structure: fixed positive, on-the-fly negative) for self-play fine-tuning with large SFT datasets, but it was designed for RLHF-style alignment, not RLVR with verifiable rewards, and it was never tested in few-shot regimes. The paper identifies several critical differences: SPIN’s iterative nature is incompatible with the need for truly online negative sampling in few-shot settings; SPIN’s uniform β was designed for standard-length preference pairs, not long chain-of-thought traces; and SPIN lacks the adaptive β strategy that FEST requires to handle varying question difficulty. FEST is not “SPIN applied to RLVR” — it is a **re-derivation of the semi-online DPO paradigm for a fundamentally different purpose**, with different theoretical grounding (adversarial training under IPM rather than self-play improvement) and different practical requirements (few-shot data, long reasoning traces, adaptive β scheduling).

This contribution is **fundamental architecture-level** rather than incremental: it identifies that a single mechanism can satisfy three orthogonal requirements simultaneously, and validates through ablation (Table 5) that removing any one component causes performance to collapse — supervised-only (RL + few-shot SFT with decaying weight) achieves only 39.91%, barely above vanilla RL’s 39.79%, while on-policy-only with our weight (RL-G with the paper’s weight schedule but no SFT guidance) collapses to 28.83%. The synergy is real and measurable.

### Innovation 3: The DPO-to-REINFORCE Decomposition as a Principled Solution to Gradient Mismatch in Hybrid Objectives

The paper identifies and solves a practical engineering problem — gradient magnitude mismatch between sequence-level DPO and token-level GRPO — through a **conceptual decomposition that has implications beyond the immediate use case**. The observation that FEST-DPO requires “exhaustive tuning of the coefficient c” (as stated in Section 3.3) points to a deeper issue: any training pipeline that mixes DPO with token-level RL algorithms will face this same challenge, and the standard solution (tuning a scalar multiplier) is brittle and problem-specific.

The innovation lies in recognizing that the DPO gradient **is not an atomic unit** — it decomposes into components that have natural token-level analogs. The term `∇ log π_θ(y⁻|x)` in the DPO gradient is structurally identical to a REINFORCE gradient with negative reward; the term `∇ log π_θ(y⁺|x)` is structurally identical to a weighted SFT gradient. This isn’t just an algebraic identity — it’s a **functional equivalence** that enables substitution.

By replacing the REINFORCE component with GRPO (which is itself a token-level variant of REINFORCE with clipping), FEST-GRPO achieves gradient-scale unification without any loss of expressiveness. The coefficient c changes from 0.01 (FEST-DPO, compensating for DPO’s larger gradients) to 1.0 (FEST-GRPO, no compensation needed), confirming that the mismatch has been resolved at the structural level rather than patched with hyperparameter tuning.

This contribution connects to a broader thread in the preference optimization literature. Several works (TOPR, 2-GRPO, and various DPO decomposition papers) have noted that DPO can be viewed as combining SFT and RL components. But FEST is, to the paper’s knowledge, the first to **operationalize this equivalence as a solution to a training instability problem** — specifically, using it to replace a component of DPO with a token-level alternative (GRPO) to match the scale of a separate objective. The paper extends HPT’s unified post-training framework in Appendix B.3 to explicitly incorporate this decomposition, showing that DPO and its GRPO-substituted variant both fit into the same generalized policy gradient template with different choices of `π₀` and advantage `A`.

The significance goes beyond FEST itself. Any future method that combines DPO-style preference learning with token-level RLVR will encounter this gradient mismatch. FEST-GRPO demonstrates a **principled solution template**: decompose the DPO loss, substitute token-level equivalents for sequence-level components, and unify the gradient scales without tuning. This is a **methodological contribution** — a design pattern rather than a one-off fix — that generalizes to other hybrid post-training pipelines.

The practical impact is clear: FEST-GRPO outperforms FEST-DPO on the main benchmarks (42.36% vs. 41.98% average accuracy in Table 2), and shows superior stability in the extreme few-shot case (64 shots in Figure 2). The theoretical justification — negative RL redistributing probability mass rather than collapsing to a single mode — provides the conceptual underpinning for why the substitution should work.

### Innovation 4: The Counterintuitive β Scaling Law for Long-Trace Few-Shot DPO

Appendix D.3 of the paper contains a finding that challenges conventional wisdom about DPO hyperparameters and, if validated more broadly, could change how practitioners configure DPO in long-reasoning settings. The standard guidance for DPO — originating from the original paper and reinforced by subsequent practice — is to use `β` in the range [0.1, 0.2]. The paper shows that in the few-shot long-trace regime, values in [0.001, 0.1] are not just acceptable but **necessary**, and that decreasing β can counterintuitively **strengthen** the learning signal rather than weaken it.

The explanation reveals a subtle interaction between three factors: chain-of-thought length, repeated training on tiny datasets, and the functional form of the DPO gradient. The effective learning signal strength is proportional to `β · σ(β(r⁻ - r⁺))`, where the log-ratio difference `Δ = r⁻ - r⁺` grows with sequence length and training repetition. For large `Δ` (the regime FEST operates in), the sigmoid `σ(βΔ)` saturates to 1, and the gradient weight is approximately `β e^{-βΔ}`. This function is **non-monotonic in β**: for fixed Δ, there exists an optimal β that balances the linear β term against the exponential suppression `e^{-βΔ}`. Decreasing β below this optimum (but not too far) actually increases the gradient weight by reducing the exponential damping.

The paper visualizes this through the “implicit advantage” z = βΔ (Figure 6). For β in [0.001, 0.1], z scales approximately linearly with β, confirming that Δ is nearly constant across this range. The minimum z (representing the strongest gradient signal) varies dramatically — larger β produces wider distributions where a few examples receive extremely strong “switch-like” signals while most receive near-zero signals; smaller β produces concentrated distributions where the signal is moderate but persistent across all examples. This is not a parameter tuning finding — it’s a **qualitative insight about the learning dynamics** of DPO under extreme length and sparsity conditions.

The implications are significant for the broader reasoning community. As chain-of-thought traces grow longer (DeepSeek-R1 uses traces exceeding 10,000 tokens; OpenAI o1’s internal traces are presumably even longer), and as few-shot post-training becomes more attractive (driven by the cost and scarcity of expert reasoning data), the standard DPO hyperparameter guidance will systematically fail. Practitioners who apply the conventional β ∈ [0.1, 0.2] to long-trace few-shot DPO will experience “switch-like” learning where the signal is intense for a few steps and then vanishes, followed by overfitting. The paper’s analysis provides both the diagnostic (monitor the implicit advantage z distribution and check for saturation) and the solution (reduce β until z falls in a moderate range, approximately [1, 10]).

This is an **empirical-theoretical contribution** — a scaling law for a specific hyperparameter regime — that could prevent systematic failures in future work. Table 9 confirms the sensitivity: using the conventional β = 0.1 uniformly drops performance to 40.22% from the optimal 41.99%, while β = 0.001 uniformly drops to 40.24%. The sweet spot is narrow, and the conventional default is outside it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary training source is the OpenR1-Math-46K-8192 dataset (46,000 mathematical reasoning problems with long chain-of-thought solutions up to 8,192 tokens). From this, 128 problems are randomly sampled with their expert reasoning traces to form the few-shot SFT dataset `$D_E$`; the remaining problems are stripped of their reasoning traces and used as the answer-only RL dataset `$D_I$` (providing only ground-truth answers for binary reward computation). For cross-dataset robustness evaluation, an alternative few-shot dataset LIMOv2-8192 (subset of 257 examples from LIMOv2 with CoT traces under 8,192 tokens) is also tested. Evaluation uses six mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AIME25 (30 questions), AMC23 (40 questions), AIME24 (30 questions), MATH-500 (500 questions), OlympiadBench (674 questions), and Minerva (272 questions). A separate out-of-distribution evaluation uses MMLU-Pro (over 12,000 problems across diverse domains).

- **Base model.** All experiments use Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, a 1.5-billion-parameter model pre-trained and fine-tuned specifically for mathematical reasoning. The authors argue this model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and sits in a useful regime where RLVR can produce meaningful improvements without saturating benchmark performance. For the few-shot setting, this relatively small model also makes the computational requirements tractable — training for 600 steps requires 4-5 days on two NVIDIA GH200 GPUs.

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation metric is **Avg@8** — the mean accuracy across 8 independent rollouts per question, reported with standard deviation. This measures the model's expected performance given 8 attempts per problem, which is directly relevant to RLVR settings where multiple samples are generated and the best is selected. **Pass@8** — the percentage of questions where at least one of the 8 rollouts is correct — is also reported as a measure of the model's exploration potential and upper-bound performance ceiling. For training monitoring, **Reward (Avg@8)** on the training sets (`$D_E$` and `$D_I$`) and **Pass@1** on the test set are tracked. All answers are verified using the math-verify library (v0.8) with exact string matching against ground-truth answers. The paper notes using a modified reward verifier implementation to avoid "occasional deadlock issues" with the standard math-verify in distributed training.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a comprehensive set of demonstration-guided RLVR methods:
  - **Vanilla RL (pure GRPO):** Standard Group Relative Policy Optimization on `$D_I$` only, with no SFT data. Serves as the lower-bound baseline demonstrating what RLVR alone achieves.
  - **RL-G:** GRPO applied to both `$D_I$` and `$D_E$` (the few-shot gold dataset), treating `$D_E$` questions identically to `$D_I$` questions during RL training.
  - **HPT** (Lv et al., 2025): Selective training that applies SFT when average rollout reward falls below a threshold (0 for Qwen models) and RL otherwise. Implemented with learning rate 5 × 10⁻⁶. In the few-shot setting, the paper found that restricting HPT to SFT-only on `$D_E$` performed better than combining with RL.
  - **HPT-G:** HPT variant that additionally applies RL training on the few-shot gold dataset `$D_E$`.
  - **ReLIFT** (Ma et al., 2026): Identifies zero-reward samples, stores them in a buffer, and executes standalone SFT steps when the buffer reaches threshold size. Implemented with learning rate 5 × 10⁻⁶.
  - **ReLIFT-G:** ReLIFT variant with RL also applied to `$D_E$`.
  - **LUFFY** (Yan et al., 2025): Interleaves SFT and RL gradients every update by treating SFT demonstrations as rollouts within a regularized importance sampling framework. Uses 8 online rollouts and 1 offline SFT sample per update.
  - **CHORD-ϕ** (Zhang et al., 2026): Conducts SFT on `$D_E$` and RL on `$D_I$` simultaneously with per-token dynamic weighting `$\phi(x) = p_x(1-p_x)$` where `$p_x$` is the probability of the sampled token. The `$\mu$` variant (uniform decaying weight) was excluded as `$\phi$` outperforms it in the original paper.
  - **SRFT** (Fu et al., 2026): Mixes RL and SFT loss from both datasets for every gradient step with entropy-based weights. Evaluated using the official checkpoint since the code was not available and the HPT codebase implementation caused training collapse.
  - **MIFO** (Yuan et al., 2025): Buffer-based SFT similar to ReLIFT but selectively updates parameters with high sensitivity during RL. Results taken directly from the original paper (no public code or checkpoint).
  
  Several methods were excluded for specific reasons: SuperRL and DyME are functionally identical to HPT in this context; SASR focuses on simpler tasks and lacks an accessible codebase; pure SFT and SPIN were tested but failed to achieve competitive results, exhibiting severe overfitting from early training steps.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The training budget is standardized across all methods: 600 training steps with a global batch size of 128 questions from each of `$D_E$` and `$D_I$`, and 8 rollouts generated per question (n = 8). This means each step processes 128 × 8 = 1,024 rollouts from `$D_E$` and 1,024 rollouts from `$D_I$`, for 2,048 total rollouts per step. The minibatch size is 512 rollouts. Generation uses temperature 1.0 with a maximum sequence length of 8,192 tokens. All methods use the AdamW optimizer with a cosine learning rate schedule decaying from 1 × 10⁻⁵ to 5 × 10⁻⁶ over the 600 steps. The paper uses GRPO with asymmetric clipping (`$\epsilon_1 = 0.2$`, `$\epsilon_2 = 0.3$` following DAPO), omits the KL regularizer and standard deviation normalization in the advantage, and applies entropy coefficient 0.0001. This standardized budget enables direct comparison across methods — any performance difference is attributable to algorithmic design, not compute allocation.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** To ensure statistical stability on smaller benchmarks (AIME25 with only 30 questions), the paper reports mean and standard deviation across 8 rollouts per question (Avg@8). For the main results in Table 2, training is conducted once per method with fixed random seeds; the standard deviations reflect variance across the 8 evaluation rollouts, not across multiple training runs. The paper reports results at 600 steps following the ReLIFT protocol. For the few-shot data selection, three different random 128-shot splits from OpenR1 and one LIMOv2 split are tested to assess robustness to dataset composition (Section 4.3). The out-of-distribution evaluation on MMLU-Pro (Section 4.4.2) uses Pass@1 with zero-shot prompting, testing generalization without any task-specific fine-tuning. No cross-validation over multiple training seeds or multiple difficulty-based train/test splits is reported — the analysis relies on the breadth of benchmarks and multiple DE configurations for robustness evidence.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### FEST Outperforms All Baselines in the 128-Shot Regime

Table 2 presents the central result of the paper: average test set accuracy (Avg@8) across six benchmarks for all methods under the 128-shot constraint. FEST-GRPO achieves the highest average accuracy of **42.36 ± 1.26%**, followed by FEST-DPO at **41.98 ± 1.24%**. These represent improvements of **+2.57 and +2.19 percentage points** over the vanilla RL baseline (39.79 ± 1.04%), and notably **outperform methods that were designed for and tested with the full 46K SFT dataset**, such as SRFT (35.05 ± 1.04%) and MIFO (which reports only partial results: 12.0 on AIME25, 19.2 on AIME24, 78.8 on MATH-500, 43.3 on Olympiad — all below FEST on those benchmarks).

The comparison with the most competitive baselines reveals:
- **vs. ReLIFT** (40.51 ± 1.03%): FEST-GRPO gains +1.85 points. ReLIFT was the strongest large-data baseline at the full 46K scale, and FEST surpasses it using 0.28% of the SFT data.
- **vs. RL-G** (40.55 ± 1.03%): FEST-GRPO gains +1.81 points. RL-G achieves competitive nominal accuracy but suffers severe overfitting on `$D_E$`, reflected in its substantially lower Pass@8 (54.84% vs. 61.06% for FEST-GRPO in Table 3).
- **vs. HPT and LUFFY** (38.75% and 37.90% respectively): FEST gains exceed +3.5 points, demonstrating that fixed-ratio blending and switching strategies designed for large data break down in the few-shot regime.

Two critical observations emerge from Table 2:

**First, methods that naively combine SFT and RL on the few-shot data collapse.** Both HPT-G (32.02 ± 0.81%) and ReLIFT-G (38.19 ± 0.77%) perform **significantly worse** than their SFT-only-on-DE counterparts, dropping by 6.73 and 2.32 points respectively below HPT and ReLIFT. This is not a minor degradation — HPT-G's 32.02% is 7.77 points below vanilla RL, meaning that adding SFT to RL on the few-shot data **actively harms** performance. The paper traces this to "abrupt performance drops on the training set mid-process" (Section 4.1, observation i), attributed to rapid distribution shifts when SFT is applied to a dataset already heavily overfitted by RL.

**Second, the pure RL baseline is stronger than prior work suggested.** With the learning rate optimized (increased from the standard 1 × 10⁻⁶ to 5 × 10⁻⁶), vanilla RL achieves 39.79%, which the paper notes is performance parity with ReLIFT on the full dataset. This raises the bar for few-shot methods: they must not only improve over a weak RL baseline but over a well-tuned one. FEST clears this higher bar, while LUFFY (37.90%) and CHORD-ϕ (37.56%) fall below it, suggesting their mechanisms are actually interfering with RL training when SFT data is sparse.

The per-benchmark breakdown (Table 2) shows FEST's gains are consistent across difficulty levels. On the hardest benchmark, AIME25, FEST-GRPO achieves 14.58 ± 3.70 versus RL's 11.67 ± 4.41 (+2.91 points). On the largest benchmark, MATH-500, FEST-GRPO reaches 81.1 ± 1.27 versus RL's 77.78 ± 1.24 (+3.32 points). On OlympiadBench (674 questions), the gain is 47.00 ± 0.95 versus 43.25 ± 1.27 (+3.75 points). The consistency across benchmarks of varying difficulty and size argues against the gains being statistical artifacts of small test sets.

#### Pass@8 Results Reveal Divergent Exploration-Exploitation Tradeoffs

Table 3 reports Pass@8 — the percentage of questions where at least one of the 8 generated rollouts is correct — which measures the model's **exploration diversity and upper-bound potential**. This metric reveals a critical distinction that Avg@8 alone obscures.

FEST-GRPO achieves the highest average Pass@8 of **61.06%**, substantially outperforming RL-G (54.84%), LUFFY (55.88%), HPT (58.10%), and ReLIFT (56.72%). The gap between FEST-GRPO and RL-G is particularly revealing: RL-G achieves competitive Avg@8 (40.55% vs. 42.36%) but much lower Pass@8 (54.84% vs. 61.06%). This means RL-G is **exploiting a narrower set of successful strategies** — when it gets a question right, it does so more consistently across rollouts, but it fails to even attempt correct solutions on a larger fraction of questions. In contrast, FEST maintains high exploration diversity, producing at least one correct solution on more questions, which creates headroom for further improvement through additional sampling or RL training. The paper explicitly notes this: "RL-G may achieve high nominal accuracy, [but] it suffers from severe overfitting on the limited `$D_E$` dataset, resulting in a significantly lower Pass@8... a pitfall FEST avoids by maintaining high exploration capability."

The baseline with the highest Pass@8 before FEST is vanilla RL at 59.67% — nearly matching FEST-DPO (60.08%). This suggests that pure RL is actually quite good at exploration (it samples diversely from the base distribution), but poor at exploitation (it cannot reliably select the correct answer among its diverse samples). FEST adds exploitation capability (via the SFT signal and on-policy DPO loss) without sacrificing exploration — hence higher Avg@8 with comparable or better Pass@8. RL-G, HPT-G, and ReLIFT-G all sacrifice exploration for exploitation, resulting in higher in-distribution accuracy at the cost of collapsed diversity and lower ceiling performance.

On the hardest individual benchmark, AIME25, FEST-GRPO achieves 36.67% Pass@8 versus RL's 36.67% (tied) and RL-G's 33.33%. On AIME24, FEST-DPO reaches 40% Pass@8 versus RL's 33.33% and RL-G's 23.33% — a dramatic +16.67 point gap that underscores how severely RL-G's exploration collapses on challenging problems.

#### FEST Scales Favorably with Shot Count and Approaches Full-Dataset Performance

Figure 2 tests FEST with 64, 128, 256, and 512 randomly selected demonstrations, plotting Avg@8 against the number of shots. The results reveal differentiated scaling behavior between FEST variants:

- **FEST-GRPO** shows greater robustness at the extreme low-data point: at 64 shots, it outperforms FEST-DPO and all baselines, maintaining a clear margin over RL. This aligns with the theoretical argument that GRPO's token-level clipping and negative advantage redistribution is more stable when SFT data is extremely sparse.
- **FEST-DPO** exhibits stronger scaling: as shots increase from 128 to 512, FEST-DPO's accuracy rises more steeply than FEST-GRPO's, eventually approaching performance comparable to HPT trained on the **full 46K SFT dataset** (represented by the dashed horizontal lines in Figure 2). This suggests that the sequence-level DPO framework excels at extracting value from additional data when available, while the GRPO variant's advantages are most pronounced in the sparsest regimes.
- **Baseline methods show poor scaling:** HPT and ReLIFT improve only marginally with additional shots in the 64–512 range, suggesting their switching mechanisms cannot effectively utilize small increases in SFT data — they need orders of magnitude more to trigger their SFT components meaningfully.

The convergence of FEST-DPO at 512 shots to full-dataset HPT performance is perhaps the paper's most striking result: it implies that with careful algorithmic design, **0.3% of the SFT data can match the performance of using 100% of it** under a less sophisticated algorithm. In other words, the SFT data requirement is not an intrinsic property of the problem but an artifact of suboptimal training methodology.

#### Performance Gains Are Consistent Across Different Few-Shot Dataset Selections

Table 4 tests robustness across four different configurations of `$D_E$`: three different random 128-shot splits from OpenR1 (the original dataset 1 from Section 4.1, plus datasets 2 and 3), and LIMOv2-8192 (a 257-example subset of a carefully curated few-shot dataset). The results demonstrate that FEST's gains are not an artifact of a lucky random seed.

For FEST-DPO, average accuracy across the three OpenR1 splits is 41.98%, 41.28%, and 41.74% — all within 0.7 points of each other and all substantially above RL (39.79%). On LIMOv2-8192, FEST-DPO achieves 41.21%, demonstrating that the method transfers to a qualitatively different few-shot dataset (LIMOv2 is explicitly curated for difficulty and diversity, unlike the random OpenR1 splits). For FEST-GRPO, the pattern is similar: 42.38%, 41.33%, 41.18% across the three OpenR1 splits, and 41.63% on LIMOv2.

The paper notes that LIMOv2 performance on `$D_E$` itself "remains relatively stationary" during training (Appendix D.4, Figure 10) — the model does not overfit on these harder, more diverse expert traces. Yet test set performance on `$D_I$` and the evaluation benchmarks continues to improve. This is indirect evidence that FEST is learning generalizable reasoning patterns from the expert traces rather than memorizing them — if it were memorizing, we would expect `$D_E$` accuracy to rise sharply (as it does for HPT-G and ReLIFT-G in Figure 7 of Appendix D.4).

#### Out-of-Distribution Generalization to MMLU-Pro

Table 6 evaluates the models trained in the main experiment on MMLU-Pro, a benchmark of over 12,000 problems spanning diverse domains (mathematics is only one subset), with zero-shot Pass@1. FEST-DPO achieves the highest accuracy at **38.68%**, followed by FEST-GRPO at **36.32%**. Both outperform all baselines: RL (34.81%), RL-G (33.18%), ReLIFT (33.99%), HPT (32.83%), CHORD-ϕ (35.82%), SRFT (33.54%), and LUFFY (31.72%).

This result is significant for two reasons. First, it addresses the concern that FEST might be overfitting to mathematical reasoning patterns from `$D_E$` and losing general capability — the MMLU-Pro gains suggest the opposite. Second, the gap between FEST-DPO and FEST-GRPO (38.68% vs. 36.32%) is larger than on the in-distribution math benchmarks, hinting that the sequence-level DPO regularization may provide better generalization properties, possibly because the token-level negative GRPO in FEST-GRPO is more aggressive in reshaping the model's distribution on `$D_E$` questions, which could slightly narrow its general reasoning diversity.

The paper notes that the baseline CHORD-ϕ performs surprisingly well on MMLU-Pro (35.82%) relative to its in-distribution performance (37.56% on the math benchmarks). This suggests that CHORD-ϕ's per-token probability weighting provides some regularization against distribution collapse, but at the cost of weaker specialization on the target math domain — a classic breadth-vs-depth tradeoff that FEST navigates more successfully.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**The three vital components (Table 5):** The paper ablates each of the three components identified in Section 3.1 — supervised learning, on-policy learning, and decaying weight — by constructing reduced variants and measuring their Avg@8 performance. The results demonstrate that optimal performance requires all three simultaneously.

- **Removing both supervised and on-policy (pure RL):** 39.79 ± 1.04%. This is the baseline — GRPO on `$D_I$` only, no use of `$D_E$` at all.
- **On-policy only without decaying weight (RL-G):** 40.55 ± 1.03%. Adding RL training on `$D_E$` without any SFT guidance and without weight scheduling provides a modest +0.76 point gain, but as discussed above, this comes at the cost of severely reduced exploration diversity (Pass@8 drops from 59.67% to 54.84%).
- **On-policy only with decaying weight (RL-G with our weight):** 28.83 ± 0.92%. This variant applies RL on `$D_E$` with the decaying weight schedule but no SFT signal. The catastrophic drop — 10.96 points below vanilla RL — is attributed to model divergence: "when the model is repeatedly exposed only to negative signals on `$D_E$` during late-stage training, it lacks sufficient constructive guidance to maintain a coherent policy, eventually leading to model collapse." This is a key negative result: on-policy negative signals without positive expert guidance are destructive, not constructive, in the few-shot setting.
- **Supervised only with fixed weight (SFT+RL on full dataset):** 37.26 ± 1.12%. This is a standard approach of mixing SFT on the full 46K dataset with RL, using fixed weights throughout. The performance is 2.53 points below pure RL, indicating that even with abundant SFT data, uniform SFT+RL blending can interfere with RLVR optimization. The paper does not specify whether this uses `$D_E$` (128-shot) or the full 46K dataset as stated, but the result reinforces the need for decaying rather than fixed SFT weights.
- **Supervised only with decaying weight (RL + few-shot SFT with our weight):** 39.91 ± 0.67%. This variant applies few-shot SFT on `$D_E$` with the decaying weight schedule, but no on-policy DPO component — it is essentially standard multi-task training where SFT on `$D_E$` gradually decays. The performance is nearly identical to pure RL (39.91% vs. 39.79%), demonstrating that SFT alone, even with careful weight scheduling, cannot extract meaningful gains from 128 examples without the on-policy contrastive component.
- **All three components (FEST-GRPO):** 42.38 ± 1.26%. The synergy is clear: supervised learning provides expert guidance (+0.12 over RL when added alone), on-policy learning provides exploration and robustness (-10.96 when added alone without supervision), but together with decaying weights they yield +2.59 over vanilla RL.

The most informative takeaway from Table 5 is the non-additivity of the components. The sum of individual improvements would be approximately `0.76 + 0.12 − 10.96 ≈ −10.08`, but the actual combined improvement is +2.59. The components are complementary in a way that simple ablation cannot decompose — supervised learning prevents the collapse that on-policy learning alone would cause, on-policy learning provides the contrastive signal that makes supervised learning effective beyond simple imitation, and decaying weights ensure the balance shifts appropriately as training progresses.

**The `$\beta$` hyperparameter (Table 9):** The paper tests six `$(\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3)$` configurations for FEST-DPO, revealing a performance peak at the chosen values (0.1, 0.01, 0.01) with 41.99 ± 1.24%. Performance is reasonably robust for values in the intermediate range:
- (0.1, 0.1, 0.1): 40.22 ± 1.19% (−1.77 from optimal). Uniformly high β saturates the sigmoid, producing switch-like learning that is too aggressive initially and vanishes too quickly.
- (0.1, 0.1, 0.05): 41.51 ± 1.32% (−0.48). Slightly reducing β₃ (for correct rollouts) helps but doesn't fully address the saturation issue.
- (0.1, 0.1, 0): 41.76 ± 1.02% (−0.23). Setting β₃ = 0 (no correction on correct rollouts) approaches optimal performance, confirming that pushing correct rollouts toward expert traces is unnecessary and potentially harmful.
- (0.01, 0.01, 0.001): 41.33 ± 2.16% (−0.66). Moving all β values down by an order of magnitude slightly reduces performance and substantially increases variance (standard deviation doubles from ~1.24 to ~2.16), suggesting that while the signal remains present, it becomes noisier at very low β.
- (0.001, 0.001, 0.001): 40.24 ± 1.22% (−1.75). Uniformly low β weakens the learning signal enough to drop performance close to the uniformly high β case, confirming the non-monotonic relationship derived in Appendix D.3.

The standard deviation pattern is notable: the optimal configuration (0.1, 0.01, 0.01) has variance ~1.24, while both extremes have variance ~1.20. The highest variance occurs at (0.01, 0.01, 0.001) with ±2.16, suggesting that intermediate β values where the sigmoid is partially saturated create more training instability than either the "switch-like" (high β) or "constant" (low β) regimes.

**Training dynamics across methods (Appendix D.4, Figures 7-9):** The paper provides detailed training curves that reveal how different methods progress over 600 steps. Three key patterns emerge:

- **On `$D_E$` accuracy** (Figure 7): Methods that apply RL to `$D_E$` (RL-G, LUFFY, HPT-G, ReLIFT-G) form a "higher group" achieving near 100% training accuracy — clear evidence of overfitting. FEST-DPO and FEST-GRPO, along with HPT, ReLIFT, and CHORD-ϕ, stay in a lower range (40–65%), maintaining generalization capacity. The lower-group methods that converge fastest (CHORD-ϕ, HPT) eventually stagnate on the test set, while FEST variants continue to improve (Figure 9), suggesting that FEST's on-policy DPO component prevents the premature convergence that plagues pure SFT blending.

- **On `$D_I$` accuracy** (Figure 8): FEST-DPO and FEST-GRPO achieve the highest and most stable training accuracy on the answer-only RL data (approximately 58–60% by step 600, versus 54–56% for baselines). The stability is notable: HPT-G and ReLIFT-G show sharp drops and recoveries (the mid-training collapse documented in Figure 5), while FEST curves are smooth and monotonically increasing.

- **Test set Pass@1** (Figure 9): FEST variants steadily improve throughout training, reaching approximately 41–42% by step 600. In contrast, LUFFY, CHORD-ϕ, and HPT plateau around 37–39% after step 200–300, and HPT-G and ReLIFT-G show erratic behavior with sharp drops. The continued improvement of FEST at late training stages (steps 400–600) while baselines stagnate suggests that the decaying SFT weight is functioning as designed — allowing RLVR to take over as the primary learning signal while preventing the SFT component from locking the model into a narrow overfitted basin.

**LIMOv2 training dynamics (Figure 10):** The training curves on LIMOv2-8192 reveal a different pattern: `$D_E$` accuracy remains low and stationary (~20–30%) throughout training for both FEST variants, while `$D_I$` accuracy and test set Pass@1 steadily improve. The paper attributes this to LIMOv2's "substantially higher reasoning difficulty," which prevents the model from easily fitting the expert traces. The fact that generalization improves without improvements on `$D_E$` reinforces that FEST is extracting generalizable reasoning patterns rather than memorizing specific solutions — the model gets better at math without getting better at reproducing the exact expert traces it's training against.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim: FEST outperforms all baselines with only 128 random demonstrations

**Supported, with important caveats about baseline strength.** Table 2 clearly shows FEST-GRPO at 42.36% and FEST-DPO at 41.98% exceeding all implemented baselines at the same 128-shot budget. The margins are substantial (1.85–4.46 points over the strongest non-FEST baselines). However, two questions deserve scrutiny.

First, **the pure RL baseline is stronger than reported in prior work**, and this is partly an artifact of the paper's own hyperparameter optimization. The authors note that they "increased [learning rate] from 1e-6 to 5e-6" which boosted RL performance to parity with ReLIFT on the full dataset. This raises the question: were the baselines (HPT, ReLIFT, LUFFY, CHORD-ϕ) similarly optimized for the few-shot setting, or were they run with their standard full-dataset hyperparameters? The paper states that learning rates were adjusted for ReLIFT and LUFFY (to 5 × 10⁻⁶) because "we observed that this higher rate significantly enhanced performance in our experiments," but it's unclear whether other algorithm-specific hyperparameters (buffer sizes for ReLIFT, SFT/RL ratios for LUFFY, weighting schedules for CHORD-ϕ) were exhaustively tuned for the 128-shot regime versus carried over from full-dataset configurations. If baselines were not given equivalent hyperparameter optimization effort, the performance gap attributed to algorithmic superiority may partially reflect tuning asymmetry.

Second, **SRFT and MIFO are evaluated under different conditions than FEST.** SRFT is tested using an official checkpoint trained on the full dataset, not at 128 shots — it serves as an upper bound on what full-dataset methods can achieve, but the 128-shot SRFT result is unknown. The note that "the code base of HPT also implemented SRFT; however, SRFT training constantly collapse with their implementation" suggests that SRFT may be brittle and difficult to adapt to the few-shot setting, but the absence of a working 128-shot SRFT baseline leaves open the possibility that SRFT with proper tuning could match FEST. MIFO's results are taken directly from the original paper with no 128-shot implementation, and the reported numbers are incomplete (no Minerva, no AMC23 results, and the benchmarks don't perfectly match this paper's evaluation suite).

#### Claim: All three vital components are necessary — ablating any one causes performance collapse

**Strongly supported, with one surprising finding that deserves further investigation.** Table 5 provides clear evidence: supervised-only achieves 39.91% (no gain over RL), on-policy-only with decaying weight collapses to 28.83% (10 points below RL), and all three together achieve 42.38%. The synergy is real and the ablation is clean.

The surprising result — "RL-G with our weight" at 28.83% — is both the paper's strongest evidence for the necessity of supervision and its least-explained finding. The paper attributes the collapse to "model divergence: when the model is repeatedly exposed only to negative signals on `$D_E$` during late-stage training, it lacks sufficient constructive guidance to maintain a coherent policy." But this explanation raises a deeper question: why does negative RL with decaying weight cause collapse, while FEST-GRPO — which also applies negative GRPO on `$D_E$` — doesn't? The answer must lie in the positive SFT signal that FEST-GRPO retains (the weighted SFT on expert traces). Without that positive anchor, the negative signal pushes the model away from its own outputs without specifying what to move toward, and the policy drifts into degenerate regions of parameter space.

This is an important finding for the broader RLVR community because it **contradicts the intuition that negative reinforcement alone is beneficial**. Zhu et al. (2025) showed that negative RL *can* redistribute probability mass toward other feasible solutions, but their experiments were not in the few-shot regime and likely had sufficient data diversity to provide implicit positive guidance through the GRPO group advantage. In the few-shot setting with only 128 questions, the "other feasible solutions" are not sufficiently represented in the batch to provide meaningful redirection. This finding should caution practitioners against applying pure negative RL in data-sparse settings without explicit positive regularization.

The ablation, however, is incomplete in one respect: it doesn't **isolate the on-policy aspect from the negative RL aspect**. The on-policy component in FEST uses the model's own rollouts as negatives. An alternative ablation would use **fixed, pre-generated negatives** (offline DPO) to test whether the on-policy property matters beyond the negative signal itself. The "supervised only with decaying weight" variant (39.91%) is essentially offline — it has no contrastive signal at all. A middle-ground ablation with offline DPO (fixed positives, fixed pre-computed negatives) would distinguish whether the benefit comes from having *any* negative signal versus having an *on-policy* negative signal. This experiment was not run and is a notable gap in the ablation design.

#### Claim: FEST matches the performance of methods trained on the full 46K SFT dataset

**Supported with important qualification.** Figure 2 shows FEST-DPO at 512 shots approaching the performance of HPT on the full 46K dataset (dashed line). However, the paper does not provide a direct numerical comparison — the dashed line on Figure 2 represents "baseline results utilizing the full 46K SFT dataset," but the specific baseline (which method, what accuracy) is not labeled. The text states it's "HPT trained on the full 46K SFT dataset" (Section 4.2), but no table compares FEST at 512 shots head-to-head against full-dataset HPT on the same evaluation benchmarks.

Moreover, the full-dataset baselines were not re-run with the optimized learning rate (5 × 10⁻⁶) — they use the original paper configurations. Given that the paper demonstrated RL alone reaches "performance parity with ReLIFT on the full dataset" when the learning rate is optimized, it's plausible that full-dataset HPT or ReLIFT would also improve with tuning, raising the bar that FEST is approaching. The comparison as presented may overstate how close FEST gets to the true ceiling of full-dataset methods with optimal hyperparameters.

#### Claim: FEST generalizes robustly to out-of-distribution tasks

**Supported but with an important "compared to what" caveat.** Table 6 on MMLU-Pro shows FEST-DPO at 38.68% and FEST-GRPO at 36.32% outperforming all baselines. This is genuine evidence of generalization beyond the math training distribution. However, MMLU-Pro is evaluated at Pass@1 (a single greedy or temperature-0.6 decode), not Avg@8. This metric choice means the comparison captures **model quality** rather than **sampling diversity**, which is a valid but distinct axis from the main results. A model could have excellent Pass@1 but poor Pass@8 (low exploration diversity but high exploitation precision), or vice versa. The paper reports Pass@1 for MMLU-Pro but Avg@8 for the math benchmarks, making the generalization claim rely on a metric shift.

Additionally, MMLU-Pro includes mathematics questions as a subset. The paper does not break down MMLU-Pro performance by domain, so it's unclear whether FEST's gains come from better general reasoning or simply better math (which happens to be tested in MMLU-Pro's math subset). A domain-stratified analysis would strengthen the OOD claim substantially.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

**No experiments on code generation or other verifiable-reward domains.** The entire evaluation is on mathematical reasoning. Code generation — the other major RLVR application — has clean reward signals (unit tests), abundant answer-only data, and well-established benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP, LiveCodeBench). Testing FEST on code would address the obvious generalization concern and is a notable omission given that the paper frames RLVR as a general paradigm, not a math-specific technique.

**No experiments at larger model scales.** All experiments use Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B. The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly: "due to computational resource constraints, our evaluation was mostly conducted with a 1.5B parameter model." This is a significant limitation for a method whose central claim is extracting value from sparse SFT data — larger models might benefit more (if they have greater capacity to generalize from few examples) or less (if they already saturate the available signal in answer-only RLVR). The scaling behavior with model size is unknown.

**No comparison against few-shot data curation methods.** The paper emphasizes that FEST works with randomly selected demonstrations, distinguishing it from LIMO and s1K which use careful curation. But a direct comparison — FEST with random 128-shot vs. FEST with curated 128-shot, or vs. LIMO-style SFT+RL — would quantify the value proposition. If curated few-shot SFT + RL outperforms FEST with random data, then the practical recommendation depends on whether curation is cheaper than the performance gap. The paper doesn't provide this comparison.

**No experiments with different random seeds for training.** The paper reports mean and standard deviation across 8 evaluation rollouts for a single training run. Training stochasticity (different weight initializations, different data ordering, different rollout sampling) is not quantified. For a method operating in the extreme few-shot regime where training dynamics can be sensitive, this is a meaningful omission. The three different DE splits provide some evidence of robustness to data selection, but not to training stochasticity.

**No latency or throughput measurements.** The paper measures compute in training steps but does not report wall-clock time per method, throughput differences, or memory requirements. FEST requires sampling additional rollouts from `$D_E$` for the DPO loss, and FEST-GRPO requires computing GRPO advantages on `$D_E$` rollouts. Whether this overhead is negligible or substantial relative to baseline methods is not quantified, making practical adoption decisions harder.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in Headline Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework depends on estimating each prompt's difficulty *before* allocating the test-time compute budget. The paper's method for doing so — generating 2048 samples per question and computing either ground-truth pass@1 (oracle) or the PRM's average final-answer score (predicted) — is **extraordinarily expensive**. The authors acknowledge this explicitly:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity" (Section 3.2)

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency gains — 4× better compute efficiency than best-of-N (Figures 4 and 8) — are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In any practical deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution. Since difficulty estimation via 2048-sample generation and PRM scoring can easily exceed the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations), the true cost of the compute-optimal approach in a realistic setting could be substantially higher than the reported figures suggest. For a system that processes many queries, the amortized cost per query might be acceptable if difficulty estimation is done once per prompt template; for one-off queries, the difficulty estimation cost could dominate and make the method less efficient than simply applying best-of-N uniformly.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides two pieces of indirect evidence that the limitation is manageable. First, Figure 4 shows that predicted difficulty bins (using the PRM's own scores without ground-truth labels) largely overlap with oracle bins in the search experiments, and Figure 8 shows a moderate gap for revisions at high budgets. This demonstrates that the approach works without ground-truth access, but does not address the sample cost. Second, the authors flag this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2), suggesting training a model to predict difficulty directly from the question text. However, no such model is developed or evaluated, and no experiments vary the number of samples used for difficulty estimation to show a trade-off curve between estimation accuracy and cost.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper suggests future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8) and frames the current approach as establishing feasibility rather than providing a deployment-ready solution. The difficulty estimation cost is a known and transparent gap, but it means the 4× efficiency figure should be understood as an upper bound that assumes a solved difficulty estimation problem.

---

### Hard Problems Remain Unsolved: Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The method fundamentally assumes that the base model already produces correct solutions at some non-trivial rate. All test-time compute strategies — search, revisions, or their combination — operate by either finding existing correct solutions more efficiently (verifier-guided search) or refining nearly-correct solutions (revisions). If the base model's pass@1 is effectively zero on a problem, no amount of test-time compute can help.

**The consequence.** Across all methods, all budgets, and all difficulty estimation regimes, the hardest difficulty bin (bin 5) shows **near-zero improvement** regardless of compute allocation. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all search methods at all budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio for revisions. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling curve is essentially flat near 0–5% — it never crosses the performance of the ~14× larger model at any value of R, with relative disadvantages of −37.2% to −52.9% depending on the setting.

The paper is candid about this (Section 7 takeaway box), but it means the approach offers **no path forward for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning** that exceeds the base model's training distribution. For any problem where the base model's inherent capability doesn't include at least occasional success, test-time compute amplifies nothing — it's multiply-by-zero scaling. This is a hard capability boundary: pretraining is the only viable path for expanding the frontier of what a model can do, while test-time compute is limited to improving efficiency within the existing frontier.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is consistent and unambiguous across every experiment. The separation into quintile bins makes the failure mode stark: bin 5 curves are flat in Figures 3, 7, and 9; FLOPs-matched comparisons in Section 7 always show pretraining dominating test-time compute on hard problems; and the paper explicitly states the boundary condition in the Section 7 discussion.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged but fundamentally unsolvable within the test-time compute paradigm. The paper positions this as a defining characteristic of the approach rather than a bug: test-time compute and pretraining compute are "not 1-to-1 exchangeable" (Section 7), and the boundary between where each is preferable is one of the paper's key contributions. The limitation is therefore appropriately scoped — the paper does not claim to solve the hard-problem frontier, only to optimize within the base model's existing capability range.

### Single Benchmark, Single Model Family: Generality Is Unproven

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions from 12,000 training questions in the Lightman et al. split) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an assertion, not an empirical finding.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or dataset-specific in ways that substantially affect the practical relevance of the results:

- **PRM quality and over-optimization behavior** depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution, calibration, and error patterns. A different base model — with better or worse native mathematical reasoning, different calibration, or different token-level uncertainty — would produce different PRM scores, different over-optimization thresholds, and potentially different optimal strategies per difficulty bin. Models with stronger native reasoning might shift the difficulty distribution upward (more questions in bins 1–2), while weaker models might push more questions into the unsolved bin 5, altering the fraction of problems for which test-time compute provides any benefit.

- **The revision model's effectiveness** depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities and its ability to condition on incorrect previous answers to produce improvements. Different model families (GPT, Claude, LLaMA, Gemma) have substantially different in-context learning profiles, and the finding that revisions help on easy problems but provide diminishing returns on hard ones may not transfer.

- **MATH is exclusively symbolic mathematical reasoning** with well-defined ground-truth answers that can be verified by exact string matching (via the Lightman et al. grading function). This enables both difficulty estimation (via pass@1) and PRM training (via Monte Carlo rollout correctness). Many important reasoning tasks — code generation, scientific reasoning, multi-step planning, open-ended generation — have different reward structures. Code generation has execution-based verification (unit tests) that is similar to MATH's binary rewards, but planning and open-ended generation lack clean correctness signals, which would require fundamentally different PRM training pipelines not explored in this work.

- **The test set is 500 questions**, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 questions each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation into ~50 questions per fold per bin for strategy selection. With only ~50 questions determining which search strategy or revision ratio is optimal per bin, the selected strategies may have high variance — a different 500-question test set might suggest different optimal strategies for the same difficulty bins. The paper reports no confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves and no bootstrap analysis of how stable the per-bin strategy selection is at this sample size.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None beyond the MATH/PaLM 2-S* results. The paper does not test on code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), scientific reasoning, or any domain outside mathematics. The authors acknowledge the single-benchmark limitation in Section 8 (future work) but provide no cross-domain validation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed experimentally. The limitation is acknowledged implicitly in the future work discussion (Section 8: "Extension to other domains and modalities"), and the widespread use of MATH in the RLVR literature provides some community validation, but the paper's specific claims about difficulty-dependent strategy selection — the core contribution — are supported only for mathematical reasoning with one model family.

---

### The ~14× Larger Model Baseline May Not Be Compute-Optimally Trained

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023). The paper acknowledges that this departs from compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022), where both data and parameters are scaled equally:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with ~14× more total FLOPs — scaling both parameter count and training data according to the Hoffmann et al. scaling laws — would likely outperform a parameters-only-scaled model at the same total FLOPs budget. By using a parameters-only-scaled baseline, the paper's pretraining baseline may be **weaker than it needs to be**, making the comparison more favorable to test-time compute than a fairer baseline would produce.

The magnitude of this effect is uncertain. If the 14× parameter-only model is significantly undertrained relative to its capacity (trained on data appropriate for a smaller model), adding more data would improve it meaningfully, potentially closing or reversing some of the gaps where test-time compute currently looks favorable (e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at R `≪` 1 for revisions). Conversely, if the parameter-only scaled model happens to be near-saturated on the available data, the gap might be small.

Additionally, the ~14× larger model uses only **greedy decoding** — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search, no revisions. In the FLOPs-matched framework, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (say, best-of-8 with an ORM) would create a much stronger baseline that is never tested. The computed-optimal policy might still win, but the margin would almost certainly be smaller, and the crossover points (where test-time compute ceases to be preferable) might shift.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides the relative performance numbers (Section 7, Figure 9, Figure 1 bar charts) but does not ablate the choice of pretraining scaling paradigm or test the larger model with any test-time compute augmentation. The numbers reported — e.g., +27.8% on easy+medium questions at R `≪` 1 for revisions — are upper bounds on the advantage of test-time compute over a stronger pretraining baseline.

**Mitigation status.** Voluntarily disclosed as a limitation and left to future work. The paper is transparent about the choice and the rationale (representativeness of common practice), but a reader should understand that the headline finding — "smaller model + test-time compute can outperform a ~14× larger model" — holds for the specific (parameters-only-scaled) pretraining recipe tested, and the margin may shrink against a compute-optimally trained larger model.

### The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate with Only Partial Mitigations

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on trajectories where all in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). This data construction — while necessary for teaching the model to fix mistakes — creates a fundamental asymmetry: the model never sees examples where the current answer is already correct and should be preserved.

**The consequence.** At test time, when the revision model encounters a correct answer in its context (produced during an earlier revision step), approximately **38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones** (Section 6.1). This is a direct consequence of the training distribution: the model has learned that in-context answers are always wrong and should be replaced, so it "revises" correct answers into wrong ones with substantial probability.

The paper mitigates this with **within-chain selection** — either majority voting or verifier-based selection across all revisions in the chain, picking the best answer from any point rather than always taking the last revision. This is effective at recovering performance (the final selected answer need not be the last revision), but it is an imperfect patch:

- It wastes compute: many of the later revisions are actively harmful, and the compute spent generating them could have been allocated to parallel exploration instead.
- It requires a reliable selection mechanism: majority voting or verifier-based selection must correctly identify the best answer in the chain, and failures of selection (picking a reverted incorrect answer over an earlier correct one) directly reduce accuracy.
- It doesn't address the root cause: the model fundamentally does not know when to stop revising, because it was never trained to recognize a correct answer as correct.

The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) further demonstrates the fragility of revision training. Attempting to optimize the revision model with RL-style training caused performance to **degrade substantially** with sequential revisions — at 256 generations, fully sequential performance dropped to ~33.5% compared to ~38.5% at the optimal ratio. This suggests the revision approach is sensitive to training methodology in ways that are not fully understood.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% figure is stated in Section 6.1. The within-chain selection mitigation is described and evaluated in Figure 6 (right), which shows sequential revisions outperform parallel only when selection is applied. The ReST$^{EM}$ failure is documented in Appendix K, Figure 16, and the authors hypothesize that "on-policy data collection in ReST$^{EM}$ exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data."

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed with within-chain selection, but the fundamental problem — the model's inability to recognize when no revision is needed — is unsolved. The paper does not explore training the model with a "no revision needed" signal or using the PRM to detect correct answers mid-chain and halt revision. This is a structural limitation of the revision training paradigm as implemented, not a minor implementation detail, and it places a hard ceiling on how much sequential revision depth can be productively used.

### Sequential Revisions Introduce Latency That the FLOPs-Based Analysis Ignores

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures test-time compute exclusively in "generations" (number of complete solutions sampled), which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but **ignores wall-clock latency entirely**. Sequential revisions are inherently serial — each revision depends on the previous one — while parallel best-of-N can be executed simultaneously given sufficient hardware.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy often favors sequential-heavy strategies for easy problems (Section 6, Figure 7). For example, a policy that allocates 128 generations as a single chain of 128 sequential revisions takes approximately **128× longer** in wall-clock time than one that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously (assuming sufficient hardware to batch all 128 in one forward pass). Even a hybrid strategy of `√128` parallel chains × `√128` sequential steps per chain is ~11× slower than fully parallel execution.

For latency-sensitive applications — interactive assistants, real-time tutoring, any deployment where users wait for responses — this trade-off is critical and unaddressed. The 4× efficiency improvement in total generations might come with a 10–100× increase in response time, making it impractical regardless of FLOPs savings. The paper frames its contributions around compute efficiency (FLOPs), but production deployments care about both cost (FLOPs) and latency (wall-clock time), and the optimal strategy for minimizing cost may be unacceptable for latency.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not report wall-clock time, latency, or any throughput measurements for any method. The phrase "latency" does not appear in the paper. The entire analysis is in the space of generation counts and FLOPs.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss this trade-off or suggest how it might be navigated (e.g., by capping the maximum sequential depth based on latency requirements, or by using speculative execution across parallel chains). For a paper whose practical implications center on deployment decisions — when to use test-time compute vs. pretraining — the absence of latency analysis is a significant gap, particularly because the strategies that the compute-optimal policy favors (sequential revisions on easy problems, beam search on medium problems) are precisely the ones with the worst latency characteristics. A practitioner reading this paper would need to separately evaluate whether the reported FLOPs gains survive translation to latency-constrained settings.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **new problem framing** that fundamentally reorients how the community should think about the role of SFT data in post-training RLVR pipelines. Before this work, the dominant paradigm in demonstration-guided RLVR treated SFT data as a strategic resource to be deployed intelligently — pull demonstrations when RL fails (HPT, ReLIFT), blend them with RL gradients at fixed ratios (LUFFY, SRFT), or weight them dynamically based on reward signals (CHORD, MIFO). The common implicit assumption across all these methods was that SFT data is abundant enough that the algorithm's job is *allocation*, not *extraction* — deciding when and how to apply expert guidance, assuming that guidance exists somewhere in a prepared dataset for the questions that need it.

FEST breaks this assumption explicitly by defining a **few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR regime** and showing that existing methods fail catastrophically when it holds (Table 2: HPT-G at 32.02% compared to 39.79% for vanilla RL; ReLIFT-G at 38.19%; Figure 5 in Appendix D.2 showing mid-training collapse). This is not an incremental finding — it's a diagnosis that the allocation paradigm is structurally mismatched to sparse-data settings, and that a fundamentally different approach is needed.

The paper's conceptual shift is from **"how should we allocate abundant SFT data?"** to **"how can we extract maximal value from whatever SFT data we happen to have?"** This reframing is significant because it opens up RLVR to problem domains where expert demonstrations are genuinely scarce — not just expensive, but effectively unobtainable at scale. The "Humanity's Last Exam" example (1,000 graduate-degree holders for 2,500 questions with only brief rationales) makes the practical stakes concrete: if the community's post-training methods require thousands or tens of thousands of expert reasoning traces, they cannot be applied to the hardest open problems that matter most. FEST demonstrates that a different optimization design — one structured around the specific requirements of few-shot regimes — can reduce the SFT data requirement by two orders of magnitude (128 vs. 5,000–46,000 demonstrations) while maintaining or exceeding performance.

The paper also resolves a latent contradiction in prior work that had gone largely unexamined. Several studies had reported positive results from combining SFT and RL (HPT, ReLIFT, LUFFY all show gains over pure RL on full datasets), while other work had found that pure RL can match or exceed SFT+RL combinations when properly tuned (the paper's own finding that optimized-lr vanilla RL reaches 39.79%, matching ReLIFT on the full dataset). These findings are not contradictory — they reflect a **data-efficiency spectrum** that prior work hadn't characterized. FEST's contribution is showing that the value of SFT data depends critically on the algorithm used to incorporate it: naive blending methods leave most of the data's value untapped, requiring large volumes to achieve modest gains, while FEST's three-component design extracts substantially more useful signal per demonstration. The implication is that much of the prior literature's SFT data usage may have been wasteful — not because the data wasn't valuable, but because the algorithms weren't designed to extract its full value under sparsity constraints.

The paper also redirects attention toward **algorithm structure** rather than **data curation** as the primary lever for few-shot post-training. The dominant approach in few-shot SFT (LIMO, s1K) has been careful heuristic selection — choosing the "right" examples based on difficulty, diversity, and reasoning quality. FEST deliberately avoids curation (three different random 128-shot splits and LIMOv2 all produce similar gains in Table 4), demonstrating that algorithm design can substitute for data engineering. This is a significant practical finding: curation pipelines are themselves expensive and domain-specific, while FEST's random-selection approach transfers across datasets with no modification. For researchers building post-training pipelines on new domains, the message is to invest in better loss functions rather than better data filters — a reallocation of engineering effort that could accelerate progress across multiple application areas.

Finally, the paper makes **verifier over-optimization and exploration collapse** legible as distinct failure modes in few-shot demonstration-guided RLVR, providing diagnostic tools for future work. The contrast between RL-G's high Avg@8 (40.55%) and low Pass@8 (54.84% in Table 3) versus FEST-GRPO's high Avg@8 (42.36%) and high Pass@8 (61.06%) reveals a systematic trade-off: methods that apply positive RL on the few-shot SFT data (RL-G, HPT-G, ReLIFT-G) converge to narrow, high-confidence solution strategies that score well on average but fail to even attempt correct solutions on many questions, while FEST maintains exploration diversity. The "RL-G with our weight" ablation (28.83% in Table 5) further shows that on-policy negative signals without positive supervision cause catastrophic drift — the model is pushed away from its own outputs with no constructive guidance on where to go. These findings establish an **exploration-exploitation taxonomy** for few-shot post-training that future methods can use for diagnosis: if Pass@8 is dropping while Avg@8 is rising, the method is over-exploiting; if training accuracy on the SFT set is near 100%, the method is overfitting; if performance collapses entirely, the method lacks sufficient positive anchoring. This diagnostic framework is a methodological contribution separable from the FEST algorithm itself.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Testing FEST on code generation benchmarks with execution-based verifiers.** The entire paper evaluates on mathematical reasoning, but the RLVR paradigm is equally important for code generation — where unit tests provide verifiable rewards and expert code solutions serve as SFT demonstrations. FEST's three-component structure (supervised signal, on-policy negative signal, decaying weight) should transfer directly: expert code traces from HumanEval or MBPP solutions serve as `D_E`, unit test pass/fail provides binary rewards on `D_I`, and the adaptive β scheduling based on test-pass solvability maps cleanly (β₁ when no rollouts pass tests, β₂ when some pass, β₃ when the current rollout passes). Key measurements would include (1) whether FEST's gains over vanilla RL are comparable to the math case (~2.5 points on average with 128 demonstrations) or larger/smaller given code's different reasoning structure, (2) whether FEST-GRPO's token-level gradient unification provides the same stability benefits when applied to code tokens (which include structural syntax with different information density than natural language math reasoning), and (3) whether random selection of 128 code demonstrations is sufficient or whether code's greater surface-form diversity (different variable names, library calls, algorithmic approaches to the same problem) demands more examples than mathematical reasoning to achieve comparable coverage. A negative result — FEST provides minimal gains on code — would be informative, suggesting that the method's effectiveness depends on properties of mathematical reasoning (formal step-by-step derivability, limited surface-form variation for a given solution approach) that don't generalize.

**Scaling FEST to larger models to test the interaction between model capacity and few-shot SFT data efficiency.** The paper uses only Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B due to computational constraints, but the central claim — that 128 demonstrations can match full-dataset performance — may have different validity at different scales. Larger models have greater capacity to generalize from few examples (they can extract more abstract reasoning patterns from each demonstration), but they also typically have stronger base performance on RLVR tasks, which could reduce the marginal benefit of SFT guidance (the model already produces correct rollouts on more questions, so fewer questions receive the "unsolvable" β₁ signal). Conversely, larger models might overfit more aggressively on tiny datasets because their greater capacity enables rapid memorization. A concrete experiment would test FEST on Qwen2.5-Math-7B or DeepSeek-Math-7B with 128-shot configurations, measuring (1) whether the absolute gain over vanilla RL scales with model size, (2) whether the optimal β values shift (larger models produce longer CoT traces with larger log-ratio differences, potentially requiring even smaller β), (3) whether the decaying weight schedule needs to change (larger models might need the SFT signal to decay faster to prevent memorization), and (4) whether FEST-GRPO maintains its stability advantage over FEST-DPO at scale. The 4-5 day training time on 1.5B parameters suggests 7B-scale experiments would require multi-GPU setups and careful optimization, but the finding would determine whether FEST's approach is primarily valuable for resource-constrained settings (small models, limited compute) or scales to the frontier.

**Combining FEST with few-shot data curation to test whether structure and selection are complementary or redundant.** The paper deliberately uses random demonstrations to stress-test the algorithm and show that curation is unnecessary for FEST to work. However, a natural follow-up asks: if curation helps standalone few-shot SFT (LIMO, s1K achieve strong results with carefully selected examples), could curation + FEST be even better than random + FEST? The experiment would compare four conditions: (1) random 128-shot with vanilla RL (baseline), (2) curated 128-shot (e.g., LIMOv2 selection criteria: difficulty, diversity, long CoT) with vanilla RL, (3) random 128-shot with FEST, (4) curated 128-shot with FEST. If (4) substantially outperforms (3), it means algorithmic structure and data curation are complementary — FEST extracts value from whatever data is available, but better data still helps. If (3) ≈ (4), it means FEST's design is so effective at extracting signal that data quality becomes nearly irrelevant — a stronger claim that would shift community practice toward algorithm development over data engineering. The paper's LIMOv2 results (Table 4: 41.21% with FEST-DPO on LIMOv2 vs. 41.98% on random OpenR1) already hint at the latter, but a controlled comparison with identical shot counts and a systematic curation ablation (varying selection criteria) would be needed to confirm. A negative result — curation helps significantly — would refine our understanding of when and why FEST's structural components matter most.

**Investigating whether the FEST-GRPO gradient decomposition generalizes to other hybrid RL-SFT objectives.** The paper's key technical move — recognizing that DPO's gradient decomposes into weighted SFT + REINFORCE, then substituting token-level GRPO for sequence-level REINFORCE — is a design pattern, not a one-off trick. Any post-training method that combines a sequence-level preference loss with token-level policy gradients faces the same gradient mismatch that FEST diagnoses (Figure 4 in Appendix D.1: DPO gradients 10-100× larger than GRPO gradients). The decomposition-substitution pattern could be applied to other hybrid methods: CHORD's per-token probability weighting could be unified with GRPO through similar decomposition; LUFFY's importance-sampled SFT + RL could be refactored to avoid the regularized importance sampling machinery if the SFT component is treated as weighted SFT in the DPO decomposition framework; online DPO variants (OAIF, iterative DPO) that generate both positives and negatives on-policy could benefit from token-level substitution when combined with RLVR objectives. A concrete research program would systematically apply the decomposition-substitution pattern to existing hybrid methods, measuring whether it (1) eliminates the need for per-method gradient balancing coefficients (analogous to FEST's `c = 1` for GRPO vs. `c = 0.01` for DPO), (2) improves training stability (fewer mid-training collapses like HPT-G and ReLIFT-G exhibit), and (3) maintains or improves final performance. A negative result — the pattern doesn't generalize to some methods — would define the boundary conditions under which sequence-level preference learning and token-level policy gradients can be cleanly unified.

**Characterizing the exploration-exploitation tradeoff in few-shot RLVR through systematic Pass@k vs. Avg@k measurement across training.** The paper's most striking diagnostic finding is the divergence between RL-G's Avg@8 and Pass@8: RL-G achieves 40.55% Avg@8 (better than vanilla RL's 39.79%) but only 54.84% Pass@8 (much worse than vanilla RL's 59.67%). This suggests that methods combining SFT and RL on the few-shot data induce a systematic shift toward exploitation (narrower, more consistent correct solutions) at the cost of exploration (fewer questions solved at all). A follow-up study would track this tradeoff dynamically across training for all methods, measuring Pass@1, Pass@4, Pass@8, Pass@16, and Pass@32 at regular intervals to construct **exploration efficiency curves** — how quickly does each method expand the set of "solvable" questions versus how quickly does it improve consistency on already-solvable questions? FEST-GRPO's Pass@8 of 61.06% versus RL's 59.67% (Table 3) suggests FEST slightly improves exploration over pure RL, but the mechanisms are unclear: does the negative GRPO on `D_E` prevent the policy from collapsing to a narrow mode, or does the weighted SFT provide new reasoning templates that expand solvability, or both? Disentangling these would require ablating the positive and negative components separately while measuring Pass@k trajectories. The practical implication is significant: if a method improves Avg@8 at the cost of Pass@32, it may look better under standard evaluation but actually have a lower performance ceiling for applications that can afford more sampling. This analysis would produce concrete guidance on which methods to use based on the inference budget available at deployment.

**Developing cheap difficulty estimation methods to close the practicality gap in compute-optimal test-time scaling.** The paper's compute-optimal framework (from the referenced prior work that FEST complements, not FEST itself) is gated by the cost of difficulty estimation — generating 2048 samples per question to bin into quintiles is often more expensive than the test-time compute being optimized. FEST's contribution is orthogonal (it's about post-training data efficiency, not inference-time allocation), but the two threads connect naturally: a model trained with FEST's few-shot demonstration guidance might produce better-calibrated uncertainty estimates (because the semi-online DPO loss provides a more robust value function through the implicit reward `β log(π/π_ref)`), which could enable cheaper difficulty estimation. A concrete experiment would use a FEST-trained model's implicit reward scores (log-ratio of current policy to reference policy) as a per-question difficulty signal, testing whether these scores correlate with actual pass@1 well enough to replace the 2048-sample estimation procedure. If the correlation is strong, FEST-trained models could serve as their own difficulty estimators — the same model that benefits from compute-optimal allocation also provides the signal needed to do the allocation. If the correlation is weak, it suggests that the implicit reward from DPO training captures preference alignment rather than capability, and separate difficulty estimation machinery remains necessary.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Post-training specialized reasoning models for domains with limited expert data.** The most direct application of FEST is in scenarios where a research team has access to abundant question-answer pairs (with verifiable ground truth) but very few expert-written reasoning traces. Consider a pharmaceutical company building a model for drug interaction reasoning: they can mine thousands of known interaction outcomes from medical databases (answer-only RL data with verifiable binary rewards: "interaction" vs. "no interaction"), but expert pharmacologists can only annotate detailed reasoning chains for a few dozen examples (explaining *why* a specific interaction occurs based on metabolic pathways). FEST's architecture maps directly: 128 expert-annotated reasoning traces become `D_E`, thousands of mined interaction pairs become `D_I`, and the verifier checks whether the model's predicted interaction matches the database record. The paper's results on MATH suggest that FEST with 128 demonstrations can match the performance of methods requiring 5,000+ demonstrations — translating to a 40× reduction in expert annotation burden. The specific numbers from Table 2 (FEST-GRPO at 42.36% vs. CHORD at 37.56% with 5K demonstrations, vs. ReLIFT at 40.51% with 8.6K) provide the quantitative justification for this approach. The key practical requirement is that the domain has verifiable ground-truth answers for the `D_I` data; any domain with answer verification (code execution, mathematical proof checking, structured prediction with ground truth, formal verification) is a candidate.

**Efficient fine-tuning of open-source models for competitive mathematics and coding benchmarks.** The paper's demonstrated performance on AIME25, AIME24, AMC23, and MATH-500 (Table 2) provides a concrete recipe for teams competing on reasoning benchmarks with limited compute. The full pipeline requires: (1) a base model (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B or similar), (2) 128 randomly selected SFT demonstrations with long chain-of-thought traces, (3) a large set of answer-only problems (the remaining 45,872 from OpenR1 or similar), (4) 4-5 days of training on two GH200 GPUs. The resulting model achieves 42.36% average accuracy across six benchmarks — competitive with methods that used the full 46K SFT dataset and substantially more compute. For academic labs or independent researchers without access to industrial-scale annotation pipelines, this represents a ~99.7% reduction in SFT data preparation cost with no performance penalty (indeed, with performance gains over several full-dataset baselines). The random selection property is practically crucial: the team doesn't need to design a curation pipeline or worry about whether their few-shot examples are "good enough" — FEST's algorithm design handles the extraction. The 38.68% Pass@1 on MMLU-Pro (Table 6) further suggests that the resulting model retains broad reasoning capability rather than over-specializing to math, making it suitable as a general reasoning backbone.

**Data-efficient self-improvement loops where models generate their own training data iteratively.** The paper's finding that FEST prevents exploration collapse (Pass@8 of 61.06% vs. 54.84% for RL-G in Table 3) has direct implications for self-improvement pipelines like STaR, ReST^EM, or rejection sampling fine-tuning. In a typical self-improvement loop, a model generates solutions on training data, correct solutions are filtered and used for the next round of fine-tuning, and the process repeats. A known failure mode is **diversity collapse** — each iteration narrows the model's output distribution, reducing Pass@k and eventually starving the pipeline of new correct solutions to learn from. FEST's semi-online DPO component (with model-generated rollouts as negatives) acts as a regularizer against this collapse: the negative RL term pushes probability mass away from the model's current outputs, actively preserving exploration diversity. A self-improvement loop using FEST would: (1) train on `D_I` with GRPO and on a small `D_E` (which could be initial correct solutions from the base model, filtered by a verifier) with semi-online DPO, (2) generate new solutions across the training set, (3) add new correct solutions to `D_E` while potentially cycling out old ones, (4) repeat. The paper's negative result with ReST^EM (Appendix K, Figure 16) is actually instructive here: naive on-policy data generation degraded performance, suggesting that FEST's structured loss (supervised + on-policy negative + decay) is specifically what enables stable iterative improvement. A practical self-improvement deployment would use FEST's loss structure from the first iteration, before the model has diverged from its base distribution, and maintain the decaying weight schedule across iterations to prevent the exploration collapse that pure RL-driven self-improvement exhibits.
