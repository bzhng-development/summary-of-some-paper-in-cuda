# Online Label Refinement (OLR): Teaching Reasoning Models to Self-Correct Under Noisy Labels by Exploiting Early Correctness Coherence in RLVR

**ArXiv:** [2604.03993](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03993)

## 🎯 Pitch

Not all noisy labels in RLVR are created equal—some actively corrupt reasoning while others simply waste training rolls, but models exhibit a surprising “Early Correctness Coherence” where their own rollouts can surface correct answers even when trained on majority-wrong data. This paper shows that by dynamically refining labels with the model’s own majority-voted answers under a stability-evaluating criterion, reasoning models can achieve substantial out-of-distribution gains without any clean-label access.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper systematically analyzes how reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) behaves under noisy supervision and introduces **Online Label Refinement (OLR)**, a method that progressively corrects potentially corrupted training labels during reasoning model training on the DAPO-Math benchmark using Qwen3-4B-Base. The core conceptual contribution is a taxonomy distinguishing **inactive noisy labels** (incorrect labels the model cannot generate or reinforce—reducing data efficiency but causing no direct harm) from **active noisy labels** (incorrect labels the model can generate and reinforce—steering the policy toward wrong distributions), and the discovery of **Early Correctness Coherence**, a training dynamic where accuracy on both clean and noisy samples rises similarly in early training before diverging, revealing that correct answers already emerge in rollout distributions even under noisy supervision. OLR exploits this phenomenon by replacing a noisy label with the model's own majority-voted answer when two conditions hold—a positive slope in the majority answer's rollout pass rate and historical consistency across updates—achieving average gains of 3.6%–3.9% on six in-distribution mathematical reasoning benchmarks and 3.3%–4.6% on three out-of-distribution tasks across noise ratios from 0.1 to 0.9, establishing that models can learn to reason robustly under noisy supervision only when the training process itself can surface correct answers that the original labeling failed to provide.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Noisy Labels Are Unavoidable in RLVR, but Nobody Studies Them

The central problem this paper tackles is both simple and pressing: **reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) depends on perfectly labeled training data, but perfect labels are unrealistic at scale.** Following the breakthrough of DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025), RLVR has emerged as the dominant paradigm for training reasoning-oriented language models, with algorithms like GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and its variants (Liu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 2025) enabling stable training without the complexity of learned reward models or human preference signals. The key enabler is the **verifiable reward**: for domains like mathematics and code, a rule-based checker can determine with certainty whether a generated solution is correct, providing a clean binary reward signal that avoids reward hacking and simplifies the RL pipeline.

However, this framework rests on a brittle assumption: that every training prompt comes with a ground-truth correct answer. In practice, two forces undermine this assumption:

- **Expert scarcity:** Curating large datasets of reasoning problems with verified solutions requires substantial domain expertise. For competition-level mathematics (the focus of this paper), only a limited pool of experts can produce reliable labels, creating a natural bottleneck on dataset size and quality.
- **Weak verifiers:** Even when ground-truth answers exist, the verification mechanism may be imperfect. Rule-based verifiers can have bugs, edge cases where they misclassify correct answers, or limitations in handling equivalent but syntactically different answers (Yan et al., 2025b). This introduces correctness errors into the reward signal.

The result is an unavoidable reality: **any large-scale RLVR deployment will encounter noisy labels—prompts paired with incorrect answers.** Despite the explosion of RLVR research, the paper notes that "the critical issue of learning under noisy labels remains largely unaddressed" (Section 2). This is the gap the paper aims to fill.

### Why Noisy Labels in RLVR Are Categorically Different from Traditional Classification

The paper argues that noisy label learning in RLVR is not simply an instance of a well-studied problem but a **fundamentally new challenge** requiring its own analysis, because RLVR's generation-and-rollout dynamics create mechanisms that have no analog in supervised classification.

**In traditional supervised classification**, noisy label learning has deep roots (Natarajan et al., 2013; Sukhbaatar et al., 2014) and a mature toolbox: noise transition matrices that model flipping probabilities between classes (Xiao et al., 2015; Patrini et al., 2017), loss correction techniques that regularize against overfitting to corrupted labels (Pereyra et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017), and small-loss-based sample selection methods that treat low-training-loss samples as likely clean (Han et al., 2018b; Jiang et al., 2018). These approaches all share a common structural assumption: **the label space is closed and finite** (e.g., 10 classes for CIFAR-10, 1,000 for ImageNet), and a "noisy label" means a ground-truth class has been replaced by a different class from the same fixed set.

**RLVR breaks every part of this assumption:**

- **Infinite label space:** The model generates natural language solutions, not class indices. A "noisy label" is an incorrect answer string in a combinatorially vast space of possible strings. There are no class boundaries, no fixed number of categories, and therefore no noise transition matrix to estimate—a noisy label may be any arbitrary string, not a known alternative class.

- **On-policy data generation:** In classification, the entire dataset (including noisy labels) is static and presented to the model for supervised learning. In RLVR, training data are generated *by the model itself* at each step: the policy samples K rollouts per prompt, the verifier scores them against the provided label, and the advantage computed from these scores drives the parameter update. This creates a **feedback loop between the model's capabilities and which labels influence training**—a dynamic that static classification never encounters.

- **Rollout feasibility as a gate on label influence:** This is the paper's key structural insight. In classification, "every wrong label in the training set contributes to the loss regardless of what the model thinks" (Section 1). In RLVR, a noisy label only matters if the model can actually generate it. The verifier checks whether generated solutions match the provided label; if the model never produces the noisy label, all rollouts receive equal (zero) reward, and the advantage for all is zero—the noisy label is **inactive** and contributes nothing to the gradient beyond wasting the rollouts. More dangerously, if the model *can* generate the noisy label, those rollouts receive positive reward and positive advantage, and the policy is actively steered toward the incorrect distribution—the noisy label is **active**.

This rollout-feasibility gate is what motivates the paper's taxonomy (Definitions 3.2 and 3.3). The distinction matters because it explains *why* some noisy label scenarios are more catastrophic than others, and it points to a mitigation strategy: if one can prevent the model from generating and reinforcing active noisy labels, the harm is contained. This taxonomy is conceptually novel—no prior work in noisy label learning, to the authors' knowledge, has identified or formalized this rollout-conditional influence mechanism.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three categories of existing work and explains why each cannot address the RLVR noisy label problem.

**Unsupervised RLVR methods that discard labels entirely.** A growing body of work eliminates the need for ground-truth labels entirely, using heuristics like majority voting (Zuo et al., 2025), contrastive consistency (Zhang et al., 2025b), or entropy minimization (Agarwal et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). These approaches treat all labels as untrusted and construct pseudo-labels from the model's own outputs. The paper's experiments (Table 2) show that these methods largely fail under 50% noise: TTRL and Co-Reward, the best unsupervised methods, only marginally outperform naive noisy training on in-distribution tasks, and other unsupervised methods lead to "severe model collapse" (Section 4.2). The conclusion is that "abandoning labels entirely is suboptimal"—the clean labels contain valuable signal that these methods discard, and at moderate noise ratios (0.3–0.5), a selective approach that retains clean labels while correcting noisy ones substantially outperforms fully unsupervised learning.

**Traditional noisy label learning methods ported from classification.** The paper tests several canonical approaches adapted to the RLVR setting: confidence penalty (Pereyra et al., 2017) applied at the token level, label smoothing (Lukasik et al., 2020) also at the token level, and small-loss selection (Gui et al., 2021). The results in Table 2 are striking: under active noise, only confidence penalty shows a marginal improvement (+1.1% on in-distribution benchmarks), while label smoothing actually degrades performance (−0.4%). Small-loss selection is catastrophic: it drops in-distribution performance by **17.4 percentage points** below naive GRPO training, performing worse than even random selection. The paper explains this counterintuitive failure with a specific mechanism: in RLVR, "small loss frequently signals uninformative samples (e.g., rollouts entirely correct or incorrect) rather than high label quality, offering little improvement signal" (Section 4.2). When a model's rollouts for a prompt are uniformly correct or uniformly incorrect, the advantage is zero and the loss is zero—but these samples provide no gradient for learning. Small-loss selection preferentially retains these useless samples while discarding informative ones, explaining the collapse.

**Verifier-based approaches that assume clean labels.** The standard RLVR pipeline with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and its descendants (Liu et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025) is designed around the assumption that the verifier's reference answer is correct. There is no mechanism for detecting or correcting label errors—the verifier simply compares generated rollouts against the provided label and returns a binary score. This means that under active noise, the verifier becomes an accomplice: it positively rewards the model for generating the wrong answer, and the advantage computation in Equation 1 amplifies this signal. The paper's experiments (Table 1, active noise section) show that at 90% noise, the model "collapses" below its initial baseline performance (35.5% dropping to 21.7% average in-distribution), confirming that naive GRPO has zero robustness to severe label corruption.

### The Paper's Position: Taxonomy, Phenomenon, and Self-Correction

The paper positions itself not as a rival to existing RLVR methods but as a **complementary module** that can be layered on top of any rollout-based RLVR algorithm (GRPO is used as the representative base). Its contributions are explicitly threefold, and they build logically on each other:

1. **Taxonomy (Section 3.1):** Before solving the problem, the paper argues we need to *understand* it. The inactive/active distinction based on rollout feasibility provides the first formal vocabulary for discussing noisy labels in RLVR, separating the harmless from the harmful.

2. **Phenomenon (Section 3.2):** The paper's empirical observation—that even under 50% noise, the model's majority-voted answer accuracy rises similarly for clean and noisy samples in early training (Figure 2)—is not an optimization trick but a discovery about what the model learns. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.4) formalizes this as **Early Correctness Coherence**, attributing it to positive cross-sample coupling via shared parameters: updates from correctly labeled samples improve the model's correct-answer probability on *all* samples, including noisy ones. This is the key insight that makes self-correction possible: the model already "knows" some correct answers for noisy samples; the problem is that the provided label overrides this knowledge.

3. **Method (Section 3.3):** Online Label Refinement operationalizes this insight by monitoring two signals—the slope of the majority answer's pass rate (indicating growing model confidence) and historical consistency (filtering transient majority answers)—and replacing a noisy label with the model's own majority answer when both criteria are satisfied. This is not an external denoising algorithm; it is a **self-supervised correction mechanism** that piggybacks on the model's evolving capabilities, requiring no additional model, no clean validation set, and negligible computational overhead compared to rollout generation (Appendix Table 9: "the additional overhead introduced by OLR is negligible compared with rollout generation").

The paper explicitly acknowledges its relationship to prior work on majority-voting-based pseudo-labeling (Zuo et al., 2025) but distinguishes OLR through its **selective, criteria-driven approach**. TTRL replaces all labels with majority votes unconditionally; OLR replaces only those that meet the slope and consistency thresholds, retaining the original label when the majority answer is unreliable. This selectivity is crucial—Figure 3(e) shows that removing either criterion reduces selection accuracy by approximately 20%, and Figure 3(d) shows that OLR selects roughly equal proportions from clean and noisy sets (over 40% each), meaning it does not simply filter for easy samples.

### The Practical Stakes: Why This Matters Beyond Academic Interest

The paper's motivation goes beyond taxonomy and theory. The stakes are practical and immediate:

- **Scaling RLVR to harder domains.** The current RLVR pipeline relies on curated datasets like DAPO-Math (Yu et al., 2025) with verified solutions. Extending this to domains where perfect verification is harder—competitive programming with edge-case bugs in test suites, scientific reasoning where rubric-based grading is approximate, or multi-step planning where correctness is partially subjective—requires robustness to imperfect supervision. OLR provides a mechanism for the model to course-correct when the external verifier is noisy.

- **Data-efficient training from weak supervision.** The paper implicitly argues for a new paradigm: instead of requiring expert-curated datasets with 100% correct labels, one can train from a large, cheaply labeled dataset with moderate noise and let OLR clean it during training. This is the noisy-label learning vision adapted to RLVR: leverage the model's emergent reasoning to bootstrap from imperfect data.

- **Self-improvement loops.** The finding that correct answers emerge in the model's rollouts even on noisy samples (Figure 2, Figure 5 in Appendix) suggests a broader principle: RLVR-trained models are already capable of producing better labels than they were given, even before any correction. This is the foundation for iterative self-improvement: train with OLR, use the refined labels to train a better model, repeat. The paper does not explore multi-round refinement, but the mechanism it establishes makes it natural.

The paper thus positions itself at the intersection of two active research areas—robust RL training and noisy label learning—and argues that their intersection requires new concepts (rollout feasibility, Early Correctness Coherence) that neither field has developed independently. By providing both a formal analysis (Theorem 3.4, Theorem 3.5) and a practical method (OLR) with extensive empirical validation across noise ratios, model sizes, and distribution shifts, the paper makes the case that noisy label mitigation in RLVR is a distinct, tractable, and important problem.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is primarily a **mechanism-analysis and method paper** whose core idea is that noisy labels in RLVR have a fundamentally different effect on training than in supervised classification, and that the training process itself generates correct answers for noisy samples that can be used to self-correct the supervision. The paper builds a system that monitors the model's own rollout statistics during RLVR training, detects when a noisy label is likely incorrect by watching for majority answers that gain consistent momentum, and replaces that label with the model's own emerging correct answer—all without requiring any external clean data, separate verification model, or human intervention.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components that interact in a tight training loop:

1. **Base policy** (`$\pi_\theta$`, e.g., Qwen3-4B-Base): The language model being trained to reason mathematically. At each training step, it generates `$K$` complete solution rollouts per prompt.

2. **Verifier with reference labels:** A rule-based checker that compares each generated solution against a provided reference answer `$\tilde{y}(x)$` and returns a binary reward `$r(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$`. The reference labels start as the (potentially noisy) training labels and may be overwritten by OLR.

3. **GRPO update engine:** Computes advantage scores from per-group reward normalization (`$\mu(x), \sigma(x)$`) and updates the policy parameters via a clipped surrogate objective with KL regularization. This is the base optimization algorithm, unmodified by the paper.

4. **Online Label Refinement (OLR) module:** Tracks per-sample rollout statistics (`$y^{\text{maj}}_t(x)$`, `$p^{\text{maj}}_t(x)$`) across training epochs, maintains a history `$\mathcal{H}_t(x)$`, and periodically checks two criteria—positive convergence slope `$S_t(x) > \delta_{\text{slope}}$` and historical consistency `$C_t(x)$`—to decide whether to replace the original label with the model's majority-voted answer.

Information flows as follows: prompts enter → policy generates rollouts → verifier scores against current labels (original or OLR-refined) → GRPO computes advantages → policy parameters are updated → OLR periodically evaluates pass-rate trajectories and may overwrite labels for the next epoch. The OLR label refinement and the GRPO policy update are the two alternating subroutines within each epoch.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the formal RLVR problem with noisy labels** (Definitions 3.1–3.3), because understanding *which* noisy labels cause harm and why requires the rollout feasibility concept—a gate that does not exist in classification. This taxonomy is the paper's conceptual foundation.

- **Second, the theoretical analysis of Early Correctness Coherence** (Theorem 3.4, Theorem A.1), because this explains *why* self-correction is possible: cross-sample coupling via shared parameters pushes the model toward correct answers even on noisy samples. Without this analysis, OLR would be an arbitrary heuristic.

- **Third, the OLR mechanism itself** (criteria, label replacement rule, Theorem 3.5), because the two criteria—positive convergence slope and historical consistency—are carefully chosen to detect *reliable* majority answers and filter out transient or accidental ones. This section explains what happens when and why each criterion matters.

- **Fourth, the practical instantiation** (hyperparameters, early learning phase, computational cost), because the theoretical criteria translate to concrete algorithm choices that affect robustness.

#### Formalizing Noisy Labels in RLVR: The Rollout Feasibility Taxonomy

The paper first establishes the standard RLVR setup. A policy `$\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$` is trained to generate a solution `$y$` for an input prompt `$x$`. At each training step, for each prompt `$x$`, the model samples `$K$` rollouts:

$$Y(x) = \{y^{(1)}, \ldots, y^{(K)}\} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$$

where `$K$` is the number of independent completions per prompt. A verifier provides a scalar reward `$r(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}$`, typically binary, indicating whether the generated solution matches a reference answer.

**What it computes:** For each prompt, the policy produces `$K$` candidate solutions by autoregressive sampling. Each solution is a full reasoning chain ending in a final answer. The verifier compares the extracted answer against the reference label and returns 1 (match) or 0 (no match). This yields a vector of `$K$` binary rewards per prompt.

**Why this setup:** The `$K$` rollouts per prompt are essential because GRPO computes advantages from within-group statistics—without multiple rollouts, there is no variance to normalize against. The binary reward structure reflects the "verifiable" in RLVR: domains like math and code have objective correctness criteria, so the reward is deterministic given the solution and reference answer, not learned.

The paper then uses **GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)** as the representative RLVR algorithm. GRPO computes the advantage for each rollout by normalizing rewards within the rollout group:

$$A(x, y^{(k)}) = \frac{r(x, y^{(k)}) - \mu(x)}{\sigma(x) + \epsilon}$$

where `$\mu(x) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K r(x, y^{(k)})$` is the group mean reward, `$\sigma(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K (r(x, y^{(k)}) - \mu(x))^2}$` is the group standard deviation, and `$\epsilon$` is a small constant to prevent division by zero.

**What it computes:** For each prompt `$x$`, the advantage `$A(x, y^{(k)})$` measures how much better (or worse) rollout `$y^{(k)}$` is compared to the average rollout from the same prompt. A rollout that gets reward 1 when most others get 0 receives a large positive advantage; a rollout that gets 1 when most others also get 1 receives a small (near-zero) advantage. The `$\sigma(x)$` denominator normalizes by the group's variability.

**Why this form:** Normalizing within the group removes the need for a learned value function (critic). The advantage is self-normalizing: if all rollouts for a prompt are correct, all advantages are zero regardless of absolute reward magnitude, so no update occurs—the model already handles this prompt. If rollouts split between correct and incorrect, the correct ones get positive advantage and the incorrect ones get negative advantage, driving the policy toward correct solutions. This is the key GRPO insight: by computing advantages relative to the group, the algorithm automatically scales updates based on how differentiated the current policy's outputs are.

The policy is then updated by maximizing:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_x \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \left[ \text{CLIP}\left(A(x, y) \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y \mid x)}, \epsilon\right) - \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}}] \right]$$

where CLIP is the standard PPO clipped surrogate objective (preventing the policy ratio from deviating beyond `$1 \pm \epsilon$` from 1), and `$D_{\text{KL}}$` is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the current policy and a reference policy, weighted by `$\beta$`.

**What it computes:** The expected clipped advantage-weighted log-likelihood of rollouts, minus a KL penalty that keeps the policy from drifting too far from a reference (typically the initial pretrained model). The clip operation prevents a single high-advantage rollout from causing an excessively large parameter update.

**Why this form:** The clip-and-KL combination is standard in PPO-based methods. The KL penalty is particularly important in RLVR because the action space is enormous (all possible reasoning chains in natural language)—without it, the policy could collapse to degenerate outputs that happen to match the labels. The paper does not modify this objective; OLR is a *data transformation* applied before GRPO sees the rewards, so it is compatible with any GRPO variant.

---

**The critical question the paper asks:** what happens when the reference label `$\tilde{y}(x)$` paired with prompt `$x$` is incorrect? That is, `$\tilde{y}(x) \neq y^\star(x)$`, where `$y^\star(x)$` is the true correct answer.

The paper's key structural insight is that whether an incorrect label causes harm depends on whether the policy can actually generate it. This gives rise to the central definition:

**Definition 3.1 (Rollout Feasibility):** A solution `$y$` is **rollout-feasible** under policy `$\pi_\theta$` if `$\pi_\theta(y \mid x) > 0$`—i.e., the model has non-zero probability of generating that exact answer string when prompted with `$x$`.

**Why this concept matters:** Rollout feasibility is the gatekeeper between what the verifier can reward and what it cannot. In RLVR, the verifier only provides positive reward when a generated rollout *exactly matches* the reference label. If the reference label is wrong but the model never generates that wrong answer (zero probability under `$\pi_\theta$`), then no rollout ever receives positive reward for the wrong answer, and the label's only effect is to waste the rollouts (all get zero reward, all advantages are zero). The label is effectively invisible to the gradient.

Based on rollout feasibility, the paper classifies noisy labels into two types:

**Definition 3.2 (Inactive Noisy Label):** `$\tilde{y}(x) \neq y^\star(x)$` is **inactive** if it is not rollout-feasible: `$\pi_\theta(\tilde{y} \mid x) = 0$`.

**Definition 3.3 (Active Noisy Label):** `$\tilde{y}(x) \neq y^\star(x)$` is **active** if it is rollout-feasible: `$\pi_\theta(\tilde{y} \mid x) > 0$`.

The paper notes an important caveat: "the correct labels for all samples should be rollout-feasible. If this condition is not met, the sample is invalid and requires external knowledge for the model to learn it, though this falls outside the scope of our research" (footnote in Section 3.1). This means the paper assumes the base model already has some non-zero probability of generating the right answer—the learning task is to *amplify* that probability, not to discover entirely novel capabilities.

**Why this taxonomy is novel and important:** In supervised classification, every mislabeled example contributes to the loss regardless of what the model currently predicts. The rollout-feasibility concept introduces a **policy-dependent filtering mechanism** that has no analog in traditional noisy label learning. An inactive noisy label is harmless because the model's own limitations prevent it from chasing the wrong answer; an active noisy label is dangerous precisely because the model *can* chase it. This distinction also implies that the set of active vs. inactive labels changes during training as the policy improves—a label that is inactive early in training may become active later as the model's capabilities expand. The taxonomy therefore captures a dynamic, policy-dependent property, not a static dataset property.

#### Theoretical Analysis: Early Correctness Coherence

The paper's central empirical observation, shown in Figure 2, is that **even under 50% noisy supervision, the model's accuracy on both clean and noisy samples rises similarly during early training.** Specifically, when computing the majority answer (the most common final answer across the `$K$` rollouts for a prompt) and comparing it to the true correct answer:

- On clean samples: accuracy improves from roughly 50% to over 60% in early training
- On noisy samples: accuracy also improves from roughly 50% to over 60% in the same period

This is the "Early Correctness Coherence" phenomenon: the two curves track each other closely during the initial phase, only diverging later when clean-sample accuracy continues improving while noisy-sample accuracy plateaus or declines.

The paper explains this phenomenon theoretically through **Theorem 3.4 (and its formal version, Theorem A.1)**, which attributes it to positive cross-sample coupling via shared parameters.

The key quantity being tracked is the **log-ratio** for a noisy sample `$x_n$`:

$$L_t(x_n) = \log \frac{p_t(y^\star(x_n) \mid x_n)}{p_t(\tilde{y}(x_n) \mid x_n)}$$

where `$p_t(y \mid x) = \pi_{\theta_t}(y \mid x)$` is the current policy's probability of generating answer `$y$` for prompt `$x$` at epoch `$t$`, `$y^\star(x_n)$` is the ground-truth correct answer, and `$\tilde{y}(x_n)$` is the noisy label.

**What it computes:** The log-ratio measures how much more likely the correct answer is compared to the noisy label under the current policy. If `$L_t > 0$`, the model prefers the correct answer; if `$L_t < 0$`, it prefers the noisy label; if `$L_t = 0$`, they are equally likely. The log-ratio compresses probability ratios into a symmetric scale—positive means favoring correctness, negative means collapse toward the noise.

**Why this form:** The log-ratio isolates the competition between the two answers of interest (correct vs. noisy) from all other possible outputs. The log transform makes the dynamics additive: changes in confidence multiply probabilities, which is equivalent to adding to the log-ratio. This additive structure enables the clean martingale decomposition in the proof.

The theoretical analysis makes three assumptions:

1. **Initial rollout bias:** `$p_0(y^\star) > p_0(\tilde{y})$`—the base model starts with higher probability for the correct answer than for the noisy label. This is realistic because pretrained models typically have some reasoning capability that makes correct answers more likely than arbitrary wrong answers (the paper verifies this empirically in Appendix Figure 5).

2. **Positive cross-sample coupling:** For a clean sample `$x_c$` and a noisy sample `$x_n$`,
$$\mathbb{E}_{x_c \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{clean}}, x_n \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{noise}}} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_c) \mid x_c) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_n) \mid x_n) \right] \geq \gamma > 0$$

   **What this means:** The gradient directions that increase the probability of the correct answer on a clean sample also tend to increase the probability of the correct answer on a noisy sample. The dot product `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_c) \mid x_c) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_n) \mid x_n)$` measures how aligned these two gradients are—if they point in similar directions, updates from clean samples inadvertently benefit noisy samples. The expectation `$\gamma$` is the average alignment across all clean-noisy pairs.

   **Why this assumption is plausible:** The model shares parameters across all prompts. If the correct answer to "Solve `$x^2 = 4$`" involves similar reasoning steps (isolate variable, take square root, consider both signs) as the correct answer to "Solve `$x^2 = 9$`", then gradient updates that improve performance on the clean prompt will also improve the probability distribution on the noisy prompt. The coupling is not magical—it reflects the compositional nature of reasoning skills encoded in shared neural parameters.

3. **Mean drift decomposition:** The average deterministic change in the log-ratio is `$\Delta_s = \gamma(1 - \rho)G_c - \rho G_n$`, where:
   - `$\rho = |\mathcal{D}_{\text{noise}}| / |\mathcal{D}|$` is the noise ratio
   - `$G_c$` is the average advantage magnitude on clean samples (how much correct rollouts are rewarded relative to incorrect ones)
   - `$G_n$` is the average advantage magnitude on noisy samples
   - `$\gamma(1-\rho)G_c$` is the *positive* contribution from cross-sample coupling: clean samples pull the log-ratio upward on noisy samples
   - `$\rho G_n$` is the *negative* contribution: noisy labels directly push the log-ratio downward on their own samples

   **What it computes:** The net expected change in the log-ratio per epoch. If `$\Delta_s > 0$`, the model's relative preference for the correct answer on noisy samples *increases* over time (Early Correctness Coherence). If `$\Delta_s < 0$`, the model's preference shifts toward the noisy label (active noise collapse).

From this decomposition, the paper defines a **critical noise ratio:**

$$\rho_c = \frac{\gamma G_c}{\gamma G_c + G_n}$$

If `$\rho < \rho_c$` (noise ratio below the critical threshold), then `$\Delta_s > 0$` and Early Correctness Coherence occurs. If `$\rho > \rho_c$`, active noise dominates and the model collapses toward generating wrong answers.

**Why this form:** The critical ratio `$\rho_c$` cleanly separates the regime where cross-sample coupling wins from the regime where direct noise reinforcement wins. The numerator `$\gamma G_c$` is the per-clean-sample benefit transferred to noisy samples; the denominator `$\gamma G_c + G_n$` is the total per-sample effect (benefit from clean coupling + harm from direct noise). When `$\gamma G_c$` is large (strong coupling and strong clean-sample advantages), `$\rho_c$` approaches 1—the model can tolerate very high noise. When `$G_n$` is large (strong reinforcement of noisy labels), `$\rho_c$` drops—even moderate noise can cause collapse.

The full theorem (Theorem A.1) formalizes this with finite-rollout concentration bounds (using Hoeffding and Azuma-Hoeffding inequalities to account for the stochasticity of sampling `$K$` rollouts per prompt, where `$K \gtrsim \log(T/\delta)$` ensures the empirical advantages concentrate around their expectations with probability `$1 - \delta$`). The result is a high-probability statement:

$$L_t(x) \geq L_0(x) + \eta t \left( \Delta_s - O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(T/\delta)}{K}}\right) \right)$$

meaning that for sufficiently many rollouts `$K$`, the log-ratio grows linearly with time at rate `$\Delta_s$` during early training.

**The theorem's operational implication:** "correct answers, with increasing probability, can gradually emerge even on noisy samples through cross-sample coupling." This is the theoretical justification for OLR: if the model's rollouts on noisy samples are increasingly likely to contain the correct answer (even if the noisy label is different), then monitoring the majority answer and using it to replace the label is a principled strategy rather than wishful thinking.

Appendix Figure 5 provides experimental verification of the theorem's assumptions: under 50% active or inactive noise, the model's majority-vote predictions on noisy samples show increasing correct-answer probability during early training, visualized as the proportion of green (correct), red (matching noisy label), and blue (neither) predictions.

---

#### Online Label Refinement (OLR): The Self-Correction Mechanism

The theoretical analysis establishes that correct answers *tend to emerge* in the model's rollouts on noisy samples. The OLR method operationalizes this: **identify when a majority answer is *reliably* correct and replace the original label with it.** The paper's insight is that not all majority answers are trustworthy—some are transient, low-confidence, or accidental—so simple majority-vote replacement (as in TTRL, Zuo et al., 2025) is insufficient. OLR adds two criteria designed to filter out unreliable majority answers.

---

**Step 1: Accumulating rollout statistics.**

At each epoch `$t$`, for each prompt `$x$`, the policy generates `$K$` rollouts:

$$Y_t(x) = \{y^{(1)}_t, \ldots, y^{(K)}_t\} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$$

The paper uses `$K = 8$` rollouts per prompt (from the experimental setup in Appendix B.1).

The **majority answer** is the most common final answer among these rollouts:

$$y^{\text{maj}}_t(x) = \arg\max_c |\{y \in Y_t(x) : y = c\}|$$

and its **pass rate** is the fraction of rollouts that yield this answer:

$$p^{\text{maj}}_t(x) = \frac{1}{K} |\{y \in Y_t(x) : y = y^{\text{maj}}_t(x)\}|$$

**What it computes:** For each prompt at each epoch, the model samples 8 solutions. The majority answer is the one that appears most frequently (ties broken arbitrarily). The pass rate is simply what fraction of the 8 rollouts voted for this answer—if 6 of 8 rollouts produce the same final answer, the pass rate is `$6/8 = 0.75$`.

**Why this aggregation:** Majority voting aggregates across the stochasticity of sampling. It is a simple frequentist estimate of the model's current preference: answers the model generates more often are more likely under `$\pi_\theta$`. The paper does not use the verifier's score here—the majority answer is purely a property of the model's output distribution, not of the external label.

The system maintains a **history** for each prompt:

$$\mathcal{H}_t(x) = \{(t', y^{\text{maj}}_{t'}(x), p^{\text{maj}}_{t'}(x)) : t' \in [1, t]\}$$

recording the majority answer and its pass rate at every epoch so far. This history is the raw material for the two OLR criteria.

---

**Step 2: Criterion 1 — Positive Convergence Slope.**

The first criterion asks: **is the model becoming more confident in this majority answer over time?** If the majority answer is correct, we expect that as training progresses, more rollouts converge to it—the pass rate should trend upward. If the majority answer is an artifact of noise or an incorrect but initially popular answer, the pass rate should not consistently increase.

The paper formalizes this by fitting a linear regression to the pass-rate trajectory. Let:

$$\mathbf{p}_t(x) = [p^{\text{maj}}_1(x), \ldots, p^{\text{maj}}_t(x)]^\top$$

be the vector of pass rates from epoch 1 to `$t$`, and let `$\mathbf{t} = [1, \ldots, t]^\top$` be the corresponding epoch indices. The **slope** of the best-fit line is:

$$S_t(x) = \frac{(\mathbf{t} - \bar{t}\mathbf{1})^\top (\mathbf{p}_t(x) - \bar{p}\mathbf{1})}{(\mathbf{t} - \bar{t}\mathbf{1})^\top (\mathbf{t} - \bar{t}\mathbf{1})}$$

where `$\bar{t} = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^t i$` is the mean epoch index and `$\bar{p} = \frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^t p^{\text{maj}}_i(x)$` is the mean pass rate.

**What it computes:** Standard ordinary least squares slope of the pass rate over time. The numerator is the covariance between time and pass rate; the denominator is the variance of time. A positive slope means the pass rate has been increasing on average; a negative or near-zero slope means it has been flat or declining.

**Why this form:** Linear regression is the simplest parametric model for trend detection. The paper needs to distinguish between pass rates that are genuinely increasing (indicating emerging correctness) versus those that are merely fluctuating. A positive slope is evidence that the model is converging toward this answer with growing confidence, which is exactly the signature predicted by Early Correctness Coherence for the correct answer on noisy samples.

The criterion is:

$$S_t(x) > \delta_{\text{slope}}$$

where `$\delta_{\text{slope}} = 0.05$` is the slope threshold (tuned via the sensitivity analysis in Figure 4a). If the slope exceeds this threshold, the majority answer passes the first check.

**Why this threshold:** Too low a threshold (e.g., `$\delta_{\text{slope}} = 0.0$`) allows even flat or barely-increasing pass rates to trigger replacement, introducing erroneous majority answers. Too high a threshold (e.g., `$\delta_{\text{slope}} = 0.2$`) filters out too many samples, providing insufficient refinement. Figure 4a shows that `$\delta_{\text{slope}} = 0.05$` is near-optimal for most settings.

---

**Step 3: Criterion 2 — Historical Consistency.**

The second criterion asks: **is this majority answer stable, or is it a one-epoch fluke?** Even with a positive slope, a majority answer might change from epoch to epoch (the model converges to answer A at epoch 5, then shifts to answer B at epoch 6). Replacing the label with a transient majority answer would introduce new errors.

The paper checks consistency by comparing the current majority answer with the **historical majority**:

$$y^{\text{hist}}_t(x) = \arg\max_y |\{(t', y^{\text{maj}}_{t'}(x)) \in \mathcal{H}_t(x) : y^{\text{maj}}_{t'}(x) = y\}|$$

**What it computes:** Across all epochs recorded in `$\mathcal{H}_t(x)$`, which answer has appeared most frequently as the majority answer? This is the answer that has been the consensus most consistently throughout training, regardless of whether it was the majority in any single recent epoch.

The consistency indicator is:

$$C_t(x) = \mathbb{I}\left[ y^{\text{maj}}_t(x) = y^{\text{hist}}_t(x) \right]$$

which is 1 if the current majority answer matches the historical majority, and 0 otherwise.

**Why this criterion matters:** The slope criterion alone can be fooled. Imagine a noisy sample where the model vacillates between answer A (epochs 1–3) and answer B (epochs 4–6). The pass rate for B might increase from epochs 4–6, producing a positive slope, but B is not historically consistent—A was the earlier majority. The consistency check prevents OLR from chasing the most recent trend at the expense of long-term stability. It embodies the intuition that a truly correct answer should be *persistently* high-confidence, not just *recently* high-confidence.

Figure 3(e) in the paper demonstrates the importance of both criteria: under active noise, OLR with both criteria achieves nearly 100% selection accuracy (the fraction of replaced labels that actually match the correct answer). Removing the slope criterion drops accuracy by approximately 20%, and removing the consistency criterion drops accuracy by a similar amount. The two criteria are complementary and non-redundant.

---

**Step 4: Label replacement rule.**

After an initial **early learning phase** of `$T$` epochs (where the model is allowed to train on the original labels without any OLR intervention, to accumulate enough rollout history for meaningful statistics), the effective label used for reward computation becomes:

$$
\hat{y}_t(x) =
\begin{cases}
y^{\text{maj}}_t(x) & \text{if } S_t(x) > \delta_{\text{slope}} \text{ and } C_t(x) = 1, \\
\tilde{y}(x) & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$

**What it computes:** At each epoch `$t$` (for `$t > T$`), after generating rollouts and computing majority answers, the system checks both criteria. If both pass (positive slope AND historical consistency), the original label `$\tilde{y}(x)$` is replaced with the model's own majority answer for reward computation in the next GRPO update. If either criterion fails, the original label is retained unchanged.

**Why this gating:** OLR is conservative by design. It only overrides the external label when the model provides strong, consistent evidence that the external label is wrong. The paper reports (from Figure 3d) that OLR selects "over 40% of samples from both clean and noisy sets" for replacement. This is important: OLR does not only replace noisy labels—it may also replace clean labels with the same (correct) majority answer, which is harmless. The key statistic is the *accuracy* of the replacements: on selected samples, the majority answer accuracy "exceeds 90% and approaches 100%" (Figure 3b), meaning OLR almost never replaces a correct label with a wrong one.

The early learning phase `$T$` is set to 5 epochs in the main experiments. The sensitivity analysis in Figure 4a sweeps `$T \in \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10\}$` and finds that `$T = 5$` provides the best in-distribution performance. A smaller `$T$` causes inaccurate slope estimation (not enough data points for the regression); a larger `$T$` prolongs training with noisy labels before correction begins, allowing active noise to do more damage.

---

**Step 5: Theoretical guarantee for OLR (Theorem 3.5).**

The paper provides a formal guarantee that OLR improves noise tolerance. Let `$\Delta$` be the probability that a noisy prompt satisfies both OLR criteria and triggers label replacement. When replacement occurs, the selected label matches the correct answer with high probability:

$$\Pr(\hat{y}_t(x) = y^\star(x)) \geq 1 - \epsilon, \quad \epsilon = O\left(\exp\left(-K\Delta_p^2\right)\right)$$

where `$\Delta_p = p^\star - \max_{c \neq y^\star} p_c$` is the probability gap between the correct answer and the most likely competing (incorrect) answer under the current policy.

**What this means in plain language:** If the correct answer has a clear probability advantage over any wrong answer (`$\Delta_p$` is large), then with `$K$` rollouts, the majority answer is exponentially likely to be correct. The `$\exp(-K\Delta_p^2)$` term comes from Chernoff bounds: the probability that an incorrect answer gets more votes than the correct answer decays exponentially with the number of rollouts and the squared probability gap.

**Why this matters for noise tolerance:** The **effective noise ratio** after OLR is:

$$\rho_{\text{eff}} = \rho(1 - \Delta) < \rho$$

because a fraction `$\Delta$` of noisy samples have their labels corrected to (probably) the right answer. This increases the **critical noise ratio** from Theorem 3.4 to:

$$\rho_c^{\text{OLR}} = \frac{\rho_c}{1 - \Delta} > \rho_c$$

meaning the system can tolerate higher initial noise ratios before collapsing. If `$\rho_c = 0.5$` (without OLR, 50% noise is the maximum tolerable) and `$\Delta = 0.4$` (OLR corrects 40% of noisy labels), then `$\rho_c^{\text{OLR}} = 0.5 / 0.6 \approx 0.83$`—the system can now tolerate 83% noise.

**The proof** (Appendix A.8) proceeds in three steps: (1) correctness of selected labels (Chernoff bound plus union bound over competing answers), (2) effective noise ratio reduction (direct algebra from the definition of `$\rho_{\text{eff}}$`), and (3) increased tolerable threshold (substituting `$\rho_{\text{eff}}$` into the stability condition from Theorem A.4). The argument is clean and self-contained, requiring only the earlier theoretical framework plus standard concentration inequalities.

---

#### Practical Instantiation and Computational Cost

**Integration with GRPO.** OLR is a preprocessing step applied to the labels before GRPO computes rewards. The GRPO update itself is unmodified—the only change is that the reference label `$\tilde{y}(x)$` may be replaced by `$\hat{y}_t(x)$`. This means OLR is compatible with any rollout-based RLVR algorithm, not just GRPO.

**Hyperparameters.** The paper provides specific values for all OLR-specific hyperparameters:

- Number of rollouts per prompt: `$K = 8$`
- Slope threshold: `$\delta_{\text{slope}} = 0.05$`
- Early learning phase duration: `$T = 5$` epochs
- Maximum response length: 4096 tokens
- Learning rate for GRPO: `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`
- KL loss weight: `$1 \times 10^{-3}$`
- Training temperature: 1.0 (for rollout generation during training)
- Evaluation temperature: 0.6 (for validation and testing)

These values are from the setup description in Appendix B.1. The sensitivity analysis in Section 4.2 and Figures 4a-b validates that performance is reasonably robust around these choices, though out-of-distribution performance under active noise shows no clear trend with respect to `$\delta_{\text{slope}}$` or `$T$`.

**Computational overhead.** The paper explicitly addresses the cost of OLR. The dominant cost in RLVR is rollout generation: `$O(NKL)$` per epoch for `$N$` prompts, `$K$` rollouts, and average rollout length `$L$`. OLR adds two operations:

1. **Majority-answer counting:** For each prompt, count which answer appears most frequently among the `$K$` rollouts. This is `$O(K)$` per prompt.

2. **Statistics maintenance:** Update the pass-rate trajectory and check the two criteria. This can be done incrementally in `$O(1)$` per prompt by maintaining running sums for the regression.

The total additional cost is `$O(NK)$` per epoch, which is negligible compared to `$O(NKL)$` for generation (since `$L$` is typically hundreds or thousands of tokens). Appendix Table 9 reports empirical timing: "training on 800 prompts takes about 3.8 hours, with negligible overhead from OLR."

**Noise injection methodology.** The paper's experimental design for creating noisy labels is worth detailing because it differs between inactive and active noise:

- **Inactive noise:** For prompts assigned to the noisy subset, the correct label is replaced with "strings that the model is unlikely to output" (Section 4.1). These are essentially random or nonsense strings that have very low or zero probability under the base model. This ensures `$\pi_\theta(\tilde{y} \mid x) \approx 0$`—the definition of inactive noise.

- **Active noise:** Static construction is impractical because "the probability that noisy labels actually appear during rollout depends on both the noise ratio and the model's output probability" (Section 4.1). Therefore, the paper constructs active noise labels **dynamically during inference** by replacing correct labels with incorrect answers that the model *actually generates* in an on-policy manner. This means that when the policy generates rollouts, some of its own incorrect outputs are selected as the active noisy labels for future epochs.

**Why dynamic active noise construction matters:** It ensures that the active noisy labels are genuinely rollout-feasible—they are literally answers the model just produced. This is a more realistic simulation of what happens when a human annotator provides a plausible-but-wrong answer that the model might independently generate. Static active noise (e.g., replacing with a known common mistake) might not be rollout-feasible for the specific model being trained, making it effectively inactive. The dynamic construction guarantees that the taxonomy distinction is meaningful in the experiments.

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Rollout feasibility as the organizing concept:** Rather than treating all noisy labels uniformly (as classification does), the paper distinguishes them by whether the current policy can generate them. This captures the RLVR-specific feedback loop and enables targeted mitigation.

- **Majority voting as the correct-answer detector (not verifier scores):** The verifier is contaminated by the noisy label—it will confirm that the noisy label matches itself. Majority voting across rollouts is label-independent: it aggregates what the model *thinks*, not what the verifier *says*. This independence is crucial—if OLR used verifier scores to select candidate answers, it would be circular under active noise.

- **Two complementary criteria (slope + consistency) rather than simple majority:** The ablation in Figure 3(e) and Figure 4b shows that both criteria are necessary—removing either drops selection accuracy by ~20%. The slope detects emerging convergence (a dynamic property), while consistency filters transient answers (a static property). Together they approximate the theoretical condition that a correct answer should be both increasingly likely and persistently dominant.

- **Early learning phase before OLR activation:** Training for `$T = 5$` epochs with original labels before starting refinement serves two purposes. First, it allows the policy to improve from clean-label samples, building the cross-sample coupling that makes correct answers emerge on noisy samples. Second, it accumulates enough history for meaningful slope and consistency computation—with only 1–2 data points, the regression is too noisy.

- **Conservative replacement (only when both criteria pass):** OLR retains the original label unless it has strong evidence to override it. This means some noisy labels persist throughout training (those where the majority answer never becomes both convergent and consistent). But the alternative—aggressive replacement of any noisy label with a potentially wrong majority answer—would be worse, as the ablation removing the consistency criterion shows.

- **Unsupervised but not fully unsupervised:** OLR uses the labels as a starting point and selectively refines them, rather than discarding them entirely (as TTRL does). This preserves the signal from genuinely clean labels while correcting the noisy ones, which the paper shows is more effective than fully unsupervised approaches across moderate noise ratios (Table 2).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Rollout Feasibility as a First-Class Concept for Understanding Noisy Label Harm

The paper's most foundational contribution is not a method but a **diagnostic concept**: the idea that a noisy label's capacity to cause damage depends on whether the current policy can generate it. This is captured in the rollout feasibility definition (Definition 3.1) and the resulting taxonomy of inactive vs. active noisy labels (Definitions 3.2–3.3). The innovation here is conceptual, not algorithmic—it reframes the problem in a way that makes previously puzzling phenomena legible.

**Before this work**, the noisy label literature treated label corruption uniformly: a wrong label is a wrong label, and its harm scales with the corruption rate. This assumption is baked into every loss-correction, sample-selection, and noise-transition-matrix method from the classification literature (Natarajan et al., 2013; Patrini et al., 2017; Han et al., 2018b). Even work on RLVR implicitly carried this assumption forward—when training with verifiable rewards, a mislabeled prompt was assumed to be categorically harmful, with the only question being how robust the optimization is to it.

**The insight this paper contributes** is that RLVR's generation-and-rollout loop creates a **policy-dependent filter** with no analog in static supervised learning. In classification, every training example contributes to the loss regardless of what the model currently predicts. In RLVR, the verifier only rewards a generated rollout if it matches the reference label; if the model never generates the noisy label (`$\pi_\theta(\tilde{y} \mid x) = 0$`), then that label contributes zero advantage and zero gradient. It is effectively invisible to training—reducing data efficiency by wasting rollouts but causing no direct steering toward wrong answers. This means that **the harm from a noisy label is not a static property of the dataset but a dynamic property of the model-dataset interaction**: a label that is inactive at epoch 1 may become active at epoch 10 as the model's capabilities expand, and vice versa.

**Why this is a fundamental shift, not incremental:** The rollout feasibility concept changes *what kind of question one asks* about noisy labels in RLVR. Instead of asking "what fraction of my labels are wrong?" one should ask "what fraction of my wrong labels can my model actually generate, and under what conditions do they become feasible?" This shifts the mitigation strategy from universal denoising (treat all noisy labels as equally harmful) to **targeted suppression of active noise** (prevent the model from generating and reinforcing wrong answers it is capable of producing). The paper's experiments confirm the practical relevance: under inactive noise at 50%, naive GRPO maintains 36.1% average in-distribution accuracy—substantial degradation from clean-label performance but far from collapse. Under active noise at 50%, that drops to 35.5%—similar in aggregate but with distinctly different per-benchmark patterns (Table 1). At 90% active noise, the model collapses to 21.7% average, below its initial 24.8% baseline, while 90% inactive noise merely flattens to 36.2%. The taxonomy captures these divergent behaviors.

This concept also provides a unifying explanation for why unsupervised RLVR methods like TTRL and Co-Reward fail at moderate noise ratios (Table 2): they treat *all* labels as untrusted, discarding the valuable signal from clean labels that the rollout-feasibility analysis shows can be safely retained as long as the noise they would reinforce remains inactive.

---

### Innovation 2: Early Correctness Coherence as a Discovered Training Dynamic with Theoretical Grounding

The paper's second contribution is the discovery and formalization of **Early Correctness Coherence**—the empirical observation that "accuracy on both clean and noisy samples increases similarly in early training" (Section 3.2) before the two curves diverge—and its theoretical explanation via cross-sample coupling. This is significant not merely as an observation but as a **diagnostic finding that reveals something non-obvious about what RLVR-trained models learn.**

**The dominant assumption in noisy label learning** is that models eventually memorize noisy labels, causing a characteristic "early learning then overfitting" pattern where performance on clean validation data peaks and then degrades (Arpit et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2020). The expectation, carried into RLVR, would be that noisy samples degrade monotonically—the model starts uncertain, then gradually learns the wrong answers, harming performance. What the paper shows instead (Figure 2) is that **correct answers emerge on noisy samples even while training against wrong labels**. The majority-voted accuracy on noisy samples rises from ~50% to over 60% during early training, tracking the clean-sample curve almost perfectly before plateauing. The model is not learning the wrong answer; it is learning the right answer *despite* being told it's wrong.

**The theoretical mechanism** (Theorem 3.4) attributes this to positive cross-sample coupling via shared parameters: gradient updates that increase the probability of correct answers on clean-labeled prompts also increase the probability of correct answers on noisy-labeled prompts, because the reasoning skills are compositional and parameter-shared. The dot-product condition `$\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_c) \mid x_c) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_n) \mid x_n)] \geq \gamma > 0$` is the formalization: the directions in parameter space that help on clean problems also help on noisy problems, even though the noisy problems' labels point elsewhere.

**Why this is a fundamental finding, not just a phenomenon:** It inverts the conventional framing of noisy labels from "corruption to be cleaned" to "latent signal to be surfaced." The model already contains correct answers for noisy samples in its rollout distribution; the problem is that the external verifier suppresses them in favor of the (wrong) reference label. This reframing makes self-correction *principled* rather than heuristic: if correct answers are emerging, the natural strategy is to detect and amplify them, not to discard the noisy samples or downweight their loss.

The paper also provides the **critical noise ratio** `$\rho_c = \gamma G_c / (\gamma G_c + G_n)$`, which cleanly partitions the parameter space into a regime where cross-sample coupling dominates (Early Correctness Coherence, `$\rho < \rho_c$`) and a regime where direct noise reinforcement dominates (active noise collapse, `$\rho > \rho_c$`). This is a predictive theoretical result, not merely an empirical curve fit—it makes specific claims about what determines the boundary (coupling strength `$\gamma$`, clean and noisy advantage magnitudes `$G_c$` and `$G_n$`) that could be tested in future work with controlled manipulations.

**Evidence anchors:** Figure 2 shows the empirical phenomenon across both noise types. Figure 5 in Appendix C provides a granular decomposition: under 50% inactive noise, the model's predictions on noisy samples shift from matching the noisy label (red) toward the correct answer (green) over training, visually confirming that cross-sample coupling is redirecting the model away from the provided supervision and toward correctness. The similarity of the active and inactive noise curves in early training (both rising from ~50% to ~60–70% before diverging) supports the claim that the coupling mechanism is noise-type-agnostic during the early phase.

---

### Innovation 3: A Criterion-Based Label Refinement Strategy That Distinguishes Reliable from Unreliable Self-Correction

The paper's third contribution is the design of **Online Label Refinement (OLR)**—specifically, the *criteria* that govern when to trust the model's own majority answer over the external label. What makes this distinctive is not majority voting itself (which is standard; Zuo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024b) but the **dual-criterion filtering mechanism** that separates reliable emerging correct answers from transient or accidental majority answers.

**Prior work on pseudo-labeling** in RLVR used unconditional replacement: TTRL (Zuo et al., 2025) replaces all labels with majority-voted answers, effectively discarding the original supervision entirely. The paper's experiments (Table 2) show this is suboptimal—TTRL underperforms OLR by 8.0 percentage points on in-distribution benchmarks under inactive noise, and fully unsupervised methods lead to "severe model collapse" in several cases. The failure mode is intuitive: majority voting is unreliable when the model's confidence is low or fluctuating, and replacing a correct label with a wrong majority answer is destructive.

**The innovation in OLR's criteria** is that they operationalize the theoretical conditions for reliability. The **positive convergence slope** criterion (`$S_t(x) > \delta_{\text{slope}}$`) tests whether the model is becoming *more confident* over time—a dynamic proxy for the theoretical claim from Theorem 3.4 that `$p_t(y^\star \mid x)$` should increase during early training through cross-sample coupling. A flat or declining pass rate suggests the majority answer is not gaining momentum, while a positive slope indicates convergence. The **historical consistency** criterion (`$C_t(x) = 1$`) tests whether the majority answer is *stable* across epochs rather than a recent fluctuation. Together, these criteria approximate the joint condition that a correct answer should be both increasingly likely (captured by the convergence slope) and persistently dominant (captured by consistency).

**Why this is conceptually novel, not just hyperparameter tuning:** The two criteria correspond to two distinct failure modes of naive majority-vote replacement, and the paper demonstrates that *both failure modes are real and consequential*. The ablation in Figure 3(e) shows that removing the slope criterion drops selection accuracy by ~20 percentage points, and removing the consistency criterion drops it by a similar amount. The degradation is comparable in magnitude, indicating the two criteria protect against *different* classes of error. The slope criterion filters out answers that are popular but not gaining traction (e.g., an easy-but-wrong answer the model initially generates often but abandons as it improves). The consistency criterion filters out answers that are recently popular but historically unstable (e.g., a transient convergence to a wrong answer during a brief phase of training). No prior work in RLVR noisy label mitigation has articulated or addressed these distinct failure modes.

**Evidence anchors:** Figure 3(d) shows that OLR selects roughly equal proportions of clean and noisy samples (~40%+ each), meaning it does not simply filter for easy samples or rely on the original label distribution for its selections. Figure 3(b) shows that for selected samples, majority-answer accuracy exceeds 90% and approaches 100%, while unselected samples remain below 70%—confirming that the criteria successfully isolate the high-confidence correct answers. Figure 4(b) and Table 8 reinforce that both criteria are individually necessary.

---

### Innovation 4: Verifier Over-Optimization Reframed as a Taxonomy-Grounded Steering Problem in RLVR

The paper's fourth contribution is more subtle but important for future research: it reframes what was previously seen as "reward hacking" or "over-optimization" in RLVR as a **taxonomy-grounded problem of active noise amplification.** The paper does not claim to solve verifier over-optimization, but it provides the conceptual vocabulary to understand it as a specific case of a broader phenomenon.

**The standard framing of RLVR failure modes** focuses on the verifier: if the reward signal is imperfect, the policy will exploit it, generating solutions that score well under the verifier but are actually incorrect (analogous to reward hacking in RLHF). This framing treats the problem as one of *reward quality*—improve the verifier, and the problem goes away.

**The paper's reframing** shifts attention from the verifier to the **interaction between the verifier and the policy's generative capabilities.** An active noisy label is exactly a verifier imperfection that the policy can *realize*—the verifier provides positive reward for a wrong answer, and the policy has non-zero probability of generating that answer, so the two conspire to create a reinforcement loop. An inactive noisy label is a verifier imperfection that the policy *cannot* realize—the verifier is equally wrong, but the policy's limitations prevent it from exploiting the error. The same verifier flaw can be harmful or harmless depending on the policy's current capabilities.

**Why this matters beyond the paper's specific method:** It suggests that robustness to label noise in RLVR should be understood as a **joint property of verifier accuracy and policy coverage**, not just verifier accuracy alone. Even a perfect verifier can be rendered harmful by a policy that generates plausible-but-wrong answers (active noise created on-policy), and even an imperfect verifier can be harmless if the policy's output distribution avoids its errors (inactive noise by construction). This reframing has downstream implications: (1) verifier improvement efforts should focus on errors that are rollout-feasible for the current or anticipated policy, not uniformly on all errors; (2) policy constraints (e.g., KL regularization to a reference model) can function as a defense against active noise by limiting how far the policy can drift toward generating verifier-exploiting outputs; and (3) the difficulty of constructing realistic active noise for experiments (which the paper addresses via dynamic on-policy construction) reflects a deeper truth—active noise is inherently policy-dependent and cannot be simulated in advance.

**Evidence anchors:** The sharp divergence between active and inactive noise at high ratios (Table 1, `$\rho = 0.9$`): 36.2% vs. 21.7% in-distribution—a 14.5 percentage point gap driven entirely by whether the noisy labels are rollout-feasible. The collapse under active noise at 90% (below baseline) vs. mere plateauing under inactive noise is a direct demonstration of the taxonomy's predictive power. The small-loss selection failure (Table 2, −17.4 percentage points) further supports the reframing: small loss in RLVR often means the advantage is zero (all rollouts correct or all incorrect), which provides no learning signal. This is a *different* mechanism than small-loss = clean-label in classification, and it only becomes visible when one thinks in terms of the generation-verification interaction rather than static label quality.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All training experiments use the DAPO-Math dataset (Yu et al., 2025), from which the paper samples 800 instances for the main experiments (default setting) and 4,000 instances for a scalability study. Noisy labels are injected synthetically at ratios of `{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9}`. For inactive noise, correct labels are replaced with "strings that the model is unlikely to output"—essentially random or nonsense answers that have near-zero probability under the base model. For active noise, incorrect labels are constructed **dynamically and on-policy** during inference: the correct label is replaced with an incorrect answer that the model actually generates during rollout, guaranteeing rollout feasibility. The paper notes (Section 4.1, footnote 2) that static active noise construction is impractical because "the probability that noisy labels actually appear during rollout depends on both the noise ratio and the model's output probability." For example, with 50% active noise (400/800 samples) and 50% model confidence on these noisy labels, rolling out each sample 8 times gives only `(1 - 0.5^8)^400 ≈ 0.21` probability that all noisy samples output noisy labels—illustrating why dynamic on-policy construction is necessary to maintain the active/inactive distinction.

- **Base model(s).** The primary model is **Qwen3-4B-Base** (Yang et al., 2025), a 4-billion parameter pretrained language model. The paper also reports experiments with **Qwen3-8B-Base** (8B parameters, Appendix C, Table 4) and **DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B** (Grattafiori et al., 2024; Appendix C, Table 5) to demonstrate that OLR's benefits transfer across model scales and families. Qwen3-4B-Base was chosen because it is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (implicitly from the experimental setup) and sits in a regime where reasoning performance is non-trivial but far from saturation, leaving substantial room for test-time and training-time interventions to matter.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **evaluation accuracy** on mathematical reasoning benchmarks, reported as **avg@32** (average across 32 samples) for small test sets (AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC) and **pass@1** for larger test sets (MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench, and all out-of-distribution tasks). For AIME and AMC, these are competition problems where the test set contains only 30 problems each, so averaging over multiple samples reduces variance. For MATH-500 (500 problems) and the others, pass@1 is a single-sample estimate. All answers are graded using exact string matching against ground-truth answers, consistent with standard mathematical reasoning evaluation.

- **Baselines.** Two categories are compared (detailed in Section 4.1 and Appendix B.2):
  - **Unsupervised methods (no ground-truth labels):** (1) TTRL (Zuo et al., 2025), which uses majority-voted outputs as pseudo-labels; (2) Co-Reward (Zhang et al., 2025b), which leverages contrastive consistency to prevent collapse in unsupervised RLVR; (3) Self-Certainty (Zhao et al., 2025), which promotes high-confidence predictions via maximizing KL divergence; (4) Token-Level Entropy (Agarwal et al., 2025), which minimizes token-level entropy; (5) Sentence-Level Entropy (Agarwal et al., 2025), which maximizes sentence-level likelihood.
  - **Traditional noisy label learning methods and regularization:** (6) Confidence Penalty (Pereyra et al., 2017), applied at the token level to discourage overconfident predictions; (7) Label Smoothing (Lukasik et al., 2020), applied at the token level to replace one-hot targets with smoothed distributions; (8) Small-loss Selection (Gui et al., 2021), which treats low-loss samples as clean and prioritizes them; (9) Random Selection, which randomly samples training instances as a control variant.

  The baseline against which all improvements are measured is **naive GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024) trained directly on the noisy labels without any denoising or mitigation. The base model's initial performance (before any RLVR training) is also reported for context.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The unit of computation is one **training epoch** with `K = 8` rollouts per prompt and a maximum response length of 4,096 tokens. All methods are compared at the same number of training steps and rollouts—there is no differential compute allocation. OLR adds negligible overhead: majority counting is `O(K)` per prompt, and statistics maintenance is `O(1)` incremental. The paper reports (Appendix B.1, Table 9) that training on 800 prompts takes ~3.8 hours with OLR, and "the additional overhead introduced by OLR is almost negligible" compared to the `O(NKL)` cost of rollout generation. All experiments use 8 × NVIDIA A100 (80G) GPUs.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is used for model selection—the paper uses a fixed training set, trains once per configuration, and evaluates on the held-out benchmarks. The sensitivity analysis for `δ_slope` and `T` (Section 4.2, Figures 4a-b) sweeps hyperparameters across a range and reports the best-performing configuration rather than using a validation-based selection protocol. This is standard for RLVR work but means the reported gains may be slightly optimistic relative to a setting where hyperparameters are chosen on a separate validation set. The paper does not report confidence intervals or standard errors across multiple training runs.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### How Does OLR Perform Under Different Noise Ratios?

The headline result appears in **Table 1**, which reports post-training accuracy for Qwen3-4B-Base under noise ratios from 0.1 to 0.9, split by inactive vs. active noise, across six in-distribution and three out-of-distribution benchmarks.

**Under inactive noise**, OLR provides consistent but modest gains at low noise ratios (0.1: +0.4% ID, +1.2% OOD) and substantial gains at moderate-to-high ratios. The peak improvement occurs at `ρ = 0.5`, where OLR achieves +8.8% average on in-distribution benchmarks (36.1% → 44.9%) and +3.8% on out-of-distribution (57.2% → 61.0%). At `ρ = 0.7`, gains moderate to +2.1% ID and +3.5% OOD. At `ρ = 0.9`, gains shrink to +1.2% ID and +5.4% OOD—the model still improves but the margin narrows. Across all ratios, OLR's average improvement is **+3.6% on in-distribution** and **+3.3% on out-of-distribution** benchmarks. Notably, even at `ρ = 0.5`, OLR maintains performance (44.9% ID) comparable to training at `ρ = 0.1` without OLR (44.5% ID), effectively neutralizing the impact of 40 percentage points of additional noise.

The per-benchmark breakdown reveals heterogeneity: on AIME24 at `ρ = 0.3`, OLR nearly doubles accuracy from 12.9% to 23.8%; on MATH-500 at `ρ = 0.5`, it recovers from 76.0% to 84.2%. However, on AIME25 at `ρ = 0.1`, OLR *decreases* accuracy from 20.4% to 19.6%—a small but notable negative result suggesting that at very low noise, the default GRPO may already be close to optimal and OLR's label refinements risk replacing some correct labels with near-correct but not exact majority answers. The out-of-distribution gains are particularly striking at high noise: at `ρ = 0.9`, OLR improves ARC-c from 76.5% to 85.9% (+9.4 points) and GPQA-diamond from 27.3% to 30.3% (+3.0 points).

**Under active noise**, the baseline degradation is much steeper, and OLR's gains are correspondingly larger in absolute terms. At `ρ = 0.5`, naive GRPO drops to 35.5% ID and 49.1% OOD, while OLR recovers to 41.9% ID (+6.4%) and 53.9% OOD (+4.8%). At `ρ = 0.9`, the baseline collapses to 21.7% ID (below the initial model's 24.8%) and 25.8% OOD (below 27.7%), while OLR achieves 25.1% ID (+3.4%) and 26.5% OOD (+0.7%)—a modest recovery but insufficient to prevent model degradation below the baseline. Across all ratios, OLR's average improvement is **+3.9% on in-distribution** and **+4.6% on out-of-distribution** benchmarks.

The pattern of gains differs from inactive noise: under active noise, the largest ID gains occur at `ρ = 0.5` (+6.4%), but the largest OOD gains occur at `ρ = 0.7` (+8.1%), driven primarily by ARC-c jumping from 66.2% to 80.1% (+13.9 points). At `ρ = 0.1`, OLR already provides substantial gains (+5.8% ID, +3.6% OOD), unlike under inactive noise where `ρ = 0.1` gains were minimal—suggesting that even a small fraction of active noisy labels causes noticeable harm that OLR can partially reverse, while a small fraction of inactive noisy labels is largely harmless.

The paper notes a critical failure mode at `ρ = 0.9` under active noise: on AIME24, OLR performs *worse* than naive GRPO (7.1% vs. 8.3%), and on AIME25, it ties (5.8% vs. 5.8%). At this extreme noise level, the majority of labels are both wrong and rollout-feasible, so the model's own majority answers—OLR's correction candidates—are themselves predominantly incorrect, making self-correction unreliable.

**Key observations from the difficulty-ratio interaction:** The inactive noise results demonstrate a "sweet spot" for OLR around `ρ = 0.3` to `ρ = 0.7`, where there are enough clean samples to drive cross-sample coupling and enough noisy samples to benefit from correction. At `ρ = 0.1`, there is little to correct; at `ρ = 0.9`, the clean signal is too weak to drive robust majority answers. Under active noise, the sweet spot shifts rightward (`ρ = 0.3` to `ρ = 0.5`) because even low active noise ratios cause proportionally more harm that OLR can address.

#### How Does OLR Perform Compared to Baseline Methods?

**Table 2** presents the core comparison at `ρ = 0.5` noise ratio, separated by inactive and active noise, with all methods trained on Qwen3-4B-Base.

**Under inactive noise** (`ρ = 0.5`), naive GRPO achieves 36.1% ID and 57.2% OOD. The best unsupervised method, TTRL, achieves 36.9% ID and 57.7% OOD—only marginally above naive training (+0.8% ID, +0.5% OOD). Co-Reward performs similarly (36.6% ID, 57.1% OOD). Self-Certainty, Token-Entropy, and Sentence-Entropy all collapse: Self-Certainty drops to 20.6% ID and 22.3% OOD (below the base model's 24.8% and 27.7%), demonstrating that "most unsupervised methods lead to severe model collapse" as the paper states. Among traditional noisy label methods, Confidence Penalty provides a moderate gain (+2.0% ID, +1.2% OOD), Label Smoothing provides a marginal gain (+1.8% ID, +0.7% OOD), Small-loss Selection shows a slight gain on ID (+0.5%) but degrades OOD (−5.6%), and Random Selection provides gains on ID (+1.9%) but degrades OOD (−2.2%).

**OLR achieves 44.9% ID and 61.0% OOD**, representing improvements of **+8.8% ID** and **+3.8% OOD** over naive GRPO. Relative to the best unsupervised method (TTRL), OLR provides +8.0% ID and +3.3% OOD. Relative to the best traditional method (Confidence Penalty), OLR provides +6.8% ID and +2.6% OOD.

**Under active noise** (`ρ = 0.5`), the landscape shifts. Naive GRPO achieves 35.5% ID and 49.1% OOD. Unsupervised methods are not directly tested under active noise with OLR in Table 2—the table splits the active noise section to compare OLR against traditional methods only. Confidence Penalty provides a +1.1% ID gain and a substantial +7.1% OOD gain (driven by GPQA-diamond improvement from 24.7% to 32.3% and ARC-c from 73.9% to 82.9%). Label Smoothing degrades ID (−0.4%) and OOD (−6.7%), performing worse than naive training. Small-loss Selection collapses catastrophically: −17.4% ID and −26.2% OOD, dropping from 35.5% to 18.1% on ID and from 49.1% to 22.9% on OOD. Random Selection also degrades (−0.7% ID, −13.6% OOD), largely due to ARC-c collapsing from 73.9% to 45.6%.

**OLR achieves 41.9% ID and 53.9% OOD**, representing improvements of **+6.4% ID** and **+4.8% OOD** over naive GRPO. Relative to the best traditional method (Confidence Penalty), OLR provides +5.3% ID. On OOD, Confidence Penalty actually outperforms OLR (56.2% vs. 53.9%), primarily because Confidence Penalty achieves 82.9% on ARC-c vs. OLR's 78.5%. This is the only setting where a baseline surpasses OLR on a metric, and the paper does not discuss this result explicitly, leaving it as an observed but unexplained anomaly in the otherwise consistent OLR advantage.

**The catastrophic failure of small-loss selection** is one of the paper's most important negative results. Small-loss selection (Gui et al., 2021), a cornerstone technique in classification noisy label learning, assumes that models learn clean patterns first and noisy patterns later, so samples with low training loss early in training are likely clean. In RLVR, the paper explains, "small loss frequently signals uninformative samples (e.g., rollouts entirely correct or incorrect) rather than high label quality, offering little improvement signal" (Section 4.2). When all rollouts for a prompt are correct, the advantage is zero and the GRPO loss is zero—these samples produce no gradient but are preferentially selected by small-loss criteria, starving the model of informative training examples. When all rollouts are incorrect, the loss is also zero (no correct rollouts to contrast against), and these samples are also selected. The result is that small-loss selection retains the least useful samples and discards the most useful ones, causing performance to fall below both naive training and random selection.

**Comparison to TTRL specifically:** TTRL's unconditional majority-vote pseudo-labeling (Zuo et al., 2025) under inactive noise marginally improves over naive GRPO (36.9% vs. 36.1% ID) but is far below OLR (44.9% ID). Under active noise, TTRL is not directly compared in Table 2's active noise section, but the table's structure implies OLR's selective criteria-driven replacement is the key advantage over TTRL's unconditional replacement. The paper's ablation (Figure 4b, discussed below) confirms that removing OLR's criteria reduces it to essentially majority-vote replacement, which performs substantially worse.

#### How Does OLR Behave During the Training Process?

**Figure 3** presents a six-panel analysis of training dynamics under 50% active noise, providing the mechanistic evidence for *why* OLR works rather than just *that* it works.

**Panel (a) — Training Accuracy:** During the early learning phase (epochs 0–5, before OLR activates), majority-answer accuracy on both clean and noisy samples rises from ~50% to over 60%, exceeding the initial 50% correct label ratio. This is the Early Correctness Coherence in action: "even with noisy supervision, the chance that the majority answer matches the correct answer increases" (Section 1). After OLR activates at epoch 5, accuracy climbs further to over 80% by the end of training—a 20 percentage point gain beyond what early learning alone achieves. The plateau at ~60% during epochs 3–5 demonstrates why OLR's intervention is necessary: cross-sample coupling pushes the model partway, but the noisy labels prevent it from fully converging without correction.

**Panel (b) — Selected vs. Unselected Accuracy:** For samples where OLR triggers label replacement, the majority-answer accuracy exceeds 90% and approaches 100% by late training. For samples where OLR does not trigger (retaining original labels), accuracy remains below 70%. Since unselected samples retain their original labels, the remaining noise consists of persistent original noisy labels (reduced by approximately 30% due to OLR corrections, per panel f) and a small fraction (<5%) of erroneous majority answers from selected samples. This validates that OLR's criteria successfully identify high-confidence correct answers: the selection mechanism is not random but specifically isolates samples where the model's internal consensus is reliable.

**Panel (c) — Pass Rate Slope:** The slope of the majority answer's pass rate remains positive throughout training for both clean and noisy samples. This is a direct empirical validation of Theorem 3.4's prediction that `p_t(y* | x)` increases over time for both groups due to cross-sample coupling. The persistent positivity is what makes the slope criterion viable—if slopes were frequently negative or near-zero, the criterion would have no discriminatory power. The paper notes that this "persistently positive slope, combined with the rising accuracy in (a), indicates that correct answers can gradually emerge with increasing probability even on noisy samples through cross-sample coupling" (Section 4.2).

**Panel (d) — Selection Ratio:** OLR selects over 40% of samples from *both* clean and noisy sets. This is important because it demonstrates that OLR does not simply filter for easy samples or rely on the original label distribution—it replaces labels on clean samples (where the majority answer matches the already-correct label, a harmless no-op) and noisy samples in roughly equal proportion. This balance suggests OLR's criteria are not biased toward one group, which would be problematic if it predominantly selected noisy samples (risk of introducing new errors) or clean samples (leaving noisy ones uncorrected).

**Panel (e) — Ablation Accuracy:** The full OLR (both criteria) achieves nearly 100% accuracy in sample selection—meaning that when OLR replaces a label, the replacement is almost always the correct answer. Removing the historical consistency criterion drops this accuracy by ~20 percentage points, and removing the convergence slope criterion also drops it by ~20 percentage points. The similarity of the drops indicates both criteria are non-redundant: each catches distinct failure modes. The paper does not report the accuracy when both criteria are removed (unconditional majority-vote replacement), but from the ablation experiments (Figure 4b, Table 8), this would presumably drop further.

**Panel (f) — Real vs. Initial Noise:** Under active noise with 50% initial noise ratio, OLR reduces the effective noise ratio by approximately 30 percentage points during the refinement phase—from 50% to ~35% of labels remaining actively corrupted. This quantifies OLR's denoising effectiveness and connects directly to Theorem 3.5's prediction that `ρ_eff = ρ(1 - Δ)`. The ~30% reduction corresponds to `Δ ≈ 0.3`, meaning roughly 30% of noisy samples satisfy both criteria and have their labels corrected.

**Appendix Figure 6** provides the corresponding six-panel analysis for **inactive noise** at `ρ = 0.5`, showing qualitatively similar dynamics: (a) early learning lifts accuracy from 50% to over 70%, followed by OLR boosting it to over 80%; (b) selected samples exceed 90% accuracy (approaching 100%), while unselected ones stay below 80%; (c) slopes remain positive throughout; (d) OLR selects over 40% from both sets; (e) both criteria are necessary; (f) OLR reduces inactive noise by 25 percentage points during refinement. The main difference from active noise is the higher plateau before OLR activation (70% vs. 60%) and the slightly lower gains from OLR (10 percentage points vs. 20), consistent with inactive noise being less harmful to begin with.

#### Does OLR's Effectiveness Scale with More Data?

**Table 3** reports results with a larger training set of 4,000 samples (5× the default 800) at `ρ = 0.5`, using Qwen3-4B-Base. Under inactive noise, OLR achieves 46.1% ID (+5.6% over 40.5% without OLR) and 63.3% OOD (+3.6% over 59.7%). Under active noise, OLR achieves 41.4% ID (+3.6% over 37.8%) and 55.7% OOD (+0.4% over 55.3%). The gains are **comparable in magnitude to the 800-sample results**: +5.6% vs. +8.8% for inactive ID (the smaller absolute gain may reflect a higher baseline with more data), and +3.6% vs. +6.4% for active ID. The OOD gains shrink substantially under active noise (+0.4% vs. +4.8%), primarily because the baseline on GPQA-diamond drops from 24.7% to 26.8% with more data, a small base rate that leaves less room for improvement.

The key takeaway is that OLR's effectiveness does not degrade with dataset size—it provides consistent gains at 5× scale, suggesting the cross-sample coupling mechanism (which relies on shared parameters) does not saturate with more prompts. This is important because it indicates OLR scales beyond the small-data regime that might artificially amplify its benefits.

#### Parameter Sensitivity Analysis

**Figure 4a** sweeps the slope threshold `δ_slope` across `{0.0, 0.025, 0.05, 0.075, 0.1, 0.2}` at `ρ = 0.5` on Qwen3-4B-Base. For in-distribution benchmarks under both noise types and out-of-distribution under inactive noise, `δ_slope = 0.05` is optimal. Lower values (0.0, 0.025) perform worse because they allow near-flat or barely-increasing pass rates to trigger replacement, introducing erroneous majority answers. Higher values (0.075, 0.1, 0.2) perform worse because they filter out too many samples, reducing the number of corrections. The paper notes that "no clear trend emerges for out-of-distribution under active noise" at different `δ_slope` values—the performance fluctuates without monotonicity—and flags this as an area for future research. This suggests that the OOD active noise setting is inherently noisier in its relationship to the hyperparameter, possibly because the OOD benchmarks require transfer of reasoning patterns that interact differently with label correction.

The sweep of early learning phase duration `T` across `{2, 4, 6, 8, 10}` (also **Figure 4a**) similarly shows `T = 5` as optimal for the same settings. A smaller `T` (2, 4) causes inaccurate slope estimation because the regression has too few data points; a larger `T` (6, 8) prolongs training with noisy labels before correction begins, allowing active noise to accumulate more harm. The same "no clear pattern" is observed for OOD under active noise. Specific numerical values are in Appendix Tables 6 and 7.

**Practical implication:** The sensitivity analysis demonstrates that OLR is not brittle to its two main hyperparameters—it outperforms naive training across the full sweep range—but neither is it hyperparameter-insensitive. The 5-axis plot clusters show differences of several percentage points between best and worst settings, so tuning is advisable for deployment. The paper's default values (`δ_slope = 0.05`, `T = 5`) represent a reasonable optimum for the tested configuration but may need adjustment for different models, datasets, or noise ratios.

#### Ablation Studies

**Figure 4b** and **Appendix Table 8** report ablation results removing each OLR criterion. The full OLR is compared against two ablated variants: OLR without the Positive Convergence Slope criterion (retaining only Historical Consistency) and OLR without the Historical Consistency criterion (retaining only the Convergence Slope).

**Removing the convergence slope criterion** (relying only on historical consistency): Under active noise, this drops ID accuracy from 41.9% (full OLR) to 37.0% (−4.9 percentage points), and OOD from 53.9% to 51.4% (−2.5). Under inactive noise, ID drops from 44.9% to 38.3% (−6.6 percentage points), and OOD from 61.0% to 56.0% (−5.0). The degradation is substantial and consistent, confirming that historical consistency alone cannot distinguish between a consistently wrong majority answer that happens to persist and a correct one that is gaining momentum.

**Removing the historical consistency criterion** (relying only on convergence slope): Under active noise, ID drops from 41.9% to 37.2% (−4.7 percentage points), and OOD from 53.9% to 53.3% (−0.6). Under inactive noise, ID drops from 44.9% to 36.9% (−8.0 percentage points), and OOD from 61.0% to 56.8% (−4.2). The consistency criterion appears particularly important under inactive noise on ID benchmarks—its removal causes the largest single dropout (−8.0 percentage points on the ID average). This aligns with the intuition that inactive noise creates fewer but more persistent wrong majority answers (since the model never generates the noisy label, the correct answer may emerge sporadically but not stably), making consistency a crucial filter.

**The relative importance of the two criteria:** Under active noise, both criteria contribute roughly equally (4.7–4.9 point drops when either is removed). Under inactive noise on ID, consistency is more important (−8.0 vs. −6.6), while on OOD they are comparable (−4.2 vs. −5.0). The paper does not explain this asymmetry but the pattern is consistent with the tabular results: inactive noise on in-distribution tasks may produce transient correct majority answers that have positive slopes but lack historical stability, making the consistency criterion essential to filter them.

**OLR on different model scales and types:** Appendix C reports results on Qwen3-8B-Base (Table 4) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (Table 5) at `ρ = 0.5`. For Qwen3-8B-Base, the base model starts at 29.5% ID and 29.8% OOD—stronger than the 4B variant. Under active noise without OLR, training achieves 40.5% ID and 46.4% OOD; OLR improves this to 41.7% ID (+1.2%) and 53.6% OOD (+7.2%). The OOD gain is particularly large, driven by ARC-c jumping from 59.6% to 75.5% (+15.9 points). Under inactive noise, gains are smaller (+0.3% ID, +1.5% OOD), likely because the 8B model's higher base capabilities mean cross-sample coupling already provides strong implicit denoising without explicit label correction. For DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, a model already fine-tuned for reasoning, the base accuracy is 34.8% ID and 24.6% OOD. Under active noise, OLR improves from 38.1% to 39.2% ID (+1.1%) and 25.1% to 27.8% OOD (+2.7%); under inactive noise, from 44.2% to 44.9% ID (+0.7%) and 31.6% to 32.7% OOD (+1.1%). The gains are smaller in absolute terms but consistent in direction, confirming OLR's applicability across model families and fine-tuning stages.

**Additional experiments verifying Early Correctness Coherence (Appendix C, Figure 5):** Detailed decomposition of the model's predictions on clean and noisy samples under both noise types at `ρ = 0.5` reveals that "the early training phase reveals that the model learns to predict true labels even on noisy examples, indicating a preference for fitting correctly labeled samples and an increasing likelihood of producing correct answers on noisy ones over time." The figure visualizes three categories: predictions matching the true correct answer (green), predictions matching the noisy label (red), and predictions matching neither (blue). On noisy samples, the green proportion grows during early training while the red proportion shrinks, directly validating the cross-sample coupling hypothesis.

#### OLR Performance on Out-of-Distribution Generalization

A notable pattern across Tables 1–3 is that OLR often provides **larger relative gains on out-of-distribution benchmarks** than on in-distribution ones. For example, under `ρ = 0.5` active noise on 800 samples (Table 1), OLR achieves +6.4% ID vs. +4.8% OOD, but under `ρ = 0.7` active noise, it achieves +2.2% ID vs. +8.1% OOD. Under `ρ = 0.9` inactive noise, it achieves +1.2% ID vs. +5.4% OOD. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that noisy labels during training cause the model to learn spurious patterns specific to the training distribution, and OLR's correction reduces this overfitting, improving transfer to OOD tasks. The paper does not make this argument explicitly, but the data support it.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments collectively demonstrate that OLR provides consistent and substantial improvements over naive GRPO training under noisy supervision across a wide range of noise ratios, noise types, model sizes, and benchmarks. However, several aspects of the experimental design and reported results warrant careful scrutiny.

**Claim 1: OLR "consistently improves robustness under both inactive and active noisy-label settings."** This claim is **supported with strong evidence across noise ratios**, with the important caveat that at `ρ = 0.9` under active noise, the improvement is marginal (+3.4% ID, +0.7% OOD) and OLR underperforms naive GRPO on AIME24 (7.1% vs. 8.3%). The claim of "consistency" holds in aggregate but not uniformly across benchmarks at extreme noise. The paper is transparent about this—the per-benchmark breakdown in Table 1 reports the AIME24 degradation explicitly—but does not discuss what it implies about OLR's limits. Specifically, at 90% active noise, the model's own majority answers are too unreliable to serve as correction candidates (only 10% of labels are clean to drive cross-sample coupling), and OLR's conservative criteria may replace labels too infrequently or with insufficiently accurate majority answers to help. This is an inherent ceiling, not a bug: OLR cannot create correct answers from nothing; it can only surface correct answers that already exist in the rollout distribution. When the clean-signal-to-noise ratio is too low, cross-sample coupling fails to produce correct majority answers, and OLR has nothing to surface.

**Claim 2: OLR provides "average gains of 3.6%–3.9% on in-distribution benchmarks and 3.3%–4.6% on out-of-distribution evaluations."** This claim is **arithmetically correct from Table 1** but is an average across noise ratios that weights `ρ = 0.1` (minimal gains) equally with `ρ = 0.5` (peak gains). The practical implication—that OLR provides ~3.5–4.5% average gain—is accurate but understates the variation: at `ρ = 0.5`, in-distribution gains are 6.4–8.8%, while at `ρ = 0.1`, they are 0.4–5.8%. A deployment decision should consider the expected noise ratio, not the average across ratios.

**Claim 3: OLR outperforms baseline methods including TTRL and small-loss selection.** This claim is **strongly supported** by Table 2 for `ρ = 0.5`. However, the baseline comparison is limited to a single noise ratio (0.5). It is possible, though unlikely, that TTRL or Confidence Penalty would surpass OLR at other ratios—the paper does not report this sweep. Additionally, the OOD result where Confidence Penalty outperforms OLR under active noise (56.2% vs. 53.9%) is reported without comment, representing a genuine case where a simpler baseline succeeds. This should temper claims of universal OLR superiority.

**Potential weaknesses in experimental design:**

- **Single training run per configuration.** The paper reports one training run per (method, noise ratio, noise type) combination. Without multiple seeds, there is no estimate of training variance. RLVR training is known to be noisy—GRPO advantage estimates depend on the specific rollouts sampled, which can vary substantially. It is possible that some of the reported differences (particularly the smaller ones, like +0.4% at `ρ = 0.1`) are within noise. The paper would be strengthened by 3–5 seed replicates with standard errors.

- **Small test sets for competition benchmarks.** AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AMC each contain only 30 problems. A single problem's correct or incorrect classification shifts accuracy by 3.3 percentage points on these benchmarks. The paper reports avg@32 to reduce variance, but this is still averaging over a small problem set. The large swings in AIME24 scores across configurations (e.g., 12.5% at `ρ = 0.5` inactive without OLR vs. 21.7% with OLR—a jump of 9.2 points, or roughly 2–3 problems) should be interpreted cautiously: OLR may be genuinely improving reasoning, or it may be getting lucky on a few borderline problems. MATH-500 (500 problems) and the OOD benchmarks (ARC-c: 1,172 problems, MMLU-Pro: 12,032 problems) provide more reliable estimates.

- **Active noise construction is dynamic but not controlled for difficulty.** Active noisy labels are constructed on-policy by replacing correct labels with incorrect answers the model actually generates. This guarantees rollout feasibility, but it does not control for *how* incorrect the noisy labels are—some may be semantically close to correct (e.g., an arithmetic error in an otherwise correct solution), while others may be entirely different approaches. The paper does not characterize the distribution of active noise difficulty, which could affect results: if active noise at `ρ = 0.3` is easier to overcome than at `ρ = 0.7` (because the model's incorrect answers are more plausible at lower noise, when the policy is stronger), then the difficulty-ratio interaction is confounded. This is not a flaw so much as an inherent challenge of studying active noise, but it means the reported gains at different ratios may not be directly comparable in terms of "noise difficulty."

- **No comparison to simple data filtering baselines.** The paper does not include baselines that filter out potentially noisy samples based on simple heuristics: e.g., discarding samples where the model's majority-voted answer is inconsistent across epochs, or discarding samples where the pass rate is below a threshold. These would be natural lightweight competitors to OLR and would help isolate whether OLR's gains come from label refinement specifically or from discarding harmful samples generally. The Random Selection baseline is a weak control—it shows that random discarding hurts, but does not test informed filtering.

- **The 8B model results show diminishing returns.** On Qwen3-8B-Base (Table 4), OLR's ID gains under inactive noise are negligible (+0.3%). This could mean that larger models are inherently more robust to label noise (stronger cross-sample coupling due to more parameters), or it could mean that the specific training configuration (800 samples, `ρ = 0.5`) is too easy for the 8B model to show differentiation. The paper does not explore whether OLR's benefits at larger scales require larger datasets, higher noise ratios, or different hyperparameters. This is a gap in the scaling narrative.

- **No analysis of which samples OLR correctly vs. incorrectly selects.** Figure 3(d) shows OLR selects ~40% of both clean and noisy samples, and Figure 3(b) shows selected samples have >90% accuracy. But the paper does not break down *which* noisy samples are correctly refined vs. which remain noisy. Are the correctly refined ones the easiest problems (where cross-sample coupling is strongest)? Are the unrefined ones the hardest? This analysis would provide actionable guidance on when OLR can be trusted and when it cannot, and it would connect back to the critical noise ratio `ρ_c` from Theorem 3.4: presumably, problems with strong cross-sample coupling (θ close to other clean problems) are more likely to be refined successfully.

- **Missing comparison to oracle relabeling.** The paper does not report an upper bound: what accuracy would be achieved if all noisy labels were magically corrected to ground truth at the moment of OLR replacement? This oracle would quantify the headroom remaining after OLR and indicate whether further label refinement (beyond OLR's current criteria) could close the gap. Without it, we cannot distinguish between "OLR is near-optimal at label refinement" and "there is substantial room for improvement in the refinement criteria."

**The experiments that would strengthen the paper but were not run:**

- Multi-round OLR (train with OLR, use refined labels to train a second model, repeat) to test whether self-improvement compounds.
- Ablation on the number of rollouts K (fixed at 8) to determine whether more rollouts improve majority-answer reliability and OLR accuracy.
- Experiments with naturally occurring noisy labels (e.g., weakly-labeled data from web scraping or student annotators) rather than synthetically injected noise, to assess realism.
- A held-out clean validation set to tune `δ_slope` and `T` in deployment-like conditions, where the optimal hyperparameters cannot be chosen by sweeping the test benchmarks.
- Computational cost analysis in FLOPs rather than wall-clock time, for comparability with the broader scaling literature.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Critical Noise Ratio Boundary Imposes a Hard Ceiling on OLR Effectiveness

**The assumption or constraint.** Theorem 3.4 establishes that Early Correctness Coherence occurs only when `$\rho < \rho_c$`, where `$\rho_c = \gamma G_c / (\gamma G_c + G_n)$` is the critical noise ratio determined by cross-sample coupling strength and advantage magnitudes. Theorem 3.5 shows OLR raises this threshold to `$\rho_c^{\text{OLR}} = \rho_c / (1 - \Delta)$`, but the correction probability `$\Delta$` is bounded by the fraction of noisy samples where the model's majority answer is both correct and meets the dual criteria. When `$\rho$` exceeds `$\rho_c^{\text{OLR}}$`, the system enters the active-noise collapse regime where Theorem A.5 predicts `$L_t(x_n) \to -\infty$`—the policy's preference for the noisy label over the correct answer becomes unbounded.

**The consequence.** At sufficiently high noise ratios, OLR provides diminishing or zero benefit regardless of hyperparameter tuning. This is not a hyperparameter sensitivity problem but a **fundamental phase transition**: below `$\rho_c^{\text{OLR}}$`, cross-sample coupling and OLR together can sustain correct-answer emergence; above it, the direct reinforcement of active noisy labels overwhelms the correction mechanism, and the model's own majority answers become too corrupted to serve as reliable correction targets. The practical implication is that OLR cannot salvage training when the proportion of actively reinforced wrong labels exceeds the system's critical threshold—a scenario that becomes more likely as noise ratios increase or as the verifier imperfections align with the model's generative tendencies.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 under active noise at `$\rho = 0.9$` is the direct empirical demonstration. OLR achieves only +3.4% in-distribution improvement (21.7% → 25.1%) and +0.7% out-of-distribution improvement (25.8% → 26.5%), and on AIME24, OLR *decreases* performance below naive GRPO (7.1% vs. 8.3%). The model's average accuracy remains below its initial pre-training baseline (24.8% ID, 27.7% OOD), confirming collapse has occurred with or without OLR. At `$\rho = 0.1$` under inactive noise, OLR provides only +0.4% ID improvement—when noise is predominantly inactive and largely harmless to begin with, the correction mechanism has little to correct. The effective operating range where OLR provides substantial gains appears to be `$\rho \in [0.3, 0.7]$` for inactive noise and `$\rho \in [0.1, 0.5]$` for active noise, with diminishing returns outside these bands.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the boundary implicitly through the theoretical analysis (Theorems 3.4, A.5, A.6) but does not discuss the practical ceiling in the context of deployment. The critical noise ratio `$\rho_c$` is expressed in terms of coupling strength `$\gamma$` and advantage magnitudes `$G_c$`, `$G_n$`, which are not measured or estimated for the experimental setup—there is no diagnostic to determine whether a given training run is operating below or above `$\rho_c^{\text{OLR}}$`. A practitioner cannot know in advance whether OLR will work for their specific noise ratio without running the experiment. The paper does not propose methods to estimate `$\rho_c$`, nor does it explore whether increasing `$K$` (rollouts per prompt) or `$\beta$` (KL regularization) can shift `$\rho_c$` upward to extend the tolerable range. This is a significant gap for practical adoption.

---

### 6.2 Active Noise Construction Is Policy-Dependent, Making the Taxonomy a Moving Target

**The assumption or constraint.** The distinction between inactive and active noisy labels depends on rollout feasibility (`$\pi_\theta(\tilde{y} \mid x) > 0$`), which is a property of the *current* policy, not a static characteristic of the dataset. The paper constructs active noise dynamically during inference by replacing correct labels with incorrect answers the model actually generates on-policy (Section 4.1): "static construction of active noise is impractical, as the probability that noisy labels actually appear during rollout depends on both the noise ratio and the model's output probability." This means active noise in the experiments is **endogenous to the training process**: the set of active noisy labels changes as the policy improves, because the model's output distribution shifts.

**The consequence.** The taxonomy is not a clean partition of a fixed dataset but a **time-varying classification** that depends on the model's current capabilities. A label that is inactive at epoch 1 (the model never generates that specific wrong answer) may become active at epoch 10 as the model's output distribution broadens or shifts. Conversely, a label that is active early in training may become inactive later if the policy learns to avoid that particular wrong answer while still generating others. This makes the inactive/active distinction **operationally ambiguous** for practitioners: at what point during training should the label type be assessed? The paper's experiments use dynamically constructed active noise, which guarantees activity at the moment of construction but does not control for activity persistence throughout training. The observed divergence between active and inactive noise curves in later training (Figure 2, Figures 3a vs. Appendix Figure 6a) may reflect not only the direct harm of active noise but also the evolving relationship between the policy and the active noise set.

**What evidence exists.** The paper provides no analysis of how the set of active noisy labels changes over training epochs. Figure 3(a) shows training accuracy for active noise, but this reflects the model's majority-vote correctness, not whether the active noisy labels remain rollout-feasible throughout. The paper notes (Section 4.1, footnote 2) that with 50% active noise and 50% model confidence, the probability that all noisy samples are actually reinforced in a given epoch is only `$\approx 0.21$`, meaning active noise is probabilistically reinforced rather than deterministically active—some active noisy labels may not trigger reinforcement in a given epoch simply because the model's rollouts happen not to produce them. This stochasticity complicates the clean theoretical distinction between inactive (never reinforced) and active (always capable of reinforcement when generated).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address the time-varying nature of the taxonomy. It treats active and inactive as fixed categories in the theoretical analysis (Theorems 3.4, A.1, A.5) and experimental reporting (Table 1 splits results by noise type). There is no tracking of how many initially-active labels remain active throughout training, how many initially-inactive labels become active, or how OLR's correction rate differs between these subgroups. This is a conceptual gap: the theoretical framework assumes static noise categories, but the experimental reality is dynamic. The paper's acknowledgment of the dynamic construction method (Section 4.1) is descriptive, not analytical—it does not explore the implications for the taxonomy's validity.

### 6.3 Single Benchmark Family and Synthetic Noise Limit Generalizability Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** All training experiments use the DAPO-Math dataset (Yu et al., 2025) with 800 or 4,000 samples, and all synthetic noise is injected by the experimenters—inactive noise via "strings that the model is unlikely to output" (Section 4.1) and active noise via on-policy incorrect answers. The evaluation benchmarks are exclusively multiple-choice or short-answer reasoning tasks, primarily mathematics (AIME, AMC, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench) with three out-of-distribution tasks (ARC-c, GPQA-diamond, MMLU-Pro) that remain multiple-choice QA formats. The paper acknowledges this implicitly through the choice to evaluate on "six in-distribution mathematical reasoning benchmarks" and "three out-of-distribution tasks" (Section 4.1), but does not test on qualitatively different domains such as code generation, open-ended reasoning, or multi-step planning.

**The consequence.** Four generalizability concerns arise. First, **domain specificity**: mathematical reasoning has a particularly clean structure—answers are uniquely correct, reasoning chains follow logical deduction, and there is typically a single right answer. This maximizes the effectiveness of majority voting (multiple rollouts tend to converge on the same correct answer) and cross-sample coupling (reasoning patterns transfer between problems). In domains with multiple valid answers (e.g., creative writing, debate, open-ended QA), majority voting is meaningless, and OLR's criteria break down because there is no single majority answer to track. Second, **synthetic noise may not represent real noise**: the paper constructs inactive noise as random strings and active noise as the model's own incorrect on-policy answers. Real-world noisy labels from expert scarcity would likely be more structured—a tired annotator might provide a partially correct solution with a sign error, or a confused annotator might provide a correct answer to a different interpretation of the question. These "near-miss" noisy labels may behave differently: they might be rollout-feasible (active) but close enough to correct that cross-sample coupling partially compensates, or they might create gradient interference patterns not captured by the paper's simple correct-vs.-noisy binary analysis. Third, **the heavy reliance on majority voting** means OLR inherits all the brittleness of majority voting as a correctness signal—it works when the correct answer is the consensus, which is true in mathematical reasoning but not universally. Fourth, **out-of-distribution evaluation is still within the multiple-choice QA paradigm**: ARC-c, GPQA-diamond, and MMLU-Pro test domain transfer but not task-format transfer. The model is always answering multiple-choice questions—it is never asked to generate free-form explanations, reason about ambiguous scenarios, or interact in a multi-turn dialogue.

**What evidence exists.** The paper demonstrates transfer across model families (Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B; Tables 1, 4, 5) and across reasoning benchmarks within the math+science domain. However, all benchmarks share the structural property of having a single verifiable correct answer. The paper does not include code generation benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP) despite code being the other canonical RLVR domain, nor does it test on tasks where verification is fuzzy or multi-dimensional. The out-of-distribution results in Tables 1–3 show OLR provides consistent gains, but the OOD tasks (ARC-c: science multiple-choice, GPQA: graduate-level QA, MMLU-Pro: academic multiple-choice) are structurally similar to the training distribution—they differ in content, not in task format or the nature of correctness.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim generalizability beyond the tested domains. The title asks "Can LLMs Learn to Reason Robustly under Noisy Supervision?" and the paper answers affirmatively for mathematical and scientific reasoning with verifiable answers. The scope is reasonably defined, but the implicit claim—that OLR addresses noisy label learning in RLVR generally—extends beyond the evidence. A practitioner working in code generation, dialogue, or open-ended reasoning would need to determine whether majority voting provides a reliable correctness signal in their domain before adopting OLR, and the paper provides no guidance on how to make this determination.

### 6.4 No Comparison to the Trivial Baseline of Early Stopping on a Clean Validation Set

**The assumption or constraint.** OLR is presented as an online, self-supervised label correction mechanism that requires no external clean data. The training loop monitors rollouts, tracks pass rates, and triggers label replacements based on internal statistics. The implicit assumption is that **clean validation data is unavailable**—otherwise, a practitioner could simply monitor validation accuracy during training, detect when overfitting to noisy labels begins (the divergence point in Figure 2a), and stop training before active noise causes collapse. This "early stopping on a clean validation set" is arguably the simplest and most standard defense against noisy labels in any supervised or semi-supervised learning setting.

**The consequence.** If a clean validation set of even modest size (e.g., 50–100 verified examples) is available, early stopping on that set may achieve comparable or better robustness than OLR, with zero modification to the training algorithm and zero additional overhead. The paper does not benchmark this baseline, making it impossible to assess how much of OLR's benefit comes from label refinement specifically versus from the simpler mechanism of avoiding late-training degradation. This is particularly relevant because the paper's own analysis (Figure 2a, Figure 3a) shows that accuracy on noisy samples plateaus or declines in later training—exactly the pattern that early stopping would prevent. The divergence point between clean and noisy sample accuracy (around epoch 5–10 in Figure 3a) is a natural stopping point that a clean validation set would detect.

**What evidence exists.** The paper provides no comparison to any form of early stopping, clean-validation-based curriculum, or data filtering that uses even a small amount of trusted data. All baselines in Table 2 are either fully unsupervised (TTRL, Co-Reward) or algorithmic noise-robust methods that, like OLR, do not use clean validation data (Confidence Penalty, Label Smoothing, Small-loss Selection). This is a significant omission because the practical question is not just "does OLR beat other methods that don't use clean data?" but "does OLR beat the simplest thing a practitioner would actually do, which is to hold out a small clean set and monitor it?" If a practitioner has the resources to curate 800 training examples with 50% noise (400 noisy labels), they almost certainly have the resources to verify 50–100 of them to create a clean validation set. The paper's framing that clean data is completely unavailable represents an artificially hard setting that may not reflect real deployment constraints.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this gap. The entire motivation (Section 1, Section 2) emphasizes expert scarcity and imperfect labeling as the justification for fully self-supervised denoising, but it does not discuss the intermediate regime where some verified data is available but not enough for full training. This is a missed opportunity: a hybrid approach that uses OLR's internal criteria for label refinement *and* a small clean validation set for early stopping or hyperparameter selection could be both more practical and more effective than OLR alone. The paper's sensitivity analysis (Figure 4a) shows that `$\delta_{\text{slope}}$` and `$T$` affect performance by several percentage points, and these hyperparameters are currently tuned by sweeping against the test benchmarks—a protocol that would be impossible in deployment. A clean validation set would solve this hyperparameter selection problem immediately.

### 6.5 Loss of Correct Answers Through Reversion Is Not Addressed

**The assumption or constraint.** OLR replaces a noisy label with the model's majority-voted answer only when both criteria (positive convergence slope and historical consistency) are satisfied. At all other epochs, and for all samples that never meet the criteria, the original label—whether correct or noisy—is retained unchanged (Equation 7). This creates an asymmetric risk: **OLR can correct noisy labels, but it cannot protect correct labels from being corrupted by the training process.** Specifically, when the model's majority answer on a *correctly labeled* sample shifts away from the correct answer (due to active noise influence from other samples or natural forgetting), OLR does nothing—it retains the correct original label and continues training. But if that correctly labeled sample later meets both OLR criteria with a *wrong* majority answer (due to the model having been shifted by noise from other samples), OLR will replace the correct label with the wrong majority answer, actively introducing new noise.

**The consequence.** In the active noise setting, where the model is being reinforced to generate wrong answers on some fraction of prompts, there is no guarantee that cross-sample coupling only transfers correct patterns—it may also transfer incorrect patterns from actively reinforced noisy samples to clean samples, causing the model's majority answer on clean samples to degrade. If this degradation occurs and the degraded majority answer meets OLR's criteria (positive slope because the wrong answer is gaining momentum, historical consistency because it persists across recent epochs), OLR will **replace a correct original label with an incorrect majority answer**, actively harming performance on what was previously a clean sample. This is a failure mode that the paper's theoretical analysis does not address: Theorem 3.5 bounds the probability that a *replaced* label is correct, assuming the model's correct-answer probability dominates, but it does not bound the probability that a *correct original* label is incorrectly overridden.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports (Section 4.2, Figure 3) that OLR selects over 40% of samples from both clean and noisy sets, and that for selected samples, accuracy exceeds 90% and approaches 100%. However, this means that **up to 10% of label replacements on originally-clean samples may be incorrect**—replacing a correct label with a wrong majority answer. The paper does not report the absolute number or fraction of clean samples that are incorrectly overridden, nor does it compare the final accuracy of originally-clean samples that were selected by OLR versus those that were not. Without this analysis, it is impossible to assess the net harm of OLR's false-positive replacements on clean samples. The small negative result at `$\rho = 0.1$` inactive noise on AIME25 (Table 1: OLR decreases from 20.4% to 19.6%) may be a manifestation of this issue—at very low noise ratios, OLR has few noisy labels to correct but can still incorrectly override clean labels, producing a net negative effect.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this failure mode. The theoretical guarantee in Theorem 3.5 (`$\Pr(\hat{y}_t(x) = y^\star(x)) \geq 1 - \epsilon$`) applies to *any* sample that triggers replacement, but it is conditional on replacement occurring and assumes `$\Delta_p = p^\star - \max_{c \neq y^\star} p_c > 0$`. If the model's probability of the correct answer on a clean sample degrades (e.g., `$p^\star$` drops below a competing wrong answer due to cross-sample noise transfer), then `$\Delta_p$` may become negative, and the guarantee's premise fails—the majority answer may be wrong despite both OLR criteria being satisfied. A natural mitigation would be to apply OLR's criteria **only to samples where the original label is suspected to be noisy**, but the paper provides no mechanism for distinguishing clean from noisy samples a priori—OLR treats all samples equally by design. This is a fundamental tension: the method's strength (no need for external clean data) is also its vulnerability (no way to protect known-clean labels from being incorrectly overridden).

### 6.6 The Early Correctness Coherence Phenomenon Is Observed but Not Predicted in Advance

**The assumption or constraint.** OLR's design relies on Early Correctness Coherence—the empirical observation that "accuracy on both clean and noisy samples increases similarly in early training" (Section 3.2)—to justify that the model's majority answers become progressively more reliable on noisy samples. The theoretical analysis (Theorem 3.4) provides a post-hoc explanation via cross-sample coupling, but it does not provide a **predictive test** that would tell a practitioner whether Early Correctness Coherence will occur for their specific model, dataset, noise type, and noise ratio. The theorem establishes conditions (`$p_0(y^\star) > p_0(\tilde{y})$`, `$\gamma > 0$`, `$\rho < \rho_c$`) that are sufficient, but none of these quantities are measured or estimated in the paper's experiments—they are theoretical constructs, not operational diagnostics.

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering OLR cannot determine in advance whether their training setup will exhibit Early Correctness Coherence and thus benefit from label refinement. If the base model's initial probability `$p_0(y^\star)$` is not higher than `$p_0(\tilde{y})$` for a significant fraction of noisy samples (violating assumption i of Theorem 3.4), cross-sample coupling may be too weak to drive correct-answer emergence. If the noise is structured such that noisy labels are semantically similar to correct answers (e.g., same reasoning chain with a sign error in the final step), the log-ratio `$L_t(x_n)$` may not increase monotonically because the noisy label receives partial reinforcement from correct reasoning steps. If the model architecture or training dynamics produce weak parameter sharing between prompts (e.g., large models with strong specialization), the cross-sample coupling `$\gamma$` may be small, and `$\rho_c$` may be lower than the nominal noise ratio. In any of these cases, OLR may fail silently: the criteria may rarely trigger, or when they do, the majority answers may be insufficiently reliable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper verifies the theorem's assumptions experimentally *for the specific setting tested*—Appendix Figure 5 shows that "the early training phase reveals that the model learns to predict true labels even on noisy examples" for Qwen3-4B-Base on DAPO-Math with synthetic noise. But this is a confirmation, not a prediction. The paper does not report experiments where Early Correctness Coherence *fails* to occur, which would delineate the boundary conditions of OLR's applicability. The sensitivity analysis (Figure 4a) explores hyperparameter robustness but not structural robustness—it does not ask "does OLR still help if we use a model with weaker cross-sample coupling?" or "does OLR work if noisy labels are near-misses rather than random strings?" The positive results at larger model scales (Tables 4, 5) and with a different model family (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) provide some evidence of transfer, but all tested settings share the structural property of mathematical reasoning with a single correct answer and strong cross-problem similarity.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address the need for predictive diagnostics. There is no discussion of how to measure `$p_0(y^\star)$` vs. `$p_0(\tilde{y})$` for a new dataset, how to estimate `$\gamma$` from a few training steps, or how to detect whether Early Correctness Coherence is occurring in the first few epochs to decide whether to activate OLR. The early learning phase (`$T = 5$` epochs) serves as an implicit detection period—OLR is inactive during this time, and the practitioner could monitor whether majority accuracy on noisy samples is rising. But the paper does not recommend this or provide thresholds for the "go/no-go" decision. In deployment, a practitioner would need to run the experiment to find out whether OLR helps, which is true of any method but particularly acute here because the underlying mechanism (cross-sample coupling) is not guaranteed.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **new diagnostic vocabulary** for reasoning about failure modes in RLVR training, shifting the conversation around noisy supervision from "how robust is the optimizer?" to "which wrong labels can the model actually generate and reinforce?" The rollout feasibility concept (Definition 3.1) and the inactive/active taxonomy (Definitions 3.2–3.3) are not incremental refinements of existing noisy label learning categories—they are **categorically new concepts** that have no analog in the classification literature because they depend on a feedback loop (policy generates, verifier scores, advantage drives update) that static supervised learning lacks. The magnitude of this shift is best described as a **reframing** rather than a paradigm shift: the paper does not propose a fundamentally new training algorithm but rather provides the conceptual tools to understand *why* existing algorithms fail in specific ways and *what properties* a solution must have.

The paper's most important reconciliatory contribution is explaining why small-loss selection—a cornerstone technique in classification noisy label learning (Han et al., 2018b; Jiang et al., 2018; Gui et al., 2021)—fails catastrophically in RLVR. The catastrophic -17.4% ID and -26.2% OOD drops in Table 2 are not just negative results; they reveal a **structural mismatch** between the assumptions of traditional noisy label methods and the mechanics of RLVR. In classification, small loss signals "model is confident and likely correct, so the label is probably clean." In RLVR, small loss often signals "all rollouts produced the same outcome, so advantage is zero and gradient is zero"—these samples are *uninformative*, not clean. Small-loss selection preferentially retains these useless samples and discards the ones with mixed correct/incorrect rollouts that actually provide learning signal. The paper's explanation of this mechanism (Section 4.2) provides a principled reason to **abandon the direct porting of classification noise-robustness methods to RLVR** and instead develop techniques that account for the generation-verification-advantage loop.

The paper also shifts where research attention should be directed. Before this work, the natural response to noisy labels in RLVR would be to improve the verifier (make it more accurate, add robustness checks, ensemble multiple verifiers). The rollout feasibility concept reveals that **verifier accuracy alone is insufficient**: a noisy label that the model cannot generate is harmless regardless of how wrong the verifier thinks it is, while a noisy label that the model *can* generate is harmful even with a perfectly accurate verifier (the verifier correctly identifies that the generated answer matches the noisy label—it's the label itself that's wrong). This means the more impactful research direction is **understanding and controlling what the policy generates**, not just improving reward accuracy. KL regularization to a reference model, already standard in GRPO (Equation 2), takes on new significance under this framing: it functions as a defense against active noise by limiting how far the policy can drift toward generating verifier-exploiting (or noise-reinforcing) outputs. Research on constrained generation, trust-region methods, and dynamic KL scheduling becomes more attractive for RLVR robustness specifically, not just for training stability generally.

The Early Correctness Coherence phenomenon (Section 3.2, Theorem 3.4) changes how we should think about **what the model learns during RLVR**. The standard narrative—reinforcement from verifiable rewards teaches the model to produce correct answers it couldn't generate before—is only partially accurate. What the paper shows is that correct answers for noisy samples are already present in the model's rollout distribution during early training; they just aren't being reinforced because the verifier is comparing against the wrong reference label. This reframes RLVR from "teaching new capabilities" to **"amplifying latent capabilities while suppressing the wrong ones that happen to match the provided labels."** This has implications for data curation: it may be more important to ensure that provided labels do not accidentally match plausible-but-wrong model outputs than to ensure every label is perfect. A single active noisy label that matches a common model mistake may cause more harm than a dozen inactive noisy labels that the model never generates.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Diagnosing and predicting the critical noise ratio `$\rho_c$` for a given model-dataset pair.** Theorem 3.4 shows that Early Correctness Coherence occurs only when `$\rho < \rho_c = \gamma G_c / (\gamma G_c + G_n)$`, where `$\gamma$` is cross-sample coupling strength and `$G_c$`, `$G_n$` are clean and noisy advantage magnitudes. None of these quantities are operationalized in the paper—they remain theoretical constructs. A direct follow-up would measure `$\gamma$` empirically by computing gradient inner products: for a set of clean and noisy prompts, compute `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_c) \mid x_c) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_n) \mid x_n)$` during early training and check whether the expectation exceeds zero. Similarly, `$G_c$` and `$G_n$` can be estimated from the observed advantages during the first few epochs. If these measurements correlate with whether OLR succeeds at different noise ratios, they become deployable diagnostics: a practitioner could run 2–3 epochs, estimate `$\rho_c$`, and decide whether their noise ratio is below the tolerable threshold before committing to a full OLR training run. This would address perhaps the largest practical gap in the paper—the inability to predict in advance whether OLR will help.

**Stress-testing OLR on near-miss noisy labels rather than random or on-policy wrong answers.** The paper constructs inactive noise as "strings that the model is unlikely to output" and active noise as the model's own incorrect outputs. Real noisy labels from expert scarcity would likely be more structured: a tired mathematician writes `$x = 2$` when the answer is `$x = \pm 2$`, missing the negative root. This label is rollout-feasible (the model generates `$x = 2$` frequently), but it is *partially correct*—the reasoning chain shares most steps with the correct solution. Such near-miss labels create gradient interference that the paper's clean-vs.-noisy binary theoretical analysis does not capture: the verifier rewards partial correctness on the shared steps and penalizes divergence only at the final answer, creating a mixed signal. A strong follow-up would construct a spectrum of noisy label types (random string, plausible-but-wrong answer, near-miss with one error, semantically equivalent but syntactically different correct answer) and measure how OLR's effectiveness varies. The hypothesis from Theorem 3.4 is that near-miss labels have higher `$G_n$` (more reinforcement because they share reasoning structure with correct answers) and thus lower `$\rho_c$`—they should be *more* harmful than random wrong answers, not less, because they receive partial positive advantage throughout the solution rather than only at the final comparison.

**Multi-round OLR as a self-improvement loop.** The paper establishes that OLR can correct ~30% of noisy labels in a single training run (Figure 3f), but does not explore whether the refined labels can bootstrap a second round of training. A natural experiment: train a model with OLR on noisy data, extract the OLR-refined labels at the end of training, use those labels to train a *new* model from scratch (or fine-tune the same base model), apply OLR again, and iterate. The critical question is whether the fraction of corrected labels `$\Delta$` increases across rounds—if round 1 corrects 30% of labels, does round 2 (trained on cleaner labels, producing better majority answers) correct 50%? Or does it plateau because the remaining noisy labels are the hard ones where cross-sample coupling is weakest? The paper's theoretical framework (Theorem 3.5) predicts `$\rho_c^{\text{OLR}} = \rho_c / (1 - \Delta)$`, and each round reduces `$\rho_{\text{eff}}$`, so multi-round refinement should progressively increase the tolerable noise ceiling. Testing this would determine whether OLR enables self-improvement or is a one-shot denoising step.

**OLR with a small clean validation set for hyperparameter selection and early stopping.** Section 6.4 of the prior analysis identified the absence of a clean-validation baseline as a significant gap. A focused follow-up would give OLR access to 50–100 clean-labeled examples (5–10% of the 800-sample training set) for two purposes: (1) selecting `$\delta_{\text{slope}}$` and `$T$` on the clean set rather than by sweeping the test benchmarks, and (2) monitoring whether OLR's label replacements are improving clean-set accuracy over time, which would detect the "OLR incorrectly overrides clean labels" failure mode discussed in Section 6.5. The experiment would compare: OLR as-is (no clean data), OLR with clean data for hyperparameter selection only, OLR with clean data for early stopping only, and OLR with both. The hypothesis is that even minimal clean data dramatically improves OLR's practical reliability by solving the hyperparameter selection problem and providing a safety check against false-positive label overrides. If 50 clean examples provide most of the benefit of the full 800-example training set in terms of guiding OLR, the cost-benefit argument for the fully unsupervised setting weakens substantially.

**Applying the rollout feasibility taxonomy to code generation RLVR.** The paper focuses exclusively on mathematical reasoning, but code generation is the other canonical RLVR domain where unit tests provide verifiable rewards. Code has different structural properties that affect the taxonomy: incorrect code can be syntactically valid and pass some tests while failing others (partial credit), plausible wrong answers may share API calls or algorithmic structure with correct solutions (creating stronger cross-sample coupling through shared programming patterns), and active noisy labels may correspond to common bugs that the model generates frequently. A replication on HumanEval or MBPP with injected noisy labels (e.g., swapping correct solutions with buggy ones that pass a subset of tests) would test whether the inactive/active distinction transfers and whether OLR's majority-voting mechanism works when multiple different correct implementations exist (violating the "single correct answer" assumption that makes majority voting reliable in math). The paper's OOD results on ARC-c and GPQA-diamond suggest some transfer, but code specifically tests the boundaries of the majority-voting assumption.

**Theoretical analysis of when cross-sample coupling fails.** Theorem 3.4 assumes positive cross-sample coupling (`$\mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_c)) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y^\star(x_n))] \geq \gamma > 0$`), and Appendix Figure 5 verifies this empirically for the tested setting. But what determines `$\gamma$`? It likely depends on the similarity between clean and noisy prompts—two geometry problems may have higher coupling than a geometry problem and a combinatorics problem—and on model architecture (wider models may have more specialized parameters, reducing coupling). A controlled experiment would train on datasets with varying prompt similarity (e.g., all algebra vs. mixed algebra/geometry/combinatorics) and measure `$\gamma$` and OLR effectiveness. The hypothesis is that OLR works best when the clean and noisy subsets are drawn from similar distributions, and degrades when they are domain-disjoint. This would provide actionable guidance: if you have a noisy dataset spanning diverse topics, you may need to apply OLR within each topic cluster separately, or you may need a larger clean subset to maintain sufficient coupling across the distribution.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Training reasoning models from crowd-sourced or web-scraped data with unknown label quality.** The paper's core scenario—800 mathematical problems with 10–90% injected label noise—is a direct model of a realistic pipeline: an organization scrapes or crowd-sources thousands of reasoning problems with purported solutions, but some fraction of the solutions are incorrect due to annotator error, deliberate misinformation, or extraction artifacts. Deploying OLR in this pipeline would allow training on the full noisy dataset without manual verification, with the model self-correcting the labels during training. The $4×$–$8×$ effective noise reduction observed in Figure 3f (50% initial noise → ~35% effective noise for active, 50% → ~25% for inactive) means that a dataset with 30% noisy labels could be cleaned to ~15–20% effective noise—potentially below the critical threshold `$\rho_c$` where naive GRPO begins to degrade substantially. The negligible computational overhead (Appendix Table 9: OLR adds no measurable wall-clock time to training) makes this a drop-in improvement to existing RLVR pipelines. For an organization currently paying experts to verify every label in a 4,000-sample dataset, OLR could reduce the verification burden to spot-checking, with the model handling routine corrections automatically.

**Improving self-play and self-improvement pipelines for reasoning models.** Several recent works (Zelikman et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2024) use the model's own generations to create training data for subsequent rounds of fine-tuning or RL. In these pipelines, the model generates solutions, some verification mechanism (majority voting, reward model, ground-truth check) selects correct solutions as training targets, and the model is retrained. A persistent problem is that verification is imperfect—false positives (incorrect solutions marked as correct) create active noisy labels for the next round. OLR provides a mechanism to catch and correct these errors during the subsequent training round, rather than requiring perfect verification upfront. The paper's multi-round potential (Section 5, discussed above) suggests this could compound: round 1 uses OLR to clean round 1's noisy labels, producing better training data for round 2, which trains a stronger model that produces better majority answers, enabling OLR to clean an even higher fraction of labels in round 2. This could make self-improvement loops more robust to verification noise, reducing the need for expensive ground-truth labels or ensemble verification in each round.

**Deploying RLVR in domains where ground-truth verification is expensive or approximate.** The paper demonstrates OLR on mathematical reasoning, but the mechanism generalizes to any domain where the model can be used to cross-validate labels. Consider training a reasoning model for legal document analysis: a dataset of "case summary + correct ruling" pairs is collected, but some rulings are wrong due to annotator error. A rule-based verifier cannot check legal correctness (it's not math), so a learned reward model is used—and learned reward models are themselves imperfect, creating verifier noise on top of label noise. OLR could be applied by generating multiple analyses per case, using majority voting on the predicted ruling, and replacing likely-noisy labels with the model's consensus. The key requirement—that correct answers emerge in the model's rollout distribution despite noisy labels—would need to be verified for legal reasoning, but the paper's OOD results on GPQA-diamond and MMLU-Pro (Table 1) suggest that cross-sample coupling transfers across domains that share underlying reasoning patterns. The conservative nature of OLR (only replacing labels when both criteria are met) is particularly well-suited to high-stakes domains where incorrect label replacement could be costly—the ~90–100% selection accuracy in Figure 3b means OLR almost never makes the labels worse, even if it doesn't correct all errors.
