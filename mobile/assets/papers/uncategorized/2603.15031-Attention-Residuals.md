## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Attention Residuals (AttnRes)**, a mechanism that replaces the fixed, uniform accumulation of standard residual connections with learned softmax attention over preceding layer outputs — allowing each layer to selectively aggregate earlier representations with input-dependent weights (e.g., a layer can learn to attend more strongly to the embedding or to specific earlier layers rather than receiving an equal-weighted sum). Scaling law experiments on MoE Transformer models (up to 48B total parameters with 3B activated, trained on 1.4T tokens using the Kimi Linear architecture) demonstrate that AttnRes consistently outperforms PreNorm baselines, with the block-wise variant **Block AttnRes** (which partitions layers into ~8 blocks and attends over block-level summaries) recovering most of the full method's gains while reducing memory and communication from O(Ld) to O(Nd), achieving a 1.25× compute equivalent advantage (matching baseline loss with 1.25× less training compute) and improving downstream performance across all evaluated tasks — including +7.5 points on GPQA-Diamond and +3.6 on Minerva Math. The method proves practical at scale only when combined with the proposed infrastructure optimizations — cross-stage caching under pipeline parallelism and a two-phase computation strategy using online softmax — establishing that cross-layer attention can be deployed with under 2% inference latency overhead.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Residual Connections Are Blind to Depth-Wise Information Needs

Residual connections — the update rule $h_l = h_{l-1} + f_{l-1}(h_{l-1})$ — are perhaps the most universally adopted architectural component in modern deep learning. They are taught as a simple gradient highway: the identity term $I$ in the backward pass gradient product $\prod (I + \partial f_j/\partial h_j)$ ensures that even the earliest layers receive a direct training signal from the loss. Without residuals, deep networks suffer vanishing gradients and fail to train entirely.

But residuals play a second role that the paper argues has been "less recognized." Unroll the recurrence:

$$h_l = h_1 + \sum_{i=1}^{l-1} f_i(h_i)$$

Every layer $l$ receives exactly the same information: the uniformly-weighted sum of all prior layer outputs. The coefficients in front of $f_i(h_i)$ are all $1$. There is no mechanism to say "layer 5's output is particularly relevant to my work here at layer 20" or "the embedding is more important for this token than the last MLP's output." The weights are fixed, uniform, and content-independent.

The authors frame this as a problem of **depth-wise information aggregation** that has been left behind while the field has aggressively optimized sequence-wise and expert-wise mixing. Self-attention (Vaswani et al., 2017) replaced uniform sequence aggregation with learned, input-dependent weights. Mixture-of-Experts (Jacobs et al., 1991) replaced uniform layer computation with learned, input-dependent expert routing. But the depth dimension — how information flows from early layers to later layers — remains governed by the same fixed $+1$ coefficients that Kaiming He introduced in 2015 for 152-layer image classifiers. In modern LLMs with 50–100+ layers, this means every layer drinks from the same undifferentiated firehose of accumulated activations.

This paper identifies three concrete failure modes from this uniform aggregation (Section 2.1, "Limitations"):

1. **No selective access.** Different layer types — attention layers vs. MLP layers — receive the identical aggregated state $h_{l-1}$, even though they perform qualitatively different computations. An attention layer might benefit from reaching back to distant token-level information while an MLP might operate best on local transformations; the uniform residual gives them no way to express this preference.

2. **Irreversible information loss.** Once information enters the accumulated residual stream, it cannot be selectively recovered. If layer 3 produces a useful representation that gets diluted by subsequent additions, layer 30 has no way to "look back" and isolate layer 3's contribution from the noise of layers 4–29. The information is still there in principle (since summation is invertible given all summands), but in practice it is buried.

3. **Output magnitude growth.** This is the PreNorm dilution problem formalized by Li et al. (2026) and Xiong et al. (2020). Under PreNorm (which applies layer normalization *before* each sublayer, making the residual path an identity), the hidden state magnitude $\|h_l\|$ grows as $O(L)$ with depth. Each layer's output $f_l(h_l)$ becomes a progressively smaller fraction of the total. To compensate — to remain influential — deeper layers are forced to learn increasingly large output magnitudes from fixed-scale normalized inputs. This is empirically visible: deeper layers produce outputs with larger norms, creating an unstable dynamic where early-layer contributions are progressively drowned out. Li et al. (2026) further argue that this growth fundamentally limits effective network depth.

The paper notes that a "significant fraction of layers can be pruned with minimal loss" (Gromov et al., 2025), which is consistent with the idea that uniform accumulation causes later layers to add diminishing marginal value — they're mostly echoing already-accumulated information rather than selectively building on specific earlier representations.

### Why This Matters: Practical and Theoretical Stakes

The problem is not merely a question of architectural aesthetics. It has direct consequences for both how models train and what they can learn.

**Training efficiency.** The scaling law experiments in Section 5.1 demonstrate that AttnRes achieves the same validation loss as a PreNorm baseline using roughly 1.25× less training compute. For the largest models in this paper (48B total parameters, 1.4T tokens), this represents a substantial reduction in GPU-hours. More importantly, the scaling curves (Figure 4) show that the gap persists and widens with compute — the fitted curves for AttnRes lie below the baseline across all compute budgets, with similar slopes but lower intercepts. This suggests the improvement is not a one-off at small scale but reflects a fundamental efficiency gain.

**Downstream capability.** The benchmark results in Table 3 are telling in their pattern. AttnRes provides the largest gains on tasks requiring multi-step compositional reasoning: GPQA-Diamond (+7.5 points), Minerva Math (+3.6), HumanEval (+3.1). Knowledge-recall tasks like MMLU (+1.1) and TriviaQA (+1.9) show smaller but still positive improvements. This pattern aligns with the hypothesis that AttnRes's mechanism — allowing later layers to selectively retrieve specific earlier representations — is particularly valuable when solving a problem requires combining information from multiple computational stages, each building on the previous.

**Training dynamics and stability.** Figure 5 reveals that AttnRes fundamentally alters how information and gradients flow through depth. Under PreNorm, output magnitudes grow monotonically with layer index — deeper layers are working harder just to be heard. Gradients concentrate disproportionately in the earliest layers (the ones closest to the loss through the identity path). AttnRes bounds output magnitudes within each block (a periodic pattern rather than monotonic growth) and produces a more uniform gradient distribution. This matters for training stability at extreme depths and for whether additional layers actually contribute to model capacity.

**Theoretical significance.** The paper positions AttnRes as completing a conceptual arc for the depth dimension that has already proven transformative for sequences. RNNs over time were replaced by Transformers with self-attention; RNNs over depth (which is what standard residuals are) should similarly be replaceable by depth-wise attention. This is not just analogy — Section 6.1 formalizes a concrete mathematical duality, and Section 6.2 shows that prior residual variants all correspond to depth-wise *linear* attention, making AttnRes the depth-wise *softmax* attention that completes the transition. Establishing this framework creates a unified language for discussing and designing depth-wise connectivity, and it opens the door to importing innovations from sequence-wise attention (efficient attention variants, positional encodings, etc.) into the depth dimension.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper's survey of related work (Section 7, Table 5) is organized around a taxonomy based on *what information each layer can access*. Understanding this taxonomy is essential to seeing where AttnRes fits and why prior methods are insufficient.

#### Category 1: Single-State Recurrence — Layer $l$ only sees $h_{l-1}$

These methods all preserve the basic recurrence form $h_l = \text{update}(h_{l-1}, f_{l-1}(h_{l-1}))$. They modify *how* the update works but not *what* information is accessible.

- **Standard residual** [He et al., 2015]: $h_l = h_{l-1} + f_{l-1}(h_{l-1})$. Fixed weights, no gating.
- **Highway networks** [Srivastava et al., 2015]: $h_l = (1-g_l) \odot h_{l-1} + g_l \odot f_{l-1}(h_{l-1})$, where $g_l$ are learned element-wise gates. This introduces input-dependence in the mixing ratio but still only accesses the compressed state $h_{l-1}$.
- **ReZero** [Bachlechner et al., 2020] and **LayerScale** [Touvron et al., 2021]: Add learned scalar or vector multipliers to the residual branch. Static after training — no input-dependence.
- **DeepNorm** [Wang et al., 2022]: Scales the residual with a fixed constant and adds normalization: $h_l = \text{Norm}(\alpha h_{l-1} + f_{l-1}(h_{l-1}))$. Addresses magnitude growth but doesn't enable selective access.
- **KEEL** [Chen and Wei, 2026]: Adds normalization to the residual input and scales the skip connection.

All of these share the fundamental limitation: $h_{l-1}$ is a single vector that conflates $h_1, f_1(h_1), f_2(h_2), ..., f_{l-2}(h_{l-2})$ into one undifferentiated sum. Even with learned gating, a layer cannot say "I want more of $f_3(h_3)$ and less of $f_7(h_7)$" because those individual contributions are not separately accessible.

The paper draws an explicit parallel here to sequence modeling: this is exactly the bottleneck that RNNs faced — compressing an entire sequence into a single hidden state — and the parallel explains why prior work on "better residual gating" has yielded only incremental improvements.

#### Category 2: Multi-State Recurrence — Layer $l$ sees $m$ streams

These methods widen the recurrent state to $m$ parallel streams, giving each layer access to a matrix $H_{l-1} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}$ rather than a single vector. Individual streams can specialize to carry different types of information, partially alleviating the compression problem.

- **Hyper-Connections** [Zhu et al., 2025], **mHC** [Xie et al., 2026]: Maintain $m$ streams updated via $H_l = H_{l-1}A_l + f_{l-1}(H_{l-1}\alpha_{l-1})\beta_{l-1}^\top$, where $A_l \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m}$ transitions between streams, $\alpha_{l-1}$ mixes streams into the layer input, and $\beta_{l-1}$ distributes the output back. The $m \times m$ transition matrices make the effective depth-mixing matrix $m$-semiseparable, allowing richer cross-layer patterns.
- **DDL** [Zhang et al., 2026]: Maintains a matrix state updated via a delta-rule erase-and-write mechanism: $H_l = (I - \beta_l k_l k_l^\top)H_{l-1} + \beta_l k_l v_l^\top$.
- **SiameseNorm** [Li et al., 2026]: Maintains two parameter-shared streams — one PreNorm (identity gradient path) and one PostNorm (bounded representations) — to reconcile the PreNorm/PostNorm trade-off.

While these methods expand the state capacity, they remain fundamentally limited by the **same input constraint**: each layer still conditions on its immediate predecessor's state. An individual stream at layer $l$ is a mixture of all prior layers' contributions across all streams, not a clean retrieval of any specific earlier layer's output. The paper notes (Section 7) that AttnRes is "orthogonal" to these methods — it provides selective access to individual earlier-layer outputs while remaining compatible with multi-stream recurrence.

#### Category 3: Cross-Layer Access — Layer $l$ can see individual earlier outputs

This is the category that most directly anticipates AttnRes. These methods grant each layer direct access to (some or all) previous layer outputs.

**Static-weight methods** assign fixed coefficients to earlier layers, learned during training but frozen at inference:

- **DenseNet** [Huang et al., 2018] (from computer vision): Concatenates all preceding feature maps and applies convolution-based pooling. Not tested at LLM scale.
- **ELMo** [Peters et al., 2018]: Learns scalar weights for each layer's output, computing a weighted sum. This is the simplest form of cross-layer attention — effectively learned, input-independent $\alpha_i$ — but ELMo used only 2–3 layers, not 50+.
- **DenseFormer** [Pagliardini et al., 2024]: Assigns learned scalar coefficients $\alpha_{i \to l}$ to each $(i, l)$ pair, trained with the model. The key limitation: these coefficients are **static** — they do not depend on the input token. The paper's ablation (Table 4) shows DenseFormer achieves no improvement over the PreNorm baseline (both 1.766–1.767), demonstrating that input-independence is crippling. The same weight combination is applied whether the token is "the" or a complex mathematical expression.
- **ANCRe** [Zhang et al., 2026]: Similar static weight approach with adaptive reassignment.

**Dynamic-weight methods** make the aggregation weights depend on the input:

- **MRLA** [Fang et al., 2023, originally for vision]: Uses element-wise sigmoid gating over all previous layers, with a separable query-key product. The paper notes this is "closer to linear attention than softmax-based retrieval" — it lacks the competitive normalization of softmax that forces sharper selection among sources.
- **MUDDFormer** [Xiao et al., 2025]: Uses a small MLP to generate position-dependent weights across four decoupled streams. More expressive but also more complex and less analyzed at scale.
- **Value Residual Learning** [Zhou et al., 2025]: Access to only a single earlier layer (not all), with the target layer selected via attention.
- **LAuReL** [Menghani et al., 2025]: Augments the residual with low-rank projections over the previous $k$ activations, trading full cross-layer access for efficiency.

**Where all prior cross-layer methods fall short.** The paper identifies several gaps:

1. **Static weights don't work at scale.** DenseFormer's failure to improve over PreNorm (Table 4, loss 1.767 vs. 1.766 baseline) is a key datapoint. If cross-layer access is to help, the aggregation must be content-dependent — whether a token should look back to the embedding or to recent layers depends on what that token represents.

2. **Scalability is untreated.** Most cross-layer methods were developed in vision or small-scale NLP contexts. The $O(Ld)$ memory for storing every layer's output (needed for backpropagation) was not a concern when $L \approx 12$ and $d \approx 512$. For a 54-layer MoE Transformer with $d$ in the thousands and pipeline parallelism across dozens of GPUs, this becomes the central obstacle. The paper's infrastructure design (Section 4) — cross-stage caching, two-phase computation, sequence-sharded KV storage — is what makes cross-layer attention *deployable* at modern LLM scale, and this engineering contribution is arguably as important as the architectural insight.

3. **No unified theoretical framework.** Prior methods were proposed as ad hoc architectural modifications. The paper's structured-matrix analysis (Section 6.2) — showing that all residual variants correspond to depth mixing matrices $M$ with different semiseparable ranks — provides a lens for understanding *why* different methods perform differently and how to design better ones. Highway = 1-semiseparable, mHC = $m$-semiseparable, Full AttnRes = dense (rank-$L$). Block AttnRes interpolates between these extremes, with effective rank between $N$ and $N+S$.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is explicit and multi-layered.

**First, as completing the sequence-depth duality.** The introduction and Section 3 frame the core idea through an analogy: "The situation parallels the challenges that recurrent neural networks (RNNs) faced over the sequence dimension before attention mechanism provided an alternative." The argument is that just as the field moved from recurrence (RNNs) to attention (Transformers) for sequences, it should similarly move from recurrence (fixed residual accumulation) to attention (learned depth-wise aggregation) for depth. AttnRes is not a tweak to residuals — it is the same category transition that transformed sequence modeling, applied to a different axis.

**Second, as unifying prior work under a common framework.** Rather than positioning AttnRes as competing with prior residual variants, Section 6.2 shows that these variants are all special cases of depth-wise linear attention, with AttnRes generalizing them to depth-wise softmax attention. This reframes the entire literature as points on a continuum: standard residual is the simplest case (all-ones mixing matrix), Highway adds input-dependent gating (1-semiseparable), mHC expands the state ($m$-semiseparable), and AttnRes makes the matrix dense with softmax-normalized weights. The paper is not rejecting prior work but showing it was climbing the same ladder.

**Third, as practical engineering, not just theory.** The paper explicitly acknowledges that Full AttnRes's $O(Ld)$ memory is currently prohibitive for large-scale training with pipeline parallelism (Section 3.1): "We anticipate that future interconnect improvements will make the full $O(Ld)$ communication practical, fully realizing the potential of Full AttnRes." For now, Block AttnRes and the infrastructure optimizations (cross-stage caching, two-phase computation) make the approach deployable today with less than 4% training overhead and less than 2% inference latency overhead. This practicality-first stance — developing a slightly less expressive variant that can actually run at scale, with rigorous accounting for system costs — distinguishes the paper from purely conceptual proposals.

**Fourth, as a drop-in replacement.** The paper emphasizes that AttnRes requires "only one RMSNorm and one pseudo-query vector $w_l \in \mathbb{R}^d$ per layer, amounting to a negligible fraction of the total parameter count" and that "all pseudo-query vectors must be initialized to zero" to ensure the method reduces to an equal-weight average at the start of training. This minimizes barrier to adoption: no hyperparameter retuning, no architecture redesign, no training recipe changes. The method slots into existing Transformer training pipelines with the same hyperparameters, which the paper demonstrates by using identical settings for baseline and AttnRes in all scaling law experiments (Section 5.1, Table 2). This is a deliberate choice to make the comparison conservative — the baseline's hyperparameters are optimized for the baseline, so AttnRes is operating at a slight disadvantage.

**Finally, as validated through comprehensive empirical analysis.** The paper provides three distinct forms of evidence: scaling laws (Section 5.1) showing the gap persists across model sizes; large-scale training with downstream evaluation (Section 5.2) showing real-world benefit on a 48B-parameter model; and ablations (Section 5.3) confirming that each design choice (softmax over sigmoid, input-dependence over static weights, RMSNorm on keys, block structure) is individually justified. The architecture sweep analysis (Section 5.4.1, Figure 7) goes a step further, showing that AttnRes shifts the optimal depth-width trade-off toward deeper, narrower networks — evidence that the method fundamentally changes how depth can be utilized, not just adds a small constant improvement.

## 3. Technical Approach

This is primarily a **systems + architecture paper** whose core idea is that replacing fixed, uniform residual accumulation with learned, input-dependent softmax attention over preceding layer outputs lets each layer selectively retrieve information from any earlier point in the network, mitigating the PreNorm dilution problem with negligible training and inference overhead at scale.

---

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system being built is **a drop-in replacement for standard residual connections in Transformer models** that allows each layer to attend over all (or blocks of) preceding layer outputs with learned attention weights, rather than receiving an equal-weighted sum. It solves the problem that PreNorm residual accumulation causes hidden-state magnitudes to grow uncontrollably with depth, progressively diluting each layer's contribution — and it does so with a lightweight mechanism (one learnable vector per layer) and infrastructure optimizations that keep training overhead under 4% and inference overhead under 2%.

---

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components:

1. **Pseudo-Query Vectors** — One learned vector $w_l \in \mathbb{R}^d$ per layer, decoupled from the layer's forward computation. These serve as queries in the depth-wise attention, determining which previous layers each layer attends to.

2. **Depth-Wise Key-Value Store** — The outputs of all (or blocks of) preceding layers, which serve as both keys and values in the attention computation. For Full AttnRes, this is every $f_i(h_i)$ for $i < l$, plus the token embedding $h_1$. For Block AttnRes, this is $N$ block-level summaries plus the current block's partial sum.

3. **Softmax Attention over Depth** — The core operation: for each layer $l$, compute attention weights $\alpha_{i \to l}$ over preceding keys using the pseudo-query $w_l$, apply RMSNorm to the keys, then compute a weighted sum of the values. This replaces the standard $h_l = h_{l-1} + f_{l-1}(h_{l-1})$ with $h_l = \sum_i \alpha_{i \to l} \cdot v_i$.

4. **Block Partitioning (for Block AttnRes)** — Layers are grouped into $N$ blocks of $S = L/N$ layers. Within each block, layer outputs are summed to form a single block representation $b_n$. Cross-block attention operates only over the $N$ block representations plus the intra-block partial sum, reducing memory and communication from $O(Ld)$ to $O(Nd)$.

5. **Infrastructure Optimizations** — Cross-stage caching under pipeline parallelism (eliminating redundant block transmissions), a two-phase computation strategy (batching inter-block queries, merging with intra-block results via online softmax), and sequence-sharded KV storage for memory-efficient prefilling. These make Block AttnRes practical at scale with near-zero overhead.

Information flows as follows: the token embedding enters as $h_1$ (stored as $b_0$) → for each layer $l$, the pseudo-query $w_l$ attends over all preceding block representations $b_0, ..., b_{n-1}$ and the current partial sum $b_n^{i-1}$ → the attention-weighted sum becomes the input to the sublayer (attention or MLP) → the sublayer's output is added to the partial sum → at block boundaries, the completed partial sum is appended to the block list → at the final layer, all $N$ block representations are aggregated to produce the output.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the formal depth-wise attention mechanism (Full AttnRes)** — the kernel function, query/key/value definitions, normalization, and how this departs from the standard residual recurrence. This is the conceptual core.

- **Second, Block AttnRes** — why full attention is infrastructurally challenging at scale, the intra-block summation and inter-block attention formulation, the pseudo-code, and how $N$ interpolates between standard residuals and Full AttnRes.

- **Third, the training infrastructure** — the challenge of pipeline parallelism with cross-layer access, cross-stage caching that reduces communication from $O(C^2)$ to $O(P^2)$, and why activation checkpointing keeps per-layer memory identical to standard architectures.

- **Fourth, the inference infrastructure** — the two-phase computation strategy (batched inter-block queries + sequential intra-block with online softmax merge), the I/O analysis showing $O(S+N)$ per-layer reads vs. naive $O(L)$, and the sequence-sharded prefilling scheme for long contexts.

- **Fifth, key design decisions and their justifications** — zero-initialized pseudo-queries, learned vs. input-dependent queries, softmax vs. sigmoid, RMSNorm on keys, block count selection, and why these choices matter for training stability and performance.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### Full Attention Residuals: The Core Mechanism

**The depth-wise mixing formula.** Full AttnRes replaces the standard recurrence $h_l = h_{l-1} + f_{l-1}(h_{l-1})$ with a weighted sum over all preceding layer outputs and the token embedding:

$$h_l = \sum_{i=0}^{l-1} \alpha_{i \to l} \cdot v_i$$

where index $i=0$ corresponds to the token embedding (so $v_0 = h_1$), indices $1 \leq i \leq l-1$ correspond to layer outputs $v_i = f_i(h_i)$, and $\alpha_{i \to l} \geq 0$ are attention weights satisfying $\sum_{i=0}^{l-1} \alpha_{i \to l} = 1$. Each layer $l$ computes its own set of attention weights over the same pool of preceding outputs, allowing different layers to attend to different sources — layer 5's attention layer might focus on the embedding while layer 5's MLP focuses on the immediately preceding layer.

**What this computes:** for each layer $l$, a convex combination of all previous representations where the mixing coefficients are determined by a learned similarity function between a layer-specific query vector and each preceding output. The normalization constraint $\sum_i \alpha_{i \to l} = 1$ ensures the result is a proper weighted average rather than an unbounded sum.

**Why this form over the standard recurrence:** the standard recurrence $h_l = \sum_{i=0}^{l-1} v_i$ (with implicit coefficients of $1$) gives equal weight to every preceding output regardless of relevance. AttnRes's weighted form lets layer $l$ suppress irrelevant earlier outputs (by assigning them near-zero weight) and amplify relevant ones (by assigning them higher weight), implementing content-dependent selective retrieval that the uniform sum cannot express.

**The kernel function for attention weights.** The attention weights are computed via a kernel function $\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ applied to the query and each key, followed by softmax normalization:

$$\alpha_{i \to l} = \frac{\phi(w_l, k_i)}{\sum_{j=0}^{l-1} \phi(w_l, k_j)}$$

where the specific kernel chosen is:

$$\phi(q, k) = \exp\left(q^\top \text{RMSNorm}(k)\right)$$

with the query $q = w_l$ being a learned parameter vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$, and the keys $k_i = v_i$ being the preceding layer outputs (or the embedding for $i=0$). RMSNorm is applied to the keys before the dot product to normalize their magnitudes.

**What this computes:** the unnormalized attention score between layer $l$'s query and source $i$'s key is the exponential of their dot product, where the key has been RMS-normalized to unit scale. Softmax then converts these scores into a probability distribution over sources. The exponential ensures positivity and creates a sharp distinction between relevant and irrelevant sources (unlike linear kernels that produce softer weighting).

**Why this kernel form:** the exponential kernel with RMSNorm achieves two properties simultaneously. First, RMSNorm on the keys prevents individual layers with naturally large output magnitudes from dominating the attention weights — without it, a single layer producing outputs with norm $10\times$ larger than others would absorb nearly all attention mass regardless of relevance. Second, the softmax normalization creates competition among sources: increasing weight on one source necessarily decreases weight on others. This competitive pressure forces the model to make meaningful selection decisions rather than attending uniformly to everything. The authors validate both choices in ablations (Table 4): removing softmax in favor of sigmoid degrades performance (loss 1.741 vs. 1.737), and removing RMSNorm from keys degrades both Full and Block AttnRes (1.743 and 1.750 respectively).

**The query, key, and value definitions.** For each layer $l$, the attention triplet is defined as:

$$q_l = w_l, \quad k_i = v_i = \begin{cases} h_1 & i = 0 \\ f_i(h_i) & 1 \leq i \leq l-1 \end{cases}$$

where $w_l \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a learned pseudo-query vector specific to layer $l$ (one per layer, not per token). Crucially, $w_l$ is a parameter decoupled from the layer's forward computation — it does not depend on the current hidden state or token representation. This is a deliberate design choice that enables the two-phase inference optimization described below, as all queries within a block can be batched together without waiting for sequential outputs.

**What this computes:** the query is a static learned embedding that encodes "what kind of information layer $l$ typically needs," while the keys and values are the actual content produced by earlier layers. The attention score $\phi(w_l, k_i)$ measures compatibility between layer $l$'s information needs and what source $i$ produced. Since $w_l$ is content-independent, this creates a *layer-type to layer-output* matching: attention layers and MLP layers each have their own learned queries (one $w_l$ for the pre-attention AttnRes and one for the pre-MLP AttnRes), letting them learn different depth-wise connectivity patterns.

**Why decouple the query from the hidden state:** an alternative design would make the query input-dependent by projecting from the current hidden state: $q_l = \text{Proj}(h_{l-1})$. The authors test this and find it *further improves* performance (loss 1.731 vs. 1.737 for learned query, Table 4), but reject it for practical reasons: it "introduces a $d \times d$ projection per layer and requires sequential memory access during decoding." The learned pseudo-query enables batching across layers and avoids adding a parameter-heavy projection to every sublayer. The small performance gap suggests that layer identity alone carries most of the signal about which sources to attend to, with token-level variation being a second-order effect.

**Zero initialization of pseudo-queries.** All $w_l$ vectors must be initialized to zero. This is a critical detail that the paper "validated empirically" to prevent training volatility. With $w_l = 0$, the dot product $w_l^\top \text{RMSNorm}(k_i)$ is zero for all $i$, so $\phi(w_l, k_i) = \exp(0) = 1$, making all attention weights equal: $\alpha_{i \to l} = 1/l$. At initialization, Full AttnRes therefore behaves identically to standard residuals (an equal-weight average of all preceding outputs), and the model gradually learns to deviate from this uniform baseline as training progresses.

**Why zero initialization:** standard random initialization of $w_l$ would create arbitrary, asymmetric attention patterns at the start of training, potentially causing large initial gradient variance as different layers receive very different blended inputs. Zero initialization ensures a smooth transition from the standard residual behavior that the optimizer already handles well, with attention specialization emerging only as the loss signal provides meaningful gradient information about which sources are useful to which layers.

**Computational and memory footprint.** For a single token, Full AttnRes requires $O(L^2 d)$ arithmetic to compute all attention weights (each of $L$ layers attends over $O(L)$ sources with $d$-dimensional dot products) and $O(Ld)$ memory to store all layer outputs. However, the authors note that this "overlaps entirely with the activations already retained for backpropagation" — the per-layer outputs $f_i(h_i)$ must be stored for the backward pass regardless of whether AttnRes uses them. In standard (non-pipeline-parallel, non-activation-checkpointed) training, Full AttnRes therefore adds no additional memory overhead.

**The practical bottleneck at scale.** Under large-scale training with activation checkpointing and pipeline parallelism, every layer output that would otherwise be freed and recomputed must now be kept alive for all subsequent layers, and under pipeline parallelism each must be transmitted across stage boundaries. Both the memory and communication overhead then grow as $O(Ld)$ — prohibitive for models with 50+ layers and hidden dimensions in the thousands distributed across dozens of GPUs. The paper notes that "the dominant cost is not local memory bandwidth but cross-stage communication," and that "this $O(Ld)$ communication overhead cannot be alleviated by local batching." This motivates Block AttnRes, which "reduces the number of cross-stage representations from $L$ to $N$."

#### Block Attention Residuals: Making Cross-Layer Attention Scalable

**The core idea.** Block AttnRes partitions the $L$ layers into $N$ blocks of $S = L/N$ layers each (assuming divisibility; otherwise the last block takes the remainder). Within each block, all layer outputs are summed into a single block representation; across blocks, full softmax attention is applied over only the $N$ block representations plus the token embedding and the current intra-block partial sum. This reduces memory and communication from $O(Ld)$ to $O(Nd)$ while preserving the ability to selectively attend to information from distant earlier blocks.

**Intra-block accumulation.** For block $n$ containing layer indices $\mathcal{B}_n$, the block representation is:

$$b_n = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{B}_n} f_j(h_j)$$

where $b_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a single vector summarizing the entire block's computation. We further define $b_n^i = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{B}n, j < i} f_j(h_j)$ as the partial sum over the first $i$ layers in block $n$, with $b_n^0 = 0$ and $b_n^S = b_n$. The token embedding $h_1$ is stored as $b_0$ and always included as a source.

**What this computes:** each block's layers are compressed into one vector by simple summation. This is lossy compression — the individual layer outputs are no longer individually accessible — but it preserves the block's aggregate contribution to the residual stream. The partial sum $b_n^i$ captures the evolving intra-block computation, allowing later layers in the same block to attend to recent within-block work.

**Why summation rather than concatenation or averaging:** concatenation would produce a representation of size $S \cdot d$, defeating the memory reduction goal. Averaging ($b_n = \frac{1}{S} \sum f_j(h_j)$) would keep magnitudes bounded but would dampen the signal from larger blocks. Summation preserves the natural magnitude growth that occurs within a block (mirroring standard residuals), and RMSNorm on the keys in the inter-block attention prevents these larger block representations from biasing the attention weights. The authors note that "the RMSNorm inside $\phi$ prevents magnitude differences between complete blocks and partial sums from biasing the attention weights."

**Inter-block attention.** For the $i$-th layer in block $n$, the value matrix is:

$$V = \begin{cases} [b_0, b_1, ..., b_{n-1}]^\top & \text{if } i = 1 \text{ (first layer of block } n) \\ [b_0, b_1, ..., b_{n-1}, b_n^{i-1}]^\top & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

The first layer of each block sees only completed previous block representations and the token embedding. Subsequent layers additionally see the partial sum $b_n^{i-1}$ containing the outputs of earlier layers in the same block. Keys are the RMSNorm-ed values, queries are the learned pseudo-vectors $w_l$, and attention weights follow the same softmax formulation as Full AttnRes.

**What this computes:** each layer computes attention over a pool of $n$ or $n+1$ sources (rather than $l$ sources in Full AttnRes). The inter-block sources provide coarse-grained access to distant earlier computation; the intra-block partial sum provides fine-grained access to recent within-block computation. The first layer of block $n$ effectively "reads in" the summary of all previous blocks, while subsequent layers within the block can refine this with local information.

**Why this structure works:** it mirrors the observation from the ablation study (Section 5.3) that "selectively accessing distant layers matters more than attending to many nearby ones." The block summaries provide the long-range selective access (which block $n-3$ contributed something useful?), while the intra-block partial sum preserves the standard residual behavior locally (each layer still gets the sum of recent outputs). The sliding-window ablation in Table 4 confirms this: retaining only the most recent $W=8$ layer outputs plus the embedding (loss 1.764) significantly underperforms Block AttnRes (loss 1.746) because it cannot reach back to distant earlier layers. Block AttnRes provides exactly this long-range access through the coarse block summaries.

**The interpolation property.** The block count $N$ interpolates between two extremes: $N = L$ (block size $S = 1$) recovers Full AttnRes exactly (each block is one layer), while $N = 1$ (block size $S = L$) reduces to standard residual connections with the embedding isolated as $b_0$ (since within the single block, the partial sum behaves like the standard residual state, and the only inter-block source is $b_0$). Empirically, the authors find that "$N \approx 8$ recovers most of the benefit across model scales" (Figure 6), with block sizes $S=2, 4, 8$ all achieving similar loss near 1.746 while larger blocks ($S=16, 32$) degrade toward the baseline.

**PyTorch-style pseudo-code.** Figure 2 provides the concrete implementation:

```python
def block_attn_res(blocks: list[Tensor], partial_block: Tensor, proj: Linear, norm: RMSNorm) -> Tensor:
    V = torch.stack(blocks + [partial_block])        # [N+1, B, T, D]
    K = norm(V)
    logits = torch.einsum('d, n b t d -> n b t', proj.weight.squeeze(), K)
    h = torch.einsum('n b t, n b t d -> b t d', logits.softmax(0), V)
    return h
```

The `proj.weight` (shape $[1, d]$) is the learned pseudo-query $w_l^\top$, which is dotted with each key via einsum. The softmax is taken over the source dimension (dimension 0, corresponding to the $N+1$ sources), and the resulting weights are applied to the values $V$ to produce the blended hidden state $h$.

**The forward pass integration.** The pseudo-code shows how Block AttnRes is inserted into a standard Transformer layer: before the self-attention sublayer, `block_attn_res` is called with the completed blocks and current partial sum to produce the input $h$; the self-attention operates on this blended representation; its output is added to the partial sum; then before the MLP sublayer, `block_attn_res` is called again (with a separate learned query $w_l$ for the MLP's depth attention) to produce the MLP's input; the MLP output is added to the partial sum. At block boundaries (every $S/2$ Transformer layers, since each block in this MoE architecture contains both attention and MLP layers), the completed partial sum is appended to `blocks` and a new partial sum is started.

**Why separate queries for attention and MLP:** the authors observe in Section 5.4.2 (Figure 8) that "pre-MLP inputs show sharper diagonal reliance on recent representations, while pre-attention inputs maintain broader receptive fields." This suggests that attention and MLP layers benefit from different depth-wise connectivity patterns: attention layers want to integrate information across many earlier layers (broader receptive field), while MLPs operate more locally. Providing separate learned queries $w_l$ for each sublayer type allows this specialization to emerge naturally during training.

#### Training Infrastructure: Cross-Stage Caching for Pipeline Parallelism

**The pipeline communication challenge.** Under pipeline parallelism with an interleaved schedule (1F1B), the model is partitioned across $P$ physical stages, each processing $V$ virtual stages (micro-batches). With standard residuals, adjacent stages only need to exchange the current hidden state $h_l$ — a fixed-size tensor of shape $[B, T, d]$. With Block AttnRes, each stage additionally needs access to all accumulated block representations for inter-block attention. Naïvely transmitting the full block history at every stage transition incurs redundant communication because the same blocks are transmitted multiple times across different virtual stages on the same physical device.

**Why naïve transmission is redundant.** Consider a setup with $P=4$ physical ranks, $V=2$ virtual stages per rank, producing on average $N_p$ block representations per physical stage. The total number of chunks across all virtual stages is $C = PV$. In the forward pass, the $j$-th chunk accumulates $j N_p$ blocks. Naïvely transmitting all accumulated blocks at every transition — as if each virtual stage were independent — incurs per-token communication cost:

$$\text{Comm}_\text{naïve} = \sum_{j=1}^{C-1} j N_p \cdot d = \frac{C(C-1)}{2} N_p d$$

which grows quadratically in $C$. For a typical setup with $P=8$, $V=4$, this means $C=32$ chunks, and the communication cost scales as $O(32^2) = O(1024)$ chunks worth of data.

**Cross-stage caching.** The key insight is that each physical stage processes multiple virtual stages in succession and can cache previously received blocks locally. The first virtual stage ($v=1$) has an empty cache and accumulates normally. For $v \geq 2$, the receiver already holds the blocks from its corresponding chunk in $v=1$, so each transition needs to convey only the $\sim P N_p$ *incremental* blocks accumulated since then. Total communication reduces to:

$$\text{Comm}_\text{cached} = \underbrace{\frac{P(P-1)}{2} N_p d}_{\text{first virtual stage}} + \underbrace{(V-1) P^2 N_p d}_{\text{subsequent virtual stages}}$$

The first virtual stage costs $O(P^2)$ (quadratic in physical stages only, not virtual stages), and each subsequent virtual stage adds $O(P^2)$ rather than $O(C^2)$. The peak per-transition cost drops from $O(C)$ to $O(P)$, a $V\times$ reduction.

**What this computes:** the total number of block-sized tensors transmitted across all pipeline stage boundaries during one forward (or backward) pass. For a concrete example with $P=4$, $V=2$, $N_p=1$, the paper's Figure 3 illustrates that caching eliminates 6 redundant block transmissions out of a naïve total, roughly halving the communication volume.

**Why this works:** pipeline schedules are deterministic — at any point in the schedule, the blocks that a stage has previously received are known. Since the same physical GPU processes multiple virtual stages, its local memory persists across virtual stage boundaries. Caching simply remembers what was received earlier and only transmits what is new. The backward pass benefits from an identical scheme (blocks needed for gradients are the same blocks needed for the forward pass, transmitted in reverse order).

**Memory overhead of caching.** Each block representation is stored exactly once across all $V$ virtual stages on a given physical device, since the second and subsequent virtual stages reuse the cached copy. The per-device memory cost for block caching is $N \cdot d$ elements per token (all $N$ blocks, each of size $d$), which is negligible compared to standard per-layer activation caches (which store tens to hundreds of activations per layer, each of shape $[B, T, d]$ or larger for intermediate attention states).

**Interaction with activation checkpointing.** Activation checkpointing discards intermediate activations during the forward pass and recomputes them during the backward pass. With Block AttnRes, the inter-block attention intermediates (attention logits, softmax outputs) are checkpointed and recomputed, so they do not add to the persistent memory footprint. The checkpointed input to each layer $p_l$ (the blended representation $h$ produced by `block_attn_res`) has the same memory size as the hidden state $h_l$ it replaces in standard architectures — one $d$-dimensional vector per token per layer. The paper states that "the per-layer activation footprint remains identical to standard architectures."

**Measured training overhead.** The paper reports that Block AttnRes "adds negligible training overhead when pipeline parallelism is not enabled; under pipeline parallelism, the measured end-to-end overhead is less than 4%." This 4% includes the additional cross-stage communication (even after caching), the inter-block attention computation, and any synchronization overhead. It is measured on the full 48B-parameter model training setup.

#### Inference Infrastructure: Two-Phase Computation and Memory-Efficient Prefilling

**The inference challenge.** During autoregressive decoding, Block AttnRes must compute inter-block attention for every generated token at every layer. A naïve implementation would scan all $N$ block representations at each of the $L$ layers, incurring $O(L \cdot N)$ memory accesses to the block cache. Since $L$ can be 50+ and $N \approx 8-10$, this is $O(500d)$ reads per token just for the attention residual computation. Additionally, during prefilling (processing the input prompt), the block representations for all $T$ sequence positions must be stored, requiring $N \cdot T \cdot d$ elements — for a 128K-token sequence with $N=8$ and $d=2048$, this is approximately 15 GB.

**Two-phase computation strategy.** The key enabling observation is that the pseudo-queries $w_l$ are learned parameters independent of the token representation. All $S$ queries within a block $\{w_l\}_{l \in \mathcal{B}_n}$ are therefore known before the block's computation begins. This allows the inter-block attention to be computed *in parallel* for all layers in the block, amortizing the memory access cost of reading the block cache.

**Phase 1: Batched inter-block attention.** All $S$ queries in block $n$ are stacked into a matrix $Q \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times d}$, and a single batched attention operation is performed against the cached block representations $[b_0, ..., b_{n-1}] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$. The output is $S$ intermediate results $\{o_l^{(1)}\}_{l \in \mathcal{B}_n}$, along with the softmax statistics (max logit $m_l^{(1)}$ and log-sum-exp $\ell_l^{(1)}$) needed for the subsequent online softmax merge:

$$\{o_l^{(1)}, m_l^{(1)}, \ell_l^{(1)}\}_{l \in \mathcal{B}_n} \leftarrow \text{ATTNWITHSTATS}(Q, K, V)$$

where $K = \text{RMSNorm}([b_0; ...; b_{n-1}])$ and $V = [b_0; ...; b_{n-1}]$. This reads the $n$ cached block representations once (not $S$ times) and produces one output per layer.

**What this computes:** for each layer in the block, the weighted sum of the completed block representations, weighted by softmax-normalized attention scores between that layer's query and each block's key. The softmax statistics capture the scale of the attention distribution, which is necessary because Phase 2 introduces additional sources that must be merged while preserving the correct normalization.

**Why batch over layers:** the block cache is shared across all layers in the block. Reading it $S$ times sequentially would incur $S \cdot n \cdot d$ reads; batching reduces this to $n \cdot d$ reads (plus $S \cdot n \cdot d$ compute for the dot products, but compute is cheap relative to memory bandwidth on modern GPUs). The paper's I/O analysis (Table 1) quantifies this: Phase 1 amortized reads per layer are $(N/S) \cdot d$ (where $N/S = n$ is the number of preceding blocks), compared to $N \cdot d$ for the naïve per-layer approach — a $S\times$ reduction.

**Phase 2: Sequential intra-block attention with online softmax merge.** Within the block, each layer $i$ processes its intra-block dependency sequentially. For the first layer of the block ($i=0$), there are no intra-block sources, so the output is simply the Phase 1 result:

$$h_l = o_l^{(1)} / \ell_l^{(1)} \quad \text{(dividing by the sum of weights to convert from unnormalized to normalized)}$$

For subsequent layers ($i \geq 1$), the intra-block attention is computed against the partial sum $b_n^{i-1}$:

$$o_l^{(2)}, m_l^{(2)}, \ell_l^{(2)} \leftarrow \text{ATTNWITHSTATS}(w_l, \text{RMSNorm}(b_n^{i-1}), b_n^{i-1})$$

which is a single-source attention (the dot product of $w_l$ with the RMSNorm-ed partial sum, exponentiated). The two attention results (inter-block and intra-block) are then merged using online softmax [Milakov and Gimelshein, 2018]:

$$m_l = \max(m_l^{(1)}, m_l^{(2)})$$

$$h_l = \frac{e^{m_l^{(1)} - m_l} o_l^{(1)} + e^{m_l^{(2)} - m_l} o_l^{(2)}}{e^{m_l^{(1)} - m_l} \ell_l^{(1)} + e^{m_l^{(2)} - m_l} \ell_l^{(2)}}$$

**What this computes:** the correctly-normalized softmax-weighted average over *all* sources (inter-block + intra-block), computed without re-reading the inter-block sources and without re-computing the full softmax denominator. The online softmax merge uses the stored statistics $(m^{(1)}, \ell^{(1)})$ from Phase 1 to adjust the scale of the inter-block result so that it can be combined with the intra-block result as if they were computed jointly. This preserves exact mathematical equivalence to computing softmax over all $n+1$ sources simultaneously.

**Why online softmax rather than re-computing:** re-computing the full softmax over all $n+1$ sources at each layer would require re-reading all $n$ block representations, defeating the purpose of Phase 1's batched read. Online softmax avoids this by storing the per-query statistics $(m^{(1)}, \ell^{(1)})$ — just two scalars per layer — and merging them with the intra-block statistics in $O(1)$ time per layer. The elementwise merge operations ($e^{m^{(1)}-m}$, the additions, the division) further admit kernel fusion with surrounding operations (RMSNorm, the sublayer computation), reducing I/O overhead.

**Total I/O analysis.** The paper's Table 1 provides a detailed accounting. For Block AttnRes per token per layer:

- **Phase 1 amortized reads:** $(N/S) \cdot d$ — the block cache is read once per block and amortized over $S$ layers. For typical values ($N=8$, $S=16$), this is $0.5d$ reads per layer.
- **Phase 2 reads:** $3d$ — reading the partial sum as key and value ($2d$), plus one read for the residual merge ($d$).
- **Writes:** $d$ — the blended hidden state $h_l$ (Phase 1 output or merged output) is written once per layer, plus one write for the residual merge ($d$).

Total: $(N/S + 5)d$ reads + $2d$ writes per layer. For the typical configuration, this is $5.5d$ reads + $2d$ writes = $7.5d$ total I/O — compared to $3d$ for standard residuals (reading $h_{l-1}$ as two operands for the residual merge, writing $h_l$). The overhead over standard residuals is $4.5d$ per layer, which the paper reports as "less than 2% on typical inference workloads" when measured end-to-end (since the attention and MLP sublayers themselves incur far more I/O).

**Comparison to Full AttnRes with two-phase optimization.** The two-phase strategy also applies to Full AttnRes, using the same block-partition-as-scheduling-device approach. For Full AttnRes, Phase 1 reads all preceding *individual* layer outputs (not block summaries), giving amortized reads of $(N-1)d$ (all previous blocks' layers) and Phase 2 reads of $(S-1)d$ (intra-block layers), totaling $(S+N-2)d$ reads per layer. This is substantially higher than Block AttnRes but still far better than the naïve $O(Ld)$. The paper provides the derivation in Appendix B.

**Memory-efficient prefilling.** During prefilling (processing the input prompt), block representations must be stored for all $T$ sequence positions simultaneously, since the attention residual for position $t$ needs access to the block representations computed at that same position in earlier blocks. The storage requirement is $N \cdot T \cdot d$ elements. For $N=8$, $T=128\text{K}$, $d=2048$ (fp16), this is $8 \times 131072 \times 2048 \times 2 \text{ bytes} \approx 4.3$ GB for the block cache alone. The paper mentions a 15 GB figure, likely including additional metadata or using a different configuration.

The paper mitigates this through **sequence sharding** across tensor-parallel devices. The block representations are sharded along the sequence dimension: each of the $P$ tensor-parallel ranks stores only $T/P$ positions. Phase 1 (inter-block attention) executes independently on each rank's local shard, producing local outputs. Phase 2 (online softmax merge) integrates with the standard tensor-parallel all-reduce communication path: the output is reduce-scattered across ranks, merged locally (since the intra-block partial sum depends on the full sequence context from the sublayer outputs, not the block cache), and reconstructed via all-gather. This reduces the per-device memory to $N \cdot (T/P) \cdot d$ — for $P=8$, the 128K-context example drops from 15 GB to roughly 1.9 GB per device. Combined with chunked prefill (processing the prompt in 16K-token chunks), the overhead further reduces to under 0.3 GB per device.

**Why sequence sharding works:** the inter-block attention (Phase 1) is independent across sequence positions — the attention weights for position $t$ depend only on the block representations at position $t$, not on other positions. This is in contrast to self-attention, where position $t$ attends to all other positions. The per-position independence means no cross-rank communication is needed during Phase 1, and the standard tensor-parallel reductions that already exist for the sublayer outputs (attention, MLP) can be reused to reconstruct the full sequence for Phase 2.

**Measured inference overhead.** The paper reports that "the end-to-end inference latency overhead is less than 2% on typical inference workloads." This includes the Phase 1 batched attention, Phase 2 sequential intra-block processing, the online softmax merge, and any kernel launch overhead. The low overhead is attributable to: (1) Phase 1 amortizing block cache reads across all layers in a block, (2) Phase 2 I/O being comparable to standard residual connections, (3) online softmax merge being elementwise and fusable, and (4) Phase 1 potentially overlapping with the first layer's sublayer computation.

#### Key Design Decisions and Their Justifications

**Zero initialization of pseudo-queries.** All $w_l$ are initialized to zero, making $\alpha_{i \to l} = 1/l$ (uniform attention) at the start of training. This ensures AttnRes initially behaves like an equal-weight average — the same as standard residuals for large $l$ — and prevents training volatility from asymmetric initial attention. The model gradually learns non-uniform attention as training provides gradient signal about which sources benefit which layers.

**Why this is necessary:** random initialization of $w_l$ would create arbitrary attention patterns at initialization, causing different layers to receive very different blended inputs. This could cause large initial gradient variance and training instability, especially at scale. Zero initialization provides a smooth on-ramp from the well-understood uniform-residual dynamics.

**Learned query vs. input-dependent query.** The default design uses a static learned $w_l$ per layer, not a projection from the hidden state. An input-dependent query ($q_l = \text{Proj}(h_{l-1})$) further improves performance (loss 1.731 vs. 1.737) but adds a $d \times d$ projection per layer and requires sequential memory access (since $h_{l-1}$ depends on the previous layer's output). The learned query's independence enables the batched Phase 1 computation and avoids the parameter overhead.

**Why the performance gap is small:** the layer identity alone carries most of the signal about which sources to attend to — a pre-attention layer at depth 20 roughly knows it needs information from the embedding and recent MLP outputs regardless of the specific token being processed. Token-level variation in attention patterns is a refinement, not a requirement, and the learned query captures the dominant layer-type-specific patterns.

**Softmax vs. sigmoid normalization.** Replacing softmax with sigmoid (each source gets an independent weight in $[0,1]$, no competitive normalization) degrades performance from 1.737 to 1.741. The authors attribute this to softmax's "competitive normalization, which forces sharper selection among sources." With sigmoid, the model can assign high weight to all sources simultaneously, approximating uniform aggregation plus a bias term — which largely recovers standard residual behavior and defeats the purpose of selective attention.

**Why competitive normalization matters:** softmax creates a zero-sum game among sources — increasing weight on one source necessarily decreases weight on others, since the weights sum to 1. This forces the model to make meaningful trade-offs: if layer 3's output is genuinely more useful than layer 7's for layer 20, the model must suppress layer 7 to amplify layer 3. Sigmoid has no such constraint, allowing the model to attend strongly to everything (or weakly to everything) without making selections.

**RMSNorm on keys.** RMSNorm is applied to the keys before the dot product with the query. Removing it degrades Full AttnRes (1.737 → 1.743) and hurts Block AttnRes more severely (1.746 → 1.750). The reason: without normalization, layers (or blocks) that produce naturally larger output magnitudes would dominate the softmax regardless of relevance. For Block AttnRes, this is especially critical because block representations accumulate many layers' outputs and can develop large magnitude differences — a complete block of 16 layers has substantially larger norm than a partial sum of 3 layers, and without RMSNorm this magnitude difference would translate directly into attention bias.

**Why RMSNorm specifically:** RMSNorm normalizes to unit RMS (root-mean-square) without subtracting the mean, preserving directional information while removing scale. This is appropriate because the dot product $w_l^\top \text{RMSNorm}(k_i)$ should measure directional similarity (is the key pointing in a direction relevant to the query?) rather than magnitude (how large was the key?). BatchNorm or LayerNorm would subtract the mean, which is unnecessary and potentially harmful since the mean carries information about the key's content, not just its scale.

**Block count selection.** The paper sweeps block sizes $S$ from $1$ to $32$ (Figure 6) and finds that $S=2, 4, 8$ all achieve similar loss (~1.746) while $S=16, 32$ degrade toward the baseline. In practice, the authors "fix the number of blocks to $\approx 8$ for infrastructure efficiency." This provides a good trade-off: enough blocks to enable meaningful long-range selective access, few enough that the memory and communication overhead ($8d$ per token for the block cache) is negligible.

**Why 8 blocks:** this is an empirical sweet spot based on the loss vs. block size curve. With $L=54$ layers (in the 48B model), 8 blocks means roughly 6–7 layers per block. The paper does not claim this is universally optimal — it depends on model depth — but the scaling law experiments (Table 2, with models of varying depth) all use $\approx 8$ blocks, suggesting the optimal block count is relatively insensitive to total depth in the range studied.

**Per-layer vs. per-head depth attention.** The authors test multi-head depth attention on Block AttnRes with $H=16$ heads, allowing different channel groups to attend to different source layers. This *degrades* performance (loss 1.752 vs. 1.746), "indicating that the optimal depth-wise mixture is largely uniform across channels: when a layer's output is relevant, it is relevant as a whole." In other words, the useful unit of depth-wise retrieval is the entire layer output, not specific feature dimensions within it. This simplifies the design and keeps the parameter count low (one $d$-dimensional vector per layer rather than $H$ vectors of dimension $d/H$).

**Why per-head attention is unnecessary:** sequence-wise attention needs multiple heads because different positions carry different semantic roles (subject, object, modifier) that are captured in different subspaces. Depth-wise attention, by contrast, retrieves entire computational stages — the output of "the 5th attention layer" or "the 3rd MLP" — which are hollistic transformations that don't naturally decompose into independent subspaces. A single query vector per layer suffices to express "I want more information from that stage."

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Residual Connections as a Depth-Wise Sequence Modeling Problem

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is to reinterpret the residual connection not as a gradient highway — its canonical justification since He et al. (2015) — but as a **depth-wise recurrence** that aggregates information across layers, analogous to how RNNs aggregate information across time. This reframing is what makes the entire AttnRes design intelligible as more than an arbitrary architectural tweak.

The field has understood residuals almost exclusively through the backward-pass lens: the identity path in the gradient chain $\prod (I + \partial f_j/\partial h_j)$ prevents vanishing gradients. This framing treats residuals as an optimization crutch — necessary for training, irrelevant for representation. The paper argues that residuals simultaneously serve a forward-pass representational role: they define *how* information from layer $i$ reaches layer $l$. The unrolled residual $h_l = h_1 + \sum_{i=1}^{l-1} f_i(h_i)$ is exactly a uniform-weighted sum over all prior outputs. This is an information aggregation mechanism, and it is a *bad one* — it's the depth-wise equivalent of averaging all tokens in a sequence with equal weight, which is what attention replaced in 2017.

What makes this reframing intellectually distinctive is that it **uncovers a structural parallel that the field had overlooked**. Prior work on improving residuals — Highway networks (Srivastava et al., 2015), ReZero (Bachlechner et al., 2020), DeepNorm (Wang et al., 2022) — all operated within the recurrence paradigm: modify the update rule $h_l = \text{update}(h_{l-1}, f_{l-1}(h_{l-1}))$ while preserving the single-state bottleneck. The paper points out that this is exactly what RNN variants (LSTM, GRU) did for sequences — they improved the gating while keeping the recurrent form, and they were ultimately surpassed by attention, which eliminated recurrence entirely. The conceptual leap is recognizing that **depth and time are formally dual**: residuals are to layers what RNNs were to tokens, and the Transformer's sequence-time insight — direct attention over all positions — should apply equally to depth.

This framing is not merely a metaphor. Section 6.2 makes it precise by showing that prior residual variants correspond to depth mixing matrices $M$ with different semiseparable ranks (Highway = 1-semiseparable, mHC = m-semiseparable), all of which are special cases of depth-wise linear attention. AttnRes is depth-wise softmax attention. The transition from linear to softmax attention over sequences was transformative for language modeling; the paper argues the same transition should be transformative over depth. This is a **fundamental shift** in how to think about depth-wise connectivity — not a refinement of the recurrent update, but an abandonment of recurrence in favor of direct attention.

The theoretical significance extends beyond this paper: once depth is recognized as an attention axis, the entire toolbox of sequence-wise attention innovations becomes available for depth — efficient attention variants, positional encodings for depth (should layer 1 be "positionally" encoded differently from layer 50?), attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2023, mirrored in the persistent embedding weights observed in Figure 8), and multi-query or grouped-query formulations. The paper's block structure ($N$ blocks) is essentially a depth-wise equivalent of sparse attention patterns, and the paper explicitly notes that incorporating linear-complexity attention alternatives is "a natural direction for future work" (Section 6.1).

The empirical payoff of this reframing is visible in how it reconciles prior contradictory findings: DenseFormer's static cross-layer weights fail (Table 4, loss 1.767 vs. baseline 1.766) because they implement depth-wise attention without softmax normalization, analogous to using uniform attention over a sequence. The competitive pressure of softmax — making the model *choose* which layers to attend to — is what makes attention work, and this property is absent from all prior cross-layer methods. The reframing provides a diagnostic: prior cross-layer methods failed not because cross-layer access is useless, but because they implemented it with the wrong kernel.

### Innovation 2: Diagnostic Analysis of PreNorm Dilution via Learned Attention Patterns

The paper surfaces a previously diagnosed but poorly evidenced phenomenon — PreNorm dilution (Xiong et al., 2020; Li et al., 2026) — and provides **concrete, mechanistic evidence** for how it manifests and how it can be mitigated, using the learned attention weights themselves as a diagnostic tool.

PreNorm dilution is the observation that under PreNorm, hidden-state magnitudes grow as $O(L)$ with depth, progressively drowning out early-layer contributions. Li et al. (2026) identified this theoretically and proposed SiameseNorm as a dual-stream fix. What the paper adds is **direct visualization of the depth-wise attention economy**: Figure 8 shows the learned weights $\alpha_{i \to l}$ for every layer ($y$-axis) attending over every source ($x$-axis). Three patterns emerge that would be invisible without learned depth-wise attention:

1. **Persistent embedding attention.** The embedding ($i=0$, leftmost column) retains non-trivial weight across all layers throughout the network. This is the model *learning* to maintain direct access to the token representation, which under standard residuals gets progressively buried under accumulating layer outputs. The persistent embedding weight is a learned solution to the dilution problem — layer 30 can still "see" the original token because it explicitly attends to $h_1$ rather than relying on $h_1$ to survive 29 rounds of addition.

2. **Diagonal dominance with selective off-diagonal retrieval.** Most weight concentrates on the immediately preceding source (the diagonal), but specific layers develop long-range skip connections — e.g., layer 15–16 in the Block AttnRes heatmap reaching back to block 3–4. These are not random; they are learned pathways for specific information routing that the uniform residual would make impossible.

3. **Layer-type specialization.** Pre-attention layers maintain broader receptive fields (distributing weight across many earlier sources), while pre-MLP layers show sharper diagonal reliance. This emerges naturally from the separate learned queries $w_l$ for attention and MLP sublayers, confirming that different computational stages have genuinely different depth-wise information needs — a fact that uniform residuals cannot accommodate.

What makes this a genuine innovation is that it **converts a theoretical pathology (dilution) into an empirically observable and automatically mitigated phenomenon**. Standard residual networks *suffer* from dilution because they have no mechanism to resist it. AttnRes *learns* to resist it because the attention weights can suppress irrelevant accumulated mass and amplify specific earlier contributions. The training dynamics in Figure 5 provide corroboration: output magnitudes under AttnRes show a bounded periodic pattern (reset at each block boundary) rather than monotonic growth; gradient magnitudes become more uniform across depth rather than concentrating in early layers. These are not separately engineered properties — they emerge from the same learned attention mechanism, confirming that depth-wise attention addresses dilution as a side effect of doing its job.

This is significant beyond raw performance because it provides a **principled diagnostic framework**. Prior work on depth-wise connectivity had to rely on end-to-end loss comparisons. AttnRes's attention weights are inherently interpretable — Figure 8 is essentially a functional connectivity diagram of the trained network, showing which layers depend on which earlier layers for which sublayer types. This opens the door to architecture analysis that was previously impossible: are there layers that serve as "attention sinks" (always receive high weight regardless of input, analogous to Xiao et al., 2023)? Do attention patterns correlate with task difficulty? Do pruned layers correspond to layers with low incoming attention weights? The paper doesn't explore these questions in depth (the analysis in Section 5.4.2 is brief), but the diagnostic capability is a genuine advance over prior methods where cross-layer connectivity was either fixed (DenseFormer) or expressed through recurrent gating that can't be straightforwardly visualized.

### Innovation 3: Demonstrating That Cross-Layer Attention Can Be Deployed at LLM Scale with Near-Zero Overhead

On its own, the architectural insight of depth-wise attention could be dismissed as theoretical — beautiful but impractical. The $O(Ld)$ memory for storing all layer outputs and the $O(L^2 d)$ computation for full attention over depth would be prohibitive for models with 50+ layers, hidden dimensions in the thousands, and pipeline parallelism across dozens of GPUs. The paper's **third major contribution is showing that this is a solvable systems problem**, not an inherent limitation, through a combination of carefully designed approximations and infrastructure optimizations that collectively reduce the overhead to under 4% in training and under 2% in inference. This is a **fundamental enabling contribution** — without it, AttnRes would be a small-scale curiosity.

The paper makes three distinct systems contributions that are individually novel and collectively decisive:

**Block AttnRes as a sparsity pattern.** The block compression ($O(L) \to O(N)$ sources) is more than a crude efficiency hack. The paper's ablation (Figure 6) shows it preserves most of Full AttnRes's benefit: block sizes $S=2, 4, 8$ all achieve similar loss (~1.746), while larger blocks degrade toward the baseline. This demonstrates that **granular per-layer access is unnecessary; coarse block-level access suffices**. The pattern mirrors what was discovered in sequence-wise attention: sparse attention patterns (Longformer, BigBird) can match dense attention because most attention weight concentrates on a few positions. Here, "positions" are depth-wise sources, and the concentration is on a few block-level summaries. The block structure is essentially a learned sparsity pattern — the model learns which *blocks* are relevant, and the intra-block summation provides a natural hierarchical decomposition. This is a **non-obvious empirical finding** with theoretical implications: it suggests that the depth-wise attention matrix has low effective rank, consistent with the semiseparable perspective in Section 6.2.

**Cross-stage caching as a communication-avoiding algorithm.** The pipeline communication optimization (Section 4.1) is clever because it exploits a property specific to pipeline schedules — the deterministic assignment of virtual stages to physical devices — to reduce redundant block transmissions. The quadratic-to-linear reduction in communication cost ($O(C^2) \to O(VP^2)$, where $C = PV$ is the total chunk count) is the difference between Block AttnRes being deployable and not. What makes this more than standard engineering is that it requires no additional memory (blocks are cached on the receiving device, which already has them) and no changes to the pipeline schedule — it's a purely local optimization that falls out of recognizing that pipeline stages have persistent memory across virtual stage boundaries.

**Two-phase computation as an online-aggregation algorithm.** The two-phase inference strategy (Section 4.2, Algorithm 1) is elegant because it simultaneously solves two problems: amortizing memory access (Phase 1 reads the block cache once per block, not once per layer) and preserving exact mathematical equivalence (Phase 2 merges intra-block results via online softmax without re-reading the cache). The online softmax trick (Milakov and Gimelshein, 2018) was developed for numerical stability in large-vocabulary softmax; repurposing it to decouple batched and sequential attention computations is a creative application that makes the two-phase design work. Without it, Phase 2 would need to re-read the block cache, defeating the amortization benefit.

The combined result — under 4% training overhead and under 2% inference latency overhead — **transforms AttnRes from a theoretical proposal into a practical architectural component**. Prior cross-layer methods (DenseFormer, MRLA, MUDDFormer) either did not address scalability or were evaluated at scales where $O(Ld)$ memory was manageable. The paper's explicit cost accounting — Table 1's per-layer I/O analysis, the communication formulas (Equations 7–8), the 15 GB → 1.9 GB memory reduction via sequence sharding — establishes a **credibility threshold** that future depth-wise connectivity proposals must meet. The paper is effectively saying: "cross-layer attention can be deployed today on current hardware, here is exactly how, and here is exactly what it costs." This is a significant contribution to the *engineering of deep architectures*, independent of the specific AttnRes mechanism.

### Innovation 4: Evidence That Depth-Wise Attention Shifts the Optimal Depth–Width Trade-off

The architecture sweep in Section 5.4.1 (Figure 7) is unusual for an architecture paper. Rather than merely showing that AttnRes improves loss at a fixed architecture, the authors conduct a controlled experiment under a fixed compute and parameter budget, sweeping 25 configurations over the $d_{\text{model}}/L_b$ vs. $H/L_b$ grid. The finding is subtle but important: **AttnRes shifts the optimal architecture toward deeper, narrower networks**. The baseline reaches its best loss at $d_{\text{model}}/L_b \approx 60$ (1.847), while AttnRes achieves its optimum at $d_{\text{model}}/L_b \approx 45$ (1.802) — and AttnRes achieves a lower loss than the baseline in every single one of the 25 configurations.

This is intellectually significant because it demonstrates that AttnRes is not merely adding capacity — it is **changing how depth can be utilized**. Under standard PreNorm residuals, increasing depth beyond a certain point yields diminishing returns because the PreNorm dilution problem makes deeper layers less effective: each additional layer's output is a smaller fraction of the total, and later layers must work harder just to contribute. AttnRes's selective aggregation allows each layer to meaningfully build on specific earlier representations rather than drowning in the accumulated sum, making additional depth more productive. The shift in the optimal $d_{\text{model}}/L_b$ (lower ratio = deeper, narrower) is quantitative evidence for this mechanism-level claim.

The finding also has practical implications for architecture design that go beyond AttnRes. If future depth-wise connectivity methods (whether AttnRes variants or new proposals) similarly shift the optimal depth–width trade-off, then **the standard heuristics for allocating parameters between depth and width** — which were developed under the uniform-residual paradigm — are not transferable. Architecture designers adopting depth-wise attention should re-optimize their depth–width ratio rather than slotting the method into a fixed architecture. The paper is careful to note that this preference for depth does not directly translate to deployment (deeper models have higher inference latency due to sequential computation; Pope et al., 2022), but it reveals *where* the method benefits most, which can inform co-design of architecture and inference system.

The fact that AttnRes outperforms the baseline in *every* grid cell (by margins of 0.019–0.063 loss) is also telling: it suggests the benefit is robust to architectural choices, not narrowly tuned. This robustness is consistent with the hypothesis that uniform residual accumulation is a fundamental bottleneck — improving it helps regardless of how the rest of the architecture is configured.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021), specifically the split from Lightman et al. (2022): 12,000 training questions and 500 test questions. The choice is deliberate because mathematical reasoning requires multi-step logical deduction rather than novel factual recall, making it a domain where test-time compute is expected to help most (the model already possesses the necessary knowledge; the challenge is drawing complex inferences).

- **Base Model.** All experiments use PaLM 2-S* (Codey) (Anil et al., 2023), which the authors argue is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and sits in a useful regime: non-trivial performance on MATH (roughly 10–19% pass@1 depending on configuration) but far from saturation, leaving room for test-time compute to make a difference. For the FLOPs-matched comparison, a second model with approximately 14× more parameters is used as the pretraining-scaled baseline (greedy decoding, no additional test-time compute).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is MATH test accuracy (%) — the fraction of the 500 test questions for which the selected final answer matches the ground truth, graded using the grading function released by Lightman et al. (2022). When analyzing difficulty-dependent behavior, accuracy is reported within each of five difficulty quintiles separately. For the architecture sweep (Section 5.4.1), the metric is validation loss.

- **Baselines.** The paper uses several baselines:
  - **Majority voting**: select the most common final answer among $N$ sampled solutions, with no learned verifier.
  - **ORM best-of-N weighted**: score $N$ solutions with an outcome reward model, then apply best-of-N weighted selection (Li et al., 2023) where solutions arriving at the same final answer have their scores summed, and the answer with the greatest total sum is selected.
  - **PRM best-of-N weighted**: score $N$ solutions with the process reward model and apply best-of-N weighted selection identically.
  - **Parallel sampling** (for revisions): generate $N$ independent solutions from the revision model and select the best via verifier or majority voting.

- **Generation Budget / Compute Accounting.** The universal unit of test-time compute is one "generation," defined as one complete sampled answer from the base LLM. For beam search and best-of-N, budget equals the number of beams or samples $N$. For lookahead search with $k$ lookahead steps, cost is $N \times (k+1)$ to account for additional rollout computation. Budgets are swept across powers of 2, typically from $2^0$ to $2^9$ (1 to 512 generations). For FLOPs-matched comparisons (Section 7), pretraining FLOPs are approximated as $X = 6ND_{\text{pretrain}}$ and inference FLOPs as $Y = 2ND_{\text{inference}}$, following standard scaling law conventions (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022).

- **Cross-Validation / Statistical Protocol.** To avoid contamination between strategy selection and evaluation, the authors use two-fold cross-validation within each difficulty bin on the 500-question test set. The best-performing strategy is selected on one fold and evaluated on the other, with results averaged. For the architecture sweep (Section 5.4.1), all 25 configurations are trained under identical compute and parameter budgets (≈6.5 × 10^19 FLOPs, ≈2.3 × 10^8 active parameters) with the same hyperparameters, ensuring that performance variation arises purely from architectural reallocation rather than overall capacity differences.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Search Against PRM Verifiers (Section 5)

The headline finding from the search experiments is that **beam search significantly outperforms best-of-N at low generation budgets but its advantage diminishes or reverses at high budgets**, due to verifier over-optimization. Compute-optimal selection of the search strategy per difficulty bin yields efficiency gains of up to 4× over best-of-N.

**Aggregate search algorithm comparison (Figure 3, left).** Across all 500 test questions with a maximum budget of 256 generations:

- At low budgets (2–8 generations), beam search with $M = 4$ significantly outperforms best-of-N weighted. The paper reports: at 4 generations, beam search ($M = 4$) achieves roughly 27% accuracy versus roughly 16% for best-of-N weighted.
- At high budgets (64–256), beam search performance flattens and falls slightly *below* best-of-N weighted. Best-of-N weighted reaches approximately 38% at 512 generations; beam search ($M = 4$) plateaus around 34%.
- Lookahead search (both $k = 1$ and $k = 3$) generally underperforms at the same generation budget due to its higher per-step cost, with the 3-step lookahead variants converging to similar performance as other methods at very high budgets but never surpassing them.
- Majority voting trails all verifier-based methods substantially, reaching only about 29% at 512 generations.

**Difficulty-bin analysis for search (Figure 3, right).** When beam search ($M = 4$) and best-of-N weighted are compared at four budget levels (4, 16, 64, 256 generations), broken out by difficulty quintile:

- **Bin 1 (easiest):** Beam search accuracy *decreases* from roughly 78% to 77% as budget goes from 4 to 256, while best-of-N weighted increases from 68% to 88%. This is the clearest evidence of PRM over-optimization — beam search finds solutions that exploit the verifier signal rather than genuinely correct answers.
- **Bin 2:** Beam search improves modestly (roughly 14% → 32%) but best-of-N weighted improves faster (roughly 14% → 60%), maintaining a clear advantage at high budgets.
- **Bin 3:** Beam search consistently outperforms best-of-N weighted across all budgets, reaching roughly 34% vs. 23% at 256 generations.
- **Bin 4:** Beam search shows the strongest relative advantage, reaching roughly 17% vs. 10% for best-of-N at 256 generations.
- **Bin 5 (hardest):** Both methods hover near 1–3% regardless of budget. No method makes meaningful progress on problems where the base model essentially never produces correct solutions.

**Compute-optimal search (Figure 4).** By selecting the best search strategy per difficulty bin at each budget level, the results show:

- At 16 generations, compute-optimal (oracle bins) achieves approximately 27% accuracy, roughly matching PRM best-of-N weighted at 64 generations — a 4× compute reduction.
- At 256 generations, compute-optimal oracle reaches approximately 39.5%, surpassing PRM best-of-N weighted at the same budget (roughly 37%).
- Compute-optimal with predicted difficulty bins tracks the oracle version closely, particularly at lower budgets. The paper notes the two curves "largely overlap," with the predicted version reaching approximately 37% at 256 generations.
- Both compute-optimal variants consistently outperform ORM best-of-N weighted (which peaks around 34% at 512 generations) and majority voting (around 29%).

**PRM vs. ORM scaling (Figure 14, Appendix F).** At 2048 samples, PRM best-of-N weighted achieves approximately 40% accuracy versus roughly 35% for ORM best-of-N weighted and roughly 30% for majority voting. The gap between PRM and ORM widens with the number of samples, confirming the PRM's superior scaling properties.

#### Revision Model Results (Section 6)

The headline finding from the revision experiments is that **sequential revisions marginally outperform parallel sampling in aggregate, but the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio depends on difficulty**: easy questions perform best with purely sequential revisions, while hard questions benefit from a balanced ratio. Compute-optimal selection of this ratio yields up to 4× efficiency gains over parallel best-of-N.

**Revision model pass@1 trajectory (Figure 6, left).** Starting from approximately 18.2% pass@1 at step 1, the revision model's per-step accuracy improves to roughly 24–25% by steps 15–20, and remains in the 23–25% range out to 64 steps. The model generalizes beyond its 4-step training horizon.

**Sequential vs. parallel (Figure 6, right).** At 64 generations:
- Sequential + best-of-N weighted: approximately 41.5%
- Parallel + best-of-N weighted: approximately 39%
- Sequential + majority: approximately 38%
- Parallel + majority: approximately 35%

Sequential outperforms parallel under both selection mechanisms, with the verifier-based gap (roughly 2.5 percentage points) being slightly narrower than the majority-based gap (roughly 3 points).

**Sequential-to-parallel ratio sweep (Figure 7, left).** For a fixed generation budget, varying the ratio reveals:
- At 256 generations, the optimal ratio is around $2^1$ to $2^3$ (2:1 to 8:1 sequential-to-parallel), achieving approximately 43–44% accuracy.
- Fully parallel (leftmost point) yields approximately 40%.
- Fully sequential (rightmost point) yields approximately 42%.
- At lower budgets (8–32 generations), fully sequential is optimal — the curves are monotonically increasing with the sequential-to-parallel ratio.

**Difficulty-dependent ratio (Figure 7, right).** At a fixed budget of 128 generations:
- **Bin 1:** Performance is essentially flat across all ratios, around 90–92%. Easy questions are insensitive to the allocation strategy.
- **Bin 2:** Slight advantage for higher sequential ratios, approximately 63% at fully sequential vs. 58% at fully parallel.
- **Bin 3:** A clear optimal ratio emerges at moderate sequential-to-parallel values (around $2^1$ to $2^3$), reaching approximately 42% vs. 35% at the extremes.
- **Bin 4:** Similar pattern, with the peak at a moderate ratio achieving roughly 18% vs. 14% at fully parallel.
- **Bin 5:** All ratios produce roughly 2–3% accuracy. No allocation strategy helps.

**Compute-optimal revisions (Figure 8).** Selecting the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio per difficulty bin:
- At 64 generations, compute-optimal oracle achieves approximately 40%, matching parallel best-of-N weighted at 256 generations — a 4× improvement.
- At 256 generations, compute-optimal oracle reaches approximately 44%, compared to roughly 41% for best-of-N weighted and 37% for parallel-only.
- Compute-optimal predicted bins perform slightly below oracle bins at high budgets (approximately 41% at 256 generations) but still substantially outperform the parallel baseline.
- Notably, the parallel baseline appears to **plateau** around 36–37% at high budgets, while compute-optimal scaling continues to improve, suggesting that the gains from adaptive allocation compound at higher budgets.

#### FLOPs-Matched Comparison: Test-Time vs. Pretraining Compute (Section 7)

The headline finding is that **test-time compute can substitute for pretraining compute, but only on problems within the base model's capability range**. On easy-to-medium questions at low inference-to-pretraining token ratios, a smaller model with compute-optimal test-time strategies can outperform a ~14× larger model. On hard problems or at high token ratios, pretraining remains preferable.

**Revisions results (Figure 9, left; Figure 1, top-right bar chart).** Comparing PaLM 2-S* with compute-optimal revisions against the ~14× larger model:

| Difficulty | $R \ll 1$ (0.16) | $R \approx 1$ (0.79) | $R \gg 1$ (22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy (bin 1) | +11.8% | +3.5% | −11.9% |
| Medium (bins 2–3) | +27.8% | +16.7% | +5.4% |
| Hard (bins 4–5) | +21.6% | negative (implied) | −37.2% |

The numbers are from the bar chart in Figure 1 (top-right). At $R \ll 1$, test-time compute outperforms the larger model across **all** difficulty levels. At $R \gg 1$, it only remains preferable on easy questions, with hard questions showing a −37.2% relative disadvantage.

**PRM search results (Figure 9, right; Figure 1, bottom-right bar chart).** The pattern is starker:

| Difficulty | $R \ll 1$ (0.16) | $R \approx 1$ (0.79) | $R \gg 1$ (22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | +19.1% | +2.2% | +2.0% |
| Medium | 0.0% | −35.3% | −30.8% |
| Hard | −3.6% | −35.3% | −52.9% |

PRM search shows weaker benefits than revisions for the FLOPs-matched comparison, with substantial disadvantages on medium and hard questions even at moderate $R$ values. On easy questions, test-time compute remains preferable across all $R$ regimes, though the margin narrows significantly.

**Figure 9 detail.** The line plots show accuracy per difficulty bin as test-time compute scales. The 14× larger model's greedy performance (indicated by stars) is placed at three x-axis positions corresponding to the three $R$ values. Where the compute-optimal scaling line is above the star, test-time compute wins. On bin 1 (purple, topmost line), the scaling line is above all three stars for revisions. On bin 5 (blue, bottommost line), the line is below all three stars and essentially flat near 0–5%, confirming that no amount of test-time compute helps on the hardest problems.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablations in this section are from Table 4 unless otherwise noted. The baseline PreNorm model achieves validation loss 1.766 on the 16-head model from Table 2.

**PRM aggregation strategy (Appendix E, Figure 13).** Comparing "min" (minimum score across steps), "prod" (product of step-level correctness probabilities), and "last" (only the PRM's prediction at the final step) step-wise aggregation: "last" achieves roughly 37% at 256 samples, "min" achieves roughly 35%, "prod" achieves roughly 27%, and ORM achieves roughly 34%. The "last" aggregation's superiority is notable because it effectively reduces the PRM to ORM-like behavior at aggregation time, yet the PRM still outperforms a separately trained ORM. The authors interpret this as evidence that step-level PRM training provides beneficial representation learning: "the step-level PRM training acts as a form of beneficial representation learning even when the intermediate predictions aren't directly used at aggregation time."

**PRM vs. ORM (Appendix F, Figure 14).** The PRM consistently outperforms the ORM, with the gap widening at higher sample counts. At 2048 samples, PRM best-of-N weighted reaches approximately 40% vs. ORM's 35%. The authors note that this gap widens with the number of samples, confirming the PRM's superior scaling properties and suggesting that the representation learning benefit compounds as more candidates are evaluated.

**Revision model verifier choice (Appendix J, Figure 15a).** The base-LM PRM underperforms the revision-specific ORM when scoring revision model outputs: sequential + base-LM PRM achieves roughly 40% at 64 generations vs. sequential + revision ORM at roughly 42%. This confirms distribution shift as a practical concern — the PRM trained on base model outputs does not transfer cleanly to the revision model's different output distribution — and validates the need for a separate verifier trained on revision model data.

**Revision history in verifier context (Appendix J, Figure 15b).** Including previous revisions in the ORM's context provides a small improvement over the no-history ablation (approximately 1–2 percentage points at 64 generations), but both variants outperform the parallel baseline. This confirms that the sequential sampling benefit is not solely attributable to the verifier seeing more context — the revision process itself generates better candidates.

**Oracle vs. predicted difficulty bins (Figures 4, 8, and Appendix C, Figures 11–12).** Both oracle and predicted bins yield qualitatively similar trends across difficulty levels. Predicted bins show slightly lower performance at high budgets in the revision setting (roughly 41% vs. 44% at 256 generations in Figure 8) but essentially identical performance in the search setting (Figure 4). This is the critical robustness check: the compute-optimal strategy works without ground-truth labels. The slight degradation in the revision setting at high budgets suggests that the predicted difficulty estimator is less accurate for revision model outputs than for base model outputs, likely due to distribution shift.

**Majority voting for revisions (Appendix B, Figure 10).** The sequential-to-parallel ratio trends observed with verifier-based selection are replicated with majority voting: easy questions are insensitive to ratio, harder questions show an optimal intermediate ratio, and fully sequential marginally outperforms fully parallel in aggregate. This demonstrates that the benefit of sequential revisions is not an artifact of the verifier seeing revision history — it holds even under the simplest selection mechanism.

**DenseFormer comparison (Table 4).** DenseFormer achieves loss 1.767 versus baseline 1.766 — effectively no improvement. This is a striking negative result: granting each layer access to all previous outputs with static, learned-but-input-independent scalar coefficients does not help. The failure of input-independent weighting is a key justification for AttnRes's input-dependent attention mechanism.

**mHC comparison (Table 4).** mHC [Xie et al., 2026] achieves loss 1.747, a meaningful improvement over baseline (1.766) but still behind Full AttnRes (1.737) and Block AttnRes (1.746). The paper notes that Full AttnRes outperforms mHC while Block AttnRes matches it at substantially lower memory I/O per layer: 5.5d versus 34d for mHC with $m=4$ streams (Table 1). This is significant because it demonstrates that explicit cross-layer attention can achieve similar or better performance than multi-stream recurrence with a fraction of the memory bandwidth cost.

**Input-dependent query ablation.** Making the query input-dependent by projecting from the current hidden state ($q_l = \text{Proj}(h_{l-1})$) further lowers loss to 1.731 (vs. 1.737 for learned query). The paper does not adopt this as the default because it "introduces a $d \times d$ projection per layer and requires sequential memory access during decoding," making the two-phase inference optimization impossible. The small 0.006 loss gap suggests that layer identity alone carries most of the signal about depth-wise connectivity.

**Input-independent mixing ablation.** Replacing the learned query and key with learnable, input-independent scalars to weigh previous layers degrades performance to 1.749 (vs. 1.737). This confirms that content-dependent weighting — where the attention score depends on the actual content of the source layer's output via the dot product with the key — is necessary for the method to work. Static learned weights (the DenseFormer approach, 1.767) and static scalar mixing (1.749) both fail relative to content-dependent attention.

**Softmax vs. sigmoid ablation.** Replacing softmax normalization with element-wise sigmoid (each source gets an independent weight in [0,1], no competitive normalization) degrades performance to 1.741 (vs. 1.737). The authors attribute this to softmax's "competitive normalization, which forces sharper selection among sources." With sigmoid, the model can assign high weight to all sources simultaneously, approximating uniform aggregation plus a bias term, which largely recovers standard residual behavior.

**Multi-head depth attention ablation.** Testing per-head depth aggregation with $H=16$ heads on Block AttnRes degrades performance to 1.752 (vs. 1.746). The authors conclude that "the optimal depth-wise mixture is largely uniform across channels: when a layer's output is relevant, it is relevant as a whole." This is a non-obvious negative result that simplifies the design — only one query vector per layer is needed, not $H$ per layer.

**RMSNorm on keys ablation.** Removing RMSNorm from the keys degrades Full AttnRes to 1.743 (vs. 1.737) and Block AttnRes to 1.750 (vs. 1.746). The degradation is more severe for Block AttnRes because block-level representations accumulate over more layers and can develop large magnitude differences; without RMSNorm, these magnitude differences bias the attention weights regardless of relevance.

**Sliding-window attention ablation.** Retaining only the most recent $W=8$ layer outputs plus the token embedding (sliding-window aggregation) improves over baseline (loss 1.764 vs. 1.766) but falls well short of both Full AttnRes (1.737) and Block AttnRes (1.746). The paper concludes that "selectively accessing distant layers matters more than attending to many nearby ones" — the long-range access provided by the block structure is crucial.

**Block size sweep (Figure 6).** Sweeping block size $S$ from 1 (Full AttnRes, loss 1.737) to 32 (loss 1.757), the paper finds that $S=2, 4, 8$ all achieve similar loss (~1.746), while larger blocks ($S=16, 32$) degrade toward the baseline (1.766). This demonstrates that coarse block-level access ($N \approx 8$) recovers most of Full AttnRes's benefit, and that the relationship between block granularity and performance is not linear — there is a plateau where block size can increase substantially with minimal loss in performance, followed by a steeper degradation as blocks become too large.

**ReST$^{EM}$ revision model (Appendix K, Figure 16).** An attempt to further optimize the revision model using ReST$^{EM}$ (Singh et al., 2024) backfires: additional sequential revisions **substantially hurt** performance. At 256 generations, fully sequential performance drops to approximately 33.5% compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio. The authors hypothesize that "on-policy data collection in ReST$^{EM}$ exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data," causing the model to fail to learn the revision task properly. This is a notable negative result that highlights the sensitivity of revision training to the data generation procedure — the simpler offline data construction with edit-distance-based pairing works, but on-policy RL-style optimization breaks it.

**Architecture sweep robustness (Figure 7).** Under a fixed compute budget (≈6.5 × 10^19 FLOPs, ≈2.3 × 10^8 active parameters), sweeping 25 configurations over $d_{\text{model}}/L_b \in \{15, 30, 45, 60, 75\}$ and $H/L_b \in \{0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7\}$ (where $L_b = L/2$ is the number of Transformer blocks): AttnRes achieves lower loss than the baseline in **every single configuration**, by margins of 0.019–0.063. Both methods share a trend of decreasing loss with growing $d_{\text{model}}/L_b$ and shrinking $H/L_b$, with optima at $H/L_b \approx 0.3$. However, the baseline reaches its optimum at $d_{\text{model}}/L_b \approx 60$ (loss 1.847), while AttnRes shifts the optimum to $d_{\text{model}}/L_b \approx 45$ (loss 1.802). Lower $d_{\text{model}}/L_b$ under fixed parameters corresponds to deeper, narrower networks, suggesting that AttnRes can exploit additional depth more effectively.

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the Search Experiments Support the Claim That Search Improves Efficiency Over Best-of-N?

The claim that compute-optimal search improves efficiency by more than 4× over best-of-N is supported in the specific sense demonstrated: at 16 generations, compute-optimal search matches best-of-N weighted at 64 generations (Figure 4). However, this claim comes with important context:

**The 4× figure is measured after difficulty is known, not including the cost of learning difficulty.** The difficulty estimation procedure generates 2048 samples per question and scores them — a cost that vastly exceeds the test-time budget being optimized. The paper acknowledges this (Section 3.2) and does not include this cost in the 4× calculation. In a deployment where difficulty must be estimated per-query, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former could dominate. The predicted difficulty bins (using PRM scores) perform nearly as well as oracle bins (Figure 4), but still require generating and scoring 2048 samples. Until a cheap difficulty estimator is developed (e.g., a lightweight classifier trained on question text alone), the 4× figure should be understood as an upper bound on what difficulty-conditioned allocation *could* achieve, not what is currently deployable.

**The compute-optimal policy is selected via cross-validation on only ~50 questions per bin per fold** (500 questions / 5 bins = ~100 per bin, split in half by 2-fold CV). This is a very small sample for strategy selection — the optimal strategy identified for, say, bin 3 at budget 64 might be noisy. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal curves, making it difficult to assess whether the observed advantage over best-of-N is statistically robust or within sampling noise. The fact that the oracle and predicted curves "largely overlap" (Figure 4) provides some reassurance, but formal uncertainty quantification would strengthen the claim.

**The search results are for a single model family (PaLM 2-S*) on a single benchmark (MATH).** The difficulty-dependent patterns — beam search over-optimizing on easy problems, helping on medium problems — depend on the specific PRM's quality and calibration. A PRM trained differently (e.g., with human labels rather than Monte Carlo rollouts, or on a different base model) might exhibit different over-optimization thresholds, shifting the difficulty bins where beam search is optimal. The paper's demonstration that PRM behavior depends on training methodology (Appendix E: "last" aggregation is best, contrary to prior work that found "min" best) suggests these results may not transfer straightforwardly to other PRM training paradigms.

**The degradation of beam search at high budgets on easy problems (Figure 3, right, bin 1) is a critical finding but is only qualitatively described.** The paper shows the accuracy curves and mentions "over-optimization of the PRM" and references "low-information repetitive steps" in Appendix M, but does not quantify how often beam search produces degenerate outputs, what fraction of errors are attributable to verifier exploitation vs. other failure modes, or whether the effect is concentrated in specific budget ranges. A more detailed failure analysis would strengthen the claim that over-optimization is the mechanism, rather than, say, beam search simply being a worse search algorithm at high branching factors for unrelated reasons.

#### Do the Revision Experiments Support the Claim That Sequential Revisions Outperform Parallel Sampling?

The claim that sequential revisions outperform parallel sampling is supported in aggregate (Figure 6, right: 41.5% vs. 39% at 64 generations), but the margin is modest (~2.5 percentage points with verifier-based selection) and the practical significance depends on deployment constraints:

**The latency penalty of sequential revisions is not experimentally quantified.** Sequential revisions are inherently serial — each revision depends on the previous one — while parallel sampling can be batched. At 64 generations with a purely sequential strategy, wall-clock time is roughly 64× that of fully parallel execution (assuming sufficient hardware to run all parallel samples simultaneously). For latency-sensitive applications, even a 2.5-point accuracy gain may not justify a 64× slowdown. The paper does not report wall-clock times for the revision experiments, making it difficult to assess the practical trade-off.

**The 38% correct-to-incorrect reversion rate is mentioned but not systematically analyzed.** The paper notes that "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones" during sequential revisions, and mitigates this with majority voting or verifier-based selection across the chain. However, the experiments do not isolate how much of the sequential advantage comes from the within-chain selection mechanism vs. the revision process itself. If the revision model is producing correct answers and then destroying them 38% of the time, a better-trained revision model (one that learns to recognize and preserve correct answers) might eliminate this problem and make sequential revisions substantially stronger. Conversely, if the 38% reversion rate is fundamental to the training methodology, sequential revisions may be inherently capped.

**The difficulty-dependent optimal ratio (Figure 7, right) is shown only at a fixed budget of 128 generations.** The paper does not report how the optimal ratio changes with budget — does the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio for bin 3 shift toward more parallel at higher budgets (as was observed in the aggregate sweep in Figure 7, left)? This would be relevant for compute-optimal allocation, but the interaction between budget level and optimal ratio per difficulty bin is not explored.

#### Do the FLOPs-Matched Comparisons Support the Claim That Test-Time Compute Can Substitute for Pretraining?

The claim that a smaller model with test-time compute can outperform a ~14× larger model is supported, but with sharp and important boundary conditions that the paper partially acknowledges:

**The 14× larger model baseline is weak in at least two ways.** First, it scales parameters only (not data), following the LLaMA paradigm rather than Chinchilla-optimal training. A compute-optimally trained larger model (scaling both data and parameters equally, per Hoffmann et al., 2022) would likely be a stronger baseline. The paper acknowledges this (Section 7) but does not run the comparison against such a model. Second, the larger model uses only greedy decoding — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search. This makes the comparison asymmetric: the smaller model gets sophisticated test-time strategies while the larger model gets none. A fairer comparison would give the larger model some (smaller, but non-zero) test-time compute budget, since in practice one would never deploy a 14× larger model without at least best-of-8 or similar.

**The $R$ values tested ($0.16, 0.79, 22$) are specific to the MATH benchmark with this training setup.** $R = D_{\text{inference}} / D_{\text{pretrain}}$ depends on both the pretraining data volume and the inference volume. For models trained on trillions of tokens and deployed for millions of queries, $R$ could be much larger or smaller than these values. The paper's conclusion that test-time compute is preferable when $R \ll 1$ and pretraining when $R \gg 1$ is directionally correct, but the specific crossover points (where the advantage flips) depend on the exact $R$ values, the model scale, and the difficulty distribution — none of which are swept systematically.

**The hardest problems (bin 5) show essentially zero improvement from test-time compute across all methods and all budgets** (Figures 3 right, 7 right, 9). This is a genuine and important negative result: test-time compute cannot create capability where none exists. But the paper does not characterize *what makes a problem hard* in a way that could predict, without running 2048 samples, whether test-time compute will help. The difficulty bins are defined post-hoc by pass@1 rate; there is no analysis of whether certain problem characteristics (multi-step reasoning? novel mathematical operations? long problem statements?) correlate with difficulty and could serve as a predictive signal. Without such analysis, the practical guidance — "use test-time compute on easy-to-medium problems, scale pretraining for hard ones" — requires knowing which problems are hard *before* solving them.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

**No combination of PRM search with the revision model.** The paper studies search and revisions as independent pipelines but never combines them — e.g., using beam search against the PRM on revision model outputs, or using the PRM to guide which revision chain to pursue. The authors acknowledge this explicitly (Section 8) as future work. This is a significant gap because the two mechanisms have complementary difficulty-dependent strengths: revisions excel on easy problems (Figure 7, right) while beam search excels on medium problems (Figure 3, right). A combined system could potentially outperform either individually across a broader range of difficulties.

**No comparison against simply using a larger base model with the same test-time compute budget.** The FLOPs-matched comparison pits a small model + test-time compute against a large model + greedy decoding. What about a medium model + best-of-N? The paper does not explore the intermediate regime — if you have a fixed FLOPs budget to spend on both training and inference, what is the optimal split between model size and test-time compute *jointly*? This is the natural extension of the Chinchilla-style analysis the paper gestures at but does not execute.

**No evaluation on non-MATH reasoning benchmarks.** The paper uses MATH exclusively for the main experiments (the downstream evaluation in Table 3 covers a broader set but is only for the final 48B model, not the scaling law experiments). MATH problems have specific properties — exact-answer grading, well-defined solution steps, symbolic reasoning — that may favor PRM-based approaches. It is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search over-optimizing on easy problems, revisions helping on easy problems) would replicate on code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), logical reasoning (ARC, BBH), or other domains.

**No latency or throughput measurements for the revision experiments.** The paper reports inference latency overhead for AttnRes itself (<2%), but not for the sequential vs. parallel revision strategies. Since sequential revisions are inherently serial, the wall-clock time implications of the compute-optimal policy (which favors sequential revisions on easy problems) are important for practical deployment and are not addressed.

**No analysis of whether the difficulty estimation cost can be amortized across queries.** In a production setting, the same MATH problems might be asked repeatedly by different users. If difficulty can be cached (estimated once per problem, reused for all future queries of that problem), the 2048-sample estimation cost becomes a one-time upfront cost rather than a per-query cost. The paper does not discuss this amortization possibility, which would substantially change the practical cost calculus.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal allocation framework — selecting which search algorithm or which sequential-to-parallel ratio to use per prompt — depends on first estimating each prompt's difficulty. The paper's method for doing so is generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted). This step is not included in the reported compute budgets. The authors acknowledge this explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

The paper frames this as an exploration–exploitation trade-off and flags it as "a key avenue for future work."

**The consequence.** The headline 4× efficiency gains — 16 generations matching best-of-N at 64 (Figure 4), 64 generations matching best-of-N at 256 (Figures 4 and 8) — are computed *after* difficulty is known. In any realistic deployment where difficulty must be estimated per-query, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution. Since difficulty estimation costs 2048 generations (8× the largest test-time budget studied, 256), it entirely dominates the budget for any single query. The 4× figure is therefore an upper bound on what difficulty-conditioned allocation *could* achieve if difficulty were known nearly for free, not what is currently achievable.

The practical consequence is that the compute-optimal framework, as described, is not directly deployable. A practitioner implementing exactly what the paper describes would find that difficulty estimation consumes more compute than the problem-solving step, producing a net efficiency *loss* compared to simply running best-of-N with the combined budget. Until a cheap difficulty estimator is developed (e.g., a lightweight classifier that predicts difficulty from the question text alone, or an adaptive scheme that estimates difficulty from a handful of initial samples), the framework remains an analytical tool rather than a deployment recipe.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 2048-sample difficulty estimation procedure is described in Section 3.2 ("Difficulty Estimation"), which explicitly notes the cost and the exploration–exploitation framing. Section 8 lists "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" as future work. The paper provides no experiments measuring how performance degrades if difficulty is estimated from, say, 8 or 32 samples rather than 2048, and no measurement of what fraction of the total compute budget difficulty estimation would consume in a realistic deployment. The predicted difficulty bins (using PRM scores rather than ground-truth labels) perform similarly to oracle bins (Figures 4, 8), confirming that ground-truth labels are not needed — but the 2048-sample cost remains.

**Mitigation status.** Not mitigated in the current work. The paper acknowledges the limitation and suggests future work on direct difficulty prediction models and adaptive estimation strategies, but no such methods are developed or evaluated. The compute-optimal policy is selected using cross-validation on bins computed from the expensive 2048-sample procedure, and there is no demonstration that the policy transfers to bins estimated with substantially fewer samples.

### Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved — Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's approach assumes that the base model already has some non-trivial probability of producing a correct answer under random sampling. This is explicit in the difficulty definition: questions are binned by pass@1 rate from 2048 samples, and bin 5 contains the questions with the lowest pass@1. The paper acknowledges this boundary condition in the Section 7 takeaway:

> "test-time compute is powerful when problems are within the base model's reach (it already produces correct solutions at some non-trivial rate), but it cannot compensate for fundamental capability gaps that larger pretraining would address"

**The consequence.** Across all methods studied — PRM search, iterative revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations — performance on bin 5 questions remains at 1–3% accuracy regardless of compute budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers near 1–3% for all methods and all budgets (4 to 256 generations). In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio at 128 generations. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%, below the 14× larger model's greedy performance at all $R$ values.

This is a fundamental capability ceiling: if the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class, no amount of sampling, search, or revision will help, because there are essentially no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find. The practical consequence is that the method offers no path forward for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning that exceeds the base model's training distribution. For such problems — which are the ones where improvement is most needed — scaling pretraining remains the only viable approach.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The flat bin 5 curves appear consistently across Figures 3, 7, and 9. The paper reports these results honestly and does not overclaim. However, there is no analysis of *what makes bin 5 problems hard* — are they multi-step problems where the model fails at step 1? Problems requiring novel mathematical operations? Problems with adversarial structure? — which would help characterize the capability boundary and suggest whether it can be pushed back through different test-time strategies or only through pretraining.

**Mitigation status.** Not mitigated. The paper acknowledges the boundary (Section 7) but does not explore whether alternative test-time strategies could help on these problems (e.g., chain-of-thought prompting, tool use, or retrieval-augmented generation). The observation that bin 5 problems are essentially unsolvable with current test-time compute is a genuine finding, but it also represents a hard limitation on the method's applicability.

---

### The Method Is Validated on a Single Benchmark with a Single Model Family

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in Sections 5–7 use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with models based on PaLM 2-S* (for the search and revision experiments) or the Kimi Linear architecture (for the large-scale training experiments in Section 5.2). The paper states it "believe[s] this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an assertion, not a demonstrated fact.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model- or benchmark-specific:

- **PRM quality and over-optimization behavior**: The PRM's calibration, the budget threshold at which beam search starts over-optimizing (Figure 3, right, bin 1), and the difficulty-dependent optimal policies all depend on the specific PRM's quality. A PRM trained differently — with a different base model, a different training procedure, or different data — would likely exhibit different over-optimization thresholds and optimal strategy selections. The paper itself demonstrates PRM sensitivity in Appendix E: "last" aggregation is best for their Monte Carlo-trained PRM, contrary to prior work on human-labeled PRMs where "min" aggregation was best. This suggests findings may not transfer across PRM training paradigms.

- **MATH-specific properties**: MATH problems have exact-answer grading, well-defined solution steps amenable to step-level verification, and symbolic reasoning requirements. It is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns — beam search over-optimizing on easy problems, revisions helping most on easy problems, neither method helping on hard problems — would replicate on code generation (HumanEval, MBPP, where correctness is defined by unit test passing), logical reasoning (ARC, BBH, where answers are multiple-choice), or tasks requiring factual retrieval rather than inference.

- **Model-scale dependence**: The difficulty-dependent patterns in Figures 3 and 7 are measured on PaLM 2-S\*, which has a specific capability profile on MATH (~10–19% pass@1). A substantially stronger or weaker model would have a different pass@1 distribution, shifting which questions fall into which difficulty bins, and potentially changing the optimal strategies per bin.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The downstream evaluation in Table 3 applies the final 48B model with Block AttnRes to a broader set of benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA-Diamond, BBH, HumanEval, etc.) and shows universal improvement over the baseline. This demonstrates that AttnRes helps across diverse tasks in the large-scale training setting. However, these are end-to-end results; they do not include the detailed difficulty-bin analyses, strategy comparisons, or FLOPs-matched trade-off analyses that constitute the paper's main contributions. The difficulty-dependent optimal policies and the 4× efficiency claims are validated only on MATH with PaLM 2-S\*.

**Mitigation status.** Partially mitigated by the multi-benchmark downstream evaluation in Section 5.2, which shows AttnRes improves performance across all tasks tested. But the more detailed analyses — search algorithm selection, sequential-to-parallel ratio optimization, FLOPs-matched comparisons against larger models — are single-benchmark, single-family. The paper does not claim these findings are universal, but it does not provide the replication that would be needed to establish their generality.

### Sequential Revisions Impose a Latency Penalty That Is Not Quantified

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model generates a chain of sequential revisions, each conditioned on the previous one. The paper's compute-optimal policy on easy problems favors purely sequential revisions (Figures 7, 8): a single chain of $N$ revisions, each depending on the prior output, versus $N$ independent parallel samples. The paper measures cost in "generations" (total number of complete solutions sampled) and reports accuracy improvements, but does not measure or discuss wall-clock latency.

**The consequence.** Sequential revisions are inherently serial — revision $k+1$ cannot begin until revision $k$ is complete — while parallel best-of-N can execute all $N$ samples simultaneously given sufficient hardware. A strategy that allocates 128 generations as 64 sequential × 2 parallel (the compute-optimal ratio at high budgets in Figure 7, left) takes roughly 64× longer in wall-clock time than a strategy that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously.

For latency-sensitive applications (interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, user-facing chatbots), even a 2.5 percentage point accuracy gain (Figure 6, right: sequential at 41.5% vs. parallel at 39% at 64 generations) may not justify a 64× increase in response time. This is not just an implementation detail — it is a fundamental tension between the compute-optimal policy's preference for sequential revisions on easy problems and the latency constraints of real deployments. A practitioner deploying this method would need to solve a joint optimization problem over accuracy, total FLOPs, *and* latency that the paper does not formulate.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports inference latency overhead for AttnRes itself (<2%, Section 4.2), but this is for the architecture-level modification (the attention residual computation), not for the sequential revision strategy. The revision experiments in Section 6 measure accuracy vs. generation budget but do not report wall-clock time, throughput, or latency. The paper does not discuss the latency–throughput trade-offs of sequential vs. parallel sampling.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge latency as a constraint on strategy selection. A simple mitigation — running multiple parallel sequential chains (say, 4 chains of 16 revisions each rather than 1 chain of 64) — is already captured in the sequential-to-parallel ratio analysis (Figure 7), but the paper does not translate this into latency-aware recommendations.

### The PRM Search and Revision Pipelines Are Studied in Isolation, Not Combined

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary mechanisms — PRM-guided search (Section 5) and iterative revisions (Section 6) — but evaluates them as independent pipelines with separate compute-optimal policies. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this gap:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

**The consequence.** The two mechanisms have complementary difficulty-dependent strengths: revisions improve the *proposal distribution* (generating better candidates, especially on easy problems where initial attempts are roughly correct and just need refinement), while PRM search improves *candidate selection* (finding the best solutions among generated candidates, especially on medium problems where the model needs to explore different strategies). A combined system — using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM to guide which revisions to pursue — could yield gains beyond either method alone. The paper's current results therefore represent a lower bound on what an integrated system could achieve.

The practical consequence is that a practitioner implementing the paper's methods must choose between search and revisions (or run both independently and commit to the better one), rather than deploying a unified system that leverages their complementary strengths. The compute-optimal policies in Figure 4 (search) and Figure 8 (revisions) are selected from disjoint strategy pools, so the reported performance numbers leave potential gains on the table.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-dependent optimal strategy analyses (Figure 3, right for search; Figure 7, right for revisions) show that the best search strategy and the best revision strategy are different at most difficulty levels. On bin 3 questions, beam search consistently outperforms best-of-N at the same budget (Figure 3, right), while revisions with a balanced sequential-to-parallel ratio are optimal (Figure 7, right). These results demonstrate that the two mechanisms have non-overlapping strengths and are therefore likely complementary, making their separation a genuine limitation rather than a minor methodological convenience.

**Mitigation status.** Not mitigated. The paper identifies the combination as future work (Section 8) but provides no experiments, analysis, or even architectural sketch of how search and revisions would be integrated. The separate revision-specific ORM (Appendix J) was necessitated by distribution shift between base model and revision model outputs, suggesting that combining search and revisions would require either a verifier robust to both output distributions or a joint training procedure — neither of which is explored.

---

### The 14× Larger Model Baseline in FLOPs-Matched Comparisons Is Artificially Weak

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 pits PaLM 2-S\* with compute-optimal test-time scaling against a model with approximately 14× more parameters. The larger model is trained by scaling parameters only (as in the LLaMA paradigm, Touvron et al., 2023), not by jointly scaling parameters and data (as in Chinchilla-optimal training, Hoffmann et al., 2022). Additionally, the larger model uses only greedy decoding — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search. The paper partially acknowledges this:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** Both choices weaken the pretraining baseline:

1. **Parameter-only scaling**: A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model at the same total FLOPs, making the pretraining baseline weaker than it could be. The paper's reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining — e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at $R \ll 1$ for revisions (Figure 1, top-right) — may shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model.

2. **Greedy decoding**: In practice, one would rarely deploy a 14× larger model without any test-time augmentation. Even a modest best-of-8 with majority voting would improve the larger model's performance, potentially altering the crossover points where test-time compute with the smaller model becomes preferable. The comparison is asymmetric: the smaller model gets sophisticated, compute-optimally-allocated test-time strategies while the larger model gets nothing.

The practical consequence is that the paper's guidance on the pretraining vs. inference trade-off — prefer test-time compute when $R \ll 1$ and problems are easy-to-medium; prefer pretraining when $R \gg 1$ or problems are hard — is directionally likely correct, but the specific quantitative trade-off points (the $R$ values and difficulty bins where the crossover occurs) are likely optimistic for test-time compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOPs-matched results (Figures 1, 9; Section 7) show that test-time compute with the smaller model can outperform the 14× larger model under certain conditions (easy-to-medium problems, low $R$), but that pretraining is preferable under others (hard problems, high $R$). The paper reports these results with appropriate caveats in Section 7. The parameter-only scaling choice is explicitly noted. However, there is no ablation or sensitivity analysis showing how the comparison would change with a Chinchilla-optimal larger model or with a larger model that receives even minimal test-time compute.

**Mitigation status.** Partially mitigated by transparency — the paper acknowledges the parameter-only scaling choice — but the comparison against a larger model with greedy decoding is not acknowledged as a limitation. A natural extension, suggested implicitly by the paper's own framework, would be to give the larger model a smaller but non-zero test-time compute budget and re-evaluate the FLOPs-matched comparison. This is not done.
