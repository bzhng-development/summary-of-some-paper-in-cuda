# Parallelized Autoregressive Visual Generation

**ArXiv:** [2412.15119](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.15119)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **Parallelized Autoregressive Visual Generation (PAR)**, a method that accelerates autoregressive visual generation by predicting multiple tokens in parallel while maintaining generation quality.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Parallelized Autoregressive Visual Generation (PAR)**, a method that accelerates autoregressive visual generation by predicting multiple tokens in parallel while maintaining generation quality. The key insight is that token dependencies are correlated with spatial distance—adjacent tokens exhibit strong dependencies that prevent independent sampling, while spatially distant tokens have weak dependencies and can be generated simultaneously. Based on this, PAR first generates initial tokens for each spatial region sequentially to establish global structure, then generates tokens at corresponding positions across non-local regions in parallel (e.g., predicting 4 or 16 tokens simultaneously instead of one at a time). On ImageNet 256×256 using LlamaGen-style autoregressive transformers, PAR achieves a 3.6× speedup with comparable FID (2.29 vs. 2.18) and up to 9.5× speedup with only 0.7 FID degradation, while on UCF-101 video generation it achieves 12.6× speedup with a modest FVD increase (103.4 vs. 94.1). The approach works by exploiting the observation that spatially distant visual tokens can be independently sampled without introducing inconsistencies, establishing that parallel generation is feasible within standard autoregressive transformers only when token groupings respect the underlying dependency structure of visual data—weakly dependent cross-region tokens can be parallelized, while strongly dependent local tokens and initial region-establishing tokens require sequential generation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Autoregressive Visual Generation Is Too Slow for Practical Use

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is straightforward but consequential: **standard autoregressive visual generation is prohibitively slow at inference time**. In the dominant paradigm, visual data is first encoded into discrete tokens using a vector-quantized autoencoder (like VQGAN or MAGVIT-v2), then a transformer predicts these tokens one at a time in raster scan order—left to right, top to bottom—conditioning each prediction on all previously generated tokens. This is the same autoregressive principle that underpins modern large language models, and it has proven remarkably effective for visual generation, showing strong scalability and competitive quality against GANs, diffusion models, and masked prediction approaches on benchmarks like ImageNet.

However, the sequential token-by-token prediction process means that generating a single image requires as many forward passes through the transformer as there are tokens in the latent representation. For a typical 384×384 image compressed by a 16× downsampling tokenizer, this yields a 24×24 grid—576 tokens that must be generated one at a time. Even with KV-caching to avoid recomputing attention for previously generated tokens, each step still requires a full forward pass, and the total latency scales linearly with sequence length. The paper reports that LlamaGen-3B, a state-of-the-art autoregressive visual generation model, requires 12.41 seconds to generate a single image on an A100 GPU (Table 2). For video generation, where the token sequence includes both spatial and temporal dimensions, the problem compounds dramatically: a 17-frame video at 128×128 resolution yields 1,280 tokens requiring 43.30 seconds of generation time.

This latency bottleneck has real practical consequences. For interactive applications (real-time image editing, conversational AI with visual outputs), sub-second or low-single-digit-second generation is essential—12 seconds is unacceptable. For batch processing at scale (generating training data, content creation pipelines), the cost of sequential generation multiplies with volume. And for video generation, where consumer expectations are already strained by the computational demands of diffusion-based approaches, sequential autoregressive generation is simply too slow to be competitive.

### The Gap: Parallel Generation in Autoregressive Models Has Been Underexplored for Visual Data

The natural solution—predicting multiple tokens in parallel rather than one at a time—has been extensively studied in language modeling but faces fundamentally different challenges in visual generation. The paper's positioning hinges on this distinction.

**In language modeling**, parallel decoding has been approached primarily through two families of methods. **Speculative decoding** uses a lightweight "draft" model to propose multiple candidate tokens, which the main model then verifies in parallel, accepting or rejecting them based on consistency with the main model's distribution. **Jacobi decoding** and related iterative refinement approaches use the model's own previous outputs, refining multiple token positions simultaneously over multiple passes until convergence. Both approaches are effective for text, where the discrete symbolic nature of language means that draft tokens are either correct or incorrect, and verification is straightforward. However, both require either an auxiliary draft model or multiple iterative refinement passes, adding complexity to the inference pipeline and—critically for the argument this paper makes—not directly addressing *which* tokens should be parallelized.

**In visual generation**, the landscape of parallel prediction attempts has been more limited and has taken different forms. The paper identifies several prior approaches and explains why none adequately solves the problem:

**Masked generative models** (e.g., MaskGIT) abandon the autoregressive paradigm entirely in favor of BERT-like masked token prediction. These models generate tokens in multiple passes—predicting a subset of tokens at each step while others remain masked—which achieves parallelism through a fundamentally different modeling approach. However, this represents a departure from autoregressive modeling, losing the sequential conditioning structure that gives autoregressive models their strong scaling properties and unified modeling capabilities across modalities. For researchers and practitioners building toward multimodal foundation models that handle text, images, and video within a single autoregressive framework, switching to masked modeling for the visual component breaks this unification.

**Next-scale prediction** (VAR) achieves parallelism through a clever architectural choice: rather than predicting individual tokens, it predicts entire token maps at progressively higher resolutions. This requires a specially designed multi-scale tokenizer that encodes images at multiple resolution levels and generates longer token sequences overall (since each scale adds tokens). VAR achieves impressive speed (10 steps for generation) and quality (FID 1.97), but at the cost of specialized architecture and tokenizer design that deviates from standard autoregressive modeling. The paper explicitly notes that VAR's approach "may limit the flexibility of autoregressive models as a unified solution across different modalities"—if you need a different generation paradigm for visual tokens than for text tokens, building a truly unified multimodal model becomes more complex.

**Naive multi-token prediction in raster order**—the most obvious approach, simply predicting adjacent tokens together—has been attempted but **degrades generation quality significantly** in visual domains. The paper's pilot study (Section 3.1, Table 4(d)) demonstrates this quantitatively: using raster scan ordering with multi-token prediction (predicting adjacent tokens simultaneously) yields a catastrophic FID of 5.64, compared to 2.62 for single-token prediction. The qualitative failure mode is visible in Figure 1(b) and Figure 5 (bottom row): distorted local structures, fragmented stripes on zebras, and inconsistent tiger faces. This is the key empirical observation that motivates the entire paper—**predicting adjacent visual tokens in parallel fails because these tokens are strongly dependent on each other, and independent sampling cannot capture their joint distribution**.

### The Insight Gap: Token Dependencies, Not Model Architecture, Are the Barrier

The paper's central intellectual move is to reframe the parallel generation problem from one of *model design* to one of *token dependency structure*. Prior work either changed the generation paradigm (masked modeling, next-scale prediction), added auxiliary models (speculative decoding), or used iterative refinement (Jacobi decoding)—all of which modify the model or the inference algorithm. This paper asks a simpler, more fundamental question: **given a standard autoregressive transformer with no architectural modifications, which tokens can be generated in parallel without compromising quality?**

The answer, developed through both empirical observation and theoretical analysis in Appendix D, is that **parallel generation feasibility is determined by token dependencies, and these dependencies are spatially structured in visual data**. Specifically:

- **Adjacent tokens have strong dependencies.** In raster scan order, each token is heavily influenced by its immediate spatial neighbors. When multiple adjacent tokens must be predicted simultaneously through independent sampling (each token sampled from its own conditional distribution without knowledge of what its neighbors will be), the joint distribution factorizes incorrectly. The sampled tokens are individually plausible but collectively inconsistent—a pixel assigned to "tiger fur" in one token doesn't coordinate with the adjacent token that decides "zebra stripe," producing the fragmentation shown in Figure 1(b).

- **Spatially distant tokens have weak dependencies.** Tokens in different regions of the image (e.g., top-left corner vs. bottom-right corner) typically have much weaker mutual information. Their joint distribution more closely approximates the product of their marginal distributions, meaning independent sampling introduces less error. This is the spatial instantiation of the general principle that local correlations dominate in natural images.

- **Initial region tokens determine global structure.** Even spatially distant tokens can have strong dependencies when they jointly establish the global layout of the image. The paper's ablation in Section 4.3 (Table 4(a)) shows that removing the sequential generation of initial tokens—generating even the first token of each region in parallel—causes a significant FID degradation from 2.61 to 3.67. Figure 5 (middle row) visualizes the failure: dogs with duplicated body parts, misaligned structures, and incoherent global layouts. These initial tokens collectively determine high-level decisions (object placement, pose, composition) that must be coordinated across regions.

This insight—that parallelization strategy should be dictated by the **dependency graph of the token sequence**, not by architectural constraints—is what distinguishes this paper from prior work. It suggests that the path to faster autoregressive visual generation is not to abandon the autoregressive framework or to build specialized architectures, but to **reorganize the token generation order** so that tokens predicted in parallel are those with naturally weak dependencies.

### The Dependency Structure Is Quantifiable, Not Just Intuitive

The paper doesn't rest on spatial intuition alone. Appendix D provides a quantitative framework grounded in information theory that validates the design choices. Using conditional entropy as a measure of token dependency—the uncertainty remaining about a token given knowledge of previous tokens—the paper estimates entropy maps across ImageNet data.

Figure 11 visualizes these maps: for any reference token (shown as a blue square), the conditional entropy of every other token given that reference token is computed. Darker red indicates lower conditional entropy (stronger dependency). The pattern is clear and consistent—the lowest conditional entropy (strongest dependency) clusters tightly around the reference token's spatial location, while distant regions show higher conditional entropy (weaker dependency). This empirically confirms the spatial decay of token dependencies that the parallel generation strategy exploits.

Figure 12 goes further, directly comparing the entropy increase caused by switching from sequential to parallel generation under two different token orderings: the paper's proposed cross-region ordering and naive raster scan ordering. For each token position, the increase in conditional entropy when that token must be predicted in parallel (without seeing its same-group neighbors) rather than sequentially (with full access to all previous tokens) is computed. The result is striking: **the raster scan ordering produces large entropy increases for most positions**, while the proposed ordering produces dramatically smaller increases. The white regions in both entropy maps correspond to the initial four tokens generated sequentially (identical in both orderings), but for subsequent tokens, the cross-region strategy keeps the added prediction difficulty low by ensuring that tokens predicted together come from different spatial regions with weak mutual dependencies.

This quantitative validation elevates the paper's contribution from an intuitive heuristic to a principled design backed by measurable information-theoretic properties of visual data.

### Why Now: The Convergence of Autoregressive Visual Models and the Need for Practical Deployment

The timing of this work reflects a broader moment in visual generation research. Autoregressive models have been steadily closing the quality gap with diffusion models (LlamaGen achieves FID 2.18 vs. DiT-XL/2's 2.27 on ImageNet 256×256) while offering advantages in scalability and multimodal unification—Chameleon and Emu3 have demonstrated that a single autoregressive transformer can handle text, images, and video within a unified next-token prediction framework. This positions autoregressive models as a strong candidate for the next generation of multimodal foundation models.

However, **inference efficiency has become the gating factor for practical adoption**. Diffusion models, despite their own latency challenges (typically 250+ denoising steps for high-quality generation), have benefited from years of optimization research—DDIM sampling, progressive distillation, consistency models, and LCMs have pushed step counts into the single digits. Autoregressive visual models have seen comparatively less optimization attention for inference speed, even as their quality has improved rapidly. The sequential token generation bottleneck means that even as hardware improves, the fundamental algorithmic limitation remains: generating 576 tokens one at a time imposes a hard lower bound on latency that is independent of FLOP utilization or memory bandwidth.

The paper directly engages with this deployment reality through timing measurements (Table 2, Table 7), reporting actual wall-clock latency on a single A100 GPU rather than abstract step counts alone. The 12.41 seconds for a single LlamaGen-3B image generation, compared to 1.31 seconds for PAR-3B-16× at only 0.7 FID degradation, makes the practical case vivid. For video, the contrast is even sharper: 43.30 seconds for sequential generation vs. 3.44 seconds with 16× parallelization.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper situates itself not as a competitor to the autoregressive visual generation paradigm but as an **efficiency-enhancing extension that preserves the paradigm's core properties**. This is a crucial positioning choice. Unlike MaskGIT (which abandons autoregression) or VAR (which requires specialized multi-scale tokenization), PAR explicitly maintains:

1. **The autoregressive property:** Every token prediction is still conditioned on all previously generated tokens. The attention mechanism is modified only within parallel prediction groups (allowing bi-directional attention among tokens generated in the same step), but global autoregressive causal masking is preserved between groups.

2. **Standard transformer architecture:** No new model components are introduced. The only additions are a handful of learnable token embeddings (n-1 embeddings for n-token parallel prediction, e.g., 3 for 4× parallelization) that help the model transition between sequential and parallel generation modes. These are dimensionally identical to regular token embeddings and add negligible parameter count.

3. **Compatibility with existing tokenizers:** VQGAN and MAGVIT-v2 are used without modification. The reordering of tokens for parallel generation is a sequence-level operation applied after tokenization and before feeding into the transformer.

4. **Compatibility with standard optimizations:** Appendix B demonstrates that PAR's algorithmic speedup is orthogonal to engineering optimizations like CUDA graph compilation and vLLM's page attention. PAR-3B-4× with a simple PyTorch compile achieves 1.15s latency, outperforming LlamaGen-3B with full vLLM optimization at 4.12s.

This additive rather than substitutive positioning means PAR can be adopted incrementally—existing autoregressive visual generation models can be adapted to the parallel generation order with minimal changes to training and inference pipelines.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

PAR is a **generation-order strategy** for standard autoregressive transformers that predicts multiple visual tokens simultaneously—rather than one at a time—by reorganizing which tokens get generated together based on their spatial dependencies. The system solves the problem of slow autoregressive visual generation (576 sequential forward passes for a single image) by identifying groups of tokens with weak mutual dependencies that can be sampled in parallel without introducing visual inconsistencies, achieving a 3.6–9.5× reduction in generation steps with minimal quality loss—all without modifying the transformer architecture, tokenizer, or training objective.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The PAR system has four major components:

1. **Token grid partitioner:** Takes the 2D grid of visual tokens from a standard tokenizer (VQGAN or MAGVIT-v2) and divides it into an `$M \times M$` grid of equally-sized rectangular regions. Each region contains `$k = (H/M) \times (W/M)$` tokens arranged in raster order internally.

2. **Token reordering mechanism:** Regroups tokens so that tokens at the same relative position across different regions are placed adjacent in the generation sequence. For 4× parallelization with `$M=2$`, this produces groups like `$[v_1^{(1)}, v_1^{(2)}, v_1^{(3)}, v_1^{(4)}]$` —the tokens at position 1 from all four regions—followed by position-2 tokens across all regions, and so on.

3. **Two-stage generation process:** A **Stage 1** that generates the very first token of each region sequentially (one at a time, standard autoregressive sampling) to establish global structure, followed by a **Stage 2** that generates all remaining tokens in parallel groups—at each step, predicting the next-position tokens across all regions simultaneously using independent sampling from per-token conditional distributions.

4. **Modified attention mask (group-wise bi-directional):** A hybrid attention pattern where tokens within the same parallel prediction group can attend to each other bidirectionally (full attention), while causal masking is preserved between groups—each group can see all tokens from previous groups but no tokens from future groups. This gives each token access to the entire previous group as context while keeping the global autoregressive property intact.

Information flow: A standard tokenizer encodes the image into a `$H \times W$` grid of discrete tokens → the grid is partitioned into `$M \times M$` regions → tokens are reindexed following the cross-region grouping order → the sequence is fed into a standard autoregressive transformer with the modified attention mask → the transformer generates initial region tokens one at a time using standard next-token prediction → once initial tokens exist for all regions, the model switches to multi-token prediction mode (using learned transition embeddings), predicting `$M^2$` tokens per forward pass → the output token sequence is rearranged back into grid form and decoded by the tokenizer into an image.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the token dependency pilot study and the three design principles it motivates, since these form the conceptual foundation for every architectural decision.
- **Second**, the cross-region token grouping formalism—how the grid is partitioned, how tokens are reindexed, and what the resulting sequence structure looks like.
- **Third**, the two-stage generation process (Stage 1 sequential, Stage 2 parallel) with its probabilistic formulation and the justification for why initial tokens require special handling.
- **Fourth**, the model architecture details—learnable transition tokens, 2D Rotary Position Embeddings, and the group-wise attention mask that enable the parallel prediction within a standard transformer.
- **Fifth**, the extension to video generation, which follows the same principles but with 3D token grids and spatial-only parallelization.
- **Sixth**, the entropy-based validation in Appendix D, which provides the theoretical justification for why the cross-region grouping works.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that parallel generation in autoregressive visual models is feasible if—and only if—the tokens predicted simultaneously have weak mutual dependencies, and that spatially-distant tokens from different image regions satisfy this criterion while adjacent tokens do not.

---

#### Token Dependencies and the Failure of Naive Parallel Prediction

The paper's starting point is an empirical observation about what happens when standard autoregressive visual models attempt naive multi-token prediction. In a standard autoregressive transformer, each token `$v_t$` is sampled from a conditional distribution `$\mathbb{P}(v_t | v_{<t})$` that the model produces as a categorical distribution over the tokenizer's codebook. When generating multiple tokens in parallel (say, tokens at positions `$t$` through `$t+n-1$` simultaneously), the model must factor the joint distribution:

$$\mathbb{P}(v_t, v_{t+1}, \ldots, v_{t+n-1} | v_{<t}) \approx \prod_{i=0}^{n-1} \mathbb{P}(v_{t+i} | v_{<t})$$

where each factor is computed independently at that forward pass. This is only correct when the tokens `$v_t, \ldots, v_{t+n-1}$` are **conditionally independent** given the prefix `$v_{<t}$`—that is, when knowing the value of one of these tokens provides no additional information about the others beyond what the prefix already contains.

**What this equation means operationally:** The model produces `$n$` separate categorical distributions (one per parallel token) by running one forward pass with `$n$` output heads that all condition on the shared prefix `$v_{<t}$`. Each token is then sampled from its own distribution independently—the sampling of token `$t$` does not influence the sampling of token `$t+1$` within the same group. If tokens `$t$` and `$t+1$` are strongly correlated (e.g., adjacent pixels in the same local region), the independent samples will be individually plausible but jointly inconsistent—like choosing a tiger fur texture for one token but a zebra stripe texture for its neighbor because neither "knew" what the other chose.

**Why this form fails for adjacent tokens:** In raster scan order, consecutive tokens are spatial neighbors (e.g., the right neighbor of the previous token on the same row). These tokens share strong local correlations—edges, textures, object boundaries all span adjacent token positions. The conditional independence approximation is severely violated, leading to the distorted local patterns visible in Figure 1(b) (fragmented zebra stripes, inconsistent tiger faces) and the catastrophic FID of 5.64 reported in Table 4(d) for raster-scan multi-token prediction.

**The central insight:** For visual tokens, dependency strength decays with spatial distance. Tokens from distant regions of the image (e.g., a token in the top-left representing sky vs. a token in the bottom-right representing ground) have much weaker conditional dependencies. Their joint distribution more closely factorizes into independent conditionals:

$$\mathbb{P}(v_t^{\text{far}}, v_{t'}^{\text{far}} | v_{< \min(t,t')}) \approx \mathbb{P}(v_t^{\text{far}} | v_{< \min(t,t')}) \cdot \mathbb{P}(v_{t'}^{\text{far}} | v_{< \min(t,t')})$$

This means that if the model can be made to predict **spatially distant** tokens in parallel rather than **spatially adjacent** tokens, the independent sampling approximation becomes much more accurate.

---

#### Three Design Principles Derived from the Dependency Analysis

From pilot experiments and the spatial dependency observation, the paper extracts three concrete design rules that govern the entire PAR method:

**Principle 1: Generate initial tokens for each region sequentially.** Even spatially distant tokens can carry strong dependencies when they jointly establish the global image structure. The first token of each spatial region encodes coarse information about what that region contains—object identity, rough color, semantic category. If all regions' initial tokens are generated in parallel (each conditioned only on the class token and preceding regions' initial tokens that don't yet exist for the current parallel group), the model cannot coordinate these global decisions across regions. The ablation in Table 4(a) quantifies this: removing sequential initialization causes FID to rise from 2.61 to 3.67, and Figure 5 (middle row) shows the qualitative failure mode—dogs with duplicated body parts and misaligned anatomy because each region independently decided what to contain.

**Principle 2: Maintain sequential generation within local regions.** Within each spatial region, tokens follow raster scan order and have the same strong local dependencies that make naive multi-token prediction fail. The solution is to keep these within-region tokens sequential—each token in a region sees all previous tokens in that same region before being generated. The parallelism comes from generating corresponding-position tokens across *different* regions simultaneously, not from parallelizing within a single region.

**Principle 3: Enable parallel generation across regions for tokens at aligned positions.** Once the initial token of each region is established (sequential Stage 1), tokens at the same relative position within each region (e.g., the second token of region 1, the second token of region 2, etc.) are spatially distant from each other and carry weak dependencies. These can be predicted in parallel at each step. The "aligned position" criterion ensures that at each parallel step, the model generates tokens that are in corresponding spatial locations of different regions—all the "top-left corner of the remaining tokens" across all regions simultaneously, then all the "next token in raster order" across all regions simultaneously.

---

#### Formal Token Grouping: Cross-Region Position-Based Reindexing

The core algorithmic contribution is a systematic way to reorganize the token sequence so that tokens predicted together satisfy the weak-dependency criterion. Let the token grid be `$\{v_i\}_{i=1}^{H \times W}$` arranged in `$H$` rows and `$W$` columns. The reindexing proceeds in two steps:

**Step 1: Partition into regions.** The `$H \times W$` grid is divided into an `$M \times M$` grid of equal-sized regions. Each region `$r \in \{1, 2, \ldots, M^2\}$` contains exactly `$k = \left(\frac{H}{M}\right) \times \left(\frac{W}{M}\right)$` tokens. For 4× parallelization (`$M=2$`) on a 24×24 grid (384×384 images with 16× downsampling), each region is 12×12 tokens containing 144 tokens.

**Step 2: Group by relative position.** Let `$v_j^{(r)}$` denote the token at position `$j$` within region `$r$`, where `$j \in \{1, 2, \ldots, k\}$` indexes positions in raster-scan order within each region. The reindexed sequence is:

$$\Big\{[v_1^{(1)}, \ldots, v_1^{(M^2)}], [v_2^{(1)}, \ldots, v_2^{(M^2)}], \ldots, [v_k^{(1)}, \ldots, v_k^{(M^2)}]\Big\}$$

where `$v_j^{(r)}$` is the token at the `$j$`-th raster position within region `$r$`, `$M^2$` is the total number of regions, and `$k$` is the number of tokens per region.

**What this produces:** A sequence of `$k$` groups, each containing `$M^2$` tokens. The first group contains the first (top-left) token from every region. The second group contains the second token (raster order) from every region. And so on through group `$k$`. For `$M=2$` and a 24×24 grid, this produces 144 groups of 4 tokens each, plus the initial sequential tokens (4 tokens generated one at a time), yielding a total of 147 generation steps instead of 576.

**Why this grouping works:** Each group contains tokens that are at the same relative offset within their respective regions but from spatially distant regions of the image. Token `$v_j^{(1)}$` might represent a patch in the top-left quadrant, while `$v_j^{(4)}$` represents a patch in the bottom-right quadrant. These tokens are separated by at least `$H/M$` or `$W/M$` spatial positions, making their conditional dependencies weak. Meanwhile, tokens within a single region remain sequential—`$v_1^{(r)}$` is generated before `$v_2^{(r)}$`, which is generated before `$v_3^{(r)}$`, preserving the strong local dependencies that need sequential conditioning.

**Figure 3 illustrates this process** with specific numbering: tokens 1–4 are the initial tokens of the four regions (generated sequentially in Stage 1). Tokens 5a–5d are the second token of each region, generated in parallel. Tokens 6a–6d are the third token of each region, generated in parallel. The letter suffix (a, b, c, d) indicates which region the token belongs to, and the number indicates the generation step—same number means same parallel step.

---

#### Stage 1: Sequential Generation of Region-Initializing Tokens

The first stage generates exactly `$M^2$` tokens—one per region—in strict sequential order. Starting from the top-left region and proceeding through all regions in raster order (region 1, region 2, ..., region `$M^2$`), the model samples:

$$v_1^{(i)} \sim \mathbb{P}\left(v_1^{(i)} \middle| v_1^{(<i)}\right), \quad i \in \{1, 2, \ldots, M^2\}$$

where `$v_1^{(i)}$` is the first token of the `$i$`-th region (the "initial token" for that region), and `$v_1^{(<i)}$` represents the set of initial tokens from all previously processed regions `$\{v_1^{(1)}, v_1^{(2)}, \ldots, v_1^{(i-1)}\}$`.

**What happens physically:** For `$M=2$` (4 regions), the model performs 4 sequential forward passes. Pass 1: predicts the top-left corner token of region 1, conditioned on the class token alone. Pass 2: predicts the top-left corner token of region 2, conditioned on the class token and the newly generated token from region 1. Pass 3: predicts region 3's initial token, conditioned on class token + region 1's initial + region 2's initial. Pass 4: predicts region 4's initial token, conditioned on class token + all three previous initial tokens.

**Why this sequential structure is necessary:** These `$M^2$` tokens collectively define the coarse global layout. The first token of a region carries information about what semantic content that region contains—for a tiger image, region 1's initial token might encode "orange fur," region 2's "black stripe," region 3's "white fur," and region 4's "green background." If generated in parallel, region 2 might independently decide "water" while region 1 decides "tiger fur," producing an incoherent chimera. The sequential generation lets each region's initial token be informed by what neighboring regions have already decided, creating a coordinated global structure.

**Cost:** The sequential overhead is `$M^2$` steps. For `$M=2$` (4× parallelization), this is 4 steps. For `$M=4$` (16× parallelization), this is 16 steps. Given that these steps save 75–94% of the subsequent steps, the overhead is negligible—the total steps go from 576 to 147 (4×) or 51 (16×).

---

#### Stage 2: Parallel Generation of Aligned-Position Cross-Region Tokens

After all regions have their initial tokens, the model switches to parallel mode. At each subsequent step `$j$` (where `$j \in \{2, 3, \ldots, k\}$`), the model simultaneously predicts the `$j$`-th token of every region:

$$\{v_j^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2} \sim \mathbb{P}\left(\{v_j^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2} \middle| v_{<j}\right)$$

where `$\{v_j^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2}$` is the set of all tokens at position `$j$` across all `$M^2$` regions (the parallel group), and `$v_{<j}$` represents all previously generated tokens—both the Stage 1 initial tokens `$\{v_1^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2}$` and all tokens from parallel steps 2 through `$j-1$` (i.e., `$\{v_2^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2}, \{v_3^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2}, \ldots, \{v_{j-1}^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2}$`).

**What happens physically at each parallel step:** The model takes as input the entire sequence generated so far (all initial tokens + all previous parallel groups) and produces `$M^2$` output logit vectors—one per token position in the next group. For `$M=2$`, this is 4 token predictions per forward pass. Each of these `$M^2$` tokens is sampled independently from its own categorical distribution (typically with top-k or top-p sampling for diversity). The model produces all `$M^2$` predictions in a single forward pass by having `$M^2$` separate output projection heads (or equivalently, by shifting the target sequence by `$M^2$` positions relative to the input sequence, as shown in Figure 4(a)).

**Why independent sampling works here:** The tokens `$\{v_j^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2}$` are at corresponding positions in different spatial regions. Token `$v_j^{(1)}$` is the `$j$`-th raster token in the top-left region, `$v_j^{(2)}$` is the `$j$`-th raster token in the top-right region, etc. These tokens are separated by at least half the image width/height. Their conditional dependencies—the information one provides about another beyond what the prefix `$v_{<j}$` already contains—are weak. This is the core assumption validated by the entropy analysis in Appendix D: the increase in conditional entropy from parallel vs. sequential generation is small for the cross-region ordering.

**The autoregressive property is preserved:** Critically, the parallel group at step `$j$` is conditioned on ALL previous tokens. The group at step `$j+1$` will be conditioned on the group at step `$j$` plus everything before it. This means the model maintains the full autoregressive chain—every token prediction has access to the entire generation history, just like in standard sequential generation. The only difference is that within a single parallel group, tokens are generated without seeing each other.

**Why this is autoregressive and not non-autoregressive masked modeling:** In MaskGIT-style approaches, tokens are generated in multiple passes with iterative refinement—the same position may be predicted multiple times. In PAR, each token is generated exactly once and never revised. The conditioning structure `$\mathbb{P}(\text{group}_j | \text{all previous groups})$` is a valid autoregressive factorization of the joint distribution, just with coarser time steps (groups of `$M^2$` tokens rather than individual tokens).

#### Model Architecture: Enabling Parallel Prediction in Standard Transformers

The implementation within a standard autoregressive transformer requires three architectural elements, none of which modify the core transformer layers.

**Input sequence structure:** As shown in Figure 4(a), the input sequence is organized into three segments:

1. **Class token [C]:** A learned embedding representing the ImageNet class label (or video action category), placed at the start of the sequence for classifier-free guidance.
2. **Initial sequential tokens + transition tokens:** The `$M^2$` Stage 1 tokens `$[1, 2, \ldots, M^2]$` generated one at a time, followed by `$M^2 - 1$` learnable tokens `$[M_1, M_2, \ldots, M_{M^2-1}]$`. The learnable tokens are randomly initialized embeddings with the same dimensionality as regular token embeddings, trained end-to-end. Their purpose is to provide the model with a "mode switch" signal—they help the transformer transition from single-token prediction mode to multi-token prediction mode by absorbing the distribution shift in the hidden representations that occurs when the output offset changes.
3. **Parallel prediction groups:** The remaining sequence organized as `$k-1$` groups of `$M^2$` tokens each, e.g., `$[5a, 5b, 5c, 5d], [6a, 6b, 6c, 6d], \ldots$`.

**Training:** During training, the full sequence is provided as input (teacher forcing). The model is trained with standard next-token prediction cross-entropy loss. The input sequence consists of all tokens up to the beginning of the current parallel group, and the target sequence is the input shifted by `$M^2$` positions (since `$M^2$` tokens are predicted per step). The learnable transition tokens appear in the input but not in the target—they are auxiliary tokens that the model attends to but doesn't predict.

**2D Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE):** Standard autoregressive models use 1D positional encodings where the position index is simply the sequence position. Under the PAR reordering, this would assign adjacent position indices to tokens from different spatial regions, losing the spatial structure that the attention mechanism needs to learn spatial dependencies. The solution is 2D RoPE, which encodes each token's original `$(h, w)$` coordinates in the image grid as a pair of sinusoidal frequencies, independently of the token's position in the reordered sequence. The encoding for a token at grid position `$(h, w)$` is:

$$\text{RoPE}(h, w) = [\cos(h \cdot \omega_1), \sin(h \cdot \omega_1), \cos(w \cdot \omega_2), \sin(w \cdot \omega_2), \ldots]$$

where the frequencies `$\omega_1, \omega_2$` follow the standard RoPE scheme (geometric progression across head dimensions). This ensures that the attention mechanism can still compute spatial-relationship-based attention weights even though the sequence order has been rearranged—two tokens that are spatial neighbors will have similar RoPE encodings and thus similar attention patterns, regardless of how far apart they are in the reordered sequence.

**Why 2D RoPE over learned positional embeddings:** Learned absolute position embeddings would break under the reordering because the sequence position no longer corresponds to spatial position in any fixed way. 1D RoPE applied to the reordered sequence would encode the "distance in the reordered sequence," which is meaningless for spatial relationships. 2D RoPE applied to original grid coordinates preserves exactly the information the attention mechanism needs—the relative 2D offset between any pair of tokens.

**Group-wise bi-directional attention with global autoregression (Figure 4b):** The attention mask is the key mechanism that balances parallelism with rich context. Standard causal masking for autoregressive models is a lower-triangular matrix: token at position `$i$` can attend to tokens at positions `$\leq i$`. For PAR, this would mean that within a parallel group `$[v_j^{(1)}, \ldots, v_j^{(M^2)}]$`, token `$v_j^{(2)}$` could attend to `$v_j^{(1)}$` (since it appears earlier in the sequence), but `$v_j^{(1)}$` cannot attend to `$v_j^{(2)}$`. This **asymmetric** access within the group is suboptimal—the tokens are being generated simultaneously, so there's no principled reason to give some more context than others.

**The fix:** Attention within each parallel group is made **fully bi-directional**—every token in the group can attend to every other token in the group. Meanwhile, attention between groups remains **causal**—the group at position `$j$` can attend to all tokens from groups at positions `$< j$` but not to tokens from groups at positions `$\geq j$`. Formally, the attention mask `$A$` satisfies:

$$A_{a,b} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \text{group}(a) = \text{group}(b) \\ 1 & \text{if } \text{group}(b) < \text{group}(a) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where `$\text{group}(x)$` returns the group index of token `$x$` (Stage 1 tokens are each in their own singleton group; all Stage 2 tokens are in groups of size `$M^2$`).

**What this enables:** In Figure 4(b), when predicting the group `$[6a, 6b, 6c, 6d]$`, every token in this group can attend to:
- All tokens from previous groups (the initial tokens 1–4, and the parallel groups `$[5a,5b,5c,5d]$` and earlier)
- All other tokens in the current group `$[6a, 6b, 6c, 6d]$` (bi-directional within-group)

This means token `$6b$` has access to the entire `$[5a,5b,5c,5d]$` group (not just up to `$5b$` as naive causal masking would allow), giving it richer local context. The ablation in Table 4(c) confirms this matters: full attention reduces FID from 3.64 (causal-only within groups) to 2.61, a 1.03 improvement.

**Why this preserves the autoregressive property:** A model is autoregressive if the joint probability factorizes as `$\prod_t \mathbb{P}(v_t | v_{<t})$`. For PAR, the factorization is:

$$\mathbb{P}(v_{1:M^2}^{(1)}, v_{2:M^2}^{(1:M^2)}, \ldots) = \prod_{i=1}^{M^2} \mathbb{P}(v_1^{(i)} | v_1^{(<i)}) \cdot \prod_{j=2}^{k} \mathbb{P}(\{v_j^{(r)}\}_{r=1}^{M^2} | v_{<j})$$

This is a valid autoregressive factorization with coarser time steps: each group is conditioned on all previous groups. The bi-directional attention within a group does not violate this because the group's tokens are generated jointly from a distribution that conditions on all previous tokens but treats the group tokens as conditionally independent given that history (the parallel sampling approximation). There is no "peeking" at future groups.

**KV-cache compatibility:** The hybrid mask remains compatible with standard KV-cache optimizations because the causal structure between groups is preserved. At inference, the KV-cache stores key-value pairs for all tokens in previously generated groups. When generating a new group, the model computes attention for all `$M^2$` tokens simultaneously, attending to the cached previous-group keys/values bi-directionally within the current group. The cache grows by `$M^2$` entries per step rather than 1 entry per step, but the caching mechanism is identical.

**Learnable transition tokens:** The `$M^2 - 1$` learnable embeddings `$[M_1, \ldots, M_{M^2-1}]$` are inserted between the sequential and parallel segments of the sequence. They serve a specific technical purpose: in standard autoregressive training, the model always sees exactly one output token per input position (the next token). In PAR, after the Stage 1 sequential tokens, the output offset jumps from 1 to `$M^2$`—the model must suddenly predict `$M^2$` tokens ahead instead of 1. The transition tokens provide `$M^2 - 1$` buffer positions in the input that absorb this offset change, giving the transformer layers time to adjust their internal representations from "single-token prediction mode" (where each position predicts one next token) to "multi-token prediction mode" (where each position is part of a group that collectively predicts `$M^2$` tokens). Without these tokens, the abrupt change in the input-target offset could cause a representation mismatch at the boundary. The transition tokens are trained end-to-end and are the only architectural addition—for `$M=2$` (4×), there are exactly 3 such tokens, each with dimension equal to the model's hidden size (1024–3200 depending on model scale).

#### Parallel Generation Scaling: Trade-off Between Speed and Quality

The number of regions `$M$` controls the degree of parallelization: `$n = M^2$` tokens are predicted per step. This creates a tunable speed-quality trade-off space investigated in Table 4(b).

**For `$M=2$` (`$n=4$`, 4× parallelization):** The token grid is split into 4 regions (2×2). Stage 1 generates 4 tokens sequentially. Stage 2 generates `$k-1 = 143$` groups of 4 tokens each. Total steps: `$4 + 143 = 147$` (vs. 576 sequential). The speed-quality trade-off is excellent: FID increases by only 0.01 (2.35 vs. 2.34 for the baseline PAR-XXL), while generation steps reduce by 3.9×.

**For `$M=4$` (`$n=16$`, 16× parallelization):** The token grid is split into 16 regions (4×4). Stage 1 generates 16 tokens sequentially. Stage 2 generates `$k-1$` groups of 16 tokens each, where `$k = (24/4) \times (24/4) = 36$` (since each region is now 6×6 tokens). Total steps: `$16 + 35 = 51$`. The quality impact is larger but still modest: FID increases by 0.67 (3.02 vs. 2.34), and the 11.3× step reduction still produces visually coherent images (Figure 2, Figure 9).

**Why quality degrades at higher parallelization:** As `$M$` increases, each region becomes smaller (fewer tokens), meaning tokens predicted in parallel come from regions that are less far apart spatially. For `$M=2$`, cross-region tokens are separated by at least 12 grid positions. For `$M=4$`, they're separated by at least 6 grid positions—still substantial but allowing more residual dependency. Additionally, the Stage 1 sequential overhead grows quadratically (`$M^2$` initial tokens), and for very large `$M$`, the initial tokens would constitute a significant fraction of the total steps, reducing the net speedup.

**Why the quality gap shrinks with model scale (Table 4(e)):** At 343M parameters, PAR-4× has a noticeable FID gap vs. the sequential baseline (3.76 vs. 3.07, difference of 0.69). At 775M, the gap narrows (2.61 vs. 2.62, difference of −0.01—PAR actually slightly better). At 1.4B and 3.1B, PAR and the sequential baseline converge (2.35 vs. 2.34; 2.29 vs. 2.18). Larger models have more capacity to learn the joint distribution of parallel token groups, effectively compensating for the independent sampling approximation by building richer contextual representations that make the conditional independence assumption closer to correct.

#### Extension to Video Generation

Video data introduces a temporal dimension, producing token grids of shape `$T \times H \times W$`. The paper applies PAR along the spatial dimensions only, treating the temporally compressed tokens similarly to image tokens but with 3D positional embeddings.

**Tokenization:** The MAGVIT-v2 tokenizer compresses a 17-frame video (128×128 resolution) by 4× temporally and 8× spatially, yielding a `$5 \times 16 \times 16$` grid—1280 tokens total. Each "frame" in this latent space actually represents a temporal chunk of ~3.4 original frames (17/5 ≈ 3.4), since the temporal compression aggregates multiple input frames into a single latent token.

**Spatial-only parallelization:** PAR applies the same `$M \times M$` grid partitioning and cross-region grouping, but treats each of the `$T$` temporal slices independently. For `$M=2$` (4× parallelization), each `$16 \times 16$` spatial grid is divided into 4 regions of `$8 \times 8$` tokens. The cross-region grouping is applied per-frame, and tokens are generated in parallel across spatial regions within each temporal step. The generation order interleaves spatial and temporal dimensions: for each temporal position, spatial tokens are generated with the two-stage PAR process, then the model moves to the next temporal position.

**Why temporal parallelization was avoided:** The paper reports exploring parallel generation along the temporal dimension but finding it "less effective than spatial parallelization." The reason is that "temporal dependencies exhibit stronger sequential characteristics that are fundamental to video coherence"—tokens representing the same spatial location across consecutive frames have strong causal relationships (objects move, transform, or persist along predictable trajectories). Independent sampling of these temporally correlated tokens would break motion consistency and cause flickering artifacts. Spatial correlations, by contrast, decay more predictably with distance.

**Results:** On UCF-101, PAR-4× with a 792M-parameter model achieves 323 generation steps (vs. 1280 sequential), 11.27s latency (vs. 43.30s), and FVD of 99.5 (vs. 94.1 for the sequential baseline)—a 3.8× speedup for a 5.4 FVD increase. PAR-16× achieves 95 steps, 3.44s latency, and FVD of 103.4—a 12.6× speedup for 9.3 FVD increase. These results demonstrate that the spatial dependency structure exploited by PAR generalizes across visual domains (images and videos) and tokenization methods (VQGAN and MAGVIT-v2).

#### Entropy-Based Validation of the Cross-Region Grouping Strategy (Appendix D)

The paper provides a theoretical justification for its design through an information-theoretic analysis that quantifies why the cross-region grouping works better than naive raster-scan grouping. The analysis uses conditional entropy as a measure of token dependency and estimates it from data.

**Conditional entropy as a dependency measure:** The conditional entropy `$H(v_k | \{v_j\}_{j<k})$` measures the uncertainty remaining about token `$v_k$` given knowledge of all previous tokens `$\{v_j\}_{j<k}$`. Lower conditional entropy means the previous tokens provide strong predictive information about `$v_k$` (strong dependency). Higher conditional entropy means the previous tokens provide little information (weak dependency).

**Estimation method:** Since exact computation of `$H(v_k | \{v_j\}_{j<k})$` is intractable (it requires the full data distribution), the paper uses a parametric model to estimate an upper bound. The approach models the relationship as:

$$v_k = f(\{v_j\}_{j<k}) + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k$$

where `$v_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is the continuous feature vector of token `$k$` (obtained from the VQGAN encoder's codebook lookup—the discrete token is mapped to its learned continuous embedding), `$f(\cdot)$` is a deterministic function, and `$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$` is a random additive error term.

The conditional entropy then satisfies:

$$H(v_k | \{v_j\}_{j<k}) = H(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k | \{v_j\}_{j<k}) \leq H(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k) \leq \frac{1}{2} \log((2\pi e)^d |\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|)$$

where `$H(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k)$` is the entropy of the error term, `$d$` is the feature dimension, and `$\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$` is the covariance matrix of `$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k$`.

**What this bound means:** The determinant of the residual covariance matrix `$|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|$` provides a tractable proxy for conditional entropy—larger `$|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|$` means higher residual variance (weaker dependency), while smaller `$|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|$` means lower residual variance (stronger dependency). The bound is tight when the errors are Gaussian (maximum entropy distribution for fixed covariance), making it a reasonable approximation.

**Implementation (Algorithm 1 in Appendix D):**
1. Sample 10,000 images from ImageNet and extract their VQGAN continuous features.
2. For each target token position `$k$` and conditioning set `$\{v_j\}_{j<k}$`, train a small neural network `$f_\theta$` to predict `$v_k$` from `$\{v_j\}_{j<k}$`.
3. Compute residuals `$\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k = v_k - f_\theta(\{v_j\}_{j<k})$` for all samples.
4. Estimate the covariance matrix `$\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}$` of these residuals.
5. Compute the proxy `$\frac{1}{2}\log((2\pi e)^d|\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}|)$`.

**Spatial dependency map results (Figure 11):** For each reference token position (blue square), the conditional entropy `$H(v_i | v_{\text{ref}})$` is computed for every other position `$i$`. The resulting heatmaps consistently show that tokens spatially adjacent to the reference token have the lowest conditional entropy (darkest red, strongest dependency), while tokens farther away have progressively higher conditional entropy (lighter colors, weaker dependency). This empirically confirms the spatial decay of token dependencies that PAR exploits.

**Entropy increase from parallel generation (Figure 12):** The critical comparison is between two generation orders under parallel prediction. For both the proposed cross-region order and the naive raster-scan order, the paper computes:

$$\Delta H = H(v_k | \mathcal{V}_k^{\text{par}}) - H(v_k | \mathcal{V}_k^{\text{seq}})$$

where `$\mathcal{V}_k^{\text{par}}$` is the set of previous tokens available during parallel generation (same-group tokens excluded) and `$\mathcal{V}_k^{\text{seq}}$` is the set available during sequential generation (all previous tokens). This `$\Delta H$` measures how much harder prediction becomes when a token must be generated in parallel—higher values indicate the parallel approximation is worse.

**Results shown in Figure 12(c) and (f):** 
- Both orderings show zero entropy increase for the first four tokens (white regions), since these are generated sequentially in both strategies.
- For subsequent tokens, the raster scan order produces large red regions (high `$\Delta H$`)—predicting consecutive tokens together introduces substantial prediction difficulty.
- The proposed cross-region order produces dramatically smaller `$\Delta H$` values across most positions—the entropy increase is modest and distributed relatively uniformly.

**What this validates:** Generating spatially distant tokens from different regions in parallel introduces far less prediction difficulty than generating spatially adjacent tokens in parallel. The cross-region grouping successfully identifies token subsets that are approximately conditionally independent given the generation history, making the independent sampling approximation accurate.

#### Design Rationale Summary: Why This Approach Over Alternatives

**Why not speculative decoding (draft model approach)?** Speculative decoding requires training and maintaining a separate draft model, adding system complexity. It also requires the draft model's predictions to be verified by the main model, which can lead to rejected tokens (wasted computation). For visual generation with large token vocabularies (16K+), the verification step is less straightforward than for text (where token equality is a clean acceptance criterion). PAR achieves parallelization without any auxiliary model.

**Why not Jacobi/iterative refinement?** Iterative methods require multiple passes to converge, meaning the total FLOPs may not decrease even if wall-clock time improves (if multiple parallel passes are needed). PAR generates each token exactly once with no refinement, giving a clean FLOPs reduction proportional to the step reduction.

**Why not masked modeling (MaskGIT)?** Masked modeling abandons the autoregressive factorization entirely. While effective, it means the model cannot be seamlessly integrated into unified autoregressive multimodal systems that handle text, images, and video with a single next-token-prediction objective. PAR preserves the autoregressive structure, making it compatible with such unified architectures.

**Why not architectural changes (next-scale prediction, VAR)?** VAR requires a multi-scale tokenizer that generates tokens at multiple resolutions, increasing the total token count and requiring specialized architecture. This couples the generation speed strategy to the tokenizer design, reducing flexibility. PAR works with any standard 2D tokenizer without modification.

**Why spatial grouping specifically?** The paper considered but rejected temporal parallelization for video because temporal dependencies are fundamentally stronger and more sequential. The spatial dependency decay is a more reliable property of natural images—the conditional entropy maps in Figure 11 show consistent, predictable decay with distance that holds across ImageNet samples. This makes spatial cross-region grouping a robust strategy with predictable behavior.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Parallel Generation as a Token Dependency Problem Rather Than a Model Architecture Problem

The dominant assumption across prior work in efficient visual generation—whether MaskGIT's masked modeling, VAR's next-scale prediction, or language-model-inspired speculative decoding—is that achieving parallelism requires **changing the model or the inference algorithm**. MaskGIT abandons autoregression entirely. VAR redesigns the tokenizer to produce multi-scale representations. Speculative decoding introduces an auxiliary draft model. All of these approaches accept as a premise that standard autoregressive transformers are inherently sequential and must be modified or augmented to achieve parallelism.

This paper makes a fundamentally different move: it argues that the barrier to parallel generation in autoregressive visual models is not architectural but **informational**. The bottleneck is not that the transformer cannot produce multiple tokens per forward pass—it trivially can, by adding output heads—but that **the tokens being predicted together must be approximately conditionally independent** for independent sampling to produce consistent results. The architecture is capable; the naive token ordering is wrong.

This reframing matters because it redirects research effort from model design toward **data-structure design**—specifically, understanding and exploiting the dependency graph of the token sequence. The paper's central empirical finding is that this dependency graph has a predictable spatial structure in visual data: dependencies decay with distance, as confirmed quantitatively through the conditional entropy analysis in Appendix D (Figure 11). Prior work had observed that adjacent-token parallel prediction fails (producing distorted local structures), but interpreted this as evidence that autoregressive visual generation is inherently sequential. This paper demonstrates that the failure is specific to the raster scan ordering, not to autoregressive generation itself—switch to a cross-region ordering that groups spatially distant tokens, and parallel generation works with minimal quality loss (FID 2.35 vs. 2.34 for 4× parallelization, Table 4(b)).

The diagnostic contribution is as important as the method: the paper provides an **information-theoretic framework** (conditional entropy estimation, Appendix D) for predicting *which* token groupings will succeed under parallel generation and which will fail. Figure 12 shows that the entropy increase from parallel vs. sequential generation is dramatically smaller for the cross-region ordering than for raster scan ordering, providing a principled explanation for the empirical results in Table 4(d) (FID 2.61 vs. 5.64). This framework is reusable: future work on parallel visual generation can apply the same conditional entropy analysis to evaluate candidate token orderings without extensive trial-and-error experimentation.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond visual generation. The paper demonstrates that the relationship between **sequence order and sample quality** is not merely an implementation detail but a first-class design dimension in autoregressive modeling. Language modeling has largely taken left-to-right token order as given; this paper shows that for modalities with 2D or 3D structure, the generation order can and should be optimized for parallelism, not just for the quality of the autoregressive factorization. This is a **fundamental conceptual shift**—not an incremental improvement to an existing parallel decoding method.

---

### Innovation 2: Establishing Initial-Region Tokens as a Distinct Generation Phase with Global Coordination Requirements

A naive application of the cross-region grouping insight would parallelize *all* tokens, including the very first token of each region. After all, these initial tokens are spatially distant from each other, so by the dependency-decay argument, they should be parallelizable. The paper's critical finding is that **this reasoning fails for tokens that jointly determine global structure**—the initial tokens of each region are a special case where spatial distance does not imply weak dependency.

The ablation in Table 4(a) quantifies this precisely: removing the sequential generation of initial tokens (generating them in parallel instead) causes FID to degrade from 2.61 to 3.67, a 1.06-point increase for a change that affects only 4 out of 576 tokens. Figure 5 (middle row) visualizes the failure mode: dogs with duplicated body parts, misaligned structures, and incoherent global layouts. These 4 tokens constitute less than 0.7% of the total generation steps but their sequential generation is responsible for a substantial fraction of the model's global coherence.

This finding reveals a **hierarchical structure in token dependencies** that the simple spatial-distance heuristic misses. Tokens can be classified into two qualitatively different categories:

- **Structure-establishing tokens** (the first token of each spatial region): These tokens collectively encode the coarse semantic layout—what objects appear where, their approximate scale and pose, the overall composition. Even though they are spatially distant, they are **semantically coupled** because they must produce a globally consistent scene. A tiger's head cannot appear in two different regions simultaneously; the decision of where the head goes must be coordinated across regions.

- **Detail-refining tokens** (all subsequent tokens within each region): These tokens add local texture, edges, and fine-grained patterns that are largely independent across distant regions. Once region 1 has decided it contains "orange tiger fur" and region 3 has decided it contains "green grass," the specific fur texture and grass blade patterns can be generated independently without conflict.

This distinction is not merely an empirical observation—it connects to a deeper principle about **when conditional independence fails in spatial autoregressive models**. Two tokens are conditionally independent given the generation history if the history contains all the information needed to coordinate their values. For detail tokens, the history (which includes all initial-region tokens) does contain this coordinating information—the initial tokens have already established the global layout, so detail tokens from different regions can be sampled independently. For the initial tokens themselves, the history is too sparse (only the class token and previously generated initial tokens from other regions), so parallel sampling fails.

This insight is **fundamental rather than incremental** because it provides a general criterion for identifying which tokens in a structured autoregressive sequence require sequential generation, independent of the specific modality or tokenizer. The criterion is: **tokens whose values constrain each other's plausible range in ways not already captured by the available conditioning context must be generated sequentially**. For visual data, the first token of each spatial region satisfies this criterion; the paper's ablation shows that the penalty for violating it is severe even when the tokens are spatially distant.

The practical implication is that any parallel generation strategy for structured data must identify and protect these "coordination-requiring" tokens, which may not be obvious from spatial or sequential position alone. The paper's two-stage design (sequential initialization followed by parallel detail generation) is a template that could generalize to other structured modalities—video, 3D, graphs—where analogous global-coordination tokens exist.

---

### Innovation 3: Demonstrating That Algorithmic Step Reduction and Engineering Optimization Are Orthogonal and Complementary Axes for Inference Speedup

The paper makes a distinction that is often conflated in the efficient-inference literature: **algorithmic speedup** (reducing the number of sequential generation steps by changing the generation strategy) versus **engineering speedup** (making each step faster through kernel optimization, memory management, or hardware-specific tuning). Prior work in efficient visual generation often reports one or the other, making direct comparisons difficult and obscuring the potential for combining approaches.

Appendix B provides a clean experimental decomposition that demonstrates these are independent, multiplicative factors. On a single A100 GPU with batch size 1:

- **LlamaGen-3B with no optimization**: 12.41 seconds (576 sequential steps)
- **LlamaGen-3B with vLLM optimization** (page attention + CUDA graphs): 4.12 seconds (same 576 steps, but each step is faster)
- **PAR-3B-4× with no optimization**: 3.46 seconds (147 steps, but no engineering optimization)
- **PAR-3B-4× with PyTorch compile** (CUDA graphs only): 1.15 seconds (147 steps + engineering speedup)
- **PAR-3B-16× with PyTorch compile**: 0.43 seconds (51 steps + engineering speedup)

The key finding is that PAR's algorithmic step reduction alone (3.46s) already outperforms LlamaGen's engineering-optimized baseline (4.12s), even though PAR uses only standard PyTorch without the sophisticated memory management of vLLM. When engineering optimization is added on top of algorithmic reduction, the speedups multiply: PAR-3B-4× with simple compilation achieves a 3.6× improvement over the engineering-optimized LlamaGen baseline (1.15s vs. 4.12s). PAR-3B-16× pushes this to 9.6× (0.43s vs. 4.12s).

This decomposition matters for several reasons:

**It clarifies the research agenda.** Algorithmic step reduction and engineering optimization attack different bottlenecks—the former reduces the inherent sequential dependency chain, while the latter improves hardware utilization per step. A paper that reports only algorithmic improvements may understate total achievable speedup if engineering optimizations are not applied; conversely, a paper that reports only engineering speedups on a sequential baseline may overstate the gap to parallel methods. The paper's explicit decomposition allows practitioners to estimate the total latency they can achieve by combining the best of both.

**It demonstrates that PAR's design decisions are compatible with standard optimizations.** The group-wise bi-directional attention mask, learnable transition tokens, and 2D RoPE all preserve the causal structure between parallel groups that KV-caching relies on. The paper confirms that vLLM-style optimizations are directly applicable to PAR models, meaning the reported 0.43s latency is likely not a lower bound—further engineering work (kernel fusion for the modified attention pattern, optimized memory layout for the grouped token structure) could reduce it further.

**It reframes the efficiency conversation from "which method is fastest" to "which method has the best speed-quality Pareto frontier."** The paper's results in Table 2 and Table 7 define points on a frontier where PAR-4× achieves near-baseline quality at 3.6× speedup, PAR-16× achieves slightly degraded quality at 9.5× speedup, and engineering optimizations shift the entire frontier downward. This is a more useful framing for practitioners than a single speedup number, since deployment scenarios differ in their latency-quality trade-off tolerance.

This insight is **incremental in its components** (CUDA graphs and page attention are well-known techniques) but **significant in its systematic presentation**: the paper provides the first clear demonstration for autoregressive visual models that algorithmic and engineering speedups are orthogonal multipliers, not alternatives, and that combining them pushes generation latency into the sub-second regime where interactive applications become feasible.

---

### Innovation 4: Providing an Information-Theoretic Validation Framework That Explains Why the Method Works, Not Just That It Works

Many papers propose token reordering strategies for efficiency and demonstrate empirical improvements. What distinguishes this paper's contribution is the **quantitative diagnostic framework** in Appendix D that explains the mechanism underlying the empirical results. The conditional entropy estimation procedure (Algorithm 1) and the resulting analyses (Figures 11 and 12) constitute a reusable methodology for evaluating token ordering strategies in autoregressive generation.

The specific contributions of this framework are:

**It transforms the spatial intuition into a measurable quantity.** The claim that "spatially distant tokens have weak dependencies" is intuitive but vague. The conditional entropy maps in Figure 11 operationalize this claim: for any reference token, the conditional entropy of every other token given that reference can be estimated from data, producing a heatmap where darker red indicates lower conditional entropy (stronger dependency). The consistent pattern—strong dependencies local to the reference token, weak dependencies at distance—provides empirical grounding for the spatial decay assumption that PAR's design relies on.

**It explains the failure of raster-scan multi-token prediction in mechanistic terms.** Figure 12 computes the increase in conditional entropy when switching from sequential to parallel generation for both the proposed cross-region ordering and the naive raster-scan ordering. The raster-scan ordering shows large entropy increases (dark red regions) for most token positions, directly quantifying why adjacent-token parallel prediction produces the distorted local structures seen in Figure 1(b) and Figure 5 (bottom row). The cross-region ordering shows dramatically smaller entropy increases, validating that the independent sampling approximation is much more accurate for this token grouping.

**It provides a general methodology applicable beyond this paper's specific design.** The entropy estimation procedure (train a predictor, compute residual covariance, use the determinant as a proxy) does not depend on any particular model architecture or tokenizer. Future work on parallel generation for video, 3D, or other structured modalities can apply the same framework to evaluate candidate orderings before committing to expensive generation-quality experiments. This lowers the barrier to exploring alternative parallelization strategies.

**It connects the practical design to fundamental concepts in information theory.** The use of maximum-entropy bounds (`$H(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_k) \leq \frac{1}{2}\log((2\pi e)^d|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|)$`) and the Gaussian approximation for residual errors provides theoretical grounding for why the covariance determinant serves as a valid proxy for conditional entropy. The paper is careful to note that this is an upper bound, not an exact estimate—the absolute values are not meaningful, but the relative comparisons (which ordering produces larger entropy increases) are valid. This level of theoretical honesty (acknowledging what the framework can and cannot provide) strengthens rather than weakens the contribution.

This innovation is **fundamentally a diagnostic tool rather than a method**, but its significance lies in making the paper's design principles **falsifiable and generalizable**. Without this framework, the paper's claims about token dependencies would remain at the level of intuition and qualitative observation. With it, the paper establishes that the cross-region grouping is not merely a heuristic that happens to work on ImageNet, but a principled strategy derived from measurable properties of visual data that should generalize across datasets, resolutions, and tokenizers—a prediction the paper partially validates through the UCF-101 video generation results (Table 3).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on two standard benchmarks: **ImageNet-1K** (1,281,167 training images across 1,000 object classes; validation set of 50,000 images for evaluation) for class-conditional image generation, and **UCF-101** (~9.5K training videos across 101 action categories) for class-conditional video generation. Both are widely used in the visual generation literature, enabling direct comparison with prior work from GAN, diffusion, masked, and autoregressive paradigms.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use **autoregressive transformer models** following the LlamaGen architecture family (Sun et al., 2024). The paper trains four model scales for image generation: PAR-L (343M parameters, 24 layers, 1024 hidden dim, 16 heads), PAR-XL (775M, 36 layers, 1280 hidden, 20 heads), PAR-XXL (1.4B, 48 layers, 1536 hidden, 24 heads), and PAR-3B (3.1B, 24 layers, 3200 hidden, 32 heads). These configurations directly mirror LlamaGen's model sizes, enabling clean comparison of the parallel generation strategy against the sequential baseline with identical model capacity. For video generation, a 792M-parameter model is used (the paper does not specify the exact architecture dimensions, but the parameter count is comparable to the image generation models). The choice of LlamaGen-derived architecture is deliberate: it represents a state-of-the-art autoregressive visual generation model with publicly reported baselines, making it possible to isolate the effect of the parallel generation strategy without confounding factors from architecture or training recipe differences.

- **Metrics.** For image generation, the primary metric is **Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)** , computed on the full ImageNet validation set (50K generated images vs. 50K real images) using the standard Inception-v3 feature space. FID measures both fidelity and diversity—lower values indicate generated images that more closely match the real data distribution. Complementary metrics include **Inception Score (IS)** (higher is better, captures both image quality and class diversity), **Precision** (fraction of generated images that look realistic relative to the real distribution), and **Recall** (fraction of the real distribution covered by generated images). For video generation, the primary metric is **Fréchet Video Distance (FVD)** , computed using a pre-trained I3D video feature extractor on all generated videos vs. the UCF-101 test set. FVD is the standard metric for video generation quality, with lower values indicating better temporal coherence and visual fidelity. For efficiency, the paper reports both **generation steps** (number of sequential forward passes through the transformer) and **wall-clock time** (measured on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU with batch size 1). Wall-clock measurements are critical because step count reductions do not translate 1:1 to latency reductions when parallel steps involve larger output heads or different memory access patterns.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a comprehensive set of methods from different generation paradigms:
  - **GAN-based:** BigGAN (Brock et al., 2019), GigaGAN (Kang et al., 2023), StyleGAN-XL (Sauer et al., 2022) — representing one-shot generation with strong fidelity but often limited diversity.
  - **Diffusion-based:** ADM (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021), CDM (Ho et al., 2022), LDM-4 (Rombach et al., 2022), DiT-XL/2 (Peebles and Xie, 2023) — iterative denoising models that currently hold state-of-the-art on many benchmarks.
  - **Mask-based:** MaskGIT (Chang et al., 2022) — the primary non-autoregressive parallel generation baseline.
  - **Next-scale prediction:** VAR-d30 (Tian et al., 2024) — achieves parallel generation through multi-scale token prediction.
  - **Continuous mask token prediction:** MAR (Li et al., 2024) — a hybrid approach combining autoregressive and masked modeling.
  - **Autoregressive:** VQGAN (Esser et al., 2021), ViT-VQGAN (Yu et al., 2021), RQTran (Lee et al., 2022), and LlamaGen (Sun et al., 2024) at multiple scales — the direct sequential-generation baselines.
  - **For video:** VideoFusion (Luo et al., 2023), Make-A-Video (Singer et al., 2022), HPDM-L (Skorokhodov et al., 2024), MAGVIT/MAGVIT-v2 (Yu et al., 2023, 2024), CogVideo (Hong et al., 2022), TATS (Ge et al., 2022), OmniTokenizer (Wang et al., 2024).

  The most important baseline is **PAR-1×** — the paper's own implementation of standard token-by-token autoregressive generation using the same architecture and training setup as the parallel models, providing a controlled within-experiment comparison that isolates the effect of the parallel generation strategy from implementation differences.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in two complementary ways. **Generation steps** count the number of sequential forward passes through the transformer — this is the algorithm-level metric that captures the inherent sequential dependency reduction. A 576-token image generated with 4-token parallel groups requires 147 steps (4 initial sequential + 143 parallel groups); with 16-token groups, 51 steps (16 initial + 35 parallel). **Wall-clock time** is measured on a single A100 GPU with batch size 1, capturing real-world latency including memory access, attention computation, and sampling overhead. The paper explicitly notes that step reduction and latency reduction are not perfectly proportional — 4× fewer steps yields 3.6× speedup (3.46s vs. 12.41s), not exactly 4×, due to the larger output computation per parallel step. All training is done with standard teacher forcing and cross-entropy loss; there is no test-time search, revision, or iterative refinement that would add unaccounted compute.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation or statistical significance testing. Results are reported as single-run metrics on the standard ImageNet validation set (50K samples) and UCF-101 test set. This is standard practice in the visual generation literature — FID and FVD are computed on large sample sets (50K for ImageNet, all generated videos for UCF-101), making the metrics relatively stable. However, the paper provides **no confidence intervals or error bars** for any reported metrics, nor does it report multiple training runs to assess variance. This is a limitation: FID can vary non-trivially across training runs even with identical hyperparameters, and the paper's key claims about small FID differences (e.g., 2.35 vs. 2.34 for PAR-XXL-4× vs. LlamaGen-XXL) would be strengthened by demonstrating that these differences are within run-to-run variance. For video generation, where the UCF-101 test set is relatively small, variance could be more significant.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Image Generation: PAR Achieves State-of-the-Art Speed-Quality Trade-offs

**Headline result (Table 2):** PAR-3B-4× achieves an FID of 2.29 with 147 generation steps and 3.46 seconds latency, compared to the baseline LlamaGen-3B's FID of 2.18 with 576 steps and 12.41 seconds — a **3.9× step reduction** and **3.6× actual speedup** with only **0.11 FID degradation**. With more aggressive parallelization, PAR-3B-16× achieves an FID of 2.88 with 51 steps and 1.31 seconds — an **11.3× step reduction** and **9.5× speedup** with **0.70 FID degradation** relative to the baseline.

**Comparison with LlamaGen across model scales (Table 2):**

| Model Scale | LlamaGen FID | PAR-4× FID | LlamaGen Steps | PAR-4× Steps |
|-------------|-------------|------------|----------------|--------------|
| L (343M) | 3.07 | 3.76 | 576 | 147 |
| XL (775M) | 2.62 | 2.61 | 576 | 147 |
| XXL (1.4B) | 2.34 | 2.35 | 576 | 147 |
| 3B | 2.18 | 2.29 | 576 | 147 |

The pattern is clear and important: **the quality gap between PAR and sequential generation shrinks with model scale**. At 343M parameters, PAR-4× lags the sequential baseline by 0.69 FID (3.76 vs. 3.07). At 775M, PAR-4× is essentially equivalent (2.61 vs. 2.62, a 0.01 advantage for PAR). At 1.4B and 3.1B, the gap is negligible (0.01 and 0.11 FID respectively). This scaling behavior — larger models compensating for the parallel sampling approximation — is a non-obvious finding that the paper highlights in Section 4.3 (Table 4(e)) but deserves emphasis: it suggests that **parallel generation quality scales favorably with model capacity**, making the approach particularly attractive for large-scale deployment where model size provides additional benefits beyond raw quality.

**PAR-XXL-16× (Table 2):** With 16× parallelization at the 1.4B scale, FID is 3.02 — a 0.68 FID increase over the sequential baseline but achieved in only 51 steps (2.28 seconds). The IS actually improves (270.6 for PAR-16× vs. 253.9 for sequential LlamaGen-XXL), suggesting parallel generation may produce more diverse or class-distinct outputs even as fidelity slightly degrades. This IS-FID trade-off is not discussed in detail in the paper but suggests that parallel sampling may affect the diversity-fidelity balance.

**PAR-3B-16× (Table 2):** FID of 2.88, IS of 262.5, Precision of 0.82, Recall of 0.56 — all achieved in 1.31 seconds. Compared to the sequential baseline (2.18 FID, 12.41s), this represents a 9.5× speedup for 0.70 FID degradation. The Precision remains identical (0.82 vs. 0.81) while Recall drops slightly (0.56 vs. 0.58), suggesting that parallel generation preserves the realism of individual samples (Precision) while slightly reducing the diversity of the generated distribution (Recall). This is consistent with the independent sampling approximation: when tokens are sampled independently, the joint distribution concentrates more on high-probability combinations, potentially reducing mode coverage.

**Comparison with non-autoregressive methods (Table 2):**

The paper positions PAR against several categories of methods:

- **Vs. GANs:** PAR-3B-4× (FID 2.29) approaches StyleGAN-XL (FID 2.30) — essentially matching the best GAN on this benchmark — while retaining the autoregressive framework's advantages for multimodal unification. BigGAN (FID 6.95) and GigaGAN (FID 3.45) are significantly worse. The key advantage over GANs is not quality but the autoregressive property: GANs are specialized for image generation and cannot trivially extend to video or multimodal generation within a unified architecture.

- **Vs. Diffusion models:** PAR-3B-4× (FID 2.29) is competitive with DiT-XL/2 (FID 2.27) and LDM-4 (FID 3.60), while achieving substantially faster inference: 3.46 seconds (3.1B parameters) vs. 11.97 seconds for DiT-XL/2 (675M parameters). However, this comparison is somewhat unfair to diffusion models because the paper reports DiT's 250-step generation; recent distillation methods (consistency models, LCMs) can reduce diffusion steps to 4–8 while maintaining quality, which would close the latency gap. The paper does not compare against distilled diffusion models, which would provide a more current baseline.

- **Vs. MaskGIT (the primary non-autoregressive parallel baseline):** PAR-3B-4× (FID 2.29, 147 steps) substantially outperforms MaskGIT (FID 6.18, 8 steps) in quality while requiring more steps. MaskGIT's 8-step generation is much faster (0.13 seconds reported in Table 2), but the 4.11 FID gap is substantial. This comparison highlights the speed-quality trade-off: MaskGIT represents the extreme speed end (few steps, lower quality), while PAR-4× represents a more balanced point (moderate steps, high quality). PAR-16× (FID 2.88, 51 steps, 1.31s) begins to close the step-count gap with MaskGIT while maintaining dramatically better quality.

- **Vs. VAR (next-scale prediction):** VAR-d30 (FID 1.97) achieves better FID than PAR-3B-4× (2.29) with even fewer steps (10 vs. 147). However, the paper argues two points: (1) VAR requires a specialized multi-scale tokenizer and generates longer total token sequences (since each scale adds tokens), which is a different modeling paradigm rather than a pure efficiency improvement; (2) VAR's 10-step generation is algorithmic — the paper does not report VAR's wall-clock time at comparable scales, making latency comparison difficult. The 2B-parameter VAR model's 0.27-second time in Table 2 is impressive, but model scale, implementation, and hardware differences are not controlled.

- **Vs. MAR (continuous mask token prediction):** MAR (FID 1.55, 943M parameters, 64 steps, 28.24 seconds) achieves the best FID in the table but requires substantially more time than PAR. This is a continuous (non-discrete) autoregressive approach that is less directly comparable to discrete-token PAR.

**The speed-quality trade-off is the key contribution, not absolute FID superiority.** PAR does not claim to beat the best methods on pure quality — VAR and MAR achieve better FID scores. The claim is about efficiency within the standard autoregressive framework: **among methods that preserve the standard autoregressive transformer architecture and discrete tokenization, PAR achieves dramatic speedup with minimal quality loss.** Table 2 supports this claim: PAR-3B-4× (FID 2.29) is comparable to the best autoregressive baselines (LlamaGen-3B FID 2.18, ViT-VQGAN-re FID 3.04) while being 3.6× faster, and no other standard autoregressive method in the table achieves comparable speed.

#### Video Generation: Spatial Parallelization Generalizes to Video with Strong Results

**Headline result (Table 3):** On UCF-101 class-conditional video generation, PAR-4× with a 792M-parameter model achieves **FVD of 99.5** with **323 generation steps** and **11.27 seconds** latency, compared to the PAR-1× sequential baseline at FVD 94.1 with 1280 steps and 43.30 seconds — a **4.0× step reduction** and **3.8× speedup** for a **5.4 FVD increase**. PAR-16× achieves FVD 103.4 with only 95 steps and 3.44 seconds — a **13.5× step reduction** and **12.6× speedup** for a **9.3 FVD increase** over the sequential baseline.

**Comparison with prior work (Table 3):**

The video generation results warrant careful interpretation because the paper's MAGVIT-v2 tokenizer implementation differs from the original. The authors note that "Our reproduced MAGVIT-v2 tokenizer uses a 64K visual vocabulary instead of the original 262K to facilitate model training" and achieve a reconstruction FVD of 32 on UCF-101. This means the absolute FVD values are not directly comparable to the original MAGVIT-v2 results (FVD 58, 840M parameters) because the tokenizer quality differs. The comparison that matters is the within-experiment comparison: PAR-1×, PAR-4×, and PAR-16× all use the same tokenizer and architecture, isolating the effect of parallel generation.

The sequential baseline (PAR-1×, FVD 94.1) is already competitive with the original MAGVIT-v2 autoregressive variant (MAGVIT-v2-AR, FVD 109), suggesting the implementation is solid despite the smaller codebook. The PAR-4× result (FVD 99.5) represents a modest quality degradation (5.4 FVD) for a 3.8× speedup. The PAR-16× result (FVD 103.4) shows that aggressive parallelization degrades video quality more noticeably than image quality — the 9.3 FVD increase for 12.6× speedup is proportionally larger than the 0.70 FID increase for 9.5× speedup in images. This is consistent with the paper's observation that temporal dependencies are stronger and less amenable to spatial parallelization: when spatial tokens are generated in parallel, the temporal coherence across frames relies entirely on the sequential temporal conditioning, and any spatial inconsistency within a frame can propagate temporally.

**Important comparisons in Table 3:**
- **Vs. OmniTokenizer:** The OmniTokenizer autoregressive baseline requires 5,120 steps and 336.70 seconds for comparable FVD (191), making PAR's efficiency gains dramatic in absolute terms.
- **Vs. MAGVIT-v2 (masked):** MAGVIT-v2 achieves FVD 58 with masked token prediction — substantially better than any PAR variant. This represents the quality ceiling that masked modeling can achieve given direct optimization for this metric, but it comes at the cost of a different modeling paradigm that doesn't transfer to autoregressive multimodal unification.
- **Vs. diffusion models:** Make-A-Video (FVD 81.3) and HPDM-L (FVD 66.3) achieve better FVD than PAR variants, but the paper doesn't report their inference times, making the speed-quality comparison incomplete.

**The video results demonstrate domain generalization** — the spatial parallelization strategy works for both VQGAN (images) and MAGVIT-v2 (videos) tokenizers, and across different spatial resolutions (24×24 for images, 16×16 per frame for video) and temporal structures (single-frame vs. 5-frame latent sequences). This is a non-trivial validation of the paper's core claim that spatial dependency decay is a general property of visual data, not an artifact of a specific tokenizer or resolution.

#### Engineering Optimization Compatibility: Algorithmic and Engineering Speedups Are Multiplicative

**Headline result (Table 7, Appendix B):** PAR-3B-4× with simple PyTorch compilation achieves **1.15 seconds** per 384×384 image, compared to LlamaGen-3B with full vLLM optimization (page attention + CUDA graphs) at **4.12 seconds** — a **3.6× speedup** for the algorithmic approach alone, even before applying equivalent engineering optimizations. PAR-3B-16× with compilation reaches **0.43 seconds**.

**Key comparisons in Table 7:**

| Method | Optimization | Latency |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| LlamaGen-3B | none | 12.41s |
| LlamaGen-3B | vLLM (full) | 4.12s |
| PAR-3B-4× | none | 3.46s |
| PAR-3B-4× | compile (CUDA graphs) | 1.15s |
| PAR-3B-16× | compile | 0.43s |

The critical insight is that PAR **without any engineering optimization** (3.46s) already outperforms the **engineering-optimized** sequential baseline (4.12s). This means the algorithmic step reduction provides benefits that exceed what can be achieved through the best available engineering optimizations on the sequential model. When engineering optimizations are applied to PAR, the benefits compound: PAR-3B-4× goes from 3.46s to 1.15s (3.0× improvement from compilation), and PAR-3B-16× reaches 0.43s.

**Why this decomposition matters:** The paper is careful to note that these optimizations are "orthogonal" — algorithmic speedup reduces the number of steps, engineering speedup reduces the cost per step. The total speedup is approximately the product: 3.9× algorithmic × 3.0× engineering ≈ 11.7× total for PAR-4× vs. unoptimized LlamaGen. This is the basis for the paper's claim that PAR "achieves a 3.6× speedup with comparable quality and up to 9.5× speedup with minimal quality degradation" — the 3.6× refers to algorithmic speedup (PAR-3B-4× vs. LlamaGen-3B, both without optimization), while the 9.5× (12.41s / 1.31s) includes both algorithmic (11.3× step reduction) and the slightly faster per-step time of the parallel model.

However, the 9.5× speedup number in the abstract requires a careful reading: it compares PAR-3B-16× without any engineering optimization (1.31s) against the unoptimized LlamaGen-3B (12.41s). This is a fair algorithmic comparison, but it's not the best possible speed for PAR — with compilation, PAR-3B-16× achieves 0.43s, a 28.9× speedup over unoptimized LlamaGen. The paper reports the more conservative 9.5× figure (without compilation) because the LlamaGen baseline also doesn't use compilation; the 3.6× speedup for PAR-4× is also an unoptimized-to-unoptimized comparison.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablations in Table 4 use the PAR-XL model (775M parameters) with the default 4× parallelization (n=4) on the ImageNet 256×256 validation set, unless otherwise noted.

**Initial sequential token generation (Table 4a):** Removing the Stage 1 sequential generation of initial region tokens — generating them in parallel instead — causes FID to degrade from **2.61 to 3.67** (a 1.06 increase) while saving only 3 steps (147 vs. 144). This is the single most impactful ablation in the paper: 4 tokens (less than 3% of total steps) account for a 1.06 FID difference. The IS drops from 259.17 to 221.36, consistent with the qualitative observation in Figure 5 (middle row) that parallel initial tokens produce "misaligned dogs with duplicated body parts" — the IS degradation suggests that class-conditional structure collapses when global coordination fails. This ablation establishes that **not all spatially distant tokens are parallelizable** — the initial tokens of each region carry global coordination requirements that sequential generation satisfies.

**Number of parallel predicted tokens (Table 4b):** Using the PAR-XXL model (1.4B parameters), increasing the parallel group size n from 1 (sequential, 576 steps, FID 2.34) to 4 (147 steps, FID 2.35) to 16 (51 steps, FID 3.02) shows a **monotonic quality-speed trade-off**. The jump from n=1 to n=4 is nearly quality-neutral (+0.01 FID for 3.9× speedup), while the jump to n=16 incurs a 0.67 FID penalty for an additional 2.9× speedup over n=4. The IS interestingly increases with parallelization (253.90 → 263.24 → 270.57), a pattern the paper does not discuss but that appears robust. This could indicate that parallel sampling improves class-distinctiveness (higher IS) at the cost of per-sample fidelity (higher FID), or it could be an artifact of the IS metric's sensitivity to mode dropping — if parallel generation concentrates probability mass on prototypical class examples, IS could increase even as overall distribution matching (FID) degrades.

**This result also validates the spatial-distance dependency hypothesis:** n=4 corresponds to M=2 regions (region size 12×12 for a 24×24 grid), where cross-region tokens are separated by at least 12 grid positions. n=16 corresponds to M=4 regions (region size 6×6), where tokens are separated by at least 6 grid positions. The larger quality degradation at n=16 is consistent with the expectation that tokens from smaller, less-distant regions have stronger residual dependencies, making the independent sampling approximation less accurate.

**Attention pattern between parallel tokens (Table 4c):** Comparing causal attention within parallel groups (each token sees only same-group tokens at earlier sequence positions) against full bi-directional attention within groups (every token sees every other token in the same group) yields a dramatic difference: **FID 3.64 with causal vs. 2.61 with full attention** (1.03 FID improvement), with identical step count (147). This ablation validates the paper's design decision to use group-wise bi-directional attention. The causal masking variant creates an information asymmetry: the first token in a parallel group sees no other same-group tokens, the second sees only the first, etc., even though all group tokens are generated simultaneously from the same context. The bi-directional variant gives all group tokens equal access to the previous group's full context, which is both more symmetric and more information-rich. The 1.03 FID gap is large enough to suggest that future work on parallel autoregressive generation should treat within-group attention as a first-class design choice, not a minor implementation detail.

**Token ordering and prediction pattern (Table 4d):** This is the most theoretically significant ablation. Comparing raster scan ordering vs. the proposed distant ordering under two conditions — single-token prediction (sequential) and multi-token prediction (parallel, n=4) — reveals:

- **Single-token prediction:** Both orderings perform similarly (FID 2.62 raster vs. 2.64 distant), with comparable IS. This establishes that the distant ordering does not inherently degrade the autoregressive factorization — when tokens are generated one at a time, the model can learn from any ordering with similar quality.

- **Multi-token prediction (parallel, n=4):** The raster scan ordering catastrophically degrades to **FID 5.64** (IS 265.46), while the distant ordering maintains **FID 2.61** (IS 259.17). The FID gap of 3.03 between the two orderings under parallel prediction is the paper's central empirical finding — it demonstrates that **the choice of which tokens to parallelize matters dramatically more than the choice of whether to parallelize**. The distant ordering groups tokens from different spatial regions (weak dependencies), while the raster ordering groups adjacent tokens (strong dependencies). The IS is actually higher for the failing raster-scan variant (265.46 vs. 259.17), which may indicate that the distorted outputs (Figure 5, bottom row) produce individually plausible but globally inconsistent images that happen to trigger high Inception Score through class-typical feature activation, even as the overall distribution (FID) is poor.

This ablation directly validates the paper's core thesis: **parallel generation fails when parallelized tokens have strong mutual dependencies**. The raster scan multi-token FID of 5.64 is worse than many single-token baselines from years earlier (e.g., VQGAN at 1.4B achieves FID 5.20 with rejection sampling), confirming that naive multi-token prediction is not merely suboptimal but fundamentally broken for visual data.

**Model scaling analysis (Table 4e):** With fixed 4× parallelization, increasing model size from 343M to 3.1B parameters improves FID from **3.76 to 2.29**, with corresponding IS improvement from 218.92 to 255.46. The step count remains constant at 147. This ablation serves dual purposes: (1) it confirms that PAR scales well with model size — larger models consistently produce better generation quality under the same parallelization strategy; (2) when compared against the sequential baselines in Table 2, it shows that the **quality gap between PAR and sequential generation shrinks with model scale** (0.69 FID gap at 343M, −0.01 at 775M, 0.01 at 1.4B, 0.11 at 3.1B). This scaling behavior is encouraging for practical deployment: the largest models, where inference latency is most painful, also benefit most from parallelization with the least quality penalty.

**Transition token design (implied ablation):** The paper does not ablate the learnable transition tokens `[M1, M2, M3]` described in Section 3.3 (Figure 4). While the text states these tokens "help the model enter parallel prediction mode" and "absorb the offset change," no experiment compares models with and without these tokens. This is a notable missing ablation — the transition tokens are the only architectural addition in the entire framework, and the paper provides no evidence that they are necessary or beneficial. Without this ablation, a reader cannot determine whether the transition tokens are essential for the 2.61 FID result or whether a simple zero-padding or no-transition design would work equally well.

**Video-specific ablations:** The paper does not report ablations on video generation — no comparison of different region partition sizes, attention patterns, or temporal-parallelization attempts (only mentioned as "explored" but "found less effective"). This limits the strength of the video results: while Table 3 shows PAR works for video, it does not provide evidence about which design choices are critical for video vs. image domains, or whether further optimization could close the FVD gap with the sequential baseline (94.1 vs. 99.5 for 4×).

**Long-range dependency handling:** The paper qualitatively demonstrates that symmetric features (deer antlers, vehicle wheels in Figure 7) are preserved under parallel generation, but provides no quantitative metric for long-range consistency. This is inherently difficult to measure — standard metrics like FID/IS don't isolate long-range dependencies from other quality factors — but the lack of quantitative evaluation means the paper's claim that PAR "successfully maintains consistency between distant but strongly related elements" remains at the level of anecdotal evidence from selected examples.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim from the executive summary: "PAR achieves a 3.6× speedup with comparable FID (2.29 vs. 2.18) and up to 9.5× speedup with only 0.7 FID degradation."**

The experiments support this claim with important qualifiers. The 3.6× speedup comparison is clean: PAR-3B-4× (147 steps, 3.46s, FID 2.29) vs. LlamaGen-3B (576 steps, 12.41s, FID 2.18), both on the same A100 GPU with batch size 1, both without engineering optimizations (Table 2). The speedup is measured as 12.41 / 3.46 = 3.59, matching the claimed 3.6×. The "comparable quality" characterization for a 0.11 FID difference is reasonable by the standards of the visual generation literature — differences under ~0.1–0.2 FID are often within run-to-run variance for these model scales.

However, the 9.5× speedup claim requires more careful parsing. The paper reports PAR-3B-16× achieves 1.31s (Table 2) vs. LlamaGen-3B's 12.41s: 12.41 / 1.31 = 9.47, matching the claimed 9.5×. The "only 0.7 FID degradation" compares PAR-3B-16× (FID 2.88) to LlamaGen-3B (FID 2.18) — a difference of 0.70. This is a non-trivial degradation: 2.88 FID places PAR-3B-16× below several baselines in Table 2 (LlamaGen-L at 343M achieves 3.07 FID; VQGAN-re at 1.4B achieves 5.20, which PAR-3B-16× substantially outperforms). The paper's characterization of 0.70 FID as "minimal degradation" is somewhat generous — in absolute terms, it's a 32% increase in FID — but in the context of the 9.5× speedup, practitioners may find this an acceptable trade-off. The paper could strengthen this claim by reporting user studies or downstream task evaluations to establish what FID differences are perceptually meaningful at this quality level.

**Missing: comparison with PAR-1× at 3.1B as the sequential baseline for the 9.5× claim.** The sequential baseline is LlamaGen-3B, not PAR-1×-3B. While the architectures are based on the same LlamaGen design, there may be implementation differences (training recipe, hyperparameters, tokenizer version) that affect the FID comparison. The paper trains PAR models from scratch rather than adapting pretrained LlamaGen checkpoints, so architectural and training differences could contribute to the FID gap beyond the parallel generation strategy itself. The paper partially addresses this by showing PAR-1× results for video (Table 3), where PAR-1× achieves FVD 94.1 — establishing a within-implementation baseline. For images, the paper does not report PAR-1× FID scores; the comparison is always against LlamaGen from prior work. Training a PAR-1× image model (same architecture, training recipe, but sequential generation) would provide a more controlled baseline.

**The speed-quality Pareto frontier is more informative than point estimates.** The paper's results define a frontier: at the high-quality end, PAR-4× at 3.1B (FID 2.29, 3.46s) is nearly indistinguishable from sequential (FID 2.18, 12.41s). At the high-speed end, PAR-16× at 3.1B (FID 2.88, 1.31s) offers dramatically faster generation with moderate quality reduction. Intermediate points (PAR-4× at smaller scales, PAR-16× at 1.4B) fill in the frontier. The paper would benefit from explicitly plotting this frontier (FID vs. latency or FID vs. steps) to help practitioners select operating points, rather than reporting only the extremes in the abstract.

**Claim: "The key insight is that token dependencies are correlated with spatial distance—adjacent tokens exhibit strong dependencies that prevent independent sampling, while spatially distant tokens have weak dependencies and can be generated simultaneously."**

This claim is well-supported by both empirical and quantitative evidence. The empirical evidence comes from Table 4(d): raster scan multi-token prediction (adjacent tokens parallelized) yields FID 5.64, while cross-region ordering (distant tokens parallelized) yields FID 2.61 — a 3.03 FID gap that directly validates the dependency-distance correlation. The qualitative evidence in Figure 5 (bottom vs. top row) makes the failure mode vivid: adjacent-token parallelization produces "distorted local patterns and broken details" while cross-region parallelization preserves coherent structures.

The quantitative evidence from Appendix D provides mechanistic validation. Figure 11 shows conditional entropy decreasing with spatial proximity across ImageNet data. Figure 12 shows the entropy increase from parallel generation is substantially smaller for the cross-region ordering than for raster scan ordering. Together, these analyses establish not just that the method works, but **why** it works: the cross-region ordering groups tokens with lower mutual information, making the independent sampling approximation more accurate.

**Limitations of the dependency analysis:**
- The entropy estimation uses continuous features from the VQGAN codebook rather than the discrete token distributions, which is a simplification. The conditional entropy of discrete tokens given discrete context is the quantity that matters for parallel sampling, and the continuous approximation may not perfectly capture discretization effects.
- The analysis is conducted on 10,000 images — sufficient for stable estimates but not enough to assess how dependency patterns vary across ImageNet categories (indoor scenes vs. natural images vs. object-centric images may have different spatial dependency structures).
- The entropy analysis provides relative comparisons (which ordering is better) but not absolute predictions (what FID to expect). The paper does not attempt to predict FID from entropy estimates, which would be a stronger validation.

**Claim: "The initial tokens in each local region play a crucial role in establishing the global structure—generating them in parallel could lead to conflicting structures across regions."**

The ablation in Table 4(a) directly supports this: removing sequential initial token generation causes FID to increase from 2.61 to 3.67 — a 1.06 degradation for a change affecting only 4 out of 147 steps (< 3% of steps). Figure 5 (middle row) shows the qualitative consequence: "dogs with duplicated body parts" and "inconsistent global structures."

However, the paper does not explore **how many** initial tokens are necessary. The ablation only tests all-or-nothing: either generate exactly one token per region sequentially, or generate zero sequentially. Would generating two initial tokens per region (8 tokens sequentially) provide better global structure? Would generating only a subset of regions' initial tokens sequentially (e.g., only the four corner regions) suffice? The paper's design choice of exactly one initial token per region is motivated by the region partition (M × M regions → M² initial tokens) but is not empirically validated against alternative choices. This matters for the 16× parallelization case, where 16 initial tokens represent a larger fraction of total steps (16/51 = 31%) and the sequential overhead is more significant.

**Claim: "PAR achieves 9.5× speedup… across both image and video generation tasks."**

The image generation claim is supported (Table 2). The video generation claim requires separate evaluation. Table 3 shows PAR-16× achieves 12.6× speedup for video (43.30s / 3.44s = 12.6), exceeding the abstract's claim of 9.5× — but the abstract's "up to 9.5×" figure appears to reference image generation specifically. The video quality at 16× parallelization (FVD 103.4) shows an increase of 9.3 FVD over the sequential baseline (94.1). Whether this is "minimal quality degradation" is debatable — FVD differences are less well-calibrated for perceptual significance than FID differences, and the paper provides no user study or visual examples (in the main paper) to contextualize the 103.4 vs. 94.1 difference.

**Weakness: Single-dataset evaluation per domain.** All image experiments are on ImageNet 256×256; all video experiments are on UCF-101 128×128 at 17 frames. These are standard benchmarks, but they represent narrow distributions: ImageNet is object-centric with relatively simple compositions; UCF-101 consists of short action clips with limited visual diversity. The paper does not evaluate on more complex or diverse datasets (e.g., COCO for text-to-image, MSR-VTT or WebVid for text-to-video, higher resolutions, longer videos). The dependency structure that PAR exploits — spatial decay of token correlations — may differ for images with complex global layouts (e.g., urban scenes with strong long-range geometric dependencies) or for longer videos where temporal dependencies dominate spatial ones.

**Weakness: No comparison against consistency-decoded diffusion models.** The paper compares against 250-step DiT and LDM baselines, but the state-of-the-art in diffusion inference speed has advanced rapidly. Consistency models, LCM-LoRA, and SDXL-Turbo can generate high-quality images in 2–8 steps. At the time of writing, a comparison against 4-step diffusion models would be more informative for practitioners than the 250-step baselines the paper uses. The paper's claim that PAR "makes autoregressive visual generation more practically usable for real-world applications" is weakened by the absence of this comparison.

**Weakness: Limited exploration of failure cases.** The paper shows qualitative examples of successful generation (Figures 2, 5 top row, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10) and includes failure modes from ablated variants (Figure 5 middle and bottom rows, Figure 1b), but does not systematically characterize when PAR-4× or PAR-16× **fails** in production. Are there ImageNet classes where parallel generation consistently underperforms sequential? Are there visual patterns (text, fine geometric structures, high-frequency textures) that are particularly vulnerable to the independent sampling approximation? The FID metric aggregates across all classes and doesn't reveal class-conditional failure modes. A per-class FID breakdown or qualitative failure analysis on the hardest classes would help practitioners assess deployment risk.

**Missing baseline: PAR with learnable generation order.** The paper fixes the cross-region grouping as a pre-determined function of spatial position. A natural comparison would be to learn the grouping from data — train a routing network that assigns tokens to parallel groups based on their features rather than their spatial coordinates. This would test whether spatial distance is the optimal grouping criterion or merely a convenient proxy for token dependency. The paper's entropy analysis suggests spatial distance is a good proxy, but a learned ordering might capture non-spatial dependencies (e.g., semantic similarity between tokens in different regions) that spatial distance misses.

**Missing ablation: Effect of 2D RoPE vs. alternative position encodings.** The paper uses 2D Rotary Position Embedding to preserve spatial information under the reordered sequence, but does not ablate this choice. Would learned position embeddings work? Would 1D RoPE on the original grid coordinates (without the reordering) suffice? The paper argues 2D RoPE is necessary because "1D RoPE applied to the reordered sequence would encode the distance in the reordered sequence, which is meaningless," but provides no experimental evidence for this claim within the PAR framework.

**Missing quantitative evaluation of long-range consistency claim.** Figure 7 shows examples of symmetric features preserved under parallel generation, but this is qualitative cherry-picking. The paper could quantify long-range consistency through metrics like: (1) mutual information between tokens at distance d in generated vs. real images, (2) detection rate of physically implausible configurations (e.g., animals with >4 legs), or (3) user studies on global coherence. Without such metrics, the claim that PAR "successfully maintains consistency between distant but strongly related elements" is anecdotal.

**The video extension is promising but underdeveloped.** The paper demonstrates that spatial parallelization works for video (Table 3) but doesn't explore temporal parallelization strategies beyond noting they were "found less effective." This misses an opportunity: for video, the temporal dimension offers additional parallelism potential that could compound with spatial parallelization. Even if naive temporal parallelization fails for the reasons the paper states (strong sequential dependencies), more sophisticated approaches — such as parallelizing only temporally distant frames or using motion-compensated temporal groupings — could yield further speedup. The paper's investigation of video parallelization is at the level of a proof-of-concept rather than a systematic study.

**Summary assessment:** The experimental evidence strongly supports the paper's central technical claim: that cross-region parallel generation based on spatial dependency decay enables substantial speedup (3.6–9.5×) with manageable quality trade-offs in standard autoregressive visual models. The ablations (Table 4) are well-designed and directly validate the three design principles from Section 3.1. The main weaknesses are: (1) limited dataset diversity (single image and video benchmark each), (2) absence of comparisons against recent accelerated diffusion methods, (3) missing ablations on transition tokens, initial-token count, and position encoding choices, (4) no quantitative evaluation of long-range consistency, and (5) thin video experiments that demonstrate feasibility but not systematic optimization. For a paper whose primary contribution is demonstrating that a specific token grouping strategy enables parallel generation, the experiments provide sufficient evidence for the core claim while leaving substantial room for follow-up work on optimization, generalization, and failure characterization.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in the Headline Speedup Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's compute-optimal policy depends on estimating the difficulty of each prompt before allocating the test-time compute budget. The method for doing so—whether using oracle pass@1 (which requires ground-truth answers) or PRM-based predicted difficulty—requires generating 2,048 samples per question and scoring them (Section 3.2). The authors explicitly flag this computational overhead:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N (Figures 4 and 8) are computed **after** difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In realistic deployment, the total cost would be:

$$\text{Total cost} = \text{difficulty estimation cost} + \text{strategy execution cost}$$

The difficulty estimation step—generating 2,048 samples per prompt—consumes **more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied** (256–512 generations). For a prompt that would receive a budget of 64 generations under the compute-optimal policy, the difficulty estimation alone costs 32× more than the actual solution attempt. This makes the 4× figure an **upper bound on achievable efficiency**, not a realized deployment gain, and means that for any practical application with modest per-prompt budgets, the total compute could be **worse** than uniform best-of-N.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.2 acknowledges the cost explicitly but does not include it in any budget calculation, figure, or table. The curves in Figures 4 and 8 show "compute-optimal" performance as a function of the **strategy execution budget only**, with difficulty treated as an input rather than a cost. No experiment measures end-to-end latency including difficulty estimation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper suggests future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8) but develops no such model and evaluates no lightweight difficulty estimator. A partial mitigation would be adaptive estimation—using a small number of initial samples to estimate difficulty, then allocating the remaining budget accordingly—but this is not explored. The limitation remains entirely unresolved in the current work.

### Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved Regardless of Budget Allocation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire framework assumes that the base model produces correct solutions at some non-trivial rate for the prompts being optimized. For difficulty bin 5 (the hardest quintile, where the base model's pass@1 is near zero), the assumption fails.

**The consequence.** Across all methods—search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations—performance on bin 5 is essentially flat near 0–5% regardless of budget (Figure 3 right, Figure 7 right, Figure 9 bottom curves). The paper states this explicitly in the Section 7 takeaway:

> "On hard problems (bins 4–5), pretraining is almost always more effective. Test-time compute provides minimal gains on problems that are fundamentally outside the base model's capability range."

This establishes a hard boundary condition: **test-time compute can amplify existing capability but cannot create it**. If the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class, no amount of search or revision will help—there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find or refine. For deployment scenarios where the problem distribution includes a long tail of genuinely difficult queries (which is typical of real-world use), the method offers no path forward—those queries will still fail, consuming whatever budget is allocated to them.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (right, bin 5) shows accuracy hovering at 1–3% for all methods at all budgets from 4 to 256 generations. Figure 7 (right, bin 5) shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of sequential-to-parallel ratio at a fixed 128-generation budget. The FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9) shows the bin 5 scaling line essentially flat near 0–5%, well below the 14× larger model's performance at all R values. The paper is transparent about this finding.

**Mitigation status.** Not mitigated—and likely cannot be mitigated within the test-time compute paradigm. The paper correctly identifies that for these problems, pretraining is the only viable path. This limitation is fundamental to the approach rather than a fixable weakness, but it bounds the method's applicability and should inform deployment decisions: if a significant fraction of the target distribution consists of problems outside the base model's reach, test-time compute allocation alone is insufficient.

### The Revision Model Has a Substantial Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). It never sees examples of what to do when the current answer is already correct. At inference time, when the model produces a correct answer during a revision chain, it may encounter this correct answer in its own context on the next revision step—a situation it was never trained to handle.

**The consequence.** The paper reports that approximately **38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones** during revision chains (Section 6.1). This means the revision model actively corrupts its own correct outputs—it does not merely fail to improve, but regresses. The mitigation employed—selecting the best answer from any point in the chain via majority voting or verifier-based selection rather than taking the final revision—is a patch that works around the problem without solving it. In practice, this means:

- The revision chain cannot be trusted to converge; later revisions may be worse than earlier ones.
- The within-chain selection mechanism adds overhead and introduces its own failure modes (the selector may pick a wrong answer that happens to appear multiple times).
- The effective length of useful revision chains is limited by the probability of reversion.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% reversion rate is stated in Section 6.1 (no table or figure is cited for this specific number, but it is discussed in the text). Figure 6 (left) shows that pass@1 plateaus around steps 15–20 and stays in the 23–25% range out to 64 steps—the model does not monotonically improve with more revisions, consistent with the reversion problem. The ReST^EM experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) shows that an RL-optimized revision model degrades substantially with sequential revisions, suggesting the problem may be exacerbated by certain training procedures.

**Mitigation status.** Partially mitigated through within-chain selection (majority voting or verifier-based) across the revision chain's history, but this is an engineering patch rather than a solution. A principled fix—training the model to recognize when no revision is needed, or including "no-change" trajectories in the training data—is not explored. The paper acknowledges the issue in Section 6.1 but does not propose a solution beyond the selection mechanism.

### Single Benchmark, Single Model Family Limits Claims of Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this is an untested assumption.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific:

- **PRM quality and over-optimization behavior** depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution, calibration, and error patterns. A different model family with different reasoning characteristics might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves, different optimal search algorithms, or different over-optimization thresholds.
- **The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples** depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families. The edit-distance-based pairing strategy may work differently for models with different output distributions.
- **The MATH benchmark** consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring symbolic reasoning and multi-step deduction. Whether the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems) generalize to other reasoning domains—code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA—or to tasks requiring factual knowledge rather than inference is entirely unknown.
- **The test set of 500 questions**, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each and further split by two-fold cross-validation, means the compute-optimal policy is **selected based on approximately 50 questions per fold per bin**. This is a small sample for strategy selection, and the selected strategies may not be robust. The paper reports no confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges the single-benchmark limitation implicitly by making no claims beyond MATH and PaLM 2-S*. There are no out-of-distribution evaluations, no cross-model experiments, and no non-math reasoning datasets. The two-fold cross-validation design is described in Section 3.2, but no analysis of variance or confidence intervals is provided for strategy selection.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's scope is explicitly limited to MATH with PaLM 2-S*, and no generalization experiments are attempted. The authors do not suggest that the specific strategies or thresholds would transfer to other domains without re-evaluation. This is a standard limitation of a single-benchmark study, but it means practitioners cannot assume the 4× efficiency gains would hold for their specific model-dataset combination without conducting their own analysis.

---

### The Search and Revision Mechanisms Are Studied Independently, Not Combined

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary axes—PRM-guided search and iterative revisions—as separate mechanisms. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

**The consequence.** The two mechanisms have complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths: revisions improve the proposal distribution (generating better candidates, effective on easy problems), while PRM search improves candidate selection (finding the best among generated candidates, effective on medium problems). The paper's results therefore represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. Specifically:

- Beam search using the revision model as the proposal distribution (instead of the base model) could combine the benefits of both—better candidates being searched more intelligently.
- The PRM could guide which revisions to pursue, deciding when a revision chain is on track versus when to restart from scratch with a new parallel sample.
- The compute-optimal allocation could select not just between search and revisions, but also hybrid strategies that use both for a single prompt.

Without these combinations, the paper's separate analyses leave open the question of whether the two mechanisms' benefits are additive, sub-additive (due to overlapping improvements), or super-additive (due to complementary strengths). The 4× efficiency gains are computed for each mechanism independently; a combined system might achieve greater efficiency or might saturate.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The separate analyses in Sections 5 and 6 demonstrate that each mechanism works independently and has difficulty-dependent behavior. The compute-optimal policies in Figures 4 and 8 select among strategies within each mechanism but never across mechanisms. Section 8 explicitly flags the combination as future work.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed in the current paper. The authors identify this as a natural next step in Section 8 but conduct no experiments on combined strategies. This is a limitation of scope rather than a flaw—the paper's contribution is the systematic analysis of each mechanism and the compute-optimal framework, not a maximally optimized system—but it means the reported results understate what the framework could achieve.

---

### Sequential Revisions Introduce Latency That Parallel Sampling Avoids

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures compute in "generations" (number of complete solutions sampled), which treats sequential and parallel computation as equivalent costs. However, sequential revisions are inherently serial—each revision depends on the previous one—while parallel best-of-N can be executed simultaneously with sufficient hardware.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy favors sequential revisions on easy problems and balanced sequential-parallel ratios on medium problems (Figures 7 and 8). For a fixed budget of N generations, a strategy that allocates a large fraction to sequential revisions (e.g., 64 sequential revisions with 4 parallel chains) requires approximately **64× longer wall-clock time** than one that runs 256 parallel samples simultaneously, even though both use the same total generation budget. For latency-sensitive applications—interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, online services with strict SLOs—the sequential-heavy strategies favored by the compute-optimal policy may be impractical regardless of their accuracy advantages.

The paper's generation-budget accounting is appropriate for measuring total FLOPs or throughput (batch processing where multiple prompts can be processed in parallel to hide latency), but it obscures the **latency vs. throughput trade-off** that matters for interactive deployment. A strategy that is "4× more efficient" in terms of total compute may be 10× slower in wall-clock time if it relies heavily on sequential revisions.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 5 (right panel) shows the sequential-vs-parallel trade-off, and Figure 7 shows the optimal ratios per difficulty bin. These analyses use generation count as the budget metric. The paper never reports wall-clock latency for revision chains, never discusses the latency implications of sequential revisions, and never analyzes the latency-throughput trade-off. The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 accounts for total FLOPs but not for serial dependency.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper explicitly uses "generations" as the compute unit throughout (Section 4) and does not discuss latency implications. This is a standard simplification in the test-time compute literature, but it matters for practitioners evaluating deployment feasibility. A partial mitigation would be to report both total FLOPs and wall-clock latency (as the paper does for the FLOPs-matched comparison) and to analyze how the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio changes when latency is included in the objective.
