Here are the takeaways from this Michael Bolin interview (tech lead for the open-source Codex, former Distinguished Engineer at Meta, who went from rewriting Android build systems to leading the terminal agent platform that's seeing hockey-stick growth at OpenAI):

**Chickenfoot and early interests**

His master's thesis project was a Firefox extension called **Chickenfoot**, which now seems remarkably prescient. It was an end-user programming tool for the web—a little REPL sidebar in Firefox where you could write commands like `enter "first name"` and it would find the nearest text box using heuristics built on accessibility tags, alt text, and proximity. Friends used it to automate real money-making tasks on Craigslist. He reflects that "a lot of what these agents are doing right now is similar, but now actually truly in natural language, not in this little JavaScript replacement."

**Working at Google**

He joined Google in the mid-2000s, drawn by the quality of engineers ("oh, man, they're taking really good people") and the company's web-forward mindset at a time when Microsoft had just killed the Internet Explorer project. He worked on Google Calendar and then Google Tasks, but noticed a pattern that would later crystallize into a career framework: Google's culture had a strong bias toward its founding strengths. "Whatever they're good at first, the founders always have a soft spot for"—search and infrastructure got the cachet, while consumer products like Calendar were "probably a call center" in terms of revenue importance, even though they reached massive audiences. He also poured enormous effort into Google's Closure JavaScript tools and even wrote a book on them, but career-wise, he was "doing all this high quality stuff and you see other people getting recognized." His lesson, later formalized into a three-step plan, is: work on things you love *and* that your employer considers invaluable. The mismatch led him to leave Google after four years.

**Overhauling Facebook's build system**

He joined Facebook around the time of the HTC phone partnership—Facebook was forking Android and shipping a native app on a hard hardware deadline. A colleague gave him advice that changed his trajectory: "I know you really like JavaScript, but like, you should really pick Java or Objective C and like get good at this or you want to be a product person." He chose the Android team and immediately ran into a build system so slow it made development excruciating. The inherited codebase used Ant with XML build scripts so convoluted that nobody wanted to modularize anything—adding a module required ~200 lines of opaque XML. The build system would "just start over from scratch" on any change.

He decided during a hackathon to build a new build system, modeled loosely on Google's Blaze. Almost everyone told him it was a bad idea—the standard tool might get 100x better, and here he was, a new hire with no credibility, going off on a "science project." One senior Android engineer (John Perlow, ex-Google) told him, "you're going to do it, just do it fast before anyone gets cancers you," and supported him. The key technical insight was incremental builds: he analyzed what actually changed and cached results so only the affected modules were rebuilt. He also made module creation trivial, which encouraged more modularization, which in turn made builds more incremental. The result was a build system—**Buck**—that was dramatically faster, and it eventually spread to iOS as well. "I started it as the Java build tool, not the everything build tool," he says, deliberately scoping it narrowly to avoid political friction. The project also taught him a lesson about credibility: early on, he'd made the mistake of saying "well, at Google we did it this way," and people rightfully didn't care. "Just because it worked there didn't mean it was going to work here."

**Rewriting Facebook's IDE**

After Buck, he briefly worked on iOS Messenger ("I still didn't like it") and encountered Xcode's scaling limits. Facebook's single monolithic app hit the scaling limits of every mobile developer tool before anyone else. When they asked Apple for help, the feedback was essentially "your project's too big, make it smaller." Meanwhile, an internal web-based IDE built on an abandoned Google Web Toolkit project (Java compiled to JavaScript) didn't feel right either. "We are the React company at this point. How would we not empower people who want to build dev tools to build on technologies that we are the leader in?" He started **Nuclide**, a desktop IDE built on top of Atom (GitHub's Electron-based editor), scoping it initially as "just for iOS" to avoid threatening the web IDE team. Leadership eventually backed Nuclide, partly because the technology-stack argument was sound and partly because his Buck success had built credibility: "the last thing worked out, like let me try this one." The combined impact led to his promotion to E8 (principal engineer)—a validation that he'd learned to align his passion with the company's needs, unlike his Google years. He also open-sourced both Buck and Nuclide, a bottoms-up decision driven by a sense of giving back and the recruiting value of showing "this is what we do, this is who we are." Buck was even adopted by Uber and Airbnb.

**Struggles after the principal promo**

Post-promotion, he "got a little over my skis" and took on web speed for facebook.com. The problem was massive: stale architecture, sluggish load times. He and a colleague even started compiling V8 from source to see if they could change JavaScript generation for better performance. None of it panned out. He calls this his **hero quest**—the ego-driven idea that "there's this Gordian knot and all these engineers are like, if only someone would come in and solve this engineering problem." But web speed required more data analysis and cross-team diplomacy than writing code from scratch, and "that's just not my strength." The learning: "I can do a lot of things, but there's a smaller subset of things that I genuinely enjoy doing and that I'm going to be a lot more successful in. We don't all have to be the best at everything."

**Building a virtual file system for Facebook**

His manager Brian O'Sullivan organized a small group to get ahead of the monorepo's inevitable scaling problem. He joined Adam Simpkins and Wes Furlong ("I thought I was the worst engineer on the project for quite a bit") to build **Eden**, a virtual file system. The core idea: most users only need a subset of the repo, so don't materialize every file on disk when you clone or switch commits. The hard part wasn't just building the VFS primitive; it was retrofitting every tool in the chain (ripgrep, IDEs, etc.) to work with it instead of naively walking the entire file system and undoing all the benefits. He and Hanson Wang built **Miles** (short for "my files"), a file-search system that indexed only file names on a cron job, maintained a clever bitmap structure for fuzzy matching, and supported queries like "at this commit, which files match?"—all in 10-20ms across a million files. It was adopted far beyond the editor use case, eventually running on 30+ servers worldwide.

**Delayed Distinguished promo and learning about influence**

As an E8 who primarily wrote code (most peers at that level "are actually kind of exclusively spent doing more influence or working across teams"), he struggled with the expectation to influence rather than just build. When Microsoft acquired GitHub in 2018, he saw that Atom would be deprecated and VS Code would kill Nuclide's foundation. He was right—Atom was eventually sunset—but he pushed so aggressively and with so much anxiety that people pushed back. "I just came down like way too hard." The promo was delayed, and he received coaching. The core lesson: recognize **triggers**—technical decisions that activate strong emotions—and don't act in the moment. "If I don't think I can have the conversation, or I'm not in the best place to have it, maybe I go talk to the person's manager instead rather than like bull in a china shop to the engineer." A year later, he had conversations to "balance the ledger" and worked it out. The issue wasn't his correctness; it was *how* he went about it.

**Joining OpenAI**

By late 2023, he was at Meta working on LLM-based developer tools (an internal Copilot-like tool called Code Compose), but he was frustrated. "We're on Llama 2, it's not the same thing" as GPT-4. He wanted to build with the best model. Three other factors: the caliber of people joining OpenAI ("I could actually work with more senior people"), the historical moment ("this would be like the most similar to starting at Google in 2000"), and the chance to finally ship a consumer product that reaches millions after years of internal dev tools reaching 20,000-40,000 colleagues.

**Research-led vs. engineering-led cultures**

The adjustment from an engineering-driven company to a research-driven one was real. "I think of anyone who comes here and says otherwise is a liar." At OpenAI, he fully accepts that the model's quality is the primary driver: "if the model weren't very good, it wouldn't really matter what we did on the harness." But he values sitting next to the research team and co-developing the product together—a key reason he left Meta rather than continuing LLM work there.

**The story behind Codex**

Codex CLI launched in April 2025 as a "one more thing" during the o4-mini livestream, open-sourced immediately. It was "pretty rushed to get it out the door," but the open-source approach meant pull requests flooded in and the repo hit 10,000-20,000 stars in a week or two. A month later, Codex Web launched—a cloud container version—but it was "a little bit ahead of its time." Users were still more into local coding agents. Through the summer, the team shifted resources back to Codex CLI, coinciding with GPT-5's release and enough staffing to also build a VS Code extension. He'd prototyped the extension multiple times before and felt strongly that "the terminal is good for a lot of things, but it has a lot of limitations"—VS Code let them avoid those compromises. August was a "crazy month": GPT-5 shipped, a refreshed terminal UI launched, an open-source GPT model was supported, and the VS Code extension followed. That confluence created the inflection point that led to "vertical growth."

On the local-vs-cloud agent debate: he believes compute for agents will be majority cloud in the limit, because "you're going to need more machines than just your laptop as a place for agents to run"—think automated issue resolution, CI pipelines, and transferring work between environments.

**How he uses Codex**

Codex usage is up 5x since the beginning of the year, with over a million active users. His own workflow has shifted: 80-90% of his code is now model-generated. For throwaway prototypes, he doesn't even look at the code ("I'm so excited about it"). For Codex's own Rust codebase, he still reviews carefully, especially the sandboxing and security-integrity code. "I do more of that by hand because I need to make sure, really sure, that that's all correct." He uses Codex heavily for refactors and splitting up monolithic PRs into reviewable commits. Code review at OpenAI now involves the agent doing multiple self-review passes, then humans doing a final pass. Pull request summaries (also AI-written) are "getting way better," making human review faster.

**Why Codex's harness is open source**

For a tool that runs on your machine and can execute arbitrary commands, transparency matters. "You're going to put this thing on my machine, I care about what it's doing." In the AI agent domain specifically, people have a lot of questions, and open source is an answer. It's also produced great bug reports and contributions they'd have missed otherwise.

**Top technical book recommendations**

When he realized he was working on a virtual file system project without really understanding operating systems, he asked his manager Aaron Kushner what book to read and got pointed to a thousand-page Addison-Wesley OS book. He read it cover to cover. "It's kind of amazing and sad or weird how far many of us can get in software engineering without really having a clue how computers work." His deeper advice: actively go deeper through the layers of abstraction, because there are problems solvable only by understanding "the cruft between these two layers"—you can get 10x improvements by eliminating what others don't see. He also recommends **CTF (capture the flag)** security competitions as "a computer decathlon": one challenge might require assembly reverse-engineering, another might require understanding a janky PHP admin page. It forces breadth in a way that's hard to generate otherwise and builds a broader diagnostic toolkit.

**Why deep technical skills are still valuable (for now)**

He doesn't have a definitive answer and acknowledges "everyone's struggling to answer that question right now." For now, the quality of questions you ask the agent determines the quality of output, so deep understanding still matters. As that layer also gets automated away, the timeline is uncertain—"things do seem to be happening faster than we expect." He also notes that at senior IC levels (E8/E9), the expectations are "a little scary"—you're benchmarked against directors who manage 100+ people. Some senior ICs manage through influence and technical credibility; others pair with managers who can match them to the right force-multiplier project. "Not actually recognizing senior managers enough who are the person who pairs the senior IC with the right project" is an underappreciated dynamic.

**How to start projects well**

His projects almost always originate from personal dissatisfaction—slow builds, bad editors, filesystem scaling pains. On how he had the confidence to attempt Buck: having seen Blaze at Google provided an "existence proof" that a better build system was possible. He identifies as a "coding machine" who can quickly build a prototype to test the basic hypothesis. "Generally, if you are determined, you find a way." He also learned from a Google Calendar weather feature: he charged ahead and implemented it by "throwing a blob of XML" into the backend, only for his tech lead to ask, "how are you storing that?" and introduce him to protocol buffers. Sometimes the willingness to charge ahead collides with the need to ask for guidance.

**Advice on writing better and career planning**

For writing: outline extensively, check if points flow linearly, and anticipate where a reader might need a bridging example. For career planning, his **three-step plan for impact**: (1) figure out what you really like to do, (2) figure out what's invaluable to your employer, (3) find the intersection and lean into it. The challenge at Google was he only did step 1; at Meta, he learned to do all three.

**Advice for his younger self**

"Be open to learning more things sooner." He went very deep on JavaScript and avoided C and Objective-C for years out of attachment to his first productive language. "It's such a relief to finally do something. And then it's also a hazard because you want to hold on to that thing." If he'd been more curious and flexible about the types of projects he'd take on, he thinks it would have accelerated his growth earlier. With Codex, that language-barrier friction may fade for the next generation.

---

His **three-step plan for impact**—finding the overlap between personal joy and employer value—threads through his entire arc, from the Closure misalignment at Google to the Buck/Nuclide/Eden wins at Meta to his bet on Codex at OpenAI. The **hero quest** anti-pattern names his recurring temptation to solo-solve Gordian knots outside his strengths, while his **triggers** framework captures the emotional self-management that finally unlocked the E9 promo. Beneath it all is a consistent engine: personal dissatisfaction as a project starter, an "existence proof" from prior art as a permission slip, and the self-image of a "coding machine" who prototypes fast and scopes narrowly to reduce political friction—a playbook that scaled from a hackathon build system to an open-source agent platform serving millions.
