Here are the takeaways from this Adam Ernst interview (Meta Distinguished Engineer IC9 who built iOS infrastructure that scaled across the company, from the native app rewrite through Component Kit):

**His middle school software company**

Adam Ernst started coding early — in middle school he founded a company called Cosmic Soft, building online testing software in a tool called Real Basic, which was essentially a cross-platform Visual Basic-inspired language. He describes Visual Basic’s appeal: "you basically have this blank canvas and you can just drag and drop buttons and text fields," double-click a button, and write a line of code like `window close`. "Really easy to get started with. Not very scalable. And basic is a horrifying language, but it was a fun way to get started." He sold it internationally through a proto-Stripe service called Ecelerate, which handled credit cards and generated unlock codes. He also accepted physical checks mailed by teachers across the US — "they’d mail me a check for 1995 and I would send them via email a code to unlock their software." The whole operation ran with his parents’ bemused awareness.

**First project and promo at Meta (the Core Data swap)**

Adam joined Facebook in 2012, just weeks before the IPO, when "all of the iOS engineers could meet in one conference room." The company was in the middle of a cultural shift: the native app rewrite had won the battle against the HTML5 approach, and native engineers were "very much ascendant." But the new codebase, built on Apple’s Core Data ORM framework, was hitting its limits as the iOS team ballooned from ~15-20 engineers toward hundreds. Core Data "falls over completely at that scale," so the mobile team needed to swap out the data layer — just as every product team was clamoring to build new features in native. The solution was **MEM models**, an immutable model system that made thread safety and mutations far more tractable.

Convincing other teams to adopt it required a multi-pronged approach. Adam’s number one tip for influencing engineers without authority: **talk in person or via video conference** — "you spend a lot of time trying to make your writing clear and accessible and have a good tone, and you can often convey that tone way faster in person." He was also sympathetic to their worldview: "I would always start the conversation saying, yes, I prefer vanilla Apple frameworks too. We’re not going down this path just because I want to invent a new framework." The team went so far as to disassemble Core Data’s source code (since it wasn’t open source) to understand why it was slow, giving them hard data to present to skeptical engineers. His most effective tactic, though, was simply **doing the migration work for them**: "if you show up and you’re like, hey, I did the work already for you. Here are the benefits. I just need you to sign off and say yes. Much easier conversation." He dubs his own archetype a "coding machine" — he just writes a lot of code. The promo to IC6 (staff equivalent) happened fast, driven by solving this scaling crisis.

**Why code review is undervalued**

Around this promo, Adam reviewed over 1,600 diffs in a half — roughly 14 per workday. He calls code review "super undervalued" because **it’s a viral vector for influence**: "if I see a concrete diff and I’m like, I don’t like how they’re doing this, and here’s why, it gives me a perfect opening to get into it." A good diff comment, in his view, explains the *why* behind the nitpick, not just what to change. "Don’t say change X to Y. Say, hey, X has some problems, so I really want you to use Y instead. But if you really feel strongly, if there’s something I’m missing, then you go ahead." He also makes a point of flagging his own missing context — "I might be making a stupid mistake, which happens" — and asking the author to fill him in and put it in the diff summary. This flexible approach means the engineer absorbs the principle, carries it into their future work, and spreads it through their own reviews. "It’s like viral, right? And you would see this spread through the code reviews that they did."

**Senior Staff (IC7) promo and Component Kit**

By ~2014, Facebook Newsfeed was "basically falling apart" — constant crashes, layout bugs, overlapping content — because the company had hired aggressively and stuffed engineers onto the hottest product. Lee Byron (co-inventor of GraphQL, then Adam’s manager) saw the React model working on the web and nudged Adam: "why don’t you just take some time and think about how would the concepts of React work in an iOS-first way?" Adam’s answer was **Component Kit**, a declarative UI framework built years before SwiftUI or React Native existed. It used React-like concepts — components, immutability, re-render-when-anything-changes, view reconciliation — but in Objective-C. "You’re not directly managing UI views, you’re instead just stating, hey, I want there to be a button that has this caption. The framework takes care of creating the actual button view."

Pushback was intense. Some engineers were convinced by seeing the same code shrink to a third its size; others by the performance wins (rendering view hierarchies on background threads, minimal main-thread work). For the holdouts, they brought in mediators — senior engineers like Alan Canestraro and Lee Byron himself to "huddle up and try to just talk through how do we find a path forward that we can all agree on." The fact that React already had credibility inside the company on the web was a huge lever: "look at what React is doing on the web. We want to do the same on iOS." Adam also acknowledged the downsides openly — the framework’s C++/Objective-C nature made it "creaky" and syntactically unusual — but his pitch was "yes, it’s a little weird, it’s not the usual way of writing code in iOS, but here’s all the amazing things we get."

The experience taught him that **allies are super useful**. Engineers like Clement Gensmer and Greg Mech, who carried weight, saw Component Kit, liked it, and "could help convince other people because I have one way of convincing people which isn’t always effective, and they have other ways." There was also a framework called Panels, led by Jonathan Dann (Adam’s mentor), that was supposed to be the next big thing and felt threatened by Component Kit. The resolution: "we found a way to compromise and we adopted some of the Panels data-source technology to power Component Kit, which was a good compromise and allowed us all to feel like we had a win. Instead of sticking to your guns on every little thing, if you can find a way to bring people into the tent, that’s really, really helpful."

Adam never doubted the direction for Newsfeed — the scrolling-list-of-complicated-nesting use case was the "perfect application" for declarative UI. But he’s clear-eyed about tradeoffs: for a "super dynamic drag and drop interface, eh, maybe not the right thing." He wasn’t "drinking the Kool-Aid" claiming it was the only way.

**His major failed project (Component Script)**

After Component Kit’s success, Adam’s manager Ari Grant — "a force of nature" — had a new nudge: break out of the per-platform silo. iOS had Component Kit, Android had Litho (a similar framework), but everything was being written multiple times. React Native existed but didn’t fit Meta’s model: it wanted to be in charge of the whole app, whereas Meta needed to slot small pieces of cross-platform UI into a large native app. So Adam spent two years building **Component Script**, a framework that ran on top of Component Kit and Litho, with a smaller, pared-down API that felt React-like. It was technically excellent: type-safe, deeply integrated with GraphQL, fully bidirectional (you could embed native inside cross-platform and vice versa, at any level). Real features shipped on it. And it failed completely.

The postmortem, which Adam published publicly in what Ryan calls "one of the best retrospectives I’ve read," surfaces the mistakes. First, **he wasn’t aiming at a particular target engineer**. "Component Kit was focused on iOS engineers that already know how to write iOS code. Let’s let them write iOS code but have excellent performance. React Native was focused on web engineers who wanted to write for mobile. Component Script was like, hey, you can write JavaScript, but it’s not React." iOS engineers didn’t want to learn JavaScript, and JavaScript engineers didn’t want a weird non-React API. Second, Adam’s group cared deeply about GraphQL for data consistency, but GraphQL tooling at the time was "slow and janky" and slowed them down. Meanwhile, a competing server-driven UI framework simply skipped data consistency — if you liked a post on one screen, the next screen might not show it — and for "60% of the time, 80% of the time, products just don’t care." Adam was "horrified" by the idea of not using GraphQL and refused to compromise, which was a strategic disadvantage. Third, he **went too wide**: "I was just going to talk to all engineers, all mobile engineers, and be like, you guys should all try out Component Script." He got pings of interest from scattered individuals, but "there wasn’t any individual team that was like, yes, this is how we want to write products from now on." Right when he finally pulled the plug, the Groups team said they were about to go all-in — but "even that would not have been enough momentum. It was too little, too late."

**How to handle a failed project**

A "meets most" performance rating was the signal that crystallized the need to kill it. "Performance did its job there. My manager at the time did the right thing and was like, this is not working." Adam’s cleanup set a standard: he helped every team that had built features on Component Script migrate back to native or React Native, then **completely deleted the framework** — no lingering one-off product left behind. "I feel like I got, if anything, a positive bump after the fact. There was enough relief of like, all right, this showed people how to wind down a project that isn’t working out." He published the postmortem publicly as a way to "rip the Band-Aid off" and avoid rehashing the conversation endlessly, and to influence future decision-making so others wouldn’t repeat the same mistakes.

His deeper regret is not listening to his gut sooner. The project ran two years, and "for the last year I knew it wasn’t going right." He had "literally sleepless nights" but his default reaction as a "coding machine" was "I just need to write more code, I just need to help more features convert and it’ll suddenly take off." The lesson: "I should have listened instead to that part of my gut that was telling me this is not going to work out. Just go do something else and find a different way to have impact."

**Thoughts on management (and why he never did it)**

Adam is unequivocal that management wasn’t for him. Reason one: "I like writing code. I really like writing code a lot, and as a manager you can’t write code." Reason two: he’s "less good at the non-code-related parts of the job — driving alignment and writing docs, I’m just not good at that stuff." Reason three: he’s effective at technical communication with engineers when he can point to a specific line of code, but "managers have to do a lot of direction-setting and influencing people without directly pointing to, like, this line of code is the problem, and that I think I’m less good at." He’s stayed on mobile for his entire Meta career — more than a decade — never formally switching teams, just rolling with reorgs. That deep investment created enormous domain expertise: "if you want to know the guts of the GraphQL code gen or value object generation or Buck or all these things, I know what’s up."

**Technical depth vs. breadth (the organic learning model)**

For engineers aiming at the highest IC levels, Adam doesn’t prescribe pure depth over breadth — he advocates an **organic, problem-driven breadth** that emerges from depth. His method: whenever something blocks his code from landing, rather than reaching for another team, "I’m gonna dive eight levels deep into their code-gen guts until I find the problem, and then I’ll either fix it myself and now I know GraphQL code gen, or I will show up to the GraphQL team and be like, hey, ran into this problem, debugged it eight levels deep, here’s the problem. How do I fix it?" This impresses the owning team, doesn’t consume their time, and organically builds knowledge across systems. "If you keep doing that enough, then you will discover a lot about a lot of different systems, and you’ll understand them, and that’ll give you so much knowledge. It’s like a superpower." The key is that the learning is reactive, not pre-studied: "they’ll come to me. I’m going to have a problem that I run into. GraphQL is going to block my code from landing. Great, now I have a reason to go delve into GraphQL code gen. I’m not going to study it from first principles and be like, well, I should go learn the GraphQL code-gen systems just because."

**IC9 expectations (flipping the switch off)**

When Ryan notes that many engineers who reach extremely high levels worry about whether their work is "IC9-level," Adam’s answer is disarmingly simple: **"I’ve flipped the switch off. I just don’t care."** He used to worry a lot, but now his approach is to do what he loves, check in with his manager frequently to ensure he’s solving problems the org actually needs, and then stop worrying. Component Script was the exception — eventually it no longer mattered to the company, and someone had to prod him — but "they prodded me and I realized it and the problem was solved." Getting that "meets most" was almost freeing: "if that happens again, I’ll know that I went too deep and too hard and didn’t listen, and now I go listen and fix it."

**Senior engineers he admires (a gallery of archetypes)**

Adam rattles off a list of engineers whose distinct strengths he admires, implicitly mapping the range of ways to be exceptional at high IC levels. **Dustin Shahidipur** works like him — writes a lot of code, enjoys that part of the job. **Wei Han** (joined Meta the same day as Adam in 2012) is a "fixer" — "she’s very good at, like, hey, this metric is not right, what is the deal?" **Michael Bolan** (no longer at Meta) was a great project starter: he could identify a need, kickstart a project like the fuzzy file-searcher Miles, and just do it end to end — "backend binaries on multiple platforms, deployment, documentation, naming." **Bob Baldwin** is an excellent communicator who "can really boil down the message." **Oliver Ricard** is a masterful writer whose posts Adam uses as the bar when telling engineers to improve their communication. And **Nolan O’Brien** is Adam’s "polar opposite" — he writes some code but not much, and is "extremely good at driving alignment between different teams and managing the Apple relationship, which is something I never had patience to do." The list is a quiet argument for self-awareness: knowing what you’re not, and valuing the complementary skills that make a senior engineering org work.

**Advice to his younger self (or: do what you like)**

When asked what he’d tell himself at Princeton graduation, Adam gives the most Adam answer possible: "Nothing. It worked out pretty well for me. I’m not screwing with it." He reveals he actually threw Facebook’s puzzle-based job application in the trash in 2010 — "I was like, I don’t have time for that" — and joining later, post-HTML5, probably worked out better. For general career advice, he lands on something he knows sounds cliché: "do what you like. It’s really hard to fake enthusiasm or find enthusiasm for something you really don’t enjoy doing. Find what you love doing and do that, and if that intersects with what the industry needs, you’re in a really lucky place." Ryan notes that the downstream effects are massive: Adam’s insane output, his curiosity, his willingness to debug ten layers deep all trace back to genuinely enjoying the challenge of solving problems. "It’s not like every single moment I’m debugging some really bad code deep in some awful system, I’m loving what I’m doing. But I like the challenge of solving problems. And so if you like that, you’re in a good place."
