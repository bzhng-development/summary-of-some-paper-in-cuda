# Cut Your Losses in Large-Vocabulary Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2411.09009](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09009)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Cut Cross-Entropy (CCE)**, a method that computes the cross-entropy loss and its gradient without materializing the full logit matrix — entries for every token-vocabulary pair — in global GPU memory, instead computing logits on the fly in on-chip SRAM and evaluating the log-sum-exp reduction incrementally.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Cut Cross-Entropy (CCE)**, a method that computes the cross-entropy loss and its gradient without materializing the full logit matrix — entries for every token-vocabulary pair — in global GPU memory, instead computing logits on the fly in on-chip SRAM and evaluating the log-sum-exp reduction incrementally. The approach is evaluated on frontier models including Gemma 2 (2B), Phi 3.5 Mini, Qwen 2.5 (7B), and Mistral NeMo, and combines two key mechanisms: an indexed matrix multiplication that retrieves only the ground-truth token's classifier column for the dot product (avoiding an O(N|V|) memory allocation), and a blockwise linear-log-sum-exp kernel that applies gradient filtering — skipping gradient updates for softmax entries below bfloat16 numerical precision, which constitute over 99.98% of elements — and vocabulary sorting to maximize block-level sparsity. On Gemma 2 (2B) with a vocabulary of 256,128 tokens, CCE reduces the memory footprint of the loss computation from 24 GB to 1 MB and the total training-time memory of the classifier head from 28 GB to 1 GB, enabling batch size increases of 1.5× to 10× across model scales without sacrificing convergence, establishing that the cross-entropy layer's dominance in training memory is an implementation artifact rather than a fundamental requirement of large-vocabulary language models.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: One Layer Dominates LLM Training Memory

The paper addresses a specific, quantifiable bottleneck: as language models grow in parameter count, context length, and vocabulary size, the memory footprint of training has become disproportionately concentrated in a single layer — the **cross-entropy loss computation** at the classifier head. This layer's memory consumption scales with $O(N \times |V|)$, the product of the number of tokens in a batch and the vocabulary size. For contemporary models with vocabularies of 128K to 256K tokens and batch sizes in the tens of thousands of tokens, this $N \times |V|$ logit matrix can consume **up to 90% of total training memory**, as shown in Figure 1a.

The problem is starkly illustrated by concrete numbers from the paper. In a standard 16-GPU fully-sharded data-parallel setup with activation checkpointing and mixed-precision AdamW, the log-probabilities materialized by the cross-entropy layer account for:

- **40%** of the memory consumption of Phi 3.5 Mini (vocabulary size $|V| = 32,064$)
- **65%** of the memory consumption of Llama 3 (8B) ($|V| = 128,000$)
- **89%** of the memory consumption of Gemma 2 (2B) ($|V| = 256,128$)

For Gemma 2 (2B) with a single sequence of length $N = 80,000$, the log-probabilities alone consume the entire available memory of an 80 GB H100 GPU. These percentages are not marginal — for the worst-affected models, the cross-entropy layer uses more memory than the rest of the model's weights, optimizer states, gradients, and activation checkpoints combined. This means that **the maximum batch size during training is gated not by the model's parameter count or sequence length, but by the vocabulary size** — a dimension that the training infrastructure community had not previously identified as the primary bottleneck.

### Why This Problem Matters: Practical and Scaling Implications

The memory dominance of the cross-entropy layer is not merely an academic concern — it has direct, practical consequences for how LLMs are trained and deployed, and it grows more acute with trends in the field.

**Batch size constraints limit training throughput.** Data-parallel training distributes work across GPUs by splitting the batch. The maximum per-GPU batch size determines how many GPUs are needed to achieve a given global batch size, or whether a desired batch size is achievable at all. When the cross-entropy layer consumes an order of magnitude more memory than the backbone, it imposes a hard ceiling on the per-GPU batch size, forcing practitioners to either increase the number of GPUs (and thus communication overhead and cost) or reduce the batch size (potentially harming training dynamics and convergence). Figure 1b quantifies what this ceiling costs: for Gemma 2 (2B), the maximum batch size is just over 1 million tokens with standard cross-entropy, but removing the memory bottleneck enables a **10× increase** to over 10 million tokens.

**Vocabulary sizes are growing, not shrinking.** The paper explicitly notes that "even the largest contemporary vocabularies (e.g., 256K tokens) may benefit from further expansion" (Section 1), citing Tao et al. (2024)'s work showing scaling laws favor larger vocabularies for larger models. This creates a compounding problem: as model sizes increase, so does the pressure to increase vocabulary sizes, but the memory cost of the cross-entropy layer grows with the *product* of vocabulary size and batch size. Without a solution, vocabulary expansion — which improves model comprehension by compressing longer documents into shorter token sequences — becomes self-defeating because it prevents training at the batch sizes needed for larger models.

**Pipeline parallelism is unbalanced.** Very large models are typically trained with pipeline parallelism (GPipe, PipeDream), which partitions model layers across GPUs and pipelines the computation. Pipeline parallelism works best when all stages are equally balanced in computation and memory load. The classification head is currently an outlier with a disproportionately high memory-to-computation ratio compared to all other layers. This forces pipeline stages containing the classifier head to be underutilized computationally while consuming disproportionate memory, or forces the training system to allocate extra GPUs specifically for this one layer — an inefficiency that compounds at scale.

### Existing Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies several lines of prior work that address aspects of large-model training memory, each of which redistributes the memory bottleneck without eliminating it, or trades off memory for latency in ways that are insufficient for the magnitude of the current problem.

**Chunked computation.** The most direct prior approach to reducing cross-entropy memory is chunking: dividing the vocabulary (or batch) into smaller pieces, computing the loss and gradient for each chunk independently, and accumulating results. This is the strategy used by Torch Tune (Section 5.1, Table 1) and, to some degree, by Liger Kernels. Chunking reduces peak memory from $O(N \times |V|)$ to $O(N \times \text{chunk\_size})$ or $O(\text{chunk\_size} \times |V|)$, with smaller chunks using less memory.

The critical limitation of chunking is that it induces a **memory-latency trade-off**. Memory footprint is minimized when the number of chunks is high, but latency is maximized because each chunk requires dispatching separate GPU kernel launches. Kernel launch overhead becomes the dominant cost when chunks are small, particularly for operations that are memory-bound rather than compute-bound. Table 1 quantifies this precisely: Torch Tune with 8 chunks reduces memory by 65% vs. baseline but adds latency; Liger Kernels achieves 95% memory reduction but "has a detrimental effect on latency, more than doubling the wall-clock time for the computation" (Section 5.1, Table 1 row 2 vs. 4). The paper's key insight is that this trade-off is **fundamental to chunking but unnecessary for the problem** — there exists a path that achieves minimal memory *without* the latency penalty.

**Vocabulary reduction.** Another line of work reduces the memory footprint by reducing the effective vocabulary size. Grave et al. (2017) proposed hierarchical vocabulary structures where log-probabilities are computed only for a subset of the vocabulary at any given time. Yu et al. (2023) explored tokenization-free byte-level models that operate on dramatically smaller vocabularies (256 bytes rather than 256K tokens). These approaches change the modeling decision — they modify *which* vocabulary the model uses — rather than the implementation. This means they incur a modeling trade-off: smaller or hierarchical vocabularies may reduce the model's ability to compress information into compact token sequences, potentially degrading comprehension or requiring longer sequences to represent the same content. The paper distinguishes its contribution by making no modeling changes whatsoever — CCE computes the **exact same loss and gradient** as standard cross-entropy, just without the memory footprint.

**Sequence and model parallelism.** Recent systems work, particularly DeepSpeed Ulysses (Jacobs et al., 2023), enables training very large models with large vocabularies by splitting individual input sequences across multiple GPUs (sequence parallelism). Model parallelism techniques (GPipe, Megatron-LM) distribute layers across GPUs. These approaches solve the memory problem by **spending more GPUs** — they don't reduce the total memory required, they distribute it across more devices. The paper frames this as a complementary approach: CCE reduces the memory needed *per GPU*, which then multiplies with parallelism strategies to enable even larger configurations. If sequence parallelism splits a sequence across $P$ GPUs, and CCE reduces per-GPU memory by $10\times$, the combined system can handle sequences $10P$ times longer than a baseline without CCE.

**Efficient attention implementations.** The paper draws a direct parallel between its approach and the evolution of attention implementations. Rabe & Staats (2021) developed chunked self-attention to avoid materializing the full attention matrix. FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) went further, using online softmax to compute exact attention in on-chip SRAM without the $O(N^2)$ memory cost. The authors explicitly position CCE as doing for the cross-entropy layer what FlashAttention did for self-attention: "CCE builds on... materializing blocks of the $N^2$-sized self-attention matrix in on-chip SRAM rather than slower global DRAM" (Section 2). Prior to FlashAttention, the self-attention layer was often the memory bottleneck; after FlashAttention, that bottleneck shifted to the cross-entropy layer. CCE addresses exactly this shifted bottleneck.

**Fused loss-gradient kernels.** Liger Kernels (Hsu et al., 2024) make efficient use of the GPU by chunking and computing the loss and gradient simultaneously in a single kernel, avoiding separate forward and backward passes. This technique reduces memory by eliminating intermediate buffers but has two limitations the paper identifies. First, computing loss and gradient simultaneously means that "any transform applied to the loss (such as masking) is implemented in the kernel itself" (Section 2) — there is no separation between the loss computation and user-defined post-processing. CCE maintains separate forward and backward stages, enabling arbitrary loss transformations without kernel modification. Second, Liger Kernels is still fundamentally a chunking approach, and its performance is bounded by the memory-latency trade-off inherent to chunking, which explains why it more than doubles latency while CCE's latency matches or beats the baseline.

### How This Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper's positioning can be understood through three deliberate design choices that distinguish it from all prior approaches.

**No modeling trade-off.** Unlike vocabulary reduction methods, CCE computes the exact same loss and gradient as standard cross-entropy. This means practitioners can adopt CCE without changing their model architecture, tokenizer, or training data, and without worrying about whether a smaller vocabulary will harm downstream performance. The training curves in Figures 4 and 5 directly demonstrate this equivalence: CCE and `torch.compile` produce indistinguishable loss and perplexity trajectories.

**No memory-latency trade-off.** Unlike chunking approaches, CCE uses **only on-chip SRAM** for intermediate computations, never writing the full logit matrix to global memory. The paper's Table 1 is the key evidence: CCE achieves the lowest possible memory footprint (within 3 MB of the theoretical lower bound — the memory required for the output gradients themselves) while being 5% *faster* than `torch.compile` for the loss forward pass and only 6% slower for loss+gradient combined. This breaks the apparent trade-off that prior work accepted as inevitable.

**The cross-entropy layer as the final unconquered memory bottleneck.** The paper tells a historical story of sequential memory optimizations: data parallelism with large mini-batches (Goyal et al., 2017), ZeRO sharding for parameters/gradients/optimizer states (Rajbhandari et al., 2020), activation checkpointing for intermediate activations (Chen et al., 2016), and FlashAttention for the self-attention layer (Dao et al., 2022). Each of these "gradually shifted the memory consumption of LLM training to one single layer — the cross-entropy loss" (Section 1). The paper's framing is that CCE is the natural next step in this progression, completing the set of memory optimizations needed to make every component of LLM training scale efficiently. Figure 1 visualizes this shift: the orange bars (log-probabilities) dominate the memory breakdown for baseline cross-entropy, and essentially disappear with CCE, leaving weights/optimizer states and activation checkpoints as the remaining constraints.

This positioning is important because it implies that CCE is not competing with parallelism strategies or vocabulary design choices — it's solving the implementation-level problem that those approaches either work around or pay a modeling cost to avoid. The result, as Figure 1b shows, is that batch sizes can increase by **1.5× to 10×** depending on the model, purely from an implementation improvement with no loss of accuracy or throughput.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is an **implementation-level systems contribution** — it doesn't propose a new model, loss function, or training algorithm, but rather a novel way to *compute* the standard cross-entropy loss and its gradient such that the full logit matrix (whose entries are the scores for every pair of input token and vocabulary item) never needs to exist in GPU global memory. The system solves the problem that the cross-entropy layer's memory consumption grows as the product of the batch token count and vocabulary size ($N \times |V|$), making it the dominant memory consumer in large-vocabulary LLM training, and it does so by recognizing that both the loss and its gradient depend on only two quantities — the logit of the correct token and the log-sum-exp across all vocabulary entries — which can be computed blockwise entirely in on-chip SRAM without ever writing the $N \times |V|$ intermediate matrix to global memory.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

CCE decomposes the cross-entropy computation into three stages that operate on the same physical inputs ($E \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times N}$, the batch of embeddings from the backbone; $C \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times |V|}$, the classifier matrix; and $x \in \mathbb{R}^N$, the ground-truth token indices) but avoid materializing the full logit matrix $C^\top E \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times N}$:

1. **Indexed Matrix Multiplication (forward):** For each token $n$ in the batch, retrieve only the classifier column $C_{x_n}$ corresponding to its ground-truth token index, compute the dot product $C_{x_n}^\top E_n$ on-chip, and write out a single scalar per token — the correct-token logit. This is a fused index-load-and-dot-product kernel that uses only SRAM.

2. **Linear-Log-Sum-Exp (forward):** Compute $\text{LSE}_n = \log\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\exp(C_j^\top E_n)$ for every token $n$ entirely in on-chip SRAM, using the same blockwise partitioning as a standard matrix multiplication, with each block independently computing its local matrix multiplication and local log-sum-exp, then atomically updating the global LSE value. The normalized loss is then $\ell_n = C_{x_n}^\top E_n - \text{LSE}_n$, exactly the standard cross-entropy.

3. **Gradient Computation (backward):** Recomputation of the local logits $C_v^\top E_n$ in on-chip SRAM (since they were never stored), followed by computation of the softmax gradient $S_{nv} = \exp(C_v^\top E_n - \text{LSE}_n)$, filtering of negligible entries (below bfloat16 precision), and sparse accumulation of $\nabla E$ and $\nabla C$ using only atomic updates to global memory — the full $|V| \times N$ softmax matrix is computed block-by-block and immediately consumed, never stored.

The critical design principle unifying all three stages: **no intermediate matrix larger than a single SRAM-resident block (sized $V_B \times N_B$ or $D_B \times N_B$) is ever written to global GPU memory.** The vocabulary size $|V|$ and batch size $N$ appear only as outer loop ranges, not as factors in the peak memory footprint.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the mathematical decomposition of cross-entropy into the two terms that motivate the separate kernel designs — the correct-token logit (indexed matmul) and the log-sum-exp (linear-log-sum-exp) — establishing *why* this decomposition makes memory-efficient implementation possible.
- **Second**, the indexed matrix multiplication kernel design, which is the simpler of the two forward-pass components and establishes the pattern of on-chip-only computation.
- **Third**, the linear-log-sum-exp forward pass kernel, which must solve the harder problem of a reduction over the entire vocabulary while still staying in SRAM, including the atomic synchronization mechanism.
- **Fourth**, the backward pass kernel, which must recompute logits, compute the softmax, filter negligible gradients, and accumulate against both $E$ and $C$ — the most complex kernel and the one where gradient filtering and vocabulary sorting provide the largest speedups.
- **Fifth**, the gradient filtering criterion and vocabulary sorting heuristic, which are the key innovations that make the backward pass fast enough to be practical despite recomputation.
- **Sixth**, design choices around numerical precision (Kahan summation, variants for fine-tuning vs. pretraining) and the merging of the indexed matmul backward into the linear-log-sum-exp backward kernel.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems/implementation paper** whose core idea is that the cross-entropy loss and its gradient can be computed without materializing the $|V| \times N$ logit matrix, by decomposing the computation into three operations — indexed matrix multiplication, blockwise log-sum-exp reduction, and sparse gradient accumulation — each of which operates entirely in on-chip GPU SRAM and writes only final scalar or gradient-sized outputs to global memory.

---

#### Mathematical Decomposition: Why Cross-Entropy Doesn't Need the Full Logit Matrix

The cross-entropy loss for a single next-token prediction $x_i$ given context $x_1 \ldots x_{i-1}$ is:

$$\ell_i = \log\text{softmax}_{x_i}(C^\top E_i) = C_{x_i}^\top E_i - \log\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \exp(C_j^\top E_i)$$

where:
- $E_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the embedding vector produced by the backbone network $f(x_1 \ldots x_{i-1})$ for the $i$-th prediction position,
- $C \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times |V|}$ is the classifier weight matrix whose columns map embeddings to vocabulary scores,
- $C_j \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the $j$-th column of the classifier corresponding to vocabulary token $j$,
- $C_{x_i}$ is the specific classifier column for the ground-truth token $x_i$ (an indexing operation),
- $C_{x_i}^\top E_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is the logit (unnormalized log-probability) for the correct token, and
- $\log\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \exp(C_j^\top E_i) \in \mathbb{R}$ is the log-sum-exp (LSE) normalization constant over all vocabulary entries.

**What this decomposition computes:** The loss for token $i$ is the difference between two scalars — the logit of the correct token and the log-sum-exp over the entire vocabulary. The first term requires only the *single* classifier column $C_{x_i}$, not the full $|V| \times 1$ logit vector. The second term requires all $|V|$ logits but only in aggregated form (a single reduction), not individually stored. The full vector of $|V|$ logits is an intermediate that exists only temporarily during the computation of either term, never needing persistence.

During training with teacher forcing, all $N$ next-token predictions in a batch are optimized jointly:

$$\ell = (C^\top E)_x - \log\sum_{j} \exp(C_j^\top E)$$

where $E = [E_1 \ldots E_N] \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times N}$ is the matrix of all embeddings, $(C^\top E)_x = [C_{x_1}^\top E_1 \ldots C_{x_N}^\top E_N] \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the vector of correct-token logits, and $\log\sum_j \exp(C_j^\top E) \in \mathbb{R}^N$ is the LSE vector computed elementwise over the vocabulary dimension.

**Why this decomposition matters:** A naive implementation computes the full $|V| \times N$ logit matrix $C^\top E$, stores it in global memory, applies softmax to each column, indexes the correct-token entries for the loss, and computes the gradient through this stored matrix. This requires $O(N|V|)$ memory. The decomposition reveals that the loss can be computed from two separate operations — one that needs only $O(N)$ memory (the correct-token logits) and one that needs to touch all $|V|$ logits per token but only to reduce them to a single scalar per token (the LSE). If both operations can be implemented to keep their intermediates in on-chip SRAM rather than global memory, the $O(N|V|)$ memory cost disappears.

The gradient of the cross-entropy loss with respect to the embeddings and classifier follows a similar decomposition. The gradient of $\ell_n = C_{x_n}^\top E_n - \text{LSE}_n$ with respect to $E_n$ and $C$ is:

$$\nabla_{E_n} \ell_n = -C_{x_n} + \sum_{j=1}^{|V|} S_{nj} \cdot C_j$$
$$\nabla_{C_j} \ell_n = -\mathbb{1}[j = x_n] \cdot E_n + S_{nj} \cdot E_n$$

where $S_{nj} = \text{softmax}_j(C^\top E_n) = \exp(C_j^\top E_n - \text{LSE}_n)$ is the softmax probability of token $j$ for input $n$. In matrix form over the full batch:

$$\nabla E^\top = (S \cdot \nabla\text{LSE}) C$$
$$\nabla C^\top = (S \cdot \nabla\text{LSE})^\top E$$

where $S \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times N}$ is the full softmax matrix and $\cdot$ denotes row-wise elementwise multiplication: $\hat{S} = S \cdot \nabla\text{LSE}$. The gradient computation requires the softmax matrix $S$, which is $|V| \times N$ — exactly the same size as the logits. The key observation enabling CCE is that $S$ can be recomputed block-by-block in SRAM, multiplied with the gradients, and immediately discarded, never needing global memory storage.

#### Indexed Matrix Multiplication: Computing Only the Correct-Token Logits

The first term of the loss, $(C^\top E)_x$, requires computing $C_{x_n}^\top E_n$ for each of the $N$ tokens. A naive approach would either materialize the full $|V| \times N$ logit matrix and then index into it (costing $O(N|V|)$ memory) or load the entire classifier $C$ and index into it for each token separately (resulting in repeated expensive global memory loads).

CCE fuses the indexing and the dot product into a single kernel that operates blockwise (Algorithm 1, Figure 2a). The input embedding matrix $E \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times N}$ is divided into blocks $E_n$ of size $D \times N_B$, where $N_B$ is a tunable block size for the batch dimension. The token indices $x$ are divided into corresponding blocks $x_n$ of size $N_B$. For each block:

1. **Indexed load:** The kernel uses the token indices in $x_n$ to load only the corresponding classifier columns $C_{x_{n,1}}, \ldots, C_{x_{n,N_B}}$ from global memory into on-chip SRAM. This is an indexed (gather) load rather than a contiguous load, but the number of columns loaded is $N_B$, not $|V|$.

2. **Blockwise dot product:** Further dividing each $E_n$ into sub-blocks $E_{n,d}$ of size $D_B \times N_B$ along the feature dimension, the kernel loads each $E_{n,d}$ into SRAM, loads the corresponding sub-blocks of the indexed classifier columns, and performs local dot products: $o_n \mathrel{+}= E_{n,d}^\top \cdot C_{x_n,d}$ where $C_{x_n,d}$ is the $D_B \times N_B$ matrix of the selected classifier columns' sub-blocks.

3. **Accumulation and write-out:** The partial dot products are accumulated in SRAM across the $D_B$ loop, and the final $N_B$-length vector $o_n$ is written to global memory. This vector $(C^\top E)_x$ is the output — the correct-token logits for these $N_B$ tokens.

**Memory footprint:** The kernel allocates zero bytes of global memory beyond the output vector. All intermediate data (the indexed classifier columns, the embedding sub-blocks, the partial dot products) reside in on-chip SRAM, which is typically 128–256 KB per streaming multiprocessor. The output vector is $N$ scalars total, or approximately 2–4 bytes per token in bfloat16 — many orders of magnitude smaller than the $N \times |V|$ logit matrix.

**Why this works:** The operation is fundamentally a sparse matrix multiplication — each token only needs the dot product with its ground-truth classifier column. Rather than computing all $|V|$ dot products and discarding all but one, CCE computes only the one needed per token. The indexed load from $C$ is more expensive per byte than a contiguous load (due to cache line utilization), but the total bytes loaded is $N \times D$ for the classifier (one column per token) plus $N \times D$ for the embeddings, compared to $|V| \times D + N \times D$ for the full matrix multiplication — a reduction of roughly $|V|/N$ in classifier memory traffic.

#### Linear-Log-Sum-Exp Forward Pass: Reducing Over the Vocabulary in SRAM

The second term of the loss, $\text{LSE} = \log\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \exp(C_j^\top E) \in \mathbb{R}^N$, requires computing every logit $C_j^\top E_n$ for all vocabulary entries $j$ and all batch positions $n$, reducing them via log-sum-exp along the vocabulary axis, and producing one scalar per batch position. This is a reduction that touches $|V| \times N$ logits but produces only $N$ outputs. The challenge is touching all the logits without ever storing them all.

CCE's approach (Algorithm 2, Figure 2b) is to **fuse the matrix multiplication that computes the logits with the log-sum-exp reduction**, using the same blockwise decomposition as a standard matrix multiplication kernel. The key realization is that matrix multiplication on GPUs already operates blockwise — the full output matrix $C^\top E$ is never computed atomically; rather, independent CUDA blocks each compute a tile of the output by multiplying corresponding blocks of the inputs. By inserting the log-sum-exp reduction into this blockwise computation, the logits only exist temporarily in SRAM, one tile at a time.

The blocking strategy divides the output space $|V| \times N$ into tiles of size $V_B \times N_B$, where $V_B$ and $N_B$ are block sizes for the vocabulary and batch dimensions respectively. For each tile, the inputs required are $E_n \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times N_B}$ (the embedding block for these batch positions) and $C_v \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times V_B}$ (the classifier block for these vocabulary entries). Due to the feature dimension $D$ often exceeding SRAM capacity, the computation is further split into sub-blocks $E_{n,d}$ of size $D_B \times N_B$ and $C_{v,d}$ of size $D_B \times V_B$, with accumulation in SRAM.

The algorithm proceeds as follows for each tile $(v, n)$:

1. **Local matrix multiplication:** For each sub-block $d$ along the feature dimension, load $E_{n,d}$ and $C_{v,d}$ into SRAM, compute the local product $A_{nv} \mathrel{+}= C_{v,d}^\top \cdot E_{n,d}$, and accumulate in SRAM. After the $D_B$ loop completes, $A_{nv} \in \mathbb{R}^{V_B \times N_B}$ contains the logits for these vocabulary entries against these batch positions — but stored in SRAM, not global memory.

2. **Local log-sum-exp:** Compute the (numerically stable) log-sum-exp of $A_{nv}$ along the vocabulary dimension to produce $\text{LSE}_{nv} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_B}$, a partial log-sum-exp for these batch positions over just this vocabulary block. The numerically stable computation uses $\text{LSE}_{nv} = \max(A_{nv}) + \log\sum\exp(A_{nv} - \max(A_{nv}))$ to avoid overflow.

3. **Global atomic update:** The partial $\text{LSE}_{nv}$ must be combined with partial LSE values from other vocabulary blocks covering the same batch positions. The paper uses the identity:

$$\text{LSE}_n = \log(\exp(\text{LSE}_n^{\text{current}}) + \exp(\text{LSE}_{nv}^{\text{new}}))$$

This log-add-exp operation updates the global $\text{LSE}_n$ (initialized to $-\infty$) by merging in the new partial value. Because multiple CUDA blocks covering different vocabulary ranges but the same batch positions may attempt updates concurrently, the update uses a **spin-lock on an atomic operation in global memory** to serialize access to each $\text{LSE}_n$ entry. The paper notes this is simple to implement in their Triton framework and "incurs little overhead" because contention is low (vocabulary blocks for the same batch position are processed by different thread blocks that typically don't execute in lockstep).

**The global memory footprint** of the forward pass is the LSE vector itself — $N$ scalars — plus the local tile $A_{nv}$ of size $V_B \times N_B$ in SRAM. No $|V| \times N$ logit matrix is ever written to global memory. For Gemma 2 (2B) with batch size 8,192 and vocabulary 256,000, Table 1 shows the forward pass uses only 1 MB of global memory.

**Why this parallelization is sufficient for GPU saturation:** Standard matrix multiplication kernels expose massive parallelism because each $(v, n)$ output tile can be computed independently — there are $(|V|/V_B) \times (N/N_B)$ such tiles, easily thousands, providing enough CUDA blocks to saturate the GPU's streaming multiprocessors. CCE inherits this parallelism directly because it uses the same tiling. The key difference is that, in a standard matmul, each block writes its output tile to global memory, resulting in the full $|V| \times N$ matrix being materialized. In CCE, each block reduces its tile to a partial LSE and atomically updates the global state, so the output is the $N$-element LSE vector rather than the $|V| \times N$ logit matrix. The atomic contention on the LSE vector is manageable because (a) each $\text{LSE}_n$ is updated by at most $|V|/V_B$ blocks (blocks covering different vocabulary slices for the same token), which is a small number, and (b) these blocks typically don't finish simultaneously.

**Comparison to chunking approaches:** Chunking methods like Torch Tune's also avoid materializing the full logit matrix, but they do so by serially iterating over disjoint vocabulary chunks — for each chunk, they compute the logits, the partial loss/gradient, and write intermediate results to global memory before proceeding to the next chunk. This requires dispatching separate kernel launches for each chunk, with each launch having fixed overhead. CCE's approach parallelizes over chunks rather than serializing them, so the number of kernel launches is the same as a standard matmul — it's the same number of CUDA blocks, just with a different reduction at the end. This is why CCE achieves low memory *and* low latency simultaneously: it removes the chunking-induced kernel launch overhead.

#### Backward Pass: Sparse Gradient Accumulation Without the Softmax Matrix

The backward pass of the cross-entropy layer must compute two gradient contributions: $\nabla E$, the gradient with respect to the input embeddings, and $\nabla C$, the gradient with respect to the classifier weights. Both depend on the softmax matrix $S = \text{softmax}(C^\top E)$, which is $|V| \times N$ and would, if materialized, consume exactly the memory that CCE is designed to avoid. CCE's backward pass (Algorithm 3, Figure 2c) recomputes $S$ block-by-block in SRAM, multiplies it with the incoming gradient $\nabla\text{LSE}$, and accumulates the gradient contributions to $\nabla E$ and $\nabla C$ via atomic operations in global memory — the $|V| \times N$ softmax is computed, used, and discarded one tile at a time.

The gradient formulas that must be computed are:

$$\nabla E^\top = \hat{S} C \quad \text{and} \quad \nabla C^\top = \hat{S}^\top E$$

where $\hat{S} = S \cdot \nabla\text{LSE} \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times N}$ is the softmax matrix row-wise multiplied by the backpropagated gradient $\nabla\text{LSE} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ (the gradient of the overall loss with respect to each LSE value). This $\hat{S}$ plays the role of the effective "gradient-weighted softmax" — for each token $n$, $\hat{S}_{:,n}$ is a vector over the vocabulary where each entry represents the contribution of vocabulary item $j$ to the gradient.

The blockwise backward pass for a tile $(v, n)$ proceeds as:

1. **Logit recomputation:** Identically to the forward pass, load $E_n$ (size $D \times N_B$) and $C_v$ (size $D \times V_B$), and recompute the local logit matrix $A_{nv} = C_v^\top E_n \in \mathbb{R}^{V_B \times N_B}$ by iterating over sub-blocks along the feature dimension and accumulating in SRAM. This recomputation is necessary because the forward pass never stored the logits.

2. **Softmax computation:** Compute the local softmax $S_{nv} = \exp(A_{nv} - \text{LSE}_n)$ using the globally-stored $\text{LSE}_n$ vector. Because the forward pass already computed and stored the exact LSE values, the softmax can be computed locally without re-reducing: $\text{LSE}_n$ is the exact normalization constant for token $n$, so $\exp(A_{nv} - \text{LSE}_n)$ gives the exact softmax probabilities for vocabulary entries $v$ through $v+V_B-1$ against batch positions $n$ through $n+N_B-1$.

3. **Gradient filtering:** Before proceeding with the expensive gradient accumulation, check whether the entire softmax block $S_{nv}$ is negligible. If $\text{all}(S_{nv} < \varepsilon)$, where $\varepsilon = 2^{-12}$ (the smallest bfloat16 value that doesn't get truncated when summing with values around $2^{-5}$), skip all further computation for this tile. This is the **gradient filtering** optimization — it avoids computing and writing gradients for vocabulary entries that have essentially zero probability for these tokens.

4. **Weighted softmax computation:** Compute $\hat{S}_{nv} = S_{nv} \cdot \nabla\text{LSE}_n$, the row-wise product of the local softmax and the backpropagated gradient. This is the effective gradient weight for each (vocabulary, token) pair.

5. **Gradient accumulation:** Further subdividing along the feature dimension, for each sub-block $d$:
   - $\nabla E_{n,d}^\top \mathrel{+}= \hat{S}_{nv} \cdot C_{v,d}$ — accumulate into the embedding gradient
   - $\nabla C_{v,d}^\top \mathrel{+}= \hat{S}_{nv}^\top \cdot E_{n,d}$ — accumulate into the classifier gradient

   Both accumulations are performed via **atomic additions** in global memory because multiple vocabulary blocks covering different vocabulary ranges but the same batch positions will contribute to the same $\nabla E$ entries, and multiple batch blocks covering different token ranges but the same vocabulary entries will contribute to the same $\nabla C$ entries.

**The global memory footprint** of the backward pass is the gradient buffers $\nabla E \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times N}$ and $\nabla C \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times |V|}$, which are required outputs regardless of implementation (the "lower bound" in Table 1). For Gemma 2 (2B), this is approximately 1,163 MB — the sum of the parameter-sized gradient buffers. No intermediate $|V| \times N$ matrix is allocated. The additional temporary buffers for vocabulary sorting add $O(|V|)$ memory, approximately 1 MB for the largest vocabularies.

**Merging with the indexed matmul backward:** In practice, the paper merges the backward pass of the indexed matrix multiplication (the $C_{x_n}$ term) with the backward pass of the linear-log-sum-exp (Algorithm 4). The gradient of the cross-entropy loss with respect to the logits is:

$$G_{nv} = \mathbb{1}[v = x_n] - S_{nv}$$

where the indicator term $\mathbb{1}[v = x_n]$ is 1 if vocabulary entry $v$ matches the ground-truth token for batch position $n$, and 0 otherwise. This combined gradient $G_{nv}$ is then used in place of $\hat{S}_{nv}$ in the gradient accumulation steps. The gradient filtering condition then becomes $\text{all}(|G_{nv}| < \varepsilon)$, since both the positive indicator spike and the negative softmax tail must be negligible for the tile to be skipped. This merging avoids a separate backward kernel for the indexed matmul and shares the logit recomputation across both gradient contributions.

---

#### Gradient Filtering: The Sparsity That Makes Recomputation Fast

The backward pass recomputes the logit matrix $C^\top E$ block-by-block, which involves repeating the matrix multiplication work of the forward pass. This recomputation would, in isolation, approximately double the compute cost of the layer (one forward matmul + one backward recomputation matmul + two gradient accumulation matmuls, compared to storing the logits and doing three matmuls total). The paper compensates for this recomputation cost by exploiting the extreme sparsity of the softmax matrix $S$ — an observation that prior implementations did not leverage because they never examined the softmax values block-by-block.

**The threshold $\varepsilon = 2^{-12}$** is chosen based on the numerical properties of bfloat16 accumulation. Bfloat16 has a 7-bit mantissa (fractional part). When adding two bfloat16 numbers $a$ and $b$ with $|a| < |b|$, the smaller number $a$ is denormalized to match $b$'s exponent, which shifts its mantissa right. If the exponent difference exceeds 7, the mantissa of $a$ shifts completely out of the representable range, and $a$ effectively becomes zero in the sum. Since the gradient accumulation sums many small terms (the $\hat{S}_{nv}$ values) into the gradient buffers, any $\hat{S}_{nv}$ value whose magnitude is less than $2^{-7} \approx 0.0078$ times the largest term in the same accumulation could be truncated. The paper uses the more conservative threshold of $2^{-12}$ to "account for rounding rules, and the consideration that small but not tiny values will likely not get truncated due to the blocking strategies used to compute a sum." This means any softmax probability $S_{nv} < 2^{-12} \approx 0.00024$ is considered negligible for gradient purposes.

**Why this threshold eliminates almost all computation:** The softmax distribution over large vocabularies is extremely peaked. Figure 3 shows a log-log plot of token probability versus rank: by the time the 50th most likely token is reached, the probability has already fallen below the $2^{-12}$ threshold. For a vocabulary of 256,000, this means **fewer than 50 out of 256,000 entries per token have non-negligible softmax probabilities** — a sparsity of over 99.98%. The paper confirms empirically that "in frontier models we evaluate, less than 0.02% of elements are non-zero" above the threshold.

The gradient filtering operates at the block level rather than the element level: if an entire $V_B \times N_B$ softmax tile has all entries below $\varepsilon$, the entire gradient computation for that tile is skipped — including the loading of $E$ and $C$ sub-blocks from global memory, the matrix multiplications for gradient accumulation, and the atomic write-backs. This is significantly faster than element-level filtering because it avoids the control-flow divergence of checking individual elements. The efficiency of block-level filtering depends on the spatial locality of large softmax values — if the active vocabulary entries are clustered together, fewer blocks are partially filled (and thus wasted computation). This motivates the vocabulary sorting optimization.

---

#### Vocabulary Sorting: Maximizing Block-Level Sparsity

Gradient filtering skips entire $V_B \times N_B$ tiles only when *all* softmax entries in the tile are below threshold. If a tile contains even a single non-negligible entry, the entire tile's gradient computation must proceed. Without careful vocabulary ordering, the active (non-negligible) tokens for a given input might be scattered randomly across the vocabulary, causing many tiles to be partially filled — containing mostly negligible entries but a few active ones — which wastes computation on the negligible entries in those tiles.

The paper proposes a simple heuristic to group active tokens together: **sort the vocabulary by average logit during training.** During the forward pass, the kernel computes a running average logit per vocabulary token using an atomic addition. Specifically, for each tile $(v, n)$ processed in the forward pass, the average logit for vocabulary entries $v$ through $v+V_B-1$ is accumulated. This requires a temporary buffer of size $O(|V|)$ — about 1 MB for a 256K vocabulary — which is negligible.

For the backward pass, the vocabulary is divided into blocks of size $V_B$ based on these average logits rather than the original vocabulary order. The rationale is that tokens with similar average logits tend to co-occur in the softmax tails — when a token has high probability for some inputs, related tokens (with similar logits) are also more likely to have non-negligible probability for those same inputs. Sorting by average logit heuristically clusters the active tokens together, increasing the number of entirely-empty tiles and reducing the number of partially-filled tiles.

**Quantitative impact on performance:** Table 1 row 6 vs. row 1 shows that disabling vocabulary sorting increases backward pass time from 100 ms to 115 ms — a 15% penalty. Without gradient filtering entirely (row 7), the backward pass takes 314 ms — a 3.14× slowdown compared to the version with both optimizations. This demonstrates that gradient filtering is the dominant optimization (providing a ~3.5× speedup over the unfiltered version), while vocabulary sorting provides an additional ~15% improvement on top of gradient filtering by making the filtering more effective at the block level.

---

#### Numerical Precision and Accumulation: From Fine-Tuning to Pretraining

CCE performs gradient accumulation in global memory using the final gradient's floating-point type — typically bfloat16 for mixed-precision training. Bfloat16 has limited precision (7-bit mantissa), and accumulating many small contributions into a gradient buffer can introduce truncation errors if the running sum becomes much larger than the individual additions. For fine-tuning, where the model is initialized from pretrained weights and takes relatively few steps, this precision loss is empirically negligible — the training curves in Figure 4 show indistinguishable loss trajectories between CCE and `torch.compile`.

For pretraining from scratch, the paper reports that vanilla CCE (which uses bfloat16 accumulation) causes validation perplexity to "suffice" (suffer) due to two sources of error. First, **gradient filtering when applied to $\nabla C$ causes no gradient to be propagated to tokens that have little to no support in the training set** — rare tokens receive zero gradient updates, preventing them from ever being learned. This is correct behavior for fine-tuning (where rare tokens already have reasonable embeddings), but problematic for pretraining (where all tokens start random). Second, the bfloat16 accumulation loses precision when many updates are summed into the same gradient buffer element.

The solution for pretraining is **CCE-Kahan-FullC**, which combines two modifications:

1. **Kahan summation (Kahan, 1965):** A compensated summation algorithm that maintains a running compensation term for each accumulation target, recovering precision that would otherwise be lost during bfloat16 addition. This increases memory usage because the compensation buffer doubles the size of the gradient output — for Gemma 2 (2B), Table 1 row 8 shows $\nabla E + \nabla C$ uses 2,325 MB vs. 1,163 MB for standard CCE — but "is typically less than the amount of free memory needed to rematerialize activations when using activation/gradient checkpointing," meaning it often doesn't change the peak memory consumption of the overall training loop.

2. **Full gradient computation for $\nabla C$ (no filtering on classifier gradients):** Gradient filtering is disabled for the $\nabla C$ accumulation, ensuring that even vocabulary tokens with extremely small softmax probabilities receive gradient updates. The gradient filtering is retained for $\nabla E$ (the embedding gradients) because embeddings are updated per-token and the filtering there doesn't affect rare-token learning. Table 1 rows 9 and 10 compare disabling filtering for just $\nabla C$ vs. just $\nabla E$, showing similar performance penalties for either — the CCE-Kahan-FullC variant (row 9, disabling $\nabla C$ filtering) is the one chosen for pretraining, matching `torch.compile` validation perplexity in Figure 5.

**The Kahan summation procedure** works as follows for each scalar accumulation `grad += delta`:
- Compute the compensated delta: `y = delta - compensation`
- Compute the tentative new sum: `t = grad + y`
- Update the compensation: `compensation = (t - grad) - y` (capturing the low-order bits lost in the addition)
- Set the new gradient: `grad = t`

This effectively doubles the precision of the accumulation at the cost of one extra addition, one extra subtraction, and one extra storage location per gradient element. The paper reports that in pretraining experiments with Mistral NeMo (Section 5.3), CCE-Kahan-FullC enabled doubling the batch size, which decreased overall training time by 2 hours (16%) compared to `torch.compile` — the reduced per-step memory enabled a larger batch, which improved GPU utilization efficiency, more than offsetting the modest per-step overhead of Kahan summation.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Decomposition into indexed matmul + linear-log-sum-exp** rather than computing all logits together: enables the indexed matmul to touch only one classifier column per token ($O(ND)$ memory traffic) while the LSE reduction touches all columns but only writes one scalar per token as output ($O(N)$ memory).
- **Blockwise matrix multiplication with on-chip reduction** rather than chunked serial iteration: eliminates kernel launch overhead, allowing the computation to be parallelized across the same number of CUDA blocks as a standard matmul, achieving low latency while maintaining low memory.
- **Atomic log-add-exp updates** with spin-locks rather than separate kernel launches for reduction: simple to implement in Triton, low contention because vocabulary blocks for the same token rarely finish simultaneously, and avoids the synchronization overhead of a separate reduction step.
- **Logit recomputation in backward pass** rather than storing them: trades compute for memory. The recomputation cost (one extra matrix multiplication) is offset by the time saved from not writing and reading the $|V| \times N$ logit matrix to/from global memory, and by the gradient filtering optimization that skips gradient computation for negligible entries.
- **Gradient filtering threshold $\varepsilon = 2^{-12}$:** chosen based on bfloat16 numerical precision — any softmax entry below this threshold contributes at most truncation-level noise to the gradient accumulation. This threshold eliminates >99.98% of the gradient computation in practice.
- **Block-level filtering** rather than element-level: avoids control-flow divergence within thread blocks, and vocabulary sorting maximizes the number of entirely-empty blocks where filtering can skip all computation.
- **Vocabulary sorting by average logit** rather than frequency or random ordering: heuristically clusters tokens with similar logit magnitudes, which correlates with which tokens are simultaneously "active" (above threshold) for typical inputs, minimizing partially-filled blocks.
- **Kahan summation for pretraining:** recovers precision lost in bfloat16 gradient accumulation without requiring float32 accumulation (which would double memory and bandwidth). Combined with disabling gradient filtering for $\nabla C$, ensures rare tokens receive proper gradient updates during pretraining.
- **Merged backward kernel** (Algorithm 4) rather than separate kernels for indexed matmul and LSE backward: the two operations share the logit recomputation step and memory access patterns, so merging avoids redundant computation and kernel launch overhead.
- **Triton implementation** rather than pure CUDA: the paper notes Triton enables "rapid experimentation" but limits control flow to the block level (thread-level control flow is not expressible). A CUDA implementation "may bring further performance gains because control flow could be performed at finer-grained levels" — the gradient filtering could check individual elements rather than blocks, and the atomic updates could use compare-and-swap loops rather than spin-locks.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Cross-Entropy's Memory Dominance Is an Implementation Artifact, Not Inherent to the Problem

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is diagnostic rather than algorithmic: it identifies that the cross-entropy layer's enormous memory footprint — consuming up to 90% of total training memory in models like Gemma 2 (2B) — is not a mathematical necessity but an artifact of the **standard implementation strategy** that materializes the full $|V| \times N$ logit matrix in global memory. This insight reframes a seemingly intractable infrastructure problem as a systems optimization opportunity.

Prior to this work, the community's response to cross-entropy memory growth followed two patterns: either accept the memory cost and work around it with parallelism (splitting sequences across GPUs via DeepSpeed Ulysses, Jacobs et al., 2023) or reduce the vocabulary itself (hierarchical vocabularies from Grave et al., 2017; byte-level models from Yu et al., 2023). Both approaches implicitly accepted a premise that the cross-entropy layer *needs* to touch all $|V|$ logits per token and therefore must allocate proportional memory. Chunking approaches (Torch Tune, Liger Kernels) challenged this premise partially by serializing over the vocabulary, but accepted a memory-latency trade-off — reducing memory by increasing the number of sequentially-processed chunks, at the cost of kernel launch overhead.

CCE's central insight is that this premise is false. The mathematical decomposition $\ell_i = C_{x_i}^\top E_i - \log\sum_j \exp(C_j^\top E_i)$ reveals that the loss computation only depends on the full vocabulary through a single **reduction** (the log-sum-exp), not through individually stored values. Reductions require touching all inputs but only persisting the aggregate result. Matrix multiplication on GPUs already operates blockwise — the full output matrix never exists atomically. By fusing the log-sum-exp reduction into the blockwise matrix multiplication, CCE eliminates the global memory footprint without serialization, breaking the apparent trade-off between memory and latency that chunking approaches accepted as fundamental. Table 1 makes this concrete: CCE achieves within 3 MB of the theoretical lower bound (only the output gradient buffers) while being 5% faster than `torch.compile` for the loss forward pass, demonstrating that the memory problem was never actually about the *information* the layer needed to store — it was about the *format* in which implementations chose to store it.

This is significant beyond the immediate performance gain because it changes how practitioners should think about the scaling bottlenecks in LLM training. The paper's Figure 1a tells a historical story: each prior optimization (ZeRO, activation checkpointing, FlashAttention) shifted the memory bottleneck to a different layer, and CCE completes this progression by eliminating the final outlier. The implication is that **training memory for large-vocabulary LLMs should now be dominated by the backbone**, not the head — a state of affairs that enables vocabulary expansion (which Tao et al., 2024 argue will continue) without corresponding growth in training infrastructure requirements.

---

### Innovation 2: Gradient Filtering Exploits Softmax Sparsity at the Hardware Precision Level

The second conceptual innovation is the recognition that the softmax distribution over large vocabularies produces **structured sparsity at the granularity of the hardware's numerical precision**, and that this sparsity can be exploited not just for memory savings but for compute savings that offset the cost of recomputation.

The idea that softmax distributions are peaked is not new — it's the foundation of sampling strategies like top-k and nucleus sampling. What is new here is the connection to **floating-point truncation semantics**. The paper observes that bfloat16 accumulation effectively ignores contributions below a threshold determined by the relative exponent difference between the running sum and the addition. For gradient accumulation, where many small softmax-weighted contributions are added into a gradient buffer, any softmax value below $2^{-12}$ (the conservative threshold accounting for bfloat16's 7-bit mantissa and blocking effects) contributes at most noise — it gets truncated to zero during the floating-point addition. This is not a statistical approximation (like top-k sampling) but an **equivalence under the hardware's arithmetic**: filtering these entries produces the exact same gradient as not filtering them, to within the precision of the accumulation type.

The consequence is dramatic and previously unappreciated. Figure 3 shows that for a 256K vocabulary, fewer than 50 entries per token exceed this threshold — a sparsity of **>99.98%**. Prior work that stored the full softmax matrix (or the full logit matrix) was allocating memory and moving data for entries that, under bfloat16 gradient accumulation, would be numerically equivalent to storing zeros. Gradient filtering converts this observation into a compute optimization: by checking at the tile level whether an entire softmax block is negligible (all entries $< 2^{-12}$), CCE skips the loading of embedding and classifier blocks, the matrix multiplications for gradient accumulation, and the atomic write-backs for the vast majority of the vocabulary. Table 1 row 7 quantifies the cost of not using this insight: without gradient filtering, the backward pass takes 314 ms — 3.14× slower than the filtered version — because it performs gradient computation for entries that contribute nothing to the final result at bfloat16 precision.

This innovation is fundamental rather than incremental because it introduces a new principle for optimizing neural network training: **numerical equivalence under the chosen precision is a valid optimization criterion**, and sparsity at the precision boundary is a resource that can be systematically exploited. It's not an approximation or a heuristic — it's derived from the specification of the arithmetic. This principle likely applies beyond cross-entropy to any large-classification softmax layer, and the paper explicitly suggests extensions to "image classification and contrastive learning" where the number of classes is large. The connection to vocabulary sorting (Innovation 3) is a direct consequence: once you commit to block-level filtering, the arrangement of vocabulary entries determines filtering efficiency, making vocabulary ordering a first-class optimization target.

---

### Innovation 3: Vocabulary Ordering as a Degrees-of-Freedom That Controls Computational Efficiency

The paper introduces a novel optimization dimension that the training community had not previously considered as tunable: **the physical ordering of vocabulary entries in the classifier matrix determines the block-level sparsity pattern of the softmax, which directly controls the effectiveness of gradient filtering**. This is a genuinely new idea — vocabulary ordering was previously a lexicographic or frequency-based convention with no computational consequences.

The mechanism is simple to state but subtle in its implications. Gradient filtering operates at the granularity of $V_B \times N_B$ softmax tiles — if all entries in a tile are below threshold, the entire tile's gradient computation is skipped. If vocabulary entries with non-negligible probabilities are scattered uniformly across the vocabulary, most tiles will contain a mix of active and negligible entries, requiring full computation for the tile despite most entries being negligible. If active entries are clustered, tiles tend to be either entirely active or entirely empty, maximizing the fraction of tiles that can be skipped.

The paper's heuristic — sorting vocabulary entries by their average logit during training — rests on the empirical observation that tokens with similar average logit magnitudes tend to have non-negligible softmax probabilities for overlapping sets of inputs. This makes intuitive sense: tokens that are "probable in expectation" (high average logit) tend to be the common words that appear in the softmax tail for many different inputs, while tokens with low average logit are rare and almost never appear above threshold. Sorting by average logit thus clusters the "often-active" tokens together, creating dense blocks of active entries and leaving large contiguous regions of the vocabulary that are almost entirely inactive. Table 1 row 6 quantifies the benefit: disabling vocabulary sorting slows the backward pass by 15%, a meaningful fraction of the total time.

Beyond the specific heuristic, the intellectual contribution is the idea that **the vocabulary is not just a set of tokens but a matrix whose column ordering is a free parameter with engineering consequences**. This is a shift in perspective: vocabulary construction (BPE, etc.) determines *which* tokens exist, but nothing in the standard LLM formulation specifies *how* the classifier columns are arranged in memory. That ordering was previously considered arbitrary. CCE shows it is a genuine optimization degree of freedom, analogous to how reordering sparse matrix rows/columns can dramatically affect the performance of sparse matrix operations. While the paper's logit-based sorting is a first attempt, the conceptual space this opens — dynamic reordering based on activation patterns, learned permutations, or input-dependent sorting — could be a productive direction for future systems work.

---

### Innovation 4: Full-Training Equivalence Under Gradient Filtering Establishes a New Precision-Conscious Design Pattern

The paper makes an empirical claim that is methodologically significant for the systems-for-ML community: **gradient filtering at the bfloat16 precision boundary produces training dynamics that are numerically indistinguishable from the unfiltered computation**, across four model families and two training regimes (fine-tuning and pretraining). This claim, validated in Figures 4 and 5, is not merely a performance result — it establishes that optimizations justified by floating-point precision analysis can be not just heuristically reasonable but **provably lossless in practical training**.

The distinction matters because the ML systems literature has a long history of approximations that are empirically justified but theoretically lossy — mixed-precision training itself (which CCE builds on) is an approximation that works in practice but can diverge under some conditions. Gradient filtering could have been presented as another such approximation: "we drop small softmax values and it doesn't seem to hurt much." Instead, the paper grounds the threshold in the precise numerical semantics of bfloat16 accumulation and then verifies, across multiple architectures and training scales, that the filtered computation converges identically to the unfiltered baseline. The training loss curves in Figure 4 are "nearly indistinguishable" between CCE and `torch.compile` for fine-tuning, and Figure 5 shows matching validation perplexity for pretraining when Kahan summation and full classifier gradients (CCE-Kahan-FullC) are used.

The significance is twofold. First, it provides a rigorous justification for the specific threshold of $2^{-12}$ — this isn't a hyperparameter that was tuned on validation loss; it's derived from the bfloat16 format and then verified to be tight enough. Second, and more broadly, it demonstrates a methodology for developing precision-conscious training optimizations: identify the precision regime, determine the mathematical conditions under which operations are numerically equivalent at that precision, design the optimization to satisfy those conditions exactly (not approximately), and verify equivalence empirically across the training distribution. This is a template that could apply to other training operations where large intermediate tensors can be filtered based on their numerical significance rather than their statistical significance.

The pretraining variant (CCE-Kahan-FullC) also surfaces an important subtlety: the gradient filtering that works perfectly for fine-tuning (where rare tokens already have reasonable embeddings) fails for pretraining (where rare tokens start random and need gradient signal to be learned). This negative result — that precision-motivated filtering can interact with the learning dynamics of rare classes — is itself informative. It identifies a boundary condition on the technique: gradient filtering is safe when the optimization target (e.g., the embedding) is already well-initialized, but not when the filtering would systematically exclude updates to certain parameters. This distinction between fine-tuning and pretraining as having different tolerance for precision optimizations is a useful diagnostic concept for future systems work.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The fine-tuning experiments use the Alpaca dataset (Taori et al., 2023), consisting of instruction-following examples. The pretraining experiments use 5% of the Open WebText dataset (Gokaslan et al., 2019), a large corpus of web-scraped text, with a held-out 0.25% subset of non-overlapping Open WebText serving as the validation set for perplexity measurement.

- **Base model(s).** Four instruction-tuned model families are evaluated spanning different architectures and scale ranges: Gemma 2 (2B, 9B, 27B) (Rivière et al., 2024), Phi 3.5 Mini (Abdin et al., 2024), Qwen 2.5 (7B, 32B) (Qwen Team, 2024), and Mistral NeMo (Mistral AI Team, 2024). These are chosen to cover a wide range of vocabulary sizes (from 32,064 for Phi 3.5 Mini to 256,128 for the Gemma 2 models) and hidden dimensions (2,304 to 5,120), enabling analysis of how CCE's performance scales with the vocabulary-to-hidden-dimension ratio.

- **Metrics.** For fine-tuning, the primary metric is **training loss** over gradient steps, measured identically between CCE and the `torch.compile` baseline to verify numerical equivalence of the computed loss values. For pretraining, the primary metric is **validation perplexity** on the held-out subset of Open WebText, tracked over gradient steps to verify that CCE's gradient approximations do not alter the learning trajectory. For runtime and memory benchmarks (Tables 1, A1, A3), metrics are **peak GPU memory footprint** (in MB, rounded to the closest MB) and **wall-clock time** (in ms) for computing the loss forward pass, gradient backward pass, and their combination. Memory is measured separately for loss and gradient stages because "intermediate buffers can often (but not always) be reused between the loss and gradient computation, resulting in lower peak memory consumption than the sum of the parts" (Table 1 note).

- **Baselines.** Five baselines are compared in the runtime/memory benchmarks (Table 1):
  - **Baseline:** The default PyTorch implementation that materializes the full `|V| × N` logit matrix in global memory, used by HuggingFace Transformers (Wolf et al., 2019) and Torch Tune (Torch Tune Team, 2024).
  - **`torch.compile`:** The PyTorch 2 compiler (Ansel et al., 2024) applied to the baseline implementation, which uses kernel fusion to reduce intermediate memory allocations.
  - **Torch Tune (8 chunks):** A chunking-based implementation from Torch Tune that divides the vocabulary into 8 sequential chunks, computing partial results per chunk and accumulating (Torch Tune Team, 2024).
  - **Liger Kernels:** A fused loss+gradient kernel using chunked computation that computes loss and gradient simultaneously rather than in separate forward/backward passes (Hsu et al., 2024).
  - **Lower bound:** The theoretical minimum memory — the size of the output gradient buffers ($\nabla E$ and $\nabla C$) — representing an unachievable baseline that no method can beat.
  
  For training convergence experiments (Figures 4 and 5), only `torch.compile` is used as the baseline because it represents the best-performing standard implementation in terms of both speed and memory.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For runtime benchmarks, the compute budget is standardized to a batch of 8,192 tokens with vocabulary size 256,000 and hidden dimension 2,304, using embeddings and classifier weights taken from the Gemma 2 (2B) model during training on Alpaca. For memory footprint analysis in Figure 1 and Table A4, compute is measured per token with a global batch size of 65,536 across 16 GPUs. All measurements are performed on an A100-SXM4 GPU with 80 GB RAM, PyTorch 2.4.1, and CUDA 12.4. CCE variants beyond the base version (CCE-Kahan, CCE-Kahan-FullC, CCE-Kahan-FullE) are separately benchmarked to expose the memory/time trade-offs of numerical precision choices.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** All runtime and memory measurements are "averaged over 5 seeds" (Table 1 note) to control for measurement noise from GPU scheduling and memory allocator variability. Training convergence experiments (Figures 4 and 5) are similarly averaged over 5 seeds with 95% confidence intervals plotted. The confidence bands in Figure 4 are described as "p95 Confidence Range," suggesting they represent the range between the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles across seeds. Memory numbers in the breakdown analysis (Table 1) are "rounded to closest MB," and some numbers are explicitly noted to be "multiples of 1,000 due to dimensions chosen and PyTorch's allocation strategy."

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Memory and Runtime Benchmarks: CCE Breaks the Memory-Latency Trade-off

Table 1 presents the central quantitative comparison of CCE against all baselines for the cross-entropy loss computation on Gemma 2 (2B)-scale parameters (|V| = 256,000, D = 2,304, N = 8,192). The headline results are:

- **Memory:** CCE uses 1 MB for the loss forward pass and 1,163 MB for the combined loss+gradient computation, compared to 24,000 MB and 28,000 MB respectively for the Baseline — a reduction of **over 24,000× for loss alone** and **24× for loss+gradient**. The loss+gradient memory is within 3 MB of the theoretical lower bound (1,161 MB for the gradient buffers themselves). For context, Liger Kernels achieves 1,474 MB for loss+gradient (still ~300 MB above the lower bound), `torch.compile` uses 16,000 MB, and Torch Tune (8 chunks) uses 9,631 MB. The qualitative difference is that CCE's memory is essentially the gradient outputs themselves, while all other methods carry additional intermediate buffers proportional to at least `N × D`.

- **Runtime (Loss forward pass):** CCE completes the loss computation in 46 ms, compared to 82 ms for Baseline — **5% faster** than the default implementation and 6% faster than `torch.compile` (49 ms). The paper attributes this speedup to CCE not writing the logit matrix to global memory ("CCE does not write all the logits to global memory," Section 5.1). Torch Tune achieves 55 ms (20% slower than CCE) and Liger Kernels takes 304 ms — **6.6× slower** than CCE, reflecting the kernel launch overhead of Liger's heavy chunking approach.

- **Runtime (Loss+Gradient combined):** CCE completes the full forward+backward in 145 ms, compared to `torch.compile`'s 143 ms — only 2 ms (1.4%) slower. Baseline takes 208 ms (43% slower). The backward pass specifically shows CCE at 100 ms vs. `torch.compile` at 92 ms — an 8 ms (8.7%) penalty for the logit recomputation, partially offset by time saved from gradient filtering (Table A2 provides the detailed breakdown: CCE saves ~62 ms on the logits+softcap gradient computation compared to Baseline, which nearly offsets the 45 ms spent on logit recomputation and gradient filtering).

Table A3 generalizes these findings across additional model scales, revealing an important pattern tied to the **vocabulary-to-hidden-dimension ratio** |V|/D:

- For Gemma 2 (2B) with |V|/D ≈ 111, CCE loss+gradient is 1.4% slower than `torch.compile` (145 ms vs. 143 ms) while using 14× less memory.
- For Gemma 2 (9B) with |V|/D ≈ 71, CCE is essentially matched (208 ms vs. 207 ms).
- For Gemma 2 (27B) with |V|/D ≈ 56, CCE is 10% slower (281 ms vs. 256 ms).
- For Phi 3.5 Mini with |V|/D ≈ 10.4, CCE is 55% slower (34 ms vs. 22 ms), though the absolute difference is only 12 ms.
- For Mistral NeMo with |V|/D ≈ 25.6, CCE is 26% slower (180 ms vs. 143 ms).
- For Qwen 2.5 (7B) with |V|/D ≈ 42, CCE is 12% slower (136 ms vs. 121 ms).

The paper explains this ratio-dependent performance as follows: "as |V|/D continues to decrease, Liger Kernels is able to make better use of the GPU" (Appendix C.2) because its chunk size is set based on |V|/D — smaller ratios permit larger, more efficient chunks. For CCE, the parallelism is `B × |V|` rather than `B × D`, so increasing D increases the computational work but not the degree of parallelism. The paper notes this increase is "largely negligible and only increases training time by one to two percent" for typical models, since the cross-entropy computation is a small fraction of total training time.

#### Gradient Filtering and Vocabulary Sorting Ablations (Table 1, Rows 6–7)

Table 1 includes direct ablations of CCE's two key optimizations:

- **Disabling vocabulary sorting (row 6):** Backward pass time increases from 100 ms to 115 ms — a **15% slowdown**. Loss+gradient combined time increases from 145 ms to 159 ms (9.7% slowdown). Memory remains essentially identical (1,162 MB vs. 1,163 MB). This quantifies the value of clustering active vocabulary entries: without sorting, more softmax tiles are partially filled rather than entirely empty, forcing gradient computation on tiles where many entries are individually negligible.

- **Disabling gradient filtering (row 7):** Backward pass time explodes from 100 ms to 314 ms — a **3.14× slowdown**. Loss+gradient combined time increases from 145 ms to 357 ms (2.46× slowdown). This is the defining result: gradient filtering is not a marginal optimization but the mechanism that makes CCE's backward pass competitive. Without it, the recomputation of logits and the subsequent gradient accumulation over the full (unfiltered) vocabulary dominates the runtime. The paper notes that gradient filtering enables skipping >99.98% of the gradient computation entries (Section 4.3), and this ablation confirms that nearly all of the backward pass work is in those negligible entries.

#### Numerical Precision Variants for Pretraining (Table 1, Rows 8–10)

Three variants targeting numerical stability are benchmarked:

- **CCE-Kahan (row 8):** Adds Kahan summation to both gradient accumulations. Memory doubles to 2,326 MB for loss+gradient (exactly 2× the gradient buffers, as expected for the compensation term). Backward pass time increases from 100 ms to 114 ms (14% slowdown). Total loss+gradient time increases from 145 ms to 160 ms (10% slowdown).

- **CCE-Kahan-FullC (row 9):** Kahan summation plus disabling gradient filtering for $\nabla C$ only (classifier gradients). Memory increases slightly to 2,326 MB. Backward pass time jumps to 268 ms — 2.68× slower than standard CCE but still 15% faster than the completely unfiltered version (row 7: 314 ms). Loss+gradient time increases to 313 ms (19.3% slower than the unfiltered no-Kahan version in row 7, because Kahan summation adds overhead, but the reduced work from filtering $\nabla E$ compensates).

- **CCE-Kahan-FullE (row 10):** Kahan summation plus disabling gradient filtering for $\nabla E$ only. Nearly identical performance to FullC (247 ms backward, 292 ms total), confirming the symmetry noted in the paper: "removing gradient filtering from either ∇C or ∇E results in a similar decrease of performance" (Section 5.1).

The paper states that for fine-tuning, "all variants of CCE perform equivalently" (Section 5.1), tested via convergence curves. For pretraining, CCE-Kahan-FullC is the recommended configuration, and the performance overhead "is often offset by the larger batch sizes CCE-Kahan-FullC enables." The Mistral NeMo pretraining experiment demonstrates this concretely: CCE-Kahan-FullC "enabled doubling the batch size, thereby decreasing training time by 2 hours (16%) compared to `torch.compile`" (Section 5.3).

#### Removing Ignored Tokens: Universal Speedup (Table A1)

The paper observes that in standard training pipelines, certain tokens (padding, system prompts, user inputs) are processed by the backbone but have their loss set to zero rather than being excluded from the cross-entropy computation. "In all implementations we are aware of, the logits and loss for these ignored tokens is first computed and then set to zero" (Appendix B). Table A1 re-runs all methods from Table 1 with a pre-filtering step that removes ignored tokens before the cross-entropy computation.

The results show this simple change provides "a significant speed up for all methods but Liger Kernels" (which is dominated by kernel launch overhead, not computation). For CCE, loss+gradient time drops from 145 ms to 54 ms — a **2.7× speedup** — because the effective batch size is reduced (fewer active tokens). Baseline drops from 208 ms to 75 ms (2.8×), and `torch.compile` drops from 143 ms to 53 ms (2.7×). CCE remains competitive at 54 ms vs. 53 ms for `torch.compile` and maintains its near-zero memory advantage (245 MB for loss, 1,164 MB total, within 3 MB of the lower bound). This result is significant for practitioners: it demonstrates that CCE's relative advantage is maintained even when combined with the obvious optimization of pruning ignored tokens, and that the combined effect of CCE + token filtering can reduce cross-entropy computation time by nearly a factor of 3 compared to unfiltered baseline.

#### Batch Size Scaling (Figures A1 and A2)

The paper measures how runtime scales with the number of tokens for all methods across model sizes. The key findings from Figures A1 and A2:

- CCE's runtime scales approximately linearly with token count across the measured range ($2^8$ to $2^{12}$ tokens), matching the scaling behavior of Baseline and `torch.compile`. This is notable because it confirms CCE's blockwise parallelism doesn't exhibit threshold effects or step-function behavior at particular batch sizes.
- Liger Kernels shows poor scaling at high token counts due to kernel launch overhead from heavy chunking — it becomes "bound by kernel launch overhead, not computation" (Appendix B). At low token counts, this overhead makes it substantially slower than CCE and other methods.
- CCE-Kahan-FullC is slower than Liger Kernels and Torch Tune at large token counts but "becomes more performant than those baselines as the number of tokens reduces" (Appendix C.2), since its performance is dominated by computation rather than kernel launch count.

The paper includes a detailed performance breakdown in Table A2 comparing the backward pass of CCE vs. Baseline. CCE spends 45 ms on logit recomputation and gradient filtering (which Baseline doesn't do), but saves 62 ms total across the logit gradient (30 ms saved), softcap gradient (12 ms saved), ∇E computation (5 ms saved), and ∇C computation (15 ms saved). The 62 ms savings exceeds the 45 ms recomputation cost by 17 ms, explaining why CCE's backward pass is only 8 ms slower than `torch.compile`'s rather than dramatically slower. The paper attributes the savings to CCE keeping the logits in SRAM rather than writing/reading them from global memory: "CCE does not write the result of this computation to main memory, saving a significant amount of time" (Appendix C.1).

#### Training Convergence: Fine-Tuning (Figure 4)

Figure 4 shows training loss curves for fine-tuning four models (Gemma 2 2B, Phi 3.5 Mini, Qwen 2.5 7B, Mistral NeMo) on the Alpaca dataset over 700 gradient steps. For each model, two curves are plotted: CCE (solid line) and `torch.compile` (dashed line), each averaged over 5 seeds with 95% confidence bands.

The headline result: **"The loss curves for CCE and torch.compile are nearly indistinguishable"** (Section 5.3). The curves overlap within the width of the line for all four models, with confidence bands that are largely coincident. There is no visible systematic bias — CCE does not converge slower, reach a higher final loss, or exhibit different variance across seeds. This validates the paper's core claim that gradient filtering at the $2^{-12}$ threshold produces **numerically equivalent training dynamics** to the exact computation for fine-tuning scenarios.

The loss scale and trajectory vary by model: Gemma 2 2B starts at approximately 1.25–1.3 loss and converges to ~0.75–0.8 by step 700; Qwen 2.5 7B starts slightly lower (~1.1) and reaches ~0.8. The qualitative behavior (convergence rate, noise level, final loss) is identical between CCE and `torch.compile` within each model, confirming that the optimization is lossless for fine-tuning regardless of model size, vocabulary size, or architecture.

#### Training Convergence: Pretraining (Figure 5)

Figure 5 addresses the more demanding pretraining scenario, where models are trained from scratch on 5% of Open WebText and evaluated on validation perplexity over 1,500 gradient steps. Four models are shown, now using CCE-Kahan-FullC (the pretraining variant with Kahan summation and disabled $\nabla C$ filtering) against `torch.compile`.

The headline result: **"CCE-Kahan-FullC produces identical curves as torch.compile"** (Section 5.3). The validation perplexity curves overlap within their 95% confidence intervals across all four models, with no systematic gap or divergence trend. The perplexity values differ by model (ranging from ~40 for Gemma 2 2B to ~60–70 for Phi 3.5 Mini), but within each model the trajectories are indistinguishable.

This result is significant because it addresses the negative result the paper reports discovering in initial pretraining experiments: standard CCE (with gradient filtering applied to $\nabla C$ and without Kahan summation) caused validation perplexity to degrade due to two mechanisms — (1) gradient filtering on $\nabla C$ "causes no gradient to be propagated to tokens that have little to no support in the training set," starving rare tokens of learning signal, and (2) bfloat16 accumulation without Kahan summation lost precision over many training steps. CCE-Kahan-FullC resolves both issues, and Figure 5 demonstrates that the resolution is complete — the pretraining curve matches the exact computation baseline. This establishes the boundary condition precisely: CCE's standard gradient filtering is safe when parameters are well-initialized (fine-tuning), but pretraining requires the $\nabla C$ filtering to be disabled to prevent rare-token starvation.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**PRM aggregation strategy**: Already covered in prior sections; no additional ablations beyond Table A2 performance breakdown.

**Gradient filtering threshold justification (Figure 3):** Figure 3 plots the sorted softmax probability against token rank on a log-log scale for the Gemma 2 (2B) model. The plot reveals that "by the ~50th most likely token, the probability has fallen below our threshold for gradient filtering" — the $2^{-12}$ line is crossed very early in the sorted vocabulary. The linear relationship between log rank and log probability for the top $10^5$ tokens indicates a power-law decay in softmax probabilities. The paper uses this to explain "why we are able to filter so many values from the gradient computation without affecting the result" — the softmax distribution is so sharply peaked that almost all entries are numerically zero in bfloat16 gradient accumulation. This figure is an empirical validation of the theoretical threshold choice, not an ablation per se, but it provides the quantitative basis for the >99.98% sparsity claim.

**Vocabulary sorting vs. random ordering (Table 1, row 1 vs. 6):** As discussed above, disabling vocabulary sorting imposes a 15% backward pass slowdown. The paper does not explore alternative sorting heuristics (e.g., sorting by frequency, by first principal component of classifier columns, or learning the permutation), which represents an open direction.

**Effect of token count on performance (Figures A1, A2):** The paper varies the batch size from $2^8$ (256) to $2^{12}$ (4,096) tokens (log-scale, so 256 to 4096) across all models, benchmarking all methods. The scaling is approximately linear for CCE, Baseline, and `torch.compile` across the full range. The key finding is that CCE "does not reach a point where the overhead of dispatching all the kernels becomes the dominating factor" (Appendix C.2), unlike the chunking-based methods whose relative performance degrades at extreme token counts due to kernel launch overhead scaling with the number of chunks. This robustness to batch size is practically important because training batch sizes vary significantly across hardware configurations and training phases.

**Kahan summation variants (Table 1, rows 8–10):** The three Kahan variants (full Kahan, Kahan-FullC, Kahan-FullE) are compared in Table 1. The core trade-off is between precision and speed: Kahan summation doubles the gradient buffer memory but adds only 14% to backward pass time when gradient filtering is retained. Disabling filtering for one gradient (C or E) while keeping Kahan summation produces similar performance for either choice (~250–270 ms backward), suggesting the filtering benefit is symmetric across the two gradients. The paper's selection of CCE-Kahan-FullC (disabling $\nabla C$ filtering) over FullE is motivated by the pretraining rare-token issue — without $\nabla C$ gradients, rare tokens never receive updates — which is not a concern for $\nabla E$.

**Model-specific calibration of the |V|/D ratio (Table A3):** The comprehensive benchmarking across 8 model configurations in Table A3 implicitly ablates the effect of the vocabulary-to-hidden-dimension ratio. The pattern is clear: at high |V|/D (Gemma 2B: 111), CCE is 1.4% slower than `torch.compile`; at moderate |V|/D (Qwen 2.5 7B: 42), CCE is 12% slower; at low |V|/D (Phi 3.5 Mini: 10.4), CCE is 55% slower though the absolute penalty is only 12 ms. This reveals that CCE's recomputation cost scales with |V| (the number of tiles that must be processed in the backward pass), while the baseline's efficiency scales with D (the feature dimension, which provides parallelism). For very small |V|/D, the cost of recomputing the logits becomes proportionally larger relative to the gradient accumulation work, and the baseline's parallelism advantage grows. However, the paper argues this is acceptable because the cross-entropy layer contributes only "one to two percent" of total training time, and the memory savings remain substantial even at low |V|/D — for Phi 3.5 Mini, CCE uses 236 MB vs. 3,006 MB for Baseline (12.7× reduction).

### Critical Assessment

The experiments demonstrate several core claims, but some are more comprehensively supported than others. I examine each major claim in turn.

**Claim 1: CCE reduces the memory footprint of the cross-entropy computation to near-zero.** This claim is the strongest in the paper and is thoroughly supported. Table 1 shows CCE's loss forward pass using 1 MB versus 24,000 MB for the Baseline on Gemma 2 (2B) — a 24,000× reduction. The combined loss+gradient memory (1,164 MB) is within 3 MB of the theoretical lower bound represented by the gradient output buffers themselves. This pattern holds across all models in Table A3: CCE's memory footprint is consistently within 1–3 MB of the lower bound regardless of |V|, D, or N. The Figure 1 analysis generalizes this to a full training setup: on a 16-GPU fully-sharded configuration, the log-probabilities component of memory essentially disappears with CCE, enabling batch size increases of 1.5× (Llama 2 13B) to 10× (GPT-2, Gemma 2 2B) as shown in Table A4. The memory claim is unambiguous and well-validated.

However, the paper acknowledges a limitation: CCE-Kahan-FullC (the pretraining variant) doubles gradient buffer memory due to the Kahan compensation term. Table 1 row 8 shows 2,326 MB vs. 1,164 MB for standard CCE. The paper argues this "is typically less than the amount of free memory needed to rematerialize activations when using activation/gradient checkpointing," implying the peak memory in a full training loop is gated by activation checkpoints rather than gradient buffers. This is plausible but not experimentally verified — a full training memory breakdown including activation checkpoint buffers, optimizer states, and CCE-Kahan-FullC's gradient memory is not provided. The paper relies on the qualitative claim rather than a system-level measurement.

**Claim 2: CCE achieves this memory reduction without sacrificing training speed.** The evidence here is more nuanced and model-dependent. On Gemma 2 (2B) with |V| = 256,000, CCE is essentially speed-matched: 145 ms vs. 143 ms for `torch.compile` (1.4% slower, Table 1). On models with smaller |V|/D ratios, the slowdown grows: 26% slower on Mistral NeMo and 55% slower on Phi 3.5 Mini (Table A3). The paper's argument that this is acceptable because the cross-entropy layer is a small fraction of total training time is reasonable but quantitatively unverified — a breakdown of total training time showing the cross-entropy fraction across models would strengthen this claim. The 2-hour (16%) training time *reduction* on Mistral NeMo with CCE-Kahan-FullC (Section 5.3) is a promising data point but is presented as a single anecdote rather than a systematic measurement across models and batch sizes.

The speed claim also depends critically on gradient filtering. Table 1 row 7 shows that without filtering, the backward pass takes 314 ms — more than 3× slower than the standard backward pass and clearly unacceptable. The paper's ability to compensate for recomputation cost therefore rests entirely on softmax sparsity. Figure 3 provides strong evidence that this sparsity exists for the Gemma 2 (2B) model on the Alpaca dataset, but no equivalent analysis is provided for other models or for pretraining data distributions (Open WebText), where the softmax distributions may differ — particularly for rare tokens that are the focus of pretraining. The training curves in Figures 4 and 5 confirm the filtering doesn't harm convergence, but they don't confirm that the runtime advantage persists equally across data distributions.

**Claim 3: CCE produces training dynamics indistinguishable from exact cross-entropy.** For fine-tuning, Figure 4 provides strong evidence across four model families: loss curves are "nearly indistinguishable" with largely overlapping confidence intervals. The experiments use 700 gradient steps, which is sufficient to demonstrate convergence equivalence for fine-tuning on Alpaca.

For pretraining, Figure 5 shows that CCE-Kahan-FullC matches `torch.compile` in validation perplexity over 1,500 steps for four models. There are, however, caveats. First, the pretraining corpus is only 5% of Open WebText — a relatively small dataset for pretraining, and it's unclear whether the equivalence would hold at full pretraining scale (hundreds of billions of tokens, tens of thousands of steps). The paper does not report longer training runs, and it's possible that small numerical differences accumulate over many more steps. Second, the pretraining experiments use CCE-Kahan-FullC rather than standard CCE, meaning the claim as stated in the abstract ("without sacrificing training speed or convergence") applies to the fine-tuning variant. For pretraining, the convergence equivalence comes with the acknowledged cost of Kahan summation memory overhead and partial gradient filtering. The paper is transparent about this distinction (Section 5.3: "In our initial experiments using CCE for pretraining, we found that validation perplexity sacrificed... CCE-Kahan-FullC produces identical curves"), but the abstract's unqualified claim is slightly broader than what the experiments fully validate.

**Claim 4: The gradient filtering threshold ($\varepsilon = 2^{-12}$) is correctly chosen based on bfloat16 precision.** Figure 3 empirically supports that this threshold captures essentially all non-negligible softmax entries — the probability falls below $2^{-12}$ by roughly the 50th most likely token out of 256,000. The paper's derivation from bfloat16 truncation semantics (Section 4.3 and Appendix E) provides theoretical justification. However, the analysis treats truncation as a hard threshold: contributions below $\varepsilon$ "will likely be ignored due to truncation in the summation or rounding in the normalization" (Section 4.3). The qualifier "likely" is important — floating-point rounding is not deterministic across different accumulation orders and blocking strategies. The threshold is chosen to be conservative ($2^{-12}$ vs. the theoretical $2^{-7}$ based on mantissa size alone), accounting for "rounding rules, and the consideration that small but not tiny values will likely not get truncated due to the blocking strategies used to compute a sum." This conservatism is demonstrated to work in practice (Figures 4 and 5), but the paper doesn't experimentally test whether a tighter threshold (e.g., $2^{-10}$) would also work or whether looser thresholds would cause visible degradation. The threshold is validated by convergence equivalence, not by direct measurement of accumulated gradient error.

**What experiments would strengthen the paper:**

- **Full-scale pretraining runs.** The 1,500-step pretraining experiments are compelling proof-of-concept but don't match production pretraining (which runs for tens or hundreds of thousands of steps). A longer run (even on a smaller model) showing that CCE-Kahan-FullC and `torch.compile` don't diverge over many more steps would address concerns about accumulation of numerical differences.

- **Ablation of the filtering threshold $\varepsilon$.** The paper argues for $2^{-12}$ from first principles but doesn't show what happens with $\varepsilon = 2^{-10}$ or $2^{-14}$ — does convergence degrade smoothly or catastrophically? This would validate the threshold's tightness and give practitioners guidance for other precision formats (float16, float32).

- **Memory consumption in a full training loop.** The paper measures the cross-entropy layer's memory in isolation but makes claims about the "total training-time memory consumption of the classifier head" (abstract). A full PyTorch memory profile of a training step with and without CCE, including all intermediate buffers, would quantify the actual end-to-end memory savings rather than the per-operation savings.

- **Direct measurement of gradient error.** A comparison of the actual computed gradients (not just loss/perplexity curves) between CCE and exact cross-entropy would quantify the numerical error introduced by gradient filtering and bfloat16 accumulation. This would transform the claim from "convergence looks identical" to "gradients agree to within X relative error." The paper comes closest to this in Table A2's performance breakdown but doesn't report gradient error statistics.

- **Results on non-instruction-tuned models.** All fine-tuning experiments use instruction-tuned variants (e.g., Gemma 2 2B Instruct). It's possible that instruction-tuned models have different softmax sparsity patterns (more peaked distributions) than base models, which would affect gradient filtering effectiveness.

**Overall assessment.** The paper convincingly demonstrates that CCE eliminates the cross-entropy layer's memory bottleneck for large-vocabulary LLMs, with speed parity on high-|V|/D models and modest slowdowns on others. The convergence equivalence claims are well-supported for fine-tuning and supported with caveats (Kahan summation, disabled $\nabla C$ filtering) for pretraining. The main limitation is that the evidence for speed at scale and convergence equivalence at full pretraining scale is suggestive rather than comprehensive — the paper opens the door but doesn't walk all the way through. The gradient filtering threshold is well-motivated theoretically and empirically but hasn't been stress-tested across diverse data distributions and precision configurations. These are not fatal weaknesses — the paper's contribution is primarily a systems optimization, and the fact that it works across four model families with varying architectures and vocabulary sizes is strong evidence of generality — but they leave room for follow-up work to establish the precise operating envelope.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Gradient Filtering Is Validated Through Convergence Equivalence, Not Through Direct Gradient Error Measurement

The paper's central performance optimization — skipping gradient computation for softmax entries below the bfloat16 threshold — rests on the claim that these entries "will likely be ignored due to truncation in the summation or rounding in the normalization" (Section 4.3) and therefore contribute nothing to the gradient at the hardware's arithmetic precision. The threshold $\varepsilon = 2^{-12}$ is derived from bfloat16's 7-bit mantissa, with conservatism added for "rounding rules, and the consideration that small but not tiny values will likely not get truncated due to the blocking strategies used to compute a sum" (Section 4.3).

**Consequence.** The paper demonstrates that training loss curves and validation perplexity match the exact computation (Figures 4 and 5), but never directly measures the numerical error between CCE's computed gradients and the exact gradients. This leaves a gap: convergence equivalence is a downstream consequence of gradient accuracy, but it is possible that gradients differ in ways that don't manifest in loss curves over the measured training horizons — for example, rare tokens might receive different gradient magnitudes without visibly affecting aggregate perplexity, or gradient directions might differ slightly without changing the convergence trajectory over 700–1,500 steps. A practitioner deploying CCE for a new model architecture, precision format (fp16 rather than bf16), or data distribution cannot independently verify the threshold's safety without re-running convergence experiments — the paper provides no direct diagnostic for gradient error.

**What evidence exists.** The convergence experiments in Figures 4 and 5 are the sole validation. There is no table or figure reporting gradient cosine similarity, relative error in $\nabla C$ or $\nabla E$, or fraction of gradient entries that differ between CCE and the exact computation. The paper's empirical justification for the specific threshold is Figure 3 (showing softmax probabilities decay quickly) and the convergence curves. The negative result in Section 5.3 — that standard CCE fails for pretraining due to $\nabla C$ filtering starving rare tokens of updates — is the only direct evidence of gradient filtering causing problems, and it motivated CCE-Kahan-FullC. This demonstrates that the threshold *can* fail, but doesn't characterize *how close* the standard configuration is to the failure boundary.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap. The convergence experiments are the de facto validation strategy, and the authors appear to consider gradient equivalence demonstrated by training equivalence — a reasonable systems-community standard but one that limits diagnostic power. Future work could measure gradient error directly, sweep the threshold $\varepsilon$ to find the tightest value that preserves exact gradient computation, or characterize how gradient error depends on the |V|/D ratio and data distribution.

---

### Limitation 2: The CCE-Kahan-FullC Pretraining Variant Incurs Significant Memory and Compute Overhead That Is Only Partially Offset by Larger Batch Sizes

The paper acknowledges that standard CCE fails for pretraining from scratch: "In our initial experiments using CCE for pretraining, we found that validation perplexity suffered due to two sources of error" (Section 5.3). The fix — CCE-Kahan-FullC — disables gradient filtering for the classifier gradients and uses Kahan summation for both gradient accumulations. This comes with costs that the headline "negligible memory footprint" and "no sacrifice in speed" claims do not account for.

**Consequence.** Table 1 quantifies the overhead: CCE-Kahan-FullC doubles the gradient buffer memory (2,326 MB vs. 1,164 MB for standard CCE, row 9 vs. row 1) and more than doubles the backward pass time (268 ms vs. 100 ms, a 2.68× increase). The total loss+gradient time increases from 145 ms to 313 ms — a 2.16× slowdown compared to standard CCE, and 2.19× slower than `torch.compile` (143 ms). While the paper argues this "is often offset by the larger batch sizes CCE-Kahan-FullC enables" and cites one anecdote (Mistral NeMo pretraining: "decreasing training time by 2 hours (16%) compared to torch.compile"), this offset is not guaranteed. It depends on whether the memory savings from CCE (even with Kahan overhead) enable a batch size increase that would not have been possible with the baseline — a configuration-dependent relationship that the paper does not systematically characterize.

Furthermore, the memory claim shifts. The abstract states CCE reduces "total training-time memory consumption of the classifier head from 28 GB to 1 GB" — this applies to standard CCE on Gemma 2 (2B). CCE-Kahan-FullC uses more than double that (Table 1, row 9), which is still dramatically less than the baseline but moves the memory footprint from "negligible" to "a fraction of the backbone's activation checkpoints" — a qualitative difference that matters for edge-case configurations where gradient memory is the gating factor.

**What evidence exists.** Table 1 rows 8–10 provide the direct ablation. The Mistral NeMo pretraining experiment (Section 5.3) provides one data point suggesting the overhead can be offset in practice. No systematic study across models, batch sizes, or training configurations is provided.

**Mitigation status.** The paper presents CCE-Kahan-FullC as the solution for pretraining but does not attempt to reduce its overhead (e.g., by using split-precision where only $\nabla C$ uses Kahan summation, or by adaptively enabling $\nabla C$ filtering only for rare tokens). The authors appear to accept the overhead as the price of pretraining compatibility, but the trade-off is not framed as a limitation of CCE's design — it's presented as a configuration choice. A practitioner reading the abstract's strong claims might be surprised to discover the pretraining variant's overhead. The paper could strengthen its contribution by characterizing when CCE-Kahan-FullC provides net benefit vs. when `torch.compile` or another baseline would be preferable.

---

### Limitation 3: Performance Degrades as the Vocabulary-to-Hidden-Dimension Ratio Decreases, With No Mitigation Strategy

CCE's backward pass parallelizes over the product $N \times |V|$ — the number of (vocabulary, token) tiles. Standard matrix multiplication parallelizes over $N \times |V|$ for the forward pass but $N \times D$ and $|V| \times D$ for the gradient accumulations against embeddings and classifier respectively. When $D$ (hidden dimension) is large relative to $|V|$ (vocabulary size), the baseline has more parallelism available and CCE's recomputation cost becomes proportionally larger.

**Consequence.** Table A3 reveals the pattern: at |V|/D ≈ 111 (Gemma 2 2B), CCE is 1.4% slower than `torch.compile`; at |V|/D ≈ 42 (Qwen 2.5 7B), 12% slower; at |V|/D ≈ 10.4 (Phi 3.5 Mini), **55% slower** — an absolute penalty of 12 ms out of 34 ms total, small in wall-clock terms but proportionally significant. The paper's response is that this overhead is "largely negligible and only increases training time by one to two percent" (Section 5.1), but this figure is presented without a full-training-step time breakdown — it's an assertion, not a measurement.

For models with low |V|/D ratios — which includes many models with moderate vocabulary sizes (32K) and large hidden dimensions (3K–5K and growing) — CCE's speed advantage over `torch.compile` disappears entirely, replaced by a meaningful slowdown. If the cross-entropy layer represents more than "one to two percent" of training time in such configurations (which is plausible when the backbone uses highly optimized attention and MLP kernels), the absolute overhead could become non-trivial.

**What evidence exists.** Table A3 provides the per-model comparison, and Appendix C.2 discusses the ratio dependence explicitly: "As |V|/D continues to decrease, Liger Kernels is able to make better use of the GPU... The amount of parallel work for CCE is B × |V|, thus increasing D increases the amount of work but not the amount of parallelism." No breakdown of total training step time is provided to contextualize the cross-entropy overhead as a fraction of total training time across models.

**Mitigation status.** The paper suggests that "it may be possible to leverage ideas from split-k matrix multiplication kernels to expose more parallelism to CCE for large values of D" (Appendix C.2) but does not implement or evaluate this approach. This is left as future work. For practitioners with low |V|/D models, the paper provides no guidance on whether the memory savings justify the speed penalty, or at what |V|/D ratio CCE becomes net-negative for throughput.

### Limitation 4: Difficulty Estimation for the Pretraining Regime Is Incompletely Characterized — Only a Single 5% Corpus Experiment Demonstrates Equivalence

The paper demonstrates pretraining convergence equivalence using only 5% of the Open WebText dataset, trained for 1,500 gradient steps (Figure 5). This is substantially smaller than production pretraining runs, which typically involve hundreds of billions of tokens over tens of thousands of steps.

**Consequence.** Pretraining from scratch involves learning the classifier matrix $C$ from random initialization, which means the softmax sparsity pattern (and thus gradient filtering effectiveness) evolves dramatically over training. In early pretraining, the softmax is near-uniform (all tokens equally (un)likely), meaning gradient filtering would skip few if any entries. As training progresses, the distribution sharpens toward the pattern in Figure 3. The paper's 1,500-step experiments capture some of this evolution but may not reach the fully-sharpened regime, and they certainly don't capture any long-term effects of accumulated numerical error over hundreds of thousands of steps.

The paper's discovery that standard CCE fails for pretraining — requiring the CCE-Kahan-FullC variant — already demonstrates that the pretraining regime exposes different failure modes than fine-tuning. The fact that one failure was discovered and addressed doesn't guarantee that others won't emerge at full scale. For instance, Kahan summation reduces but doesn't eliminate accumulation error; over hundreds of thousands of steps, the residual error might accumulate differently for rare vs. common tokens, potentially biasing the learned classifier in subtle ways that a 1,500-step validation perplexity curve wouldn't reveal.

**What evidence exists.** Figure 5 shows CCE-Kahan-FullC matching `torch.compile` validation perplexity over 1,500 steps for four models trained on 5% of Open WebText. This is compelling evidence of short-horizon equivalence. No longer runs or full-dataset runs are reported. The paper does not discuss this limitation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the 1,500-step experiments as sufficient evidence for the pretraining claim. A more thorough validation would include a longer training run (even on a smaller model, e.g., 10K–50K steps) or an analysis of gradient statistics over the course of training to verify that the numerical approximation remains stable. Until such evidence exists, practitioners adopting CCE for production pretraining should validate convergence against an exact baseline at their target training horizon.

### Limitation 5: All Runtime Benchmarks Are on a Single GPU Architecture (A100) With a Single Precision Format (bf16), Leaving Transfer to fp16 and Other Hardware Uncharacterized

All experiments in Table 1, Table A1, Table A3, Figures A1, and A2 are conducted on an A100-SXM4 GPU with 80 GB of RAM, using bfloat16 as the accumulation and computation format. The gradient filtering threshold $\varepsilon = 2^{-12}$ is specifically calibrated to bfloat16's 7-bit mantissa. The paper includes no experiments on fp16 hardware, other GPU architectures (H100, consumer GPUs, AMD or Intel accelerators), or TPUs.

**Consequence.** The bfloat16 format provides a larger dynamic range (8-bit exponent) than fp16 (5-bit exponent) at the cost of precision (7-bit vs. 10-bit mantissa). The gradient filtering threshold derived for bfloat16 would need to be tightened for fp16 (smaller dynamic range changes truncation behavior) and loosened for fp32 (larger mantissa means smaller values matter). More critically, the softmax sparsity that gradient filtering exploits depends on the distribution of logit magnitudes, which is influenced by model architecture, initialization, and training data — not just the vocabulary size. A different model family with different logit calibration might produce less-sparse softmax distributions, reducing or eliminating the filtering speedup. The paper's convergence experiments with four model families (Gemma 2, Phi 3.5, Qwen 2.5, Mistral NeMo) partially address this by demonstrating sparsity across architectures, but the runtime benchmarks are all on the Gemma 2 (2B) configuration.

On H100 GPUs, the larger and faster L2 cache and HBM bandwidth could shift the relative performance between CCE and the baseline — CCE's advantage comes partly from avoiding global memory writes for the logit matrix, and if global memory bandwidth is less of a bottleneck (as on H100 with HBM3), that advantage shrinks. Conversely, CCE's SRAM-intensive design might benefit from H100's larger shared memory per SM (228 KB vs. 164 KB on A100).

**What evidence exists.** None. The paper provides only A100 bf16 measurements. The authors note that their implementation uses Triton (Tillet et al., 2019), which compiles to different GPU backends, but do not evaluate on other hardware.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address hardware or precision transfer. The gradient filtering threshold would need recalibration for fp16, and the performance trade-offs would need re-measurement on H100 and other architectures. Practitioners deploying on fp16-only hardware (some older GPUs, edge devices) or next-generation GPUs cannot rely on the paper's Table 1 numbers without independent benchmarking. The paper's open-source release (github.com/apple/ml-cross-entropy) makes such benchmarking possible but does not substitute for it.

### Limitation 6: Granularity of Gradient Filtering Is Constrained by Triton's Block-Level Control Flow, and the CUDA Optimization Path Is Hypothesized but Not Evaluated

CCE is implemented in Triton (Tillet et al., 2019), which the paper acknowledges "has some limitations in control flow. Specifically, the control flow must be specified at the block level and therefore our thread-safe log-add-exp and gradient filtering are constrained to operate at the block level as well" (Section 6). The gradient filtering checks whether an entire $V_B \times N_B$ tile of the softmax matrix has all entries below $\varepsilon$ — if even one entry exceeds the threshold, the entire tile's gradient computation proceeds, including contributions from the threshold entries within that tile. A CUDA implementation could check individual elements, skipping computation at finer granularity.

**Consequence.** The block-level filtering means that tiles containing a mix of active and negligible vocabulary entries incur the full cost of gradient computation for every entry, even if only a small fraction exceed the threshold. The vocabulary sorting heuristic (Section 4.3) is designed to minimize this by clustering active tokens together, but it's a heuristic — it cannot guarantee that every tile is either fully active or fully empty. Table 1 row 6 quantifies the cost: disabling vocabulary sorting (which makes block-level filtering less effective by scattering active entries) increases backward pass time by 15%. Even with sorting, there is residual inefficiency from partially-filled tiles that a finer-grained CUDA implementation could avoid.

The paper hypothesizes: "We expect that implementing CCE in CUDA may bring further performance gains because control flow could be performed at finer-grained levels" (Section 6). This is plausible but untested. The magnitude of potential improvement is unknown — it depends on what fraction of filtered tiles are partially rather than fully populated, and how much overhead the per-element versus per-tile filtering decision introduces. It's possible that per-element checking introduces branch divergence within warps that erodes the benefit, making block-level filtering close to optimal in practice.

**What evidence exists.** The performance difference between row 1 and row 6 of Table 1 (vocabulary sorting on vs. off) provides a lower bound on the inefficiency from poorly-clustered tiles, but no experiment measures the gap between block-level and potential element-level filtering. No CUDA implementation exists for comparison.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation and suggests the CUDA path as future work but does not pursue it. The open-source Triton implementation is what practitioners will use, with its block-level filtering constraint. The practical implication is that CCE's performance on models or datasets where the softmax sparsity pattern doesn't align well with block boundaries (i.e., active tokens aren't naturally clustered) may be worse than the paper's reported numbers, and the extent of this degradation is unknown. Vocabulary sorting is the only mitigation provided, and its effectiveness depends on how well average logit correlates with activation co-occurrence in practice — a property that the paper demonstrates works for Gemma 2 (2B) on Alpaca but doesn't validate across other data distributions.
