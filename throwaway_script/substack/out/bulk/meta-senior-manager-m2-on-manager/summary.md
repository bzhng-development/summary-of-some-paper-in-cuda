Here are the takeaways from this Stefan Mai interview (was a Senior Manager (M2) at Meta, previously an engineering manager at Amazon, and now co-founder of Hello Interview):

**Early career and the “death march” at Amazon**

He joined Amazon’s early advertising group in 2012, before its pivot to a massive profit center. It was a time when Amazon’s reputation for grinding engineers down was already forming, though the infamous *New York Times* article hadn’t yet been published. His first week set the tone: his manager assembled the team and announced, “I’ve got an idea. This is what we’re gonna build, and we’re gonna work nights and weekends until it happens.” It was a death march that defined the next six to eight months.

Now he says he wouldn’t accept that kind of demand, but at the time, having just quit another job and publicly committed to Big Tech, he leaned in completely. The experience gave him a raw taste of Amazon and became the basis for much of his early development as both an engineer and a leader. The grind wasn’t constant—50- to 60-hour weeks were common, but consistent nights and weekends ebbed and flowed. He describes that initial march as “emblematic of a lot of people’s experience, at least on the negative side with Amazon.”

**Becoming a manager at Amazon—probably too early**

Because his manager was a senior manager who neglected people management in favor of working directly with design on the product, Stefan became the de facto tech lead and acting manager for the team under him. “My boss really didn't have time for a lot of the niceties that normal managers would provide,” so Stefan filled the vacuum. He pushed hard for the title and got it, but looking back, he believes he got the promotion prematurely. The manager transition, he stresses, “is really fraught for a lot of people. They make a ton of mistakes early on, and I'm no different.”

His early mistakes were elementary: he wasn’t spending enough time understanding the goals and objectives of his team members. He knew it mattered, but didn’t know how to break through to people or get them to open up about what really drove them. He calls this one of the core challenges of moving from peer to manager—the **power dynamic is corrosive** to the relationships you had as an IC. Getting a friend to say they’re having a good or bad time isn’t enough; you need deeper knowledge of career objectives and the hidden drags on their work. His advice for new managers is to practice **active listening**: show real interest, use sub-verbals (“ums,” “ahs,” “I heard you”), reflect back what you heard, and ask curious, non-transactional questions. Most engineers, he says, are used to communicating in “almost like APIs.”

**Storytelling as a manager’s bedrock skill**

Management is a soft discipline where output metrics are murky, so a lot of it comes down to storytelling. He encourages people to first notice how pervasive stories already are in daily life—how you motivate yourself out of bed, how you recall details—and then to critique them. Once you can be critical of your own internal stories, you can practice getting better. For him, storytelling came naturally; every few months he’d look back at everything the team had accomplished and “paint it in the clearest picture so that people could feel proud of their work.” That reflective narrative not only motivates but provides a foundation for selling the future: “Here’s where we came from. Here’s where we are now and here’s how it’s gonna get us to the promised land.”

His central insight here is that **as a manager, you get to define what winning actually is**. If you can build a story where the team feels they’re winning, all the downstream problems—zero-sum mentalities, bickering, motivation—tend to resolve themselves. If that story is missing, those problems fester and become insurmountable.

**Why he left Amazon—assimilation, compensation, and impact**

After a long tenure, three things pushed him out. The first was a creeping suspicion that Amazon’s success depended on **assimilating into its “peculiar ways,”** and that becoming a good Amazonian might not make him a good engineering leader. “I wasn't sure whether I was just overfitting to this Amazon opportunity.” Second, he received a top rating with no compensation change because the stock had appreciated—a move he found indefensible. Third, when Facebook reached out, a team working on integrity—child safety and counter-terrorism—offered a qualitative impact his supply-chain and ML work at Amazon couldn’t match.

He sketches Amazon’s leadership style through **Kent Beck’s explore/expand/extract framework**: Amazon naturally gravitates toward the *extract* phase, optimizing operations with deep managerial oversight at every level. The benefit is intense discipline. The cost is that it stifles creativity. Every layer of management is buried in the details of the layer below—it’s “very effective at this growth stage, where the sole purpose in some sense is just to move 1% on your revenue or to drive your cost down by 1%.”

**Transitioning to machine learning**

The motivation was partly aesthetic: the idea of “implying a program from data” was seductive next to a lifetime of explicitly instructing computers. He dove in by reading books, competing on Kaggle, and building models on the side, but the real unlock was applying ML within his current role to build judgment and instinct. He warns that many people treat the pivot into AI as a study session, learning the fundamentals without crossing into execution. The fundamentals matter, but “your ability to be effective as either a leader or an engineer is also dependent on your judgment and instinct, which only can be honed if you actually go and build something.”

**The M2 promotion at Meta—tumult, trust, and managing managers**

He was promoted to senior manager about three and a half years in—after cycling through seven managers, one roughly every six months. The turbulence held him back, and part of his promotion story is learning to navigate that kind of organizational instability. His first move was to embed as an engineer: join the on-call, write code, build trust. It gave him credibility that peers who tried to remain hands-off never developed. “How people perceive you, especially as a manager, is going to either be a huge headwind or a major tailwind for your progression.”

Later he was given another manager to lead. She performed well, but the dynamic of leading another leader introduced a new layer of complexity. His crispest warning: **assume that leading other managers is just like leading engineers and you will fail very quickly—often via mutiny**. In a manager stack, leaf-node managers are implicitly competing with the person above them. If the org isn’t growing, a direct report’s path forward might mean displacing you. So managers of managers need to recognize the **gamesmanship happening under the covers**. People’s personal objectives—money, position, influence, interesting problems—are rarely perfectly aligned with the organization’s goals, and subtle behaviors, from coalition-building to “cookie-licking” projects, all flow from those personal drives.

Ryan notes how these zero-sum behaviors get louder when org growth slows. Stefan agrees: “the zero sum mentality usually happens when the winning starts to slow down.” That’s why the imperative of winning is so foundational, and why leadership styles must shift as an org matures. He contrasts Amazon and Meta: Amazon scaled by building teams as dependable API primitives—Werner Vogels’ “fitness functions”—which worked well but left value stranded in the seams and made the company slow to adapt; Meta made the org “very malleable,” requiring leaders who could work soft alignment without rigid boundaries.

**The skill gap from M1 to M2**

The biggest delta is learning to credibly add value to managers reporting to you without disintermediating them. A rookie M2 might set up skip-levels and inadvertently start undermining a manager by asking leading questions—then “a week later, Jim's gonna walk into your office and go… now Jan doesn't feel I'm being supportive enough.” A very small action as a senior leader can have a **magnification effect**, creating problems you then have to solve. He stresses that leadership chains expect new M2s to make these mistakes, just as they expect new M1s to stumble, but in the first few weeks those subtle errors are enough to sink you. “It’s kind of a scary environment to operate in, to be honest.”

**Engineering vs. manager career growth and meritocracy**

At high levels, he finds attribution becomes opaque and “a little bit fuzzy.” Senior ICs in calibrations sometimes talk a big game without clear evidence of their direct contribution, and the same ambiguity applies to managers. Over a long horizon—four years of consistent success—things become undeniable, but on a half-by-half basis it’s dubious. He thinks that at IC6 and IC7 it’s a bit more transparent; for M1s you can often look at the team holistically and make a decent judgment. But outsiders without full context will struggle, which means the system struggles with the perception of fairness even when it’s genuinely trying. “At a certain level it is, it’s difficult to have the data.”

**Amazon vs. Meta culture**

Amazon’s core strength is discipline: structured engineering design, predictable inter-team contracts, SLAs you can page on. Meta’s strength is openness and collaboration—engineers organically self-organized across the globe without manager involvement, and a centralized data warehouse made ML work frictionless in ways Amazon’s fragmented data-lake culture didn’t permit. On average Stefan believes Meta engineers were a bit stronger, but less disciplined; design reviews were “fuzzy and kind of hand wavy.” For people moving between the two, the unstructured environment at Meta can be paralyzing, while Amazon’s process-heavy culture can feel stifling. He still occasionally meets someone and thinks, “Amazon’s a better fit for you.”

**Performance systems and “additional equity”**

Both companies run a similar two-tier system: a public ratings process plus a private, senior-leader-level discussion for allocating extra investment. At Meta that’s additional or discretionary equity; at Amazon, a “top tier” designation. The goal is to identify the rocket ships and the people whose departure would break the organization. The underlying principle is simple: “in some sense, getting into those positions can be very lucrative just because they’re going to try to put those golden handcuffs on you as tightly as they can.” Where they differ: Meta was far more willing to double down on the upper quintile with outsized equity grants. Amazon’s frugality (“nobody likes” that leadership principle, Stefan deadpans) restrained that kind of concentration.

**Low-performer quotas and PIPs**

Every company of scale uses quotas to counteract the natural inflation of performance reviews. Stefan estimates the bottom bucket usually captures 7 to 15% of people, sometimes up to 20. The nightmare scenario for a manager is telling a team how hard they’ve crushed it all half, then being forced to drop someone into the low bucket—having to go back and reverse that message. Better practice is for managers to be aware of who might land in that quartile and message appropriately from the start. On PIPs, he says they are first and foremost a legal paper trail, but that trail’s credibility depends on people actually making it through. At Amazon, HR mostly checked boxes—“it was a pretty savage culture”; at Meta during his tenure, HR pushed managers to exhaust every option before letting someone go, sometimes to a fault. In most cases managers genuinely root for someone to recover, because they dislike firing people. But at the largest scale, “terminations are part of the picture and it's important that companies do it fairly, with sensitivity to the people involved. But they have to do it. Otherwise, the company won't exist for long.”

**AI cheating in interviews and the shifting landscape**

At his company Hello Interview, they’ve even seen candidates test cheating setups on mock interviews—talking to a friend in the background. Remote interviews were always cheatable, but modern tools have made it much worse. In-browser detection (page-focus tracking, copy-paste monitoring) catches the least sophisticated, but Stefan is more optimistic about the interview itself evolving than about anti-cheat countermeasures. He sees more companies leaning on design interviews—not just system design but lower-level design—because reasoning about abstractions and coupling is load-bearing in an AI-coded world. He doesn’t see LeetCode-style interviews disappearing soon; though painful, “broken is probably a step too far—it’s serving companies' needs.”

**Cracking OpenAI and Anthropic**

OpenAI was “notorious for asking the same questions in basically every interview,” so deep functional-area preparation was disproportionately effective—not memorization, but targeted readiness. Behavioral and retrospective interviews carry enormous weight: these companies want to see “have you walked the walk?” A critical calibration is **leaning toward technical accomplishments** and away from organizational maneuvering. The archetypal staff engineer story—“I wrote seven docs, aligned six teams, and wrote five lines of code”—won’t resonate the same way. “You have to recognize that in order to really nail it.”

**The job-hopping calculus**

For ambitious junior people, moving often creates growth by forcing constant generalization and discomfort. He personally stayed longer because he craves depth and finds the real impact shows up after the two-year mark. At senior levels, however, a pattern of moving every 18 months to two years is a “pretty bad sign.” It takes a year to ramp a senior hire; if they leave six months later, the investment is wasted. At mid-level, a two-year-ish tenure is fine, but at Staff-plus, hiring managers will second-guess. He flags a reverse trap: the snowball of credibility you build in a long tenure can become a shackle if you get permanently typecast as “that person for that system.” If you’re not being challenged anymore, moving may be the way out.

**Growth spurts and writing**

His biggest growth came during transitions—moving to Facebook stretched his leadership identity in unexpected ways, and pivoting deeper into ML created productive external pressure. But the subtler through-line was writing. Amazon’s intense writing culture, despite its time-wasting nitpicking, taught him that “good writing means good thinking.” His top tip for engineers: **become a critic first**. Read documents, figure out what hits and what misses, scan for whether you can get the big picture in seconds, look for imprecision. Amazon’s PR FAQ exercise—writing the press release for a feature before building it—was formative. If the final story can’t excite you, the product won’t either. A PR FAQ that opens with “a single pane of glass” is dead on arrival; one that opens with “50% reduction in time spent” moves people.

**Finding motivation beyond the M2 promotion**

After realizing he’d likely hit a cap on climbing the ladder, he asked himself, “What’s next? And if you can finally ask yourself that question and then work backwards… why not now?” For more than a decade he’d told himself he wanted to start a business. Eventually he faced the lie and left. He’s drawn to problems he can tell his kids about, problems that set the stage for the future. Money matters, but the relentless title march had become secondary.

**Advice to his younger self**

He’d tell himself to find mentorship sooner—just asking someone three questions could have saved months of toil. More fundamentally, he’d double down on **cultivating agency**. The moments he wrote out explicit career plans—where he was, where he wanted to go, a step-by-step checklist—paid incredible dividends he still under-emphasizes. When he became a senior manager, he handed his director a list of things he planned to accomplish, even though he didn’t yet know how to do them. His director later told him that was the moment he knew Stefan would succeed. “For a lot of people, that lack of agency is what holds them back.” The world, he says, proves surprisingly malleable to those kinds of plans. “Force yourself to come up with a plan and ask yourself, what do you really want?”

---

His fixation on **defining what winning actually is** threads through everything: the storytelling playbook, the “zero sum mentality” that emerges when winning slows down, and the leadership-style shift demanded as an org moves through the **explore/expand/extract** lifecycle. That same frame explains why OpenAI interviews prize technical achievements over organizational maneuvering—they’re still in an *explore* phase where breakout velocity matters more than alignment. Underneath it all sits his bedrock advice to **cultivate agency**; the plan he handed his director echoes the PR FAQ exercise he learned at Amazon—writing the story of success before knowing how to build it, then making the world malleable enough to fit.
