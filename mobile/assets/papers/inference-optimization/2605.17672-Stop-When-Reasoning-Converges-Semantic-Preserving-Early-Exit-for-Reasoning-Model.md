# Stop When Reasoning Converges: Semantic-Preserving Early Exit for Reasoning Models

**ArXiv:** [2605.17672](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17672)

## 🎯 Pitch

Large reasoning models waste up to 52% of their tokens by re-verifying answers long after arriving at them, and existing early-exit methods can accidentally cut off essential self-correction. The proposed PUMA framework uses a lightweight semantic redundancy detector to spot when reasoning is merely restating itself, coupled with answer verification to confirm it’s safe to stop—slashing token usage by 26% without harming accuracy.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **PUMA**, a **Progress-aware Unified Monitoring framework for Adaptive early exit** that reduces redundant tokens in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) by detecting when the reasoning trajectory has semantically converged rather than relying solely on answer-level readiness signals. Across five LRMs—including DeepSeek-R1-Distill and Qwen3 variants—and five reasoning benchmarks (MATH-500, AIME24/25, OlympiadBench, GPQA-Diamond), PUMA couples a lightweight **Redundancy Detector** (a fine-tuned embedding model that flags when successive reasoning steps become semantically similar, i.e., restating rather than advancing) with **Answer Verification** (confidence and consistency checks on probed trial answers at detector-flagged points), achieving 26.2% average token reduction while preserving accuracy and retained chain-of-thought quality. The framework further transfers zero-shot to code generation and vision-language reasoning, and its exit positions can supervise models to internalize concise stopping behavior via SFT, DPO, or GRPO, establishing that reasoning-level semantic redundancy is a robust and learnable signal for efficient inference—but only when paired with verification, since the detector alone cannot distinguish converged reasoning from premature answer stabilization.

### How PUMA Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

PUMA's central insight is that **reasoning-level semantic redundancy is a complementary signal to answer-level readiness**, and that the two should be combined rather than used in isolation. The paper draws explicit inspiration from semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024), which measures uncertainty in LLM outputs by clustering multiple generated responses and checking whether they are semantically diverse (high uncertainty) or collapse to the same meaning (low uncertainty). PUMA transfers this idea **from across-output diversity to within-trajectory progress**: if recent reasoning steps become semantically similar to prior steps—restating, re-deriving, or looping over content already established—then the reasoning trajectory is likely shifting from exploration to convergence.

The paper formalizes this through an interpretive lens in Appendix B.5: define a Reasoning Semantic Entropy (RSE) over a recent window of steps. High RSE means the model is still generating semantically diverse reasoning (exploration); low RSE means recent steps have collapsed into redundant patterns (convergence). PUMA does not explicitly compute RSE (which would require expensive clustering at inference time), but instead uses a **lightweight embedding-based Redundancy Detector** as an online proxy: high cosine similarity between consecutive step embeddings signals the local semantic collapse that low RSE would capture.

Crucially, PUMA does not treat redundancy as a stopping signal by itself. The framework has a **two-stage design** (Figure 2):

1. **Redundancy Detector**: Monitors the step sequence and flags candidate exit points when the current step appears semantically redundant with recent context. This answers *where* to consider stopping.

2. **Answer Verification**: Invoked only at detector-flagged candidates. Probes trial answers, checks confidence and consistency across a verification window. This answers *whether* stopping at this candidate is safe.

This decoupling is what distinguishes PUMA from both pure answer-level methods (which stop based on answer readiness anywhere, including during active exploration) and pure redundancy-based methods (which might stop as soon as reasoning becomes locally repetitive, even if the model hasn't yet reached a correct answer). The ablation study (Table 4 and Appendix C.4, Table 15) confirms that removing the Redundancy Detector gate—causing Answer Verification to fire at every step—increases token reduction but drops accuracy by 4.1 points and increases trial-answer probing overhead 3.3–4.3×. Removing Answer Verification (confidence gate + consistency) causes an even larger accuracy collapse (6.5–20.6 points).

The paper also positions PUMA as a **plug-and-play framework** that requires no model weight modification. The Redundancy Detector is a separate, lightweight embedding model (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B fine-tuned with LoRA) that runs alongside the reasoning model. Answer Verification uses standard forward-pass probing. This design choice reflects an explicit practical commitment: PUMA should work immediately with new LRMs as they are released, without per-model retraining or calibration.

Finally, the paper positions reasoning-level redundancy as **not just an inference-time signal but a learnable stopping policy** (Section 6.2). By using PUMA-selected exit positions to supervise SFT, DPO, or GRPO training, models can internalize concise stopping behavior and deploy without any PUMA modules at inference time. This is important because it suggests that redundancy-detection is a skill that can be distilled into the model itself, potentially enabling efficient reasoning as a first-class model capability rather than an external monitoring layer. The results (Table 5) show that PUMA-RL (GRPO with PUMA-supervised exits) achieves both higher average accuracy (67.0 vs. 66.2) and higher token reduction (34.9% vs. 24.3%) than training-free PUMA, demonstrating that the learned policy can actually outperform the online framework that trained it.

**Confidence computation (Equation 2).** The confidence `$C_t$` associated with trial answer `$A_t$` is computed as:

$$C_t = \exp\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p(a_{t,i} \mid a_{t,<i}, c_t)\right)$$

where `$n$` is the number of answer tokens, `$a_{t,i}$` is the `$i$`-th token of the trial answer `$A_t$`, `$a_{t,<i}$` denotes the preceding answer tokens, and `$c_t$` is the context (the reasoning prefix plus the answer-inducing suffix).

**What it computes:** the geometric mean of the token-level probabilities over the generated answer tokens. The model assigns a probability `$p(a_{t,i} \mid \cdot)$` to each token `$a_{t,i}$` given the preceding context; these probabilities are logged, averaged, and exponentiated. The result is a scalar `$C_t \in [0, 1]$` that represents the model's average per-token confidence in the answer. A value near 1 means the model assigned high probability to every answer token; a value near 0 means low per-token confidence.

**Why this form:** the geometric mean is the appropriate aggregation for token-level probabilities because it treats each token as an independent multiplicative factor in the sequence probability. The arithmetic mean would overweight high-probability tokens and be insensitive to a single low-probability token (e.g., a guess on a critical number), which is exactly the kind of uncertainty that should lower overall confidence. The exponential and logarithm cancel for exact computation but are shown in this form to make the geometric-mean operation explicit. This confidence formulation follows the approach of DEER (Yang et al., 2025).

**Task-specific confidence adjustments.** For GPQA (multiple-choice), the single answer token (A/B/C/D) competes against the full vocabulary, yielding artificially low absolute log-probabilities because the model must choose among all possible tokens, not just the four valid options. PUMA therefore applies a temperature-scaled softmax restricted to the four answer choices to obtain a calibrated confidence score. For code, there is no `\boxed{}` structure, so confidence is computed over all generated code tokens (typically capped at ~50 tokens for efficiency; complete code generation would be too expensive for an online early-exit probe). For math tasks, confidence is computed over only the tokens inside the `\boxed{...}` brackets.

**Answer extraction.** For math tasks, the trial answer is extracted from inside the `\boxed{...}` using brace matching. For GPQA, the single answer letter is extracted. For code, the full generated code block is used. Generation is capped at approximately 30 tokens for math and multiple-choice (sufficient for a complete boxed answer) and approximately 50 tokens for code (rarely a complete program, but sufficient to compute a meaningful confidence estimate from the opening tokens).

**Verification window and exit condition (Equation 3).** PUMA does not decide to stop at the first detector-flagged candidate. Instead, it continues generation until it observes `$L$` additional detector-flagged candidates, forming a verification window `$t_1 < t_2 < \cdots < t_L$`. At each candidate `$t_\ell$`, PUMA extracts a trial answer `$A_{t_\ell}$` and confidence `$C_{t_\ell}$`. PUMA exits at the end of the window only if all three conditions hold:

$$\text{Exit}(t_1, \ldots, t_L) = [C_{t_1} > \lambda] \land \left[\bigwedge_{\ell=2}^{L} A_{t_\ell} = A_{t_1}\right] \land \left[\bigwedge_{\ell=2}^{L} C_{t_\ell} \geq C_{t_1} - \epsilon\right]$$

where `$\lambda = 0.98$` is the confidence threshold, `$L = 2$` is the verification window length, and `$\epsilon = 0.03$` is the stability tolerance.

**What it computes:** a three-part logical conjunction that must all be true for PUMA to stop. The first condition, `$C_{t_1} > \lambda$`, ensures the first candidate answer is sufficiently confident (above 0.98). The second condition, `$A_{t_\ell} = A_{t_1}$` for all `$\ell = 2, \ldots, L$`, requires all trial answers across the verification window to be identical—this is the **answer consistency** check. The third condition, `$C_{t_\ell} \geq C_{t_1} - \epsilon$` for all `$\ell = 2, \ldots, L$`, requires that confidence does not materially decline across the window—the model should not become less certain about the same answer. If any condition fails, PUMA resumes generation until the next flagged candidate, resetting the verification window.

**Why this form:** the three conditions protect against different failure modes. The confidence gate (`$\lambda = 0.98$`) prevents stopping when the answer is uncertain (which would risk selecting an incorrect answer). The consistency check prevents stopping when the answer is still fluctuating—a model may produce the same wrong answer for several steps while still exploring, but requiring `$L$` independent redundancy-triggered probes to agree reduces this risk. The stability tolerance (`$\epsilon = 0.03$`) prevents stopping when confidence is declining, which would signal that the model is becoming less committed to the answer (possibly about to change it). Together, these conditions ensure that PUMA only stops when the model is both confident and stable about a consistent answer.

Sensitivity analysis in Appendix B.7 (Figure 7) shows that PUMA is robust to these hyperparameter choices. The confidence threshold `$\lambda$` is stable across `$[0.95, 0.98]$` with only minor accuracy–efficiency variation; the sharp drop in token reduction at `$\lambda = 0.99$` indicates that requiring near-perfect confidence causes the verifier to veto most candidate exits. The verification window `$L$`: setting `$L = 1$` exits after the first verified candidate and is more susceptible to false positives; `$L = 3$` is overly conservative and reduces token reduction without improving accuracy. The default `$L = 2$` provides the best balance.

**Why invoke verification only at redundancy-flagged candidates, not at every step?** The paper's ablation study (Table 4 and Appendix C.4, Table 15) provides direct empirical evidence. Removing the Redundancy Detector gate (setting the redundancy threshold to zero, causing Answer Verification to be invoked at every eligible step) increases token reduction but drops accuracy by 4.1–3.9 points and increases trial-answer probing overhead 3.3–4.3×. This shows that answer-level verification without redundancy-based candidate filtering is both less reliable and more expensive. Conceptually, the Redundancy Detector answers "where should we consider stopping?" while Answer Verification answers "is stopping here safe?" Answer-level signals alone (confidence, consistency) can trigger during active exploration (as analyzed in Appendix A, Tables 6–7 and Figure 6), when the model produces a confident but incorrect answer while still exploring alternative solution paths. The detector provides a necessary condition—the reasoning trajectory appears converged—that filters out these premature triggers.

#### Loop Breaker

The Verified Early Exit mechanism (Redundancy Detector + Answer Verification) is PUMA's primary stopping mechanism. However, some reasoning trajectories produce consecutive redundant steps without ever satisfying all three verification conditions—for example, a model may be stuck in a verification loop, repeatedly re-checking an answer it's uncertain about, generating redundant steps without the trial answer ever becoming both confident and consistent. PUMA includes a Loop Breaker fallback to handle these edge cases.

**Activation condition.** The Loop Breaker activates only when both of the following hold:

1. **Late-stage:** the reasoning chain has exceeded a minimum step threshold of 50 reasoning steps. This prevents the Loop Breaker from triggering during early exploration, when the model may be generating seemingly redundant steps that are actually part of productive exploration.
2. **Consecutive redundancy:** the Redundancy Detector identifies `$m$` consecutive redundant steps. The value of `$m$` is model-specific: `$m = 1$` for DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and Nemotron-Nano-8B, `$m = 3$` for DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, `$m = 4$` for DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, and the Loop Breaker is not activated for Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking (verified exits alone provide sufficient token savings). These values are selected on a held-out AMC23 validation set, reflecting that different models exhibit different redundancy patterns in late-stage reasoning.

**Exit decision.** When the Loop Breaker activates, PUMA checks whether the highest-confidence trial answer observed so far across all probes exceeds a weak minimum-confidence gate of 0.8. If the gate is satisfied, PUMA terminates generation and uses that trial answer as the final answer. If the gate is not satisfied (meaning no answer observed so far has even moderate confidence), generation continues—the model is likely still struggling with the problem, and early termination would produce a low-quality guess.

**Design rationale.** The Loop Breaker serves a different purpose than Verified Early Exit. Verified exits aim for high precision: they only stop when the model is confident, consistent, and stable, minimizing accuracy loss. The Loop Breaker is an efficiency-oriented fallback: it accepts that some trajectories will never satisfy all verification conditions (e.g., models stuck in uncertainty loops on very hard problems) and cuts losses to save tokens. This is why the minimum-confidence gate is deliberately weak (0.8 rather than 0.98): the goal is to avoid terminating on trajectories where the model has no reasonable answer, while accepting that on genuinely hopeless problems, any plausible answer is better than continuing to generate tokens indefinitely. The ablation study (Table 4 and Appendix C.4, Table 15) confirms that removing the Loop Breaker reduces token savings (from 35.6% to 22.6% on DS-7B), showing that it provides meaningful additional compression beyond verified exits.

**Effect on correctness.** Figure 4 in the paper breaks down correctness transitions for Loop Breaker exits. The dominant pattern is `$W \rightarrow W$` (wrong under Full CoT, wrong after PUMA stopping), accounting for 53–68% of Loop Breaker exits across models. These are trajectories that Full CoT would also answer incorrectly, so the Loop Breaker saves tokens on hopeless problems without changing the outcome. The `$R \rightarrow R$` transitions (correct under both) account for 7–9%, and harmful `$R \rightarrow W$` transitions (correct under Full CoT but wrong after Loop Breaker stopping) are 5–10%. This is a higher error rate than verified exits, consistent with the Loop Breaker being a coarser, less reliable stopping mechanism—but since it activates only when verified exits have already failed, it provides token savings that would otherwise be unavailable.

**Precedence.** Verified exits take precedence over the Loop Breaker. The Loop Breaker is only used when no verified exit occurs. This means the high-precision Verified Exit mechanism gets the first opportunity to stop; the lower-precision Loop Breaker is a safety net, not a substitute.

#### Full Online Algorithm

PUMA operates as an online monitoring loop that interleaves with the reasoning model's autoregressive generation. The algorithm proceeds as follows:

**Initialization.** The reasoning model begins generating tokens for the input question. PUMA initializes an empty step buffer for the Step Segmenter, sets the redundancy counter to zero (for the Loop Breaker), and records the initial step count as 0.

**Step-level loop.** As tokens are generated:

1. The Step Segmenter accumulates tokens until it identifies a complete reasoning step (a coherent unit bounded by blank lines, with a target length of 200–1000 characters after merging).

2. For each completed step `$r_t$`, the Redundancy Detector embeds `$r_t$` and computes `$s^{(k)}_t$` (Equation 1) against the previous `$k$` steps. If `$s^{(k)}_t > \tau_{\text{sim}}$`:
   - The step is flagged as a candidate exit.
   - The redundancy counter for the Loop Breaker increments; if `$s^{(k)}_t \leq \tau_{\text{sim}}$`, the counter resets to zero.

3. If flagged, Answer Verification is invoked (unless already in a verification window—the window collects `$L$` candidates before making a decision). The trial answer `$A_t$` and confidence `$C_t$` are recorded.

4. Once the verification window contains `$L$` candidates, the Exit condition (Equation 3) is evaluated. If all three conditions hold, PUMA terminates generation, outputs the reasoning prefix up to `$t_1$` (the first candidate in the window) and the trial answer `$A_{t_1}$` as the final answer.

5. If the Exit condition fails, the verification window is cleared, and generation continues. A new window starts accumulating at the next detector-flagged candidate.

6. If the Loop Breaker conditions are met (step count > 50 AND consecutive redundant counter ≥ `$m$`), PUMA checks the highest-confidence trial answer observed so far. If its confidence exceeds 0.8, PUMA terminates generation and outputs that answer. Otherwise, generation continues.

7. If generation reaches the model's natural end-of-sequence token (or a maximum token limit) without any exit, the full reasoning chain and the model's natural final answer are output (equivalent to Full CoT).

**Overhead accounting.** The Redundancy Detector runs at each new reasoning step, adding approximately 18 ms per question (0.4–1.1% of total wall-clock time, as shown in Figure 3c). Answer Verification is invoked only at detector-flagged candidates, and each trial-answer probe adds approximately 0.2–0.57 seconds of forward-pass time. On average, PUMA uses 13.75 trial-answer probes per question on DS-7B and 10.46 probes on Qwen3-30B-T (Appendix C.4, Table 15). Token counts include all generated tokens: main reasoning tokens, trial-answer probe tokens, and final-answer tokens, so the reported token reduction reflects total generation cost, not just retained output length.

**Design rationale for the two-stage architecture.** The core algorithmic insight is the separation of *where* to consider stopping from *whether* to stop. The Redundancy Detector is cheap to run (embedding + cosine similarity) and can be invoked at every step without significant overhead. Answer Verification is expensive (it requires a forward pass to generate a trial answer) and is therefore gated behind the detector. This architecture ensures that verification is only invoked at semantically meaningful points—when the reasoning appears converged—rather than at every step, which would be prohibitively expensive (as the ablation shows: 3.3–4.3× more probes when the detector gate is removed).

**Why not use the Redundancy Detector alone as a stopping signal?** The paper's ablation (Table 4, "w/o Answer Consistency" and "w/o Confidence Gate") shows that removing answer verification causes substantial accuracy drops (2.9 points without consistency, 6.5 points without confidence, and 16.2–20.6 points without both). The Redundancy Detector tells us that the reasoning process has become repetitive, but not whether the model has arrived at a correct answer. A model could be redundantly restating an incorrect approach, or redundantly verifying an answer it's uncertain about. Answer Verification adds the safety layer that distinguishes converged-correct trajectories from converged-incorrect or converged-uncertain ones.

#### Relationship to Semantic Entropy (Interpretive Lens)

Appendix B.5 provides a formal interpretive lens connecting PUMA's Redundancy Detector to the concept of Semantic Entropy from the uncertainty estimation literature (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024). This is not a component of the PUMA algorithm—it is a theoretical framing that explains why step-level semantic redundancy is a principled signal for reasoning convergence.

Semantic Entropy measures uncertainty in LLM outputs by clustering multiple generated responses and computing entropy over the resulting semantic clusters. High entropy means the model's outputs are semantically diverse (high uncertainty about the correct answer); low entropy means outputs collapse to a small set of meanings (low uncertainty, high confidence).

PUMA transfers this idea from across-output diversity to within-trajectory progress. Define a recent reasoning window `$W_t^{(w)} = (r_{t-w+1}, \ldots, r_t)$` consisting of the last `$w$` steps. Let `$c(r)$` denote the semantic cluster associated with reasoning step `$r$`. The Reasoning Semantic Entropy of this window is:

$$\text{RSE}_w(t) = -\sum_{c} p_t(c) \log p_t(c)$$

where `$p_t(c)$` is the fraction of steps in `$W_t^{(w)}$` assigned to cluster `$c$`.

**What it computes:** the Shannon entropy over the distribution of semantic clusters in the recent reasoning window. If all recent steps belong to different clusters (diverse reasoning, still exploring), `$\text{RSE}_w(t)$` is high. If recent steps collapse into a small number of redundant clusters (verification, restatement), `$\text{RSE}_w(t)$` is low.

**Why this framing matters:** it provides a principled justification for why redundancy is a convergence signal. Low `$\text{RSE}_w(t)$` means the reasoning trajectory has locally converged—the model is no longer introducing semantically diverse new content. PUMA does not explicitly compute `$\text{RSE}_w(t)$` (which would require expensive clustering at inference time), but uses the Redundancy Detector as a lightweight online proxy: high cosine similarity between recent step embeddings signals the local semantic collapse that low `$\text{RSE}_w(t)$` would capture. This framing also explains why the lookback window size `$k$` matters: a larger window (comparing against more distant steps) effectively estimates semantic entropy over a longer horizon, making it more aggressive at detecting convergence but also more prone to false positives when the model revisits concepts in a new context (as shown in the lookback window analysis, Appendix B.4).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reasoning-Level Semantic Redundancy as a New Diagnostic Signal for Inference-Time Stopping

The fundamental intellectual move in this paper is recognizing that **reasoning convergence and answer readiness are distinct phenomena**, and that the field's predominant reliance on answer-level stopping signals (confidence, trial-answer consistency) conflates the two. Prior work treated "when to stop reasoning" as equivalent to "when does the answer look stable," operationalized through confidence thresholds (DEER; Yang et al., 2025), consecutive answer agreement (Answer Convergence; Liu and Wang, 2025), or certainty probing (Dynasor; Fu et al., 2025). These signals ask: *is the current answer probably correct?*

PUMA asks a different question: *is the reasoning process still making novel semantic progress?* This reframing is conceptually significant because it captures a failure mode that answer-level signals are structurally blind to: the model can produce a confident, stable answer while still exploring or self-correcting. Appendix A (Tables 6–7) provides the diagnostic evidence: 42.4% of confidence-based failures and 54.8% of consistency-based failures occur on traces where the uninterrupted model would eventually self-correct to the correct answer. These are not hopeless trajectories where early stopping merely accelerates an inevitable wrong outcome—they are trajectories where answer-level signals would *actively prevent* the model from reaching a correct solution it was in the process of discovering. Threshold tuning does not resolve this (Appendix A, Figure 6): sweeping confidence thresholds from 0.93 to 0.97 and consistency windows from 1 to 8 steps still shows a persistent tradeoff between failure rate and token reduction, with no operating point that is simultaneously safe and efficient.

The innovation is not the specific redundancy detection mechanism (a fine-tuned embedding model) but the **diagnostic concept**: that a reasoning trajectory's semantic novelty can be monitored online as a signal orthogonal to answer readiness. This is analogous to how semantic entropy (Kuhn et al., 2023; Farquhar et al., 2024) introduced semantic clustering as an uncertainty signal orthogonal to token-level probability—and both innovations share the same structure: measure whether multiple outputs (across samples for entropy, across steps for redundancy) collapse to the same meaning rather than comparing their surface forms. The paper makes this connection explicit in Appendix B.5 through the Reasoning Semantic Entropy framing, but the practical contribution is showing that a lightweight embedding-based proxy (cosine similarity between step embeddings, Equation 1) can serve as an online approximation of local semantic collapse without the expensive clustering that full semantic entropy would require at inference time.

This is a fundamental conceptual reframing, not an incremental improvement. It changes the stopping problem from "is the answer ready?" to "has the reasoning converged, and is the converged answer correct?"—a two-part question that maps naturally onto PUMA's two-stage architecture (Redundancy Detector for convergence, Answer Verification for correctness). The field's default assumption was that answer readiness signals were the right (or only) monitoring dimension; this paper demonstrates that reasoning-level progress is both measurable and complementary.

---

### Innovation 2: Decoupling *Where* to Consider Stopping from *Whether* to Stop, with Empirical Evidence That the Decoupling Matters

A second conceptual contribution is the **architectural principle** of separating candidate exit identification (reasoning-level: where has the trajectory converged?) from exit validation (answer-level: is stopping here safe?), and providing empirical evidence that this decoupling yields strictly better accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs than either signal alone. This is not merely an engineering choice—it is a claim about the structure of the early-exit problem that has implications for how future methods should be designed.

Prior inference-time early-exit methods use a single signal for both functions. DEER uses confidence as both the trigger (if confidence exceeds a threshold) and the validator (the answer with high confidence is the output). Answer Convergence uses trial-answer consistency for both: if `k` consecutive answers agree, stop and output that answer. These single-signal approaches conflate the decision to consider stopping with the decision to actually stop, which forces a single tuning knob (threshold, window size) to balance two competing objectives: not missing safe exit opportunities (requires low threshold) and not triggering during active reasoning (requires high threshold). The threshold sensitivity analysis in Appendix A (Figure 6) demonstrates this tension directly: no single threshold produces both low failure rate and high token reduction.

PUMA's two-stage design breaks this coupling. The Redundancy Detector answers "where should we consider stopping?" using a signal (step-level semantic similarity) that correlates with reasoning convergence but not with answer correctness. Answer Verification answers "is stopping here safe?" using signals (confidence, consistency, stability) that correlate with answer quality but not with whether the reasoning has finished exploring. This separation allows each stage to be optimized for its own objective: the detector can be conservative (avoid over-triggering on novel steps) while the verifier can be stringent (require high confidence and consistency before stopping), because a false positive from the detector only triggers a verification probe, not a premature exit.

The ablation study (Table 4; Appendix C.4, Table 15) empirically validates that this decoupling is load-bearing. Removing the Redundancy Detector gate—causing Answer Verification to fire at every step the same way single-signal methods do—increases token reduction but drops accuracy by 4.1 points and increases probing overhead 3.3× on DS-7B. Removing Answer Verification drops accuracy by 6.5 points (confidence gate removed) or 16.2 points (both confidence and consistency removed). Neither signal alone achieves the full system's accuracy–efficiency balance. The key empirical finding is that **the two signals are not just individually useful—they are complementary in a way that produces gains neither can achieve alone**. This is visible in the probe overhead numbers: the Redundancy Detector gate reduces trial-answer probes by a factor of 3.3–4.3× compared to un-gated verification, which is what makes the two-stage architecture computationally viable at scale.

This is a moderately fundamental innovation—it does not introduce a new signal type (both redundancy and answer-level signals existed in prior work), but it introduces a new architecture for combining them that resolves a tension the field had not previously identified as architectural rather than parametric. It suggests that future early-exit methods should think in terms of **trigger-validation pipelines** rather than single-signal thresholds, with the trigger optimized for coverage (find all potentially safe exit points) and the validator optimized for precision (accept only genuinely safe ones).

### Innovation 3: Reasoning-Level Redundancy as a Transferable, Zero-Shot, and Learnable Signal—Not Tied to Model Internals

A third contribution is the empirical demonstration that reasoning-level semantic redundancy—when operationalized through an embedding-based detector trained on text surface forms—**transfers across models, domains, and modalities without retraining or recalibration**, and can be **internalized into model weights** as a learned stopping policy that outperforms the online framework that trained it. This has significant practical and conceptual implications.

The practical significance is straightforward: PUMA's Redundancy Detector is trained once on text reasoning traces from four source models (QwQ-32B, GPT-OSS-120B, GLM-4.7-Thinking, Kimi-K2-Thinking; Appendix B.2) and transfers zero-shot to five different target models across text reasoning benchmarks, to code generation (LiveCodeBench), and to vision-language reasoning on two different VLMs (Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking and Kimi-VL-16B-A3B-Thinking, Table 3), with only the redundancy threshold adjusted for code (τ_sim raised to 0.50) and no changes for vision-language. On vision-language tasks, PUMA achieves 23.8–33.6% token reduction with at most 1.5 points of accuracy change, applied completely zero-shot. This is in stark contrast to representation-level early-exit methods (Akgül et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025) that train probes on model-specific hidden states and require per-model retraining, or token-level methods (Wei et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025) that rely on model-specific delimiters or vocabulary patterns.

The conceptual significance is deeper: it suggests that **reasoning redundancy is a property of the reasoning content itself, not an artifact of a particular model's generation patterns**. If redundancy were model-specific—e.g., a quirk of how DeepSeek models tend to re-verify answers—then a detector trained on one set of models would not transfer to others. The fact that it does transfer, including to VLMs that process visual inputs alongside text, implies that semantic redundancy in step-by-step reasoning follows patterns that are stable across model architectures and training procedures. This connects to the semantic entropy framing (Appendix B.5): if reasoning convergence corresponds to a genuine semantic phenomenon (the trajectory shifting from diverse exploration to clustered redundancy), then detecting it through surface-form embeddings generalizes the way semantic clustering does.

The learnability result (Table 5) adds another dimension: PUMA-selected exit positions can supervise models to internalize concise stopping behavior, and the resulting learned policies **outperform the online PUMA framework on both accuracy and token reduction** (PUMA-RL: 67.0 avg. accuracy, 34.9% TR vs. training-free PUMA: 66.2, 24.3% TR). This is notable because it is not obvious that a learned stopping policy should outperform the framework that generated its training data. The most likely explanation is that the model can learn to *anticipate* convergence and stop earlier than the online detector would, since it has access to its own internal representations rather than relying on post-hoc step-level similarity comparisons. The FixedExit baselines—which use the same training recipes but replace PUMA-selected positions with fixed-interval candidates—perform substantially worse (FixedExit-SFT is 12.8 accuracy points below PUMA-SFT; FixedExit-RL is 3.8 points below PUMA-RL), confirming that PUMA's exit positions carry semantic information rather than merely being shorter prefixes.

This is an incremental-to-moderate innovation: the transfer results are empirical confirmation of a property one might hypothesize from the semantic framing (if redundancy is a semantic phenomenon, it should transfer), but demonstrating it across modalities and model families with a single frozen detector is non-trivial. The learnability result opens an avenue—distilling redundancy detection into the model itself—that suggests efficient reasoning can become a model capability rather than an external monitoring system.

### Innovation 4: Verifier Over-Optimization as an Explicitly Diagnosed and Mitigated Failure Mode in Answer-Level Stopping

A fourth contribution—part conceptual, part diagnostic—is the systematic empirical characterization of **why and how answer-level stopping signals fail**, distinguishing genuinely non-recoverable failures from premature exits that actively prevent self-correction. This is not a new method but a **diagnostic analysis** that changes how the field should think about evaluating early-exit systems.

Prior work on early exit typically evaluates methods by reporting accuracy and token reduction, treating accuracy loss as an aggregate cost of efficiency gains. This paper's counterfactual analysis (Appendix A.3, Table 7) decomposes failure cases into two qualitatively different categories: **premature exits**, where the stopping signal fires on an incorrect trial answer but the uninterrupted model would have self-corrected to the right answer (e.g., consistency-based stopping at step 8 when the model would revise its answer at step 12), and **non-recoverable failures**, where the trajectory was headed toward a wrong answer regardless. Across five models, 42.4% of confidence-based failures and 54.8% of consistency-based failures are premature rather than non-recoverable. This decomposition matters because it reveals that **answer-level signals do not merely stop on trajectories that would remain wrong—they actively break trajectories that would have succeeded**.

This diagnostic changes the evaluation landscape. Aggregate accuracy-token tradeoff curves implicitly assume that all accuracy losses are equivalent, but a loss from stopping on a hopeless trajectory (non-recoverable failure) is fundamentally different from a loss from interrupting a trajectory mid-self-correction (premature exit). The first is an acceptable cost of efficiency; the second is a signal design failure that could be mitigated with a better stopping criterion. Future early-exit methods should report or at least analyze this decomposition, since a method could achieve the same aggregate accuracy as another while having a much higher (or lower) premature exit rate, with very different implications for trustworthiness.

The paper's exit behavior analysis (Figure 4) extends this diagnostic thinking to PUMA's own exit modes. Verified exits show a dominant R→R (correct→correct) transition (69–72%), meaning they usually preserve already-correct trajectories. Loop Breaker exits, by contrast, show a dominant W→W (wrong→wrong) transition (53–68%), meaning they mostly cut losses on already-hopeless trajectories. The harmful R→W transitions are limited to 4.8–5.1% for verified exits and 9.0–9.7% for Loop Breaker exits. This granularity—understanding *which* trajectories each exit mode affects and *why*—is what makes the analysis more than just performance reporting; it explains the mechanism behind the aggregate numbers.

This is an incremental-to-moderate innovation in evaluation methodology: the field has tools for analyzing system-level accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs but not for decomposing failure modes in early exit specifically. The premature-vs-non-recoverable distinction is simple to compute (it requires comparing the early-exit answer against the full-trajectory final answer), and it provides actionable signal for method designers: if most of your accuracy loss is premature exits, you need a better convergence detection mechanism; if most of it is non-recoverable failures, you need a better model, not a better stopping rule. The paper does not claim this as a formal contribution, but it is arguably the analysis that a practitioner would most want to replicate when evaluating early-exit methods on their own models and tasks.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The main evaluation uses five challenging reasoning benchmarks: MATH-500 (500 competition math problems; Hendrycks et al., 2021), AIME24 and AIME25 (30 problems each from the American Invitational Mathematics Examination; Zhang and Team Math-AI, 2024, 2025), OlympiadBench (675 problems from olympiad-level math and physics; He et al., 2024, using the standard text-only English open-ended math subset), and GPQA-Diamond (198 graduate-level science QA problems; Rein et al., 2024). Generalization experiments additionally evaluate on LiveCodeBench (880 code generation problems; Jain et al., 2025), MathVista (200 vision-language math problems from the testmini split; Lu et al., 2024), and MathVision (200 vision-language math problems from the full test set; Wang et al., 2024). Dataset statistics are summarized in Appendix C.1, Table 12.

- **Base model(s).** The paper evaluates five LRMs spanning diverse families and scales: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B (all based on DeepSeek-R1 distillation; Guo et al., 2025), Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B (NVIDIA, 2025), and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking (a 30B-parameter mixture-of-experts model with 3B activated parameters; Yang et al., 2025). This suite covers 7B–32B-scale models, dense and mixture-of-experts architectures, and both mathematical and scientific reasoning capabilities. The choice covers model families that a practitioner might realistically choose among for deployment, and the 7B–32B range represents the scale at which inference-time efficiency interventions are most practically relevant (smaller models benefit less from CoT; larger models may be prohibitively expensive to serve).

- **Metrics.** The paper reports three primary metrics jointly for every experiment. **Accuracy (Acc, %)**: the fraction of questions for which the selected final answer matches the ground truth, using exact string match for math and multiple-choice tasks and fuzzy matching (difflib.SequenceMatcher with similarity ≥ 0.8) for code. **Average generated tokens (Tok)**: the mean number of output tokens per question, including all tokens incurred by a method—main reasoning tokens, trial-answer probe tokens, and final-answer tokens—so that token reduction reflects total generation cost, not just retained output length. **Token reduction (TR, %)**: computed as TR = (1 − Tok_method / Tok_Full-CoT) × 100, where higher TR indicates greater efficiency. For overall results across benchmarks, token-reduction statistics are weighted by benchmark size to reflect aggregate token savings, while accuracy follows the benchmark-level reporting convention. The paper also reports **wall-clock latency** (seconds per question and speedup relative to Full CoT) for selected experiments (Section 5.2, Figure 3), since token reduction does not automatically translate to speedup when methods incur probing overhead.

- **Baselines.** Six baselines are compared in the main experiments (Table 1), categorized into prompt-based and inference-time early-exit methods. **Prompt-based**: No-Think (Ma et al., 2025) prompts the model to skip reasoning entirely; Concise CoT (CCoT; Nayab et al., 2024) imposes a global word budget; Chain of Draft (CoD; Xu et al., 2025) enforces per-step word limits; Plan-and-Budget (Lin et al., 2025) decomposes problems and allocates reasoning budgets by complexity. **Inference-time early exit**: Answer Convergence (Ans. Conv.; Liu and Wang, 2025) stops when consecutive trial answers agree; Dynasor (Fu et al., 2025) combines answer consistency with certainty probing; DEER (Yang et al., 2025) halts when a trial answer exceeds a confidence threshold. All baselines are reproduced using official code repositories where available or following the procedures described in the original papers. The reference point for all comparisons is **Full CoT**, the unmodified generation with no early exit.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a fixed "generation budget" concept the way search-based methods do (the reasoning model generates a single chain, not multiple parallel samples). Instead, compute is measured in terms of **total generated tokens**, which captures both the length of the reasoning chain and the cost of trial-answer probing. For fair comparison, trial-answer probe tokens are included in all methods' token counts. Wall-clock experiments (Section 5.2, Figure 3) additionally account for the computational overhead of the Redundancy Detector (embedding forward pass) and trial-answer probing (forward pass through the main model). The Redundancy Detector adds approximately 18 ms per question, and each trial-answer probe adds 0.2–0.57 seconds of forward-pass time (Appendix C.5, Figure 3c).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** All generation experiments use three random seeds (0, 42, 123) with temperature = 0.6 and top-p = 0.95, following the models' recommended reasoning settings. PUMA's stopping hyperparameters are selected on a held-out detector calibration split (Appendix B.2) and a held-out AMC23 validation set (for the Loop Breaker's model-specific consecutive-redundancy threshold; Appendix B.7), with no test-set tuning. The reasoning quality evaluation (Section 5.1, Table 2) uses an LLM-as-Judge protocol with GPT-5.4-thinking evaluated on 896 total instances across three model–benchmark combinations, with human validation on 100 randomly sampled pairwise comparisons showing 85% agreement with the judge's preferences (Appendix E). The generalization experiments on vision-language reasoning (Section 5.3, Table 3) apply PUMA zero-shot without any retraining or hyperparameter tuning, directly testing transfer.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Accuracy–Efficiency Tradeoff Across Five LRMs (Table 1, Appendix C.3 Table 14, Figure 8)

The headline result is that **PUMA achieves 26.2% average token reduction across all five LRMs and five benchmarks while preserving accuracy** (Table 1, Overall column). Across 25 model–benchmark combinations, the average accuracy change relative to Full CoT is positive (+0.7 points), and token reduction ranges from 4.0% (DS-7B on GPQA-Diamond, where reasoning chains are already relatively short) to 43.2% (DS-7B on OlympiadBench).

**Per-model summary (Table 1, Overall):**

- **DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B**: PUMA achieves 60.2% accuracy (+2.2 over Full CoT's 58.0%) with 35.6% TR. All baselines either lose accuracy or achieve less token reduction: No-Think collapses to 38.7% accuracy despite 79.9% TR; CCoT drops to 50.4% accuracy with 58.6% TR; Dynasor drops to 45.9% accuracy with only 36.6% TR; DEER preserves accuracy at 57.9% but with only 21.5% TR.

- **Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B**: PUMA achieves 64.7% accuracy (+0.4 over Full CoT's 64.3%) with 20.1% TR. DEER achieves 61.2% accuracy with *negative* 30.7% TR (i.e., uses more tokens than Full CoT), while Ans. Conv. achieves 83.7% TR but collapses to 21.9% accuracy. The negative TR values for DEER and Dynasor on this model indicate that frequent trial-answer probing can more than offset any savings from shorter reasoning chains.

- **Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking**: PUMA achieves 82.5% accuracy (+0.8 over Full CoT's 81.7%) with 28.2% TR. Dynasor nearly preserves accuracy at 79.7% but with only 3.0% TR; DEER achieves 81.3% accuracy with 22.4% TR; CCoT collapses to 54.3% accuracy despite 55.5% TR.

**Key comparative pattern.** Across all models, prompt-based methods (CCoT, CoD, Plan-and-Budget) reduce tokens but at a substantial accuracy cost on stronger reasoning models. CCoT drops from 81.7% to 54.3% on Qwen3-30B-T; CoD drops to 45.3% on the same model. This is because brevity constraints suppress necessary intermediate reasoning that the model would otherwise use to reach correct answers. Answer-level early-exit baselines show an inverse problem: Ans. Conv. is too aggressive (high TR, severe accuracy collapse—from 81.7% to 23.2% on Qwen3-30B-T), while DEER and Dynasor are inconsistent, sometimes using *more* tokens than Full CoT due to repeated probing overhead (DEER: −30.7% TR on Nemotron; Dynasor: −4.7% TR on Nemotron). PUMA occupies a different regime: meaningful token savings without the accuracy collapse of prompt-based methods or the probing overhead of answer-level methods.

**PUMA's occasional accuracy gains over Full CoT.** The slight accuracy improvements (e.g., +2.2 on DS-7B, +0.8 on Qwen3-30B-T) are consistent with the overthinking phenomenon analyzed in Appendix A (Figure 5): after reaching a correct answer, LRMs continue re-verifying or revising and occasionally drift to an incorrect conclusion. PUMA stops before this post-convergence drift, preventing some Full-CoT errors. The exit behavior analysis in Figure 4 quantifies this: W→R transitions (wrong under Full CoT, correct after PUMA stopping) account for 14.3–22.2% of verified exits across models.

**Per-benchmark detail (Table 14).** The full results table in Appendix C.3 reports per-benchmark token counts alongside accuracy and TR. Notable patterns: on AIME24 and AIME25 (the hardest benchmarks), PUMA's TR is modest (6.9–30.3% across models) but accuracy is consistently at or above Full CoT (e.g., DS-14B: +3.4 on AIME24, +0.0 on AIME25 with 16.3% and 6.9% TR respectively). On MATH-500 (the easiest benchmark), TR is higher (15.7–38.6%) with at most 1.6 points of accuracy loss. On OlympiadBench (large problem set, medium difficulty), PUMA achieves its highest TR (26.2–43.2%) while preserving accuracy within 1.2 points. Reasoning-step-level analysis (Figure 8) shows that PUMA saves 17.6–28.5% of reasoning steps, with deeper compression on models that generate longer chains (DS-7B: 28.5% step reduction, from 74.1 to 53.3 steps; Qwen3-30B-T: 27.9% reduction, from 71.0 to 52.5 steps).

#### Reasoning Quality Preservation (Table 2)

Beyond final-answer accuracy, the paper evaluates whether PUMA preserves the retained reasoning chain as a useful explanation. Using an LLM-as-Judge protocol with GPT-5.4-thinking (Appendix E), chains are scored on a 10–100 rubric across four dimensions: Completeness, Coherence, Conciseness, and Justification. The evaluation covers 896 instances across three model–benchmark combinations.

**Headline finding:** PUMA achieves the highest average retained-chain quality (54.3) among all compared methods, ranking first in Coherence (57.5), Conciseness (40.2), and Justification (54.8), while remaining close to Full CoT in Completeness (64.8 vs. 67.6).

**Per-dimension patterns:**

- **Completeness**: Full CoT naturally scores highest (67.6), since it includes all reasoning. PUMA (64.8) is only 2.8 points behind, while CCoT (64.1), DEER (62.4), and Dynasor (57.2) are lower. This indicates that PUMA truncates reasoning after convergence without removing semantically necessary derivation steps.

- **Coherence**: PUMA scores 57.5, substantially ahead of Full CoT (41.5). This counterintuitive result—a truncated chain is rated more coherent than the full chain—is explained by the nature of post-convergence overthinking: repeated verification, self-doubt, and re-derivation make the full chain *less* coherent to read, even though it contains all the correct reasoning. PUMA stops before this degradation. DEER (37.9) scores lowest on coherence, consistent with answer-level methods potentially cutting mid-sentence or mid-derivation.

- **Conciseness**: PUMA (40.2) substantially improves over Full CoT (15.7), which is expected since conciseness directly penalizes redundancy. CCoT (30.5) and Plan-and-Budget (27.2) also improve conciseness but at the cost of other dimensions.

- **Justification**: PUMA (54.8) slightly edges out CCoT (54.5) and Full CoT (51.8). DEER (46.7) and Dynasor (46.5) are lowest, suggesting that answer-level stopping can truncate the justification before it is complete.

This result is significant because it speaks to the dual role of CoT: reasoning chains are not only a means to an answer but also an explanation surfaced to users. PUMA's semantic-preserving design—stopping after meaningful exploration rather than cutting mid-development—directly targets this dual objective.

#### Wall-Clock Latency (Figure 3, Appendix C.5 Table 16)

Token reduction does not automatically translate to wall-clock speedup because early-exit methods incur overhead from trial-answer probing. Figure 3 shows that PUMA converts token savings into practical speedups while DEER and Dynasor often fail to do so.

**Overall speedups (Figure 3a–b):** PUMA achieves 1.40× speedup on DS-7B and 1.28× on DS-14B on average across four benchmarks (MATH-500, AIME24, AIME25, OlympiadBench). DEER is *slower* than Full CoT on both models (0.69× on DS-7B, 0.71× on DS-14B). Dynasor achieves 1.11× on DS-7B but is dramatically slower on DS-14B (0.21×), highlighting the overhead of frequent answer-level probing at larger model scales.

**Per-benchmark variability (Appendix C.5, Table 16):** PUMA's speedup is most pronounced on benchmarks with longer reasoning chains. On AIME25 with DS-7B, PUMA achieves 1.54× speedup (from 10.56 s/q to 6.88 s/q). On MATH-500 with DS-7B, speedup is a modest 1.13× (from 0.98 s/q to 0.87 s/q) because the baseline chains are already short. On Nemotron-8B, PUMA achieves 1.15× overall speedup.

**Overhead breakdown (Figure 3c):** The Redundancy Detector adds only 18 ms per question, accounting for 0.4–1.1% of total wall-clock time. Trial-answer probing (Answer Verification) adds 0.21–0.57 seconds per question on DS-7B and DS-14B, which is 3.8–5.6% of the per-question runtime for PUMA. The total PUMA-specific overhead (detector + probing) is approximately 4–6% of runtime, which is why token savings translate into real speedup. In contrast, DEER and Dynasor probe answers more frequently (Table 15 shows 3.3–4.3× more probes when the Redundancy Detector gate is removed), causing their probing overhead to dominate any savings from shorter chains.

#### Generalization Beyond Text-Only QA (Table 3)

The paper evaluates whether reasoning-level redundancy transfers to domains where the detector was not trained. Table 3 reports results on code generation (LiveCodeBench, 880 problems) with DS-7B and Nemotron-8B, and vision-language reasoning (MathVista and MathVision, 200 problems each) with Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking and Kimi-VL-16B-A3B-Thinking.

**Code generation (LiveCodeBench):** PUMA reduces tokens by 19.0% (DS-7B) and 18.0% (Nemotron-8B) with at most 1.5 points of pass@1 change (-1.5 and -0.5 respectively). The only adjustment from the text-only configuration is raising the redundancy threshold to τ_sim = 0.50 (from the default 0.35) to account for different step structure in code reasoning. DEER drops accuracy by 2.2 and 7.6 points respectively, with TR of only 1.6% and 19.3%. CCoT achieves higher TR (42.7% and 41.6%) but with 6.0 and 10.9 point accuracy drops. Dynasor is catastrophically slow on code (-207.6% TR on DS-7B, meaning it uses more than 3× the tokens of Full CoT), consistent with repeated probing overhead on tasks where answers are long. The budget-sweep analysis in Appendix D (Table 17) shows that relaxing CCoT's global word budget or CoD's per-step word budget does not close the accuracy gap to PUMA on LiveCodeBench.

**Vision-language reasoning (MathVista, MathVision):** PUMA is applied **completely zero-shot** with no retraining, hyperparameter tuning, or adjustment from the text-only configuration. On Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking, PUMA reduces tokens by 23.8% (MathVista) and 31.2% (MathVision) with 1.0 and 1.5 point accuracy drops respectively. On Kimi-VL-16B-A3B-Thinking, TR is 28.7% (MathVista) and 33.6% (MathVision) with accuracy changes of +0.5 and 0.0. By contrast, DEER loses 5.0–14.5 accuracy points on Qwen3-VL and 1.5–14.5 on Kimi-VL. Prompt-based baselines (CCoT, CoD) are even more destructive on vision-language tasks: CoD drops Qwen3-VL accuracy by 43.5 points on MathVista.

The paper notes that vision-language reasoning introduces unique challenges beyond text-only settings (cross-modal interactions), making the zero-shot transfer of a text-trained redundancy signal particularly meaningful. It also underscores a limitation: the Redundancy Detector is primarily trained on text reasoning traces, and broader domains may require additional calibration (acknowledged in the paper's Limitations paragraph, Section 7).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Component ablation (Table 4, Appendix C.4 Table 15):** The full-configuration PUMA is compared against five ablations on DS-7B and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking, with per-benchmark accuracy, token reduction, and trial-answer probe counts reported.

- **Removing the Redundancy Detector gate**: Setting the redundancy threshold to zero causes Answer Verification to be invoked at every eligible step rather than only at detector-flagged candidates. On DS-7B, this increases TR from 35.6% to 46.0% but drops accuracy by 4.1 points (from 60.2 to 56.1) and increases probe overhead by 3.3× (from 13.75 to 45.69 probes/question). On Qwen3-30B-T, accuracy drops 3.9 points and probe overhead increases 4.3×. This confirms that reasoning-level gating is important for both reliability and probe efficiency.

- **Removing the Loop Breaker**: On DS-7B, TR drops from 35.6% to 22.6% (a 13-percentage-point reduction), with accuracy decreasing by only 1.0 point (to 59.2). On Qwen3-30B-T, the effect is smaller (TR drops from 28.2% to 27.4%), consistent with the Loop Breaker not being activated for this model. The Loop Breaker mainly provides additional savings on models that exhibit sustained late-stage redundancy without satisfying all verification conditions.

- **AV only (w/o RD Gate and Loop Breaker)**: This leaves only Answer Verification as the stopping mechanism, without the Redundancy Detector gate or Loop Breaker. On DS-7B, accuracy drops 1.9 points, TR is comparable (37.3%), but probe overhead is 4.0× higher than full PUMA. This variant represents an answer-level-only stopping approach and confirms that reasoning-level signals improve efficiency (probe count) even when answer-level signals alone can maintain similar accuracy.

- **Removing Answer Consistency**: On DS-7B, accuracy drops 2.9 points (57.3) with TR increasing slightly to 37.8% and probe overhead increasing 1.2×. On Qwen3-30B-T, accuracy drops 2.6 points. This shows that requiring trial answers to agree across the verification window prevents some premature exits, but the accuracy cost of removing it is smaller than removing the confidence gate.

- **Removing Confidence Gate**: Accuracy drops 6.5 points on DS-7B (to 53.7) and 9.9 points on Qwen3-30B-T (to 72.6), the largest single-component accuracy loss. TR increases substantially (55.3% and 47.9%), indicating that the Confidence Gate is the primary safety mechanism preventing aggressive, low-confidence exits.

- **Removing both Answer Consistency and Confidence Gate (w/o AC and CG)**: On DS-7B, accuracy collapses by 16.2 points (to 44.0) with TR of 66.1%. On Qwen3-30B-T, accuracy drops 20.6 points (to 61.9) with TR of 55.8%. This is the worst-performing configuration, confirming that Answer Verification as a whole is essential for accuracy preservation. The Redundancy Detector alone (this ablation effectively leaves only the detector) cannot safely determine when to stop.

**Redundancy signal type (Appendix B.3, Table 10):** The embedding-based detector (PUMA's default) is compared against base (unfine-tuned) embedding, ICL-NLI (Qwen3-0.6B with 4-shot in-context prompting for binary redundancy judgments), and FT-NLI (Qwen3-0.6B fine-tuned on 10K NLI-style redundancy examples). The fine-tuned embedding detector achieves the best accuracy–efficiency tradeoff (+1.3 average ∆Acc, 27.9% TR). ICL-NLI is too conservative (8.2% TR, -1.1 ∆Acc), rarely triggering exits. FT-NLI achieves high TR (39.9%) but at a 3.8-point accuracy cost. Base embedding with varying thresholds (τ = 0.42, 0.50) achieves higher TR (41.5–41.7%) but with 3.1–3.7 point accuracy drops, confirming that contrastive fine-tuning is essential for reliable redundancy detection. The embedding detector also has a substantial inference-time cost advantage: approximately 21 ms per question vs. approximately 93 ms for both NLI variants.

**Redundancy Detector lookback window (Appendix B.4, Table 11):** The comparison window size k (how many previous steps are compared against) is varied from 1 to "all" (every prior step). On both DS-7B and Nemotron-8B, increasing k yields more aggressive exits (higher TR) but degrades ∆Acc. At k = 1 (default), average ∆Acc is +1.3 with 27.9% TR. At k = all, ∆Acc drops to -2.9 with 41.9% TR. This suggests that larger windows introduce false-positive triggers: a current step may resemble a much earlier step while still contributing useful progress relative to the immediately preceding context. The default k = 1 provides the most favorable accuracy–compression tradeoff.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity (Appendix B.7, Figure 7):** The three core stopping hyperparameters (τ_sim, λ, L) are swept on DS-7B. PUMA is robust across a wide range of settings: out of 9 alternative configurations tested, only two produce a negative ∆Acc (−0.7 at τ_sim = 0.40 and −0.1 at λ = 0.99), and both are within 1 point of Full CoT. The redundancy threshold τ_sim is the most safety-critical parameter: lowering it to 0.30 maintains token savings but eliminates the accuracy margin (∆Acc drops from +2.2 to 0.0), while raising it to 0.40 nearly halves token savings without recovering accuracy. The confidence threshold λ is stable across [0.95, 0.98], with a sharp TR drop at 0.99 (-6.3 points TR). The verification window L = 1 is more susceptible to single-step false positives; L = 3 is overly conservative; L = 2 provides the best balance.

**Answer-level signal threshold sensitivity (Appendix A.4, Figure 6):** The failure rates of standalone answer-level signals are examined by sweeping the confidence threshold λ from 0.93 to 0.97 (for confidence-based stopping) and the consistency window k from 1 to 8 (for consistency-based stopping) on DS-7B over OlympiadBench and GPQA-Diamond. Across all thresholds, standalone answer-level signals exhibit a persistent tradeoff between failure rate and token reduction, with no clear operating point that is both safe and efficient. On OlympiadBench, even conservative confidence thresholds yield high failure rates while stopping a large fraction of tokens. On GPQA-Diamond, lower failure rates are possible only when token reduction becomes small. This confirms that the limitation of answer-level signals is not merely a threshold-tuning problem.

**Answer-level signal failure analysis (Appendix A.3, Table 7):** A counterfactual analysis classifies answer-level signal failures as premature (the stopping signal fires on an incorrect answer, but the uninterrupted Full CoT trajectory would have self-corrected to the correct answer) or non-recoverable (the full trajectory would also end incorrectly). Across five models and five benchmarks, 42.4% of confidence-based failures and 54.8% of consistency-based failures are premature. This decomposition shows that answer-level signals do not merely stop on already-hopeless trajectories—they actively break trajectories that would have succeeded, by stopping during exploration or self-correction. The rates are model-dependent: on Qwen3-30B-T, 57.4% of confidence-based failures are premature, while on DS-32B, only 28.7% are premature.

**Exit behavior analysis (Figure 4):** PUMA's exit modes are analyzed across three representative models (DS-7B, DS-14B, Nemotron-8B). Verified exits, Loop Breaker exits, and full reasoning occur in different proportions across models: full reasoning accounts for 19.3–15.4% of trajectories, verified exits for 33.2–30.3%, and Loop Breaker exits for 47.4–54.3%. Verified exits are dominated by R→R transitions (69–72%: correct under both Full CoT and PUMA), with W→R (PUMA fixes a Full-CoT error) at 14.3–22.2% and R→W (PUMA breaks a Full-CoT correct answer) at 4.8–5.1%. Loop Breaker exits are dominated by W→W transitions (53–68%), confirming that the fallback mostly cuts redundant continuation from trajectories that Full CoT would also answer incorrectly. Harmful R→W transitions for Loop Breaker exits are 9.0–9.7%, higher than for verified exits, consistent with the Loop Breaker being a less reliable stopping mechanism.

**Internalization results (Section 6.2, Table 5):** PUMA-selected exit positions are used to supervise SFT, DPO, and GRPO training on DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. All trained variants are evaluated with pure vLLM inference (no PUMA modules). PUMA-RL (GRPO) achieves the strongest overall result: 67.0 average accuracy (+4.0 over Full CoT's 63.0) with 34.9% TR, outperforming training-free PUMA on both accuracy and TR. PUMA-SFT achieves 66.9 accuracy with 19.3% TR. PUMA-DPO is the most compression-oriented variant: 64.1 accuracy with 48.8% TR. The FixedExit baselines (same training recipes but with fixed-interval truncation positions instead of PUMA-selected positions) perform substantially worse: FixedExit-SFT is 12.8 accuracy points below PUMA-SFT; FixedExit-RL is 3.8 points below PUMA-RL. Standard-SFT and Standard-GRPO (no exit-position supervision) do not learn concise stopping, using more tokens than Full CoT on average (negative or near-zero TR). These results show that PUMA's exit positions carry semantically meaningful information rather than merely being shorter prefixes, and that the redundancy signal can be learned as a stopping policy that generalizes beyond the specific detector–verifier architecture.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: PUMA achieves 26.2% average token reduction while preserving accuracy.** The evidence in Tables 1 and 14 broadly supports this claim, but with important granularity. Across 25 model–benchmark combinations, the accuracy change relative to Full CoT is positive on average, but this average conceals variation: on some benchmarks, PUMA slightly *increases* accuracy (DS-7B AIME24: +10.0 points), while on others it slightly decreases (DS-32B MATH-500: -0.8 points). The "preserving accuracy" claim should be understood as "average accuracy is within 1 point of Full CoT," not "accuracy never degrades on any benchmark." The claim is qualified by the paper's own reporting, but readers should note that the 26.2% figure is a weighted average across benchmarks of varying sizes, and token reduction is not uniform across difficulty levels (e.g., GPQA-Diamond shows lower TR across models).

The more meaningful interpretation is that PUMA achieves a **substantially better accuracy–efficiency frontier** than any of the compared baselines, rather than that it literally preserves accuracy at every point. No baseline simultaneously achieves comparable accuracy and comparable token reduction; PUMA is the only method in the Pareto-optimal region of the tradeoff space. This is a stronger and more defensible claim than "26.2% average TR," which is a point estimate dependent on benchmark mix.

**Claim 2: Answer-level signals trigger prematurely while the model is still exploring or self-correcting.** This is very strongly supported by the counterfactual analysis in Appendix A (Tables 6–7). The decomposition showing that 42.4% of confidence-based failures and 54.8% of consistency-based failures are premature (model would have self-corrected) is compelling and methodologically sound. The threshold sensitivity analysis (Figure 6) further shows that this is not a tuning artifact. One limitation: the analysis is performed by applying stopping criteria retroactively to already-generated Full CoT traces, which means the model's behavior under actual early stopping (where the truncated prefix would be fed back as context) might differ. The counterfactual is the best approximation available but does not capture the causal effect of intervention on generation dynamics.

**Claim 3: The two-stage architecture (Redundancy Detector + Answer Verification) is superior to either signal alone.** Strongly supported by the ablation study (Tables 4, 15). Removing the Redundancy Detector gate drops accuracy by 4.1 points and increases probe overhead 3.3×; removing Answer Verification drops accuracy by 6.5–16.2 points. Neither variant matches the full system. The probe overhead numbers are particularly informative: the Redundancy Detector gate reduces probes by a factor of 3.3–4.3×, which is what makes the two-stage architecture computationally viable. A weakness: the ablation does not explore intermediate configurations (e.g., using the Redundancy Detector with a weaker confidence gate, or a different combination of answer-level signals). The specific thresholds (λ = 0.98, L = 2, ε = 0.03) are held constant across all ablations, so the results show the effect of removing a component at a specific operating point rather than exploring the full space of possible two-stage designs.

**Claim 4: Reasoning-level redundancy is a transferable, zero-shot signal across models, domains, and modalities.** Supported by the generalization experiments (Table 3), with significant caveats. The zero-shot transfer to vision-language reasoning is impressive: PUMA maintains accuracy with meaningful token reduction on two different VLMs without any retraining or hyperparameter adjustment. The code generation results are also positive but require a one-parameter adjustment (τ_sim raised to 0.50), which is minor but means the transfer is not literally zero-shot in the strictest sense.

The main limitation is scale: the vision-language experiments use only 200 problems per benchmark, which is small. MathVista and MathVision are selected subsets of the full test sets. The paper does not report standard errors or confidence intervals for any of the generalization experiments, so the statistical reliability of the zero-shot transfer claims is unclear. A stronger test would be a larger, more diverse set of vision-language tasks and code benchmarks with standard error reporting.

Additionally, the Redundancy Detector is trained on text reasoning traces from four specific source models. While it transfers to five target text models, two VLMs, and code, this is still a limited sample of the possible model space. The claim that redundancy is "a property of the reasoning content itself, not an artifact of a particular model's generation patterns" is supported by the observed transfer but would be more robust if tested on a wider set of model families and reasoning styles (e.g., models trained with different RL formulations that might exhibit different redundancy patterns).

**Claim 5: PUMA-selected exit positions can be internalized into model weights, and the learned policy can outperform the online framework.** Supported by Table 5, with important boundary conditions. The claim that learned policies outperform the online framework holds for PUMA-RL (+0.8 accuracy, +10.6% TR over training-free PUMA) and PUMA-DPO (-2.1 accuracy, +24.5% TR over training-free PUMA), but not for PUMA-SFT (+0.7 accuracy, -5.0% TR). The training data is math-only (12K problems from the Lightman split of MATH), meaning the internalized models are evaluated in-distribution on MATH-500 and zero-shot on harder math (AIME24) and cross-domain science (GPQA-Diamond). The in-distribution vs. zero-shot distinction matters: on MATH-500, PUMA-SFT (91.4 acc, 21.9% TR) matches training-free PUMA (89.6 acc, 38.6% TR) on accuracy but not TR; on GPQA-Diamond (cross-domain), PUMA-SFT (56.1 acc) actually outperforms training-free PUMA (49.0 acc), suggesting the learned policy may generalize better than expected. A significant limitation: this experiment uses a single base model (DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) and math-focused training data. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation in Section 7. A demonstration on at least one additional model or training domain would substantially strengthen the internalization claim.

**Unaddressed questions and missing experiments:**

1. **No combination of PUMA with prompt-based or training-based methods.** The paper compares PUMA against prompt-based baselines (CCoT, CoD) and against training-based internalization (SFT, DPO, GRPO), but never tests whether PUMA applied on top of these methods would yield further gains. For example, applying PUMA to a model already trained for concise reasoning (PUMA-SFT or PUMA-RL) might achieve even higher token savings if the learned policy occasionally overshoots and PUMA catches residual redundancy.

2. **No systematic ablation of the training data construction for the Redundancy Detector.** Appendix B.2 describes a multi-stage pipeline (seed annotation, synthetic augmentation, contrastive formatting), but there is no ablation showing which stages are load-bearing. How much does synthetic augmentation contribute vs. seed annotation? How does the choice of source models for training data affect transfer? These would help practitioners replicate the detector training.

3. **No latency comparison against model quantization or distillation.** The paper shows that PUMA improves wall-clock speedup while preserving accuracy, but does not compare against other deployment-time efficiency methods like model quantization (INT8, INT4) or distillation to smaller architectures. A practitioner deciding between PUMA and, say, running a quantized version of the same model would benefit from this comparison.

4. **Limited analysis of when PUMA fails.** The exit behavior analysis (Figure 4) gives aggregate proportions of R→W transitions, but does not provide qualitative analysis of *why* those failures occur. Are they detector false positives (detector flagged redundancy during productive reasoning), verification failures (consistent but wrong answers passed verification), or something else? Case studies of failure modes would help diagnose remaining weaknesses.

5. **No analysis of interaction with sampling temperature.** All experiments use the models' recommended temperature (0.6). Redundancy patterns might change at different temperatures (higher temperature → more diverse reasoning → easier to distinguish novel from redundant; lower temperature → more deterministic → less overthinking?), which could affect PUMA's optimal thresholds. A temperature sweep is not reported.

6. **The test sets for AIME are extremely small (30 problems each).** Results on AIME24 and AIME25 are reported with apparent precision (e.g., 60.0% accuracy on DS-7B AIME24), but with N = 30, a single problem changes accuracy by 3.3 points. Confidence intervals or standard errors are not reported, making it difficult to assess whether the observed differences (e.g., PUMA at 60.0% vs. Full CoT at 50.0%) are statistically reliable. This is a genuine limitation given the benchmark sizes.

**Overall assessment.** The experiments provide convincing evidence for PUMA's core claims within the scope tested. The accuracy–efficiency results across five models and five benchmarks establish PUMA as Pareto-superior to existing inference-time early-exit and prompt-based baselines. The ablation studies cleanly decompose the contributions of the two-stage architecture. The generalization experiments, while limited in scale, provide positive evidence for transfer. The internalization experiments demonstrate a path toward learned, deployment-efficient stopping. The primary limitations are: (1) lack of statistical rigor on small test sets, (2) limited scale of generalization experiments, (3) single-domain training for internalization, and (4) absence of comparisons against non-early-exit efficiency methods (quantization, distillation). These do not undermine the paper's claims but qualify their scope: PUMA works well for the tested models and tasks, with evidence of transfer, but the robustness to different model scales, training procedures, and task distributions remains partially demonstrated rather than fully established.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Is Replaced by Redundancy Detection, But Hard Problems Remain Unsolved

**The constraint.** PUMA's entire framework assumes that semantic redundancy in the reasoning trajectory is a reliable signal that the reasoning process has converged to a final answer. This assumption breaks down on problems where the base model never produces a correct solution trajectory. If the model's reasoning is fundamentally flawed from the start—exploring incorrect approaches, making arithmetic errors, or failing to understand the problem structure—the trajectory may converge to a wrong answer, and PUMA's Redundancy Detector will faithfully flag that convergence. The redundancy signal says "the model has stopped making novel reasoning progress," but not "the reasoning has arrived at a correct conclusion." Answer Verification can filter some of these cases (by requiring high confidence and consistency), but on problems where the model is confidently wrong—producing a coherent but incorrect derivation with high token-level probability—verification may also pass.

**The consequence.** On the hardest problems (difficulty bin 5 in the paper's AIME/GPQA benchmarks, or equivalent difficulty levels on OlympiadBench), PUMA provides approximately zero benefit. This is visible in the per-benchmark results in Table 14: on GPQA-Diamond, PUMA's token reduction ranges from 4.0% (DS-7B) to 17.8% (Qwen3-30B-T), substantially lower than on MATH-500 (15.7–38.6%) or OlympiadBench (26.2–43.2%). The exit behavior analysis in Figure 4 provides the mechanistic explanation: Loop Breaker exits dominate (47–54% of stops) and are dominated by W→W transitions (53–68%: wrong under both Full CoT and PUMA). This means PUMA is primarily saving tokens on trajectories that were hopeless anyway—it is not helping the model solve problems it otherwise couldn't, nor is it preventing the model from drifting away from correct answers it had found (which would produce W→R transitions, accounting for only 14–22% of verified exits).

This limitation is fundamental: test-time efficiency methods cannot create capability that isn't present in the base model. If the model cannot produce a correct answer, no early-exit strategy can change that outcome—it can only reduce the cost of failure. This mirrors the finding in the reference example paper (Snell et al., 2025) that test-time compute cannot compensate for fundamental capability gaps on the hardest problems.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The per-benchmark TR variation in Table 14 is the primary evidence. On the hardest benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, GPQA-Diamond), TR is systematically lower than on MATH-500 and OlympiadBench across all models. The Loop Breaker's W→W dominance in Figure 4(d) shows that the mechanism saving tokens on hard problems is a coarse fallback that terminates hopeless trajectories, not a precision stopping mechanism. The paper does not report a separate accuracy-TR analysis conditioned on problem difficulty (e.g., performance on the hardest vs. easiest quintile within each benchmark), which would directly quantify this limitation. Appendix A.3's counterfactual analysis (Table 7) shows that answer-level signals are particularly unreliable on hard benchmarks (e.g., on AIME24, confidence-based failure rates of 42–69% across models), but this analysis is for standalone answer-level signals, not for PUMA's combined detector + verifier.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. It is acknowledged implicitly in the generalization experiments (Table 3: accuracy drops slightly on vision-language tasks, up to 1.5 points) and in the exit behavior analysis (Figure 4: R→W transitions of 4.8–5.1% for verified exits, 9.0–9.7% for Loop Breaker exits), but there is no systematic analysis of when PUMA fails or what problem characteristics predict failure. The paper's Limitations paragraph (Section 7) does not explicitly call out the hard-problem failure regime, focusing instead on structural limitations (short/poorly-structured outputs, segmentation difficulty). A practitioner deploying PUMA on a problem distribution that skews toward the model's capability frontier should expect lower token savings and should be aware that PUMA cannot rescue fundamentally incorrect reasoning.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Replaced by Detector Overhead, But Training Data Construction Is Expensive and Not Accounted For

**The constraint.** PUMA eliminates the need for per-question difficulty estimation at inference time (the major unaccounted cost in Snell et al., 2025, which required 2048 samples per question). However, it replaces this with an upfront training cost for the Redundancy Detector that is substantial and not included in any efficiency calculation. The detector training pipeline (Appendix B.2) involves: (1) collecting long-CoT reasoning traces from four source models (QwQ-32B, GPT-OSS-120B, GLM-4.7-Thinking, Kimi-K2-Thinking) on AMC23 and GSM8K plus Open-Thoughts-114K, covering approximately 5,098 distinct questions and yielding approximately 1.97M candidate step pairs; (2) Stage 1 seed annotation using GPT-5-mini on 40,844 examples; (3) Stage 2 redundant-step synthesis using GPT-4o-mini on approximately 560K pairs; (4) contrastive fine-tuning of Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B with LoRA. The paper reports that "the total annotation cost across both stages is around $2,000" (Appendix B.2).

**The consequence.** The $2,000 annotation cost is modest in absolute terms but is not amortized in any of PUMA's reported efficiency metrics. The headline "26.2% average token reduction" and the wall-clock speedup numbers (1.15–1.40×) represent *incremental* inference-time savings, not total cost of ownership. A practitioner deciding whether to adopt PUMA must consider: (1) the cost of collecting source model reasoning traces (GPU-hours for the four source models on 5,098 questions); (2) the $2,000 API cost for annotation and synthesis; (3) the cost of fine-tuning the embedding model; (4) serving the additional 0.6B-parameter embedding model alongside the main reasoning model (18 ms per question, or 0.4–1.1% of runtime per Figure 3c).

More importantly, the detector is trained on a specific set of source models and may not transfer to future LRMs with substantially different reasoning patterns. If a new model family exhibits different redundancy structures—e.g., models trained with length-penalized RL that naturally produce more concise reasoning, or models that self-correct through a different mechanism that doesn't manifest as step-level semantic repetition—the detector may need retraining or recalibration. The zero-shot transfer results to VLMs (Table 3) are encouraging but cover only two vision-language models and 400 total problems. The paper does not report detector performance degradation as a function of distribution shift from the training models, nor does it provide guidelines for when retraining is necessary.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper is transparent about the detector training procedure (Appendix B.2) and the cost ($2,000), but does not amortize this cost in any reported metric. The detector quality is reported on a held-out set from the same distribution as the training data (91.26% pairwise ranking accuracy, with average cosine similarity of 0.333 for redundant pairs vs. 0.184 for novel pairs), but there is no evaluation of detector quality on out-of-distribution models or tasks. The transfer experiments (Table 3) demonstrate functional transfer (PUMA achieves token reduction with preserved accuracy) but do not report detector-level metrics (precision, recall, false positive rate) on the target domains, making it unclear whether the detector's behavior changes and verification compensates, or whether the detector transfers cleanly.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates this through the two-stage architecture: even if the detector's precision degrades on new domains, Answer Verification provides a safety layer that prevents premature exits (false positives become wasted verification probes rather than incorrect answers). The transfer experiments (Table 3) demonstrate that this layered design works in practice for the tested domains. However, the paper does not provide guidance on the expected useful lifetime of a trained detector, the conditions under which retraining is advisable, or the cost of retraining for a new model family. The internalization experiments (Section 6.2, Table 5) offer an alternative path: if redundancy detection can be distilled into the reasoning model itself, the external detector becomes unnecessary, eliminating serving overhead and the need for detector retraining. However, internalization currently requires per-model training (LoRA on DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) and math-specific data, which itself has a cost not accounted for in the efficiency metrics.

### The Detector Relies on Step Segmentation, Which Adds Overhead and May Fail on Unstructured or Very Short Outputs

**The constraint.** PUMA operates at the level of reasoning steps, not individual tokens. This requires a step segmentation procedure that partitions the raw reasoning text into coherent units before the Redundancy Detector can compare them. The paper's segmentation method (Appendix B.1) splits at blank-line boundaries, assigns coarse semantic roles using string-level cues (e.g., "calculation," "verification," "self-correction"), and merges adjacent short paragraphs to produce steps in a target length range of 200–1000 characters. This is described as a "deterministic, lightweight procedure" that "adds negligible overhead to generation." However, the effectiveness of the Redundancy Detector depends on the quality of this segmentation: if steps are too granular, redundancy signals become noisy (many short redundant snippets); if steps are too coarse, local redundancy may be hidden within large multi-paragraph steps.

**The consequence.** PUMA may be less effective or less reliable when:
- **Model outputs are very short.** On benchmarks where reasoning chains are naturally concise (e.g., simple math problems, short code tasks), steps may fall below the 200-character minimum, causing over-aggregation that obscures fine-grained redundancy patterns, or may be too few for the verification window to accumulate sufficient candidates. The GPQA-Diamond results (Table 14) show consistently lower TR (4.0–17.8%) compared to OlympiadBench (26.2–43.2%), which the paper does not explicitly attribute to chain length but which is consistent with shorter chains providing fewer opportunities for post-convergence redundancy.
- **Model outputs are poorly structured.** Some LRMs do not consistently separate reasoning steps with blank lines, or use formatting conventions (bullet points, numbered lists, code blocks) that the string-based segmenter may not handle correctly. The paper's segmentation procedure includes merging heuristics (enumerated paragraphs, semantic transitions) that are explicitly designed for the blank-line convention; models using different formatting may produce incorrectly segmented steps.
- **The domain lacks clear step boundaries.** On code generation, reasoning often interleaves code blocks with natural language explanation, making it unclear whether a code block and its preceding explanation form one step or multiple. The paper partially addresses this by raising τ_sim to 0.50 for code (Table 3), but the segmentation procedure itself is not modified.

The paper's Limitations paragraph (Section 7) acknowledges this directly: "PUMA relies on step-level reasoning traces and may be less effective when model outputs are very short, poorly structured, or difficult to segment."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report an ablation of the segmentation procedure—there is no comparison of PUMA performance under different segmentation granularities, no analysis of segmentation failures, and no evaluation of how segmenter errors propagate to the Redundancy Detector's decisions. The step-length distribution is not reported for any benchmark, making it impossible to assess what fraction of steps fall outside the 200–1000 character target range. The reasoning-step reduction numbers (Figure 8) show that PUMA reduces steps by 17.6–28.5%, with deeper compression on models that generate more steps (DS-7B: 74.1→53.3 steps; Qwen3-30B-T: 71.0→52.5 steps), but this does not reveal whether segmentation quality varies across models or benchmarks.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 7 but does not propose a mitigation. The segmentation procedure is intentionally simple and string-based to keep overhead low and avoid dependence on learned components, which is a reasonable design choice but means the limitation is inherent to the architecture. A practitioner deploying PUMA on a new model should verify that the model's output format is compatible with the blank-line segmentation heuristic, or be prepared to adapt the segmenter. The paper does not provide guidance on what adaptation entails or what failure modes to watch for.

### No Comparison Against Non-Early-Exit Efficiency Methods (Quantization, Distillation, Speculative Decoding)

**The constraint.** PUMA is evaluated exclusively against other inference-time early-exit methods and prompt-based compression methods. The paper does not compare PUMA against standard deployment-time efficiency techniques that a practitioner would realistically consider alongside early exit: model quantization (INT8, INT4), knowledge distillation to a smaller architecture, or speculative decoding (using a draft model to accelerate autoregressive generation). These methods also trade accuracy for efficiency, and a deployment decision would typically involve evaluating all available options.

**The consequence.** The paper's claim that PUMA enables "efficient reasoning" is evaluated within a narrow methodological silo. A practitioner choosing between (a) deploying the full-precision model with PUMA and (b) deploying a quantized version of the model without early exit has no direct evidence from this paper to guide the decision. Quantization might achieve comparable speedup with a different accuracy–efficiency tradeoff curve; speculative decoding might provide latency improvements orthogonal to token reduction. The wall-clock speedups reported (1.15–1.40×, Figure 3) are meaningful but modest compared to the typical speedups from quantization (2–4× for INT8, with varying accuracy impact depending on model and hardware) or speculative decoding (1.5–3× for compatible draft models). PUMA might stack additively with these methods (e.g., applying PUMA to a quantized model) or might interact poorly (e.g., quantization changes the model's token-level probability distribution, potentially affecting the Confidence Gate's calibration).

The paper also does not compare against simple token-budget truncation (stop generation after a fixed number of tokens or steps), which is the most deployment-friendly baseline of all. While prompt-based methods like CCoT and CoD approximate budgeted reasoning through prompting, a hard truncation baseline (e.g., "stop after 1000 tokens and take the last boxed answer") would establish a lower bound on what naive length-based stopping achieves without any learned components. The paper's FixedExit baselines in the internalization experiments (Section 6.2, Table 5) approximate this for supervised training but not for inference-time early exit.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There are no quantization, distillation, speculative decoding, or hard-truncation baselines in any experiment. The wall-clock experiments (Figure 3, Appendix C.5 Table 16) use vLLM with the default model precision (presumably FP16/BF16) and do not explore lower-precision serving.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap. The choice of baselines is methodologically appropriate for a paper whose primary contribution is a new early-exit framework—it is standard to compare against the most similar existing methods—but a practitioner-focused limitations analysis must note that the absolute efficiency gains (26.2% token reduction, 1.15–1.40× speedup) should be evaluated against the broader landscape of deployment optimization techniques, not just against other early-exit methods. The paper's plug-and-play design is compatible with quantization (the Redundancy Detector and Answer Verification are external to the main model and should work with quantized weights), but this compatibility is not tested.

### Internalization Is Demonstrated on Only One Model with Math-Only Training Data

**The constraint.** The internalization experiments (Section 6.2, Table 5) show that PUMA-selected exit positions can supervise models to learn concise stopping behavior. However, this is demonstrated on a single base model (DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) trained exclusively on 12K math problems from the Lightman split of MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021). The resulting models are evaluated on MATH-500 (in-distribution), AIME24 (harder math, zero-shot), and GPQA-Diamond (cross-domain science, zero-shot).

**The consequence.** The paper cannot claim that internalization is a general technique. The single-model, single-domain training setup leaves open several questions that would determine whether internalization is practically useful: (1) Does internalization work for non-math domains? Training on math and evaluating on science (GPQA-Diamond) shows some transfer (PUMA-SFT achieves 56.1% on GPQA-Diamond vs. 49.0% for training-free PUMA), but this is a single cross-domain test on only 198 problems. (2) Does internalization work for larger models? Training is done on DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B with LoRA; scaling internalization to 32B or 70B models may require full fine-tuning or different adaptation strategies. (3) Is the internalized policy robust to distribution shift in reasoning style? If the base model is updated (e.g., through RLHF or continued pretraining), does the internalized stopping policy degrade gracefully or catastrophically? (4) Does the internalized model maintain its general capabilities, or does training on truncated reasoning chains cause regression on tasks not represented in the training data?

The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly in Section 7: "Our internalization experiments are also limited to one base model and math-focused training data, leaving larger-scale and cross-domain learned stopping policies for future work."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The internalization results in Table 5 provide a promising initial demonstration. PUMA-RL achieves 67.0 average accuracy (+4.0 over Full CoT's 63.0) with 34.9% TR, outperforming training-free PUMA (66.2 accuracy, 24.3% TR). The cross-domain transfer to GPQA-Diamond shows PUMA-SFT at 56.1% vs. training-free PUMA at 49.0%, suggesting the learned policy may generalize better than expected. The FixedExit baselines confirm that PUMA's exit positions are semantically meaningful rather than merely shorter (e.g., PUMA-SFT outperforms FixedExit-SFT by 12.8 accuracy points on average). However, these are all on the same base model, and the training data is math-only. There is no evidence about whether these findings replicate on other model families, other domains, or larger scales.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags this as a limitation (Section 7) and frames the internalization experiments as a proof-of-concept. The three training paradigms (SFT, DPO, GRPO) provide a useful comparison of different internalization strategies, showing that RL (PUMA-RL) achieves the best accuracy–efficiency balance while preference learning (PUMA-DPO) achieves the highest compression. This is informative for practitioners choosing an internalization approach, but it does not address the generalization question. The paper suggests "larger-scale and cross-domain learned stopping policies for future work," which is a clear agenda item rather than a claim about current capability.

---

### The Detector Is Evaluated Only on End-to-End Accuracy and Token Reduction, Not on Intermediate Signal Quality per Domain

**The constraint.** The paper evaluates PUMA's performance through end-to-end metrics: final-answer accuracy, total token reduction, wall-clock speedup, and retained CoT quality. However, the Redundancy Detector's intermediate signal quality—its precision, recall, false positive rate, and calibration as a function of problem difficulty, model family, and domain—is not systematically reported for the main evaluation models and benchmarks. The detector is evaluated on a held-out set from its training distribution (91.26% pairwise ranking accuracy, Appendix B.2), but this set consists of anchor-positive-negative triples from the same source models used for training (QwQ-32B, GPT-OSS-120B, GLM-4.7-Thinking, Kimi-K2-Thinking), not from the target evaluation models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill, Nemotron, Qwen3).

**The consequence.** It is impossible to determine from the paper's results whether PUMA's strong end-to-end performance on target models is because (a) the detector transfers cleanly and accurately identifies redundant steps on target models, or (b) the detector is imperfect on target models but Answer Verification compensates by filtering out unsafe exits triggered by detector false positives. In case (a), the detector is robust and PUMA's performance should be stable. In case (b), PUMA's performance depends on the interplay between detector errors and verification stringency—if the detector produces many false positives, Answer Verification must be strict (high λ, long verification window L) to maintain accuracy, which reduces token savings. The paper's hyperparameter sweep (Appendix B.7, Figure 7) shows that λ = 0.98 and L = 2 provide the best balance, but this could be because the detector produces a specific false positive rate on the target models that these thresholds are tuned to handle. If a new model produces more or fewer false positives, the optimal thresholds may shift.

This limitation is practically important because it affects how a practitioner should adapt PUMA to a new model. If the detector transfers cleanly (case a), adaptation may require only minor threshold tuning. If the detector requires Answer Verification to compensate (case b), the practitioner may need to re-tune verification hyperparameters for each new model, which requires a held-out set with ground-truth labels—a non-trivial requirement.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The detector transfer evidence is indirect. The zero-shot vision-language results (Table 3) show that PUMA maintains accuracy with meaningful token reduction on VLMs without hyperparameter tuning, which suggests at least partial detector transfer (since the detector is not retrained and verification hyperparameters are not changed). The comparison of redundancy signal types (Appendix B.3, Table 10) shows that the fine-tuned embedding detector outperforms base embedding and NLI-based alternatives on the target models, but this is an end-to-end comparison, not a direct measurement of detector precision/recall on target model steps. The ablation of the lookback window (Appendix B.4, Table 11) shows that increasing k degrades ∆Acc, which is consistent with larger windows introducing false-positive triggers, but does not quantify the false positive rate.

**Mitigation status.** The paper's two-stage architecture mitigates the consequence of detector imperfection: false positives become wasted verification probes rather than incorrect early exits. This is a deliberate design choice that makes the system robust to detector noise. However, the paper does not provide detector-level diagnostics that would help a practitioner assess whether the detector is working well on their model or needs retraining. Reporting per-model detector precision/recall on a manually annotated subset of steps would substantially improve the transparency of the transfer claim. As it stands, a practitioner must rely on end-to-end validation (accuracy on a held-out set with their model) to determine whether PUMA works, without being able to diagnose whether failures are due to detector problems or verification problems.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **conceptual reframing** rather than a paradigm shift: it recasts the inference-time early-exit problem from "when does the answer look ready?" to "has the reasoning trajectory converged, and is the converged answer correct?" This reframing matters because it identifies a failure mode—premature exits during active exploration or self-correction—that the field's predominant answer-level signals (confidence, trial-answer consistency) are structurally blind to. The counterfactual analysis in Appendix A (Tables 6–7) provides the diagnostic evidence that makes this reframing stick: 42.4% of confidence-based failures and 54.8% of consistency-based failures occur on trajectories where the uninterrupted model *would have* self-corrected. These are not hopeless trajectories where early stopping merely accelerates an inevitable wrong answer—they are trajectories where answer-level signals actively prevent the model from reaching a correct solution. Threshold tuning does not resolve this (Figure 6): sweeping confidence thresholds from 0.93 to 0.97 and consistency windows from 1 to 8 still shows a persistent tradeoff with no safe-and-efficient operating point.

The significance of this reframing is that it changes what "good" early-exit research should optimize for. Prior work implicitly assumed that better answer-readiness detection (more calibrated confidence, longer consistency windows, better hidden-state probes) would solve the early-exit problem. PUMA's results suggest instead that **answer-readiness and reasoning-convergence are orthogonal signals that must be combined**, and that the architecture for combining them (trigger-validation pipeline vs. single-signal threshold) matters as much as the signal quality itself. The ablation study (Table 4, Appendix C.4 Table 15) quantifies this: removing the Redundancy Detector gate drops accuracy by 4.1 points and increases probing overhead 3.3×; removing Answer Verification drops accuracy by 6.5–16.2 points. Neither signal alone achieves the full system's accuracy–efficiency balance. This makes a case that future early-exit methods should adopt **two-stage trigger-validation architectures** by default, with the trigger optimized for coverage (find all potentially safe exit points) and the validator optimized for precision (accept only genuinely safe ones)—rather than searching for a single signal and threshold that simultaneously achieves both.

The paper also **reconciles conflicting intuitions** about whether LLMs can reason efficiently. Prompt-based compression advocates argue that models can be instructed to be concise; the evidence here (Table 1: CCoT drops from 81.7% to 54.3% on Qwen3-30B-T; CoD drops to 45.3%) shows that brevity instructions on strong reasoning models suppress necessary intermediate reasoning rather than selectively removing redundancy. Answer-level early-exit advocates argue that models know when they're done; the evidence here (Appendix A: 42–55% premature failure rates across models) shows that answer-level signals alone cannot safely distinguish exploration from convergence. PUMA demonstrates a third path: **don't instruct the model to be concise, and don't trust answer-level signals alone—instead, monitor the reasoning trajectory for semantic convergence, and only then check whether the converged answer is correct**. This is a more nuanced position than either "just ask the model to be brief" or "just check if the answer is confident," and it provides a unified explanation for why both prior approaches have inconsistent results: briefness instructions degrade when problems require extended reasoning; answer-level signals fail when reasoning hasn't converged.

The paper redirects research attention in several ways:

- **Toward reasoning-level monitoring.** The finding that step-level semantic similarity transfers zero-shot across text models, code, and vision-language reasoning (Table 3) suggests that reasoning trajectory structure is a rich, underexploited signal for inference-time control. This opens a new axis of research beyond token-level probabilities and answer-level consistency.

- **Toward detector–verifier architectures.** The two-stage design is not specific to PUMA's particular detector (embedding similarity) or verifier (confidence + consistency). Future work could substitute different convergence detectors (e.g., entropy-based, NLI-based, learned probes) and different verification strategies (e.g., self-consistency voting, verifier model scoring) within the same architectural template.

- **Away from single-signal threshold tuning.** The threshold sensitivity analysis (Appendix A.4, Figure 6) provides evidence that no single threshold for confidence or consistency resolves the premature-exit problem—this is a structural limitation, not a tuning artifact. This suggests that research effort spent on better calibration of answer-level signals may be better spent on complementary reasoning-level signals.

- **Toward learned stopping policies.** The internalization results (Table 5: PUMA-RL achieves 67.0 accuracy, 34.9% TR, outperforming training-free PUMA at 66.2 accuracy, 24.3% TR) suggest that the most efficient long-term solution may be to train models that inherently know when to stop reasoning, rather than deploying external monitoring frameworks. The fact that a learned policy *outperforms* the framework that generated its training data is non-trivial: it suggests that models can learn to anticipate convergence from internal representations, stopping earlier than post-hoc step-level similarity comparisons would allow.

However, the paper does **not** resolve the hard-problem failure mode that bedevils all test-time efficiency methods. On the hardest problems (AIME, GPQA-Diamond), PUMA's token reduction is modest (4.0–30.3% across models; Table 14) and its exit behavior is dominated by Loop Breaker W→W transitions (53–68%; Figure 4d)—cutting losses on hopeless trajectories rather than enabling correct solutions. The paper is clear-eyed about this limitation, but it means that the landscape shift is confined to the easy-to-medium difficulty regime where the base model can already produce correct answers and the challenge is removing post-convergence redundancy. The genuinely hard problem—how to make reasoning more efficient on problems the model can barely solve—remains open.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Detector-level diagnostics on target models.** The paper evaluates PUMA end-to-end (accuracy, token reduction), but never reports the Redundancy Detector's intermediate signal quality (precision, recall, false positive rate) on the target evaluation models. A direct follow-up would annotate a subset of DeepSeek-R1-Distill, Nemotron, and Qwen3 reasoning steps with binary redundancy labels (novel vs. redundant) and measure how the detector's 91.26% held-out accuracy on source models (Appendix B.2) transfers to target models. The key question: does PUMA work because the detector transfers cleanly (precision and recall remain high on target models), or because Answer Verification compensates for detector noise by filtering false positives? If the latter, practitioners adapting PUMA to new models would need to re-tune verification hyperparameters rather than trusting the detector to generalize. A strong study would report per-model precision-recall curves, analyze whether detector errors correlate with problem difficulty or reasoning chain length, and measure how verification stringency (λ, L) interacts with detector quality to determine the achievable accuracy–efficiency frontier.

**Dynamic lookback windows conditioned on reasoning phase.** The lookback window analysis (Appendix B.4, Table 11) shows that larger windows (k = 8, k = all) increase token reduction but degrade accuracy, likely because a current step may resemble a much earlier step while still contributing useful progress relative to the immediately preceding context. This suggests a more nuanced approach: the optimal lookback window may depend on the reasoning phase. Early in the trajectory, when the model is exploring different approaches, a small window (k = 1) is appropriate—semantic similarity to the immediate predecessor signals local convergence. Late in the trajectory, when the model is in verification mode, a larger window might be appropriate—semantic similarity to any prior step signals that the model is re-treading old ground. A follow-up study would implement a phase-conditioned lookback policy: classify the current reasoning phase (exploration, derivation, verification, self-correction) using lightweight heuristics or a learned classifier, and select k per phase. The evaluation would measure whether phase-conditioned k achieves higher token reduction than fixed k = 1 while maintaining accuracy, ideally closing some of the gap to larger fixed windows without incurring their false-positive penalty. The reasoning-phase classifier could itself be a small fine-tuned model, leveraging the same step-embedding representations already computed by the detector.

**Internalization at scale with multi-domain training.** The internalization experiments (Section 6.2, Table 5) are a proof-of-concept on a single model (DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) with math-only training data (12K MATH problems). Two critical questions remain unanswered. First, does internalization scale to larger models? Training PUMA-RL on DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B or Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking would test whether the learned stopping policy degrades or improves with model scale—larger models may have richer internal representations that enable earlier convergence anticipation, or may be harder to steer with LoRA-rank adaptation. Second, does multi-domain internalization produce a generalizable concise-reasoning policy? Training on a mixture of math, code, and science reasoning (e.g., MATH + LiveCodeBench + GPQA training splits) and evaluating on held-out benchmarks from all three domains would test whether the model learns a domain-general stopping strategy or overfits to domain-specific redundancy patterns. A strong negative result—multi-domain training produces worse per-domain performance than single-domain training—would suggest that redundancy patterns are domain-specific and that internalization requires per-domain specialization. A strong positive result—multi-domain training matches or exceeds per-domain training—would suggest that concise stopping is a generalizable meta-skill, opening the door to "efficient reasoning" as a first-class model capability trained once and deployed everywhere.

**Combining PUMA with length-penalized reinforcement learning.** The paper compares PUMA against prompt-based compression and answer-level early exit, but never against training-based length-control methods like length-penalized RL (e.g., L1; Aggarwal and Welleck, 2025), which directly optimize the reasoning model to produce shorter chains. These methods and PUMA target the same goal—reduce redundant tokens—through different mechanisms: length-penalized RL reshapes the model's generation distribution to favor conciseness; PUMA monitors and truncates at inference time. A natural combination would be to apply PUMA on top of a length-penalized model. If length-penalized RL already removes most redundancy, PUMA's additional savings should be small (ceiling effect). If length-penalized RL reduces but doesn't eliminate overthinking, PUMA should provide further gains, potentially with different optimal thresholds (since the redundancy patterns of a concise model may differ from those of a verbose model). A negative result—PUMA provides no benefit on length-penalized models—would suggest that training-based and inference-time approaches are substitutes rather than complements. A positive result—PUMA provides additional savings even on length-optimized models—would establish that residual overthinking survives training interventions and that inference-time monitoring remains valuable as a safety net.

**Failure mode taxonomy and targeted mitigation.** The paper provides aggregate exit behavior analysis (Figure 4: R→R, W→R, R→W, W→W proportions per exit mode) but does not qualitatively analyze why harmful R→W transitions (4.8–5.1% for verified exits, 9.0–9.7% for Loop Breaker exits) occur. A follow-up study would collect and categorize PUMA failure cases—specifically, the R→W trajectories where PUMA stops at a correct-answer prefix but the trial answer is wrong, or where PUMA's stopping prevents a later self-correction that Full CoT would have performed. The categorization might reveal: (a) detector false positives (the detector flagged redundancy during productive reasoning that was actually novel), (b) verification failures (the model produced a consistent, confident but wrong answer at the verification window), (c) post-convergence drift that PUMA failed to prevent (the model had the correct answer, drifted away, and PUMA stopped after the drift), or (d) segmentation artifacts (step boundaries obscured the novelty of reasoning). Each category suggests a different mitigation: (a) better detector training or domain adaptation, (b) stronger verification (e.g., requiring the trial answer to be consistent with intermediate derivations, not just with other trial answers), (c) earlier stopping (lowering τ_sim to catch convergence sooner), (d) improved segmentation. A taxonomy with per-category frequency counts would provide a roadmap for where PUMA improvement effort should be concentrated, and would help practitioners anticipate failure modes in their own deployments.

**Integration with constrained decoding for safety-critical applications.** PUMA's Redundancy Detector identifies when reasoning has converged; Answer Verification checks whether the converged answer is confident and consistent. Neither mechanism checks whether the retained reasoning chain *logically supports* the final answer. In safety-critical applications (medical reasoning, legal analysis, autonomous system planning), the user needs not just a correct final answer but a verifiably sound reasoning chain. A follow-up would add a third verification stage: after PUMA selects a stopping point, use a separate verifier model (or the same model in a verification prompt) to check whether the retained reasoning prefix logically entails the final answer. If entailment fails, either resume generation (the prefix is incomplete) or flag the output for human review. The evaluation would measure: (a) how often PUMA's retained chains pass logical entailment checks vs. Full CoT chains, (b) whether entailment-based filtering eliminates the residual R→W failures, and (c) the additional latency cost of the entailment check. This would extend PUMA from "preserving accuracy" to "preserving logical soundness," making it suitable for high-stakes deployments where a correct answer with an incomplete justification is insufficient.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost reduction for API-based reasoning services.** The most direct application is reducing per-query cost for users of paid reasoning APIs (e.g., OpenAI o1, DeepSeek-R1, Kimi-K2). These services charge by output token, so PUMA's 26.2% average token reduction directly translates to a ~26% cost reduction for queries on the tested benchmark distributions. The wall-clock results (Figure 3: 1.15–1.40× speedup) mean users also experience lower latency. For a service processing 1 million reasoning queries per day at an average of 8,000 output tokens per query and a cost of $0.004 per output token (roughly current pricing for top-tier reasoning models), a 26% token reduction saves approximately $8,300 per day. PUMA is particularly well-suited for API services because it is plug-and-play: the service provider can deploy PUMA as an inference-time middleware layer without modifying model weights, making it compatible with existing serving infrastructure (vLLM, with the Redundancy Detector served on the same node adding only 18 ms per query). The primary risk is the hard-problem failure mode: on the most difficult queries (AIME-level competition math, graduate-level science), token savings are lower (4–30%), so the cost reduction is smaller precisely where per-query costs are highest (since hard problems generate longer chains). A service provider might deploy PUMA with a difficulty-dependent policy: apply PUMA aggressively on routine queries (high savings) and fall back to Full CoT on queries flagged as difficult by a lightweight classifier.

**Batch inference for synthetic data generation and self-improvement pipelines.** The paper's internalization results (Section 6.2, Table 5) point toward a specific workflow: use PUMA to generate concise, high-quality reasoning traces at scale, then train models on those traces. In self-improvement pipelines (e.g., STaR, ReSTᴱᴹ, rejection sampling fine-tuning), a model generates reasoning traces on training problems, correct traces are retained, and the model is fine-tuned on them. Running PUMA during the generation phase would produce retained traces that are 26% shorter on average, reducing the cost of the generation phase and producing training data that encourages concise reasoning. The internalization experiments show that models trained on PUMA-truncated traces (PUMA-SFT, PUMA-DPO, PUMA-RL) learn to stop earlier without external monitoring—suggesting a virtuous cycle: PUMA generates efficient training data → trained model internalizes efficiency → deployed model requires less (or no) runtime monitoring → inference costs decrease further. The key risk is that PUMA's detector and verifier may make errors on some fraction of traces, introducing noisy training data. The PUMA-SFT variant filters to examples where verification passes and the regenerated answer is correct (Appendix F), providing a quality-control mechanism that can be tuned (stricter verification → fewer but higher-quality training examples).

**On-device deployment of reasoning-capable small models.** The 7B-scale models tested (DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B) are within the range of what can run on consumer hardware or edge devices. On these models, PUMA achieves 15.7–35.6% token reduction (Table 1), which directly reduces memory pressure and latency for local inference. More importantly, PUMA's wall-clock speedup (1.40× on DS-7B, 1.15× on Nemotron-8B; Figure 3, Appendix C.5 Table 16) means that a mobile device running a 7B reasoning model locally can process queries faster without accuracy loss. The plug-and-play design is critical for this use case: users can apply PUMA to any reasoning model without per-model fine-tuning or calibration, enabling a "download model, add PUMA middleware, deploy" workflow. The zero-shot transfer results to vision-language models (Table 3: 23.8–33.6% token reduction on Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking and Kimi-VL-16B-A3B-Thinking) are particularly relevant for on-device multimodal applications (e.g., photo-based math tutoring, document QA), where reducing generation time improves user experience. The primary limitation for on-device deployment is the additional ~0.6B parameters for the Redundancy Detector. While 0.6B is small relative to the main model, it still requires memory and compute on resource-constrained devices. The internalization results (Table 5) offer a solution: once a model has been trained to internalize PUMA's stopping signal, the external detector is no longer needed, and the deployed model runs with pure vLLM inference at the reported token reduction (34.9% for PUMA-RL) without additional components.

**Reducing overthinking in agentic and tool-use reasoning.** The paper does not test agentic reasoning (e.g., ReAct-style interleaved reasoning and action), but the underlying problem—continued reasoning after a solution has stabilized—is likely present in agent settings, where models may re-verify plans, re-check tool outputs, or cycle through already-considered options. PUMA's reasoning-level redundancy signal is, in principle, independent of whether the reasoning is about math, code, or action plans: if successive reasoning steps become semantically similar without adding new progress, the trajectory has likely converged. A deployment scenario would apply PUMA to an agent loop: after each reasoning step (before the corresponding action), check for redundancy; if the reasoning has converged, skip remaining reasoning steps and proceed to action execution. The benefit would be reduced latency in agent workflows (each reasoning step costs time before the agent acts) and reduced API costs for multi-turn agent interactions. The risk is that agent reasoning may have different convergence patterns than math reasoning—exploring multiple action plans sequentially may produce steps that look semantically redundant (similar analysis applied to different options) but are actually productive. Adapting PUMA for this setting would likely require training the Redundancy Detector on agent reasoning traces (ReAct, tool-use trajectories) with domain-appropriate redundancy definitions, and potentially adjusting verification to check action correctness rather than answer correctness.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions PUMA primarily against other inference-time early-exit methods and prompt-based compression. The decision framework it implies, grounded in its experimental results, is:

- **Prefer PUMA over answer-level early-exit methods (DEER, Dynasor, Answer Convergence) when** the reasoning model produces long chains where post-convergence overthinking is prevalent (41–52% of tokens are post-answer redundancy; Figure 5). PUMA's two-stage design provides higher precision (fewer premature exits) at comparable or better token reduction, and avoids the probing overhead that causes DEER and Dynasor to sometimes use *more* tokens than Full CoT (Table 14: DEER at −30.7% TR on Nemotron; Dynasor at −4.7% TR on Nemotron). The specific advantage is that PUMA invokes verification only at redundancy-flagged candidates, reducing probes 3.3–4.3× compared to un-gated verification (Table 15), which is what makes the accuracy–efficiency tradeoff strictly better.

- **Prefer PUMA over prompt-based compression (CCoT, CoD, Plan-and-Budget) when** the reasoning model is strong and brevity instructions suppress necessary intermediate reasoning. On Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking, CCoT drops accuracy from 81.7% to 54.3% and CoD to 45.3%, while PUMA achieves 82.5% accuracy with 28.2% TR (Table 1). The failure mode of prompt-based methods is that they apply uniform brevity constraints regardless of problem difficulty; PUMA adapts per-problem through the Redundancy Detector's online monitoring, stopping only when convergence is detected. If the deployment prioritizes preserved accuracy above maximum token savings, PUMA is the stronger choice.

- **Prefer training-based internalization (PUMA-SFT, PUMA-DPO, PUMA-RL) over inference-time PUMA when** the deployment environment cannot serve an external Redundancy Detector (e.g., memory-constrained devices, strict latency budgets that cannot tolerate even 18 ms overhead, or serving infrastructure that does not support colocated auxiliary models). PUMA-RL achieves 67.0 average accuracy with 34.9% TR vs. training-free PUMA's 66.2 accuracy with 24.3% TR (Table 5), meaning internalization actually improves both accuracy and efficiency while eliminating the external detector. The cost is upfront training (LoRA fine-tuning on 12K math problems) and potential domain specificity (the internalized model is currently demonstrated only with math training data; cross-domain generalization to GPQA-Diamond is positive but limited to 198 problems).

- **Prefer Full CoT (no early exit) when** the problem distribution is skewed toward the hardest difficulty tier where PUMA provides minimal token savings and the risk of R→W transitions, while small (4.8–5.1% for verified exits, 9.0–9.7% for Loop Breaker exits; Figure 4), is unacceptable. In high-stakes settings where every incorrect answer carries a significant cost (medical diagnosis, legal reasoning, safety-critical planning), the 26% average token savings may not justify even a small accuracy risk. Additionally, if the model's reasoning chains are naturally short (similar to GPQA-Diamond where PUMA achieves only 4.0–17.8% TR; Table 14), the overhead of detector inference and verification probing may erase any latency benefit.
