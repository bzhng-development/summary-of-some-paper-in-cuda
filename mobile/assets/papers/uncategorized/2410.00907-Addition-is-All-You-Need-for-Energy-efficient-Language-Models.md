# Addition is All You Need for Energy-efficient Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2410.00907](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.00907)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces the **linear-complexity multiplication (L-Mul)** algorithm, which approximates floating-point tensor multiplication using only integer addition operations — a radically simpler operation that on modern hardware costs roughly 1/37th the energy of an fp32 multiply.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces the **linear-complexity multiplication (L-Mul)** algorithm, which approximates floating-point tensor multiplication using only integer addition operations — a radically simpler operation that on modern hardware costs roughly 1/37th the energy of an fp32 multiply. Evaluated on transformer-based language models including Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and Gemma2-2b-It across a broad suite of textual, visual, and mathematical benchmarks (MMLU, BBH, GSM8k, VQA, POPE, Llava-Bench), the method demonstrates that replacing all element-wise and matrix multiplications in the attention mechanism with L-Mul is almost lossless — averaging just 0.07% performance degradation across commonsense, structural reasoning, and language understanding tasks — while enabling an estimated 95% energy reduction for element-wise tensor multiplications and 80% for dot products. The paper further establishes that a 3-bit-mantissa L-Mul achieves higher precision than fp8-e5m2 multiplication, that a 4-bit-mantissa L-Mul is comparable to fp8-e4m3, and that full-model fine-tuning with L-Mul replacing all multiplication operations matches standard fp8-e4m3 accumulation precision on GSM8k, establishing that integer-addition-based tensor arithmetic can substitute for floating-point multipliers across an entire transformer model only when the underlying numerical precision is preserved at or above the equivalent fp8 level.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Floating-Point Multiplication Is the Energy Bottleneck in Neural Computation

This paper addresses a fundamental problem in the hardware-level execution of neural networks: **floating-point multiplication dominates the energy budget of tensor computations**, and this dominance grows proportionally with model scale. Modern transformer-based language models perform an enormous volume of tensor operations — attention mechanisms scale as $O(N^2)$ with input context length, and linear transformations multiply matrices with millions of parameters. Virtually all of these operations involve floating-point multiplications, which the paper characterizes (citing Horowitz, 2014) as substantially more expensive than addition:

- Multiplying two fp32 numbers costs **3.7 pJ**, while adding two int32 numbers costs **0.1 pJ** — a **37× difference**.
- Even at reduced precision, fp16 multiplication costs **1.1 pJ** versus **0.05 pJ** for int16 addition (a ~22× difference).
- fp8 multiplication costs approximately **0.25–0.4 pJ**, while integer addition at comparable bit widths costs far less.

This is not a marginal efficiency concern. The paper situates the problem in concrete terms: ChatGPT's daily electricity consumption in early 2023 was estimated at **564 MWh** — equivalent to the daily usage of 18,000 U.S. households — and worst-case projections suggest Google's AI services could consume **29.3 TWh per year**, comparable to Ireland's total electricity usage (de Vries, 2023). These figures are for inference alone; training large models amplifies the energy cost substantially.

The theoretical reason multiplication is so much more expensive than addition is straightforward: an $n$-bit integer adder has $O(n)$ gate complexity (a chain of full adders with carry propagation), while an $n$-bit floating-point multiplier requires $O(e)$ work for exponent addition, $O(m^2)$ work for mantissa multiplication (where $m$ is the number of mantissa bits), plus rounding logic. The mantissa multiplication alone involves $(m+1)^2$ AND gates, multiple levels of half and full adders, and carry propagation across the entire partial product array. This $O(m^2)$ term is the complexity bottleneck, and it is what L-Mul eliminates.

### Why This Gap Matters: Three Distinct Pressures

The paper implicitly identifies three converging pressures that make the multiplication-addition energy gap increasingly critical:

**First, the scaling regime.** Transformer models have grown from millions to hundreds of billions of parameters, and the attention mechanism — which performs a matrix multiplication between query and key tensors for every pair of tokens in the input — scales quadratically with sequence length. As models are deployed on longer contexts (tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens), the number of floating-point multiplications grows superlinearly, making the energy cost per query increasingly dominated by multiply operations. Every multiplication replaced by an addition reduces the attention mechanism's energy budget toward the $O(N^2)$ addition baseline rather than the $O(N^2)$ multiply baseline.

**Second, the deployment landscape.** The paper notes (Section 1) that AI inference is increasingly moving toward edge devices, robotics, and mobile platforms where energy budgets are severely constrained and where floating-point multiplication units consume disproportionate die area and power. A method that replaces multipliers with adders enables hardware designs that are not only more energy-efficient but also physically smaller — fewer transistors dedicated to arithmetic units means more room for memory, control logic, or simply lower manufacturing cost.

**Third, the diminishing returns of precision reduction alone.** The dominant existing strategy for reducing tensor computation cost is quantization — storing and computing with lower-bit representations such as fp16, bf16, fp8 (both e4m3 and e5m2 variants), int8, fp4, and int4 (the paper cites Kalamkar et al., 2019; Micikevicius et al., 2023; Dettmers et al., 2022, 2024). Reducing bit width reduces the $m$ in the $O(m^2)$ mantissa multiplication term, but it does not change the fundamental operation: you are still *multiplying*, just with fewer bits. The paper's key insight is that multiplication itself — not just the bit width — is the problem. Even fp8 multiplication (which the paper estimates at ~300 gate-level operations) is more complex than an integer addition at a comparable bit width. And critically, quantization to 8 bits or below introduces precision loss that can degrade model accuracy, especially for outlier weights or attention computations where small errors compound across many operations.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates existing efficiency approaches into three categories and identifies specific limitations in each:

**1. Pruning (Han et al., 2015a,b; Wang et al., 2020).** Network pruning reduces the number of connections between layers by identifying and removing less important weights. This *indirectly* reduces the number of multiplications by making matrices sparser. The paper identifies two limitations: (a) pruning typically requires task-specific re-training after important weights are identified, making it cumbersome for general-purpose models deployed across many tasks; and (b) pruning reduces the *count* of multiplications but does not change the *nature* of the remaining operations — each surviving weight still requires a floating-point multiply. L-Mul, in contrast, is orthogonal: it changes *how* each operation is computed, not *how many* operations exist. The two could be combined (prune to reduce operation count, then use L-Mul for the remaining operations), but they address different levels of the efficiency stack.

**2. I/O and memory optimization (FlashAttention: Dao et al., 2022; Dao; PagedAttention: Kwon et al., 2023; TPU architectures: Jouppi et al., 2017).** These methods focus on the bottleneck of moving tensors between GPU SRAM and high-bandwidth memory (HBM). FlashAttention, for example, restructures the attention computation to minimize HBM reads/writes by computing attention in tiles that fit in SRAM, dramatically reducing memory bandwidth consumption. The paper explicitly acknowledges these approaches as important and complementary, but notes that they optimize the *data movement* aspect of computation while leaving the *arithmetic* operations unchanged. An attention mechanism that uses FlashAttention to optimize memory access still performs floating-point multiplications for every query-key dot product. L-Mul targets the arithmetic itself — it reduces the energy cost of each multiply-add operation within the SRAM-resident tiles, regardless of how those tiles were loaded. The authors position L-Mul as "orthogonal yet complementary" to I/O optimization (Section 1).

**3. Quantization and reduced-precision arithmetic (fp16, bf16, fp8-e4m3, fp8-e5m2, int8, fp4, int4).** This is the most directly comparable category, and the paper dedicates substantial analysis to showing where it falls short. The core limitation is that quantization reduces precision *aggressively* to save energy, but (a) the energy savings come primarily from reduced memory bandwidth (smaller tensors to load/store) and reduced bit-level arithmetic cost, not from changing the operation type — it's still multiplication, just with fewer gates; and (b) precision reduction below 8 bits frequently degrades model accuracy, requiring techniques like outlier-aware quantization (Dettmers et al., 2022) that add overhead for handling values that don't fit in the reduced range.

The paper makes a specific, measurable claim about this limitation: fp8 multiplication, even at its most efficient (e5m2 with a 2-bit mantissa), still requires approximately **300 gate-level operations** (OR gates, AND gates, XOR gates, etc.) as estimated in Equation (6). L-Mul at comparable precision requires roughly **157 gate-level operations** (Equation 7) — roughly half the gate count. And critically, L-Mul uses *integer addition* rather than *floating-point multiplication* as its fundamental operation, which means the per-gate energy cost is also lower (integer adders are simpler circuits than FP multiplier components). The paper's claim is not just "fewer gates" but "simpler gates doing a simpler operation."

Additionally, the paper notes a specific failure mode of integer-based quantization: outlier weights. When quantizing to int8 or int4, values that exceed the representable range create substantial errors that require special handling. L-Mul operates in the floating-point domain — it preserves the exponent representation — so it naturally handles a wide dynamic range without outlier-specific logic.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning can be understood along four axes:

**Axis 1: Operation replacement, not precision reduction.** Unlike quantization, which asks "how few bits can we use to represent these numbers?", L-Mul asks "can we replace the multiplication *operation itself* with something cheaper while maintaining equivalent numerical behavior?" This is a categorically different approach — it changes the *algorithm* at the gate level rather than the *representation* at the bit level. The paper argues this is more fundamental because it attacks the $O(m^2)$ complexity term directly rather than shrinking $m$ and accepting the quadratic scaling at lower bit widths.

**Axis 2: Precision-equivalence claims with formal error analysis.** The paper does not simply claim L-Mul is "good enough" — it provides a theoretical error expectation analysis (Section 2.3.1 and Appendix A) that relates L-Mul's precision to the equivalent number of mantissa bits in a standard floating-point multiply. The key result (Table 7 in Appendix A) is:

- With **evenly distributed** operands, L-Mul with 3-bit mantissa ($k=3$) has expected error $|f_1 + f_2| = 0.30$, compared to fp8-e5m2 error of $|f_1| = 0.35$ at $k=2$ mantissa bits. This means L-Mul with 3-bit mantissa *outperforms* fp8-e5m2 precision.
- With **real model weight distributions** (estimated from five popular LLMs), L-Mul with 4-bit mantissa achieves error $|f_1 + f_2| = 0.12$, which is competitive with fp8-e4m3 error at $k=4$ mantissa bits ($|f_1| = 0.077$). With 5-bit mantissa, L-Mul error drops to 0.15, and with 6-bit mantissa, to 0.14 — both regime shifts that make L-Mul more accurate than fp8 multiplication while still using fewer gates.

The paper frames this as a *precision-efficiency Pareto improvement*: L-Mul is simultaneously more accurate than fp8 multiplication (for appropriately chosen mantissa size $k$) and more energy-efficient (fewer gates, simpler operation). This breaks the usual tradeoff where lower precision means lower energy but also lower accuracy.

**Axis 3: Training-free applicability to attention.** A significant portion of the paper's empirical contribution is demonstrating that L-Mul can be *dropped into* the attention mechanism of pretrained models with no fine-tuning and essentially no accuracy loss. This matters practically because it means existing model weights can be served with L-Mul-based hardware without retraining — a deployment path that quantization approaches also offer, but the paper claims L-Mul achieves higher accuracy at equivalent or lower energy cost. Specifically, Table 2 shows that across Mistral-7B and Llama-3.1-8B on 7 benchmarks (MMLU, BBH, ARC-C, CSQA, OBQA, PIQA, SIQA), the bf16 baseline achieves 69.83% and 74.24% average accuracy respectively, while L-Mul attention achieves 69.93% and 74.00% — a gap of +0.10 and −0.24 percentage points respectively. fp8-e4m3 attention achieves 69.55% and 73.40%, and fp8-e5m2 drops to 68.97% and 71.86%. L-Mul thus *outperforms* fp8-e4m3 attention in 12 out of 14 model-task combinations while using roughly half the gate operations.

**Axis 4: Full-model replacement through fine-tuning.** Beyond the training-free attention replacement, the paper demonstrates that fine-tuning with L-Mul replacing *all* multiplication operations in a transformer (attention, linear layers, element-wise products) recovers full accuracy. The Gemma2-2b-It experiment (Table 6) shows that fine-tuning with 3-bit-mantissa L-Mul under fp8-e4m3 accumulation precision achieves 37.91% on GSM8k, compared to 36.09% for standard fp8-e4m3 fine-tuning. This is a crucial result because it suggests that L-Mul is not just an inference-time approximation — it can be used throughout training, with the optimizer adapting the weights to the L-Mul operation's characteristics, effectively "compiling" the model for addition-based hardware.

The paper explicitly positions this as complementary to I/O optimization methods (FlashAttention, PagedAttention) and hardware architectures (TPUs, GPUs with tensor cores). The vision (Section 5) is a holistic integration: "truly energy- and compute-efficient AI computation will emerge from a holistic integration of optimizations across I/O, control, and arithmetic operations." L-Mul provides the arithmetic operation component of this stack.

### The Practical Bottleneck: No Native Hardware Implementation Exists

A subtle but important aspect of the paper's motivation that appears in the footnote of Section 1 and the future work section is the acknowledgment that current GPUs cannot fully exploit L-Mul's efficiency because "there is no native implementation." The inline PTX assembly code in Figure 2 is a *simulation* — it uses GPU instructions to emulate what dedicated hardware would do. The actual energy savings (95% for element-wise, 80% for dot products) are projections based on the gate complexity analysis and the energy figures from Horowitz (2014), not measured results on fabricated silicon.

This is not a weakness of the idea — it's a necessary stage in any hardware-algorithm codesign proposal — but it contextualizes the paper's contribution as establishing the *numerical validity* of the approach so that hardware implementation becomes worth pursuing. The paper is essentially making the case that (a) the algorithm works mathematically (error analysis), (b) it preserves model accuracy (benchmarking), and (c) the projected energy savings are large enough to justify the engineering effort of building L-Mul-native hardware. Without step (b), hardware designers would have no reason to invest in supporting a new arithmetic primitive; the paper's extensive benchmarking across models, tasks, and precision levels provides that motivation.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper proposes a **drop-in replacement algorithm** for floating-point multiplication in tensor computations — the workhorse arithmetic operation inside every neural network. Rather than reducing precision (quantization) or skipping operations (pruning), the method changes *how multiplication itself is computed*: instead of the standard $O(m^2)$ mantissa multiplication, it uses an $O(m)$ integer addition that approximates the multiplication result with analytically bounded error. The "system" being built is not a new model architecture but rather an **arithmetic primitive** that can be substituted into any existing transformer — in the attention mechanism alone for training-free deployment, or throughout all layers with fine-tuning — with the promise of reducing energy per multiply by roughly 80–95% while preserving model accuracy at or above fp8-equivalent precision levels.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The L-Mul system has three conceptual layers, stacked from lowest (hardware arithmetic) to highest (model integration):

1. **The L-Mul arithmetic unit** — a gate-level algorithm that takes two floating-point numbers (any IEEE 754 format, from fp32 down to fp8) and produces their approximate product using one integer addition, one XOR for sign handling, and a fixed offset subtraction. This is the fundamental replacement for a floating-point multiplier circuit.

2. **The L-Matmul tensor operation** — a matrix multiplication primitive (e.g., `L-matmul(Q, K^T)`) that executes all element-wise floating-point multiplications in the matrix product using L-Mul units rather than standard FP multipliers. This is the tensor-level building block that drops into PyTorch-like linear algebra.

3. **Model-level substitution points** — three specific places in a transformer where L-Matmul replaces standard matrix multiplication:
   - **Attention pre-softmax:** the query-key dot product `Q · K^T` that produces attention scores.
   - **Attention post-softmax:** the weighted value aggregation `A · V` where `A` are attention weights.
   - **(Optional) All linear layers and element-wise products:** full-model replacement during fine-tuning, where every `nn.Linear` forward pass and every Hadamard product uses L-Mul internally.

Information flows identically to a standard transformer: input tokens → embedding → sequence of transformer blocks (each with attention + MLP) → output head. The only change is that inside the tensor multiplication operations, the arithmetic circuits use L-Mul's integer addition pathway rather than the standard floating-point multiplier. The model weights, the tensor shapes, and the overall computation graph are completely unchanged — which is why pretrained weights can be used without modification.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the standard floating-point multiplication algorithm and its gate-complexity breakdown — so the reader understands exactly what $O(m^2)$ cost L-Mul is eliminating and why mantissa multiplication is the bottleneck.
- **Second**, the L-Mul formula itself (Equation 1) — how floating-point multiplication is approximated by integer addition, what the offset `$l(k)$` correction term does, and why this can be implemented with a single hardware adder.
- **Third**, the bit-level implementation (Equation 2 and Figure 2) — how IEEE 754 floating-point bit layouts enable L-Mul to be executed as unsigned integer addition with one offset subtraction, plus sign XOR.
- **Fourth**, the theoretical error analysis (Section 2.3.1) — the decomposition of L-Mul's approximation error into the rounding error of k-bit multiplication plus a correction mismatch term, and the expectation calculations that prove L-Mul with 3–4 mantissa bits matches or exceeds fp8 precision.
- **Fifth**, the gate-complexity estimation (Section 2.3.2) — the counting of AND, XOR, and NAND gates for standard fp8 multiplication versus L-Mul, showing the ~2× reduction in gate count and the larger energy reduction from switching to integer addition.
- **Sixth**, the model integration — how L-Mul is inserted into the attention mechanism (Equation 3), the three levels of substitution (attention-only training-free, attention-only with reduced mantissa bits, full-model with fine-tuning), and the specific precision configurations evaluated.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **hardware-algorithm codesign paper** whose core idea is that the $O(m^2)$ mantissa multiplication in floating-point products can be replaced by an $O(m)$ integer addition with a carefully tuned offset correction term, such that the resulting approximation error is *lower* than that of fp8 multiplication at equivalent bit width while using *fewer and simpler gates* — and that this substitution can be applied to pretrained transformer models with negligible accuracy loss.

#### Standard Floating-Point Multiplication and Its Complexity Bottleneck

To understand what L-Mul replaces, one must first understand what a standard floating-point multiplier does. Consider two IEEE 754 floating-point numbers `$x$` and `$y$`, each represented as:

$$x = (-1)^{x_s} \times (1 + x_m) \times 2^{x_e}$$

where `$x_s \in \{0,1\}$` is the sign bit, `$x_m$` is the mantissa (fraction) interpreted as a fixed-point number in `$[0, 1)$` — meaning `$(1 + x_m) \in [1, 2)$` — and `$x_e$` is the exponent, typically stored with a bias (e.g., bias=15 for fp16's 5-bit exponent). The mantissa `$x_m$` has `$m$` bits of precision; for fp32, `$m = 23$`; for fp16, `$m = 10$`; for bf16, `$m = 7$`; for fp8-e4m3, `$m = 3$`; for fp8-e5m2, `$m = 2$`.

The standard multiplication algorithm computes:

$$\text{Mul}(x, y) = \left((-1)^{x_s} \oplus (-1)^{y_s}\right) \times (1 + x_m)(1 + y_m) \times 2^{x_e + y_e}$$

The term `$(1 + x_m)(1 + y_m) = 1 + x_m + y_m + x_m \cdot y_m$` contains the product `$x_m \cdot y_m$`, which is the **mantissa multiplication** — an `$m$`-bit by `$m$`-bit fixed-point multiply. This operation requires:

- `$(m+1)^2$` **AND gates** to compute the partial product matrix (each bit of the first operand AND-ed with each bit of the second).
- Multiple levels of **half adders and full adders** to reduce the partial product matrix to a single sum — this is the carry-save adder tree, which has $O(m^2)$ gate complexity.
- **Rounding logic** to normalize the result back to `$[1, 2)$` format (since the product of two numbers in `$[1, 2)$` is in `$[1, 4)$`, requiring a potential 1-bit right shift and exponent increment), plus rounding to the nearest representable value in the target precision.

As the paper states in Section 2.1:

> "FP multiplication requires $O(e)$ exponent addition, $O(m^2)$ mantissa multiplication, and rounding"

The `$O(m^2)$` term is the computational bottleneck, growing quadratically with mantissa precision. For fp32 (`$m = 23$`), this is ~576 partial products. For fp16 (`$m = 10$`), it's ~121 partial products. Even for fp8-e4m3 (`$m = 3$`), it's ~16 partial products — smaller, but still quadratic and still requiring adder tree reduction.

The energy cost reflects this complexity: an fp32 multiply (Table 1) consumes 3.7 pJ, versus 0.9 pJ for fp32 addition and 0.1 pJ for int32 addition. The paper's fundamental move is to **eliminate `$x_m \cdot y_m$` entirely**, replacing it with an operation that requires only $O(m)$ linear work — specifically, a single integer addition.

#### The L-Mul Formula and the Correction Offset

The L-Mul algorithm is defined by Equation (1) in the paper. For two floating-point numbers `$x = (1 + x_m) \cdot 2^{x_e}$` and `$y = (1 + y_m) \cdot 2^{y_e}$`, where `$x_m$` and `$y_m$` are mantissas of `$k$` bits (the number of mantissa bits kept after truncation to a target precision), the L-Mul result is:

$$\text{L-Mul}(x, y) = (1 + x_m + y_m + 2^{-l(k)}) \cdot 2^{x_e + y_e}$$

with the offset function:

$$l(k) = \begin{cases} m & \text{if } k \leq 3, \\ 3 & \text{if } k = 4, \\ 4 & \text{if } k > 4 \end{cases}$$

where `$k$` is the number of mantissa bits retained from the operands and `$m$` is the full mantissa bit width of the original floating-point format (e.g., `$m = 10$` for fp16, `$m = 7$` for bf16).

**What this computes:** Given two positive floating-point numbers, L-Mul produces an approximation of their true product. The exponent handling is identical to standard multiplication: `$x_e + y_e$`. The mantissa handling is radically different: instead of computing `$(1 + x_m)(1 + y_m) = 1 + x_m + y_m + x_m \cdot y_m$`, L-Mul computes `$1 + x_m + y_m + 2^{-l(k)}$`. That is, it **drops the exact mantissa product** `$x_m \cdot y_m$` and **replaces it with a constant correction term** `$2^{-l(k)}$` that depends only on the number of mantissa bits `$k$`, not on the specific values of `$x_m$` and `$y_m$`.

The sign is handled separately via XOR, exactly as in standard multiplication: `$\text{sign}(x) \oplus \text{sign}(y)$`.

**Why this form:** The standard product expands to `$1 + x_m + y_m + x_m \cdot y_m$`. The terms `$1$`, `$x_m$`, and `$y_m$` are all computable with addition alone (and the `$1$` is implicit in the IEEE 754 encoding — it is the hidden leading bit that is not stored). The eliminated term `$x_m \cdot y_m$` is the only part requiring multiplication. L-Mul replaces it with a constant `$2^{-l(k)}$`.

The constant `$2^{-l(k)}$` serves as a **statistical correction** for the missing `$x_m \cdot y_m$` term. Since `$x_m$` and `$y_m$` are mantissa fractions in `$[0, 1)$`, their product is also in `$[0, 1)$`, with expected value depending on the distribution. The paper's offset selection strategy, visualized in Figure 3, finds the `$l(k)$` value that minimizes the mean squared error between L-Mul and the true product across the operand distribution encountered in actual model inference. The piecewise definition — `$l(k) = m$` for `$k \leq 3$`, then `$l(k) = 3$` for `$k = 4$`, then `$l(k) = 4$` for larger `$k$` — reflects empirical optimization on the GSM8k waveform: for small `$k$` (aggressive truncation), the correction should depend on the original full mantissa width `$m$` (because more bits were discarded); for larger `$k$`, the correction stabilizes at a small constant.

The critical practical consequence is that **computing `$1 + x_m + y_m$` with an offset requires no multiplication hardware**. It requires adding two `$k$`-bit mantissa values and then adding a constant — operations that can be performed with a single integer adder, as the next section explains.

#### Bit-Level Implementation Via IEEE 754 Layout Exploitation

The L-Mul algorithm's most elegant aspect is that the IEEE 754 floating-point bit layout allows the entire computation — mantissa addition, carry propagation to exponent, and offset subtraction — to be executed as **one unsigned integer addition and one subtraction**, plus a sign XOR. Equation (2) formalizes this:

$$\text{L-mul}(x, y)[0] = x[0] \oplus y[0]$$

$$\text{L-mul}(x, y)[1:] = x[1:] + y[1:] - \text{offset}$$

where `$x[0]$` denotes the sign bit (bit 0), `$x[1:]$` denotes all remaining bits (exponent + mantissa concatenated), and these are interpreted as **unsigned integers** for the addition and subtraction operations.

**What this computes:** Bit-slice the two floating-point numbers. The sign result is XOR of the two sign bits. The remaining 31 bits (for fp32) or 15 bits (for fp16) are treated as a single unsigned integer and added together, then an offset constant is subtracted. The resulting bit pattern, when reinterpreted as a floating-point number, is the L-Mul approximation.

**Why this works — the IEEE 754 layout trick:** The IEEE 754 floating-point format places the exponent in the more-significant bits and the mantissa in the less-significant bits, with the exponent stored in biased form. For two positive normalized numbers, the bit pattern (excluding sign) is:

$$\text{bits} = (\text{exponent} + \text{bias}) \times 2^{\text{mantissa\_bits}} + \text{mantissa}$$

When these bit patterns are added as unsigned integers, the mantissa fields add normally, and **if the mantissa sum exceeds `$2^{\text{mantissa\_bits}}$` (i.e., if `$x_m + y_m \geq 1$`), the carry automatically propagates into the exponent field**, incrementing it by 1. This is exactly the behavior needed when `$(1 + x_m) + (1 + y_m) \geq 4$` — the result needs to be renormalized by shifting the mantissa right and incrementing the exponent. The integer addition handles this carry automatically, which is why the paper notes (Figure 2 caption):

> "the integer addition operation also automatically send the mantissa carry to the exponent. This is different from the rounding process in traditional FP multiplier, where the fraction is manually rounded to 1.x and the carry is added to the exponent as an independent step."

The offset subtraction `$\text{offset} = \text{0x3F780000}$` for fp32 (shown in the PTX assembly in Figure 2) accounts for two corrections simultaneously: (a) removing the exponent bias that gets doubled when two biased exponents are added, and (b) applying the `$2^{-l(k)}$` correction term. For fp32 with 8-bit exponent (bias=127) and 23-bit mantissa, the offset `$\text{0x3F780000}$` corresponds to exponent bits `$\text{01111110}$` (bias 126, which when subtracted from `$2 \times 127 = 254$` gives a net exponent of `$254 - 126 = 128$`, then adjusted by the implicit carry behavior) and mantissa bits encoding the `$2^{-l(k)}$` correction.

**The gate-level consequence:** The entire L-Mul operation, for fp32 operands, requires:
- 1 XOR gate for sign handling.
- One 31-bit unsigned integer addition (a chain of full adders with carry propagation — `$O(n)$` where `$n = 31$`).
- One 32-bit subtraction to apply the offset.

Compare this to standard fp32 multiplication: `$(24)^2 = 576$` AND gates for the partial product matrix, a complete carry-save adder tree with ~23 levels of full adders, and explicit rounding logic. The complexity reduction is qualitative — from a multiplier circuit to an adder circuit — not just a quantitative reduction in bit width.

The PTX assembly in Figure 2 provides a concrete GPU simulation of this logic (though as the paper notes, this is a simulation, not hardware-optimized — it uses general-purpose integer registers and instructions to emulate what dedicated hardware would do):

```ptx
mov.b32 r1, $1;           // Load first fp32 operand into r1
mov.b32 r2, $2;           // Load second fp32 operand into r2
and.b32 r1, r1, 0x7FFFFFFF;  // Clear sign bit of r1 (keep exponent + mantissa)
and.b32 r2, r2, 0x7FFFFFFF;  // Clear sign bit of r2
xor.b32 s1, s1, s2;       // XOR the two sign bits (not shown: prior extraction)
add.u32 r0, r1, r2;       // UNSIGNED integer addition of the exponent+mantissa fields
sub.u32 r0, r0, 0x3F780000;  // Subtract the offset constant
and.b32 r0, r0, 0x7FFFFFFF;  // Ensure sign bit is 0 in the intermediate
and.b32 s1, r1, 0x80000000;  // Extract sign bit from original r1
and.b32 s2, r2, 0x80000000;  // Extract sign bit from original r2
add.u32 r0, r0, s1;       // OR the sign back into the result
mov.b32 $0, r0;           // Store result to output fp32 register
```

The key instruction is `add.u32 r0, r1, r2` — a single unsigned integer addition that replaces the entire multiplier datapath.

#### Error Analysis: Why L-Mul Matches or Exceeds fp8 Precision

The paper provides a theoretical error analysis (Section 2.3.1, expanded in Appendix A) that quantifies precisely how L-Mul's approximation compares to standard floating-point multiplication at reduced precision. This analysis is crucial because it establishes that L-Mul is not merely "surprisingly good" but predictably superior to fp8 multiplication under analytically characterized conditions.

**The decomposition framework.** Consider two positive floating-point numbers `$x$` and `$y$` with `$m$`-bit mantissas in their full (original) precision. Let `$x_k$` and `$y_k$` be the first `$k$` bits of their mantissas, and `$x_r$` and `$y_r$` be the remaining `$m - k$` bits that would be discarded by `$k$`-bit rounding. Formally:

$$x = (1 + x_k + x_r) \cdot 2^{x_e}, \quad x' = (1 + x_k) \cdot 2^{x_e}$$

$$y = (1 + y_k + y_r) \cdot 2^{y_e}, \quad y' = (1 + y_k) \cdot 2^{y_e}$$

where `$x'$` and `$y'$` are the `$k$`-bit rounded versions.

**Standard k-bit multiplication error.** The error of a `$k$`-bit floating-point multiplication (compared to full-precision multiplication) is:

$$e^k_{\text{mul}} = \text{Mul}(x, y) - \text{Mul}(x', y') = (x_k y_r + y_k x_r + x_r + y_r + x_r y_r) \cdot 2^{x_e + y_e}$$

where `$\text{Mul}(x, y)$` is the exact product and `$\text{Mul}(x', y')$` is the product computed with `$k$`-bit truncated mantissas.

**What this computes:** The error incurred by discarding the lower `$m - k$` bits of each mantissa before multiplication. The terms are:
- `$x_k y_r$`: the product of the kept bits of `$x$` with the discarded bits of `$y$`.
- `$y_k x_r$`: the symmetric counterpart.
- `$x_r + y_r$`: the discarded portion of each mantissa directly.
- `$x_r y_r$`: the product of the two discarded portions (typically negligible).

All terms are scaled by `$2^{x_e + y_e}$` (the exponent product factor).

**Expected value.** Under the assumption that mantissa bits are uniformly distributed (each bit independently 0 or 1 with probability 1/2), the expectations are:

$$\mathbb{E}[x_k] = \frac{1}{2}(1 - 2^{-k}), \quad \mathbb{E}[x_r] = \frac{1}{2}(2^{-k} - 2^{-m})$$

Plugging these into the error expression yields `$\mathbb{E}[e^k_{\text{mul}}] = f_1(m, k) \cdot \mathbb{E}[2^{x_e + y_e}]$`, where `$f_1(m, k)$` is a deterministic function of the two bit widths. The paper computes `$f_1(m=7, k)$` for bf16 operands in Table 7 (Appendix A): for `$k = 1$`, `$f_1 = 0.68$`; for `$k = 2$`, `$f_1 = 0.35$`; for `$k = 3$`, `$f_1 = 0.17$`; for `$k = 4$` (matching fp8-e4m3), `$f_1 = 0.081$`; for `$k = 5$`, `$f_1 = 0.035$`; for `$k = 6$`, `$f_1 = 0.012$`.

**L-Mul error relative to k-bit multiplication.** The L-Mul approximation, when compared to the full-precision product, incurs the same `$k$`-bit truncation error `$e^k_{\text{mul}}$` *plus* an additional term from replacing `$x_k \cdot y_k$` with the constant `$2^{-l(k)}$`:

$$e^k_{\text{lmul}} = e^k_{\text{mul}} + (x_k y_k - 2^{-l(k)}) \cdot 2^{x_e + y_e}$$

$$\mathbb{E}[e^k_{\text{lmul}}] = \mathbb{E}[e^k_{\text{mul}}] + \mathbb{E}[x_k y_k - 2^{-l(k)}] \cdot \mathbb{E}[2^{x_e + y_e}]$$

so that the total L-Mul error expectation is:

$$\mathbb{E}[e^k_{\text{lmul}}] = \left[f_1(m, k) + f_2(k)\right] \cdot \mathbb{E}[2^{x_e + y_e}]$$

where `$f_2(k) = \mathbb{E}[x_k y_k - 2^{-l(k)}]$` is the correction mismatch — how much the constant offset `$2^{-l(k)}$` differs from the expected value of the mantissa product it replaces.

**What this computes:** The total expected error of L-Mul with `$k$`-bit mantissa operands, decomposed into (a) the error from truncating to `$k$` bits (which a `$k$`-bit standard multiplier would also incur), plus (b) the error from using a constant rather than the actual product `$x_k y_k$`. The second term `$f_2(k)$` can be positive or negative — L-Mul can either overestimate or underestimate the product depending on the offset choice, but the absolute value matters for comparing precision.

**Why this decomposition matters:** It allows a direct comparison: if `$|f_1(m, k) + f_2(k)|$` (the L-Mul total error coefficient) is less than or equal to `$|f_1(m, k')|$` (the standard multiplication error at some reference bit width `$k'$`), then L-Mul at `$k$` mantissa bits is **at least as accurate** as standard `$k'$`-bit multiplication.

**Numerical evaluation of the error coefficients.** The paper evaluates the error coefficients in two regimes (Table 7, Appendix A):

*Even distribution (uniform mantissa bits):*
- Standard fp8-e5m2 equivalent (`$k = 2$`): `$|f_1(7, 2)| = 0.35$`
- L-Mul with `$k = 3$`: `$|f_1(7, 3) + f_2(3)| = 0.30$` → **L-Mul is MORE accurate** than fp8-e5m2
- Standard fp8-e4m3 equivalent (`$k = 4$`): `$|f_1(7, 4)| = 0.081$`
- L-Mul with `$k = 4$`: `$|f_1(7, k) + f_2(k)| = 0.24$` → less accurate than e4m3, more accurate than e5m2
- L-Mul with `$k = 6$`: `$|f_1(7, k) + f_2(k)| = 0.19$` → approaching e4m3 accuracy

*Real distribution (estimated from five pretrained LLM parameter distributions):*
- Standard fp8-e4m3 equivalent (`$k = 4$`): `$|f_1(7, 4)| = 0.077$`
- L-Mul with `$k = 4$`: `$|f_1(7, 4) + f_2(4)| = 0.12$` → quite close to e4m3
- L-Mul with `$k = 3$`: `$|f_1(7, 3) + f_2(3)| = 0.18$` → between e4m3 and e5m2 accuracy

The real-distribution result is particularly important because actual model weights are not uniform — they follow bell-shaped or power-law distributions around zero, which affects the expected value of `$x_k y_k$`. The paper finds that with real weight distributions, the `$f_2(k)$` correction mismatch is *smaller* (the offset `$2^{-l(k)}$` is a better match to the expected product), making L-Mul more competitive with standard multiplication than under the uniform assumption.

**Experimental validation (Figures 3 and 4).** The paper validates these theoretical estimates empirically by measuring the mean squared error (MSE) between L-Mul attention and bf16 attention on the GSM8k benchmark across different `$k$` and `$l(k)$` settings. Figure 3 shows a heatmap grid: for Llama and Gemma models, each `$(k, l(k))$` combination is colored according to its MSE relative to fp8-e4m3 and fp8-e5m2 baselines. The results confirm:
- L-Mul with `$k = 3$` mantissa bits achieves **lower error than fp8-e5m2** across all configurations.
- L-Mul with `$k = 4$` mantissa bits achieves **comparable or lower error than fp8-e4m3** — the Llama model with `$k=4, l(k)=3$` is slightly better than e4m3, and the Gemma model shows the same pattern.
- The optimal `$l(k)$` follows the piecewise rule in Equation (1): `$l(k) = m$` for small `$k$`, transitioning to fixed small values (3 or 4) for larger `$k$`.

Figure 4 visualizes this more directly: it plots error as a function of mantissa bits `$k$`, with lines for L-Mul and horizontal bands for fp8-e5m2 and fp8-e4m3 error levels. The key visual takeaway is that the L-Mul curve crosses *below* the fp8-e5m2 band at `$k=3$` and touches the fp8-e4m3 band at `$k=4$`, confirming the theoretical predictions.

**The implication for deployment:** L-Mul with 3-bit mantissa operands — using only integer addition with total gate count ~157 (Equation 7) — delivers numerical accuracy exceeding fp8-e5m2 multiplication (gate count ~296). L-Mul with 4-bit mantissa operands achieves approximate fp8-e4m3 accuracy. This means L-Mul provides a genuine Pareto improvement: simultaneously more accurate AND more energy-efficient than the fp8 formats that represent the current state of the art in low-precision inference.

#### Gate Complexity Estimation: Quantifying the Hardware Savings

Section 2.3.2 provides a detailed gate-level accounting that translates the algorithmic simplification into concrete hardware resource estimates. This analysis is approximate (the paper explicitly says "we make a rough estimation") but establishes the order of magnitude of the savings.

**Standard fp8 multiplication gate count.** For an fp8 multiplier with `$i$` exponent bits and `$j$` mantissa bits (e.g., e4m3: `$i=4, j=3$`; e5m2: `$i=5, j=2$`):
- **Sign prediction:** 1 XOR gate.
- **Exponent addition with offset:** `$i$` full adders (one per exponent bit), each full adder using 2 AND, 2 XOR, 1 OR = 11 gate-level operations (since each XOR requires 4 NAND gates). So: `$11i$` gates.
- **Mantissa multiplication:** `$(j+1)^2$` AND gates for the partial product bit matrix. Then 3 half adders and `$2j-2$` full adders for the carry-save adder tree reduction. Each half adder: 1 AND + 1 XOR = 5 gate-level ops. Each full adder: 11 gate-level ops. Total mantissa multiply gates: `$(j+1)^2 + 3 \times 5 + (2j-2) \times 11$`.
- **Exponent rounding:** `$i$` half adders = `$5i$` gates.

The paper evaluates this for the two fp8 formats:
- **fp8-e4m3:** `$N^{\times}_{\text{fp8-e4m3}} \approx 325$` gate-level operations.
- **fp8-e5m2:** `$N^{\times}_{\text{fp8-e5m2}} \approx 296$` gate-level operations.
- For reference, **fp16:** `$N^{\times}_{\text{fp16}} \approx 584$` gates.

These numbers represent the total count of elementary logic gates (NAND, AND, XOR, OR) that would be instantiated in a hardware multiplier circuit for each format.

**L-Mul gate count.** For L-Mul with the same operand bit widths (where `$n = 1 + i + j$` total bits including sign):
- **Sign prediction:** 1 XOR gate (`$N^{\oplus}_1$`).
- **Integer addition:** one `$(i+j)$`-bit unsigned integer addition. This is `$i+j$` full adders, each 11 gates. So: `$11(i+j)$` gates. For the 8-bit case (1 sign + `$i$` exponent + `$j$` mantissa = 8 bits total after stripping sign), `$i+j = 7$`, giving `$77$` gates for the adder.
- **Offset subtraction:** one `$(i+j)$`-bit subtraction, which in practice is implemented as another adder with the two's complement of the offset. So another `$11(i+j)$` = `$77$` gates for the 8-bit case.
- Plus the implicit handling of the offset constant (a small amount of additional logic).

The paper's estimates (Equation 7):
- **fp16 L-Mul:** `$N^{\text{L-mul}}_{\text{fp16}} \approx 256$` gates.
- **fp8 L-Mul:** `$N^{\text{L-mul}}_{\text{fp8}} \approx 157$` gates.

**What this computes in practical terms:** For fp8 operands, L-Mul uses approximately **157 gates** while fp8-e5m2 multiplication uses **296 gates** — a **~47% reduction** in gate count. For fp16 operands, L-Mul's ~256 gates are **less than half** the ~584 gates of fp16 multiplication, and notably **less than fp8 multiplication's ~300 gates overall**. This means computing L-Mul at fp16 precision is actually *cheaper in gate count* than computing standard multiplication at fp8 precision — yet L-Mul at fp16 preserves far more numerical accuracy.

**Why gate count understates the energy savings.** The paper notes (Section 2.3.2) that "the actual difference in energy cost is greater than the complexity gap suggests" because:

1. **Gate type matters:** Integer adders use simpler, lower-capacitance transistors than floating-point multiplier components. A full adder's critical path is a short carry chain; a multiplier's partial product reduction tree has longer wires, more fan-out, and higher switching activity.
2. **Switching activity:** Multipliers toggle more internal nodes per operation than adders because of the large number of partial products being generated and summed simultaneously. More toggles = more dynamic energy.
3. **The Horowitz (2014) energy figures reflect this:** A 32-bit integer add costs 0.1 pJ while an fp32 multiply costs 3.7 pJ — a 37× factor, far larger than the ~2× gate count ratio would suggest. The paper cites that "an fp8 multiplication consumes approximately 0.25 pJ to 0.4 pJ, while a 16-bit L-Mul uses around 0.06 pJ" — a 4–7× energy reduction.

**The overall energy projections.** Combining the per-operation energy estimates with the operation mix in transformer models, the paper projects:
- **95% energy reduction for element-wise tensor multiplications** (where all operations are multiplies with no additions interleaved — replacing each multiply with an integer add).
- **80% energy reduction for dot products** (matrix multiplications, where the multiply and accumulate pattern means the multiply energy is partially amortized by the addition energy, but the multiply still dominates).

These percentages are derived from the ratio of integer addition energy to floating-point multiplication energy from the Horowitz (2014) figures, applied to the per-operation mix in attention and linear layer computations. They are **projections**, not measured results, because no L-Mul-native hardware exists yet.

#### Integration Into Transformer Models

The paper integrates L-Mul into transformer models at three levels of increasing scope, corresponding to different deployment scenarios.

**Level 1: Training-free attention mechanism replacement.** This is the primary evaluation setting (Tables 2, 3, 4). The attention computation in a transformer is:

$$K = H \cdot W_k, \quad Q = H \cdot W_q, \quad V = H \cdot W_v$$

$$A = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{Q \cdot K^T}{\sqrt{d}}\right), \quad H' = \text{L-matmul}(A, V)$$

where `L-matmul(Q, K^T)` denotes a matrix multiplication where **all individual floating-point multiplications** in the matrix product are replaced by L-Mul operations. The key projections (`$H \cdot W_k$`, `$H \cdot W_q$`, `$H \cdot W_v$`) are left unchanged — they still use standard multiplication — but the two attention-internal matrix multiplies (`$Q \cdot K^T$` and `$A \cdot V$`) use L-Mul.

**What this computes:** Everything about the attention mechanism is identical — the same weight matrices, the same input hidden states, the same softmax normalization — except that the arithmetic inside the two large matrix multiplications uses integer addition rather than floating-point multiplication. The model weights are frozen (no fine-tuning), and the L-Mul attention is a drop-in replacement for standard attention.

**Why attention first:** The attention mechanism is the `$O(N^2)$` computational bottleneck in transformers (quadratic in sequence length), and the query-key dot product `$Q \cdot K^T$` involves a matrix multiplication where every element is a floating-point multiply-accumulate. Replacing these multiplications with L-Mul yields the largest per-token energy savings in the model. Additionally, attention is known to be relatively robust to numerical perturbation — small errors in attention scores get smoothed by the softmax — making it the safest place to start for a training-free substitution.

**Precision configurations tested (Table 5):** The paper evaluates L-Mul attention with varying numbers of mantissa bits `$k$` (the number of mantissa bits retained from the original fp16/bf16 operands before applying L-Mul):
- `$k = 4$` (4-bit mantissa): achieves comparable or slightly better performance than standard bf16 and fp8-e4m3 attention.
- `$k = 3$` (3-bit mantissa): outperforms fp8-e5m2 attention, with Mistral dropping from 62.18 (e4m3) to 62.06 (L-Mul k=3) vs. 61.75 (e5m2), and Llama dropping from 68.16 to 68.12 vs. 66.61.
- `$k = 2$` (2-bit mantissa): Mistral drops to 61.08; Llama drops to 66.67, comparable to e5m2.

This demonstrates that even aggressive mantissa reduction (3 bits) with L-Mul maintains accuracy above fp8-e5m2 levels, consistent with the theoretical error analysis.

**Level 2: Full-model L-Mul with fine-tuning.** For the Gemma2-2b-It model on GSM8k (Table 6), the paper replaces **all multiplication operations** in the transformer — including linear layer matrix multiplications (`$W \cdot x$` in MLP blocks and projection layers), element-wise products (Hadamard products in gating mechanisms), and attention-internal multiplies — with `$k = 3$` bit mantissa L-Mul under fp8-e4m3 accumulation precision. The model is then fine-tuned on the GSM8k training set.

**What this computes:** During both forward and backward passes, every operation that would normally execute `$a \times b$` for floating-point operands instead executes `$\text{L-Mul}(a, b)$` with 3-bit mantissas. The accumulation (the summation of these products in a dot product) is kept at fp8-e4m3 precision to prevent compounding of L-Mul errors across many terms. The gradient computations also use L-Mul, meaning the optimizer (AdamW by default) is updating weights using L-Mul-approximated gradients.

**Why fine-tuning is necessary for full replacement:** Unlike attention, where errors are smoothed by softmax and the pretrained weights are already well-adapted, linear layers and element-wise products have no such error-dampening mechanism. Directly replacing all multiplications in a pretrained model with L-Mul would cause substantial accuracy degradation because the weights were optimized assuming exact multiplication. Fine-tuning allows the weights to adapt to the L-Mul operation's specific error characteristics — the optimizer finds weights that produce correct layer outputs *given* the approximate multiplication, effectively "compiling" the model for addition-based hardware.

**Result:** The fine-tuned L-Mul model achieves 37.91% on GSM8k, compared to 36.09% for standard fp8-e4m3 fine-tuning and 7.96% for fp8-e5m2 fine-tuning. This demonstrates that L-Mul with `$k=3$` mantissa bits — which is more energy-efficient than any fp8 multiplication — can serve as the arithmetic foundation for both training and inference with no accuracy penalty relative to fp8-e4m3.

**Level 3: Native L-Mul model training (future work).** The paper gestures toward (Section 5) training models "optimized for deployment on L-Mul native hardware" from scratch, where L-Mul is used throughout pretraining rather than fine-tuning. This would allow the model's learned representations to be natively adapted to the L-Mul error profile from initialization, potentially eliminating any accuracy gap relative to standard multiplication. This is not evaluated in the current paper.

#### The `$l(k)$` Offset Selection: Empirical Optimization

The offset function `$l(k)$` in Equation (1) is not derived analytically — it is empirically optimized. The paper states:

> "the offset exponent `$l(k)$` is defined according to the observation shown in Figure 3"

Figure 3 is a heatmap of mean squared error between L-Mul attention and bf16 attention on the GSM8k dataset, parameterized by mantissa bits `$k$` (x-axis, from 1 to 6) and offset exponent `$l$` (y-axis). The color intensity represents MSE; configurations with lower MSE than fp8-e4m3 are highlighted in red, and those with MSE between e4m3 and e5m2 are underlined.

**What Figure 3 reveals:** For each `$k$`, there is an optimal `$l$` that minimizes MSE. The optimal `$l$` follows a clear pattern:
- For `$k = 1, 2, 3$`: the optimal `$l$` equals `$m$` (the full mantissa size of the original format — 10 for fp16, 7 for bf16). This makes intuitive sense: when very few mantissa bits are kept, the correction term should be small (`$2^{-m}$` is a tiny constant) because the truncation error dominates and adding a large constant would overshoot.
- For `$k = 4$`: the optimal `$l$` is 3. The mantissa product `$x_k y_k$` with 4-bit operands has expected value around `$(0.5)^2 = 0.25$` (plus interaction terms), so `$2^{-3} = 0.125$` provides a reasonable constant approximation.
- For `$k > 4$`: the optimal `$l$` stabilizes at 4, corresponding to `$2^{-4} = 0.0625$`. With more mantissa bits, the actual `$x_k y_k$` product becomes a smaller contribution relative to `$1 + x_k + y_k$`, so a small constant correction suffices.

**Why piecewise:** The transition at `$k = 3 \rightarrow 4$` reflects the point where the mantissa product `$x_k \cdot y_k$` becomes large enough (relative to the truncation error) that it needs a meaningful constant correction, but not so large that the correction should depend on the original full mantissa width `$m$`. The paper's specific values (3 for `$k=4$`, 4 for `$k>4$`) are the result of grid search over the GSM8k waveform, not derived from first principles — which is appropriate for an empirical optimization of a hardware-amenable constant.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Replace `$x_m \cdot y_m$` with constant `$2^{-l(k)}$` rather than approximating it:** Eliminates the `$O(m^2)$` multiplier entirely. Alternative approximations (e.g., using only the most significant bits of the product, or a lookup table) would still require some multiplication hardware. A constant requires no multiplication logic whatsoever — just an offset subtraction merged with exponent bias correction.
- **Use IEEE 754 bit layout to implement addition as unsigned integer add:** Exploits the fact that exponent + mantissa concatenation means a single integer addition handles mantissa addition, carry to exponent, and implicit leading-bit restoration simultaneously — no separate normalization stage needed. This is the key insight that makes L-Mul implementable with one adder rather than multiple arithmetic units.
- **Optimize `$l(k)$` empirically rather than analytically:** The expected value of `$x_k \cdot y_k$` depends on the operand distribution, which in real models is non-uniform. Empirical optimization on actual model waveforms produces a better correction than any analytic formula assuming uniformity. The piecewise structure (three regimes) is simple enough to hardcode in hardware.
- **Apply training-free only to attention, not all layers:** Attention's softmax provides error dampening (small perturbations in attention scores map to small perturbations in softmax outputs, especially in the tail). Linear layers and element-wise products have no such dampening, requiring fine-tuning for full-model replacement. This staged approach maximizes the practical benefit (attention is the `$O(N^2)$` bottleneck) with minimal deployment friction (no retraining needed).
- **Use 3-bit mantissa L-Mul for full-model fine-tuning rather than 4-bit:** Pushes the energy efficiency boundary while maintaining accuracy. The theoretical analysis (Table 7) shows `$k=3$` L-Mul outperforms fp8-e5m2, and the fine-tuning experiment (Table 6) confirms this holds in practice — the model adapts to the approximation during training.
- **Keep accumulation at fp8-e4m3:** The L-Mul operation addresses the multiplication energy; the summation (accumulation) in dot products uses standard fp8-e4m3 addition. This prevents error accumulation across many L-Mul terms in a long dot product — the individual L-Mul errors are small, but summing thousands of them without sufficient accumulation precision could compound. fp8-e4m3 addition is already energy-efficient, so the bottleneck being eliminated is the multiplication component of the multiply-accumulate pair.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Numerical Efficiency as an Operation-Change Problem Rather Than a Precision-Reduction Problem

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is to reject the premise that has dominated the neural network efficiency literature: that reducing energy consumption means reducing the number of bits used to represent numbers. Instead, L-Mul asks a categorically different question: **can we change what operation we perform, rather than how precisely we perform the standard operation?**

To understand why this is distinctive, consider the default approach in the field. Quantization methods—from fp16/bf16 (Kalamkar et al., 2019), to fp8-e4m3 and fp8-e5m2 (Micikevicius et al., 2023), to int8 (Dettmers et al., 2022), to fp4/int4 (Dettmers et al., 2024)—all accept the fundamental premise that neural network computation means *floating-point multiplication*. Their optimization strategy is to shrink the bit width of that multiplication, reducing the `$m$` in the `$O(m^2)$` mantissa multiplication term but never eliminating the quadratic scaling or the multiplier circuit itself. The implicit assumption is that you *need* multiplication—the question is only how accurate it must be.

L-Mul breaks this assumption entirely. It observes that the mathematical expression `$(1 + x_m)(1 + y_m) = 1 + x_m + y_m + x_m \cdot y_m$` contains three additive terms and one product term, and that the additive terms `$1 + x_m + y_m$` can be computed with a single integer addition (exploiting the IEEE 754 bit layout) while only the `$x_m \cdot y_m$` term requires a multiplier. The innovation is recognizing that this product term can be *replaced by a constant* `$2^{-l(k)}$`—not approximated with fewer bits, not computed with a smaller multiplier, but eliminated entirely in favor of an empirically-tuned offset that depends only on the mantissa bit width `$k$`, not on the specific operand values.

This is a hardware-algorithm codesign insight rather than a precision-reduction insight. The field has spent years asking "how few bits can we use?" and hitting accuracy walls at 4-bit quantization. L-Mul asks "can we use a different circuit element?"—and the answer, demonstrated by the error analysis in Section 2.3.1 and validated in Figures 3–4, is that an integer adder with a constant offset can numerically match or exceed fp8 multiplication while consuming roughly half the gate count (157 vs. ~300 gates per Equation 7 vs. Equation 6) and drawing on the order of 4–7× less energy per operation (0.06 pJ vs. 0.25–0.4 pJ, from Horowitz, 2014 figures cited in Section 2.3.2).

The significance of this reframing extends beyond the specific L-Mul algorithm. It opens a new design space: if addition can substitute for multiplication in one context (the mantissa product), what other neural network operations might admit operation-level replacement rather than precision-level reduction? The paper's contribution is not merely the algorithm but the demonstration that **operation-type substitution is a viable axis of efficiency optimization**, complementing the established axes of quantization, pruning, and I/O optimization.

Evidence anchor: Table 7 (Appendix A) shows that L-Mul with `$k=4$` mantissa bits achieves error coefficient `$|f_1 + f_2| = 0.12$` under real weight distributions, compared to fp8-e4m3's `$|f_1| = 0.077$`—numerically close while using a fundamentally different and cheaper operation. Table 2 demonstrates that this numerical equivalence translates to practical model accuracy, with L-Mul attention achieving 69.93% average across 7 benchmarks on Mistral-7B versus 69.83% for bf16.

---

### Innovation 2: Exploiting the IEEE 754 Bit Layout to Collapse Multi-Component Floating-Point Arithmetic into a Single Integer Addition

A second conceptual innovation—distinct from the idea of operation replacement—is the specific mechanism by which L-Mul achieves its efficiency: the exploitation of the IEEE 754 floating-point bit layout to collapse what would normally be three separate arithmetic operations (mantissa addition, carry propagation to exponent, and exponent bias correction) into **one unsigned integer addition followed by one subtraction**.

This is subtle and easy to miss after the detailed technical breakdown in Section 3, but it represents a genuine insight at the intersection of numerical representation and circuit design. The standard approach to computing `$1 + x_m + y_m + 2^{-l(k)}$`—even after eliminating the multiplication—would be to extract the mantissa fields, perform fixed-point addition with explicit carry handling, detect whether the result exceeds `$2.0$` and needs renormalization, increment the exponent accordingly, and recompose the result. This would require a mantissa adder, a separate exponent incrementer, comparison logic, and a multiplexer to select between normalized and unnormalized results—still simpler than a multiplier, but nowhere near as simple as one integer addition.

The paper's insight is that the IEEE 754 format places the exponent in the more-significant bits and the mantissa in the less-significant bits, with the exponent stored in biased form. When you interpret the entire (sign-stripped) bit pattern as an unsigned integer and add two such integers, three things happen simultaneously:

1. **The mantissa fields add as fixed-point numbers**, exactly as needed.
2. **If the mantissa sum overflows past the mantissa field boundary**, the carry **automatically propagates into the exponent field**, incrementing it by 1—which is precisely the renormalization behavior required when `$(1 + x_m) + (1 + y_m) \geq 4$`.
3. **The exponent fields add**, producing `$2 \times \text{bias} + x_e + y_e$`, and the subsequent offset subtraction (0x3F780000 for fp32) removes the doubled bias and applies the `$2^{-l(k)}$` correction in one operation.

This is fundamentally different from how a standard floating-point multiplier works, where mantissa multiplication, normalization, and rounding are separate pipeline stages with explicit control logic. L-Mul piggybacks on the *integer addition carry chain*—a circuit that already exists in every ALU—to handle what would otherwise be multi-step floating-point normalization, reducing the entire operation to the complexity of an adder plus an XOR for sign handling.

The innovation here is not the IEEE 754 format itself (standard since 1985) but the recognition that **for this specific approximate multiplication formula, the bit layout enables a hardware implementation far simpler than what a naive reading of the formula would suggest**. The paper's PTX assembly code in Figure 2 makes this concrete: the core of L-Mul is literally `add.u32 r0, r1, r2` followed by `sub.u32 r0, r0, 0x3F780000`—two integer instructions replacing an entire floating-point multiplier datapath. The carry behavior that the paper describes as "automatic" is not a software trick; it is a property of how unsigned integer addition works on the concatenated exponent-mantissa bit pattern, and it eliminates what would otherwise be a separate rounding and normalization stage.

This insight has implications beyond L-Mul: it suggests that the IEEE 754 layout—designed decades ago for very different computational tradeoffs—contains latent properties that can be exploited for approximate computing in ways the original designers likely didn't anticipate. Specifically, the interleaving of exponent and mantissa in a way that makes integer addition semantics align with floating-point normalization requirements is a coincidence that L-Mul is the first algorithm to systematically exploit.

Evidence anchor: The PTX assembly in Figure 2 demonstrates the complete L-Mul fp32 implementation in 7 instructions (plus register moves), compared to the hundreds of gate-level operations in a standard fp32 multiplier (estimated at ~584 in Equation 6 for fp16, substantially more for fp32). The carry propagation behavior is described in the Figure 2 caption: "the integer addition operation also automatically send the mantissa carry to the exponent. This is different from the rounding process in traditional FP multiplier."

### Innovation 3: The Statistical Correction Offset as a Bridge Between Hardware Simplicity and Numerical Accuracy

The third conceptual contribution is the method by which L-Mul achieves accuracy competitive with or exceeding fp8 multiplication despite eliminating the mantissa product: rather than approximating `$x_m \cdot y_m$` with a reduced-precision multiplication (which would still require a multiplier circuit), L-Mul replaces it with a **statistically chosen constant** `$2^{-l(k)}$` whose value depends only on the mantissa bit width `$k$`, not on the operands.

This is a deceptively simple idea with a sophisticated justification. The naive approach to approximating `$(1 + x_m)(1 + y_m)$` with addition would be to simply drop the `$x_m \cdot y_m$` term entirely, computing `$1 + x_m + y_m$`. But this systematically underestimates the true product (since `$x_m \cdot y_m \geq 0$` for positive operands), creating a bias that would compound across the thousands of multiplication operations in a single transformer forward pass.

The paper's innovation is to add back a **constant correction** `$2^{-l(k)}$` that approximates the expected value of `$x_m \cdot y_m$`. The choice of `$l(k)$` is not analytically derived from uniformity assumptions—which would produce suboptimal results because real model weights follow non-uniform, typically bell-shaped distributions. Instead, the paper empirically optimizes `$l(k)$` by sweeping across values and measuring the mean squared error against full-precision multiplication on actual model computation waveforms (Figure 3).

The resulting piecewise function—`$l(k) = m$` for `$k \leq 3$`, `$l(k) = 3$` for `$k = 4$`, `$l(k) = 4$` for `$k > 4$`—encodes a non-obvious relationship: when few mantissa bits are kept (aggressive truncation), the correction should be tiny (dependent on the full original mantissa width `$m$`), because the truncation error dominates and adding a large constant would overshoot. When more mantissa bits are kept, the correction stabilizes at a small fixed value (`$2^{-3}$` or `$2^{-4}$`), because the actual `$x_m \cdot y_m$` product with `$k$`-bit operands has expected value in that range.

This is fundamentally different from how quantization handles the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff. Quantization methods preserve the *operation* (multiplication) exactly and accept error from reducing the *operand precision*. L-Mul preserves the operand precision (the exponent and `$k$` mantissa bits are used at full accuracy) and accepts error from replacing the *operation* with a simpler one plus a statistical correction. The correction offset is what makes this viable—without it, the systematic bias from dropping `$x_m \cdot y_m$` would be too large. With it, the error becomes zero-mean (or nearly so) with bounded variance that is analytically characterized in Section 2.3.1.

The theoretical error analysis (Equations 4–5 and Table 7) formalizes this by decomposing L-Mul error into the truncation error that a `$k$`-bit standard multiplier would also incur (the `$f_1(m,k)$` term) plus the correction mismatch from using the constant rather than the true product (the `$f_2(k)$` term). The key finding is that `$f_2(k)$` is small enough that L-Mul's total error coefficient `$|f_1 + f_2|$` is comparable to or smaller than standard `$k$`-bit multiplication's `$|f_1|$` at equivalent mantissa widths—meaning the constant approximation is actually *better* than a low-precision multiplier for some operand distributions.

Evidence anchor: Figure 3 provides the empirical optimization of `$l(k)$`, with the heatmap showing regions where L-Mul error is lower than fp8-e4m3 (highlighted in red) and between e4m3 and e5m2 (underlined). Table 7 quantifies the resulting error coefficients, showing `$|f_1 + f_2| = 0.12$` for `$k=4$` L-Mul under real weight distributions versus `$|f_1| = 0.077$` for fp8-e4m3 standard multiplication—close enough that the practical model accuracy is indistinguishable (Table 2).

### Innovation 4: Empirical Discovery That Attention Mechanisms Are Robust to Multiplication-Approximation Error but Not to fp8 Truncation Error

A finding that emerges from the benchmarking experiments (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5) and that constitutes a substantive empirical contribution is that **attention mechanisms are differentially sensitive to different types of numerical error**. Specifically, attention layers tolerate the approximation error introduced by L-Mul (replacing `$x_m \cdot y_m$` with a constant) significantly better than they tolerate the truncation error introduced by fp8 quantization (reducing operand precision to 2–3 mantissa bits while keeping exact multiplication).

This is not an obvious result. One might expect that any perturbation to the attention computation would degrade accuracy proportionally to the perturbation magnitude, and that the method with lower total numerical error would be the one that degrades less. But the data in Table 2 shows a clear pattern: across 14 experiments (7 benchmarks × 2 models), L-Mul attention—which uses a cruder *operation* but preserves more *operand bits*—matches or outperforms fp8-e4m3 attention, which uses exact multiplication but with severely truncated operands.

Specifically: on Mistral-7B, L-Mul achieves 69.93% average accuracy versus 69.83% for bf16 (a +0.10 gain) and 69.55% for fp8-e4m3. On Llama-3.1-8B, L-Mul achieves 74.00% versus 74.24% for bf16 (−0.24) and 73.40% for fp8-e4m3. In 12 of 14 comparisons, L-Mul outperforms fp8-e4m3. The drop to fp8-e5m2 is severe: 68.97% and 71.86% respectively.

What makes this a genuine contribution rather than just a reported number is the implication it carries for hardware-algorithm codesign: **operation approximation may be a better strategy than operand truncation for attention-heavy workloads**. The softmax nonlinearity in attention provides natural error dampening—small perturbations in individual attention scores map to small perturbations in the softmax output, especially for tokens that are not the attention maximum. But this dampening works better against zero-mean or small-bias errors (like L-Mul's constant offset, which is statistically tuned) than against systematic errors from operand truncation (where small mantissa values are lost entirely, creating hard zeros in the attention matrix).

The paper doesn't develop this theoretical explanation in detail, but the empirical pattern is robust and carries a practical message: if you're deploying transformers on energy-constrained hardware, replacing the multipliers in attention with L-Mul-style adders preserves more accuracy than reducing attention operands to fp8, even though the latter uses "real" multiplication. This is a finding that changes how one thinks about the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff—it's not just about how much error you introduce, but about what *kind* of error the downstream computation is robust to.

Evidence anchor: Table 2 directly compares L-Mul, fp8-e4m3, fp8-e5m2, and bf16 attention across 7 benchmarks on two models. Table 5 reinforces the pattern by showing that L-Mul with `$k=3$` mantissa bits (68.12% on MMLU for Llama) substantially outperforms fp8-e5m2 (66.61%), even though L-Mul uses a cruder operation—the error type matters more than the magnitude. Table 3 shows the same pattern on GSM8k mathematical reasoning, where L-Mul achieves 58.52% average and even slightly *improves* over bf16 (58.17%) on two of three models.

### Innovation 5: The Precision-Efficiency Pareto Improvement as a Hardware-Capability Argument for New Arithmetic Units

The paper's final substantive contribution is not algorithmic but **evidentiary**: it provides the empirical and analytical foundation for the claim that L-Mul represents a **Pareto improvement** over existing fp8 multiplication—simultaneously more accurate AND more energy-efficient—which constitutes a hardware-capability argument rather than a typical accuracy-efficiency tradeoff claim.

In the standard narrative of model efficiency, one accepts an accuracy penalty in exchange for energy savings: fp16 is less accurate than fp32 but saves energy; fp8 is less accurate still but saves more. The decision is always about where to operate on the accuracy-efficiency curve. L-Mul, if the paper's analysis holds, breaks this curve: it claims to be *above* the fp8 multiplication point on both axes simultaneously—more accurate (lower numerical error, per Table 7 and Figures 3–4) and more energy-efficient (fewer gates, simpler circuit, per Equations 6–7 and the Horowitz energy figures).

This matters because it changes the decision calculus for hardware designers. If L-Mul were merely "almost as good as fp8 multiplication but cheaper," a hardware team would have to weigh the engineering cost of implementing a new arithmetic unit against the energy savings. But if L-Mul is *better* than fp8 multiplication in both accuracy and efficiency, the argument shifts: fp8 multipliers become strictly dominated hardware components that should be replaced by L-Mul adders in any new design targeting energy-constrained AI inference. There is no tradeoff to evaluate—L-Mul is simply the superior primitive for the precision range that fp8 currently occupies.

The paper supports this claim with multiple lines of evidence:

- **Gate complexity:** L-Mul fp8 requires ~157 gates vs. ~296–325 for fp8 multiplication (Equations 6–7)—roughly half the hardware resources.
- **Energy per operation:** L-Mul fp16 estimates at ~0.06 pJ vs. ~0.25–0.4 pJ for fp8 multiplication (Section 2.3.2)—a 4–7× energy reduction per operation.
- **Numerical accuracy:** L-Mul with 3-bit mantissa achieves lower error than fp8-e5m2; L-Mul with 4-bit mantissa approaches fp8-e4m3 accuracy (Table 7, Figures 3–4).
- **Model-level accuracy:** L-Mul attention matches or exceeds fp8-e4m3 attention on real benchmarks (Tables 2–4), and full-model L-Mul fine-tuning matches standard fp8-e4m3 fine-tuning (Table 6).

The Pareto improvement claim is not without nuance. The paper acknowledges (footnote in Section 1) that "due to the absence of native implementation, GPUs cannot fully exploit the efficiency of the L-Mul algorithm"—the measured energy savings are projections based on gate counts and Horowitz (2014) energy figures, not silicon measurements. Actual fabricated L-Mul units might have different energy characteristics due to wire capacitance, clock tree overhead, or integration challenges. And the accuracy advantage over fp8-e4m3 is small (Table 2 shows differences of 0.3–0.6 percentage points in most cases), meaning the primary practical benefit is the energy reduction rather than the accuracy gain.

Nevertheless, the contribution is significant as a **capability demonstration**: the paper establishes that an algorithm exists which provably (in the analytical sense of Section 2.3.1) and empirically (in the benchmarking sense of Section 3.3) dominates fp8 multiplication on the accuracy-efficiency frontier. This is the kind of result that motivates hardware investment—it answers the question "if we build this, will it be better than what we have?" with a quantitative yes.

Evidence anchor: The Pareto claim is distributed across the paper but crystallized in the comparison of Equation (7) (157 gates for fp8 L-Mul), Equation (6) (~300 gates for fp8 multiplication), Table 7 (L-Mul error coefficients vs. standard multiplication error coefficients), and Tables 2–4 (real model accuracy). The conclusion that L-Mul "achieves higher precision but consumes significantly less bit-level computation" appears in the abstract and is defended by this multi-level evidence.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

**Dataset.** The paper evaluates on a broad suite of benchmarks spanning textual reasoning, mathematical problem-solving, and visual-language tasks. For language tasks: **MMLU** (Hendrycks et al., 2020) with 57 multi-choice subjects under 5-shot prompting; **BigBench-Hard (BBH)** (Srivastava et al., 2023), using a subset of 17 multi-choice tasks under few-shot prompting; a **Commonsense** aggregation of 5 datasets — ARC-Challenge (Clark et al., 2018), CSQA (Saha et al., 2018), OBQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), and SIQA (Sap et al., 2019); and **GSM8k** (Cobbe et al., 2021) with its standard 1,000-question test split, evaluated under few-shot chain-of-thought prompting for training-free experiments and zero-shot for fine-tuning experiments. For vision tasks: **VQAv2** (Goyal et al., 2017), **VizWiz** (Gurari et al., 2018), **TextVQA** (Singh et al., 2019) for visual question answering; **POPE** (Li et al., 2023) across random, adversarial, and popular splits for object hallucination; and **Llava-Bench** (Liu et al., 2024) for free-form visual instruction following, scored by GPT-4o. This breadth — covering structured reasoning, factual knowledge, social commonsense, arithmetic, and multimodal understanding — is deliberate: it tests whether L-Mul's numerical approximation degrades any specific type of neural computation more than others, rather than claiming success on a single narrow task.

**Base models.** Three text-only models and one vision-language model are evaluated, all in the 2–8 billion parameter range: **Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct** (Dubey et al., 2024), **Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3** (Jiang et al., 2023), **Gemma2-2b-It** (Team et al., 2024), and **Llava-v1.5-7b** (Liu et al., 2024). These are chosen to represent a range of model families (Meta, Mistral AI, Google) and scales (2B, 7B, 8B), testing whether L-Mul's behavior generalizes across architectures and training recipes rather than being an artifact of a single model's weight distribution. The models span both dense (Llama, Mistral, Gemma) and vision-language (Llava) architectures, covering the two dominant transformer deployment scenarios. All models are used with their publicly released pretrained weights — no custom pretraining is performed.

**Metrics.** For classification and multiple-choice tasks (MMLU, BBH, ARC-C, CSQA, OBQA, PIQA, SIQA, VQAv2, VizWiz, TextVQA), the metric is **accuracy** — the fraction of test examples where the model's selected answer matches the ground truth. For GSM8k, accuracy is measured as the fraction of test problems where the model's final numeric answer matches the reference solution after the grading function from Cobbe et al. (2021) is applied. For POPE, accuracy is reported separately for random, adversarial, and popular splits plus an aggregate average. For Llava-Bench, the metric is **relative score** assigned by GPT-4o evaluating response quality across three dimensions (complex reasoning, conversation, detail description) and an overall average — this is a model-based evaluation rather than exact match. For the precision analysis experiments (Figures 3 and 4), the metric is **mean squared error (MSE)** between the L-Mul or fp8 attention output and the bf16 attention output when running the same model on the same input — these are internal activation comparisons, not task accuracy.

**Baselines.** Three precision baselines are compared against L-Mul at each evaluation point:
- **BFloat16 (bf16):** The full-precision baseline using the model's native bf16 weights and standard floating-point multiplication throughout. This is the upper bound — any accuracy loss relative to bf16 represents the cost of the approximation.
- **Float8 e4m3:** Standard fp8 multiplication with 4 exponent bits and 3 mantissa bits (Micikevicius et al., 2023), applied to the same tensor operations as L-Mul. This represents the current state-of-the-art in low-precision floating-point inference — it is the precision level L-Mul aims to match or exceed.
- **Float8 e5m2:** Standard fp8 multiplication with 5 exponent bits and 2 mantissa bits, applied identically. This is a sparser-precision baseline with wider dynamic range but coarser mantissa granularity — L-Mul with `$k=3$` mantissa bits is theoretically predicted to outperform this.

For the full-model fine-tuning experiment (Table 6), the baseline is standard fp8-e4m3 accumulation precision fine-tuning with standard multiplication, compared against L-Mul with fp8-e4m3 accumulation and `$k=3$` mantissa-bit operands. The paper does not compare against int8 quantization or pruning baselines in any experiment — the comparison is exclusively against floating-point precision reduction.

**Generation budget and compute accounting.** The paper does not measure runtime or wall-clock energy, and all experiments are conducted in simulation on Nvidia GPUs using the PTX assembly emulation shown in Figure 2. The "compute budget" is quantified indirectly through two theoretical metrics established in Sections 2.3.1 and 2.3.2:
- **Gate complexity** (Equations 6–7): the estimated number of elementary NAND-equivalent gates needed to implement each operation in hardware. L-Mul fp8 requires ~157 gates versus ~296–325 for fp8 multiplication.
- **Energy per operation** (Table 1, from Horowitz, 2014): derived figures where int32 addition costs 0.1 pJ and fp32 multiplication costs 3.7 pJ, with fp8 multiplication estimated at 0.25–0.4 pJ and L-Mul at ~0.06 pJ.

No experiments measure actual energy consumption or gate-level simulation — the efficiency claims (95% reduction for element-wise, 80% for dot products) are projections based on these theoretical figures, not experimental measurements. This is a significant distinction: the paper experimentally validates *numerical accuracy* (can L-Mul match fp8 precision?) but only analytically estimates *energy efficiency* (how much energy would L-Mul save if implemented in hardware?).

**Cross-validation and statistical protocol.** There is no cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or confidence interval reporting in this paper. All results are single-run evaluations on fixed test sets — e.g., MMLU is evaluated on its standard test split once per model per precision configuration, and the reported accuracy is the point estimate. For the `$l(k)$` offset selection (Figure 3), the optimization is performed by sweeping `$k$` and `$l(k)$` values and measuring MSE on the GSM8k dataset, which means the offset function is tuned on GSM8k data and then evaluated on the same GSM8k benchmark in Table 3 — a potential circularity, though the offset is also used across all other benchmarks (MMLU, BBH, Commonsense, VQA) which were not involved in its selection. For the fine-tuning experiment (Table 6), the Gemma2-2b-It model is fine-tuned on the GSM8k training split and evaluated on the GSM8k test split — standard practice, with no held-out validation fold mentioned.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Training-Free Attention Replacement: Precision and Accuracy Across Benchmarks

The central empirical claim of the paper is that replacing the floating-point multiplications in the attention mechanism with L-Mul is **almost lossless** — defined as producing accuracy within ~0.1 percentage points of bf16 on average — and that L-Mul consistently **outperforms fp8 multiplication** at both e4m3 and e5m2 precision levels while using fewer computational resources.

**Textual benchmarks (Table 2).** The headline result across Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on 7 benchmarks (BBH, MMLU, ARC-C, CSQA, OBQA, PIQA, SIQA):

- **Mistral-7B:** bf16 achieves 69.83% average accuracy. L-Mul achieves 69.93% — a **+0.10 percentage point gain** over bf16. fp8-e4m3 achieves 69.55% (−0.28 vs. bf16), and fp8-e5m2 drops to 68.97% (−0.86). L-Mul outperforms fp8-e4m3 on 6 of 7 individual benchmarks and outperforms fp8-e5m2 on all 7.
- **Llama-3.1-8B:** bf16 achieves 74.24%. L-Mul achieves 74.00% (−0.24 vs. bf16). fp8-e4m3 achieves 73.40% (−0.84), and fp8-e5m2 drops substantially to 71.86% (−2.38). L-Mul outperforms fp8-e4m3 on 6 of 7 benchmarks, with MMLU being the sole exception (L-Mul 68.54% vs. e4m3 68.16% — L-Mul still wins, but the paper reports e4m3 as 68.16% in the table, while L-Mul is 68.54%, so this is not an exception; rather, ARC-C shows L-Mul 82.17% vs. e4m3 81.66%, PIQA 83.30% vs. 83.51%, SIQA 46.06% vs. 45.34% — L-Mul wins on 5 of 7, loses on 2 by small margins).

The average across both models: bf16 is 72.04%, L-Mul is 71.97% — a gap of **0.07 percentage points**, consistent with the abstract's "almost lossless" claim. The fp8-e5m2 average is 70.42%, meaning L-Mul is 1.55 points more accurate than e5m2 while using roughly half the gate operations (~157 vs. ~296).

The per-benchmark pattern reveals no systematic degradation on any task type: BBH (structured reasoning) shows L-Mul at 55.87% vs. bf16 55.85% on Mistral; MMLU (knowledge) shows 62.19% vs. 62.20%; ARC-C (science reasoning) shows 76.11% vs. 75.94% — L-Mul actually *improves* in several cases. This is consistent with the interpretation that L-Mul's error is zero-mean and small enough to be drowned out by other sources of variance in the forward pass.

**GSM8k mathematical reasoning (Table 3).** This benchmark is particularly important because mathematical reasoning involves precise multi-step computation where numerical errors could compound across reasoning steps. Under few-shot chain-of-thought prompting with L-Mul attention only:

- **Mistral-7B:** bf16 52.54%, L-Mul **52.92%** (+0.38), fp8-e4m3 52.39%, fp8-e5m2 50.19%.
- **Llama-3.1-8B:** bf16 76.12%, L-Mul 75.63% (−0.49), fp8-e4m3 75.44%, fp8-e5m2 71.80%.
- **Gemma2-2b-It:** bf16 45.87%, L-Mul **47.01%** (+1.14), fp8-e4m3 45.94%, fp8-e5m2 44.43%.

The average across three models: bf16 58.17%, L-Mul 58.52% (+0.35), fp8-e4m3 57.92%, fp8-e5m2 55.47%. L-Mul *improves* average accuracy relative to bf16, and the gain is particularly notable on the smallest model (Gemma2-2b, +1.14 points). This is surprising — one would expect approximation error to hurt mathematical computation — and the paper does not provide a detailed explanation beyond the general claim that the attention mechanism is robust to this type of error. The Gemma2 improvement might be a statistical fluctuation (1,000 test questions, ~46% baseline means ~460 correct answers; a 1.14-point swing is ~11 questions, which is within reasonable variance for a single evaluation run), but the paper does not report multiple runs to quantify variance.

fp8-e5m2 shows consistent degradation across all three models (drops of 2.35, 4.32, and 1.44 points respectively), confirming that 2-bit mantissa multiplication with exact multiplication is worse than L-Mul's 3–4 bit mantissa addition-based approximation. This reinforces the paper's central argument: operation approximation (L-Mul) can outperform operand truncation (fp8) at equivalent or lower hardware cost.

**Vision-language tasks (Table 4).** Evaluated on Llava-v1.5-7b with L-Mul replacing attention multiplications only:

- **POPE (object hallucination):** L-Mul achieves 86.57% / 83.19% / 85.34% on random / adversarial / popular splits vs. bf16 at 86.20% / 83.17% / 85.13%. L-Mul is *slightly better* on all three splits, with the aggregate improving from 84.83% to 85.03% (+0.20).
- **Llava-Bench (instruction following):** L-Mul achieves 64.90% / 58.70% / 43.30% on complex / conversation / detail dimensions vs. bf16 at 66.80% / 57.60% / 41.40%. The overall score is identical at 57.50%. The complex reasoning dimension shows a 1.9-point drop, but conversation and detail improve — suggesting GPT-4o's evaluation has its own variance.
- **VQAv2:** L-Mul 91.78% / 58.93% / 70.73% on yes/no / number / other vs. bf16 91.88% / 59.04% / 70.56%. The aggregate is 78.06% vs. 78.03% — effectively identical.
- **VizWiz:** L-Mul 78.54% / 50.48% / 73.78% / 38.41% on yes/no / number / unanswerable / other vs. bf16 77.19% / 45.24% / 71.75% / 38.19%. L-Mul is *uniformly better*, with the aggregate improving from 49.31% to 50.16% (+0.85).
- **TextVQA:** L-Mul 57.41% vs. bf16 57.90% — a 0.49-point drop, the largest gap in the vision suite but still small.

The vision results are notable because they test a fundamentally different architecture (vision encoder + language model with cross-modal attention) and demonstrate that L-Mul's benefits transfer. The average across all vision metrics (aggregating the sub-splits of POPE, Llava-Bench, VQAv2, VizWiz, and TextVQA) is not reported in the paper, but eyeballing the numbers suggests L-Mul is within ±0.5 points of bf16 on every task, with a slight positive bias (it wins more often than it loses, though the margin is small).

#### Mantissa-Reduction Ablation: How Few Bits Can L-Mul Use?

The paper quantifies the accuracy impact of reducing the number of mantissa bits `$k$` in L-Mul attention on MMLU (Table 5), comparing directly against fp8-e4m3 and fp8-e5m2:

- **Mistral-7B:** fp8-e4m3 = 62.18%, fp8-e5m2 = 61.75%. L-Mul **`$k=4$`** = 62.16% (essentially identical to e4m3, −0.02), **`$k=3$`** = 62.06% (outperforms e5m2 by +0.31), **`$k=2$`** = 61.08% (below e5m2 by −0.67).
- **Llama-3.1-8B:** fp8-e4m3 = 68.16%, fp8-e5m2 = 66.61%. L-Mul **`$k=4$`** = 68.43% (exceeds e4m3 by +0.27), **`$k=3$`** = 68.12% (essentially matches e4m3, −0.04, and substantially outperforms e5m2 by +1.51), **`$k=2$`** = 66.67% (comparable to e5m2, +0.06).

The key takeaway is that L-Mul with `$k=3$` mantissa bits — which the gate complexity analysis (Equation 7) estimates at ~157 gates, roughly half of fp8 multiplication's ~300 — maintains accuracy between fp8-e4m3 and fp8-e5m2 levels, and much closer to e4m3. This confirms the theoretical prediction from Table 7: L-Mul `$k=3$` with `$|f_1 + f_2| = 0.18$` (real distribution) sits between fp8-e5m2 (`$|f_1| = 0.35$`) and fp8-e4m3 (`$|f_1| = 0.077$`). At `$k=4$`, L-Mul effectively matches or exceeds e4m3 accuracy — the gate count is still lower than fp8 multiplication (though the paper doesn't give a separate gate estimate for `$k=4$` L-Mul vs. `$k=3$` L-Mul; both use 8-bit operands per Equation 7, so the gate count should be identical — the `$k$` parameter determines which mantissa bits are truncated from the full-precision operands before L-Mul is applied, not the adder width).

At `$k=2$`, performance degrades to near e5m2 levels, consistent with the truncation error dominating the approximation error. The paper does not test `$k=1$` — presumably because the error would be unacceptably large, and the theoretical error coefficient `$|f_1 + f_2| = 0.68$` for `$k=1$` under even distribution (Table 7) is roughly double fp8-e5m2's expected error — but this omission leaves open the question of where the "cliff" in accuracy occurs.

#### Full-Model Fine-Tuning with L-Mul (Table 6)

The paper goes beyond training-free attention replacement to demonstrate that L-Mul can serve as the arithmetic foundation for **both training and inference** of an entire transformer model. Using Gemma2-2b-It fine-tuned on the GSM8k training set with all multiplication operations (attention, linear layers, element-wise products) replaced by `$k=3$` mantissa L-Mul under fp8-e4m3 accumulation precision:

- **Standard fp8-e4m3 fine-tuning:** 36.09% zero-shot accuracy on GSM8k test.
- **L-Mul `$k=3$` fine-tuning:** 37.91% — a **+1.82 percentage point improvement** over the standard fp8-e4m3 baseline.
- **fp8-e5m2 fine-tuning:** 7.96% — a catastrophic drop indicating that 2-bit mantissa exact multiplication is insufficient for training.

This is a striking result: not only does L-Mul *match* fp8-e4m3 fine-tuning accuracy, it exceeds it by nearly 2 points. The paper does not fine-tune a bf16 baseline for comparison (the Gemma2-2b-It bf16 zero-shot performance is not reported in this table), so it's unclear whether the 37.91% is also competitive with full-precision fine-tuning or whether both fp8 and L-Mul are degraded relative to bf16. The bf16 few-shot performance on Gemma2-2b-It is 45.87% from Table 3, but that's few-shot, not fine-tuned zero-shot — the numbers are not directly comparable.

The catastrophic failure of fp8-e5m2 (7.96%) is informative: it demonstrates that for training, the 2-bit mantissa is insufficient to represent gradient updates with enough precision for the optimizer to converge. This is consistent with the known difficulty of training at extremely low precision — gradients require more dynamic range than forward-pass activations. L-Mul's avoidance of this failure mode at `$k=3$` (which is more energy-efficient than any fp8 format) is a strong practical argument for its use in training.

**What's missing:** The fine-tuning experiment uses only one model (Gemma2-2b), one task (GSM8k), and one `$k$` setting (`$k=3$`). There is no sweep over mantissa sizes in the fine-tuning setting, no comparison to L-Mul `$k=4$` fine-tuning (which might be even more accurate), and no evaluation on other tasks or larger models. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by positioning full-model training as a direction where L-Mul "can enhance training efficiency without compromising the fine-tuning model's performance" (Section 3.3) rather than claiming comprehensive validation. The result is suggestive rather than definitive — it proves that L-Mul training is *possible* and can recover accuracy, but does not characterize the scaling behavior or generality of this finding.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**`$l(k)$` offset optimization (Figure 3):** The offset function `$l(k)$` in Equation (1) — which determines the constant correction term `$2^{-l(k)}$` — is tuned by sweeping `$k$` from 1 to 6 and `$l$` values and measuring mean squared error between L-Mul and bf16 attention outputs on the GSM8k dataset. The resulting heatmap (Figure 3) shows that the optimal `$l$` follows the piecewise pattern encoded in Equation (1): `$l(k) = m$` (the full original mantissa width) for `$k \leq 3$`, then `$l(k) = 3$` for `$k = 4$`, then `$l(k) = 4$` for `$k > 4$`. The red-highlighted cells (configurations with lower error than fp8-e4m3) cluster at `$k \geq 4$` with `$l = 3$` or `$l = 4$`, confirming that 4-bit mantissa L-Mul with properly chosen offset matches or exceeds fp8-e4m3 precision. The underlined cells (configurations between e4m3 and e5m2 error) span `$k = 3$` with various `$l$` values, showing that 3-bit mantissa L-Mul consistently outperforms fp8-e5m2. This grid search is performed on both Llama and Gemma models, and the optimal offsets are consistent across them, suggesting the piecewise rule generalizes across model families rather than overfitting to one weight distribution. However, the offset was optimized on GSM8k — a mathematical reasoning dataset — and then applied to all other benchmarks; the paper does not test whether task-specific offset optimization would yield different `$l(k)$` values for, say, visual QA versus text understanding.

**Error vs. mantissa bits sweep (Figure 4):** The paper plots L-Mul's average error as a function of mantissa bits `$k$` and overlays horizontal bands representing fp8-e5m2 and fp8-e4m3 error levels. The L-Mul curve is monotonically decreasing with `$k$` (more mantissa bits = lower error), crossing below the e5m2 band at `$k=3$` and approaching the e4m3 band at `$k=4$`. At `$k=6$`, L-Mul achieves error substantially below both fp8 formats. This figure validates the theoretical error analysis from Section 2.3.1 and Appendix A visually: the gap between the L-Mul curve (which uses addition) and the fp8 bands (which use exact multiplication at reduced precision) demonstrates that L-Mul with 4+ mantissa bits is *more accurate* than fp8 multiplication with fewer mantissa bits. The paper notes (Section 3.2) that "L-Mul using 6-bit mantissa FP operands approximates the lowest average error, significantly outperforming both fp8 formats" — the gate count for 12-bit L-Mul (1 sign + 5 exponent + 6 mantissa = 12 bits) is estimated at ~201 gates (Equation 8), which is still lower than fp8 multiplication's ~300 gates.

**Mantissa truncation in attention (Table 5):** The ablation of mantissa bits `$k$` in the attention mechanism, discussed above under "Mantissa-Reduction Ablation," doubles as a robustness check: it demonstrates that L-Mul performance degrades gracefully as `$k$` decreases, and that the `$k=3$` / `$k=4$` regime — which the gate analysis shows is the sweet spot for energy efficiency — maintains accuracy competitive with or exceeding fp8 multiplication. The fact that Llama-3.1-8B with `$k=2$` L-Mul (66.67% MMLU) is comparable to fp8-e5m2 (66.61%) confirms that even at 2 mantissa bits, L-Mul's operation-approximation error is not worse than fp8's operand-truncation error — the total numerical perturbation is similar.

**Full-model replacement vs. attention-only replacement:** Although not presented as a formal ablation table, the contrast between the training-free attention replacement results (Tables 2–4) and the full-model fine-tuning result (Table 6) constitutes an implicit ablation of *which layers* can tolerate L-Mul without retraining. The attention-only replacement is nearly lossless without fine-tuning; replacing all layers requires fine-tuning to recover accuracy (and in the one tested case, exceeds fp8-e4m3 fine-tuning accuracy). The paper does not report the accuracy of full-model L-Mul *without* fine-tuning — this would be the relevant ablation to quantify how much accuracy is lost before retraining, and whether attention is uniquely robust or whether other layers also tolerate the approximation to some degree. This is a notable omission: a table showing bf16 vs. L-Mul-all-layers-no-fine-tuning across a few benchmarks would clarify the scope of the training-free claim.

**Vision-language model ablation across task types (Table 4):** The Llava-v1.5-7b results span five distinct task types (object hallucination detection, instruction following, general VQA, accessibility-focused VQA, and text-reading VQA). The consistency of L-Mul's performance across these — ranging from POPE (binary yes/no hallucination questions) to Llava-Bench (free-form generation scored by GPT-4o) to TextVQA (reading text in images) — serves as a robustness check that L-Mul's approximation does not interact negatively with any particular attention pattern (cross-modal attention vs. self-attention, short vs. long sequences, precise token-level reasoning vs. holistic image understanding). The largest gap is TextVQA's 0.49-point drop, which the paper does not discuss in detail but which is small in absolute terms.

**Negative result: fp8-e5m2 fine-tuning failure (Table 6):** The 7.96% accuracy for fp8-e5m2 fine-tuned Gemma2-2b-It on GSM8k is a strong negative result. It demonstrates that 2-bit mantissa exact multiplication — which L-Mul `$k=3$` outperforms in accuracy while using fewer gates — is fundamentally insufficient for training, even though it may be marginally acceptable for inference (fp8-e5m2 attention achieves 44.43% on Gemma2-2b few-shot GSM8k in Table 3). This asymmetry between training and inference sensitivity is not explored further but is practically important: it suggests that the precision floor for training is higher than for inference, and that L-Mul's ability to operate at `$k=3$` with accuracy exceeding e5m2 but using addition-based hardware places it in a uniquely favorable position for training workloads.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental results broadly support the paper's core claim that **L-Mul achieves comparable or superior accuracy to fp8 multiplication while using fewer and simpler hardware resources**. The evidence is consistent across three model families, two modalities, and a wide range of task types, with L-Mul matching bf16 attention to within 0.07 percentage points on average (Table 2) and outperforming fp8-e4m3 attention in 12 of 14 model-benchmark comparisons. The theoretical error analysis (Table 7, Figures 3–4) and the gate complexity estimation (Equations 6–7) provide a coherent quantitative story: L-Mul lies on a superior point of the accuracy-efficiency frontier.

However, several limitations constrain the strength and generality of the conclusions:

**The energy efficiency claim is entirely theoretical, not experimental.** The paper's headline numbers — 95% energy reduction for element-wise multiplications, 80% for dot products — are projections based on multiplying gate-count estimates from Section 2.3.2 by per-operation energy figures from Horowitz (2014), a 10-year-old survey of CMOS energy costs. No actual energy measurements are reported, no hardware is fabricated, and even the GPU experiments use PTX assembly to *emulate* L-Mul semantics, not to measure the energy or latency of actual L-Mul execution. The 95% and 80% figures should be understood as **upper-bound estimates of potential savings** given idealized hardware implementation, not as experimentally validated results. A fabricated L-Mul adder unit might have different energy characteristics due to wire capacitance, clock distribution overhead, or the practical challenges of integrating a new arithmetic unit into existing memory hierarchies. The paper would be strengthened by at minimum a cycle-accurate hardware simulation (e.g., using Verilog or VHDL synthesis to estimate actual gate count, critical path, and energy on a standard cell library) — but this is absent.

**The difficulty estimation analog from the reference example does not apply here** — L-Mul does not use difficulty estimation — but the paper has its own form of **circularity concern**: the `$l(k)$` offset function was optimized on GSM8k data (Figure 3), and then GSM8k is included as a benchmark where L-Mul "improves" average performance (Table 3). This is a mild form of training-on-test-set contamination, though the offset depends only on the operand distribution (which is model-wide, not task-specific), and the offset transfers to all other benchmarks without further tuning. A cleaner protocol would have tuned `$l(k)$` on a held-out calibration dataset (or even on random tensors matching the model's weight distribution) and then evaluated on all benchmarks including GSM8k without further adjustment.

**Single-run evaluations with no variance reporting.** Every benchmark result in Tables 2–6 is a single point estimate — there are no error bars, standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multi-seed evaluations. For a benchmark like MMLU (57 tasks, ~14,000 questions total across test splits), the sampling variance from a single forward pass is likely small, but for smaller benchmarks like BBH (17 tasks, unspecified but likely a few hundred questions per task) or the individual commonsense datasets, the variance could be several tenths of a percentage point. This matters because the paper's central claim — that L-Mul is "almost lossless" — rests on differences of 0.1–0.5 percentage points that could plausibly arise from sampling noise alone. The +0.10 gain for Mistral L-Mul over bf16 (69.93% vs. 69.83%) is particularly suspect as evidence of improvement rather than noise; the appropriate interpretation is "no detectable degradation," not "L-Mul improves accuracy."

**The full-model fine-tuning result is limited to one model, one task, one precision setting.** Table 6 shows that L-Mul `$k=3$` fine-tuning on Gemma2-2b-It with GSM8k achieves 37.91% vs. 36.09% for fp8-e4m3 — but this is a single data point. We don't know whether this result generalizes to larger models (where L-Mul errors might compound differently across more layers), to other tasks (where gradient precision requirements might differ), or to other `$k$` values (would `$k=4$` fine-tune even better?). The 37.91% is also low in absolute terms — Gemma2-2b is a small model, and 38% on GSM8k indicates the model is struggling with the task regardless of precision. Testing on a model that achieves 60%+ would provide a more discriminating signal of whether L-Mul fine-tuning truly matches standard fine-tuning.

**No comparison to integer quantization methods.** The paper positions L-Mul against fp8 multiplication baselines but does not compare against int8 quantization (Dettmers et al., 2022) or int4 quantization (Dettmers et al., 2024), which are widely used in practice and represent the most direct competitors for energy-efficient inference. An int8 baseline would use integer multipliers (which are cheaper than FP multipliers but still more expensive than adders) — comparing L-Mul's accuracy against int8 attention would clarify whether the addition-based approach genuinely improves upon the integer-quantization status quo, or whether similar accuracy could be achieved by simply quantizing the attention operands to int8 and using standard integer multiplication. This is a significant omission, given the paper's framing around replacing multiplication with addition as the key innovation.

**No latency or throughput measurements.** The paper measures "compute" in gate counts and projected energy, but not in wall-clock time. For a practitioner deciding whether to adopt L-Mul-based hardware or wait for it to be fabricated, the relevant metrics include inference latency (seconds per query) and throughput (queries per second per watt). The paper provides neither, even in simulation. A simple PTX instruction count comparison (standard fp16 multiply vs. the emulated L-Mul sequence in Figure 2) would give a rough sense of the GPU-level speedup or slowdown from emulation, but this is not reported.

**The claim that attention mechanism replacement is "training-free" hides a subtle caveat.** All experiments use pretrained model weights that were trained with standard floating-point multiplication. The model never "saw" L-Mul during training, so the fact that it works training-free indicates that attention is robust to this specific perturbation. But this also means the weights are suboptimal for L-Mul — the forward pass uses a different arithmetic than what the weights were optimized for. A model trained from scratch (or even fine-tuned) with L-Mul attention could potentially achieve higher accuracy than the training-free approach, because the weights would adapt to exploit (or compensate for) L-Mul's specific error characteristics. The paper's full-model fine-tuning experiment (Table 6) demonstrates this adaptation for the "all layers" case, but doesn't test attention-only fine-tuning. The training-free results should be interpreted as a **lower bound** on what L-Mul can achieve with co-design.

**The `$k=3$` setting is claimed to be "more efficient than fp8 e5m2" but the gate analysis groups all 8-bit L-Mul together.** Equation (7) estimates `$N^{\text{L-mul}}_{\text{fp8}} \approx 157$` gates regardless of `$k$` — the gate count depends on the total bit width of the operands (sign + exponent + mantissa = 8 bits for fp8 formats) and the L-Mul adder width, not on how many of those mantissa bits were truncated from a higher-precision source. The energy savings relative to fp8 multiplication come from using an adder instead of a multiplier, which is true for any `$k$`. The accuracy advantage of `$k=3$` over e5m2 and `$k=4$` over e4m3 comes from keeping more mantissa bits than the corresponding fp8 format (3 vs. 2 for e5m2, 4 vs. 3 for e4m3) while still using addition. This means the precision-efficiency tradeoff is not: "L-Mul is always better"; it's: "for a given gate budget (~157 gates for fp8-width L-Mul), you get accuracy equivalent to fp8-e4m3." The paper could be clearer that the mantissa size `$k$` is a knob that controls accuracy at *no additional hardware cost* — the adder is the same width regardless.

**Missing experiment: training-free full-model L-Mul.** The paper shows training-free attention replacement works (Tables 2–4) and fine-tuned full-model replacement works (Table 6). But what happens if you replace ALL multiplications with L-Mul in a pretrained model *without* fine-tuning? The paper doesn't report this number. It's plausible that linear layers and element-wise products are more sensitive to the approximation error than attention (no softmax dampening, error accumulates across layers), and the resulting accuracy might be poor. This would define the boundary of where L-Mul can be dropped in versus where retraining is required, which is practically useful information the paper does not provide.

In summary: the experiments convincingly demonstrate that L-Mul's numerical approximation preserves model accuracy at or above fp8 levels when applied to attention mechanisms across diverse tasks and models. The evidence for full-model training with L-Mul is preliminary but positive (one model, one task). The evidence for energy savings is entirely theoretical — well-motivated by gate counting and established energy figures, but not experimentally validated. The paper succeeds in its primary goal of establishing numerical validity to motivate hardware implementation, but stops short of the measurement-based evidence that would be needed to claim proven energy savings.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Energy Efficiency Claims Are Entirely Theoretical, Not Experimentally Validated

**The assumption or constraint.** Every headline energy figure in this paper — 95% reduction for element-wise tensor multiplications, 80% for dot products, the 0.06 pJ per L-Mul operation estimate — is a **projection**, not a measurement. These numbers are derived by combining two separate analytic estimates: (a) the gate-count analysis in Equations (6–7), which approximates the number of NAND-equivalent logic gates in L-Mul (~157 for fp8-width) versus standard fp8 multiplication (~296–325), and (b) per-operation energy figures from Horowitz (2014), a decade-old survey of CMOS energy costs for different arithmetic operations at different bit widths. The paper contains no fabricated hardware, no Verilog/VHDL synthesis results with standard cell libraries, no SPICE-level circuit simulation, and no cycle-accurate energy modeling. The PTX assembly code in Figure 2 is explicitly described as a "numerical simulation," not a hardware implementation. The footnote in Section 1 acknowledges this directly: "Due to the absence of native implementation, GPUs cannot fully exploit the efficiency of the L-Mul algorithm."

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to invest in L-Mul-native hardware — or whether to design an ASIC around L-Mul adders rather than fp8 multipliers — has no experimental evidence about actual energy consumption, die area, critical path delay, or achievable clock frequency. The gap between gate-count estimates and real silicon is substantial: wire capacitance, clock tree overhead, register file access energy, and the practical difficulties of integrating a non-standard arithmetic unit into existing memory hierarchies can easily erode a projected 80% saving to 30% or less. The Horowitz (2014) figures are for idealized arithmetic units in isolation; a complete multiply-accumulate pipeline with L-Mul would include operand fetching, result writeback, and control logic, none of which are accounted for. Similarly, the claim that L-Mul uses "significantly less computation resource than 8-bit floating point multiplication" (abstract) conflates gate count — which is estimated — with wall-clock energy and latency — which are not. Gate count is a proxy for static power and silicon area, but dynamic energy depends on switching activity per operation, which the paper does not model. A multiplier's internal nodes may toggle at different rates than an adder's internal nodes; the gate-count ratio (157 vs. ~300) does not necessarily translate to the same energy ratio.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides gate-level counting (Section 2.3.2, Equations 6–8) and references Horowitz (2014) for per-operation energy, but there is no experimental measurement of energy, power, latency, throughput, or even simulated cycle counts. The benchmarking experiments (Tables 2–6) validate numerical accuracy only — they demonstrate that L-Mul's output is close enough to standard multiplication's output that model accuracy is preserved. The energy savings remain a **theoretically motivated hypothesis** that the numerical accuracy results license investigating, but do not confirm.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this. Section 5 (Future Work) states an intention to "implement the L-Mul and L-Matmul kernel algorithms on hardware level" and develop "programming APIs for high-level model design," but no timeline, methodology, or preliminary synthesis results are provided. The paper's framing — "applying the L-Mul operation in tensor processing hardware can potentially reduce 95% energy cost" (abstract, emphasis added) — is appropriately hedged, but the hedging is easily missed when the specific percentages (95%, 80%) are quoted as results rather than projections. This limitation is fundamental: until fabricated or at minimum synthesized, the paper's central practical claim remains unvalidated.

---

### Full-Model L-Mul Is Validated on Only One Small Model, One Task, and One Precision Setting

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's most ambitious claim is that L-Mul can replace **all** floating-point multiplications in a transformer — not just attention — during both training and inference, with accuracy equivalent to fp8-e4m3. The evidence for this claim rests on a single experiment: fine-tuning Gemma2-2b-It (a 2-billion-parameter model) on the GSM8k mathematical reasoning dataset with `$k=3$` mantissa-bit L-Mul and fp8-e4m3 accumulation precision (Table 6). No other model sizes, no other tasks, no other `$k$` settings, and no multi-seed evaluation are reported for the full-model replacement scenario.

**The consequence.** A practitioner who reads Table 6 and concludes that L-Mul is a drop-in replacement for fp8 multiplication across all transformer workloads is extrapolating from a single data point. Several specific uncertainties follow from this limitation:

- **Model scale.** Gemma2-2b is the smallest model tested. Larger models (7B, 8B, 13B, 70B+) have more layers, and L-Mul approximation errors compound across each layer's forward pass and each layer's gradient computation. It is possible that at 70B parameters, the accumulated error from L-Mul in every linear layer and element-wise product is large enough to destabilize training or degrade convergence, even though at 2B parameters the same error is tolerable. Scaling behavior of L-Mul training is completely uncharacterized.

- **Task diversity.** GSM8k is a specific task format — grade-school math word problems with a single numeric answer — whose gradient signal may be more or less tolerant of L-Mul's approximation error than other tasks. Fine-tuning on MMLU (multi-choice knowledge), summarization (long-form generation), or code generation (structured output with precise syntax) could exhibit different sensitivity. The training-free results show that L-Mul attention works across diverse tasks (Tables 2–4), but attention-only error and full-model error behave differently — attention's softmax provides dampening that linear layers lack.

- **Mantissa size.** Only `$k=3$` is tested for full-model fine-tuning. The training-free attention results (Table 5) show that increasing `$k$` from 3 to 4 improves accuracy (e.g., Mistral from 62.06 to 62.16 on MMLU), and it is plausible that `$k=4$` L-Mul fine-tuning would further exceed fp8-e4m3 accuracy. Conversely, `$k=2$` might fail to train entirely (as fp8-e5m2 did, at 7.96% in Table 6). The full-model precision floor is unknown.

- **Statistical reliability.** The 37.91% vs. 36.09% difference between L-Mul `$k=3$` and fp8-e4m3 fine-tuning on GSM8k (1,000 test questions) represents a shift of ~18 questions. Without multi-seed training runs or confidence intervals, it is impossible to determine whether this is a genuine improvement or sampling noise — and the paper does not claim improvement, merely "comparable performance," which is the appropriate interpretation given the single-run nature of the experiment.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 6 is the sole data point. The paper does not provide full-model L-Mul results for Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B, or Llava-v1.5-7b. It does not evaluate full-model L-Mul on any benchmark other than GSM8k. It does not sweep `$k$` in the fine-tuning setting. It does not compare L-Mul `$k=3$` fine-tuning against bf16 fine-tuning (so the absolute performance ceiling is unknown — does bf16 fine-tuning achieve 40%? 50%? The gap between L-Mul and full-precision training is unmeasured).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation. The fine-tuning result is presented as evidence that L-Mul "can enhance training efficiency without compromising the fine-tuned model's performance" (Section 3.3), but the narrow scope of the evidence is not discussed. Section 5 gestures toward future training of "textual, symbolic, and multi-modal generative AI models optimized for deployment on L-Mul native hardware," implying that comprehensive training validation is future work. A practitioner should treat the full-model training result as a **proof-of-concept demonstration** that L-Mul training can converge and recover accuracy, not as a validated claim of generality across models, tasks, or precision settings.

---

### Training-Free Applicability Is Demonstrated Only for the Attention Mechanism; Full-Model Training-Free Behavior Is Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's most practically appealing result — that L-Mul can be dropped into pretrained models "without any additional training" (Section 1) and achieve near-lossless accuracy — is validated **exclusively for attention mechanisms**. In every training-free experiment (Tables 2, 3, 4, 5), the linear layer matrix multiplications (`$W \cdot x$` in MLP blocks, projection layers, embedding layers) and element-wise products are left in their original bf16 precision with standard multiplication. Only the two matrix multiplications inside attention — `$Q \cdot K^T$` and `$A \cdot V$` — are replaced with L-Mul.

The paper never reports what happens when a pretrained model has ALL multiplications (attention + linear + element-wise) replaced with L-Mul without fine-tuning. This is a notable omission because such a deployment scenario — take an existing model checkpoint, quantize/approximate everything, serve it with no retraining — is precisely what makes quantization methods like int8 (Dettmers et al., 2022) practically valuable. A user who wants to serve Llama-3.1-8B with L-Mul on hypothetical future hardware wants to know whether they need to fine-tune first, or whether they can deploy the released weights directly.

**The consequence.** The paper's results license only the claim "L-Mul attention is training-free," not "L-Mul is training-free." The distinction matters because:

- **Linear layers have no softmax dampening.** Attention's softmax nonlinearity provides error smoothing — a perturbation `$\epsilon$` in the attention scores maps to a perturbation of roughly `$\epsilon \cdot \text{softmax}'$` in the attention weights, and for tokens with near-zero attention the sensitivity is very low. Linear layers (`$y = Wx$`) have no such dampening; every error in computing `$W_{ij} \cdot x_j$` propagates directly to the output activation. A full-model L-Mul replacement without fine-tuning could accumulate errors across dozens of linear layers, potentially causing substantial accuracy degradation even if each individual L-Mul error is small.

- **Element-wise products have multiplicative error propagation.** Gating mechanisms (e.g., in LLaMA-style architectures with SwiGLU activations) use element-wise products where the operands are often similar magnitudes. An L-Mul error in a gate value directly scales the gated activation, and errors in earlier layers compound through subsequent layers' nonlinearities.

- **The embedding and output projection layers** involve large matrix multiplications where L-Mul errors could distort token probabilities in ways that attention's softmax does not correct (the final softmax over the vocabulary operates *after* the output projection, so errors in that projection directly affect logit values without smoothing).

Without quantifying the training-free accuracy of full-model L-Mul, the paper cannot claim that L-Mul is a drop-in replacement for existing models — only that L-Mul-based attention is.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides evidence for the two extremes: training-free attention-only works (Tables 2–4), and fine-tuned full-model works for one small model on one task (Table 6). The intermediate case — training-free full-model — is not reported. The paper also does not isolate which components beyond attention are most sensitive: replacing only the MLP linear layers with L-Mul while keeping attention in bf16, or replacing only element-wise products, would help characterize the error budget, but such ablations are absent.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this gap. The abstract claims "directly applying L-Mul to the attention mechanism is almost lossless," and the introduction states experiments "with full-precision model weights show that replacing standard multiplication operations with L-Mul in the attention mechanism is almost lossless" — both statements are accurate as written but could be misread as claiming full-model applicability. The full-model experiment in Table 6 is clearly labeled as involving fine-tuning, which partially addresses the gap but does not fill it. Section 5 suggests future work on training L-Mul-native models from scratch, implying the authors recognize that optimal L-Mul deployment involves co-design rather than post-hoc approximation, but the specific question of training-free full-model accuracy remains unanswered.

---

### No Comparison Against Integer Quantization Baselines, Which Are the Most Direct Competitors

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates L-Mul against three baselines: bf16 (full precision), fp8-e4m3, and fp8-e5m2. It does not compare against int8 quantization (Dettmers et al., 2022), int4 quantization (Dettmers et al., 2024), or any other integer-arithmetic-based efficiency method. The paper's framing — that L-Mul's core advantage is replacing floating-point multiplication with integer addition — makes integer quantization methods the most natural comparison point: int8 quantization already uses integer arithmetic (integer multiplication, not addition, but still substantially cheaper than floating-point), and if int8 achieves comparable accuracy to L-Mul with lower engineering effort (since int8 hardware support is widespread), the case for new L-Mul hardware is weakened.

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating whether to advocate for L-Mul-native hardware faces a missing comparison. Int8 quantization is a mature, widely deployed technique with established accuracy-efficiency tradeoffs. Several specific comparisons are needed but absent:

- **Accuracy comparison.** Does L-Mul attention achieve higher, lower, or equivalent accuracy compared to int8-quantized attention? At what bit width? The paper shows L-Mul `$k=3$` outperforms fp8-e5m2 on MMLU (68.12% vs. 66.61% for Llama, Table 5) — but how does this compare to int8 attention, which would use 8-bit integer multiplication rather than floating-point? If int8 attention is also lossless relative to bf16 (as is often the case for moderate quantization), then L-Mul's accuracy advantage over fp8 may not translate to an advantage over the simpler integer alternative.

- **Energy comparison.** Int8 multiplication (0.2 pJ per Table 1, from Horowitz 2014) is 18.5× cheaper than fp32 multiplication (3.7 pJ) but 2× more expensive than int32 addition (0.1 pJ). L-Mul claims to replace multiplication with addition, so it should beat int8 multiplication in energy. But by how much, and is the margin worth building new hardware for, given that int8 multiply-accumulate units are already standard in modern GPUs and TPUs?

- **Hardware availability comparison.** Unlike L-Mul, int8 inference is supported on virtually all current AI accelerators (Nvidia GPUs since Turing, Google TPUs, Apple Neural Engine). This means the energy savings from int8 are *realized today*, while L-Mul's savings are contingent on future hardware. The paper does not discuss this deployment-time tradeoff.

The paper's exclusive focus on floating-point baselines (fp8 variants) creates an implicit framing that the choice is between fp8 multiplication and L-Mul addition. But in practice, the choice for many deployment teams is between fp8, int8, and (in the future) whatever L-Mul-native hardware could provide. Without int8 baselines, the paper cannot locate L-Mul in the existing efficiency landscape.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The word "integer" appears in the paper only in the context of L-Mul's internal implementation (integer addition) and in the energy table (Table 1). There are no experiments with int8-quantized models, no comparisons of L-Mul accuracy against int8 attention, and no discussion of how L-Mul relates to the integer quantization literature beyond a brief mention in Related Work that quantization methods "spend significant time to handle outlier weights" (Section 4). This statement is not substantiated with data showing L-Mul handles outliers better than int8, nor is outlier handling tested in any experiment.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The Related Work section (Section 4) discusses quantization as a separate category and notes that "comparing to the quantization methods, our method requires less computation but achieves higher accuracy" — but this comparison is made in prose only, without experimental evidence. The absence of int8 baselines is a significant gap for any paper claiming to advance the state of the art in efficient neural network arithmetic, because integer quantization is the most widely deployed alternative to floating-point precision reduction. Future work that includes int8 (and ideally int4) baselines would substantially strengthen the case for L-Mul.

### The `l(k)` Offset Function Is Tuned on a Benchmark That Is Subsequently Used for Evaluation

**The assumption or constraint.** The offset function `$l(k)$` — the piecewise rule in Equation (1) that determines the constant correction term `$2^{-l(k)}$` — is optimized by sweeping `$k$` and `$l$` values and measuring the mean squared error between L-Mul attention and bf16 attention on the **GSM8k dataset** (Figure 3). The resulting `$l(k)$` function is then used in all subsequent experiments, including the GSM8k few-shot evaluation in Table 3, where L-Mul is reported to achieve higher average accuracy than bf16 (58.52% vs. 58.17%).

**The consequence.** The GSM8k results in Table 3 are not fully independent of the tuning procedure that produced `$l(k)$`. While `$l(k)$` is selected to minimize MSE on internal attention activations, not to maximize downstream task accuracy, and the MSE optimization uses the same GSM8k data that later appears in the few-shot accuracy evaluation, the possibility of overfitting exists. Specifically: the `$l(k)$` that minimizes MSE on GSM8k's attention patterns might be slightly different from the `$l(k)$` that would minimize MSE on a held-out dataset, and this difference could bias the GSM8k accuracy results in favor of L-Mul. The magnitude of this bias is likely small — `$l(k)$` depends on the operand distribution (which is model-wide, not task-specific) and the GSM8k inputs are just one source of query/key/value tensors — but the protocol is not clean: the offset was chosen using data from the test benchmark, and then that benchmark is reported as evidence of L-Mul's effectiveness.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports that the `$l(k)$` values were optimized on GSM8k in Section 3.2: "We first visualize the mean square errors obtained by different `$l(k)$` selections with different models on the GSM8k dataset in Figure 3." The benchmarking results on GSM8k appear in Table 3. The paper does not discuss whether `$l(k)$` was also tuned on other benchmarks or whether a separate calibration dataset was used. All non-GSM8k benchmarks (MMLU, BBH, Commonsense, VQA, etc.) are genuinely held-out with respect to the `$l(k)$` tuning, and the strong results on those benchmarks (Table 2, Table 4) suggest that the GSM8k-tuned offset transfers well — the piecewise rule is likely capturing a property of the operand distribution rather than GSM8k-specific patterns.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a potential circularity. The transferability of `$l(k)$` across benchmarks is an implicit validation — if the offset were overfit to GSM8k, MMLU and VQA accuracy would presumably suffer, and they do not — but the paper does not discuss this explicitly. A cleaner protocol would have used a separate calibration dataset (e.g., a subset of the GSM8k *training* split, not the test split used for evaluation, or data from an unrelated corpus) to tune `$l(k)$`, ensuring that all benchmark evaluations are strictly out-of-sample. The practical impact of this limitation is likely minor given the strong cross-benchmark generalization, but it affects the rigor of the specific claim that L-Mul *improves* GSM8k accuracy relative to bf16 — that +0.35 percentage point average gain (Table 3) may partially reflect the offset being optimized on the same data distribution.

### Latency and the Serial Nature of Integer Addition Are Not Addressed, Despite Being Material to Deployment Feasibility

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures computational cost exclusively through two static metrics: gate count (Equations 6–7) and estimated energy per operation (Table 1). It does not discuss **latency** — the wall-clock time required to execute one L-Mul operation — or **throughput** — the number of operations per second a hardware unit can sustain. The implicit assumption is that an adder is always faster than a multiplier, but this is not guaranteed in practice without careful pipeline design.

**The consequence.** For a practitioner designing inference hardware, latency is often as important as energy. An fp8 multiplier, despite its higher gate count, can be deeply pipelined: partial product generation, carry-save addition, and final carry-propagate addition can be separated into pipeline stages, allowing a new multiplication to begin every clock cycle even if each individual multiplication takes multiple cycles to complete. An integer adder is shallower in logic depth, but the critical path — the carry chain from least significant to most significant bit — is `$O(n)$` in the operand width `$n$` and cannot be arbitrarily pipelined without breaking the carry propagation, which would require speculative carry prediction or carry-select adders that increase area and energy.

If L-Mul's adder has a longer critical path per operation than a pipelined multiplier's per-stage delay, the clock frequency of an L-Mul-based accelerator might need to be lower, reducing throughput. The paper provides no timing analysis — no critical path length, no gate delay estimates, no maximum clock frequency projections — so a hardware designer cannot determine whether L-Mul's energy savings come at the cost of lower throughput.

Additionally, the paper's compute-optimal strategy for model integration indirectly touches on a related issue: in the training-free attention replacement, only the attention matrix multiplies use L-Mul. The linear layer matrix multiplies — which constitute the majority of FLOPs in a transformer (since MLP intermediate dimensions are typically 4× the hidden dimension) — remain in bf16 with standard multipliers. This means that even in an L-Mul-native chip, a significant fraction of the arithmetic units would still be standard floating-point multipliers, and the overall chip energy savings would be less than the per-operation L-Mul savings suggest. The paper does not analyze this system-level energy budget: what fraction of a transformer's total FLOPs are attention-internal multiplies versus linear layer multiplies? If attention is 30% of total FLOPs and L-Mul saves 80% of attention energy, the total chip energy savings are ~24%, not 80%. This is still substantial but qualitatively different from the headline numbers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There is no timing analysis, no pipeline depth discussion, no clock frequency estimation, and no system-level FLOP breakdown between attention and MLP layers. The PTX assembly in Figure 2 shows 7 instructions for the emulated L-Mul on a GPU, but this is a numerical simulation using general-purpose integer registers, not a hardware implementation — the instruction count reflects GPU ISA constraints, not L-Mul circuit timing.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address latency or throughput at all. Section 5 gestures toward hardware implementation as future work, which would necessarily involve timing closure and pipeline design, but the current paper provides no guidance on whether L-Mul's circuit characteristics are favorable or challenging for high-frequency operation. This is a significant omission for a paper whose primary contribution is a hardware-algorithm codesign proposal: energy is only one axis of the efficiency space, and latency/throughput is the other. A complete case for L-Mul-native hardware would need to demonstrate that adders can match the throughput of multipliers at the target clock frequency, or accept a throughput tradeoff and argue that the energy savings justify it. Neither argument is made.
