# SwitchHead: Accelerating Transformers with Mixture-of-Experts Attention

**ArXiv:** [2312.07987](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07987)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **SwitchHead**, a novel Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) method for the self-attention layer of Transformers that reduces both compute and memory requirements while matching the language modeling performance of parameter-matched dense baselines.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **SwitchHead**, a novel Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) method for the self-attention layer of Transformers that reduces both compute and memory requirements while matching the language modeling performance of parameter-matched dense baselines. The authors evaluate SwitchHead on C4, Wikitext-103, peS2o, and Enwik8 using Transformer XL and RoPE-based models at 47M and 262M parameter scales, demonstrating that applying MoE selectively to the value and output projections — while reusing a single key and query projection per head — allows the model to compute up to 8× fewer attention matrices than standard multi-head attention. For the 262M parameter model trained on C4, SwitchHead matches the baseline perplexity with only 44% of the compute and 27% of the memory, and in zero-shot downstream evaluation achieves more than 3.5% absolute improvement on BLiMP compared to a compute-matched dense Transformer, establishing that attention heads contain significant redundancy that can be eliminated through context-dependent expert selection without loss of expressivity — but only when the expert mechanism is applied to value and output projections rather than to keys and queries, where parameter competition degrades performance.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Attention Is a Compute and Memory Bottleneck That MoEs Have Left Untouched

The paper addresses a specific and well-defined gap: while the Mixture of Experts (MoE) paradigm has been widely and successfully applied to the feedforward (MLP) layers of Transformers to improve resource efficiency, **the self-attention layer has remained largely unaccelerated by MoE techniques**. This is not for lack of trying — prior work has attempted MoE-based attention — but existing methods fail to deliver the kind of dramatic resource reductions that MoE-MLPs achieve, often requiring complex regularization to train stably and still falling short of matching dense baselines in the parameter-matched setting.

This matters enormously for both practical and scientific reasons. On the practical side, the self-attention mechanism's computational cost scales quadratically with sequence length ($O(T^2)$) and linearly with the number of heads. For long sequences — increasingly common in modern LLM applications — computing and storing attention matrices can dominate both the FLOPs budget and the memory footprint. The paper notes in Section 1:

> "Given that attention also accounts for a considerable amount of compute and memory usage in Transformers (especially for long context sizes), using MoE for attention has potential to further improve resource efficiency."

A model that could reduce the number of attention matrices computed while preserving the expressive power of many-headed attention would directly translate to faster training, lower inference costs, and reduced memory requirements — making LLM development more accessible to researchers without massive compute budgets. The paper explicitly connects to this motivation:

> "However, training large Transformers requires a considerable amount of computing power and memory, which is not accessible to most researchers, academic institutions, and even companies."

On the scientific side, the problem touches a deeper question: **why do Transformers need so many attention heads?** Modern LLMs routinely use tens of heads (GPT-3 uses 96 heads per layer in its largest configuration; LLaMA models use 32-64). The paper explicitly hypothesizes several possible explanations in Section 2.2:

1. **Multiple input provision**: many heads provide diverse inputs for whatever operations the network performs at each step.
2. **Specialization**: heads may be specialized, with different operations using different subsets of heads — implying not all heads are needed simultaneously for any given token.
3. **Initialization diversity**: many heads may simply provide diverse outputs due to different random initializations, some being more successful than others during training.

The third hypothesis in particular raises an intriguing possibility: if heads succeed or fail based partly on initialization luck, then many heads might be redundant at inference time — they exist to provide "insurance" during training. If this redundancy could be exploited through conditional computation, the resource savings would be substantial.

### Prior Approaches and Why They Fall Short

The paper identifies three existing lines of work that attempt to address this problem, each with significant limitations:

**Mixture of Attention Heads (MoA) [Zhang et al., 2022].** This is the most directly comparable prior work. MoA treats each attention head as an expert, using a router to select which heads are active per token. However, MoA has several critical shortcomings that the paper documents both analytically and empirically:

- **Structural inefficiency**: In MoA, *each selected expert requires its own attention matrix*. If $k$ experts are selected from a pool of $E$, MoA must compute $k$ separate attention matrices ($A^h$ for each active head $h$). This fundamentally limits the resource savings — you can only save compute by selecting fewer than the total number of heads, and every selected head incurs the full $O(T^2)$ attention matrix cost.

- **High resource requirements to match baselines**: The paper's experiments (Table 1) show that MoA requires many active heads to match dense Transformer performance. For the 47M parameter model on Wikitext-103, MoA with 8 active heads achieves 12.77 perplexity vs. 12.31 for the dense baseline — and still underperforms. To get close (12.60 perplexity with 4 heads), it uses 223.5M MACs vs. SwitchHead's 170.4M MACs achieving better perplexity (12.27). For the 262M parameter model, MoA with 8 heads uses 2.9G MACs and 9.9M floats of memory — substantially more than SwitchHead's 2.0G MACs and 2.9M floats.

- **Training instability requiring multiple regularizers**: MoA uses a competitive softmax-based activation in the selection network. As the paper notes in Section 5 (Related Work), softmax-based routing is prone to "expert collapse" — a well-known MoE pathology where the router learns to always select the same few experts, wasting the rest of the parameters. MoA requires *three separate regularizers* to prevent this, adding complexity and hyperparameter sensitivity.

**Mixture of h-1 Heads [Peng et al., 2020].** This work proposes a gating mechanism that reweights head contributions, but only reduces the total number of attention heads by one — essentially compensating for the parameters consumed by the selection logic. The paper identifies that:

> "Their goal was not to reduce resource usage but to have better predictive performance."

This approach provides minimal resource savings and uses a softmax-based competitive selection mechanism that, like MoA, risks collapse. To mitigate this, the authors resort to training the gating function only on some steps — a fragile engineering trick.

**Low-rank attention approximations [Nguyen et al., 2022].** Motivated by the observation that attention matrices are often low-rank, this work constructs a small number of "global attention matrices" and derives per-head matrices as weighted combinations of these globals. However, the paper identifies a fundamental limitation:

> "They average the logits, not the final matrix, so each individual head-specific matrix has to be computed. This means that in the best case, they can only save half of the computation associated with the attention matrix because the readout (Eq. 3) is still needed."

The key insight here is subtle: averaging in logit space still requires computing every head's attention logits individually — you can't skip the $O(T^2)$ attention matrix computation per head. Only the subsequent softmax and value projection can benefit from the low-rank structure. Memory savings are similarly limited because each head's attention matrix must still be stored (or recomputed in the backward pass).

### The Structural Insight That Prior Work Missed

Understanding why SwitchHead succeeds where prior methods fail requires examining *where* exactly the MoE is applied within the attention computation. The paper's key architectural insight, developed in Section 2.2, is that the expert selection should happen **independently on the source side (values) and destination side (outputs), outside the attention core**, rather than at the level of entire heads.

To appreciate this, recall the standard multi-head attention formulation (Section 2.1). The computation for a single head $h$ involves:

1. Project inputs to queries, keys, and values: $Q^h = xW^h_Q$, $K^h = xW^h_K$, $V^h = xW^h_V$
2. Compute the attention matrix: $A^h = \text{softmax}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}} Q^h K^{h^\top})$
3. Project values using attention weights: $A^h V^h$
4. Project to output: this contributes $A^h V^h W^h_O$ to the final output

Crucially, all four projections ($W^h_K, W^h_Q, W^h_V, W^h_O$) are head-specific. A naive approach would be to make *heads* the experts — select a subset of heads to activate, as MoA does. But this forces the attention matrix to be computed *per selected expert*, because each expert has its own queries and keys that determine its attention pattern.

SwitchHead takes a fundamentally different approach. It **redefines what a "head" is**: a head is simply one computed attention matrix, with a single shared query and key projection. The MoE is applied to the **value and output projections** independently:

$$V^h = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}^h_S} s^h_S[e] \cdot x W^{h,e}_V$$

$$y = \sum_{h=0}^{n_{\text{heads}}-1} \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}^h_D} s^h_D[e] \cdot A^h V^h W^{h,e}_O$$

where $s^h_S$ and $s^h_D$ are the source-side and destination-side selection weights (computed via sigmoid), and $\mathcal{E}^h_S, \mathcal{E}^h_D$ are the sets of $k$ active experts selected via top-$k$.

This design means that **only one attention matrix is computed per head**, regardless of how many experts are selected for value and output projections. The expensive $O(T^2)$ attention matrix computation is amortized over multiple experts. This is why SwitchHead can reduce the number of attention matrices by up to 8× (e.g., from 16 heads in the dense baseline to 2 heads in SwitchHead for the 262M model on Wikitext-103, per Table 2) while maintaining performance through expert diversity in the projections.

### The σ-MoE Connection: Non-Competitive Activation Matters

SwitchHead inherits its expert selection mechanism from σ-MoE [Csordás et al., 2023], a recent MoE method for MLP layers. The critical design choice is using a **sigmoid-based non-competitive activation** rather than the softmax-based competitive routing used in most prior MoE work (including MoA).

This is not a minor implementation detail — it has profound implications for training stability. In softmax-based routing, the experts compete for each token: the softmax forces the router scores to sum to 1, meaning that if one expert's score increases, others must decrease. This creates a positive feedback loop where initially popular experts become increasingly dominant (the "rich get richer" dynamic), leading to expert collapse where only a few experts are ever used.

Sigmoid routing eliminates this competition: each expert's selection score is computed independently ($s = \sigma(x W_S)$), and the top-$k$ are selected. The paper emphasizes this in Section 2.2:

> "Following the σ-MoE method, we use a non-competitive selection function (sigmoid in Eq. 4)."

The result is that SwitchHead "does not require regularization or extra tricks for stable training" (Abstract), a stark contrast to MoA's requirement of three separate regularizers. This simplicity is practically important: it removes hyperparameters that would need tuning for new datasets or model scales, making the method more robust and easier to adopt.

### Why Parameter-Matched Evaluation Matters

The paper explicitly positions itself in the **parameter-matched setting**, following Csordás et al. [2023], rather than the FLOPs-matched setting common in MoE scaling literature (e.g., Fedus et al., 2022). This choice is deliberate and reflects a different goal.

In FLOPs-matched evaluation, MoE models typically have many more total parameters than the dense baseline (because only a subset of experts is active per token, the per-token FLOPs can match while total parameters are much larger). This evaluates whether MoEs can scale to enormous parameter counts efficiently — a valid question for datacenter-scale training.

The parameter-matched setting asks a different question: **given a fixed parameter budget, can we replace dense attention with MoE attention and save resources without losing performance?** This is the relevant question for resource-constrained settings (academic labs, on-device deployment, edge computing) where total model size is the binding constraint. The paper is explicit about this framing in Section 3:

> "We conduct our experiments in a parameter-matched setting which better reflects the task of language modeling (than the FLOPS-matched setting often used to evaluate MoEs)."

In this setting, every parameter allocated to an expert mechanism is a parameter *not* available for other parts of the model — so inefficient MoE designs actively hurt performance by consuming the parameter budget without providing commensurate benefit. This is why the ablation in Section 3.1 (which projections to make MoE) is critical: making the wrong projections into MoEs "uses the parameter budget that can be otherwise spent on other parts of the network."

### The Gap SwitchHead Fills

Synthesizing the above, the paper identifies and fills a specific gap in the literature:

**Existing MoE methods for attention (MoA, Peng et al.) produce modest resource savings at best, require complex stabilization, and often cannot match dense baselines in the parameter-matched setting.** The root cause is structural: by making entire heads the unit of expert selection, they force expensive attention matrix computations per selected expert.

**Low-rank approximations (Nguyen et al.) provide only partial savings** because they must compute per-head attention logits individually.

**σ-MoE demonstrated successful parameter-matched MoE for MLPs** but explicitly did not address attention, leaving open the question of whether the same principles (non-competitive activation, independent source/destination routing) could be applied to attention layers.

SwitchHead fills this gap by showing that **MoE applied to value and output projections, with non-competitive sigmoid routing and independent source/destination selection, enables dramatic resource reductions while matching dense performance** — without requiring regularization tricks. The paper further shows that this can be combined with σ-MoE MLPs to create "SwitchAll," a fully MoE-based Transformer, confirming there are no negative interactions between the two mechanisms.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper develops **SwitchHead**, a modified self-attention layer for Transformers that uses Mixture of Experts (MoE) to dramatically reduce computational cost and memory usage. The system is a drop-in replacement for standard multi-head attention: it computes far fewer attention matrices (the expensive $O(T^2)$ operation) by sharing a single query-key pair across multiple learned "expert" projections for the value and output transformations, selecting which experts to use on a per-token basis using a simple, non-competitive gating mechanism. The problem it solves is the redundancy of attention heads in standard Transformers — empirically, tens of heads are needed for good performance, but not all are equally useful for every token — and the shape of the solution is to factorize the attention computation so that head diversity is provided through cheap, context-dependent linear projections rather than through expensive, per-head attention matrix computations.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The SwitchHead attention layer has five major components that process a sequence of token representations:

1. **Input Representations** — a matrix $x \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$ containing the hidden state for each of the $T$ tokens in the sequence, produced by the previous Transformer layer.

2. **Per-Head Key and Query Projections (shared, not MoE)** — for each of the $n_{\text{heads}}$ attention heads, a single learned linear projection maps the input to queries ($Q^h = xW^h_Q$) and keys ($K^h = xW^h_K$). These are standard, non-MoE projections — every head has exactly one key matrix and one query matrix, and they are used to compute the attention pattern for that head.

3. **Source-Side Expert Selection and Value Projection (MoE)** — for each head $h$, a small router network takes the input $x$ and produces a score vector $s^h_S \in \mathbb{R}^E$ (where $E$ is the number of value experts per head). The top-$k$ scoring experts are selected, and the value representation $V^h$ is computed as the weighted sum of the selected experts' projections: $V^h = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}^h_S} s^h_S[e] \cdot (xW^{h,e}_V)$. This happens *before* the attention matrix is applied, so only one $V^h$ is produced per head regardless of $k$.

4. **Attention Matrix Computation and Value Projection** — for each head $h$, the standard scaled dot-product attention is computed: $A^h = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{\text{head}}}}Q^h K^{h^\top}\right)$, and the values are projected: $A^h V^h$. Since $n_{\text{heads}}$ is small (typically 2–4 in SwitchHead vs. 10–16 in the dense baseline), only a few $T \times T$ attention matrices are computed and stored.

5. **Destination-Side Expert Selection and Output Projection (MoE)** — for each head $h$, a second router network produces destination-side scores $s^h_D \in \mathbb{R}^E$, selects the top-$k$ output experts, and the final contribution from head $h$ is $\sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}^h_D} s^h_D[e] \cdot A^h V^h W^{h,e}_O$. The per-head contributions are summed to produce the layer output $y \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$.

Information flows through these components sequentially: input → key/query projection → (source routing + value expert mixing) → attention matrix → (value projection) → (destination routing + output expert mixing) → sum over heads → output. The key structural property is that the MoE selections happen *outside* the attention core — values are mixed into a single representation before the attention matrix is applied, and outputs are mixed after — so the number of attention matrices scales with $n_{\text{heads}}$ rather than with $n_{\text{heads}} \times k$.

#### The Resource Model: Where Standard Attention Spends Its Budget

Before explaining how SwitchHead saves resources, the paper provides a detailed accounting of where standard multi-head attention spends compute and memory (Appendix A.2). Understanding this baseline is essential because SwitchHead's design choices are precisely targeted at the dominant cost terms.

For a standard Transformer XL attention layer with $n_{\text{heads}}$ heads, sequence length $T$, context length $CT$ (where $C-1$ past chunks are included), head dimension $d_{\text{head}}$, and model dimension $d_{\text{model}}$, the total number of multiply-accumulate operations (MACs) is:

$$N^{\text{XL}}_{\text{MAC}} = n_{\text{heads}} \left( 4T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}} + 2C T^2 d_{\text{head}} + 2C T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}} \right)$$

where the three terms inside the parentheses represent:
- **$4T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$**: the cost of computing the four linear projections per head — key ($xW^h_K$), query ($xW^h_Q$), value ($xW^h_V$), and the per-head output contribution ($A^h V^h W^h_O$). Each multiplies a $T \times d_{\text{model}}$ input by a $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{head}}$ matrix, costing $T d_{\text{model}} d_{\text{head}}$ MACs.
- **$2C T^2 d_{\text{head}}$**: the cost of computing the attention matrix ($Q^h K^{h^\top}$, which is $T \times d_{\text{head}}$ times $d_{\text{head}} \times CT$, costing $C T^2 d_{\text{head}}$) and projecting the values ($A^h V^h$, which is $T \times CT$ times $CT \times d_{\text{head}}$, also costing $C T^2 d_{\text{head}}$). This term scales quadratically with sequence length — it is the dominant cost for long sequences.
- **$2C T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$**: the cost of computing the Transformer XL relative position encodings, which involves matrix multiplications between position embeddings and the queries/keys.

The corresponding memory requirement (in number of floats stored for backpropagation) is:

$$N^{\text{XL}}_{\text{mem}} = n_{\text{heads}} \left( 4T d_{\text{head}} + 2C T^2 + 2C T d_{\text{head}} \right)$$

where:
- **$4T d_{\text{head}}$**: storage for the key, query, value, and projected-value tensors, each of shape $T \times d_{\text{head}}$ (or $CT \times d_{\text{head}}$ for keys and values in XL).
- **$2C T^2$**: storage for the attention matrix both before and after the softmax activation. This term dominates memory for long sequences — a 2048-token sequence with $C=2$ requires storing $2 \times 2 \times 2048^2 \approx 16.8$ million floats *per head*.
- **$2C T d_{\text{head}}$**: storage for the position encoding tensors.

**What this accounting reveals.** The critical observation — which motivates the entire SwitchHead design — is that the number of heads $n_{\text{heads}}$ multiplies *everything*. Reducing $n_{\text{heads}}$ from 16 to 2 would naively divide all costs by 8, but the paper's experiments (Table 2) show that a 2-head dense Transformer significantly underperforms: on C4 at 47M parameters, the 2-head model achieves 23.71 perplexity vs. 22.71 for the 10-head model; on Wikitext-103 at 262M, the 2-head model achieves 10.09 vs. 9.80 for the 16-head model. The performance gap exists despite keeping the total parameter count the same (by increasing $d_{\text{head}}$ to compensate for fewer heads). This demonstrates that **many heads are genuinely necessary for good performance** — they are not merely redundant copies that can be pruned without consequence.

**Why many heads might be necessary.** The paper offers three hypotheses in Section 2.2: heads provide diverse inputs for downstream operations, heads are specialized for different operations, or heads provide initialization diversity where some happen to learn useful functions while others do not. SwitchHead is designed to preserve the benefits of having many distinct projections (hypotheses 1 and 2) while eliminating the cost of computing many separate attention matrices (hypothesis 3 suggests the attention patterns themselves may be largely redundant).

#### The SwitchHead Formulation: Decoupling Experts from Attention Matrices

The paper develops SwitchHead from first principles, starting with a naive approach and refining it. The key equations (7–10 in the paper) define the architecture.

**Source-side expert selection.** For each head $h \in \{1, \dots, n_{\text{heads}}\}$, the source-side router computes:

$$s^h_S = \sigma(x W^h_S)$$

$$\mathcal{E}^h_S = \text{arg top}_k(s^h_S, k), \quad \mathcal{E}^h_S \subset \{1, \dots, E\}$$

where $x \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$ is the input sequence, $W^h_S \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times E}$ is the learned router weight matrix for head $h$ (source side), $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function applied element-wise, $s^h_S \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times E}$ contains independent selection scores for each token and each expert, $\text{arg top}_k$ selects the indices of the $k$ largest scores per token, and $\mathcal{E}^h_S$ is the set of selected expert indices (size $k$). The sigmoid is applied per element, meaning each expert's score $s^h_S[t, e]$ for token $t$ is $\frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(x[t] \cdot W^h_S[:, e]))}$, computed independently of all other experts' scores.

**What this computes:** for each token in the sequence and each of the $n_{\text{heads}}$ heads, a learned linear function produces $E$ independent scalar scores (one per value expert candidate), each passed through a sigmoid to produce a value in $(0, 1)$. The $k$ experts with the highest scores are selected for that token in that head. The computation is $T \times n_{\text{heads}} \times E$ independent sigmoid evaluations, each depending only on the token's representation and the expert's learned weight vector.

**Why sigmoid and top-$k$:** the sigmoid makes selection **non-competitive** — expert $e$'s score does not depend on expert $e'$'s score. This prevents the "rich get richer" dynamic of softmax routing where popular experts suppress others. The top-$k$ then provides sparsity (only $k$ of $E$ experts are active per token), which is necessary for computational savings. The combination of independent scores + hard top-$k$ selection means the model can learn to use different experts for different tokens without any expert needing to "compete" against others — it just needs its own score to be among the top $k$.

**Value projection with MoE.** The value representation for head $h$ is:

$$V^h = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}^h_S} s^h_S[e] \, x W^{h,e}_V$$

where $W^{h,e}_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{head}}}$ is the value projection matrix for expert $e$ of head $h$, $s^h_S[e]$ is the sigmoid score for that expert (acting as a scalar weight), and the sum is over the $k$ selected experts. The result $V^h \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{head}}}$ is a single value matrix per head.

**What this computes:** instead of having one value projection per head ($V^h = x W^h_V$), SwitchHead has $E$ candidate value projections per head, and for each token, a weighted average of the top-$k$ experts' projections is used. The mixing happens *before* the attention matrix is applied — there is still only one $V^h$ matrix per head. This means the expensive step $A^h V^h$ (which multiplies a $T \times CT$ attention matrix by a $CT \times d_{\text{head}}$ value matrix) is performed exactly once per head, not $k$ times.

**Why mix values before attention:** if experts were mixed *after* attention (as MoA does, mixing $A^h V^{h,e}$ outputs), each expert would require its own attention matrix $A^h$, multiplying the dominant $O(T^2)$ cost by $k$. Mixing before attention amortizes the $O(T^2)$ cost over $k$ experts. The tradeoff is that all selected value experts share the same attention pattern $A^h$ — but since the query and key are shared within the head, the attention pattern is already determined regardless of which value experts are selected.

**Destination-side expert selection.** Symmetrically, for the output projection:

$$s^h_D = \sigma(x W^h_D)$$

$$\mathcal{E}^h_D = \text{arg top}_k(s^h_D, k), \quad \mathcal{E}^h_D \subset \{1, \dots, E\}$$

with $W^h_D \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times E}$. The output projection becomes:

$$y = \sum_{h=0}^{n_{\text{heads}}-1} \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}^h_D} s^h_D[e] \, A^h V^h W^{h,e}_O$$

where $W^{h,e}_O \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{head}} \times d_{\text{model}}}$ is the output projection matrix for expert $e$ of head $h$, $A^h \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times CT}$ is the attention matrix for head $h$, $A^h V^h \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{head}}}$ is the attention-weighted value representation, and $y \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$ is the layer output.

**What this computes:** the attention output $A^h V^h$ is projected to the model dimension using a weighted combination of $k$ output experts, with the weights determined by independent sigmoid scores on the destination (query) side. The results from all $n_{\text{heads}}$ heads are summed. The output expert selection depends on the token being processed (the same token whose representation is used as input to $W^h_D$), but all tokens share the same set of selected experts per head per position.

**The key architectural property.** The number of attention matrices computed is $n_{\text{heads}}$ — exactly one per head — regardless of $E$ (the total number of experts per head) or $k$ (the number of active experts). The total number of projection experts is $n_{\text{heads}} \times E$ for values and $n_{\text{heads}} \times E$ for outputs, but only $n_{\text{heads}} \times k$ are active per forward pass. This decouples the diversity of projections (which scales with $E$) from the cost of attention (which scales with $n_{\text{heads}}$).

Note that keys and queries are *not* MoE in the best configuration: $K^h = x W^h_K$ and $Q^h = x W^h_Q$ are single, head-specific projections. This is an empirical finding (Section 3.1) that we will examine in detail below.

#### The Expert Selection Mechanism: Sigmoid Routing and Its Advantages

The gating mechanism deserves careful attention because it is what makes SwitchHead trainable without the complex regularization that plagues MoA and other softmax-based MoE methods.

**The router weight matrices.** For each head $h$, there are two learned router matrices:
- $W^h_S \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times E}$ for source-side (value) expert selection
- $W^h_D \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times E}$ for destination-side (output) expert selection

Each column of $W^h_S$ (or $W^h_D$) is a learned vector in the model's hidden space. The dot product $x[t] \cdot W^h_S[:, e]$ measures how much token $t$'s representation "matches" expert $e$'s preferred input pattern. Passing this through a sigmoid produces a score in $(0, 1)$ that can be interpreted as an independent "affinity" between the token and that expert.

**Non-competitive activation: the sigmoid.** The function $\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$ has a crucial property that distinguishes it from the softmax used in most MoE routers: **each output dimension depends only on the corresponding input dimension**. Formally, $\partial \sigma(z)_i / \partial z_j = 0$ for $i \neq j$, whereas for softmax, $\partial \text{softmax}(z)_i / \partial z_j \neq 0$ for all $i, j$.

This independence means:
1. **No expert competition during training.** If expert $e$ is useful for a particular token, the gradient signal will increase $W^h_S[:, e]$'s alignment with that token's representation, regardless of what other experts are doing. In softmax routing, increasing one expert's score necessarily decreases others' (since scores must sum to 1), creating a zero-sum dynamic that can cause expert collapse.
2. **Multiple experts can be simultaneously "confident" for the same token.** The sigmoid allows $s^h_S[t, e_1] \approx 0.9$ and $s^h_S[t, e_2] \approx 0.9$ simultaneously, meaning both experts contribute strongly. Softmax would force one to be near 1.0 and the other near 0.0, preventing the model from combining multiple specialized projections.
3. **No load-balancing loss needed.** Standard MoE models (including MoA) require auxiliary losses to encourage uniform expert utilization and prevent collapse. Because sigmoid routing doesn't create competition, experts naturally diversify through gradient signals — each expert learns to be useful for the subset of tokens where it improves the loss, without needing to "fight" other experts for the right to be selected.

**Top-$k$ selection for sparsity.** After computing the sigmoid scores, only the $k$ experts with the highest scores are retained; the rest are zeroed out. This is the only source of sparsity in the model — without it, all $E$ experts would contribute weighted sums, providing no computational savings. The $\text{arg top}_k$ operation is non-differentiable (it's a discrete selection), but gradients flow through the selected experts' sigmoid weights, which remain differentiable.

**Separate source and destination routing.** Having two independent routers ($W^h_S$ and $W^h_D$) for values and outputs reflects the asymmetry of attention: the source side (keys and values) corresponds to the information being attended *to*, while the destination side (queries and outputs) corresponds to the information being attended *from*. A token might need different value projections when serving as a source for other tokens' attention than when producing its own output. The paper's ablation (Table 6) implicitly validates this: the configuration with only output MoE and no value MoE (V=N, O=Y) achieves 12.30 perplexity, nearly matching the full 10-head baseline (12.31), while the configuration with only value MoE (V=Y, O=N) achieves 12.45 — suggesting output-side expert diversity is more critical, but both provide benefits.

**Shared selection variant.** For additional speedup, the paper explores sharing the selection between source and destination sides ($s^h_S = s^h_D$, a single router). This reduces the number of top-$k$ and sorting operations, providing a modest wall-clock acceleration at the cost of a small perplexity increase (22.81 vs. 22.53 for the 47M C4 model, per Table 4). This represents a practical tradeoff for deployment scenarios where inference latency is the binding constraint.

#### Which Projections Should Be MoE? The Critical Ablation

One of the paper's most important empirical findings is that **not all attention projections benefit from being MoE** — and applying MoE to the wrong projections actively hurts performance in the parameter-matched setting. Section 3.1 and Table 6 provide an exhaustive ablation.

**The experimental setup.** Using a 47M parameter SwitchHead model with $n_{\text{heads}} = 2$ and $E = 5$ experts per head, trained on Wikitext-103, the authors test all 16 possible combinations of whether the key ($K$), query ($Q$), value ($V$), and output ($O$) projections are MoE or fixed per head. All models are parameter-matched (same total parameter count), so adding MoE to one projection consumes parameter budget that could have been used to increase $d_{\text{head}}$, $d_{\text{ff}}$, or other model dimensions.

**The results (Table 6).** The configurations are sorted by perplexity, with the dense 10-head baseline (12.31) and 2-head baseline (12.74) shown for reference:

- **Best: V=Y, K=N, Q=N, O=Y — 12.27 perplexity (beats 10-head baseline)**
- Second: V=N, K=N, Q=N, O=Y — 12.30 (beats 10-head baseline)
- Dense 10-head: 12.31
- V=Y, K=Y, Q=N, O=Y — 12.37
- V=Y, K=N, Q=Y, O=Y — 12.42
- V=Y, K=N, Q=N, O=N — 12.45 (value-only MoE)
- V=N, K=N, Q=Y, O=Y — 12.45 (output-only MoE with query MoE)
- V=Y, K=N, Q=Y, O=N — 12.51
- V=Y, K=Y, Q=Y, O=Y — 12.57 (all MoE)
- ... (intermediate configurations) ...
- **Worst: V=N, K=Y, Q=Y, O=N — 12.90 (key + query MoE, no value/output MoE)**

**Analysis of the pattern.** Three clear findings emerge:

1. **Output projection MoE is the single most important component.** The second-best model (O=Y only, everything else fixed) achieves 12.30, nearly matching the full SwitchHead. No model without output MoE (O=N) beats the 10-head baseline — the best O=N configuration is V=Y, K=N, Q=N, O=N at 12.45, which is substantially worse than 12.31. This suggests that having multiple, context-dependent output projections is critical for the model to make effective use of the attention output.

2. **Value projection MoE provides additional benefit on top of output MoE.** Adding value MoE to output MoE (V=Y, O=Y) improves from 12.30 to 12.27. The gain is small but consistent — in the larger 262M experiments (Table 2), the V+Y+O+Y configuration continues to be the one used.

3. **Key and query MoE are detrimental in the parameter-matched setting.** Adding MoE to keys, queries, or both consistently *increases* perplexity compared to keeping them fixed. For example, comparing V=Y, O=Y (12.27) to V=Y, K=Y, Q=Y, O=Y (12.57) shows a 0.30 perplexity degradation from adding key and query MoE. The worst configuration (12.90) has key and query MoE without value or output MoE — the parameters are being spent on the wrong projections.

**Why key and query MoE hurt.** The paper explains this in Section 3.1 and Appendix A.3:

> "This is possible because we perform all our experiments in a parameter-matched setting. Allocating parameters to these projections uses the budget that can be otherwise spent on other parts of the network."

In more concrete terms: every expert added to the key or query projections consumes $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{head}}$ parameters that could have been used to increase $d_{\text{head}}$ (making all projections more expressive) or $d_{\text{ff}}$ (making the MLP layers more expressive). Since the keys and queries are only used to compute attention patterns — and the paper's hypothesis is that attention patterns themselves are largely redundant across heads — the model gets more value from having richer value and output representations than from having diverse attention patterns. Additionally, the paper notes in Appendix A.3 that "allowing the parameter budget to increase, more experts always help" — the negative effect is specific to the zero-sum parameter competition in the matched setting, not an inherent property of key/query MoEs.

**The selected configuration for all subsequent experiments.** Based on this ablation, the paper uses **MoE for value and output projections only, with fixed per-head key and query projections**, throughout all remaining experiments (Tables 1–5, Tables 7–8). This configuration is what the paper refers to simply as "SwitchHead."

#### Resource Accounting for SwitchHead

Having established the architecture, the paper derives the computational and memory costs of SwitchHead to compare analytically with dense attention and MoA (Appendix A.2).

**SwitchHead MACs.** For the configuration with MoE values and outputs but fixed keys and queries:

$$N^{\text{SwitchHead}}_{\text{MAC}} = n_{\text{heads}} \left( 2T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}} + 2T k d_{\text{head}} (d_{\text{model}} + 1) + 2C T^2 d_{\text{head}} + 2C T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}} \right)$$

where the terms represent:
- **$2T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$**: the cost of the two fixed (non-MoE) projections — keys and queries. Each is a $T \times d_{\text{model}}$ by $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{head}}$ multiplication.
- **$2T k d_{\text{head}} (d_{\text{model}} + 1)$**: the cost of the two MoE projections — values and outputs. Each involves $k$ matrix multiplications of size $T \times d_{\text{model}}$ by $d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{head}}$, costing $T k d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$, plus $T k d_{\text{head}}$ for the weighted averaging step (multiplying each expert's output by its sigmoid score and summing). The " + 1" accounts for this averaging.
- **$2C T^2 d_{\text{head}}$**: the attention matrix computation and value projection — identical to the dense case (this is the cost SwitchHead does *not* multiply by $k$).
- **$2C T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$**: the position encoding computation, identical to the dense case.

**Comparing to dense attention.** The critical comparison is between SwitchHead's compute and the dense baseline's compute. The dense model uses some number of heads $n'_{\text{heads}}$ (typically 10–16 in the paper). SwitchHead uses a much smaller $n_{\text{heads}}$ (typically 2–4), a larger $d_{\text{head}}$ (to match parameter count), and $k$ active experts. The savings come from two sources:

1. **Reduced attention matrix cost**: the $2C T^2 d_{\text{head}}$ term is multiplied by $n_{\text{heads}}$ (small) instead of $n'_{\text{heads}}$ (large). For the 262M Wikitext-103 model, $n_{\text{heads}} = 2$ for SwitchHead vs. $n'_{\text{heads}} = 16$ for the dense baseline — an 8× reduction in the number of attention matrices, directly reducing the dominant $O(T^2)$ cost.

2. **Reduced projection cost from fewer heads**: even though each SwitchHead projection is more expensive (larger $d_{\text{head}}$ and MoE overhead), the number of heads is dramatically smaller. The MoE overhead adds $2T k d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$ compared to $2T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$ for dense projections, but with $k=2$ or $k=4$, this is a modest factor.

The paper quantifies this concretely in Table 2: for the 262M C4 model, SwitchHead uses 2.4G MACs vs. 5.4G MACs for the 16-head dense baseline — a 56% reduction (SwitchHead uses 44% of the baseline compute). The memory usage (number of floats stored) is 5.6M vs. 21M — a 73% reduction (SwitchHead uses 27% of the baseline memory).

**SwitchHead memory usage.** The paper notes that memory usage "is not affected by $k$" with a smart kernel implementation, because the intermediate per-expert projections can be computed and immediately combined without storing all $k$ of them simultaneously. The memory formula is identical to the dense case (Equation 12) but with SwitchHead's specific values of $n_{\text{heads}}$ and $d_{\text{head}}$:

$$N^{\text{SwitchHead}}_{\text{mem}} = n_{\text{heads}} \left( 4T d_{\text{head}} + 2C T^2 + 2C T d_{\text{head}} \right)$$

The savings in memory come primarily from the reduced $n_{\text{heads}}$ — storing $2 \times T^2$ attention matrices instead of $16 \times T^2$ produces roughly an 8× reduction in the dominant memory term.

**Comparison with MoA (Equations 14–15).** The paper provides MoA's resource formulas to show why SwitchHead is structurally more efficient:

$$N^{\text{MoA}}_{\text{MAC}} = (2n_{\text{heads}} + 2)T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}} + 2n_{\text{heads}} C T^2 d_{\text{head}} + 2C T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$$

$$N^{\text{MoA}}_{\text{mem}} = (2n_{\text{heads}} + 2)T d_{\text{head}} + 2n_{\text{heads}} C T^2 + 2C T d_{\text{head}}$$

The critical difference is in the $2n_{\text{heads}} C T^2 d_{\text{head}}$ term (and corresponding memory term). In MoA, $n_{\text{heads}}$ is the number of *selected* (active) experts per token, so the $O(T^2)$ attention matrix cost is multiplied by the number of active experts. To match dense performance, MoA needs $n_{\text{heads}}$ to be relatively large (4–8 active heads per Table 1), so the quadratic cost is multiplied by 4–8. In SwitchHead, the $O(T^2)$ cost is multiplied by $n_{\text{heads}}$ (the number of computed attention matrices, typically 2–4), which is independent of $k$, the number of active experts per head.

**Concrete comparison from Table 1.** For the 47M Wikitext-103 model, MoA with 4 active heads achieves 12.60 perplexity using 223.5M MACs and 1.3M floats of memory. SwitchHead with 2 heads achieves better perplexity (12.27) using only 170.4M MACs (24% less compute) and 0.8M floats of memory (38% less memory). The gap widens at the 262M scale: MoA needs 8 active heads (2.9G MACs, 9.9M memory) to achieve 9.50 perplexity, while SwitchHead achieves 9.55 perplexity with 2 heads (2.0G MACs, 2.9M memory) — 31% less compute and 71% less memory.

#### The Hyperparameter Selection Procedure for Parameter-Matched Experiments

The paper follows a systematic procedure to configure SwitchHead models in the parameter-matched setting (Section 3):

> "First, we set $n_{\text{heads}} \times E$ to be the same as $n_{\text{heads}}$ of the dense baseline. We start with setting $n_{\text{heads}} = 2$ and $k = 2$, which provide the most resource reductions. If the resulting model underperforms, we increase $k$. If $k = 4$ underperforms as well, we set $n_{\text{heads}} = 4$ and $k = 2$. We always set $d_{\text{head}}$ so that the total number of parameters of the resulting model matches the number of parameters of the baseline."

Breaking this down step by step:

1. **Match total expert count to baseline head count.** Set $n_{\text{heads}} \times E = n'_{\text{heads}}$, where $n'_{\text{heads}}$ is the number of heads in the dense baseline. For the 47M models with $n'_{\text{heads}} = 10$, this gives $2 \times 5 = 10$ experts total (2 heads × 5 experts each). For the 262M models with $n'_{\text{heads}} = 16$, the configurations vary: for Wikitext-103, $n_{\text{heads}} = 2$ with $E = 8$ (2 × 8 = 16 total experts); for C4, $n_{\text{heads}} = 4$ with $E = 4$ (4 × 4 = 16). The idea is that the total number of distinct projection matrices (experts) should be comparable to the baseline's number of heads, so the model has similar expressive capacity.

2. **Start with the most resource-efficient configuration.** $n_{\text{heads}} = 2$ minimizes the number of attention matrices. $k = 2$ (selecting 2 of $E$ experts per token) provides sparsity while still allowing expert combination. This is the configuration that would provide maximum resource savings if it works.

3. **Increase $k$ if performance is insufficient.** If $n_{\text{heads}} = 2, k = 2$ underperforms the dense baseline, increase to $k = 3$ or $k = 4$. More active experts per token means the model can combine more specialized projections, at the cost of additional compute (more projection multiplications, though still only one attention matrix). Table 2 shows examples: the 47M C4 model uses $k=3$ (with $E=5$), while the 47M Wikitext-103 model uses $k=2$ — suggesting C4 is a harder dataset requiring more expert diversity.

4. **Increase $n_{\text{heads}}$ if increasing $k$ is insufficient.** If even $k = 4$ doesn't match baseline performance, increase to $n_{\text{heads}} = 4$ and $k = 2$. This doubles the number of attention matrices but provides more independent attention patterns. Table 2 shows this for the 262M C4 model ($n_{\text{heads}} = 4, E = 4, k = 2$) and the 262M peS2o model ($n_{\text{heads}} = 4, E = 4, k = 2$). The 262M Wikitext-103 model uses $n_{\text{heads}} = 2, E = 8, k = 4$ — the procedure found that staying with 2 heads but increasing $k$ to 4 was sufficient.

5. **Adjust $d_{\text{head}}$ for exact parameter matching.** After choosing $n_{\text{heads}}$, $E$, and $k$, the head dimension $d_{\text{head}}$ is set so that total parameters (including all projection matrices, router matrices, and MLP layers) match the baseline. A larger $d_{\text{head}}$ increases the expressiveness of each individual projection (both expert and non-expert), but also increases the cost of all operations that depend on it (including the $O(T^2)$ attention matrix computation). This creates a tradeoff: with fewer heads, $d_{\text{head}}$ can be larger, partially compensating for the reduced head count.

**Why this procedure is reasonable.** The paper notes that this "reasonably simple procedure ensures a good amount of resource savings, while avoiding doing an expensive hyperparameter search." The procedure's logic follows from the resource analysis: minimizing $n_{\text{heads}}$ provides the largest savings (reducing the quadratic attention cost), while $k$ provides a finer-grained knob for trading off expert diversity against compute. The stepwise approach (try $k=2$, then $k=4$, then increase $n_{\text{heads}}$) explores the Pareto frontier of resource vs. performance in a structured way.

**Concrete hyperparameters from the paper (Appendix A.5, Table 9).** Key configurations from the main experiments:

| Model | Dataset | $n_{\text{heads}}$ | $d_{\text{head}}$ | $E$ | $k$ | $T$ | $n_{\text{layers}}$ | $d_{\text{ff}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SwitchHead 47M | C4 | 2 | 76 | 5 | 3 | 256 | 16 | 2080 |
| SwitchHead 47M | Wikitext-103 | 2 | 76 | 5 | 2 | 256 | 16 | 2080 |
| SwitchHead 47M | peS2o | 2 | 76 | 5 | 3 | 256 | 16 | 2080 |
| SwitchHead 262M | C4 | 4 | 112 | 4 | 2 | 512 | 18 | 4188 |

The dense baselines have the same $d_{\text{model}} = n_{\text{heads}} \times d_{\text{head}}$, $n_{\text{layers}}$, $T$, and similar $d_{\text{ff}}$ (adjusted for parameter matching). For the 47M C4 baseline: $n_{\text{heads}} = 10$, $d_{\text{head}} = 41$, giving $d_{\text{model}} = 410$ (SwitchHead: $n_{\text{heads}} = 2$, $d_{\text{head}} = 76$, $d_{\text{model}} = 152$). Note that SwitchHead's $d_{\text{model}}$ is substantially smaller because it doesn't need to split the representation across many heads — this smaller model dimension reduces the cost of all other operations in the Transformer (MLP layers, layer norm, etc.), contributing to the overall resource savings beyond just the attention layer.

**Training hyperparameters.** All models use Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.00025, batch size 64, gradient clipping with maximum norm $\kappa$ (0.1 for small models, 0.25 for large models), and learning rate warmup of 4000 steps for large models. Dropout is applied to MLP layers: 0.1 for small models, 0.2 for large ones. Transformer XL models use a single additional chunk of context ($C = 2$ in the resource formulas), both during training and validation. The RoPE-based models (Appendix A.4) use the same training configuration but with square attention matrices (no XL cache).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Unit of Expert Selection in Attention Should Be Projections, Not Heads

The paper's most consequential conceptual move is to reject the natural default — making attention *heads* the unit of expert selection — and instead apply MoE at the finer granularity of individual *projection matrices*. This is not an obvious design choice, and virtually all prior work on MoE attention (MoA [Zhang et al., 2022], the h−1 mixture of Peng et al. [2020]) took the head-as-expert approach as self-evident. After all, MoE for MLPs makes the entire feedforward block the expert; the analogy for attention would be to make each head an expert. SwitchHead abandons this analogy.

The significance of this shift becomes clear when you examine *why* the head-as-expert approach limits resource savings. When an entire head is the expert, activating that expert requires computing its attention matrix — because each head has its own queries and keys that determine where it attends. The $O(T^2)$ attention matrix cost is multiplied by the number of active experts. This structural coupling between expert selection and attention computation is what forced MoA to use many active heads (4–8 in the paper's experiments, Table 1) to match dense performance, capping the achievable savings. MoA with 4 active heads still computes 4 attention matrices per layer; the savings only come from the heads it *doesn't* select, and Table 1 shows it doesn't even match the dense baseline until it uses 8 active heads at the 262M scale — at which point the savings are minimal.

SwitchHead's insight is that the diversity provided by multiple heads — what makes them necessary for good performance — can be factored into two independent dimensions: *what* information is extracted from the attended tokens (values) and *how* that information is used to update the current token (outputs). Both can be made context-dependent through MoE without affecting the attention pattern, which is determined by a single shared key and query per head. The attention pattern itself — which tokens attend to which — appears to require far less diversity than the projections that process the attended information. This is an empirical claim, validated by the fact that SwitchHead can reduce from 16 heads to 2 heads on the 262M Wikitext-103 model (Table 2: 9.77 vs. 9.80 perplexity) while MoA needs 8 active heads just to get close (9.50 perplexity, Table 1). The structural consequence — that the number of attention matrices scales with $n_{\text{heads}}$ rather than $n_{\text{heads}} \times k$ — delivers an 8× reduction in the dominant $O(T^2)$ cost that MoA structurally cannot achieve.

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental improvement. It changes the question from "which heads should be active?" to "how can we diversify the projections without diversifying the attention patterns?" The answer — independent source-side and destination-side routing with shared key-query pairs — is not an obvious extension of prior MoE work; it requires recognizing that the expensive part of attention (the matrix) and the expressive part of attention (the projections) can be decoupled.

---

### Innovation 2: Non-Competitive Activation Eliminates the Need for MoE Regularization Without Sacrificing Performance

MoE training has been plagued by a persistent practical problem: expert collapse, where the router learns to always select the same few experts, wasting the parameters of the rest. The standard solution, used in virtually all large-scale MoE systems from Shazeer et al. [2017] through Switch Transformers [Fedus et al., 2022] and MoA [Zhang et al., 2022], is to add auxiliary load-balancing losses that penalize uneven expert utilization. MoA requires *three separate regularizers* (Section 5). These losses add hyperparameters that must be tuned, introduce training objectives that compete with the primary language modeling loss, and often fail to completely prevent collapse in practice.

SwitchHead demonstrates that this entire edifice of regularization is unnecessary if the selection function is non-competitive. The paper inherits the sigmoid-based routing from σ-MoE [Csordás et al., 2023] — where it was validated only for MLP layers — and shows it transfers cleanly to attention without modification. The innovation is not the sigmoid itself (which is trivial) but the empirical demonstration that **competitive pressure between experts, not sparsity or discrete selection, is the root cause of collapse in MoE training**. This is a diagnostic insight with implications beyond attention MoEs.

Why does this matter beyond just "one less hyperparameter to tune"? Because it changes how we think about expert specialization. In softmax-based routing, experts are forced into a zero-sum competition: for each token, one expert's gain is another's loss. This creates a training dynamic where small initial advantages compound — an expert that is slightly more useful early in training receives more gradient, becomes more useful, and eventually crowds out others. The load-balancing loss is a hack that fights this dynamic with an opposing force, creating an unstable equilibrium that requires careful tuning.

Sigmoid routing eliminates the competition entirely. Each expert's activation is an independent function of the input, producing a score in (0,1) that doesn't depend on other experts' scores. The top-$k$ selection then provides sparsity without competition — being selected requires only that your score is among the top $k$, not that you "beat" other experts in a relative sense. The paper's empirical evidence that this works without any auxiliary loss (no load-balancing terms are mentioned anywhere in the method or appendix) and without expert collapse (Section 4 shows experts develop interpretable specializations on ListOps — value experts specialize by operation type, output experts distinguish numbers from delimiters) provides strong support for this diagnosis.

This is an **incremental refinement** of the σ-MoE approach (extending it from MLPs to attention), but the conceptual contribution — identifying competitive pressure as the specific mechanism causing collapse, rather than sparsity or discreteness — is novel and practically important. It means future MoE designs for any Transformer component can potentially drop load-balancing losses if they adopt non-competitive selection, dramatically simplifying training.

---

### Innovation 3: Parameter-Matched Evaluation Reveals That Not All Projections Benefit from MoE — Output Projections Matter Most

The paper's exhaustive ablation of which projections to make MoE (Table 6, Section 3.1) produces a finding that is both counterintuitive and practically crucial: **making the key and query projections MoE actively hurts performance**, while making the value and output projections MoE is sufficient to match or exceed dense baselines. The best configuration (V=Y, K=N, Q=N, O=Y: 12.27 perplexity) beats the 10-head dense baseline (12.31), while the worst (V=N, K=Y, Q=Y, O=N: 12.90) is dramatically worse than even a 2-head dense model (12.74).

This result is significant because it contradicts the natural intuition that *more MoE is better*. A reasonable prior would be: if MoE is good for attention, let's apply it everywhere — keys, queries, values, and outputs. Prior work (MoA, Peng et al.) didn't systematically investigate this question because their design (head-level routing) coupled all projections together — you couldn't independently decide which projections to make conditional.

The paper's explanation — parameter competition in the matched setting — is equally important. Every parameter allocated to a key or query expert is a parameter *not* available for increasing $d_{\text{head}}$, $d_{\text{ff}}$, or other model dimensions. Since the keys and queries only determine the attention pattern — which the paper's results suggest is highly redundant across heads — the model gets more value from having richer value and output representations. The paper notes (Appendix A.3) that this effect is specific to the parameter-matched regime: "allowing the parameter budget to increase, more experts always help." In a FLOPs-matched or unlimited-parameter setting, key and query MoEs might be beneficial. This boundary condition — that the optimal MoE configuration depends on the evaluation regime — is an important clarification for practitioners choosing between parameter-matched and FLOPs-matched deployment.

This is primarily a **practical diagnostic finding** rather than a theoretical advance, but its implications are substantial: it tells future MoE-attention designers exactly where to spend their expert budget for maximum return, and it provides a template for similar ablations in other architectures (e.g., should MoE be applied to the projection layers in cross-attention? To the gates in GLU variants?).

---

### Innovation 4: Attention Head Redundancy Is in the Projections, Not the Attention Patterns

The paper's analysis in Section 4 provides a provocative hypothesis about *why* Transformers need many heads and *where* the redundancy lies. By visualizing attention maps from both a dense 8-head Transformer and a parameter-matched 2-head SwitchHead on the ListOps synthetic task (Figures 2–5), the paper shows that the maximum-over-heads attention maps are **qualitatively similar** between the two models. Both models learn comparable patterns (attending to numerical arguments of operations, tracking list structure). The SwitchHead achieves this with 2 attention matrices; the dense model needs 8.

This suggests that the diversity provided by multiple heads is not primarily in *where* the model attends (the attention patterns), but in *what the model does with the attended information* (the value and output projections). If attention patterns were the source of diversity, reducing from 8 to 2 heads would lose expressive power that couldn't be recovered by making the projections richer. The fact that it *can* be recovered implies the attention patterns are substantially redundant — many heads are computing similar or identical attention maps and only differ in their projections.

The paper also identifies induction heads [Olsson et al., 2022] in both SwitchHead and the dense Transformer (Figure 6a–b), and notes that typical vertical-line attention patterns appear in both (Figure 6c–d). This is evidence that SwitchHead doesn't lose the ability to form the specific attention patterns known to be important for in-context learning — it just doesn't need separate heads to form them multiple times.

This insight has implications beyond resource savings. It suggests that **future Transformer architectures could potentially use even fewer attention matrices than current models** if they invest the saved parameters in more sophisticated value and output computations — for example, deeper per-token processing, larger value dimensions, or more complex output mixing. It also provides a partial answer to the paper's opening question ("why do Transformers need so many heads?"): the heads provide diversity in the *processing* of attended information, not in the attention patterns themselves. The attention patterns can be shared.

This is a **conceptual contribution** supported by qualitative evidence (attention map visualizations) rather than quantitative metrics. It is the most speculative of the paper's claims — two models trained from different initializations will never have identical attention maps, so the similarity argument relies on visual inspection — but it provides a testable hypothesis that future work could investigate more rigorously (e.g., by measuring attention pattern similarity across heads in trained Transformers, or by constraining different heads to share attention patterns and measuring the performance impact).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses four language modeling datasets: **C4** [Raffel et al., 2020] (a large web-crawled corpus), **Wikitext-103** [Merity et al., 2017] (a smaller, curated Wikipedia-based corpus), **peS2o** [Soldaini and Lo, 2023] (academic papers from S2ORC), and **Enwik8** [Hutter, 2006] (a character-level compression benchmark). For C4 and peS2o — which are large enough to exceed the training budget — the models are trained on the first $10^5 \times T \times N_{\text{batch}}$ tokens. For Wikitext-103 and Enwik8, the full training set is used. All subword-level datasets use a SentencePiece tokenizer with an 8k vocabulary. The test sets used for perplexity evaluation are the standard held-out splits for each dataset. For zero-shot downstream evaluation, the paper uses **Lambada** [Paperno et al., 2016] (word prediction requiring broad context), **BLiMP** [Warstadt et al., 2020] (linguistic minimal pairs probing grammatical knowledge), and **Children's Book Test (CBT)** [Hill et al., 2016] (cloze-style completion from children's books).

- **Base model(s).** Two model scales are used: **47M parameters** (small) and **262M parameters** (large). The primary architecture is **Transformer XL** [Dai et al., 2019] with a context size twice the current chunk length, chosen because the authors "found them to consistently and significantly outperform RoPE-based baselines for a fixed amount of compute" (Appendix A.4). To demonstrate that SwitchHead is not tied to Transformer XL, the paper also reports results with standard **RoPE** [Su et al., 2021] positional encodings and square attention matrices (no XL cache) at 45M and 244M parameter scales. For Enwik8 (character-level), only the 41M scale is tested. The baseline models use 8–16 attention heads depending on the scale: 10 heads for 47M models, 16 heads for 262M models (with 2-head variants also reported as lower bounds). The choice of these model sizes reflects the authors' compute budget constraints, stated explicitly in Section 6: "We chose this experimental setting taking into account our compute-budget and confidence in our results which are consistent across various configurations."

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **perplexity** for word-level datasets (Wikitext-103, C4, peS2o) and **bits per character (bpc)** for Enwik8. For zero-shot downstream tasks, **accuracy** or **token-level accuracy** is reported depending on the task: Lambada uses exact-match word prediction accuracy, BLiMP reports accuracy on grammaticality judgments, and CBT reports accuracy on cloze-form completion. For all tables, models are sorted by the primary metric to make the rank ordering clear.

- **Baselines.** Five types of baselines are used across the paper's experiments:
  1. **Dense Transformer with many heads**: the parameter-matched architecture with the same head count as $n_{\text{heads}} \times E$ in SwitchHead (typically 10 or 16 heads). This is the primary comparison.
  2. **Dense Transformer with few heads**: same parameter count but with only 2 or 4 heads (matching SwitchHead's $n_{\text{heads}}$). This demonstrates the lower bound that SwitchHead must beat — naively reducing heads destroys performance.
  3. **MoA** [Zhang et al., 2022]: Mixture of Attention Heads, the most directly comparable prior work, using softmax-based competitive routing with three regularizers. Tested at various numbers of active heads (2, 4, 6, 8, 12).
  4. **SwitchAll baselines**: combining SwitchHead attention with σ-MoE MLP layers [Csordás et al., 2023], compared to dense Transformers with identical parameter counts.
  5. **MAC-matched SwitchHead**: a variant where $d_{\text{head}}$ and $n_{\text{heads}}$ are increased until the total MAC count matches the dense baseline, resulting in more total parameters. This probes how much SwitchHead benefits from additional capacity when allowed to match the baseline's compute rather than its parameter count.
  6. **Shared-selection SwitchHead**: a variant where source-side and destination-side expert selection is shared ($s^h_S = s^h_D$), reducing the number of sorting and top-$k$ operations at the cost of some expressivity.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in **multiply-accumulate operations (MACs)**, following the convention of Zhang et al. [2022]. The paper provides detailed per-layer formulas for MACs in Appendix A.2 (Equations 11–15), summing the cost of linear projections, attention matrix computations ($QK^\top$ and $AV$), and position encoding operations. For Transformer XL models with context size $CT$, the attention matrix is $T \times CT$ rather than square. Memory usage is reported in **number of floats** that must be stored for backpropagation, including attention matrices (before and after softmax), projection outputs, and position encodings. For wall-clock time measurements (Section 3.7), the paper reports milliseconds per training iteration and GPU RAM usage, measured on RTX 3090 (for 47M models) and V100 (for 262M models) with the same codebase and attention core implementation, varying only the projection mechanism. All compute and memory numbers in tables are **per attention layer, per sequence**; total resource usage scales linearly with the number of layers and batch size.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. The paper uses fixed train/validation/test splits as provided by each dataset. Perplexity is computed on the standard held-out test set. The parameter-matching procedure (matching total parameter count between SwitchHead and baselines) is done by adjusting $d_{\text{ff}}$ (and sometimes $d_{\text{head}}$) to compensate for the parameter difference, as described in Section 3. When this matching is imperfect (as in the SwitchAll models, where the σ-MoE kernel constrains expert sizes to multiples of 4), the actual parameter counts are reported (e.g., 259M for the large SwitchAll model vs. the 262M baseline). All models are trained for 100k batches with a batch size of 64, using the Adam optimizer with learning rate 0.00025 and gradient clipping.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### SwitchHead vs. Dense Transformers: Parameter-Matched Performance

Table 2 is the central result table. Across four datasets and two model scales, SwitchHead consistently **matches or slightly exceeds the perplexity of the many-head dense baseline while using substantially fewer resources**. The headline numbers for the 262M models on C4: SwitchHead achieves 16.23 perplexity using 2.4G MACs and 5.6M floats of memory, compared to the 16-head baseline's 16.28 perplexity using 5.4G MACs and 21M floats — a 56% reduction in compute and 73% reduction in memory with equivalent (actually slightly better) performance. For Wikitext-103 at the same scale: SwitchHead achieves 9.77 perplexity (2.0G MACs, 2.9M memory) vs. the 16-head baseline's 9.80 perplexity (5.4G MACs, 21M memory).

The contrast with the **few-head dense baseline** is equally important because it demonstrates that the head reduction alone does not explain the success. For the 262M C4 model: the 2-head dense Transformer achieves 17.09 perplexity — almost a full point worse than SwitchHead's four-head model (16.23) and 0.81 worse than the 16-head baseline (16.28). This gap exists despite the dense 2-head model having the same parameter count (achieved by increasing $d_{\text{head}}$). For the 262M Wikitext-103 model, the 2-head dense baseline achieves 10.09 perplexity vs. SwitchHead's 9.77 — a 0.32 gap. These results directly support the paper's core claim: the many projections provided by experts, not the many attention patterns provided by heads, are what make multi-head attention effective. Simply increasing $d_{\text{head}}$ to compensate for fewer heads does not recover the lost performance; context-dependent expert mixing does.

On the smaller scale (47M), the pattern holds: C4 22.53 (SwitchHead, 2 heads, 203M MACs) vs. 22.71 (10-head baseline, 453M MACs) vs. 23.71 (2-head dense baseline, 453M MACs). Wikitext-103: 12.31 (SwitchHead, 170M MACs) vs. 12.32 (10-head baseline, 453M MACs) vs. 12.73 (2-head dense baseline, 453M MACs). Note that the 2-head dense baseline *uses the same MACs as the 10-head baseline* in the 47M models — this is because $d_{\text{head}}$ is increased to match parameters, but MACs depend on $T d_{\text{head}} d_{\text{model}}$, which grows with $d_{\text{head}}$ even though $n_{\text{heads}}$ decreases. SwitchHead achieves lower MACs through a smaller $d_{\text{model}}$ (because $d_{\text{model}} = n_{\text{heads}} \times d_{\text{head}}$ for dense models but can be set independently for SwitchHead).

For peS2o at 47M parameters, SwitchHead (12.84 perplexity) essentially ties the 10-head baseline (12.83) while using 203M vs. 453M MACs. At 262M, it lags very slightly: 9.86 vs. 9.78. This is the only case where SwitchHead doesn't match or beat the baseline in Table 2, and the gap is small (0.08 perplexity for a 55% compute reduction). In the SwitchAll configuration (Table 3), peS2o at 262M with SwitchAll achieves 9.86 — matching the dense baseline exactly.

For the character-level Enwik8 dataset at 41M parameters, SwitchHead achieves 1.10 bits/character — identical to the 8-head dense baseline — using 709M MACs and 2.8M floats vs. the baseline's 1.6G MACs and 10M floats. The 2-head dense baseline achieves 1.13 bpc, confirming again that fewer heads with larger dimension underperforms.

**Key observation across all datasets:** The resource savings are most dramatic in memory (2.8–5.6M floats vs. 10–21M floats), which is dominated by the $2CT^2$ term for storing attention matrices. Reducing $n_{\text{heads}}$ from 10–16 to 2–4 directly reduces this term by a factor of 3–8. The compute savings are also substantial (44–56% of baseline MACs) but are partially offset by the MoE projection overhead ($2T k d_{\text{head}}(d_{\text{model}} + 1)$) and the larger $d_{\text{head}}$ per head.

#### Comparison with MoA: Efficiency Gap at Equivalent Performance

Table 1 provides the direct comparison between SwitchHead and MoA, tested on Wikitext-103 at both scales. The central finding is that **MoA can approach or slightly exceed SwitchHead's perplexity, but only by using substantially more resources**, and at lower resource budgets, MoA significantly underperforms.

For the 47M model: SwitchHead (2 heads) achieves 12.27 perplexity with 170.4M MACs and 0.8M memory. MoA with 4 active heads achieves 12.60 perplexity (worse) with 223.5M MACs and 1.3M memory (more of both). MoA with 8 active heads achieves 12.77 perplexity (still worse than SwitchHead) with 390.2M MACs and 2.6M memory — more than double SwitchHead's compute. MoA with 2 active heads achieves 12.84 perplexity with 140.1M MACs and 0.7M memory — lower resources than SwitchHead but with substantially worse perplexity (+0.57 over SwitchHead). This creates a clear Pareto frontier where SwitchHead dominates: at any given resource budget, SwitchHead achieves better perplexity than MoA, and at any given perplexity target, SwitchHead uses fewer resources.

For the 262M model: MoA with 8 active heads achieves the best perplexity (9.50) — slightly better than SwitchHead's 9.55 — but uses 2.9G MACs and 9.9M memory, compared to SwitchHead's 2.0G MACs and 2.9M memory. This is a 45% compute premium and a 3.4× memory premium for a 0.05 perplexity improvement. MoA with 4 active heads (9.69 perplexity, 1.7G MACs, 5.1M memory) uses less compute than SwitchHead but substantially more memory (1.76×) with worse perplexity (+0.14). MoA with 2 active heads (9.87 perplexity) falls well below both the SwitchHead and the dense baseline.

The structural reason for MoA's disadvantage is visible in the resource numbers: MoA's memory scales with $n_{\text{heads}}$ (number of active experts) times $CT^2$ because each active head requires its own attention matrix. SwitchHead's memory scales with $n_{\text{heads}}$ (number of computed attention matrices, 2) regardless of $k$ (active experts per head, up to 4 in these experiments). When MoA reduces active heads to 2 (matching SwitchHead's $n_{\text{heads}}$), its performance collapses because it doesn't have the expert diversity in value/output projections that SwitchHead provides.

#### Zero-Shot Downstream Performance

Table 4 reports zero-shot transfer from C4-trained models to Lambada, BLiMP, and CBT. The 47M SwitchHead achieves 20.4% on Lambada (tied with the dense Transformer's 20.4%), 75.7% on BLiMP (vs. 73.6% for dense, a +2.1% absolute improvement), on CBT no result is reported for 47M models. The 262M SwitchHead achieves 29.4% on Lambada (vs. 28.2% for dense), 79.6% on BLiMP (vs. 76.1%, a +3.5% absolute improvement), and 83.3% on CBT (vs. 83.6%, essentially tied). The BLiMP improvement is the most notable: SwitchHead appears to better preserve or learn grammatical knowledge compared to the dense baseline, possibly because the output expert specialization allows more targeted processing of grammatical structures. The performance is consistent across tasks — SwitchHead never substantially underperforms the baseline and sometimes notably outperforms it.

The **MAC-matched models** (where compute, not parameters, is matched to the dense baseline) show the expected improvement from their additional parameters: 47M MAC-matched achieves 21.18 perplexity (vs. 22.71 baseline), 23.5% Lambada (+3.1% over baseline), 77.1% BLiMP (+3.5% over baseline). The 262M MAC-matched achieves 15.43 perplexity, 30.2% Lambada, 79.4% BLiMP, 84.2% CBT — substantial improvements across the board. These results demonstrate that SwitchHead scales effectively with additional compute budget without hitting the over-optimization walls that limit some test-time compute methods.

The **shared selection variant** (single router for both source and destination) shows small performance degradation: 47M achieves 22.81 perplexity (vs. 22.53 for full SwitchHead), 20.0% Lambada (vs. 20.4%), 74.6% BLiMP (vs. 75.7%). At 262M: 16.49 perplexity (vs. 16.23), 28.6% Lambada (vs. 29.4%), 79.4% BLiMP (vs. 79.6%), 82.7% CBT (vs. 83.3%). The degradation is consistently small, making shared selection a viable option when the additional acceleration from reduced routing operations is important.

#### SwitchAll: Combining Attention MoE with MLP MoE

Table 3 demonstrates that SwitchHead can be combined with σ-MoE MLP layers [Csordás et al., 2023] without negative interactions. The SwitchAll models use the same σ-MoE hyperparameters as Csordás et al. [2023] and the same SwitchHead configurations from Table 2.

For the 47M Wikitext-103 model, SwitchAll achieves 12.17 perplexity — better than both the baseline (12.32) and SwitchHead alone (12.31). For the 262M Wikitext-103 model, SwitchAll achieves 9.81 — essentially tied with the baseline (9.80) and marginally behind SwitchHead alone (9.77), but note that SwitchAll here has 259M parameters vs. 262M for the baseline due to imperfect parameter matching (the σ-MoE kernel constrains expert sizes to multiples of 4, making exact matching impossible). For C4, SwitchAll at 47M achieves 22.09 (vs. 22.63 baseline), and at 262M achieves 16.45 (vs. 16.58 baseline) — both improvements, though the 262M model is 259M actual parameters. For peS2o at 47M, SwitchAll achieves 12.56 (vs. 12.83 baseline); at 262M, 9.86 (vs. 9.78 baseline, a slight underperformance).

**The key finding** is the absence of negative interaction: the combination of attention MoE and MLP MoE does not degrade performance beyond what either method does individually. The slight fluctuations (sometimes SwitchAll outperforms the baseline, sometimes it's slightly worse) are within the range expected from imperfect parameter matching and training variance. This is practically important because it means practitioners can independently apply σ-MoE to MLPs and SwitchHead to attention without worrying about compatibility.

#### RoPE-Based Models: Generality Beyond Transformer XL

Table 7 (Appendix A.4) replicates the main findings using standard RoPE positional encodings with square attention matrices (no XL cache). The pattern is consistent with the Transformer XL results. For Wikitext-103 at 45M parameters: SwitchHead with 2 heads achieves 12.75 perplexity vs. 12.78 for the 10-head dense baseline (essentially tied) using 285.6M MACs and 1.3M memory vs. 560.9M MACs and 6.1M memory. The 2-head dense baseline achieves 12.96 perplexity — confirming again that fewer heads alone degrades performance. At 244M parameters: SwitchHead with 4 heads achieves 10.00 perplexity vs. 10.17 for the 16-head baseline (a small improvement for SwitchHead) using 4.2G MACs and 18.4M memory vs. 6.4G MACs and 37.7M memory. For C4 at 45M: SwitchHead achieves 23.69 vs. 23.79 baseline. At 244M: 16.41 vs. 16.35 — essentially tied.

Table 8 shows zero-shot performance for the RoPE models trained on C4. The 45M SwitchHead achieves 23.69 perplexity, 20.9% Lambada (vs. 23.76 and 20.3% baseline), 77.3% BLiMP (vs. 73.8%). The 243M SwitchHead at 16.41 perplexity / 30.5% Lambada / 79.9% BLiMP / 83.8% CBT compared to the baseline at 16.35 / 29.8% / 76.1% / 83.9%. The consistent BLiMP improvement (3.5% for 45M, 3.8% for 243M) suggests this is not an artifact of the Transformer XL architecture.

The MAC-matched RoPE models again show substantial gains: 54M model achieves 22.18 perplexity, 22.6% Lambada, 77.4% BLiMP. The 314M model achieves 15.63 perplexity, 30.5% Lambada, 80.5% BLiMP, 84.6% CBT — all improvements over their parameter-matched counterparts.

#### Wall-Clock Time Measurements

Table 5 provides real-world training speed and memory measurements for the full pipeline (including MLP layers, optimizer, and multi-GPU gradient synchronization). For the 47M model on a single RTX 3090: the dense Transformer trains at 473ms/iteration and uses 20.5GB of GPU RAM. SwitchHead trains at 342ms/iteration (0.72× baseline time, a 28% speedup) and uses 13.5GB (0.65× baseline memory). MoA is also measured for reference: 412ms/iteration (0.87×) and 15.3GB (0.75×). SwitchHead is faster than MoA (342 vs. 412ms/iter) and uses less memory (13.5 vs. 15.3GB).

For the 262M model on 8 V100 GPUs: the dense Transformer trains at 670ms/iteration and uses 20.5GB per GPU. SwitchHead trains at 442ms/iteration (0.65× baseline, a 35% speedup) and uses 12.5GB (0.61× baseline memory). MoA at this scale (with 8 active heads, the configuration needed to approach baseline perplexity per Table 1) trains at 851ms/iteration — actually 27% *slower* than the dense baseline — and uses 16.4GB (0.80× baseline memory). SwitchHead is nearly 2× faster than MoA (442 vs. 851ms) and uses 24% less memory.

The paper notes that the Triton kernel used for SwitchHead's MoE projections "is currently around 60% of the speed of a single dense matrix multiplication of the size of a single expert with cuBLAS" (Section 6), suggesting that further optimization could increase the speedup. At 60% efficiency, if the kernel reached 80–90% of cuBLAS speed (which the paper estimates as achievable), the wall-clock advantage would grow.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Which projections should be MoE?** (Table 6, Section 3.1): The exhaustive 16-configuration ablation on 47M Wikitext-103 models (2 heads, 5 experts per head) shows that **output projection MoE is necessary, value projection MoE provides additional benefit, and key/query MoE is harmful in the parameter-matched setting**. The best configuration (V=Y, K=N, Q=N, O=Y) achieves 12.27 perplexity (beating the 10-head baseline at 12.31). Configurations without output MoE never beat the baseline: the best O=N model is V=Y, K=N, Q=N, O=N at 12.45, substantially worse. Adding key or query MoE to an already-good configuration consistently degrades it: V=Y, K=N, Q=N, O=Y (12.27) → V=Y, K=Y, Q=N, O=Y (12.37, +0.10) → V=Y, K=N, Q=Y, O=Y (12.42, +0.15) → V=Y, K=Y, Q=Y, O=Y (12.57, +0.30). The monotonic degradation with each added key/query expert strongly supports the claim that these parameters are better spent elsewhere. The worst configuration (V=N, K=Y, Q=Y, O=N at 12.90) is dramatically worse than even the 2-head dense baseline (12.74), showing that key/query MoE without value/output MoE is actively damaging.

**Number of active experts per head ($k$)**: The hyperparameter selection procedure (Section 3) implicitly tests different $k$ values. For 47M models: Wikitext-103 works with $k=2$ (Table 9), while C4 and peS2o require $k=3$ to match the baseline — consistent with C4 being a larger, more diverse dataset requiring more expert diversity per token. For 262M models: Wikitext-103 needs $k=4$ (with $E=8$), while C4 and peS2o work with $k=2$ (with $E=4$). This non-monotonic relationship (larger datasets don't always need larger $k$) reflects the interaction between $E$ (total experts, determining specialization granularity) and $k$ (active experts, determining per-token combination flexibility). When $E$ is large (8 experts for 262M Wikitext-103), more need to be active ($k=4$) to capture sufficient diversity; when $E$ is smaller (4 experts for 262M C4), $k=2$ suffices.

**Number of attention heads ($n_{\text{heads}}$)**: The procedure also tests when to increase $n_{\text{heads}}$ from 2 to 4. For 262M models: Wikitext-103 succeeds with $n_{\text{heads}}=2, k=4$, but C4 and peS2o require $n_{\text{heads}}=4, k=2$. This suggests that C4 and peS2o benefit from having more independent attention patterns (4 heads) even if each head has fewer active experts (2), while Wikitext-103 benefits more from expert diversity within fewer attention patterns. The resource implications differ: $n_{\text{heads}}=4$ doubles the attention matrix memory (which is the dominant cost), while $k=4$ increases only the projection compute (which is relatively cheaper). The paper's procedure correctly identifies the more resource-efficient configuration first ($n_{\text{heads}}=2$ with higher $k$) and only falls back to more heads when necessary.

**Shared vs. separate source/destination selection**: Table 4 reports this ablation for both parameter-matched and MAC-matched models. At 47M: shared selection degrades perplexity from 22.53 to 22.81 (+0.28), Lambada from 20.4% to 20.0% (−0.4%), BLiMP from 75.7% to 74.6% (−1.1%). At 262M: perplexity degrades from 16.23 to 16.49 (+0.26), Lambada from 29.4% to 28.6% (−0.8%), BLiMP from 79.6% to 79.4% (−0.2%), CBT from 83.3% to 82.7% (−0.6%). The degradation is consistently present but small — typically 0.2–0.3 perplexity points — which may be acceptable in latency-constrained deployments where the reduction in routing operations provides a meaningful speedup. The fact that shared selection still substantially outperforms the 2-head dense baseline (which suffers from having no MoE at all) confirms that most of SwitchHead's benefit comes from the existence of multiple projections, not from the separation of source and destination routing.

**RoPE vs. Transformer XL**: Tables 7–8 demonstrate that SwitchHead's performance is not tied to the Transformer XL architecture. The RoPE-based models at both scales show the same pattern: SwitchHead matches or slightly beats the dense baseline perplexity while using substantially fewer resources. The BLiMP improvement transfers as well. This robustness check is important because Transformer XL uses a non-standard attention mechanism (attending to a context window that extends beyond the current chunk), and it would be concerning if SwitchHead only worked in this specific setting.

**MAC-matched scaling**: Tables 4 and 8 show that when SwitchHead is allowed to use more parameters to match the baseline's compute budget, performance improves substantially — suggesting SwitchHead is not operating at the limit of what the MoE attention mechanism can express. The 47M MAC-matched model (63M actual parameters) improves C4 perplexity from 22.53 to 21.18; the 262M MAC-matched model (376M actual parameters) improves from 16.23 to 15.43. This demonstrates that the parameter-matched configuration represents a point on a scaling curve, not a ceiling — with more compute, SwitchHead can productively use additional capacity.

**Interpretability analysis on ListOps** (Figures 2–5): While not a quantitative ablation, the paper's qualitative analysis on the ListOps synthetic task provides evidence that SwitchHead's attention maps are not degenerate. The maximum-over-heads attention maps for the dense 8-head Transformer (Figure 4) and the 2-head SwitchHead (Figure 3) are visually similar — both models learn to attend to the numerical arguments of list operations (MIN, MAX, MED) and track list structure. Individual SwitchHead heads (Figure 5) show interpretable behavior: "output experts specialize according to different operations, while the input [value] ones distinguish numbers and closed parentheses. The attention map itself appears to distribute information about contiguous chunks of numbers." The presence of induction heads in the 47M language model (Figure 6a) — a specific attention pattern known to be important for in-context learning [Olsson et al., 2022] — in both SwitchHead and the dense Transformer (Figure 6b) confirms that SwitchHead doesn't lose the ability to form these specialized patterns despite having far fewer attention matrices.

**Parameter count matching precision**: The SwitchAll models in Table 3 have slightly fewer parameters than the dense baselines (259M vs. 262M for the large models). This is not a problem for the paper's argument — if anything, it makes SwitchAll's competitive performance slightly *more* impressive — but it does mean the perplexity comparisons at 262M are not exactly parameter-matched. The 47M SwitchAll models are exactly matched.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does SwitchHead genuinely match dense Transformer performance with reduced resources?

**Largely supported, with important caveats about scale and dataset scope.** The evidence in Table 2 is comprehensive across four datasets and two model sizes: SwitchHead matches or slightly exceeds the dense baseline's perplexity in 7 out of 8 comparisons (the exception is peS2o at 262M, where SwitchHead trails by 0.08 perplexity — a negligible gap). The resource reductions are real and substantial: 44–56% of baseline MACs and 27–38% of baseline memory in the main configurations. The wall-clock measurements (Table 5) confirm these savings translate to training speed: 28–35% faster iterations with 35–39% less GPU memory.

However, **the claim is demonstrated only at modest model scales** (47M, 262M) on datasets that are either relatively small (Wikitext-103) or subsampled (C4, peS2o). The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation in Section 6: "Our models are modest in size compared to the current state-of-art LLMs." At billion-parameter scales, attention patterns may become more diverse (the redundancy hypothesis might break down), expert collapse might be harder to avoid (even with sigmoid routing), and the overhead of expert selection might become more significant relative to the attention matrix compute. The paper provides no evidence that the claimed resource reductions scale to larger models.

The parameter-matched setting itself deserves scrutiny. In this setting, SwitchHead's $d_{\text{model}}$ is typically smaller than the dense baseline's (e.g., $2 \times 76 = 152$ vs. $10 \times 41 = 410$ for the 47M C4 model from Table 9). This means SwitchHead's MLP layers, layer norms, and input/output embeddings operate in a lower-dimensional space, which contributes to the resource savings beyond what the attention mechanism alone provides. The paper's resource comparison is fair (it compares the full model, not just the attention layer), but it means the reported savings are not purely from the attention innovation — they also benefit from the smaller $d_{\text{model}}$. This is not a weakness (it's a legitimate advantage of the architecture), but it means that applying SwitchHead to an existing pretrained model (which has a fixed $d_{\text{model}}$) would not yield the same savings as training from scratch with a smaller $d_{\text{model}}$.

#### Does SwitchHead demonstrate that attention head redundancy is in the projections, not the patterns?

**Supported qualitatively but not quantitatively.** The paper's evidence for this claim consists of visual comparisons of attention maps (Figures 2–6) and the empirical finding that SwitchHead matches performance with far fewer attention matrices. The visual similarity is suggestive but subjective — two models with different random initializations may produce attention maps that look similar to humans but compute importantly different functions. The paper provides no quantitative metric of attention pattern similarity (e.g., centered kernel alignment, mutual information, head-to-head correlation), no analysis of whether SwitchHead's two attention patterns can be decomposed to recover the dense model's 8 or 16 attention patterns, and no ablation showing what happens if heads are forced to share attention patterns (which would directly test the redundancy hypothesis). The claim is a plausible interpretation of the results but should be treated as a hypothesis rather than an established finding.

#### Does the MAC-matched variant demonstrate that SwitchHead scales with compute?

**Supported but with an important asymmetry.** MAC-matched SwitchHead models improve performance over parameter-matched models (e.g., 47M C4: 21.18 vs. 22.53 perplexity; 262M C4: 15.43 vs. 16.23). However, the paper does not compare MAC-matched SwitchHead to MAC-matched dense models — the comparison is always to the parameter-matched dense baseline. A fair MAC-matched comparison would be: SwitchHead with $n_{\text{heads}}$ active and adjusted $d_{\text{head}}$ vs. a dense model with $n'_{\text{heads}}$ heads and the same total MACs. It's possible that a dense model with more heads and smaller $d_{\text{head}}$ would outperform the parameter-matched baseline using the same MACs as SwitchHead. The paper does not run this experiment, so the claim that "MAC-matched models outperform the others by a large margin" (Section 3.5) is relative to parameter-matched, not MAC-matched, baselines.

#### Does SwitchHead eliminate the need for MoE regularization?

**Strongly supported by the absence of any regularization in the method description.** The paper describes no load-balancing loss, no auxiliary training objectives, and no expert dropout or capacity factors. The sigmoid-based routing is the only selection mechanism. This contrasts sharply with MoA, which "requires three different regularizers" (Section 3.2). The fact that SwitchHead trains successfully across four datasets, two model sizes, and two positional encoding schemes without expert collapse is strong evidence that non-competitive activation solves the collapse problem. The ListOps analysis (Section 4) showing that experts develop interpretable specializations provides additional qualitative evidence that experts are being used diversely without explicit regularization.

However, the paper provides no quantitative analysis of expert utilization (e.g., the distribution of how often each expert is selected, the entropy of the selection distribution, or metrics that would detect "dead" experts). This is a notable omission. In standard MoE literature, expert utilization is a primary concern, and auxiliary losses are explicitly designed to balance loads. The paper's claim that collapse doesn't occur would be strengthened by showing utilization statistics, even if only to demonstrate that they're healthy.

#### Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper

- **Scaling to larger models (1B+ parameters)**: The most obvious missing experiment. The paper argues that the evidence "is enough for a research group with a larger amount of resources at their disposal to verify our findings" (Section 6), but this is an invitation, not a demonstration. The resource savings are most impactful at large scale, but the behavior of MoE routing at scale may differ.

- **Long-sequence experiments**: The paper notes that attention cost scales quadratically with sequence length, and the memory savings from fewer attention matrices should be most dramatic for long sequences. Yet all experiments use modest sequence lengths (256–1024 tokens, per Table 9). An experiment scaling sequence length and measuring the resource advantage would directly validate one of the paper's key motivational claims.

- **Quantitative attention pattern analysis**: Measuring the similarity of attention patterns between SwitchHead and dense models, or between different heads within each model, would strengthen the redundancy claim. The paper's qualitative analysis is suggestive but leaves the central hypothesis untested.

- **Inference-only measurements**: All wall-clock measurements are for training. Inference is often more latency-constrained, and the expert selection overhead (sigmoid + top-$k$ per head per token) might be more significant relative to forward-pass compute than to training-time forward+backward compute. Inference-only benchmarks would be valuable for practitioners.

- **Direct comparison with MAC-matched dense baselines**: As noted above, the MAC-matched SwitchHead models are compared only to parameter-matched baselines. A fairer comparison would match total MACs between SwitchHead and a dense model with adjusted architecture.

- **Expert utilization statistics**: The paper claims no collapse without regularization but provides no quantitative evidence that experts are used uniformly or diversely. Entropy of selection, frequency histograms, or per-token expert diversity metrics would substantiate this claim.

- **Training from different initializations**: All results are from single training runs. Given the standard deviation of language model training (even with fixed hyperparameters, different random seeds can produce meaningfully different perplexities), the consistent but small gaps between SwitchHead and baselines (often 0.01–0.05 perplexity) could be within training noise. Multiple seeds or standard deviations would clarify whether SwitchHead genuinely matches or underperforms.

#### Where the paper's claims hold conditionally

The central claim — "SwitchHead matches dense Transformer performance with reduced compute and memory" — holds clearly at the 47M–262M scale on the tested datasets, with the architecture variants and training configurations described. The resource savings are quantified precisely and are substantial (4× memory reduction, ~1.5–2× compute/speed improvement). The generality claim (works with both Transformer XL and RoPE, works with MoE MLPs) is well-supported.

The claim does *not* extend to scales beyond what was tested, to sequence lengths beyond 512–1024 tokens, to inference-only scenarios (where latency, not throughput, is the metric), or to settings where exact parameter matching is impossible (e.g., applying SwitchHead as a drop-in replacement to an existing pretrained model with fixed $d_{\text{model}}$). The paper is appropriately circumspect about these limitations in Section 6, but readers should be careful not to over-interpret the efficiency claims as universally applicable.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Scale of Empirical Validation: Modest Model Sizes Leave Large-Scale Behavior Unverified

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments are conducted at 47M and 262M parameters on datasets that are either intrinsically small (Wikitext-103, Enwik8) or subsampled (C4, peS2o — trained on only the first `10^5 × T × N_batch` tokens). The paper is explicit about this in Section 6:

> "Our models are modest in size compared to the current state-of-art LLMs. However, training such models is estimated to cost millions of dollars, which we cannot afford."

The authors frame this as a practical constraint of their compute budget and argue that the evidence provided is "enough for a research group with a larger amount of resources at their disposal to verify our findings in a state-of-the-art model." This is an invitation, not a demonstration — the paper provides no experimental evidence at the 1B+ parameter scale where MoE methods are most commonly deployed and where the resource savings would be most impactful.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of SwitchHead's behavior could change qualitatively at larger scales. The central hypothesis — that attention patterns are redundant and can be shared across many value/output experts — was validated at scales where models have 10–16 heads. Modern LLMs routinely use 32–96 heads per layer (GPT-3 uses 96; LLaMA-65B uses 64). It is unknown whether the redundancy holds at these scales or whether attention patterns become more diverse and specialized, requiring more independent heads than SwitchHead's 2–4. If the redundancy hypothesis breaks down, SwitchHead would need to increase `n_heads`, eroding the resource savings.

Expert utilization could also change at scale. The paper provides no quantitative evidence of expert balance even at the tested scales (no selection entropy, load histograms, or frequency-of-use statistics). At billion-parameter scales, sigmoid-based routing without load-balancing losses might exhibit different dynamics — perhaps experts slowly drift toward uniformity, or perhaps subtle forms of collapse emerge that are invisible at 47M–262M parameters. The σ-MoE paper [Csordás et al., 2023] was also validated only at modest scales (up to 500M parameters), so the non-competitive activation approach to preventing collapse has no track record at large scale for any Transformer component.

The interaction between SwitchHead's expert selection overhead and model-parallel training is also unexamined. Section 6 notes:

> "Model-parallel training requires the implementation of a load-balancing system that can dynamically move experts between GPUs."

Without such a system, the expert parameters (which are head-specific and numerous when `n_heads × E` is large) could not be distributed across devices, making large-scale training infeasible. The paper's experiments all fit on 1–8 GPUs; scaling to hundreds of GPUs would require infrastructure that does not currently exist for this architecture.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The scale limitation is acknowledged but not measured. All experiments in Tables 1–5 are at 47M and 262M parameters. The largest model has 18 layers with `d_model = 448` (262M C4 model: `n_heads = 4`, `d_head = 112`, per Table 9). The paper does not include any scaling trend analysis that would predict behavior at larger sizes — no experiment varying model size while measuring the resource savings ratio, no extrapolation of the perplexity gap between SwitchHead and the dense baseline as a function of scale, no study of how optimal `n_heads`, `k`, and `E` change with total parameter count.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation experimentally. It acknowledges it candidly (Section 6) and defers verification to future work by better-resourced groups. The diversity of tested configurations (four datasets, two positional encoding schemes, two model sizes) provides some evidence that the method is not brittle to these variables, but this does not substitute for scale. The paper also notes that "we estimate that 80-90% should be achievable with a more optimal kernel" for the Triton-based MoE implementation, suggesting that some of the practical barriers to large-scale training are engineering problems rather than fundamental limitations — but this remains speculative without a demonstrated implementation.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For in the Headline Resource Savings

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's resource accounting — the MAC counts in Equations 11–13, the memory formulas, and the wall-clock measurements in Table 5 — compares SwitchHead's attention layer cost to the dense baseline's attention layer cost. However, this accounting excludes a critical cost that does not exist in the dense baseline: the computation required for expert selection itself. For each head `h`, the source-side router computes `s^h_S = σ(x W^h_S)` and performs `arg top_k(s^h_S, k)`, and the destination-side router does the same with `W^h_D`. These operations require:

- Two matrix multiplications per head: `x W^h_S` and `x W^h_D`, each costing `T d_model E` MACs. With `n_heads` heads, the total routing projection cost is `2 n_heads T d_model E` MACs.
- Sigmoid activation on a `T × E` tensor per head (cheap but non-zero, at roughly `n_heads T E` FLOPs).
- Top-k selection on a `T × E` tensor per head, which requires sorting or partial-sorting operations. The paper's Triton kernel must implement this efficiently, and the paper acknowledges it is "currently around 60% of the speed of a single dense matrix multiplication of the size of a single expert with cuBLAS" (Section 6) — meaning the expert selection is not only additional compute but is currently *less efficient per FLOP* than the dense operations it replaces.

The paper's MAC formulas (Equations 11–13) do include the projection cost `2 T k d_head (d_model + 1)` for the MoE value and output projections, accounting for the weighted averaging of expert outputs. But the routing projections `x W^h_S` and `x W^h_D` are not included in any reported MAC count. The paper states in Appendix A.2: "the expert selection logic needs minimal additional resources, which can be ignored." Whether this is truly negligible depends on the ratio of `E` (experts per head) to `d_head` (head dimension). For the 262M Wikitext-103 model from Table 9: `n_heads = 2`, `d_head = 132`, `E = 8`, `d_model = 2 × 132 = 264`. The routing projection cost is `2 × 2 × T × 264 × 8 = 8,448 T` MACs per layer, compared to the attention matrix cost of `2 × 2 × 2 × T^2 × 132 ≈ 1,056 T^2` (with `C=2`). For the sequence length `T=512` used in this model, the routing cost is roughly 4.3M MACs versus 277M MACs for the attention matrix — about 1.5% overhead. This is indeed small for this configuration, but for models with larger `E` or shorter sequences (where the `T^2` attention cost is smaller relative to the linear projection costs), the overhead could become more significant.

**The consequence.** The reported resource savings (SwitchHead uses 44% of baseline compute, 27% of baseline memory) slightly overstate the actual advantage by omitting a cost that the dense baseline does not incur. For the configurations used in the paper, the overhead is genuinely small (likely 1–3% of total attention layer MACs), so the overstatement is minor. However, this omission matters for two reasons:

1. **It obscures a scaling trend.** As `E` (the number of experts per head) increases, the routing cost grows linearly with `E` while the attention cost grows with `d_head`. If future work attempts to scale SwitchHead to hundreds of experts per head (to further increase projection diversity without increasing `n_heads`), the routing cost could become non-negligible. The paper provides no analysis of this tradeoff.

2. **The implementation efficiency matters.** The paper notes the Triton kernel is at 60% of cuBLAS efficiency. If the routing operations are even less optimized (sorting and top-k on GPU are notoriously difficult to make fast, especially for small `k`), the wall-clock overhead could be larger than the MAC count suggests. Table 5 shows SwitchHead trains 1.4–1.5× faster than the baseline, confirming the overhead is manageable in the current implementation, but this is at scales where the attention matrix computation still dominates. For very short sequences or inference with key-value caching (where the attention matrix is small or precomputed), the routing overhead would represent a larger fraction of the total cost.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly. The paper does not report MAC counts for the routing projections, does not measure the wall-clock time of the expert selection in isolation, and does not provide an ablation that quantifies the overhead as a function of `E` or `n_heads`. The wall-clock measurements in Table 5 include the full pipeline (MLP layers, optimizer, gradient sync), making it impossible to isolate the attention-layer-specific overhead. The statement that routing resources "can be ignored" is asserted without evidence.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It provides no analysis of routing overhead, no ablation measuring its cost, and no recommendation for practitioners on when `E` becomes large enough that the overhead matters. The shared selection variant (Section 3.6, Table 4) reduces the number of routing operations by half (one router instead of two), which the paper frames as a speedup option — but this is about reducing the existing overhead, not about measuring it. A simple experiment measuring perplexity vs. wall-clock time for varying `E` at fixed `k` and `n_heads` would clarify the overhead tradeoff but is not included.

### Expert Specialization and Utilization Are Not Quantitatively Characterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims that SwitchHead "does not require regularization or extra tricks for stable training" (Abstract) and that the sigmoid-based non-competitive activation prevents expert collapse. This claim is central to the method's practical appeal: unlike MoA (which needs three regularizers) and most other MoE systems (which need load-balancing losses), SwitchHead should be simpler to train and tune. However, the paper provides no quantitative evidence that experts are actually being used diversely or that collapse does not occur — only qualitative visualizations on a synthetic task (ListOps, Section 4) showing that "output experts specialize according to different operations, while the input ones distinguish numbers and closed parentheses."

**The consequence.** Without quantitative utilization statistics, several failure modes cannot be ruled out:

- **Silent expert collapse**: some of the `E` experts per head might never (or very rarely) be selected, effectively wasting their parameters. The model would still train and might still match baseline perplexity (the remaining experts could compensate), but the parameter efficiency would be worse than reported — the effective number of utilized experts would be less than `E`, meaning the model is not actually getting the full benefit of its parameter budget.

- **Token-position correlation**: sigmoid routing could learn to select the same experts for all tokens (or for all tokens of a certain type), producing a de facto fixed projection that doesn't actually adapt to context. The ListOps visualizations suggest this doesn't happen (experts specialize by token type), but language modeling is far more complex, and the paper provides no evidence either way.

- **Layer-dependent utilization**: different layers might use experts very differently. Early layers might collapse to using a single expert while later layers distribute activations evenly. The aggregate perplexity metric would not reveal this, and the optimal configuration (`E`, `k`, `n_heads`) might vary per layer — a dimension the paper does not explore.

Standard MoE papers report the coefficient of variation of expert usage, the fraction of tokens routed to each expert, entropy of the selection distribution, or similar metrics. The absence of any such analysis in this paper is a significant gap — it means the claim of "no collapse" is supported only by the absence of training failure, not by positive evidence of healthy expert diversity.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Only qualitative: Figure 5 in the appendix shows, for a 6-layer, 2-head SwitchHead trained on ListOps, the per-head attention maps with source-side and destination-side expert selection scores visualized alongside. The captions note that "dark blue always corresponds to 1, while white is 0" for the selection maps, suggesting that experts are being selected with varying weights across tokens and that the pattern is not uniform. This is suggestive but applies only to a tiny synthetic model on a simple algorithmic task with a vocabulary of 15 tokens (brackets, operations, digits). The 47M Wikitext-103 model in Figure 6 shows attention patterns but does not show expert selection weights.

No quantitative metrics appear anywhere in the paper: no histograms of expert selection frequency, no per-layer utilization statistics, no comparison of the entropy of the routing distribution to what would be expected under uniform usage, no measure of how many experts are "dead" (never selected or selected with negligible weight). The parameter-matching procedure (which increases `k` or `n_heads` when perplexity is insufficient) provides indirect evidence that more active experts improve performance, but this doesn't address whether the *inactive* experts (`E - k` per head per token) are actually used across the dataset as a whole.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The analysis in Section 4 focuses on attention maps, not expert utilization. The authors likely view the matching perplexity as sufficient evidence that the mechanism works, but this conflates "the model achieves good performance" with "the experts are being used as intended." A model could achieve good perplexity while using only 3 of its 5 experts per head — it would still outperform a 2-expert model, but the claimed efficiency would be inflated (parameters are being wasted on unused experts). Without utilization statistics, a practitioner cannot know whether SwitchHead's resource savings come from genuine expert diversity or simply from having a smaller `d_model` and more efficient attention (with the extra expert parameters being dead weight).

### The Parameter-Matched Setting Assumes Training from Scratch and Does Not Apply to Existing Pretrained Models

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper compare SwitchHead models trained from scratch against dense Transformer baselines trained from scratch, with architectures adjusted so that the total parameter counts match. This is a clean experimental design for establishing that SwitchHead is a viable alternative architecture, but it elides a practically important question: **can SwitchHead be applied to an existing pretrained dense model to reduce its inference cost without retraining?**

The reason this matters is that SwitchHead's resource savings come partly from a reduced `d_model`. The dense 10-head baseline for 47M C4 has `d_model = 10 × 41 = 410`; the SwitchHead model has `d_model = 2 × 76 = 152` (Table 9). This smaller `d_model` means the MLP layers, layer norms, and embeddings all operate in a lower-dimensional space, contributing to the resource savings beyond what the attention mechanism alone provides. If one were to take an existing pretrained dense Transformer with `d_model = 410` and replace its attention layers with SwitchHead while keeping `d_model` fixed, the head dimension would need to be `d_head = 410 / n_heads = 205` (for `n_heads = 2`), which is much larger than the `d_head = 76` used in the paper's 47M SwitchHead. The attention matrix cost scales with `d_head`, so the savings would be substantially reduced — the attention matrix computation would cost `2 C T^2 × 205` instead of `2 C T^2 × 76`, a 2.7× increase.

More fundamentally, the entire Parameter-matching procedure described in Section 3 — setting `n_heads × E = n'_heads`, adjusting `d_head` and `d_ff` for exact parameter count, trying larger `k` and then larger `n_heads` if performance is insufficient — only works when designing the architecture from scratch. It provides no guidance for how to retrofit an existing model.

**The consequence.** The paper's resource savings numbers (44% compute, 27% memory) are specific to models designed from scratch with SwitchHead. They do not apply to the common scenario of taking a pretrained LLM (e.g., LLaMA, GPT-3) and modifying its attention mechanism for cheaper inference. A practitioner in that scenario would need to either: (1) train a completely new model with SwitchHead from scratch (expensive and may not replicate the original model's capabilities), (2) somehow initialize a SwitchHead model from a pretrained dense model (not obvious — the weight matrices have different shapes and semantics), or (3) apply SwitchHead with the original `d_model` (reducing or eliminating the savings from the smaller model dimension). None of these scenarios are explored.

Additionally, the paper's finding that key and query MoE hurt performance in the parameter-matched setting (Table 6) might not hold if the parameter budget is not constrained — as the paper notes in Appendix A.3, "allowing the parameter budget to increase, more experts always help." A retrofit scenario where parameters can be added (increasing total model size) rather than redistributed might benefit from a different configuration than the one the paper recommends.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All experiments use matched parameter counts between SwitchHead and baselines (values reported in Tables 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, and detailed in Table 9). The MAC-matched experiments (Tables 4, 8) increase `d_head` and `n_heads` to match the baseline's compute rather than its parameter count, but these still train from scratch and produce models with *more* parameters than the baseline (e.g., 63M vs. 47M for the small MAC-matched model). No experiment takes a pretrained dense model and converts it to SwitchHead, and no experiment varies `d_model` independently of the attention architecture to isolate how much of the resource savings comes from the smaller `d_model` versus the SwitchHead mechanism itself.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation at all. It presents the parameter-matched setting as a deliberate and well-motivated choice (Section 3: "We conduct our experiments in a parameter-matched setting which better reflects the task of language modeling"), but it does not discuss the implications of this choice for the applicability of the method to existing models. A brief discussion of whether SwitchHead can be used as a drop-in replacement or whether it requires training from scratch would help practitioners understand the scope of the method's applicability.

### Sequence Length Scaling Behavior Is Not Evaluated Despite Being the Primary Motivation for Attention Efficiency

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper motivates SwitchHead partly by the quadratic scaling of attention cost with sequence length. From Section 1:

> "Given that attention also accounts for a considerable amount of compute and memory usage in Transformers (especially for long context sizes), using MoE for attention has potential to further improve resource efficiency."

The resource formulas (Equations 11–15) confirm that the attention matrix computation (`2 C T^2 d_head`) is the term that dominates for long sequences, and that reducing `n_heads` directly reduces this term. This is the core structural advantage of SwitchHead: the number of `T^2` attention matrices scales with `n_heads` (2–4) rather than with `n_heads × k` or with the total number of expert projections.

However, all experiments in the paper use relatively short sequence lengths. Table 9 shows: 256 tokens for 47M Transformer XL models, 512 tokens for 262M Transformer XL models and the 41M Enwik8 model, 512 tokens for 45M RoPE models, and 1024 tokens for 244M RoPE models. These are modest by modern standards — production LLMs routinely handle 2048–8192 tokens, and long-context models extend to 32K–128K tokens. At these short lengths, the quadratic attention cost may not dominate the linear projection costs, meaning the experiments are conducted in a regime where SwitchHead's structural advantage is *least* pronounced.

**The consequence.** The paper's resource savings numbers are measured at sequence lengths where the `O(T^2)` attention cost competes with the `O(T)` projection costs. As sequence length grows, the attention cost becomes increasingly dominant, and the savings from reducing `n_heads` should become *larger* (because a greater fraction of the total compute is in the attention matrix). The reported 44% compute usage at `T=512` is therefore a *lower bound* — SwitchHead should look even better at `T=2048` or `T=4096`. However, this extrapolation is untested, and several factors could complicate it:

- **Memory savings scalability**: The memory savings from fewer attention matrices (reducing the `2 C T^2` storage term per head) should grow with `T^2`, potentially making SwitchHead dramatically more memory-efficient at long sequences. But the paper's longest tested sequence is 1024 tokens, and the memory measurements in Table 5 are for training (which includes optimizer states, gradients, and activations from all layers), making it difficult to isolate how the attention-specific memory scales.

- **Expert selection overhead becomes negligible**: As `T` grows, the `O(T)` routing cost (`2 n_heads T d_model E`) becomes vanishingly small compared to the `O(T^2)` attention cost. This means the unaccounted-for routing overhead (Limitation 2 above) matters even less at long sequences. But this favorable scaling is not demonstrated.

- **Attention pattern diversity at long range**: The paper's central hypothesis — that attention patterns are redundant and can be shared — was tested at short sequences where the attention patterns capture relatively local syntactic and semantic relationships. At very long sequences (tens of thousands of tokens), attention patterns might need to capture qualitatively different types of relationships (document-level structure, long-range coreference, retrieval-like operations) that require more diverse attention patterns. If so, SwitchHead might need more independent heads (`n_heads`) at long sequence lengths, eroding the savings.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. No experiment varies sequence length. All models use fixed sequence lengths (256–1024 tokens) as specified in Table 9. The paper does not plot perplexity or resource usage as a function of sequence length, does not test how the optimal configuration (`n_heads`, `k`, `E`) changes with `T`, and does not validate that the savings ratio improves (or at least holds) as sequences get longer.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap. The resource formulas (Equations 11–15) analytically show that the savings should scale favorably with `T`, but the paper does not provide any experimental confirmation. Given that long-sequence efficiency is one of the primary motivations stated in the introduction, this is a significant omission. The authors may have been constrained by compute budget (longer sequences are more expensive to train), but even a small-scale experiment with varying `T` at a fixed model size would have provided evidence for the claimed scaling behavior.

### The Single-Seed Training Protocol Leaves Uncertain Whether Perplexity Differences Are Meaningful

**The assumption or constraint.** All results in the paper appear to be from single training runs. The paper does not report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or results from multiple random seeds for any experiment. The hyperparameter selection procedure (Section 3) — trying `k=2`, then `k=4`, then increasing `n_heads` — operates on single-run perplexity values, meaning the architectural choices are made based on measurements that may include non-trivial training noise.

This matters because many of the perplexity differences between SwitchHead and baselines are very small. In Table 2, the 262M C4 comparison is SwitchHead 16.23 vs. baseline 16.28 — a difference of 0.05 perplexity. The 262M peS2o comparison is 9.86 vs. 9.78 (0.08 difference). The 47M Wikitext-103 comparison is 12.31 vs. 12.32 (0.01 difference). Language model training with fixed hyperparameters but different random seeds can easily produce perplexity variations of 0.1–0.3 at these scales, meaning the observed differences may be within the noise floor. If the baseline actually outperforms SwitchHead by 0.2 perplexity (within typical training variance), the central claim of "matching performance with reduced resources" would be weakened.

The zero-shot downstream results in Table 4 show more substantial gaps, particularly on BLiMP (47M: 75.7% vs. 73.6%, a 2.1% gap; 262M: 79.6% vs. 76.1%, a 3.5% gap). These are likely above the noise floor for these benchmarks (which typically have standard deviations of 0.5–1.5% depending on model size and training stability), providing stronger evidence that SwitchHead genuinely matches or exceeds the baseline. But the central language modeling results — the primary metric the paper optimizes for — have differences small enough that single-seed uncertainty is a real concern.

**The consequence.** The paper's conclusions about exactly matching or slightly exceeding baseline performance may not be robust. With multi-seed evaluation, the true picture might be: SwitchHead's perplexity is statistically indistinguishable from the dense baseline (supporting the claim), or SwitchHead slightly but significantly underperforms (weakening the claim but still practically valuable given the resource savings), or SwitchHead slightly but significantly outperforms (strengthening the claim). The paper cannot distinguish these scenarios with single-run data. The practical implication for a practitioner is that training a SwitchHead model may or may not match baseline perplexity on their dataset — the paper provides evidence that it can, but not that it reliably does across training runs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. No standard deviations, error bars, confidence intervals, or multi-seed results are reported. The paper does not mention the number of training runs, but the consistent lack of variance reporting and the presence of perfectly tied values (e.g., Enwik8 at 1.10 bpc for both SwitchHead and the baseline) strongly suggests single runs. The cross-validation protocol used for some related work (e.g., two-fold cross-validation in the test-time compute paper) is absent here. The paper's hyperparameter selection procedure — a sequential decision process based on single-run results — would benefit from variance estimates to determine whether increasing `k` or `n_heads` genuinely improves performance or just samples a better random seed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. No mention is made of training variance, seed sensitivity, or statistical testing. The authors may consider this acceptable because the primary contribution is the architectural design and the resource savings, with exact perplexity matching being a secondary claim. However, given that the paper's central tables use perplexity differences of 0.01–0.08 to argue for performance equivalence, reporting variance would substantially strengthen (or appropriately qualify) these claims.
