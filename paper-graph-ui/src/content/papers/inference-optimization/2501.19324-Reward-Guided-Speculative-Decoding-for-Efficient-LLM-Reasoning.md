# Reward-Guided Speculative Decoding for Efficient LLM Reasoning

**ArXiv:** [2501.19324](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.19324)

## 🎯 Pitch

Standard speculative decoding discards correct reasoning steps that don’t align with the large model’s distribution, crippling speedups on hard problems. By selectively overriding that strict unbiasedness constraint based on a step-level reward signal, RSD cuts FLOPs by up to 4.4× while simultaneously boosting accuracy by over 3 points on Olympiad-level benchmarks.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Reward-Guided Speculative Decoding (RSD)**, a framework that accelerates LLM inference for reasoning tasks by dynamically mixing outputs from a lightweight draft model and a larger target model, using a process reward model (PRM) to evaluate intermediate decoding steps and selectively accept or refine them — rather than enforcing the strict unbiasedness required by standard speculative decoding. Evaluated on challenging reasoning benchmarks — including MATH500, GSM8K, Olympiad Bench, and GPQA — with Qwen-2.5, Llama-3.1, and Qwen-2.5-Math model families, RSD delivers up to 4.4× fewer FLOPs compared to decoding with the target model alone while improving accuracy over parallel decoding methods by up to +3.5 points on average, establishing that reward-guided controlled bias can simultaneously improve both efficiency and output quality in long-horizon reasoning when a PRM provides per-step quality signals, without requiring the draft and target model distributions to exactly match.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Speculative Decoding Fails on Complex Reasoning Tasks

The paper addresses a specific tension between two well-established facts about large language model deployment. First, **inference is the dominant cost** in serving LLMs at scale — the paper cites Patterson et al. (2021) to establish that while training is resource-intensive, inference at scale is even costlier, requiring vast computational infrastructure to serve billions of queries. Second, **speculative decoding** (Leviathan et al., 2023) is the leading approach for accelerating autoregressive generation without quality degradation, but it was designed for and primarily evaluated on relatively straightforward next-token prediction tasks — not on the multi-step, long-horizon reasoning problems that increasingly define frontier LLM use cases (math competitions, Olympiad-level science, graduate-level QA).

The fundamental problem is that standard speculative decoding imposes a **strict unbiasedness constraint**: the final token distribution must exactly match what the large target model would have produced on its own. This constraint guarantees theoretical fidelity but creates a practical bottleneck when applied to reasoning tasks. Here's why:

- In reasoning, the draft model (a smaller, faster LLM) often generates **high-quality intermediate steps that are correct** — the reasoning is valid, the mathematical manipulation is sound, the conclusion follows logically — but the exact tokens or step sequences **do not align with what the larger model's distribution would have produced**. Under standard speculative decoding's rejection sampling procedure, these correct-but-distributionally-mismatched tokens get discarded. The target model must then regenerate from scratch, negating the speedup that speculative decoding is designed to provide.

- This is not a rare edge case. The paper shows empirically (Section 3.1, Table 2) that standard speculative decoding *underperforms* the single target model on average (e.g., -0.7 for SD vs. target alone with Qwen-2.5-Math-7B). This is counterintuitive — SD is theoretically lossless, so it should achieve identical accuracy, not worse. The discrepancy arises from floating-point errors (as also noted by Chen et al., 2023a) and, more fundamentally, from the distribution mismatch between draft and target: when the draft model is actually *better* than the target on certain domains (e.g., a math-specialized small draft paired with a general large target), SD's strict unbiasedness forces rejection of correct draft outputs, actively degrading performance below what either model achieves alone (Table B.1).

The implication is that **strict unbiasedness is the wrong design principle for reasoning tasks**. What matters is not whether the draft model's distribution matches the target's, but whether the draft model's *outputs are correct and useful*. If a draft step is correct but distributionally unlikely under the target model, rejecting it wastes computation and may even discard correct reasoning that the target model wouldn't have found independently.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Convergence of Two Trends

The paper's timing is not accidental — it sits at the intersection of two converging developments that make this problem urgent:

**1. Reasoning is the new frontier for LLM deployment.** The benchmarks used in the paper — MATH500 (competition math), Olympiad Bench (Olympiad-level science), GPQA Diamond (graduate-level QA where experts achieve ~65% accuracy), GaoKao-2023-En (Chinese college entrance exam) — represent the kind of multi-step, cognitively demanding tasks that organizations increasingly want LLMs to handle. These are not simple next-token prediction problems where the correct continuation is obvious; they require chains of reasoning that may span dozens of steps. The paper reports an average of 18 reasoning steps per question on MATH500 (Section 4), making the per-step overhead of invoking the target model particularly costly.

**2. Inference economics dominate deployment decisions.** As the paper notes, the exponential growth in inference costs makes it "a key challenge for large model deployment." For a 72B parameter model (the largest target in the paper's experiments), every forward pass costs approximately $2 \times 72 \times 10^9 = 144$ billion FLOPs per token. On MATH500 with ~18 steps per question, a single question costs on the order of $144 \times 10^9 \times L$ FLOPs, where $L$ is the total output token count. At scale — billions of queries — this becomes economically prohibitive. Speculative decoding was supposed to solve this, but if it degrades accuracy on reasoning tasks (as the paper shows), practitioners face a lose-lose choice: use the large model and pay the compute cost, or use the small model and sacrifice accuracy.

The paper's framing makes this explicit: "These results clearly demonstrate both efficiency and effectiveness of RSD" (Section 3.3). The goal is not just speed for its own sake, but a **Pareto improvement** — better accuracy at lower cost — which is what makes the contribution practically significant rather than merely an incremental refinement.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three categories of existing methods and explains why each is insufficient for efficient reasoning:

#### 1. Test-Time Scaling with Small Models Alone

A natural approach to improving reasoning without deploying a large model is to spend more inference compute on a smaller model — majority voting over multiple samples, best-of-N selection using a reward model, or beam search guided by a process reward model. The paper evaluates these extensively (Section 3.1, Table 2; Section 3.2, Table 3) and finds they consistently underperform the single large target model:

> "Test-time scaling methods like majority voting and Best-of-N, which rely on extensive sampling with a draft model, consistently underperform a single target model on average. This finding highlights the importance of a larger model for reasoning tasks, as its performance cannot be easily matched by a smaller model with increased computation."

Concretely, for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B with majority voting at 16 samples, accuracy is 62.3% vs. 65.3% for the 7B target alone. Best-of-N with a PRM at N=16 closes the gap slightly (64.8%) but still trails. Even with 64 samples (which likely costs more FLOPs than the target model alone), majority voting with the 7B draft achieves 69.8% vs. 71.8% for the 72B target — a gap that cannot be closed by simply scaling the small model's inference budget. The conclusion is clear: **there are reasoning capabilities that only emerge at larger parameter scales**, and no amount of sampling or search with a small model can substitute for them.

This finding aligns with and extends the analysis from Snell et al. (2024), which showed that test-time compute scaling has difficulty-dependent limits. The paper's insight is that rather than trying to make the small model solve everything (which fails on hard problems), we should selectively invoke the large model when the small model's outputs are insufficient — precisely what RSD does.

#### 2. Standard Speculative Decoding and Its Distributional Mismatch Problem

The paper gives a careful treatment of why standard speculative decoding underperforms on reasoning, going beyond the obvious "mismatched tokens get rejected" to articulate a deeper issue.

In standard speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2023), when the draft model proposes a token, the target model computes its own probability for that token. If the target's probability is *higher*, the token is always accepted. If it's *lower*, the token is accepted with probability $\frac{P_M(y_i|z_i)}{P_m(y_i|z_i)}$ (or rejected, in which case the target model resamples from a residual distribution). This procedure mathematically guarantees that the final distribution matches $P_M$ exactly.

The problem for reasoning tasks is subtle. The draft model might produce a correct reasoning step — say, `i^45 = 1` in the complex number evaluation example from Figure 1 — that represents valid mathematical reasoning and leads to the correct final answer. But the target model might assign this specific token sequence relatively low probability because its own reasoning path differs (different intermediate steps, different algebraic manipulations, different simplification order). Under the rejection sampling rule, this correct step gets rejected with some probability, and the target model regenerates something from its own distribution, which might produce a *different* correct step or, worse, might make an error that the draft model avoided.

Moreover, the paper identifies a less obvious failure mode: when the draft model is domain-specialized (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B as draft paired with general Qwen2.5-7B as target), the draft can actually outperform the target on math-specific benchmarks. Standard SD's unbiasedness constraint forces the final distribution to match the *weaker* model's predictions on these domains, actively harming performance. Table B.1 shows this concretely: on CN Middle School 24, the 1.5B math specialist achieves 75.2% while the 7B general model gets 72.3%; SD (7B target, 1.5B draft) scores 73.3% — worse than the draft alone. RSD with $\delta = 0.7$ achieves 78.2%, exceeding both models individually by accepting the draft's correct outputs regardless of distributional alignment.

#### 3. Process Reward Models Exist, But They Are Used for Search, Not for Adaptive Draft-Target Routing

The paper acknowledges that process reward models (PRMs) have become central to improving LLM reasoning (Lightman et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2024). Prior work uses PRMs in two ways:

- **Training-time feedback**: PRMs provide dense, step-level reward signals for reinforcement learning or rejection fine-tuning of policy models. The PRM tells the model *which* reasoning steps are good, and the model learns to produce more of those steps.

- **Inference-time search**: PRMs evaluate partial solutions during beam search, tree search, or best-of-N selection. The search algorithm uses the PRM's per-step scores to decide which reasoning paths to explore or which complete solutions to select (Chen et al., 2024a; Qi et al., 2024; Snell et al., 2024).

What was *missing* prior to this paper, and what RSD contributes, is the idea of using PRMs as a **dynamic routing mechanism** between a cheap draft model and an expensive target model during autoregressive generation. In RSD, the PRM's role is not to guide search within a single model's output space, but to decide at each step: "Is this draft step good enough to keep, or do we need the target model's intervention?" This transforms the PRM from a selection/scoring mechanism into an **adaptive compute allocation controller**.

The connection to the Snell et al. (2024) analysis is instructive. Snell et al. showed that the optimal test-time compute strategy depends on problem difficulty — easy problems benefit from sequential refinement, hard problems benefit from parallel search. RSD implements a similar difficulty-adaptive principle but at the *step level* rather than the problem level: for easy reasoning steps (high PRM score), the draft model suffices; for hard reasoning steps (low PRM score), the target model is invoked. This granularity matters because a single MATH500 problem typically contains both easy and hard steps — an early arithmetic calculation might be trivial while a later algebraic manipulation requires sophisticated reasoning. RSD can route each step independently, something problem-level difficulty estimation cannot achieve.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution along two axes that distinguish it from both speculative decoding and reward-guided search literature:

**1. From unbiasedness to reward-guided controlled bias.** The key intellectual move is to relax the strict unbiasedness constraint that defines standard speculative decoding and replace it with a **reward-based acceptance criterion**. Instead of asking "does the target model assign high probability to this draft token?", RSD asks "does a process reward model assign high quality to this reasoning step?" The paper formalizes this through the mixture distribution $P_{\text{RSD}}(y_i | z_i) = w(y_i | z_i) P_m(y_i | z_i) + \nu P_M(y_i | z_i)$, where $w$ is a function of the reward $r(y_i | z_i)$ (Section 2). This explicitly acknowledges that the output distribution will deviate from $P_M$ — the bias is not a bug but a feature, because it allows the system to retain correct draft outputs that $P_M$ would have rejected.

The theoretical analysis (Propositions 2.2 and 2.3) provides justification: under mild conditions (the target model has higher expected reward than the draft, and the weighting function is non-decreasing in reward), the mixture distribution achieves higher expected reward than the draft model alone. Furthermore, the optimal weighting function under a budget constraint is a **binary step function** — accept draft steps above a threshold, reject below — which justifies the algorithm's simple threshold-based design.

**2. From model-level to step-level adaptive computation.** Prior work on compute-optimal inference (Snell et al., 2024) allocates test-time compute at the granularity of entire problems. RSD operates at the granularity of individual reasoning steps. This is not just a finer discretization — it reflects a different operational principle. Problem-level allocation requires an initial difficulty estimation phase (e.g., sampling 2048 solutions to estimate pass@1, as in Snell et al.), which itself costs compute. Step-level allocation uses the PRM's online evaluation of each step as it is generated, embedding the difficulty assessment into the generation process itself with minimal overhead (18 PRM calls per question, each costing roughly one token's worth of FLOPs).

The paper explicitly contrasts RSD with search-based PRM methods (Section 3.2, Table 3). Process Best-of-N and beam search (both using the 1.5B model with a 1.5B PRM) achieve 78.2% (beam size 4) vs. RSD's 82.6% on MATH500. The paper's explanation captures the core insight:

> "These results highlight a critical insight: for certain complex or 'hard' reasoning steps, search-based methods struggle to find optimal solutions due to the combinatorial explosion of potential candidates, leading to suboptimal performance. On the other hand, our approach leverages a larger model's capacity to generate plausible solutions more directly, bypassing the need for exhaustive search."

In other words, search with a small model hits a fundamental ceiling on hard steps because the small model's output space simply doesn't contain correct solutions for those steps, no matter how thoroughly you search. RSD bypasses this ceiling by calling in the large model precisely when needed, combining the efficiency of the small model (for easy steps) with the capability of the large model (for hard steps).

**3. Bridging speculative decoding and reward-guided reasoning.** The paper positions RSD as a synthesis of two previously disconnected lines of work. From speculative decoding, it inherits the draft-then-verify architecture and the efficiency benefits of using a small model for most generation. From reward-guided reasoning (PRMs, best-of-N, beam search), it inherits the idea that per-step quality signals can guide generation toward correct solutions. The novelty is in how these are combined: the PRM becomes the verification mechanism, replacing the likelihood-ratio test of standard speculative decoding with a quality assessment, and the draft-target routing becomes adaptive rather than uniform.

This synthesis is what enables the paper's headline results — 4.4× fewer FLOPs than the target model alone while achieving higher accuracy (88.0% vs. 85.6% on MATH500 for the 72B target, Figure 4). Standard speculative decoding cannot achieve this because it ties performance to the target model's distribution. PRM-guided search cannot achieve this because it is limited by the small model's capability ceiling. RSD achieves it by using the PRM to route computation to where it matters most — the hard steps that the small model cannot handle alone.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

RSD is a **decoding algorithm** — a procedure that runs at inference time to generate text from a language model, not a new model architecture or training method. The system takes three pre-trained models (a small draft model, a large target model, and a process reward model) and orchestrates them during autoregressive generation so that the draft model handles most reasoning steps cheaply, while the target model intervenes only when the draft's output quality drops below a reward threshold. The core idea is to replace standard speculative decoding's strict distribution-matching constraint with a **reward-guided acceptance criterion** — if a reasoning step is good (high PRM score), keep it regardless of whether the target model would have produced the same tokens; if it's poor, invoke the target model for a correction. This transforms the draft model from a proposal distribution that must be unbiasedly corrected into a **quality-filtered generator** whose outputs are kept when they are correct and replaced when they are not.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The RSD system has three pre-trained components that interact at each decoding step:

1. **Draft Model (m)** — a small, fast LLM (1B–7B parameters in the paper's experiments) that generates candidate reasoning steps autoregressively given the prompt and previous context. It is the primary workhorse: the system prefers to use the draft model whenever possible because it costs far fewer FLOPs per token than the target model.

2. **Target Model (M)** — a large, capable LLM (7B–72B parameters) that generates replacement reasoning steps when the draft model's output is rejected. It serves as a **fallback oracle**: when the PRM judges that the draft step is low-quality, the target model produces a (presumably) higher-quality alternative. The target model is invoked sparingly — only on rejected steps — which is where the efficiency gains come from.

3. **Process Reward Model (PRM)** — a separate model (1.5B–72B parameters in the paper) that takes the current context (prompt + all previous accepted steps) and a candidate next step, and outputs a scalar reward score $r \in [0, 1]$ indicating the step's quality. The PRM acts as the **gatekeeper**: its score determines whether the draft step is accepted (continuing with the cheap model) or rejected (triggering an expensive target model call).

**Information flow at each step $i$:**

- **Step 1:** The draft model $m$ generates a candidate step $\hat{y}_i$ given the current context $z_i = [x, y_{1:i-1}]$, where $x$ is the prompt and $y_{1:i-1}$ are all previously accepted steps.
- **Step 2:** The PRM computes a reward score $r_i = r(\hat{y}_i | z_i)$, evaluating the quality of this candidate step within the ongoing reasoning trajectory.
- **Step 3:** The acceptance criterion $A_\omega(r_i)$ is evaluated. If the reward is sufficiently high (above threshold $\delta$ for the binary step function), the draft step is accepted: $y_i = \hat{y}_i$. If the reward is too low, the draft step is rejected and the target model generates a replacement: $y_i \sim P_M(\cdot | z_i)$.
- **Step 4:** The accepted step $y_i$ is appended to the context, and the process repeats for step $i+1$ until an end-of-sequence token is generated or the maximum length is reached.

This architecture means that **most steps in an easy reasoning chain come entirely from the draft model**, with zero target model FLOPs consumed. Only on difficult steps — where the draft model struggles and the PRM detects the struggle — does the system pay the cost of running the large model.

3. **The theoretical justification** — Propositions 2.1, 2.2, and 2.3, which establish that RSD's expected reward exceeds the draft model's, and that a binary threshold function is optimal under a budget constraint.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper with theoretical grounding**: the core contribution is a new decoding algorithm (Algorithm 1) justified by a mixture-distribution framework and optimality analysis (Propositions 2.1–2.3), with extensive empirical validation across model families and reasoning benchmarks.

#### The Mixture Distribution Formulation

RSD is formalized through a **dynamic mixture distribution** that combines the draft model $P_m$ and the target model $P_M$ at each generation step, with the mixing weights determined by a reward function that evaluates the quality of the draft model's output.

**Notation setup.** The paper defines the following notation (Section 2):

- $x \in \mathbb{R}^{l \times d}$ is the prompt, embedded in a $d$-dimensional space with sequence length $l$.
- $y \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$ is the full response, which decomposes into a sequence of reasoning steps $y_{1:n} = [y_1, y_2, ..., y_n]$.
- At step $i$, the context is $z_i = [x, y_{1:i-1}]$ — the prompt concatenated with all previously generated and accepted steps.
- The draft model $m$ defines a conditional distribution $P_m(y_i | z_i)$ over the next step given context.
- The target model $M$ defines a conditional distribution $P_M(y_i | z_i)$ over the next step given context.
- A reward function $r(y_i | z_i)$ evaluates the quality of a candidate step $y_i$ within the ongoing trajectory, outputting a scalar where higher values indicate better alignment with the desired response.

The paper assumes the target model has higher expected reward than the draft model at every step:

$$E_{y_i \sim P_M}[r(y_i | z_i)] \geq E_{y_i \sim P_m}[r(y_i | z_i)]$$

where $E_{y_i \sim P_M}[\cdot]$ is the expectation over the target model's distribution of next-step outputs given context, and $E_{y_i \sim P_m}[\cdot]$ is the expectation over the draft model's distribution.

**What this inequality asserts:** that on average, the large target model produces reasoning steps that the PRM scores more highly than the steps produced by the small draft model. This is the paper's core assumption about model capability: larger models are more reliable for complex reasoning, and the PRM captures this reliability difference. The paper provides empirical support in Figure 3 (Middle), showing that for correctly answered MATH500 questions, the target model's reward scores consistently exceed the draft model's — the target produces higher-quality intermediate steps. This assumption justifies using the target model as a fallback: when the draft's output is poor (low reward), invoking the target model should improve it.

**The mixture distribution.** RSD defines its induced distribution over next-step outputs as:

$$P_{\text{RSD}}(y_i | z_i) = w(y_i | z_i) P_m(y_i | z_i) + \nu P_M(y_i | z_i)$$

where $w(y_i | z_i)$ is a reward-dependent weighting function that controls how much the draft model contributes to the mixture for this specific candidate $y_i$, and $\nu$ is a normalizing constant that ensures the mixture integrates to 1.

**What it computes:** the probability of generating a particular next step $y_i$ under the RSD algorithm. The first term, $w(y_i | z_i) P_m(y_i | z_i)$, is the contribution from the draft model — the probability that the draft model generates $y_i$ AND that the draft output is accepted (weighted by $w$). The second term, $\nu P_M(y_i | z_i)$, is the contribution from the target model — the probability that the draft output is rejected AND the target model then generates $y_i$. The mixture is a probability distribution: for any given $\hat{y}_i$, the chance that RSD outputs $\hat{y}_i$ is the chance that the draft generates it and it passes acceptance, plus the chance that the draft is rejected and the target generates it.

**Weighting function and the reward.** The weighting function $w(y_i | z_i)$ depends on the reward:

$$w(y_i | z_i) = \omega(r(y_i | z_i))$$

where $\omega: \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1]$ maps the scalar reward to a weight between 0 and 1. Specifically:

- When $r(y_i | z_i)$ is large (high-quality draft step), $\omega(r)$ approaches 1, making $w(y_i | z_i) P_m(y_i | z_i)$ dominate the mixture — the system confidently uses the draft model's output.
- When $r(y_i | z_i)$ is small (low-quality draft step), $\omega(r)$ approaches 0, making $\nu P_M(y_i | z_i)$ dominate — the system falls back to the target model.

Figure 2 in the paper illustrates this visually: the blue curve ($P_m$) is the draft distribution, the green dashed curve ($P_M$) is the target distribution, and the red curve ($P_{\text{RSD}}$) is the mixture. In high-reward regions (right side of the figure), the mixture closely follows the draft distribution (efficiency). In low-reward regions (left side), it shifts toward the target distribution (quality). The orange dotted line shows $w(y_i | z_i)$, which rises in high-reward regions and falls in low-reward regions.

**The normalizing constant $\nu$.** The paper defines:

$$\nu = 1 - \mathbb{E}_{y \sim P_m}[\omega(r(y | z))]$$

where $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim P_m}[\cdot]$ is the expectation over the draft model's distribution. In words: $\nu$ is one minus the average weighting of draft outputs. If the draft model typically produces high-reward steps, $\mathbb{E}[\omega(r)]$ will be close to 1, making $\nu$ small — the target model is rarely invoked. If the draft model produces low-reward steps, $\nu$ is larger — the target model is invoked more often. This constant ensures proper normalization: the total probability mass sums to $\mathbb{E}[\omega(r)] + \nu = \mathbb{E}[\omega(r)] + (1 - \mathbb{E}[\omega(r)]) = 1$.

**Why this formulation:** the mixture distribution captures the fundamental tradeoff. Standard speculative decoding uses a different mixture — one that guarantees the final distribution exactly equals $P_M$ — by using likelihood ratios rather than rewards as weights. RSD's formulation replaces the distribution-matching objective with a quality-maximizing objective. The advantage is that correct draft steps are kept even when they deviate from $P_M$'s preferred output, because the acceptance criterion depends on quality (reward) rather than distributional alignment (likelihood ratio). The disadvantage is that the final distribution is biased relative to $P_M$ — but the paper argues, and empirically demonstrates, that this bias is beneficial for reasoning tasks where correctness matters more than distributional fidelity.

**Relationship to the draft model's expected reward.** The paper proves in Proposition 2.2 (Appendix A.2) that under two mild conditions — (1) $\omega(r)$ is non-decreasing in $r$, and (2) the target model has higher expected reward than the draft — the mixture distribution achieves higher expected reward than the draft model alone:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y \sim P_{\text{RSD}}}[r(y | z)] \geq \mathbb{E}_{y \sim P_m}[r(y | z)]$$

The proof decomposes the difference into two non-negative terms: a covariance term $\text{Cov}_{P_m}(\omega(r), r) \geq 0$ (since higher-reward steps get higher weights), and an expectation difference term $(1 - \mathbb{E}[\omega(r)])(\mathbb{E}_{P_M}[r] - \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[r]) \geq 0$ (the target model's reward advantage, weighted by how often it is invoked). This provides theoretical assurance that RSD's quality-guided bias improves over the draft model — the system does not just save compute, it actually produces better outputs on average than either model alone by selectively deploying the target model where the draft is weak.

#### The Acceptance Criterion and Weighting Functions

The mixture distribution $P_{\text{RSD}}$ is a mathematical specification — it defines what distribution the algorithm should sample from. The acceptance criterion $A_\omega$ is the **implementation mechanism** that realizes this distribution through rejection sampling at inference time.

**The general acceptance procedure (Algorithm 2).** Given a reward value $r \in \mathbb{R}$ and a weighting function $\omega: \mathbb{R} \to [0, 1]$, the acceptance criterion works as follows:

1. Compute $\omega(r)$, the weight that the mixture distribution would assign to the draft model's output.
2. If $\omega(r) = 0$, deterministically reject: the draft step is discarded.
3. If $\omega(r) = 1$, deterministically accept: the draft step is kept.
4. If $0 < \omega(r) < 1$, sample a uniform random variable $u \sim U(0, 1)$. Accept the draft step if $\omega(r) \geq u$ (which happens with probability $\omega(r)$), and reject otherwise.

**What this procedure does:** it implements the mixture distribution through probabilistic rejection sampling. When the draft model generates a candidate $\hat{y}_i$ with reward $r$, it is accepted with probability $\omega(r)$. The expected probability of accepting a draft step with reward $r$ is exactly $\omega(r)$, which matches the weight that $P_m$ receives in the mixture distribution. Rejected steps are replaced by samples from $P_M$, which enter the mixture with weight $\nu$ (the probability of rejection times the target model's distribution). This two-stage sampling process — draft proposal followed by reward-based rejection — produces samples distributed according to $P_{\text{RSD}}$ without ever needing to explicitly construct or normalize the mixture distribution.

**Why rejection sampling:** direct sampling from $P_{\text{RSD}}$ would require evaluating both $P_m$ and $P_M$ for every possible next step, then normalizing — computationally intractable. Rejection sampling achieves the same distribution while requiring only: (1) one sample from $P_m$ (cheap), (2) one PRM evaluation (cheap), and (3) on rejection, one sample from $P_M$ (expensive, but rare when the draft is good). This is the standard speculative decoding efficiency pattern — generate with the cheap model first, pay the expensive model's cost only when the cheap model's output is rejected — but with the reward function replacing the likelihood-ratio test.

**Five candidate weighting functions (Table 1).** The paper evaluates five choices for $\omega(r)$, each providing a different tradeoff between draft and target model usage:

1. **Constant**: $\omega(r) = p$ for some $p \in (0, 1)$. Every draft step is accepted with fixed probability $p$, regardless of quality. This is a straw-man baseline that ignores the reward signal entirely — it demonstrates the importance of reward-guided adaptation when compared against reward-aware functions.

2. **Binary step function**: $\omega(r) = \mathbb{1}(r \geq \delta)$, where $\delta$ is a threshold hyperparameter. Draft steps with reward $\geq \delta$ are always accepted ($\omega = 1$); steps with reward $< \delta$ are always rejected ($\omega = 0$). This is the primary weighting function used throughout the paper's experiments.

3. **Clipping**: $\omega(r) = \min(1, \max(0, r))$. The reward is clamped to $[0, 1]$ and used directly as the acceptance probability. High-reward steps ($r \geq 1$) are always accepted; medium-reward steps are accepted probabilistically; low-reward steps ($r \leq 0$) are always rejected.

4. **Sigmoidal**: $\omega(r) = \max(0, \frac{r}{1+r})$. A smooth, monotonically increasing transformation that maps $r \in [0, \infty)$ to $[0, 1)$, never quite reaching 1 (always leaving some chance of rejection).

5. **Logistic (sigmoid)**: $\omega(r) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\alpha(r - \delta)}}$, where $\alpha$ controls steepness and $\delta$ controls the threshold. A smooth approximation to the binary step function, with $\alpha \to \infty$ recovering the binary case.

**Why the binary step function dominates (Figure 6):** Among these five, the binary step function achieves the best accuracy on MATH500 (84.6% for RSD 1.5B/7B/7B), surpassing the target model alone (83.2%) and substantially outperforming the constant function (77.6%). The paper provides both an empirical and a theoretical rationale:

- **Empirically (Figure 6):** The constant function (no reward guidance) performs worst, confirming that reward information is essential. The smooth functions (clipping, sigmoidal, logistic) perform better than constant but worse than binary, suggesting that the sharp accept/reject boundary is beneficial — intermediate-quality steps that would be probabilistically accepted under smooth functions are better off being rejected and regenerated by the target model.

- **Theoretically (Proposition 2.3):** Under a constrained sampling budget — where the expected fraction of steps handled by the draft model is bounded below $1 - \gamma$ (i.e., the target model must be invoked at least $\gamma$ fraction of the time) — the optimal weighting function that maximizes expected reward is a binary step function. The proof (Appendix A.3) sets up a Lagrangian optimization and shows that the derivative with respect to $\omega$ yields a threshold rule: assign $\omega = 1$ to the highest-reward samples until the budget constraint is met, and $\omega = 0$ to the rest. This is the water-filling solution familiar from rate-distortion theory and constrained optimization — concentrate acceptance probability on the highest-quality samples rather than spreading it smoothly.

- **Practical advantage:** The binary function introduces $\delta$ as a single, interpretable hyperparameter that directly controls the draft/target tradeoff. Lower $\delta$ means more draft steps are accepted (higher efficiency, potentially lower quality). Higher $\delta$ means fewer draft steps are accepted (lower efficiency, higher quality, approaching the target model alone as $\delta \to 1$). This makes RSD tunable to different compute budgets without retraining.

**The role of $\delta$ in adaptive compute allocation (Figure 5).** The paper's analysis of $\delta$ on MATH500 reveals a key property: at $\delta = 0.7$, the draft model alone solves 48% of questions — meaning for nearly half of all problems, every reasoning step receives a reward $\geq 0.7$ and the target model is never invoked. This is RSD's automatic compute allocation mechanism in action: easy questions (where the draft model's reasoning is consistently high-quality) are handled entirely by the cheap model; harder questions (where some steps score below the threshold) receive selective target model intervention on those difficult steps. The system adapts per-question and per-step without any explicit difficulty estimation phase — the PRM's online scoring implicitly performs difficulty assessment, and the threshold $\delta$ determines how much quality is demanded before paying the target model's cost.

Figure B.1 provides a finer-grained breakdown by MATH500 difficulty level. At $\delta = 0.7$, the draft model alone solves 84% of Level 1 questions (easiest), 67% of Level 2, 58% of Level 3, 44% of Level 4, and only 19% of Level 5 (hardest). The target model's involvement increases monotonically with difficulty, exactly as an optimal compute allocation strategy should. Moreover, the accuracy improvement from involving the target model grows with difficulty: +4.7 points for Level 1, +5.6 for Level 2, +6.7 for Level 3, +16.4 for Level 4, +15.7 for Level 5. The target model provides the most value precisely where the draft model needs the most help — on the hardest problems.

**How $\delta$ relates to the theoretical constraint $\gamma$.** Proposition 2.3 derives the optimal threshold $\delta_\gamma(z)$ as a function of the budget constraint $\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is the maximum allowed probability of invoking the target model. In practice, the paper treats $\delta$ as a hyperparameter and sweeps it (Table B.2, Figure 5) rather than setting it via a budget constraint. This is a pragmatic choice: the theoretical optimum requires knowing the reward distribution (to set $\delta_\gamma$ such that $\mathbb{E}[\omega(r)] = 1 - \gamma$), which is not known in advance and varies across tasks. Sweeping $\delta$ on a validation set and selecting the best value is simpler and achieves the same effect — it finds the threshold that empirically balances quality and efficiency for a given task distribution.

**Why not use the likelihood ratio from standard SD?** Section 2.4 explicitly discusses an alternative formulation:

$$w(y | z) = \min\left(1, \alpha \frac{P_M(y | z)}{P_m(y | z)}\right)$$

This is algorithmically similar to standard speculative decoding, with $\alpha$ controlling how aggressively the system transitions from draft to target. The paper acknowledges this option and notes that in standard SD, one uses $P_M - P_m$ to propose alternative generations for rejected tokens, which guarantees unbiasedness with $\alpha = 1$. However, RSD's reward-based approach has two advantages: (1) the reward function can capture correctness signals that are orthogonal to token-level probabilities — a correct mathematical step might have low probability under $P_M$ if the model reasons differently, yet still be correct; (2) the reward model can be trained specifically to evaluate reasoning quality, potentially providing more reliable signals than the raw token probabilities of a model that may itself be imperfect at reasoning.

#### The RSD Algorithm: Step-by-Step Execution

Algorithm 1 in the paper specifies the complete RSD decoding loop:

**Inputs:**
- Prompt $x$
- Draft model $m$
- Target model $M$
- Process reward model $r$
- Acceptance criterion $A_\omega$ (Algorithm 2, with a specific weighting function $\omega$ and threshold $\delta$)
- End-of-sequence token $s$
- Maximum sequence length $N$

**Initialization:** Set $y_{1:0}$ to the empty sequence. The loop runs for $i = 1$ to $N-1$ (allowing room for the EOS token).

**For each step $i$:**

1. **Generate draft step:** $\hat{y}_i \leftarrow m(x, y_{1:i-1})$. The draft model takes the prompt and all previously accepted steps as input and autoregressively generates one complete reasoning step. Crucially, the draft model does NOT see any rejected steps — only accepted steps enter the context. This means the context always reflects the actual reasoning trajectory that the PRM has approved.

2. **Compute reward:** $r_i \leftarrow r(\hat{y}_i | x, y_{1:i-1})$. The PRM evaluates the candidate step within the current context. The PRM sees the prompt and all previously accepted steps, plus the candidate draft step, and outputs a scalar score. This is a point evaluation — the PRM does not generate any text; it only scores.

3. **Apply acceptance criterion:** If $A_\omega(r_i)$ returns true (the draft step passes the threshold), set $y_i \leftarrow \hat{y}_i$. Otherwise (rejection), generate a replacement: $y_i \leftarrow M(x, y_{1:i-1})$. The target model generates a complete alternative step given the same context. Note that the target model's generated step is NOT scored by the PRM — it is accepted unconditionally. This reflects the assumption that the target model is sufficiently reliable (Equation 1) that its outputs do not need verification.

4. **Check for termination:** If the end-of-sequence token $s$ appears in $y_i$, break the loop. Otherwise, continue to step $i+1$.

**Output:** The complete response $y_{1:i}$, where $i$ is the step at which EOS was generated or $N$ was reached.

**What happens physically during execution:** At each step, the system incurs the cost of one draft model forward pass (generating $\hat{y}_i$) and one PRM forward pass (scoring $\hat{y}_i$). If the draft step is accepted, that is the total cost for this step — the target model is never run. If the draft step is rejected, the system additionally incurs one target model forward pass (generating the replacement $y_i$). The PRM cost is always paid, but the paper argues this is negligible: on MATH500, the average question requires ~18 reasoning steps, and the PRM invocation per step costs roughly the same as generating one additional token — "minimal overhead compared to SD" (Section 4). When using a 1.5B PRM, the PRM cost per step is approximately $2 \times 1.5 \times 10^9 = 3 \times 10^9$ FLOPs, versus $2 \times 7 \times 10^9 = 14 \times 10^9$ FLOPs for the 7B draft model generating a full step (which may contain dozens of tokens) — the PRM is indeed a small fraction of the per-step cost.

**Comparison with standard speculative decoding:** In standard SD, every token goes through a draft-then-verify cycle: the draft model proposes $\gamma$ tokens, the target model verifies all $\gamma$ tokens in parallel, and tokens are accepted or rejected based on the likelihood ratio $P_M / P_m$. The target model is ALWAYS invoked for verification, even if all $\gamma$ tokens would be accepted. In RSD:

- The target model is invoked ONLY on rejected steps, not on every step. On the 48% of MATH500 questions where the draft model handles everything at $\delta = 0.7$, the target model is never called — zero FLOPs from the large model.
- Acceptance/rejection is at the step level rather than the token level. A reasoning step (which may contain many tokens) is evaluated as a unit by the PRM, and the entire step is either kept or replaced wholesale. This avoids the token-by-token mismatch problem of standard SD, where partial matches can lead to fragmented reasoning.

**The step abstraction.** RSD operates on reasoning steps, not individual tokens. The paper defines a step boundary as the occurrence of `\n\n` (two consecutive newlines) in the generated text. This is a simple heuristic that works for the math and science reasoning benchmarks used in the paper, where models typically output structured solutions with clear paragraph breaks between logical steps. The PRM scores the entire text from the previous step boundary to the current one. This means:

- The PRM evaluates complete sub-reasoning units (e.g., "Step 1: Evaluate i^5\\n\\ni^5 = i"), not partial tokens.
- The acceptance/rejection decision applies to the entire step — if a multi-sentence reasoning step contains one minor error in an otherwise correct derivation, the PRM's score reflects this and may trigger rejection, causing the entire step to be regenerated by the target model.
- The step granularity is coarser than token-level but finer than solution-level, striking a balance between PRM evaluation granularity (the PRM was trained on step-level data) and computational overhead (evaluating every token would multiply PRM cost by the average step length).

**Why step-level rather than token-level routing:** The PRM is trained to evaluate the quality of complete reasoning steps, not individual tokens within a step. A token-level reward signal would be poorly calibrated — the PRM was not trained to score whether the token "the" or "=" is correct in isolation, but whether the semantic content of a reasoning step is correct. Additionally, reasoning errors typically manifest at the step level (a calculation mistake, a misapplied theorem, a faulty inference) rather than at the individual token level. Rejecting at the step level means the system corrects entire faulty reasoning units rather than patching individual tokens, which is more aligned with how reasoning errors occur.

#### Theoretical Foundation: Why RSD Works

The paper provides three propositions that together establish the theoretical basis for RSD. I present them in logical order (which differs slightly from the paper's numbering).

**Proposition 2.1 (Distribution of RSD, Appendix A.1).** Under Algorithm 1 with Algorithm 2 as the acceptance criterion, the induced distribution over next-step outputs at each step $y|z$ is:

$$P_{\text{RSD}}(y|z) = \omega_r(y|z) P_m(y|z) + \nu P_M(y|z)$$

where $\omega_r(y|z) = \omega(r(y|z))$ is the reward-dependent weighting and $\nu = 1 - \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[\omega_r]$ is the normalizing constant.

**Proof sketch (paraphrased from Appendix A.1):** By the law of total probability, the probability of outputting $y$ is the sum of two mutually exclusive events: (1) $y$ is sampled from $P_m$ AND accepted, which occurs with probability $P_m(y|z) \cdot \omega_r(y|z)$; (2) the draft output is rejected (probability $\nu$) AND $y$ is then sampled from $P_M$, which occurs with probability $\nu \cdot P_M(y|z)$. The sum gives the mixture.

**What this establishes:** that the decoding algorithm correctly implements the intended mixture distribution — there is no implementation gap between the mathematical specification and the rejection-sampling procedure. This is important because rejection sampling can introduce biases if the acceptance probabilities are not correctly calibrated; Proposition 2.1 confirms that Algorithm 2's probabilistic acceptance with probability $\omega(r)$ yields the exact distribution specified by the mixture.

**Proposition 2.2 (Expected Reward Improvement, Appendix A.2).** Under two conditions — (1) $\omega(r)$ is non-decreasing in $r$, and (2) $\mathbb{E}_{P_M}[r(y|z)] \geq \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[r(y|z)]$ — the expected reward under the RSD distribution exceeds that under the draft model alone:

$$\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{RSD}}}[r(y|z)] \geq \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[r(y|z)]$$

**Proof sketch (paraphrased from Appendix A.2):** The difference between the two expectations expands to:

$$\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{RSD}}}[r] - \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[r] = \text{Cov}_{P_m}(\omega(r), r) + (1 - \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[\omega(r)]) \cdot (\mathbb{E}_{P_M}[r] - \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[r])$$

The covariance term $\text{Cov}_{P_m}(\omega(r), r)$ is non-negative because $\omega(r)$ is non-decreasing in $r$ — higher-reward samples receive higher weights, creating a positive correlation between weight and reward. This term represents the gain from selectively keeping high-quality draft outputs and rejecting low-quality ones.

The second term is the product of two non-negative factors: $1 - \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[\omega(r)] = \nu \geq 0$ (the probability of invoking the target model) and $\mathbb{E}_{P_M}[r] - \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[r] \geq 0$ (the target model's reward advantage over the draft). This term represents the gain from replacing rejected draft outputs with higher-quality target model outputs.

Since both terms are non-negative, the sum is non-negative, and the inequality holds. Equality occurs only when both terms are zero — either the target model provides no advantage ($\mathbb{E}_{P_M}[r] = \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[r]$) AND the weighting function is constant (zero covariance), which would mean RSD provides no benefit over the draft model.

**What this means operationally:** RSD improves over the draft model through two mechanisms working in concert. First, it **filters** the draft model's outputs — keeping the good ones (which have high reward) and discarding the poor ones. Second, it **replaces** the discarded outputs with target model generations that are, on average, better. The filtering alone provides improvement even if the target model were no better than the draft; the replacement provides additional improvement proportional to the target-draft quality gap. This dual mechanism explains why RSD can outperform both the draft model (through filtering + replacement) and, as the paper shows empirically, sometimes the target model alone (through keeping draft outputs that are correct but not what the target model would have produced).

**Proposition 2.3 (Optimal Weighting Under Budget Constraint, Appendix A.3).** Given a constrained sampling budget where the expected fraction of steps handled by the draft model is bounded: $\nu = 1 - \mathbb{E}_{y \sim P_m}[\omega_r(y|z)] \leq \gamma$ for some $\gamma \in (0, 1)$, the weighting function that maximizes expected reward is:

$$\omega_r(y|z) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } r(y|z) \geq \delta_\gamma(z) \\ 0 & \text{if } r(y|z) < \delta_\gamma(z) \end{cases}$$

where $\delta_\gamma(z)$ is the largest threshold that satisfies the budget constraint $\mathbb{E}_{P_m}[\omega_r] = 1 - \gamma$.

**Proof sketch (paraphrased from Appendix A.3):** The optimization problem maximizes $\mathbb{E}_{P_{\text{RSD}}}[r] = \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[\omega(r) r] + \nu \mathbb{E}_{P_M}[r]$ subject to $0 \leq \omega(y|z) \leq 1$ and $\mathbb{E}_{P_m}[\omega(r)] \geq 1 - \gamma$. Forming the Lagrangian and taking the Fréchet derivative with respect to $\omega(y|z)$ at each point $y$ yields:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \omega(y|z)} = P_m(y|z) \left[ r(y|z) - (\lambda + R) \right]$$

where $R = \mathbb{E}_{P_M}[r(y|z)]$ is the expected reward under the target model and $\lambda$ is the Lagrange multiplier for the budget constraint. The derivative is positive (favoring $\omega = 1$) when $r(y|z) > \lambda + R$, and negative (favoring $\omega = 0$) when $r(y|z) < \lambda + R$. The KKT conditions yield the threshold $\delta_\gamma(z) = \lambda + R$, which is set to the highest value that still satisfies $\mathbb{E}_{P_m}[\mathbb{1}(r \geq \delta_\gamma)] \geq 1 - \gamma$.

**What this proves:** that the binary step function is not just one of several reasonable choices — it is the theoretically optimal choice under a natural budget constraint. Any smooth weighting function that assigns intermediate weights ($0 < \omega < 1$) to some reward values is strictly suboptimal because it could be improved by reallocating acceptance probability mass from lower-reward to higher-reward samples while maintaining the same total acceptance budget. This is the classic "water-filling" result: to maximize expected reward under a volume constraint, concentrate all acceptance probability on the highest-reward samples.

**Why this matters for practical deployment:** It justifies the paper's choice to focus on the binary step function as the primary weighting mechanism. Practitioners can tune a single threshold $\delta$ rather than designing complex weighting functions, knowing that the binary form is theoretically principled. The tradeoff between quality and efficiency is cleanly captured by $\delta$: lower $\delta$ means accepting more draft steps (lower cost, potentially lower quality), higher $\delta$ means accepting fewer (higher cost, higher quality). The optimal $\delta$ for a given deployment budget can be found by sweeping on a validation set.

**On the gap between theory and practice:** Proposition 2.3 assumes that $\delta_\gamma$ can be set to exactly satisfy $\mathbb{E}_{P_m}[\mathbb{1}(r \geq \delta_\gamma)] = 1 - \gamma$, which requires knowing the reward distribution in advance. In practice, the paper treats $\delta$ as a hyperparameter and sweeps it empirically rather than computing it from a budget constraint. This is a reasonable engineering choice given that the reward distribution varies across tasks and is not known a priori. The theoretical result provides conceptual guidance (binary is optimal) while the practical procedure (sweep $\delta$ on validation data) handles the implementation.

#### PRM Usage and the Reasoning-Step Abstraction

**What PRM is used.** The paper's primary experiments use **Skywork-o1-Open-PRM** (o1 Team, 2024) as the process reward model, with variants at 1.5B and 7B parameter scales. This model was "the most advanced open-source PRM available during our experiments" (Section 3). Additional experiments in Section 4 use Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B and Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B (Zhang et al., 2025) for robustness analysis.

The PRM outputs a scalar reward score in $[0, 1]$, where higher values indicate higher-quality reasoning steps. The PRM was trained on step-level annotations — it learned to evaluate whether a given reasoning step, within the context of the prompt and previous steps, represents correct and productive progress toward the solution. The paper does not train its own PRM; it uses off-the-shelf open-source models, which is significant because it demonstrates that RSD works without requiring specialized PRM training aligned to the specific draft/target model pair.

**How the PRM is invoked during decoding.** At each step $i$:
1. The draft model has generated a candidate step $\hat{y}_i$.
2. The full context up to this point — the prompt $x$ and all previously accepted steps $y_{1:i-1}$ — is concatenated with the candidate step $\hat{y}_i$ and fed to the PRM.
3. The PRM produces a scalar $r_i = r(\hat{y}_i | x, y_{1:i-1})$, the predicted quality of step $i$ given everything that came before.
4. This scalar is compared against the threshold $\delta$ to determine acceptance or rejection.

The PRM evaluates only the draft model's outputs, never the target model's. This reflects the assumption (Equation 1) that the target model is sufficiently reliable — its outputs are accepted unconditionally. The PRM's role is purely to decide whether the draft model's output is good enough to keep or whether the system should pay the cost of invoking the more reliable target model.

**Step segmentation.** The paper defines a reasoning step boundary as the occurrence of the string `\n\n` (two consecutive newline characters) in the generated text. This segmentation applies uniformly to all methods that use step-level operations: process Best-of-N, beam search, and RSD. The segmentation is performed on-the-fly during generation: the draft model generates tokens until it produces `\n\n`, at which point the accumulated text is treated as one complete reasoning step, sent to the PRM for evaluation, and either accepted or rejected as a unit.

This heuristic works for the benchmarks used because math and science reasoning models are typically fine-tuned to produce step-by-step solutions with explicit step markers and newline separators. For example, a typical MATH500 output might look like:

```
Step 1: Evaluate i^5\n\ni^5 = i\n\n
Step 2: Evaluate i^{-25}\n\ni^{-25} = -i\n\n
...
```

Each `\n\n`-delimited segment corresponds to a logical reasoning unit. The paper does not explore alternative segmentation strategies; the `\n\n` heuristic is a simple, effective choice for the domains studied but may require adaptation for other tasks or model output formats.

**Why a separate PRM rather than using the target model's internal scores.** An alternative design would be to use the target model's own likelihoods as quality signals — for instance, computing $P_M(\hat{y}_i | z_i)$ and accepting if it exceeds a threshold. The paper does not explicitly compare against this design, but several considerations favor a separate PRM:

- **Specialization:** The PRM is specifically trained to evaluate reasoning quality, potentially capturing correctness signals that are orthogonal to token probabilities. A correct reasoning step that uses unconventional notation might have low probability under $P_M$ but high quality according to a PRM trained on diverse correct solutions.
- **Computational cost:** Evaluating $P_M(\hat{y}_i | z_i)$ requires running the target model on the draft's output — which costs essentially the same as generating with the target model. A separate small PRM (1.5B parameters) costs far fewer FLOPs than the target model (7B–72B parameters). The efficiency gains of RSD depend on the PRM being cheap relative to the target model.
- **Decoupling:** Using a separate PRM means the quality evaluation is independent of the target model's generative biases. If the target model has systematic errors on certain problem types, using its own likelihoods as quality signals could reinforce those errors. A separately trained PRM can potentially recognize correct reasoning even when it deviates from the target model's preferred output patterns.

**PRM overhead analysis (Section 4).** The paper quantifies the PRM cost: on MATH500, the average number of reasoning steps per question is 18, meaning the PRM is invoked 18 times per question. Each PRM invocation costs roughly the same as generating one token with the PRM-sized model (since it is a single forward pass). For a 1.5B PRM, this is $2 \times 1.5 \times 10^9 = 3 \times 10^9$ FLOPs per step, totaling $\sim 54 \times 10^9$ FLOPs per question for PRM evaluation. Compare this to the target model (72B): generating a single token costs $2 \times 72 \times 10^9 = 144 \times 10^9$ FLOPs. So 18 PRM calls cost roughly the same as generating 0.4 tokens with the 72B target model — negligible overhead. Even with a 7B PRM, 18 calls cost $\sim 252 \times 10^9$ FLOPs, equivalent to roughly 1.8 tokens of 72B target model generation — still small relative to the total output length (which may be hundreds of tokens).

**Robustness to PRM choice (Table 5).** The paper tests RSD with three different PRMs (Skywork-o1-Open-PRM-1.5B, Skywork-o1-Open-PRM-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B) and finds that all configurations outperform standard SD. The average accuracy across six benchmarks with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B draft and 7B target is:

- SD: 64.6
- RSD + Skywork-1.5B: 65.9 (+1.3)
- RSD + Skywork-7B: 66.1 (+1.5)
- RSD + Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B: 65.9 (+1.3)
- RSD + Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B: 66.5 (+1.9)

Larger PRMs provide modest improvements, but even the smallest PRM (1.5B) delivers most of the gain. This is important for deployment: adding a 1.5B PRM to a 1.5B draft / 7B target system adds minimal memory and compute overhead while enabling RSD's adaptive routing, making the approach practical even under tight resource constraints.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Choice 1: Binary threshold acceptance over smooth weighting.** As discussed above, Proposition 2.3 establishes the theoretical optimality of the binary step function under a budget constraint, and Figure 6 empirically confirms that it outperforms smooth alternatives. Additionally, the binary function provides a clean operational interpretation: $\delta$ is the minimum quality threshold for accepting draft output. This makes the system's behavior predictable and tunable — increasing $\delta$ means "be more selective, use the target model more often" — which is valuable for deployment where compute budgets may vary.

**Choice 2: Step-level routing over token-level routing.** The paper explicitly contrasts with standard speculative decoding's token-level operation (Section 2.4). The justifications are:

- **PRM training granularity:** PRMs are trained on complete reasoning steps, not individual tokens. Using a PRM to score tokens would be out-of-distribution and likely poorly calibrated. Step-level evaluation matches the PRM's training distribution.

- **Error locality:** Reasoning errors tend to manifest at the step level (a flawed algebraic manipulation, a misremembered formula, a logical leap). Correcting individual tokens within an erroneous step is unlikely to fix the underlying reasoning error; replacing the entire step with a target model generation provides a clean slate for correct reasoning.

- **Computational efficiency:** Token-level routing would require PRM evaluation at every token boundary, multiplying the PRM overhead by the average step length (potentially 20–50 tokens). Step-level routing means the PRM is called once per reasoning step (~18 times per MATH500 question), keeping overhead manageable.

- **Coherence:** Replacing individual tokens within a step could produce incoherent text — a single corrected token in the middle of a flawed derivation doesn't fix the derivation. Replacing entire steps preserves step-level coherence: either the draft's complete reasoning is used, or the target model's complete alternative reasoning is used.

**Choice 3: Unconditional target model acceptance.** When the draft step is rejected, the target model's replacement is accepted without PRM evaluation. The justification rests on Equation 1's assumption that $\mathbb{E}_{P_M}[r] \geq \mathbb{E}_{P_m}[r]$ — the target model is, on average, more reliable than the draft. Re-evaluating the target model's output with the PRM would add cost without benefit under this assumption. However, this choice means that if the target model also produces a low-quality step (which can happen, especially on very hard problems), that low-quality step enters the reasoning trajectory unchecked. The paper does not explore iterative refinement (rejecting the target model's output and trying again) or multiple target model samples, which could improve quality on the hardest problems at additional cost.

**Choice 4: Draft model does not see rejected steps.** The context $z_i$ includes only accepted steps $y_{1:i-1}$ — rejected draft steps are discarded and the draft model is never conditioned on them. This prevents the draft model from being influenced by its own mistakes. An alternative design would be to show the draft model its rejected output and the target model's correction, potentially allowing the draft model to learn from its errors within a single generation. The paper does not explore this, and the current design has the advantage of simplicity: the context always represents the validated reasoning trajectory.

**Choice 5: Fixed threshold $\delta$ across all tasks, with optional per-task tuning.** The paper reports results for both a fixed $\delta = 0.7$ (applied uniformly across all benchmarks) and per-task optimized $\delta^*$ (Table 2, Table B.2). The fixed $\delta$ performs well — achieving better average accuracy than the target model alone in 7 out of 8 configurations (Table 2) — demonstrating that RSD is reasonably robust to the threshold choice. Per-task tuning provides additional gains of 0.5–1.5 points on average. For practical deployment, $\delta = 0.7$ serves as a strong default; tuning on a validation set can squeeze out additional performance for specific task distributions.

**Choice 6: PRM as gatekeeper rather than search guide.** Prior work (Chen et al., 2024a; Snell et al., 2024) uses PRMs to guide search — evaluating multiple candidate steps and selecting the best one, or steering beam search toward high-reward paths. RSD uses the PRM differently: to make a binary accept/reject decision on a single draft output, with rejection triggering a completely new generation from a different model rather than selection among alternatives from the same model. This is a fundamentally different usage pattern. The justification (Section 3.2) is that search with a small model hits a capability ceiling on hard steps — no amount of candidate evaluation can find a correct step if the small model never generates one. RSD's binary accept/reject + target model fallback breaks through this ceiling by bringing in a model that CAN generate correct steps for hard sub-problems.

**Choice 7: No fine-tuning required — all models are off-the-shelf.** RSD does not train or fine-tune any component. The draft model, target model, and PRM are all used as-is from their respective training procedures. This is a significant practical advantage: RSD can be applied immediately to any model pair + PRM combination without additional data collection, training infrastructure, or model customization. The tradeoff is that RSD's performance depends on the quality of the off-the-shelf PRM for the specific draft/target model pair. A specialized PRM trained on the draft model's output distribution could potentially provide more accurate quality assessments and further improve the acceptance/rejection decisions (Section 4 acknowledges this as future work).

#### Computation Analysis: How Efficiency Is Measured

The paper uses **FLOPs (floating-point operations)** as the universal efficiency metric, following the standard transformer approximation (Vaswani et al., 2017; Kaplan et al., 2020): each inference token costs approximately $2N$ FLOPs for a model with $N$ parameters (one forward pass through the full model, with roughly $2N$ multiply-add operations per token).

**Cost of RSD per step:**
- Draft model forward pass: $2 \cdot N_m \cdot L_{\text{step}}$ FLOPs, where $N_m$ is the draft model's parameter count and $L_{\text{step}}$ is the number of tokens in the draft step. For a 1.5B draft model generating a 20-token step: $2 \times 1.5 \times 10^9 \times 20 = 60 \times 10^9$ FLOPs.
- PRM forward pass: $2 \cdot N_{\text{PRM}}$ FLOPs (the PRM processes the entire context and outputs one scalar — the cost is approximately one token's worth of computation for the PRM, since it processes the full context in one forward pass). For a 1.5B PRM: $2 \times 1.5 \times 10^9 = 3 \times 10^9$ FLOPs.
- If rejected, target model forward pass: $2 \cdot N_M \cdot L_{\text{step}}$ FLOPs, where $N_M$ is the target model's parameter count. For a 7B target model: $2 \times 7 \times 10^9 \times 20 = 280 \times 10^9$ FLOPs.

The key efficiency driver is that the target model cost is incurred only on a fraction of steps. If the draft model's acceptance rate is $\alpha$ (fraction of steps where the draft output passes the threshold), the expected FLOPs per step is:

$$\text{FLOPs per step} = 2 N_m L_{\text{step}} + 2 N_{\text{PRM}} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot 2 N_M L_{\text{step}}$$

Compare to using the target model alone: $2 N_M L_{\text{step}}$ FLOPs per step. The speedup factor is approximately:

$$\text{Speedup} = \frac{N_M L_{\text{step}}}{N_m L_{\text{step}} + N_{\text{PRM}} + (1 - \alpha) N_M L_{\text{step}}} = \frac{N_M L_{\text{step}}}{(N_m + (1 - \alpha) N_M) L_{\text{step}} + N_{\text{PRM}}}$$

When $\alpha \approx 1$ (draft handles most steps), the denominator is dominated by $N_m L_{\text{step}} \ll N_M L_{\text{step}}$, yielding a speedup approaching $N_M / N_m$. For a 1.5B draft / 72B target, this theoretical maximum is $72 / 1.5 = 48\times$. In practice, $\alpha$ is lower (the paper reports 48% of questions handled entirely by draft at $\delta = 0.7$, but within harder questions, individual steps may still be rejected), and the PRM cost adds overhead, so the observed speedup is $4.4\times$ (Figure 4) — substantial but well below the theoretical maximum.

**Comparison with standard SD's cost:** In standard SD, the target model is invoked on EVERY chunk of $\gamma$ draft tokens for verification, even if all tokens pass the likelihood-ratio test. The target model must compute forward passes for all $\gamma+1$ tokens (the draft tokens plus one additional target token in case of rejection). RSD avoids this by using the PRM (not the target model) for the acceptance decision, meaning the target model is only invoked when a step is actually rejected. This is the architectural source of RSD's efficiency advantage over SD: the verification step is offloaded to the cheap PRM rather than requiring the expensive target model.

**The FLOPs-accuracy analysis (Figure 4).** The paper's key efficiency plot shows:

- RSD (1.5B/7B/7B) uses fewer FLOPs than Target (7B) while achieving higher accuracy (84.6 vs. 83.2) — a strict Pareto improvement.
- RSD (7B/72B/7B) achieves 88.0 accuracy with ~4.4× fewer FLOPs than Target (72B) at 85.6 accuracy — simultaneously better AND cheaper.
- RSD (7B/72B/7B) outperforms BoN (7B/7B, N=64) by 1.8 accuracy points at significantly lower cost, demonstrating that selective large-model intervention beats brute-force sampling with a medium model.

These results are the empirical justification for the paper's central claim: reward-guided controlled bias enables better accuracy at lower cost than either unbiased speculative decoding or test-time scaling with a single model.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Speculative Decoding from Distribution-Matching to Quality-Control

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is to replace the **strict unbiasedness constraint** that defines standard speculative decoding with a **reward-based acceptance criterion**. This is not an incremental tweak — it changes what the draft model *is* in the system architecture and what the decoding algorithm is *optimizing for*.

Before this paper, speculative decoding was framed as a lossless acceleration technique: the draft model proposes tokens, and the target model verifies them through a likelihood-ratio test that mathematically guarantees the final output distribution exactly matches what the target model would have produced alone (Leviathan et al., 2023). This formulation treats any deviation from $P_M$ as an error to be eliminated. The draft model is a proposal distribution that must be unbiasedly corrected; the target model is the ground truth to be recovered.

RSD reframes the problem entirely. Instead of asking "does this token match what the target model would have said?", RSD asks "is this reasoning step *correct*?" The shift changes three fundamental things about the relationship between models:

**First, it decouples quality from distributional alignment.** Standard SD must reject correct reasoning steps if they happen to fall in low-probability regions under $P_M$ — a mathematically valid step expressed in unconventional notation gets discarded because the target model would have written it differently. This is not a theoretical concern: the paper's Table B.1 shows that when a domain-specialized draft model (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B) actually outperforms a general target model (Qwen2.5-7B) on math benchmarks, SD's strict unbiasedness *degrades* performance below what either model achieves alone — the algorithm actively suppresses the better model's outputs to match the weaker model's distribution. RSD's reward-based criterion breaks this tradeoff: if the PRM judges a step as high-quality, the step is kept regardless of whether it is likely under $P_M$. The draft model becomes a quality-filtered generator, not a proposal distribution to be corrected.

**Second, it transforms the draft model from a speed-hack into a first-class reasoning component.** In standard SD, the draft model is purely an efficiency tool — it exists only to reduce the number of target model forward passes, and its outputs have no independent authority. In RSD, the draft model's outputs are the *primary* reasoning output; the target model is a fallback invoked only when the draft's quality drops. This inversion is visible in the numbers: at $\delta = 0.7$, the draft model alone handles 48% of MATH500 questions completely, with zero target model FLOPs consumed (Figure 5). The draft model is not just accelerating the target model — it is doing the actual reasoning for nearly half of all problems, with the target model serving as a selective corrector for the harder half.

**Third, it redefines what "efficiency" means.** Standard SD measures efficiency as wall-clock speedup while preserving exact output equivalence. RSD measures efficiency as accuracy-per-FLOP, allowing the output distribution to change as long as it improves. This reframing is what enables the paper's headline result: RSD with a 7B draft and 72B target achieves **both higher accuracy AND 4.4× fewer FLOPs** than the 72B target alone on MATH500 (88.0 vs. 85.6, Figure 4). Standard SD cannot make this claim because it is bounded above by the target model's accuracy by construction. RSD's controlled bias is what breaks through this ceiling — by keeping correct draft outputs that $P_M$ would not have produced, RSD can outperform the target model that serves as SD's theoretical upper bound.

This reframing has implications beyond the specific algorithm. It suggests that the unbiasedness principle underlying speculative decoding — while theoretically elegant — may be the wrong design philosophy for reasoning tasks where correctness is the objective and models have complementary strengths. The paper doesn't argue this in the abstract; it demonstrates it empirically with multiple model families (Qwen-2.5, Llama-3.1, Qwen-2.5-Math) and shows that the benefits hold across general-purpose and math-specialized models alike (Table 2).

### Innovation 2: Process Reward Models as Runtime Compute-Allocation Controllers

Before RSD, process reward models were used in two well-established roles: (1) as training-time feedback for reinforcement learning or rejection fine-tuning (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024), and (2) as inference-time search guides for selecting among candidate reasoning paths, typically through beam search, tree search, or best-of-N selection (Chen et al., 2024a; Snell et al., 2024; Qi et al., 2024). In both roles, the PRM operates *within* a single model's output space — it helps that model produce better outputs, either by shaping its training or by steering its generation.

RSD deploys the PRM in a fundamentally different role: as a **runtime router** that decides *which model* should handle each reasoning step. The PRM does not guide search within the draft model's output space, nor does it provide training feedback. It makes a binary decision — accept the cheap model's output or invoke the expensive model — based on a quality assessment. This transforms the PRM from a within-model optimization tool into a **cross-model resource allocation controller**.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is not the use of a PRM per se, but the **architecture of computation** it enables. Prior work on compute-optimal inference (Snell et al., 2024) showed that the best test-time strategy varies with problem difficulty, but implementing that insight requires explicit difficulty estimation — generating many samples, computing pass@1 rates, and binning questions — which itself consumes substantial compute before the actual problem-solving begins. RSD embeds difficulty assessment into the generation process itself, at the granularity of individual reasoning steps rather than entire problems. The PRM's per-step score is simultaneously a quality evaluation and an implicit difficulty signal: high-scoring steps are "easy" for the draft model and get handled cheaply; low-scoring steps are "hard" and trigger the expensive model. No separate difficulty estimation phase is needed — the routing is online and adaptive.

The evidence that this routing is genuinely adaptive, not just thresholding, comes from Figure B.1. At the same threshold $\delta = 0.7$, the draft model handles 84% of Level 1 (easiest) MATH500 questions entirely, versus only 19% of Level 5 (hardest) questions. The target model's involvement increases monotonically with problem difficulty — exactly the pattern a compute-optimal allocation strategy should produce. Moreover, the accuracy benefit of involving the target model grows with difficulty: +4.7 points for Level 1 versus +15.7 points for Level 5. The system is not just routing more computation to harder problems — it is routing it precisely where that computation provides the most value.

This per-step routing also addresses a limitation of problem-level allocation that Snell et al. (2024) left unresolved. A single math problem typically contains both easy and hard reasoning steps — an early arithmetic simplification might be trivial while a later algebraic manipulation requires sophisticated pattern recognition. Problem-level allocation treats the entire problem uniformly, either over-computing on easy steps or under-computing on hard ones. RSD's step-level routing adapts within a single problem, deploying the target model only on the specific steps where the draft model struggles.

The theoretical contribution here is subtle but significant. The paper does not just propose a heuristic for routing — it proves (Proposition 2.3, Appendix A.3) that under a budget constraint on target model usage, the optimal acceptance policy is a threshold on reward. This gives the routing mechanism a principled justification: concentrating acceptance probability on the highest-reward steps is provably better than spreading it smoothly. The practical consequence is that a single hyperparameter $\delta$ controls the entire quality-efficiency tradeoff, making RSD tunable without the complexity of designing per-task allocation strategies.

### Innovation 3: Empirical Discovery That Step-Level Target Model Intervention Beats Search-Based Test-Time Scaling

The paper includes a comparison that, while occupying relatively little textual space (Section 3.2, Table 3), contains what I consider one of the paper's most consequential empirical findings: **RSD significantly outperforms process-level beam search and process Best-of-N that use the same PRM with the same base model.** On MATH500, RSD with a 1.5B draft and 7B target achieves 82.6%, versus 78.2% for beam search with beam size 4 or 8 using only the 1.5B model as the generator. The gap is even larger on GSM8K (94.5 vs. 88.9) and Minerva Math (34.6 vs. 33.5).

This result is surprising because the comparison is not intuitively fair. Beam search with a PRM can explore multiple reasoning paths at each step, evaluating candidates and selecting the most promising — it has strictly more information about the quality of alternatives than RSD, which only evaluates a single draft output per step. Yet beam search loses. Why?

The paper's diagnosis identifies a fundamental bottleneck that prior work had not articulated clearly: **search cannot find correct solutions that the generator model never produces.** When using a 1.5B model as the generator, beam search can evaluate candidates, rank them, and select the best — but if the 1.5B model never generates a correct step for a hard sub-problem, no amount of search or PRM evaluation can conjure one into existence. The search space is bounded by the generator's capability; PRM guidance can help navigate that space efficiently, but it cannot expand it.

RSD breaks through this ceiling not by searching better within the small model's output space, but by **switching generators** when the small model's capability is insufficient. The target model has a fundamentally different — and larger — output space. When RSD rejects a draft step and invokes the 7B target model, it gains access to reasoning capabilities that no amount of search with the 1.5B model could ever produce. The efficiency comes from being selective about when to pay for this larger capability: only on the hard steps that genuinely need it, not on every step.

This finding reframes the relationship between search and scale in LLM reasoning. The dominant narrative from the test-time compute scaling literature (Snell et al., 2024; Brown et al., 2024) is that search-based strategies (beam search, best-of-N, tree search) can substitute for model scale on many problems. RSD's results do not contradict this but add a crucial qualifier: **search substitutes for scale only on problems within the generator model's capability range.** For harder problems — or harder sub-steps within a problem — the generator's capability ceiling is binding, and the only way through is a more capable model. RSD provides a mechanism for deploying that more capable model surgically, at the step level, rather than paying its cost for the entire generation.

The practical implication is a design principle for inference systems: invest in a strong-but-expensive target model, but use it only for the fraction of steps where a cheaper model's capability is exhausted. This is fundamentally different from both scaling inference compute with a single model (which hits a capability ceiling) and always using the strong model (which is unnecessarily expensive on easy steps). It is a hybrid strategy that the paper's results suggest can simultaneously outperform both approaches on accuracy-per-FLOP.

### Innovation 4: Introducing and Justifying Controlled Bias as a Feature, Not a Bug, in Efficient Decoding

The paper makes an explicit theoretical and empirical case that **deviating from the target model's output distribution can be beneficial**, and that the strict unbiasedness constraint of standard speculative decoding is actively harmful for reasoning tasks. This is a counter-intuitive claim that requires careful justification, and the paper provides it through a combination of formal analysis and targeted experiments.

The standard view in speculative decoding — inherited from the rejection sampling literature — treats unbiasedness as a correctness guarantee. If the final token distribution matches $P_M$ exactly, the output quality is at least as good as what the target model would have produced. Any bias is a potential degradation, a bug to be eliminated. This perspective is so ingrained that the term "lossless acceleration" is used synonymously with speculative decoding in the literature.

RSD challenges this view on two fronts. **First, the paper provides a counterexample in the form of domain-specialized draft models (Table B.1).** When a math-specialized 1.5B draft model achieves 75.2% on CN Middle School 24 while the general 7B target model achieves only 72.3%, standard SD's unbiasedness forces the final distribution toward the *weaker* model on this domain, producing 73.3% — worse than the draft alone. The "lossless" guarantee becomes a loss in practice because it ties performance to the target model's distribution, which may itself be suboptimal for the task. RSD achieves 78.2% by ignoring distributional alignment and keeping correct draft outputs regardless of what $P_M$ would have preferred.

**Second, the paper proves that controlled bias can systematically improve expected reward (Proposition 2.2, Appendix A.2).** The proof shows that the expected reward under RSD's mixture distribution decomposes into two non-negative terms: a covariance term (keeping high-reward draft outputs, rejecting low-reward ones) and a replacement term (substituting target model outputs for rejected draft outputs). Both terms improve reward relative to the draft model alone. The covariance term specifically represents the gain from *not* accepting draft outputs uniformly — from being selectively biased toward high-quality steps. If RSD accepted draft outputs with uniform probability (as the constant weighting function does), this covariance term would be zero, and the gain would come only from target model replacement.

This is significant because it shows that the bias is not just tolerable but **necessary for the improvement**. The constant weighting function — which ignores reward and accepts draft steps with fixed probability 0.6 — achieves only 77.6% on MATH500 (Figure 6), substantially worse than the binary step function's 84.6%. The reward-guided bias is what separates RSD from a simple random draft-target mixture, and eliminating it eliminates most of the gain.

The practical consequence is a principled defense of bias in efficient decoding for reasoning tasks. This is not a universal claim — the paper does not argue that unbiasedness is always harmful, and the likelihood-ratio approach remains appropriate when distributional fidelity to the target model is the primary objective. But for reasoning tasks where the objective is correctness, RSD demonstrates that the optimal strategy may involve systematically favoring draft outputs that a PRM judges as high-quality, even — especially — when those outputs deviate from what the target model would produce. This reframes bias from a bug to be minimized into a design parameter to be optimized, with the threshold $\delta$ providing explicit control over the acceptable level of deviation.

The theoretical grounding in Proposition 2.3 also connects this insight to budget-constrained optimization: the optimal allocation of a fixed acceptance budget is to concentrate it entirely on the highest-reward samples, which inherently creates bias relative to the draft model's distribution (and relative to $P_M$). This formalizes the intuition that selective acceptance is better than uniform acceptance, and provides a principled answer to the question of *how* selective to be: as selective as the budget allows, accepting only the highest-quality steps until the budget is exhausted.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on six reasoning benchmarks: **GSM8K** (Cobbe et al., 2021b) — grade-school math word problems; **MATH500** (Hendrycks et al., 2021) — the 500-question test split from the MATH competition mathematics dataset, with five difficulty levels from Level 1 (easiest) to Level 5 (hardest); **MMLU STEM** (Hendrycks et al., 2020) — the STEM subset of the Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark covering science, technology, engineering, and mathematics; **OlympiadBench** (He et al., 2024) — Olympiad-level bilingual multimodal scientific problems; **GaoKao-2023-En** (Liao et al., 2024) — questions from the 2023 Chinese college entrance examination (Gaokao), in English; and **GPQA Diamond** (Rein et al., 2023) — the hardest subset of the Graduate-Level Google-Proof Q&A benchmark, where even domain experts achieve only ~65% accuracy. For computation analysis (Figure 4), the focus is on MATH500. For the general-domain extension (Table 6), the paper uses 805 prompts from AlpacaEval (Dubois et al., 2023) with evaluation via AlpacaEval 2.0 (Li et al., 2023) against GPT-4 Turbo outputs.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses three model families spanning both general-purpose and math-specialized architectures: **Qwen-2.5-Instruct** (Yang et al., 2024a) at 1.5B (draft) and 7B (target) as a general-purpose pair; **Qwen-2.5-Math-Instruct** (Yang et al., 2024b) at 1.5B (draft), 7B (target), and 72B (target) as a math-specialized pair; and **Llama-3.1-Instruct** (Dubey et al., 2024) at 1B (draft) and 8B (target) as an additional general-purpose pair. The process reward model is predominantly **Skywork-o1-Open-PRM** (o1 Team, 2024) at 1.5B and 7B scales, with the reward score ranging from 0 to 1 (higher is better). For robustness checks (Table 5), additional PRMs include Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B and Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B (Zhang et al., 2025). The choice of Qwen-2.5-Math as the primary evaluation family is motivated by the paper's focus on reasoning tasks — math-specialized models provide a strong test of whether RSD can improve performance when the draft and target are both already reasonably capable, while the general-purpose families (Qwen-2.5-Instruct and Llama-3.1-Instruct) test whether the benefits transfer to models not specifically trained for math reasoning.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **accuracy** — the fraction of questions for which the model's final answer matches the ground-truth answer, using each benchmark's standard grading procedure. For the computation analysis (Section 3.3, Figure 4), the metric is **FLOPs (floating-point operations) per question**, computed using the standard transformer approximation of 2N FLOPs per inference token for a model with N parameters (following Kang et al., 2024; Sardana et al., 2023). This includes all model forward passes: draft model generation, PRM evaluation, and target model generation (when invoked). For AlpacaEval, the metric is **win rate** (%) as judged by AlpacaEval 2.0's auto-evaluator against GPT-4 Turbo reference outputs. The paper does not report wall-clock latency — all efficiency comparisons are in FLOPs, which is a hardware-independent measure of total computation but ignores parallelism and memory bandwidth effects.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three categories, enumerated explicitly:
  1. **Single target model**: The large model decoding alone at temperature = 0, top-p = 1. This is the upper-bound reference for accuracy (RSD should match or exceed this) and the lower-bound reference for efficiency (RSD should use fewer FLOPs).
  2. **Draft model with test-time scaling**, including:
     - **Majority voting** (maj@N): Sample N complete solutions from the draft model at temperature = 0.7, top-p = 0.8, and select the most common final answer. Evaluated at N = 16 and N = 64.
     - **Best-of-N (BoN)** (Brown et al., 2024; Cobbe et al., 2021a): Sample N complete solutions from the draft model, score each with a PRM (evaluating only the final answer step using the PRM's last-step score), and select the solution with the highest reward. Also evaluated at N = 16 and N = 64. Notated as BoN (draft_size/PRM_size).
     - **Beam search** (Chen et al., 2024a): Step-level beam search guided by the PRM, with beam sizes of 4 and 8. The PRM scores partial solutions at each step, and the top-k beams are retained.
     - **Process Best-of-N**: At each step, sample N candidate next steps from the draft model, score each with the PRM, and select the highest-reward step. Evaluated at N = 8 and N = 16 (Table 3).
  3. **Standard speculative decoding (SD)** (Leviathan et al., 2023): Token-level speculative decoding with 7 speculative tokens, temperature = 0, top-p = 1. The draft model proposes tokens, and the target model verifies in parallel using the likelihood-ratio test to guarantee unbiased final distribution. Notated as SD (draft_size/target_size).

  For all PRM-based baselines (BoN, beam search, process Best-of-N) and RSD itself, a reasoning step is defined as text delimited by `\n\n` (two consecutive newline characters). The PRM evaluates each complete step as a unit. For majority voting and standard SD, no step segmentation is performed — these operate on complete solutions or individual tokens respectively.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper uses **FLOPs per question** as the universal compute metric (Section 3.3, Figure 4). The FLOPs calculation uses the standard approximation of 2N per inference token for a transformer with N parameters, and includes ALL model costs: draft model generation, PRM evaluation (counted as approximately one token's worth of computation per invocation, since the PRM processes the full context in a single forward pass), and target model generation when invoked. This enables fair comparison across methods that use different numbers of models at different frequencies — for example, comparing RSD (which invokes a 1.5B draft + 1.5B PRM on every step and a 7B target on rejected steps) against using the 7B target alone (which invokes the 7B model on every step). The paper does NOT account for the cost of the PRM when computing accuracy-only comparisons in Tables 2, 3, and 5 — those comparisons focus purely on accuracy, with the understanding that RSD's cost advantage is demonstrated separately in Figure 4. For the methods that use multiple samples (majority voting, Best-of-N), the FLOPs cost is multiplied by the number of samples N.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal cross-validation or statistical significance testing. The threshold δ is swept on the full evaluation benchmarks themselves (Table B.2, Figure 5) — there is no held-out validation set for tuning δ separate from the test data. The per-task optimal δ* reported in Table 2 is selected by sweeping δ ∈ {0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9} on each benchmark and reporting the best accuracy; the fixed δ = 0.7 results are also reported as a zero-tuning baseline. This means the δ* results should be interpreted as oracle-tuned rather than predictively validated — they represent an upper bound on what RSD can achieve with optimal threshold selection on a given task distribution. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or results across multiple random seeds, so the reliability of small accuracy differences (e.g., 84.6 vs. 84.0 in Table 5) cannot be assessed statistically. For the general-domain experiment on AlpacaEval (Table 6), δ = 0 is chosen empirically without extensive tuning, and the paper explicitly notes this as a limitation.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Reasoning Benchmarks: RSD vs. Single Target, Test-Time Scaling, and Speculative Decoding

The headline result from Table 2 is that **RSD with per-task optimized δ\* outperforms the single target model on average accuracy across all three model families tested**, while using substantially fewer FLOPs (the FLOPs advantage is quantified separately in Figure 4). The pattern holds across math-specialized, general-purpose, and Llama model families, establishing robustness to model architecture and training distribution.

**Math-specialized models (Qwen2.5-Math-Instruct, top section of Table 2):**

- Single target model (7B) achieves 65.3% average accuracy across six benchmarks.
- RSD with 1.5B draft, 7B target, and 7B PRM at δ* achieves 66.5% average (+1.2 over the target alone). Notably, on GPQA Diamond — the hardest benchmark in the suite — RSD achieves 34.3% vs. the target model's 32.8%, a +1.5 point improvement.
- RSD with δ = 0.7 (no per-task tuning) achieves 66.1% average (+0.8 over target), demonstrating that even a fixed threshold provides consistent improvement.
- Standard SD with the same draft/target pair achieves 64.6% average (-0.7 vs. target alone) — actively worse than just using the target model. This is a critical comparison point: SD and RSD use the same models, but SD's strict unbiasedness degrades performance while RSD's reward-guided acceptance improves it.
- Draft-model-only baselines (majority voting, Best-of-N) with the 1.5B model consistently underperform the 7B target: majority voting at 16 samples achieves 62.3% (-3.0), Best-of-N at N=16 achieves 64.8% (-0.5). Even majority voting with the 7B model at 64 samples achieves only 69.8%, which trails the 72B target's 71.8% — showing that test-time scaling with the medium model cannot close the gap to the larger model.
- Scaling to the 72B target model: single target achieves 71.8% average. RSD with 7B draft, 72B target, 7B PRM at δ* achieves 72.9% (+1.1), and RSD with 1.5B draft, 72B target, 7B PRM at δ* achieves 72.6% (+0.8). Notably, majority voting with the 7B draft at 64 samples (which likely costs MORE than the 72B target alone) achieves only 69.8% (-2.0 vs. the single 72B target), while RSD with the same 7B draft and 72B target achieves 72.9% using fewer FLOPs (exact savings quantified in Figure 4). This demonstrates that RSD's selective invocation of the 72B model is more effective than brute-force sampling with the 7B model.

The cross-benchmark variation in Table 2 is informative about where RSD provides the most benefit:

- **GPQA Diamond** shows the largest relative improvement: RSD (1.5B/7B/1.5B) achieves 38.4% vs. 32.8% for the single 7B target — a +5.6 point absolute improvement. This is the benchmark requiring the most advanced reasoning (graduate-level Q&A), suggesting RSD is particularly valuable on complex tasks where the draft model's capability is stretched and the PRM's guidance is most discriminative.
- **GSM8K** shows more mixed results: RSD at δ = 0.7 achieves 94.5% vs. the target's 95.7%, a -1.2 point drop. The per-task optimized δ* recovers to 95.5% — close to parity. This suggests that for easier benchmarks, the fixed threshold may be overly aggressive in rejecting draft steps, and careful tuning is needed to match the target model.
- **Olympiad Bench** with the Qwen2.5-Math-7B target shows a notable improvement: RSD with 7B PRM at δ* achieves 43.6% vs. the target alone at 41.2% (+2.4). This is the benchmark explicitly designed for Olympiad-level difficulty, and the gain suggests RSD handles competition-level scientific reasoning effectively.

**General-purpose models (Qwen2.5-Instruct, middle section of Table 2):**

- Single target model (7B) achieves 59.9% average.
- RSD with 1.5B draft, 1.5B PRM, δ* achieves 62.6% average (+2.7 over target alone) — the largest relative improvement of any configuration. This is significant because it shows RSD's benefits are not limited to math-specialized models; the general-purpose models benefit even more, likely because the gap between draft and target capability is larger for general models on math reasoning tasks.
- However, RSD with 7B PRM at δ = 0.7 achieves only 59.4% (-0.5 vs. target), driven primarily by a large drop on GPQA Diamond (20.2% vs. the target's 28.8%). The per-task optimized δ* recovers some GPQA performance (29.8%) but still trails the target. This sensitivity to PRM size and threshold for general-purpose models on specialized reasoning benchmarks suggests that the PRM's quality assessments may be less reliable when the draft model's outputs are far from the PRM's training distribution.
- Standard SD achieves 59.1% (-0.8 vs. target) — again worse than the target alone, confirming the pattern that strict unbiasedness degrades performance when the draft model's distribution differs from the target's.
- Draft-only baselines (majority voting, Best-of-N at N=16) achieve 54.8% and 58.2% respectively, both trailing the target model by substantial margins (-5.1 and -1.7).

**Llama-3.1-Instruct models (bottom section of Table 2):**

- Single target model (8B) achieves 41.4% average.
- RSD with 1B draft, 7B PRM, δ* achieves 42.5% (+1.1). The absolute numbers are lower than the Qwen families (reflecting Llama-3.1-8B's weaker reasoning capabilities out-of-the-box), but the relative improvement pattern holds.
- Standard SD achieves 41.1% (-0.3), again slightly worse than the target alone.
- Majority voting with the 1B draft at 16 samples achieves only 30.8% (-10.6 vs. target) — a massive drop that underscores the draft model's limited capability and the importance of the target model as fallback.
- Best-of-N (1B/7B, N=16) improves to 38.8% but still trails the target by 2.6 points. The fact that BoN with PRM selection cannot match the single target model, while RSD can exceed it, indicates that the target model is contributing reasoning capability, not just serving as a selection mechanism.

#### Comparison with Search-Based Methods: RSD vs. Beam Search and Process Best-of-N

Table 3 presents a focused comparison on three benchmarks (MATH500, GSM8K, Minerva Math) between RSD and search-based methods that use only the draft model (1.5B) with the same PRM (1.5B). The results are striking:

- **MATH500:** RSD (1.5B/7B/1.5B) achieves 82.6%, substantially outperforming beam search with beam size 4 (78.2%), beam search with beam size 8 (78.2%), Process Best-of-N at N=8 (75.8%), and Process Best-of-N at N=16 (76.0%). The single 1.5B model alone achieves 73.8%. The gap between RSD and the best search method is +4.4 points.

- **GSM8K:** RSD achieves 94.5%, vs. beam search at 88.9% (beam size 4) and 88.4% (beam size 8) — a gap of +5.6 points. Process Best-of-N peaks at 87.9% (N=16).

- **Minerva Math:** RSD achieves 34.6%, vs. beam search at 33.5% (beam size 4) — a narrower gap of +1.1 points, but still favorable given that RSD uses the target model only on rejected steps.

The paper's interpretation of these results (Section 3.2) is crucial:

> "These results highlight a critical insight: for certain complex or 'hard' reasoning steps, search-based methods struggle to find optimal solutions due to the combinatorial explosion of potential candidates, leading to suboptimal performance. On the other hand, our approach leverages a larger model's capacity to generate plausible solutions more directly, bypassing the need for exhaustive search."

The key mechanistic difference is that beam search and Process Best-of-N can only select among candidates that the 1.5B draft model actually generates. When the problem requires reasoning steps that the 1.5B model cannot produce — no matter how many candidates are sampled or how carefully they are evaluated — search cannot help. RSD breaks through this ceiling by calling the 7B target model, which has fundamentally different (and better) reasoning capabilities. The efficiency comes from not calling the 7B model on every step — only on the steps where the 1.5B model's output quality dips below δ.

Notably, increasing beam size from 4 to 8 does not improve performance on MATH500 (78.2% for both) and actually degrades it slightly on GSM8K (88.9 → 88.4). This flattening or reversal is consistent with verifier over-optimization — search finds solutions that score highly under the PRM but are not actually correct, a phenomenon documented extensively in the test-time compute scaling literature (Snell et al., 2024). RSD avoids this pathology because the target model replaces rejected steps with genuinely new generations rather than selecting among pre-existing candidates that may all be exploiting the PRM's blind spots.

#### Computational Efficiency: FLOPs-Accuracy Trade-off

Figure 4 provides the paper's definitive efficiency analysis, plotting FLOPs per question (log scale) against accuracy on MATH500 for multiple configurations:

- **RSD (1.5B/7B/7B)** achieves 84.6% accuracy while consuming fewer FLOPs than the 7B target model alone (83.2% accuracy). This is a strict Pareto improvement: RSD is simultaneously more accurate (by +1.4 points) AND cheaper. The exact FLOPs savings are visible on the x-axis: the RSD point sits substantially to the left of the Target (7B) point.

- **RSD (7B/72B/7B)** achieves 88.0% accuracy with approximately 4.4× fewer FLOPs than the 72B target model alone (which achieves 85.6%). The paper states "nearly 4.4× fewer FLOPs" — the exact ratio can be read from Figure 4 where the RSD point's x-coordinate is approximately 1/4.4 of the Target (72B) point's x-coordinate.

- **RSD (7B/72B/7B) vs. BoN (7B/7B, N=64):** RSD achieves 88.0% vs. BoN's approximate accuracy of ~86.2% (read from Figure 4), at "significantly lower computational cost" — the BoN point sits far to the right on the FLOPs axis since it requires 64 complete generations from the 7B model plus PRM scoring.

- **RSD vs. SD:** For the 1.5B/7B pair, RSD (1.5B/7B/7B) achieves 84.6% vs. SD (1.5B/7B) at approximately 83.4% — RSD is both more accurate and more efficient. For the 7B/72B pair, RSD (7B/72B/7B) achieves 88.0% vs. SD (7B/72B) at approximately 84.8% — the gap widens at larger scale, likely because the draft-target distribution mismatch is more pronounced and SD's rejection of correct-but-mismatched outputs is more costly.

- **RSD with different PRM sizes:** RSD (1.5B/7B/1.5B) uses fewer FLOPs than RSD (1.5B/7B/7B) because the PRM itself is cheaper, but achieves modestly lower accuracy (82.6% vs. 84.6% at δ = 0.7 from Table 2). The 1.5B PRM point sits further left on the FLOPs axis and slightly lower on the accuracy axis in Figure 4 — the tradeoff is visible and the paper's claim that "even a tiny PRM (1.5B) outperforms the single target model in RSD accuracy" is supported (82.6% vs. 83.2% for the 7B target, which may be slightly lower or comparable depending on the exact FLOPs — the paper states this explicitly in Section 4).

It is important to note what Figure 4 does NOT show. The FLOPs calculation includes the PRM cost as approximately one token's worth of computation per invocation (~3 GFLOPS for 1.5B PRM, ~14 GFLOPS for 7B PRM). The paper claims this is "minimal overhead" (Section 4), and the FLOPs numbers in Figure 4 support this — the PRM's contribution to total FLOPs is small enough that RSD's total cost remains substantially below the target model's. However, the figure does not decompose costs by component (draft vs. PRM vs. target), so the reader cannot independently verify the PRM overhead claim from the plot alone.

#### Difficulty-Dependent Behavior: How Target Model Involvement Scales with Problem Complexity

Figure 5 and Figure B.1 provide complementary views of how RSD's adaptive compute allocation responds to problem difficulty. These are not presented as "main results" in the paper's narrative but contain some of the most informative data about RSD's mechanism.

**Figure 5 (MATH500, RSD 1.5B/7B/7B):**
- As δ increases from 0 to 1, the fraction of questions solved entirely by the draft model decreases from 100% (at δ = 0, no rejection) to 0% (at δ = 1, all steps rejected, equivalent to using the target model alone).
- At the recommended default δ = 0.7: 48% of questions are solved by the draft model alone (zero target model FLOPs), and accuracy is 84.6% — higher than both the draft-alone baseline (~73.8% at δ = 0, read from Figure 5) and the target-alone baseline (83.2% at δ = 1.0).
- The accuracy curve peaks at δ = 0.7 and declines slightly at higher δ values — δ = 0.8 achieves slightly lower accuracy (visually ~84.2%) and δ = 0.9 lower still. This non-monotonicity is important: it means that aggressive rejection (high δ, relying more on the target model) does NOT monotonically improve accuracy. The target model alone (δ = 1.0) achieves lower accuracy than RSD at δ = 0.7, consistent with the Table 2 results showing RSD outperforming the single target model. The interpretation is that the draft model sometimes produces correct reasoning steps that the target model would not have produced — when these are accepted (as they are at δ = 0.7), the system benefits; when they are rejected (at δ = 1.0), that benefit is lost.

**Figure B.1 (MATH500, RSD 1.5B/7B/7B, by difficulty level):**
- At δ = 0.7, the percentage of questions solved by draft model only is: Level 1 (easiest) — 84%, Level 2 — 67%, Level 3 — 58%, Level 4 — 44%, Level 5 (hardest) — 19%. This monotonic decrease confirms that the PRM's reward scores effectively track problem difficulty — harder problems produce lower-scoring draft steps, triggering more target model invocation.
- The accuracy improvement from involving the target model (comparing δ = 0 to, say, δ = 0.7 or the peak accuracy within each level) varies dramatically by level: Level 1 gains ~+4.7 points, Level 2 ~+5.6, Level 3 ~+6.7, Level 4 ~+16.4, Level 5 ~+15.7. The target model provides the most value on the hardest problems — exactly where the draft model's capability is most limited.
- For the easiest questions (Level 1), accuracy is already above 90% at δ = 0 and shows only minor improvement from target model involvement. The system automatically allocates very little target compute to these questions (only 16% get any target model steps at δ = 0.7), which is the correct efficiency decision — the marginal benefit of the target model on already-high-performing questions is small.

This per-level analysis is the paper's strongest empirical evidence that RSD achieves **automatic compute allocation** without explicit difficulty estimation. The PRM's per-step scores serve as implicit difficulty signals, and the threshold δ controls how much quality is demanded before paying the target model's cost. The system naturally allocates more target model compute to harder problems and harder steps, without any separate difficulty estimation or binning phase.

#### Model Merging: Reducing Deployment Complexity

Table 4 (with details in Table B.3) investigates whether merging the PRM with either the draft or target model can reduce the number of distinct models that must be served while maintaining RSD's performance advantage:

- **Merging 1.5B draft + 1.5B PRM** (using linear interpolation with MergeKit, Goddard et al., 2024): RSD with this merged model and the 7B target achieves 65.0% average accuracy, compared to 65.9% for the unmerged configuration — a -0.9 point drop, but still better than SD's 64.6%.
- **Merging 7B target + 7B PRM**: RSD with the 1.5B draft and this merged model achieves 66.7% average — +1.4 over the unmerged 7B PRM configuration (66.1%). The paper notes this is "consistent with observations reported by Yadav et al. (2024)" about model merging sometimes improving performance.
- Both merged configurations outperform SD (64.6%), meaning that the reduction from 3 served models (draft + target + PRM) to 2 (merged draft-PRM + target, or draft + merged target-PRM) is viable without sacrificing RSD's core advantage over standard speculative decoding.

The merging procedure is notably simple: only the shared transformer layers are merged (the PRM's scalar projection head and the policy model's lm_head are retained separately), with interpolation weights [0.6, 0.4] for the policy model and PRM respectively. The paper tests only this basic configuration and explicitly leaves "more advanced merging methods for future work."

#### Robustness to PRM Choice

Table 5 evaluates RSD with four different PRMs (Skywork-o1-Open-PRM-1.5B, Skywork-o1-Open-PRM-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B) using Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B draft and 7B target at δ = 0.7:

- **All four PRMs outperform SD** (64.6%): RSD with Skywork-1.5B achieves 65.9% (+1.3), Skywork-7B achieves 66.1% (+1.5), Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B achieves 65.9% (+1.3), Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B achieves 66.5% (+1.9).
- **Larger PRMs provide modest gains**: The 72B PRM improves over the 1.5B PRM by +0.6 points on average (66.5 vs. 65.9). This is a relatively small improvement for a 48× increase in PRM parameters, suggesting diminishing returns — the 1.5B PRM already captures most of the useful quality signal.
- **All PRMs produce gains on GPQA Diamond**: Skywork-1.5B achieves 38.4% vs. the target's 32.8% (+5.6); Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B achieves 33.3% (+0.5). The Skywork PRM actually outperforms the math-specialized Qwen2.5-Math PRM on GPQA — an interesting result suggesting that the general reward model's training data may cover the diverse reasoning patterns in graduate-level QA better than the math-specific PRM's training data.
- **On MMLU STEM**, the Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B achieves 75.6%, substantially higher than Skywork-7B's 72.3% — the math-specialized PRM's advantage shows on STEM-focused benchmarks with clear mathematical structure.

The paper concludes that RSD is "robust to the choice of PRM" — the gains hold across different PRM architectures and training procedures, though the magnitude varies. This is practically important because it means practitioners can use whatever strong PRM is available, without needing to train a custom one aligned to their specific draft/target pair.

#### General-Domain Extension: RSD with an Outcome Reward Model

Table 6 tests whether RSD's mechanism extends to open-ended generation where only an outcome reward model (ORM), not a process reward model, is available. Using Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (draft), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (target), and Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2 (Liu et al., 2024) as the ORM, with δ = 0:

- Draft model alone: 7.09% win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0 vs. GPT-4 Turbo.
- Target model alone: 24.47% win rate.
- RSD: 18.85% win rate — substantially better than the draft alone (+11.76 points) but still below the target model alone. The paper notes that "65% tokens are generated by the draft model only without any intervention of the target model," so the majority of generation is done cheaply, but the quality falls short of the full target model.
- The paper explicitly acknowledges this as preliminary: "We didn't extensively tune the reward threshold, and empirically chose δ = 0" and "We believe that a general-domain PRM and dedicated tuning of δ could further boost the performance."

This experiment is significant for what it reveals about RSD's boundary conditions. On structured reasoning tasks with step-by-step outputs and a trained step-level PRM, RSD can exceed the target model's accuracy. On open-ended generation with only an outcome-level reward signal, RSD improves over the draft model but does not match the target model — the quality signals provided by an ORM (evaluating complete outputs) are coarser than those from a PRM (evaluating individual reasoning steps), and the threshold-based acceptance is less well-calibrated for generation quality than for reasoning correctness.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Threshold δ (Figure 5, Table B.2):** Sweeping δ ∈ {0.0, 0.1, ..., 1.0} on MATH500 with RSD (1.5B/7B/7B) reveals that accuracy peaks at δ = 0.7 (84.6%), declines slowly above 0.7, and drops sharply below 0.6. The accuracy at δ = 0.0 (draft model only) is ~73.8%; at δ = 1.0 (target model only) is 83.2%. RSD's peak exceeds both, confirming that the mixture outperforms either model alone. Table B.2 extends the sweep across all benchmarks for both the 1.5B/7B and 7B/72B configurations, showing that δ = 0.7 is a strong universal default — it achieves the best or near-best accuracy in most settings. However, per-task optimal δ varies: for GPQA with RSD (72B/1.5B/1.5B), δ = 0.9 achieves 44.4% vs. δ = 0.7's 40.4%, a +4 point difference that highlights the value of task-specific tuning on the hardest benchmarks.

- **Weighting function choice (Figure 6):** Comparing the five weighting functions from Table 1 under comparable inference costs (RSD 1.5B/7B/7B on MATH500): binary step (δ = 0.7) achieves 84.6%, logistic (α = 1, δ = 0.6) achieves 79.2%, sigmoidal achieves 81.4%, clipping achieves 81.8%, and constant (p = 0.6) achieves 77.6%. The target model alone achieves 83.2%; the draft alone achieves ~73.8%. The key findings: (a) the constant function performs worst among all reward-aware functions, confirming that reward information is essential — random acceptance/rejection provides little benefit; (b) all smooth functions outperform the draft model but underperform the binary step function, supporting Proposition 2.3's theoretical claim that a threshold function is optimal under budget constraints; (c) the binary step function is the only one that exceeds the target model alone, suggesting that the sharp accept/reject boundary prevents the system from keeping medium-quality draft steps that are better off being regenerated by the target model.

- **PRM size (Tables 2, 5, and Figure 4):** Larger PRMs provide modest accuracy improvements: on the Qwen2.5-Math 1.5B draft / 7B target configuration, Skywork-1.5B PRM achieves 65.9% average at δ = 0.7, while Skywork-7B PRM achieves 66.1% (+0.2). The Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-72B achieves 66.5% (+0.6 over the 1.5B PRM). The pattern is consistent: larger PRMs help, but the gains are small relative to the parameter increase. The 1.5B PRM is sufficient to capture the quality signal needed for effective draft/target routing. This has practical implications — a 1.5B PRM adds minimal memory and compute overhead, making RSD deployable even when resources are tight.

- **Model merging (Tables 4, B.3):** Merging the 7B target with the 7B PRM (linear interpolation, 0.6/0.4 weights) produces a merged model that, when used as both target and PRM in RSD with a 1.5B draft, achieves 66.7% average accuracy — higher than using the unmerged 7B PRM (66.1%). Merging the 1.5B draft with the 1.5B PRM achieves 65.0% — lower than the unmerged configuration (65.9%) but still above SD (64.6%). This demonstrates that RSD can function with only 2 served models instead of 3, reducing deployment complexity. The performance improvement from merging larger models is consistent with Yadav et al. (2024), who observed that merging can sometimes provide regularization benefits that improve downstream performance.

- **Domain-specific draft model advantage (Table B.1):** On CN Middle School 24 and College Math (Yang et al., 2024b; Tang et al., 2024), the 1.5B math-specialized draft model actually outperforms the 7B general target: 75.2% vs. 72.3% on CN Middle School 24, and 48.0% vs. 46.8% on College Math. Standard SD achieves 73.3% and 46.9% respectively — worse than the draft alone in both cases, because SD's strict unbiasedness rejects the draft's correct outputs when they don't match the target's distribution. RSD at δ = 0.7 achieves 78.2% and 48.2% — exceeding both models individually, because it keeps the draft's correct outputs (which are better than the target's) while using the target model to correct the draft's errors. This is a clean demonstration of RSD's advantage over SD when the draft model is domain-specialized.

- **Difficulty-level decomposition (Figure B.1):** As described in the main results section above, the per-level analysis shows that target model involvement increases monotonically with MATH500 difficulty level at a fixed δ, and that the accuracy benefit from target involvement grows with difficulty. This is not presented as a formal ablation but serves as a validation that RSD's automatic compute allocation is genuinely adaptive — it is not simply accepting/rejecting uniformly across all problems, but is routing more compute to harder problems where it provides more value.

- **General-domain ORM experiment (Table 6):** Using an ORM instead of a PRM on AlpacaEval, RSD (δ = 0) achieves 18.85% win rate — substantially above the draft model (7.09%) but below the target model (24.47%). The experiment demonstrates that RSD's mechanism functions without a step-level PRM (the acceptance/rejection still works with an outcome-level reward signal), but the quality ceiling is lower. The paper does not ablate different ORM thresholds or compare against alternative ORM-based selection strategies (e.g., Best-of-N with the ORM), which would help contextualize the 18.85% result.

- **Negative result: ReST^EM-trained revision model degrades (not in this paper, but mentioned for context):** The paper does not include this experiment — it is a finding from the reference paper (Snell et al., 2024) and is not replicated here. I note this only because the prior summary incorrectly attributed it to this paper. There is no revision model training or ReST^EM experiment in the RSD paper.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: RSD delivers up to 4.4× fewer FLOPs while achieving better accuracy than the target model alone.**

*What was tested:* The claim is evaluated on MATH500 (Figure 4), where RSD (7B/72B/7B) achieves 88.0% accuracy vs. the 72B target's 85.6%, with the FLOPs ratio read from the x-axis. The FLOPs calculation uses the standard 2N-per-token approximation and includes draft, PRM, and target costs.

*What the experiments demonstrate:* The claim is **quantitatively supported** for this specific configuration on this specific benchmark. The 4.4× figure comes from comparing the FLOPs at the RSD (7B/72B/7B) point against the Target (72B) point in Figure 4.

*What is not demonstrated / genuine weaknesses:*
- The FLOPs analysis is only shown for MATH500 (Figure 4). The paper states RSD achieves efficiency gains on all benchmarks (Table 2 shows accuracy improvements), but the actual FLOPs savings on GSM8K, Olympiad Bench, GPQA, etc. are not quantified. Given that the step counts and acceptance rates likely vary across benchmarks (GPQA has different reasoning patterns than MATH), the 4.4× figure may not generalize. A table or figure showing FLOPs-accuracy tradeoffs for at least one additional benchmark would substantially strengthen this claim.
- The FLOPs calculation ignores the memory access and communication overhead of switching between three different models (draft, PRM, target) during decoding. In practice, loading and unloading model weights between GPU memory and compute units incurs latency that the FLOPs count does not capture. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by using FLOPs rather than wall-clock time, but the claim of "4.4× fewer FLOPs" should be understood as a FLOPs reduction, not necessarily a 4.4× wall-clock speedup. Actual throughput may be limited by the need to keep three models in memory or pay context-switching costs.
- The 72B target model uses greedy decoding (temperature = 0). In many production deployments, the target model would be used with sampling (temperature > 0) and potentially with its own test-time compute strategies. The comparison is between RSD (which uses a draft model and selective target intervention) and the target model in its simplest possible configuration. A fairer baseline might give the target model some fraction of RSD's total FLOP budget — say, allowing the 72B model to generate multiple samples with majority voting or Best-of-N up to the same FLOPs cost as RSD. The paper's BoN comparison partially addresses this: RSD (7B/72B/7B) outperforms BoN (7B/7B, N=64) at lower cost, but BoN with the 72B model itself is not tested.

**Claim 2: RSD improves accuracy over parallel decoding methods by up to +3.5 points on average.**

*What was tested:* Table 2 compares RSD accuracy against multiple baselines (majority voting, Best-of-N, SD, single target) across six benchmarks and three model families. The "+3.5" figure comes from comparing RSD against SD (not against all parallel decoding methods). Specifically, looking at the Qwen2.5-Instruct results: RSD (1.5B/1.5B, δ*) achieves 62.6% average vs. SD's 59.1% — a difference of +3.5 points.

*What the experiments demonstrate:* The claim is **numerically accurate** for the stated comparison, but "+3.5" is the maximum observed gap, not the typical gap. The improvement over SD varies by configuration: +1.0 for Qwen2.5-Math 1.5B/7B, +2.7 for Qwen2.5-Instruct 1.5B/7B, +1.0 for Llama-3.1 1B/8B. The "+3.5" figure cherry-picks the best case. A more representative summary would be "RSD outperforms SD by +0.8 to +2.7 points on average, depending on the model family."

*What is not demonstrated:*
- The comparison with parallel decoding methods other than SD is mixed. RSD outperforms majority voting and Best-of-N with the draft model by large margins (e.g., +3.0 over maj@16, +1.2 over BoN N=16 for Qwen2.5-Math), but these baselines use only the draft model — they are not competing on equal footing since RSD has access to the target model. A comparison against Best-of-N with the target model at comparable FLOPs would be more informative about whether RSD's routing mechanism is better than simply sampling more from the target model.
- Standard SD is evaluated at temperature = 0, top-p = 1 with 7 speculative tokens. The paper does not ablate the number of speculative tokens or the decoding parameters — it is possible that SD's underperformance is partly due to suboptimal SD hyperparameters rather than a fundamental limitation of unbiased speculative decoding. Prior work (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023a) shows that SD's performance is sensitive to the number of speculative tokens and the alignment between draft and target distributions; the paper's conclusion that "SD underperforms in practice" may be specific to the chosen SD configuration.

**Claim 3: RSD serves as an automatic compute allocation mechanism, routing less compute to easy questions and more to hard questions.**

*What was tested:* Figure 5 shows that at δ = 0.7, 48% of MATH500 questions are solved by the draft model alone. Figure B.1 breaks this down by difficulty level, showing the draft-only fraction decreases from 84% (Level 1) to 19% (Level 5).

*What the experiments demonstrate:* The claim is **qualitatively supported** — the target model is indeed invoked more often on harder questions. The monotonic relationship between difficulty level and target model usage is clear and consistent with the claim.

*What is not demonstrated:*
- The difficulty levels in Figure B.1 are MATH500's human-annotated difficulty labels, which are based on problem complexity, not on the model's actual performance. The paper does not compare against an oracle compute allocation (e.g., one that knows exactly which steps the draft model gets wrong) to quantify how close RSD's allocation is to optimal. Without an optimality benchmark, we can say RSD is "adaptive" but not necessarily "compute-optimal."
- The paper does not compare RSD's implicit compute allocation against an explicit difficulty-estimation approach (e.g., generating a few samples first to estimate difficulty, then allocating compute as in Snell et al., 2024). Such a comparison would reveal whether the PRM's online quality assessments are as effective as dedicated difficulty estimation — or whether the overhead of explicit estimation is justified by better allocation decisions.
- The compute allocation is entirely controlled by a single threshold δ. This means the system cannot distinguish between "this step is hard because the draft model is weak on this type of reasoning" and "this step is hard because the problem is genuinely difficult." A more sophisticated allocation might use different strategies for these two cases (e.g., invoke the target model for the former, try a different draft sampling strategy for the latter), but RSD's binary accept/reject mechanism cannot make this distinction.

**Claim 4: RSD is robust across model families, PRMs, and benchmarks.**

*What was tested:* Table 2 covers three model families and six benchmarks. Table 5 covers four PRMs. Table B.1 covers two additional benchmarks (CN Middle School 24, College Math). The general-domain extension (Table 6) tests a different task format.

*What the experiments demonstrate:* The core pattern — RSD outperforms SD and the draft model, and often outperforms the target model — is **replicated across all three model families on all six reasoning benchmarks**. The magnitude of improvement varies, but the direction is consistent. RSD with δ = 0.7 outperforms the target model alone in 7 out of 8 configurations in Table 2 (the exception being Qwen2.5-Math-7B target at δ = 0.7 with 1.5B PRM, which achieves 65.9% vs. the target's 65.3% — a marginal +0.6 that is positive but small). The PRM robustness experiment (Table 5) shows that all four tested PRMs produce positive gains over SD, with the magnitude varying from +1.3 to +1.9.

*What is not demonstrated / genuine weaknesses:*
- **Test set sizes are small and results lack statistical rigor.** MATH500 has 500 questions; GPQA Diamond has a subset of GPQA (exact size not specified in the paper, but GPQA typically has ~500 total questions, with Diamond being the hardest subset). MMLU STEM, Olympiad Bench, and GaoKao-2023-En similarly have test sets likely in the hundreds. With no reported confidence intervals or standard deviations, the reliability of small accuracy differences (e.g., 66.1 vs. 65.9, a 0.2-point gap in Table 5) is unknown. A 0.2-point difference on a 500-question test corresponds to exactly 1 question — results at this granularity are highly sensitive to individual question variation.
- **The δ threshold is tuned on the test benchmarks themselves** (Table B.2). The δ* results are oracle-tuned — the best threshold is selected after observing test-set performance. This means the δ* results represent an upper bound that may not be achievable in deployment where test labels are unavailable. The paper mitigates this by also reporting δ = 0.7 (a fixed threshold) results, which are generally strong, but the gap between δ = 0.7 and δ* (up to 1.3 points in some configurations, e.g., GPQA for RSD 72B/1.5B/1.5B: 40.4% vs. 44.4%) indicates that per-task tuning provides non-trivial gains that would not be available in a truly zero-shot deployment.
- **The benchmarks are overwhelmingly math and science reasoning tasks.** GSM8K, MATH500, Olympiad Bench, GaoKao-2023-En, and MMLU STEM are all quantitative/scientific reasoning benchmarks. GPQA Diamond is the only benchmark that includes non-STEM graduate-level questions (though it is still heavily science-focused). The general-domain experiment on AlpacaEval is preliminary and shows RSD underperforming the target model. The claim of robustness "across diverse reasoning tasks" should be qualified: the evidence supports robustness across **math and science reasoning tasks** specifically. Whether RSD works for legal reasoning, commonsense reasoning, multi-hop question answering, or other reasoning types is not tested.
- **All PRMs tested are trained on similar data distributions** (math and science reasoning, from Skywork and Qwen teams). A truly out-of-distribution PRM — trained on code generation, for example — might perform differently. The paper's "robustness to PRM choice" is better characterized as robustness across different model scales and training procedures within the same domain (math/science PRMs), not robustness across PRMs with fundamentally different training objectives.

**Missing experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

1. **Wall-clock latency measurements.** The paper reports only FLOPs. The three-model architecture (draft, PRM, target) may incur context-switching costs that FLOPs alone do not capture, especially if the models do not all fit in GPU memory simultaneously. Wall-clock time on standard hardware would make the efficiency claims more practically interpretable.

2. **Comparison against target model with equivalent FLOP budget.** A natural baseline: give the target model a Best-of-N or majority voting budget matching RSD's total FLOPs, and compare accuracy. This would test whether RSD's selective draft usage is genuinely better than simply running the target model fewer times but with sampling diversity.

3. **Ablation of step segmentation strategy.** The use of `\n\n` as step boundaries is a heuristic. How sensitive is RSD to the segmentation? What if steps are segmented by sentence, or by fixed token counts? The PRM's scoring may depend on whether it receives complete logical units vs. text fragments.

4. **Analysis of rejected steps.** What kinds of draft steps get rejected? Are they genuinely incorrect, or are they correct but stylistically different from what the PRM expects? A qualitative analysis of rejection patterns would illuminate whether RSD's filtering is removing errors (good) or just suppressing valid-but-unconventional reasoning (potentially problematic).

5. **Combining RSD with SD for rejected steps.** Section 4 mentions that "SD (draft+target) can be utilized to regenerate the step" when a draft step is rejected, but this is not experimentally evaluated. Given that SD is the standard acceleration method, quantifying whether RSD+SD outperforms RSD with full target model regeneration would be valuable.

6. **Sensitivity to PRM calibration.** The PRM outputs scores in [0, 1], and δ = 0.7 is used as the default threshold. But what if the PRM is miscalibrated — e.g., systematically overconfident on certain step types? An experiment that artificially perturbs PRM scores (adding noise, applying monotonic transforms) would test whether the threshold mechanism is robust to PRM calibration errors, or whether it depends on well-calibrated absolute scores.

7. **Iterative rejection handling.** When the target model replaces a rejected draft step, that target-generated step is accepted unconditionally. What if the target model also produces a poor step? An experiment with multiple rounds of rejection (draft → target → draft again, or target → target) on the hardest problems might reveal whether additional target model invocations can rescue particularly difficult reasoning steps.

In summary, the paper's experiments provide **strong evidence for RSD's effectiveness on math and science reasoning benchmarks across multiple model families**, but the efficiency claims would be strengthened by wall-clock measurements and broader benchmark coverage, the robustness claims require qualification (math/science reasoning specifically, with PRMs trained on similar domains), and several natural baselines (target model with equivalent FLOP budget, combined RSD+SD, iterative rejection) remain untested. The paper's central innovation — replacing distribution-matching with quality-based acceptance in speculative decoding — is convincingly validated by the consistent accuracy improvements over standard SD across all tested configurations, which is the key comparison since SD uses exactly the same models but with a different acceptance criterion.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Efficiency Gains Are Measured in FLOPs, Not Wall-Clock Latency — and the Three-Model Architecture May Incur Substantial Context-Switching Overhead

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper quantifies all efficiency improvements using FLOPs (floating-point operations), computed via the standard 2N-per-inference-token approximation for transformer models (Section 3.3, Figure 4). FLOPs capture total arithmetic operations but ignore memory access patterns, GPU utilization, model loading overhead, and the serial dependencies that govern wall-clock latency. RSD requires three distinct models — draft, PRM, and target — to be available during decoding, with the PRM invoked at every reasoning step (~18 times per MATH500 question) and the target model invoked selectively on rejected steps.

The paper does not report any wall-clock timing measurements, throughput figures, or latency benchmarks. All efficiency claims — including the headline "4.4× fewer FLOPs" — are stated purely in FLOP terms. The paper acknowledges the PRM's computational cost only in FLOP terms (Section 4: "adding an additional PRM for RSD incurs minimal overhead compared to SD"), without addressing whether context-switching between three separate models introduces latency that FLOPs do not capture.

**The consequence.** If the draft, PRM, and target models do not all fit simultaneously in GPU memory, the system must swap models in and out of memory during decoding. Each swap involves loading model weights from CPU memory or disk into GPU VRAM — a process whose latency is dominated by memory bandwidth, not FLOPs. The draft model is invoked at every step; the PRM is invoked at every step; the target model is invoked intermittently. The sequence of operations is inherently serialized: generate draft step → score with PRM → decide to invoke target model or not → append to context → repeat. The PRM's forward pass itself may be cheap in FLOPs (equivalent to ~1 token of computation), but if the PRM resides in a different memory space from the draft model, the overhead of switching between them could dominate the per-step cost.

This means the "4.4× fewer FLOPs" figure cannot be directly translated to "4.4× faster wall-clock generation." In the worst case — e.g., on a single-GPU deployment where only one model fits in memory at a time — the model-switching overhead could eliminate most or all of the FLOPs advantage. The paper's efficiency claims are therefore best understood as an **upper bound** on achievable speedup, requiring hardware configurations where all three models can be co-resident in memory with negligible context-switching cost.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper contains zero wall-clock measurements, zero latency analysis, and zero discussion of memory requirements or model-loading overhead. The FLOPs-accuracy figure (Figure 4) is the only efficiency quantification. Section 4's discussion of PRM overhead is purely FLOPs-based. The inference backend (vLLM, Kwon et al., 2023) is mentioned (Section 3) but no details are given about how models are managed in memory during RSD's multi-model decoding — whether they are co-resident, swapped, or served from separate devices.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the latency implications of its three-model architecture, propose strategies for reducing context-switching overhead (e.g., model merging, Section 4's preliminary experiments notwithstanding), or provide guidance on hardware requirements for achieving the reported FLOPs savings in wall-clock time. The model merging experiment (Tables 4, B.3) reduces the number of served models from 3 to 2, which would mitigate context-switching overhead, but the paper does not connect this experiment to latency considerations or measure whether the merged models actually improve wall-clock performance. A practitioner evaluating whether RSD is suitable for their deployment cannot determine, from the reported results, what actual speedup to expect on their hardware.

### Limitation 2: The Hardest Problems Remain Largely Unaddressed — RSD's Gains Concentrate on Problems the Draft Model Can Partially Solve

**The assumption or constraint.** RSD's mechanism depends on the draft model producing reasoning steps that are sometimes correct and sometimes incorrect. The PRM distinguishes between these cases, and the target model replaces the incorrect ones. This implies that RSD improves upon the draft model by **filtering** (keeping good draft steps) and **replacing** (substituting high-quality target steps for poor draft steps). But for problems where the draft model produces essentially no correct intermediate reasoning — where the PRM assigns low scores to nearly every step — RSD degenerates to running the target model on every step, because every draft step is rejected.

The paper's Figure B.1 quantifies this boundary explicitly: at δ = 0.7 on MATH500 Level 5 questions (the hardest quintile), the draft model alone solves only 19% of questions (meaning the target model is invoked on at least some steps for 81% of Level 5 questions). Even with target model intervention, the accuracy improvement on Level 5 is from approximately 50% (draft alone, read from Figure B.1) to approximately 65% (with target model, read from Figure B.1 at the peak δ value). This +15.7 point gain is substantial in absolute terms but still leaves ~35% of the hardest questions incorrect — a failure rate far higher than on easier questions.

**The consequence.** RSD does not solve the problem of reasoning on questions that are fundamentally beyond the draft model's capability. For problems where the draft model has near-zero chance of generating correct intermediate steps, the PRM will reject nearly every draft step, and RSD reduces to running the target model — but at higher cost than the target model alone, because the system still pays for draft model generation and PRM evaluation on every rejected step. In this regime, RSD is **strictly worse than using the target model directly**: it achieves the same accuracy (since all draft steps are rejected) but with higher FLOPs (draft + PRM costs on top of target costs).

This failure mode is not hypothetical. On MATH500 Level 5, the 19% draft-only question rate at δ = 0.7 means that for the other 81% of Level 5 questions, the target model is invoked on at least some steps — and for a subset of those questions, the target model may be invoked on nearly every step. The per-step cost in this regime is: draft model generation + PRM evaluation + target model generation = more than the target model alone would cost. The paper's aggregate FLOPs advantage (Figure 4) masks this per-question heterogeneity: RSD saves FLOPs on easy and medium questions (where the draft model handles most steps) but may actually cost MORE on the hardest questions (where the draft model is consistently rejected). The overall 4.4× FLOP reduction is an average that includes both questions where RSD uses nearly zero target FLOPs and questions where RSD uses more FLOPs than the target model alone.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure B.1 provides the most direct evidence. The draft-only rate drops from 84% (Level 1) to 19% (Level 5) at δ = 0.7, demonstrating that target model usage increases sharply with difficulty. However, the paper does not report per-level FLOP costs, the fraction of Level 5 questions where RSD uses MORE FLOPs than the target alone, or the accuracy of RSD vs. target alone specifically on Level 5. The aggregate FLOPs-accuracy plot (Figure 4) merges all difficulty levels, obscuring the cost penalty on the hardest subset. The accuracy tables (Table 2) similarly report only aggregate benchmark scores, not per-level breakdowns (Figure B.1 is the only difficulty-level analysis, and it reports accuracy and draft-only rates but not FLOPs).

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged, not addressed. The paper's analysis of Figure B.1 explicitly notes that "harder questions need more involvement of the target model" and treats this as evidence that RSD achieves automatic compute allocation — more compute to harder problems. But the paper does not discuss the regime where this allocation degenerates into paying more than the target model alone. Section 4's discussion of future work mentions combining RSD with SD for rejected steps, which would reduce the cost of rejected-step regeneration, but this is not explored experimentally and would not change the fundamental issue that on very hard problems, the draft model provides no useful signal and its invocation becomes pure overhead. A practical deployment would benefit from a **gating mechanism** that detects when a problem is too hard for the draft model and falls back to target-only decoding entirely, avoiding the wasted cycles of draft generation and PRM evaluation. The paper does not develop or evaluate such a mechanism.

### Limitation 3: The Threshold δ Is Tuned on Test Benchmarks — Reported δ\* Results Are Oracle-Tuned Upper Bounds, and Cross-Task Transfer of δ Is Not Established

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports two sets of RSD results: those using a fixed threshold δ = 0.7 (applied uniformly across all benchmarks) and those using per-task optimized δ\* (swept on each benchmark to find the best accuracy, reported in Table B.2). The δ\* results represent what RSD achieves when the threshold is selected with knowledge of test-set performance — effectively, an oracle tuning procedure that is not available in deployment. The paper's headline average accuracy improvements (e.g., "+1.2" for RSD with Qwen2.5-Math 7B target at δ\*) are based on oracle-tuned thresholds.

The paper does not use a held-out validation set for threshold selection, cross-validation within benchmarks, or any procedure that would estimate how well a threshold chosen on one task transfers to another. The δ = 0.7 results serve as the zero-tuning baseline, but δ = 0.7 was itself selected based on sweeps showing it works well across the evaluated benchmarks — it was not chosen a priori from a principled criterion.

**The consequence.** The δ\* results represent an **overestimate of RSD's achievable accuracy in deployment**. A practitioner deploying RSD on a new task does not have access to test-set labels to tune δ. They must either use a fixed default (like δ = 0.7) or tune δ on a separate validation set constructed from the target task distribution. The gap between δ = 0.7 and δ\* in Table 2 indicates how much accuracy is left on the table when using the fixed default:

- For Qwen2.5-Math 7B target with 1.5B PRM: δ = 0.7 achieves 65.9% average, δ\* achieves 66.3% — a +0.4 gap.
- For Qwen2.5-Instruct 7B target with 1.5B PRM: δ = 0.7 achieves 61.8%, δ\* achieves 62.6% — a +0.8 gap.
- On individual benchmarks, the gap can be larger: GPQA Diamond with RSD (72B/1.5B/1.5B) shows δ = 0.7 at 40.4% vs. δ\* at 44.4% (Table B.2) — a +4.0 point gap, suggesting that the optimal threshold for GPQA is substantially different from the MATH500-tuned default.

The cross-task variability of the optimal δ is itself a concern. The per-task δ\* values in Table B.2 range from 0.6 to 0.9 across different benchmarks, even within the same model family. For RSD (7B/1.5B/1.5B): δ\* = 0.6 for GaoKao-2023-En, 0.7 for MATH500, 0.8 for GSM8K, and 0.9 for MMLU STEM (read from Table B.2's accuracy peaks). This variation means that no single fixed δ is optimal across all tasks. The paper does not provide guidance on how to predict the best δ for a new task without labeled validation data, nor does it analyze what task properties (difficulty distribution, step count, domain) predict the optimal threshold.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table B.2 provides the raw sweep data showing δ\* variation across benchmarks. Table 2 reports both δ = 0.7 and δ\* results, allowing the reader to see the gap. Figure 5 shows the sweep on MATH500 and the accuracy peak at δ = 0.7. The paper does not report cross-validation results, validation-set tuning experiments, or analysis of whether a δ chosen on one benchmark transfers to others.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the inclusion of δ = 0.7 results alongside δ\* results. The paper's practice of reporting both numbers is transparent — the reader can see what is achievable with a fixed threshold versus oracle tuning. The fixed δ = 0.7 results outperform the target model alone in 7 out of 8 configurations in Table 2, demonstrating that a well-chosen default does not require per-task tuning to be beneficial. However, this "well-chosen default" was itself selected with knowledge of the full benchmark suite — the paper swept δ on MATH500 (Figure 5) to pick δ = 0.7, then validated it on the other benchmarks. This is a reasonable engineering choice but leaves open the question of whether δ = 0.7 would be optimal (or even good) for an entirely new task distribution. The paper suggests δ = 0.7 "works well universally" (Table 5 caption) based on its performance across the evaluated benchmarks, but does not test this claim on held-out tasks or domains beyond the six reasoning benchmarks.

### Limitation 4: Evaluation Is Confined to Math and Science Reasoning Benchmarks with Step-Segmentable Outputs — General-Domain Applicability Is Not Established, and the Preliminary ORM Experiment Shows RSD Underperforms the Target Model

**The assumption or constraint.** RSD's design depends critically on two properties of the task and model outputs: (1) the existence of a process reward model that can evaluate intermediate reasoning steps for quality, and (2) the ability to segment generated text into discrete reasoning steps (using the `\n\n` delimiter heuristic). The paper's entire empirical validation — six benchmarks totaling thousands of questions — consists exclusively of math and science reasoning tasks (GSM8K, MATH500, MMLU STEM, Olympiad Bench, GaoKao-2023-En, GPQA Diamond, Minerva Math). These tasks share key properties: they have unambiguous correct answers, their solution processes follow structured step-by-step reasoning, the models tested produce outputs with clear paragraph breaks between logical steps, and open-source PRMs trained specifically for math/science reasoning evaluation are readily available.

The paper includes one general-domain experiment (Table 6) using AlpacaEval, an open-ended instruction-following benchmark, with an outcome reward model (ORM) instead of a PRM. The results show RSD achieving 18.85% win rate — substantially above the draft model (7.09%) but below the target model alone (24.47%). The paper explicitly acknowledges this is preliminary, noting that "We didn't extensively tune the reward threshold, and empirically chose δ = 0" and that "a general-domain PRM and dedicated tuning of δ could further boost the performance."

**The consequence.** The paper's positive results — particularly the headline claim that RSD can outperform the target model while using fewer FLOPs — have only been demonstrated for a narrow class of tasks: structured math and science reasoning with clearly delineated intermediate steps, evaluated by domain-specific PRMs. This does not establish that RSD works for:
- **Open-ended generation** (dialogue, creative writing, summarization) where no step boundaries exist and output quality is multi-dimensional.
- **Code generation** where reasoning steps might be functions or code blocks rather than natural language paragraphs.
- **Multi-hop question answering** where reasoning steps might be implicit rather than explicitly delimited.
- **Tasks requiring factual knowledge** where the quality of a step depends on external world knowledge rather than logical deduction from the context.
- **Tasks evaluated by ORMs rather than PRMs**, where the reward signal is coarser (whole-output evaluation) and less informative for per-step routing.

The AlpacaEval experiment (Table 6) is the only evidence regarding these domains, and it shows RSD underperforming the target model. The paper attributes this to the lack of a PRM and lack of threshold tuning, but these are precisely the conditions that would obtain in a general-domain deployment — there are no widely available general-domain PRMs, and threshold tuning on a new task requires labeled validation data. The 18.85% win rate, while better than the draft model, is substantially worse than the 24.47% from the target model and worse than what one might achieve by simply using the target model with the same total FLOP budget.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 6 provides the only general-domain evidence. The paper's reasoning benchmarks (Tables 2-5, Figures 4-6) are all math/science. Section 4's discussion of future work mentions "a general-domain PRM" as a gap: "To the best of our knowledge, there is not yet a PRM for general-domain generation." The `\n\n` segmentation heuristic is described in the default setting (Section 3) as applying to "process Best-of-N, beam search and RSD," but the paper does not discuss how this heuristic would need to change for tasks where models do not produce `\n\n`-delimited reasoning steps, or what happens when the heuristic fails to produce meaningful step boundaries.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the domain limitation — the AlpacaEval experiment is included specifically to test general-domain applicability, and the result (RSD < target model) is reported honestly without being buried. The discussion section (Section 4) explicitly acknowledges the lack of general-domain PRMs as a current limitation and suggests that if such PRMs were developed, RSD could be applied more broadly. However, this is a pointer to future infrastructure rather than a mitigation of the current limitation. The paper does not propose or evaluate alternative step-segmentation strategies for domains without natural paragraph breaks, nor does it explore whether token-level reward signals (e.g., from a critic model) could substitute for step-level PRM scores in domains where step boundaries are ambiguous. A practitioner considering RSD for a non-math deployment currently has no evidence that it will outperform — or even match — the target model alone, and the one data point (AlpacaEval) suggests it may not.

### Limitation 5: The Draft Model's Correct Outputs Are Accepted, but the Draft Model Also Sees Only Accepted Steps — Errors That Pass the PRM Threshold Contaminate the Entire Subsequent Reasoning Trajectory

**The assumption or constraint.** In RSD's decoding loop (Algorithm 1), the draft model generates step i conditioned on the context $z_i = [x, y_{1:i-1}]$, which contains only **accepted** steps — all previously rejected draft steps are discarded, and the draft model never sees them. Similarly, the PRM evaluates the draft step within this accepted-only context. This design ensures that the reasoning trajectory consists entirely of steps that passed the PRM's quality threshold (plus unconditionally accepted target model steps). However, it also means that if the PRM erroneously accepts a flawed reasoning step — a false positive in the acceptance decision — that error becomes permanently embedded in the context for all subsequent steps.

The PRM is not perfect. It was not trained specifically on the draft model's output distribution (the paper uses off-the-shelf PRMs), and its quality assessments can be wrong. A draft step that scores above the threshold δ but contains a subtle mathematical error — say, a sign flip that seems plausible but is incorrect — would be accepted and committed to the reasoning trajectory. The draft model, conditioned on this erroneous context, may compound the error in subsequent steps. The PRM, evaluating subsequent steps in the context of the earlier error, may fail to detect the compounding errors because the later steps are logically consistent with the (incorrect) earlier step. The paper provides no mechanism for detecting or recovering from these false-positive acceptances after the fact.

**The consequence.** False-positive acceptances introduce **unrecoverable errors** into the reasoning chain. Standard speculative decoding also commits accepted tokens permanently, but its acceptance criterion is based on the target model's probabilities — if the target model would have produced a different (correct) token, the draft token is rejected with calibrated probability. RSD's PRM-based criterion has no such calibration guarantee. The PRM might assign a score of 0.75 to a step that is actually incorrect (above the δ = 0.7 threshold), leading to acceptance of an error that propagates through the rest of the solution.

This is particularly dangerous because RSD is designed to use the target model on steps it "knows" are problematic (low PRM score), but it has no mechanism for revisiting steps it "thought" were good (high PRM score but actually wrong). The target model is only invoked on rejected steps — there is no retroactive correction of earlier accepted-but-incorrect steps. If step 3 of a 10-step solution contains an error that the PRM misses, steps 4–10 may be logically consistent but ultimately produce a wrong answer, and the target model — even if invoked on steps 7 or 8 — cannot correct the root error at step 3 because it only sees the accepted context up to that point.

The paper does not quantify the false-positive rate of the PRM at different thresholds, so the magnitude of this problem is unknown. At δ = 0.7, the fact that RSD achieves higher accuracy than the target model alone on MATH500 (84.6% vs. 83.2%, Table 2) suggests that false-positive acceptances are not so frequent as to outweigh the benefits of keeping correct draft steps. But the accuracy gap is small (+1.4 points), meaning even a modest false-positive rate could be eating into potential gains.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not directly measure PRM false-positive rates, analyze accepted-but-incorrect steps, or compare the types of errors made by RSD versus the target model alone. Figure 3 (left and middle) shows that the target model has higher reward scores than the draft model on average (supporting the PRM's discriminative ability), but this is an aggregate statistic that does not reveal false-positive rates. The accuracy results in Table 2 are consistent with false-positive acceptances being a real but not catastrophic problem (RSD outperforms the target despite them), but no direct evidence is provided.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the possibility of PRM false positives, propose mechanisms for detecting or recovering from them (e.g., backtracking when later steps receive consistently low scores, maintaining multiple candidate trajectories, or using the target model to verify accepted draft steps after the fact), or analyze whether specific failure modes in RSD's outputs can be traced to early-step false-positive acceptances. The unconditional acceptance of target model steps (Algorithm 1) means there is no verification of the target model's replacements either — the system trusts the target model absolutely, and if the target model makes an error when regenerating a rejected step, that error also enters the trajectory permanently. This is a fundamental architectural limitation: RSD assumes the PRM and target model are sufficiently reliable that their accepted outputs need no further verification, and it provides no recourse when this assumption fails.

### Limitation 6: RSD Is Compared Against a Weak Speculative Decoding Baseline — SD's Underperformance May Be Partly Due to Suboptimal Configuration (7 Speculative Tokens, Temperature 0, No Tree Speculation) Rather Than a Fundamental Flaw of Unbiased Decoding

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central comparative claim is that RSD outperforms standard speculative decoding because RSD's reward-guided controlled bias is superior to SD's strict unbiasedness constraint for reasoning tasks. This claim depends on SD being evaluated fairly — i.e., with a configuration that represents the best achievable performance of unbiased speculative decoding. The paper evaluates SD with a fixed configuration: 7 speculative tokens, temperature = 0, top-p = 1 (Section 3). No ablation of the number of speculative tokens is performed; no comparison against tree-based speculative decoding (Miao et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024b) is conducted; no alternative SD rejection schemes (e.g., using different divergence measures beyond likelihood ratio) are tested.

The choice of 7 speculative tokens is a standard default in the speculative decoding literature, but the optimal number depends on the alignment between draft and target model distributions. For reasoning tasks where the draft model produces long, structured steps, 7 tokens may be too few — the draft model might produce a correct multi-token reasoning fragment that gets truncated by the token limit, forcing a target model verification before the step is complete. Alternatively, 7 tokens may be too many if the draft and target diverge early, causing most draft tokens to be rejected and wasting target model computation on verification of tokens that will not be accepted. The paper does not provide any sensitivity analysis or optimization of SD's hyperparameters.

**The consequence.** If SD's underperformance is partly attributable to suboptimal configuration rather than a fundamental limitation of unbiased decoding, then the paper overstates the advantage of reward-guided bias over distribution-matching. A better-tuned SD configuration — e.g., with more or fewer speculative tokens, with tree-based speculation to increase acceptance rates, or with an acceptance scheme that uses the PRM alongside likelihood ratios — might close some or all of the gap between SD and RSD. The paper's conclusion that "strict unbiasedness leads to worse performance compared to the draft model" (Section 3.1) and that RSD's bias is beneficial could be weakened if SD were fairly optimized.

This is particularly relevant because the paper's own discussion (Section 2.4) acknowledges that RSD and SD can be combined — "RSD is not inherently opposed to SD; in fact, they can be seamlessly combined to enhance efficiency" — and suggests using SD to regenerate rejected steps. But SD is never evaluated in any configuration other than the single default, making it unclear whether the RSD-vs-SD comparison is a comparison of acceptance criteria (reward vs. likelihood ratio) or a comparison of decoding architectures (step-level rejection + target regeneration vs. token-level verification).

Additionally, tree-based speculative decoding methods (which generate multiple draft candidates in parallel and verify them jointly) have been shown to substantially increase acceptance rates over standard single-sequence SD. The paper does not compare against these methods, which could narrow or eliminate SD's efficiency disadvantage by reducing the number of rejected tokens. If tree-based SD achieves higher acceptance rates on reasoning tasks than standard SD, the efficiency gap between RSD and the best available unbiased decoding would be smaller than the paper reports.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** SD results are reported in Table 2 for all configurations, and in Figure 4 for MATH500. The SD configuration (7 speculative tokens, temperature 0, top-p 1) is described in Section 3. No ablation of SD hyperparameters is performed. No comparison against tree-based SD is made. The paper's discussion of combining RSD with SD (Section 4) is purely speculative — no experimental results are reported.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge that SD's underperformance might be partly due to configuration, does not perform sensitivity analysis on SD hyperparameters, and does not compare against tree-based or other advanced SD variants. The fixed SD configuration is treated as representative of what unbiased speculative decoding can achieve, but the literature on speculative decoding (Miao et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2024b) demonstrates substantial variation in performance depending on the drafting strategy, speculation length, and verification algorithm. A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt RSD or invest in better-tuning of standard SD would find no guidance in this paper, because the comparison is between optimized RSD (with tuned δ) and a single fixed SD configuration. The paper's claim that SD's strict unbiasedness is the root cause of its underperformance, rather than suboptimal hyperparameter selection, is plausible but not rigorously demonstrated.

A related concern is that the paper's SD implementation may suffer from the floating-point error issue it cites from Chen et al. (2023a) — the paper notes that "SD often underperforms in practice" due to floating-point errors, implying that part of SD's accuracy deficit is an implementation artifact rather than a theoretical limitation of unbiased decoding. If this is the case, then comparing RSD against a numerically robust SD implementation would be a fairer test of the reward-vs-likelihood acceptance criterion. The paper does not report what steps it took to mitigate floating-point errors in SD or whether the observed SD accuracy deficit is consistent with known floating-point issues in speculative decoding implementations.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

RSD introduces a conceptual shift in how the field thinks about efficient decoding for reasoning tasks: it reframes speculative decoding from a **distribution-matching problem** into a **quality-control problem**. This is not a paradigm shift in the sense of overturning foundational assumptions — the core draft-then-verify architecture remains — but it is a substantive reframing that changes what efficiency means and what the draft model's role is.

Before this paper, the speculative decoding literature operated under what we might call the **unbiasedness orthodoxy**: the objective was to recover the target model's output distribution exactly while spending fewer FLOPs. This framing made sense when the target model was the gold standard and any deviation was potential degradation. RSD challenges this orthodoxy by demonstrating that for reasoning tasks, the target model is NOT a gold standard — it is simply one source of outputs, and the draft model can sometimes produce better ones. The evidence is direct: on CN Middle School 24, a 1.5B math-specialized draft outperforms a 7B general target (75.2% vs. 72.3%), and standard SD's unbiasedness forces the final distribution toward the weaker model, yielding 73.3% — worse than the draft alone (Table B.1). RSD achieves 78.2% by ignoring distributional alignment and keeping correct draft outputs regardless of what the target model would have produced.

This reframing has immediate consequences for how the field values different components of the inference stack:

**It elevates the process reward model from a training-time or search-time tool into a runtime infrastructure component.** PRMs were previously used either as training signals (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024) or as search guides within a single model's output space (Chen et al., 2024a; Snell et al., 2024). RSD deploys the PRM as an active inference-time router that makes per-step decisions about which model to invoke. This is a new role that creates demand for lightweight, fast PRMs optimized not just for accuracy but for inference latency — the 1.5B PRM used in the paper costs ~3 GFLOPS per invocation, and the fact that it provides most of the benefit of a 72B PRM (Table 5: 65.9% vs. 66.5% average) means the field should invest in making small PRMs even more discriminative rather than scaling them up.

**It redefines the draft model as a first-class reasoning component rather than a speed-hack.** In standard SD, the draft model exists purely to reduce target model forward passes — it has no independent authority over outputs. In RSD, the draft model's outputs are the primary generation on nearly half of MATH500 questions (48% at δ = 0.7, Figure 5) and an even larger fraction on easier problems (84% of Level 1 questions, Figure B.1). This means the draft model's quality directly determines end-to-end accuracy on a large fraction of problems. The implication for model selection is that the draft model should be chosen not just for speed (token generation rate) but for **complementary reasoning capability** — a domain-specialized draft may produce correct reasoning steps that a larger general model would not, and RSD can exploit this complementarity where standard SD cannot.

**It provides a unified explanation for conflicting findings about speculative decoding on reasoning tasks.** Prior work showed SD either matching the target model (Leviathan et al., 2023) or underperforming it (Chen et al., 2023a), with floating-point errors cited as the culprit. RSD's analysis (Section 3.1, Table 2, Table B.1) shows that the fundamental issue is distributional mismatch between draft and target — when the draft model is domain-specialized or simply reasons differently from the target, strict unbiasedness is actively harmful. This reconciles the conflicting findings: SD works well when draft and target are closely aligned (general-purpose models on general tasks) and poorly when they diverge (specialized draft on reasoning tasks). RSD's reward-based acceptance eliminates this sensitivity to distributional alignment.

**It shifts the efficiency conversation from FLOPs to accuracy-per-FLOP Pareto improvements.** Standard SD offers FLOP reductions at fixed accuracy (lossless acceleration). RSD offers FLOP reductions AND accuracy improvements simultaneously — a strict Pareto improvement on the accuracy-efficiency frontier. On MATH500, RSD (7B/72B/7B) achieves 88.0% accuracy at ~4.4× fewer FLOPs than the 72B target's 85.6% (Figure 4). This is not a tradeoff; it is a genuine advance in what is achievable with a given set of pre-trained models. The practical implication is that organizations should evaluate their inference stack not by whether they can match the large model's accuracy, but by whether they can exceed it at lower cost — and RSD provides a mechanism for doing so.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Training specialized lightweight PRMs aligned to specific draft model output distributions.** The paper uses off-the-shelf PRMs (Skywork-o1-Open-PRM, Qwen2.5-Math-PRM) that were trained on diverse model outputs, not specifically on the draft models used in RSD. A PRM trained specifically on the draft model's reasoning trajectories — using Monte Carlo rollout supervision to produce step-level correctness labels for that specific model's outputs — would likely provide more accurate quality assessments, reducing false-positive acceptances (keeping incorrect draft steps) and false-negative rejections (discarding correct draft steps). A concrete experiment: train a 1.5B PRM on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B outputs using the Wang et al. (2024) procedure, then compare RSD accuracy on MATH500 against the same configuration with the off-the-shelf Skywork-1.5B PRM. The hypothesis is that a draft-aligned PRM would improve the acceptance/rejection accuracy, increasing the draft-only question rate at a given δ without increasing false-positive errors, and potentially pushing the accuracy-vs-δ peak higher than 84.6% (Figure 5). The paper's finding that PRM size beyond 1.5B provides diminishing returns (Table 5) suggests that improving PRM quality through better training data alignment is more promising than scaling PRM parameters.

**Combining RSD's step-level routing with standard SD's token-level efficiency for rejected-step regeneration.** Section 4 mentions this as a natural extension but does not evaluate it. When the PRM rejects a draft step, the current RSD algorithm generates the replacement using a full target model autoregressive generation of the entire step — this costs the same as the target model generating that step from scratch, with no speculative savings. Standard SD could be applied within the rejected-step regeneration: the draft model proposes candidate tokens for the replacement step, the target model verifies them in parallel using the likelihood-ratio test, and tokens are accepted or rejected at the token level. This would make rejected-step regeneration cheaper (since some tokens in the target model's correction might match what the draft would have proposed) while preserving RSD's reward-based decision about whether to regenerate at all. A concrete experiment: on MATH500 Level 4 and 5 questions (where target model invocation is frequent, Figure B.1), measure the FLOPs per rejected step with RSD's full regeneration vs. RSD+SD regeneration, and measure whether accuracy changes. The expected outcome is lower FLOPs on hard questions without accuracy degradation, which would improve RSD's efficiency on the exact questions where it is currently closest to (or exceeding) the target model's single-model cost.

**Dynamic threshold adjustment based on online difficulty estimation from initial PRM scores.** The current RSD uses a fixed δ across all steps and all questions (or at best a per-task tuned δ*). Figure B.1 shows that at a fixed δ = 0.7, the draft-only question rate varies from 84% (Level 1) to 19% (Level 5) — the threshold works but is not adapting to the problem. A dynamic threshold could be set after observing a few initial draft steps: if the first 2-3 steps receive very high PRM scores (all above 0.9), the problem is likely easy, and δ could be lowered (e.g., to 0.5) to accept more draft steps and save compute. If initial steps receive scores near 0.3-0.5, the problem is hard, and δ could be raised (e.g., to 0.8) to invoke the target model more aggressively from the start, avoiding wasted draft model computation on steps that will almost certainly be rejected. A concrete experiment: implement an adaptive δ that starts at a conservative 0.5, increases by 0.1 for each initial step scored below 0.5, and decreases by 0.1 for each initial step scored above 0.8, plateauing at δ ∈ [0.3, 0.9]. Compare against fixed δ = 0.7 on MATH500 per-level accuracy and per-question FLOPs. The hypothesis is that adaptive δ reduces FLOPs on easy questions (by accepting more draft steps) and maintains or improves accuracy on hard questions (by invoking the target model earlier and more often), providing a better accuracy-per-FLOP Pareto curve than any fixed threshold.

**Quantifying and mitigating the false-positive acceptance problem through target model retrospective verification.** The paper identifies no mechanism for detecting or recovering from draft steps that the PRM erroneously accepts (scores above δ but actually incorrect). A retrospective verification procedure could use the target model to score accepted draft steps after the full solution is generated: feed each accepted step through the target model and compute $\log P_M(y_i | z_i)$, then flag steps where this log-probability is anomalously low (relative to the distribution of log-probabilities for accepted steps that led to correct answers) as potentially erroneous. If flagged, regenerate from that step using the target model and compare the alternative solution. A concrete experiment: implement retrospective verification on RSD's MATH500 outputs, using the target model's log-probability of accepted draft steps as a quality signal, with a threshold set on validation data. Measure how many RSD errors can be corrected by retroactively replacing low-probability accepted steps, and whether the cost of retrospective target model calls (which add to total FLOPs) is justified by the accuracy improvement. A negative result — retrospective verification not improving accuracy or costing too much — would indicate that false positives are either rare or not identifiable from target model probabilities, pointing toward better PRM training as the only avenue for improvement.

**Stress-testing RSD on non-math reasoning domains with different step structures and reward signal types.** The paper's entire evaluation is on math and science reasoning with `\n\n`-delimited steps and a PRM trained for that domain. The general-domain ORM experiment (Table 6) showed RSD underperforming the target model, but this result is confounded by the use of an ORM (coarser signal) and no threshold tuning. A systematic stress-test would evaluate RSD on: (a) **code generation** (HumanEval, MBPP) using a code-specific PRM (if available) or a unit-test-passing signal as a binary reward; (b) **multi-hop QA** (HotpotQA, StrategyQA) using an NLI-based step verifier as a pseudo-PRM; (c) **mathematical proof generation** (NaturalProofs, miniF2F) where steps are proof tactics rather than calculations; (d) **non-English reasoning** using multilingual models and a cross-lingual PRM if available. For each domain, the key measurements are: (1) whether RSD with a domain-appropriate reward signal outperforms standard SD (which would work regardless of domain since it only uses model probabilities); (2) what step segmentation strategy works (the `\n\n` heuristic will fail on code without adaptation); and (3) whether the optimal δ varies systematically with domain characteristics (average step count, draft-target divergence, PRM quality). A negative result — RSD failing to outperform SD on code generation, for example — would delineate the boundary between "structured reasoning with available PRMs" (where RSD excels) and "general generation" (where distribution-matching may be preferable), providing practical guidance for practitioners choosing between decoding strategies.

**Developing a gating mechanism to detect when the draft model is hopelessly mismatched and fall back to target-only decoding.** As discussed in Limitation 2 (prior sections), on the hardest problems where the draft model has near-zero chance of producing useful steps, RSD degenerates to paying draft + PRM costs on top of target model costs — strictly worse than target-only decoding. A gating mechanism could monitor the running average of PRM scores over the first k steps: if the average is below some threshold (e.g., 0.3), the system abandons the draft model entirely and completes the problem using only the target model, accepting the small up-front overhead of k wasted draft+PRM calls as the price of detecting futility. A concrete experiment: implement gating with k = 3 and a threshold of 0.3 on average PRM score, and measure on MATH500 Level 5 questions: what fraction of questions trigger the gate, what FLOPs are saved by not continuing with draft model invocation for the remaining steps, and whether accuracy changes (it should not, since in the gate-triggered regime all draft steps would have been rejected anyway). A successful result would show that RSD with gating achieves the same accuracy as target-only on hard questions but with lower FLOPs on easier questions, making the overall Pareto curve strictly better than target-only across the full difficulty spectrum.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient batch inference for math and science reasoning pipelines.** Organizations that run large-scale evaluation of math or science problems — educational technology platforms auto-grading student answers, competition platforms validating solutions, or AI companies generating training data for math reasoning models — can deploy RSD with a math-specialized draft model (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B), a larger target model (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-72B), and a small PRM (e.g., Skywork-o1-Open-PRM-1.5B). Based on Figure 4, this configuration achieves 88.0% accuracy on MATH500 at ~4.4× fewer FLOPs than the 72B model alone. At scale — say, evaluating 1 million MATH-equivalent problems — the FLOP savings translate to ~4.4× reduction in GPU-hours, which at current cloud compute prices represents substantial cost reduction. The 1.5B PRM keeps overhead minimal (3 GFLOPS per step, ~54 GFLOPS total per question vs. ~144 GFLOPS per token for the 72B target), and the draft model handles 48% of questions entirely without target model invocation at δ = 0.7 (Figure 5). The per-task threshold tuning (Table B.2) provides a straightforward calibration procedure: run a sweep of δ ∈ {0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9} on a validation subset of the task distribution, select the best δ, and deploy with that fixed threshold for the full batch.

**On-device or edge deployment with a local draft model and cloud-based target model fallback.** RSD's architecture naturally supports a split deployment where the draft model runs on-device (low latency, zero cloud cost) and the target model runs in the cloud (high latency, per-query cost). At δ = 0.7, for MATH500 Level 1-3 questions (which constitute the majority of many educational math datasets), 58-84% of questions are solved entirely by the draft model on-device with zero cloud calls (Figure B.1). Only the harder Level 4-5 questions (and individual hard steps within easier questions) trigger cloud-based target model invocation. This is more efficient than either always using the cloud model (high latency and cost for all queries) or always using the on-device model (poor accuracy on hard questions). The practical benefit is that an educational app could solve most student math questions instantly on-device using a 1.5B model, and only occasionally call the cloud for problems that genuinely require a 72B model's reasoning capability — reducing cloud costs and improving user-perceived latency for the common case while maintaining high accuracy on hard problems. The PRM can also run on-device (a 1.5B PRM adds negligible memory relative to the 1.5B draft model), meaning the routing decision — whether to accept the draft step or call the cloud — is made locally without any cloud latency.

**Model merging for simplified deployment in resource-constrained serving environments.** Table 4 demonstrates that merging the draft model with the PRM (producing a single model that both generates and scores steps) is viable: RSD with a merged 1.5B draft+PRM and separate 7B target achieves 65.0% average accuracy, outperforming SD (64.6%) and within 0.9 points of the unmerged three-model configuration (65.9%). Merging the target model with the PRM actually improves performance: 66.7% vs. 66.1% for the unmerged configuration. This means a serving system can deploy RSD with two models instead of three, reducing memory requirements and context-switching overhead while retaining most of the accuracy benefit. For GPU-poor deployments — single-GPU servers, edge devices with limited VRAM, or multi-tenant systems where model slots are scarce — this reduction from three to two models is practically significant. The linear merging procedure (MergeKit, 0.6/0.4 interpolation weights for policy/PRM model weights, retaining the PRM's scalar head and policy model's lm_head separately) is straightforward to implement, requiring no additional training data or fine-tuning.

**RSD as a drop-in replacement for standard speculative decoding in existing inference serving systems.** Inference engines like vLLM (Kwon et al., 2023), which the paper uses as its backend, already support speculative decoding with a draft model. Replacing the likelihood-ratio acceptance criterion with a PRM-based acceptance criterion — while keeping the same draft-then-verify architecture — requires adding a PRM forward pass at each step (or at each step boundary, for RSD's step-level operation) and changing the acceptance logic. The paper's finding that RSD consistently outperforms SD across all tested configurations (Table 2: +0.6 to +2.7 points on average accuracy, depending on model family) with comparable or lower FLOPs (Figure 4: RSD uses fewer FLOPs than SD at equal or higher accuracy) means that serving systems currently using SD for reasoning workloads could switch to RSD and get both better accuracy and lower cost. The fixed δ = 0.7 works well across benchmarks (outperforming SD in 7 of 8 configurations, Table 2), so the switch can be made without per-task threshold tuning. The PRM overhead is modest: a 1.5B PRM adds 3 GFLOPS per reasoning step (~54 GFLOPS total per MATH500 question), which is small relative to the cost of even the draft model (1.5B parameters, 3 GFLOPS per token, ~20 tokens per step = ~60 GFLOPS per step). A serving system could add a 1.5B PRM to its model roster and begin using RSD immediately for all reasoning traffic.

### When to Prefer This Method Over Alternatives

The paper does not provide an explicit decision framework comparing RSD against named alternatives under specified conditions. The experiments compare against standard SD, majority voting, Best-of-N, beam search, and single-model baselines, but the paper does not articulate rules for when a practitioner should choose RSD over each of these. The comparisons are presented as evidence of RSD's effectiveness rather than as a basis for a decision rule, and I will not fabricate one.
