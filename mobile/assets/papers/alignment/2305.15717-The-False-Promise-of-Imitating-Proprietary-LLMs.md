# The False Promise of Imitating Proprietary LLMs

**ArXiv:** [2305.15717](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15717)

## 🎯 Pitch

Fine-tuning open-source LLMs on ChatGPT outputs makes them sound fluent and authoritative, but this largely teaches the model to mimic style rather than improve factual accuracy or core knowledge. Crowdworkers are easily fooled by this stylistic imitation, rating copycat models as competitive with ChatGPT, while targeted benchmarks expose that the actual capabilities gap remains essentially unchanged.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper critically analyzes the efficacy of **model imitation**—the practice of fine-tuning a weaker open-source language model on outputs from a stronger proprietary system like ChatGPT (e.g., Alpaca, Self-Instruct)—to cheaply close the capabilities gap. The authors train imitation models using base LMs ranging from 1.5B to 13B parameters (GPT-2, LLaMA) on both broad-coverage imitation data from ShareGPT-Mix and task-specific data for Natural Questions, then evaluate using crowd workers, GPT-4 pairwise comparisons, and canonical NLP benchmarks (MMLU, Natural Questions, HumanEval). They find that while imitation models are rated competitively with ChatGPT by human evaluators—approximately 70% of outputs are judged equal or better—this success reflects the models learning ChatGPT's **style** (fluent, authoritative, well-structured outputs) rather than its **factuality** or underlying capabilities, as targeted automatic evaluations reveal that imitation closes little to none of the gap to ChatGPT on knowledge-intensive tasks and can even cause performance regressions. The central conclusion is that model imitation is a **false promise**: there exists a substantial raw capabilities gap between open and closed LMs that cannot be bridged by fine-tuning on imitation data, establishing that scaling the base model itself (e.g., increasing parameters from 7B to 13B) improves performance far more than adding additional imitation data, and that the highest-leverage action for improving open-source models is developing better base LMs rather than taking the shortcut of imitating proprietary systems.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Can Open-Source Models Cheaply Match Proprietary Systems Through Imitation?

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **if you have a weaker open-source language model and access to a stronger proprietary model via API, can you fine-tune the weaker model on the stronger model's outputs to close the performance gap?** This question matters because it speaks to whether the future AI landscape will be dominated by a few companies with proprietary systems, or whether open-source models can remain competitive through lightweight imitation strategies.

The approach the paper scrutinizes—model imitation—works as follows: a company like OpenAI releases a powerful LM (ChatGPT, GPT-4) behind a black-box API. Users can query the API but cannot see the model's weights, training data, or architecture. An imitator collects a dataset of API inputs and outputs, then fine-tunes a publicly available open-source LM (e.g., LLaMA) on those outputs. The hope is that this fine-tuning distills the proprietary model's capabilities into the open-source model at a fraction of the cost of training a comparable model from scratch.

This hope gained substantial traction in early 2023. Projects like Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023), Vicuna (Chiang et al., 2023), Koala (Geng et al., 2023), and GPT4All (Anand et al., 2023) all followed this recipe, and many claimed near-parity with proprietary systems. Vicuna, for instance, was reported to achieve "90% of the quality of ChatGPT and Google Bard" (Chiang et al., 2023). These claims propagated widely: the authors cite a prominent industry analysis (Patel and Ahmad, 2023) titled "Google 'We Have No Moat, and Neither Does OpenAI'," which crystallized a growing sentiment that open-source models were rapidly closing the gap and that proprietary AI companies would soon have no competitive advantage.

The paper's opening paragraph frames the stakes explicitly:

> "This presents an important question, whose answer will have profound future implications: will the most powerful LMs be closed-source or will they be freely distributed for anyone to use, modify, and extend? Both possibilities have important pros and cons, and implications on policy, corporate strategy, and the future of scientific inquiry." (Section 1)

The authors position their work as a **critical audit** of this optimism. Rather than proposing a new imitation method or claiming to close the gap further, they systematically test whether the imitation approach actually works across multiple dimensions of model capability.

### Why This Problem Is Important

The significance of this question operates on multiple levels:

**1. Economic and competitive dynamics in AI.** If model imitation works well, it undermines the business model of companies investing billions in proprietary model development. Why pay for API access to ChatGPT when you can fine-tune LLaMA-7B on a few hundred thousand ChatGPT outputs and get comparable performance? The paper cites "companies that look to build moats by using off-the-shelf LMs with proprietary fine-tuning datasets" as potentially "comparatively more vulnerable to imitation" (Section 5). Conversely, if imitation does *not* work, companies that establish "a capabilities gap using large amounts of data, compute, or algorithmic advances are the ones who are best positioned to build and maintain competitive advantages."

**2. The open-source AI ecosystem's strategic direction.** The paper's findings carry a direct action implication: if imitation is ineffective, then the open-source community should invest its limited resources differently. Rather than collecting ever-larger imitation datasets, effort should flow toward "the difficult challenge of developing better base LMs" through scaling, improving pre-training data quality, architectural innovations, and better pre-training methods (Section 4.3). This is not a minor reallocation—it's a fundamentally different research program. Building a better base LM requires enormous compute, data curation, and algorithmic advances. Collecting imitation data is comparatively cheap and accessible. The paper's conclusion that the former is the higher-leverage action has significant implications for where academic labs, startups, and non-profit research organizations should direct their efforts.

**3. Scientific understanding of what fine-tuning can and cannot do.** The paper engages a deeper theoretical question about the nature of language model capabilities. It articulates and provides evidence for a particular view of the pretraining/fine-tuning relationship:

> "We thus agree with the view that pre-training is the main source of an LM's capabilities, and that finetuning acts as a lightweight method to train the model to extract its own knowledge" (Section 5, citing Schulman, 2023).

In this framing, fine-tuning teaches the model how to *format and present* knowledge it already possesses from pre-training, but does not inject genuinely new factual knowledge or reasoning capabilities. If this view is correct, then fine-tuning on a stronger model's outputs cannot make a weaker model more knowledgeable—it can at best make it better at presenting what it already knows in a fluent, well-structured manner. The paper's empirical results (imitation models learn style but not factuality) are a direct test of this hypothesis, and the findings provide substantial support for it.

**4. Practical deployment and safety.** If imitation models appear competent to human evaluators while actually being less factual than the proprietary systems they imitate, this creates a **deceptive failure mode**: users may over-trust these models because they *sound* authoritative. The paper explicitly notes this danger:

> "imitation models actually embody some of the worst aspects of AI assistants: their answers sound confident but are less factual than ChatGPT." (Section 4.4)

In deployment contexts where factual reliability matters (medical advice, legal guidance, technical documentation), a model that learned style without substance could cause real harm. The paper's qualitative example in Figure 2 makes this vivid: the imitation model outputs a completely incorrect explanation of actor-critic algorithms (confusing basic concepts about what the critic does) in a format that mirrors ChatGPT's authoritative, well-structured answer. A non-expert reader would have difficulty distinguishing the correct from the incorrect answer.

**5. Ethical and legal questions.** The paper raises but does not fully resolve questions about IP and research ethics: "whether the open-source community should continue to advance progress by 'stealing' what OpenAI and other companies have done, as well as what legal countermeasures companies can take to protect and license intellectual property" (Section 7). If imitation doesn't actually capture the most valuable aspects of proprietary models (their factual knowledge and reasoning capabilities), this changes the legal and ethical calculus. It might mean that imitation is both legally fraught *and* technically ineffective—a double strike against the approach.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies several categories of prior work, each with specific limitations:

**1. Broad-coverage imitation models (the main target of critique).** A wave of 2023 projects attempted to broadly imitate proprietary systems:

- **Alpaca** (Taori et al., 2023): fine-tuned LLaMA on 52K instruction-following demonstrations generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003 using the Self-Instruct method.
- **Vicuna** (Chiang et al., 2023): fine-tuned LLaMA on approximately 70K user-shared conversations from ShareGPT, claiming to achieve 90% of ChatGPT's quality.
- **Koala** (Geng et al., 2023): fine-tuned LLaMA on dialogue data including ShareGPT conversations and other sources.
- **GPT4All** (Anand et al., 2023): trained an assistant-style chatbot by distilling outputs from GPT-3.5-Turbo on a large corpus.
- **Self-Instruct** (Wang et al., 2022a): developed a method for generating instruction-tuning data by prompting a strong LM to create new task examples from seed tasks.

The paper's critique is not that these models are useless—the authors acknowledge they are "far better at following instructions" than their base models (Section 1). The problem is that the **evaluation methodologies used by these projects are insufficient** to distinguish style improvements from capability improvements. As the authors note, Vicuna's "90% quality" claim was based on GPT-4 pairwise evaluations. The present paper shows that GPT-4 evaluations exhibit the same pattern as crowdworker evaluations: they rate style improvements highly while missing factual errors. This means the reported near-parity may be measuring something closer to "sounds like ChatGPT" than "knows what ChatGPT knows."

The authors explicitly position themselves against the optimistic narrative:

> "These claims have since been propagated out into the broader tech community, leading many to believe that open-source LMs are rapidly closing the gap to their closed-source counterparts and that top AI companies will soon have no competitive advantage." (Section 2)

**2. Task-specific imitation (more successful but limited).** The paper also reviews work on **local** or task-specific imitation, where a model is fine-tuned to imitate ChatGPT on a narrow domain:

- Sun et al. (2023): investigated using ChatGPT as a re-ranking agent for search.
- Hsieh et al. (2023): proposed "distilling step-by-step" to train smaller models that outperform larger ones by learning from GPT-3's reasoning chains.
- Honovich et al. (2022): developed "Unnatural Instructions" for tuning models with minimal human labor.

The paper's own task-specific imitation experiment (NQ-Synthetic, Section 4.3 and Table 1) confirms that this approach is far more effective: fine-tuning on 6,000 ChatGPT-generated Natural-Questions-like examples substantially improves NQ accuracy (from 17% to 22% for 7B, from 20% to 27% for 13B). However, task-specific imitation solves a fundamentally different problem than broad-coverage imitation. It requires knowing in advance which task you want to perform, collecting targeted data for that task, and accepting that the model's improvements are confined to that task. This does not address the broad-coverage scenario that projects like Alpaca and Vicuna aim for.

**3. Model distillation and imitation attacks (conceptually related but practically different).** The paper connects model imitation to the older literature on model distillation (Hinton et al., 2014) and model stealing attacks (Tramèr et al., 2016; Orekondy et al., 2019; Wallace et al., 2020). However, it identifies critical differences:

- **In distillation**, the training data, model architecture, and hyperparameters of the teacher are *known*, and one often has access to the teacher's full probability distribution (logits). In imitation, the proprietary model is a black-box API—only input/output text is available, not probabilities.
- **In prior model stealing work** for language tasks (Krishna et al., 2020; Pal et al., 2019), the models are typically trained from scratch or fine-tuned on task-specific data (e.g., BERT for classification). The proprietary advantage is the company's internal training data. In the ChatGPT era, the proprietary advantage also includes a **stronger base LM** that no open-source model can match in raw capability.

This last point is crucial and underappreciated in the earlier imitation literature. When Wallace et al. (2020) studied imitation of machine translation systems, the imitator and target had access to comparable base models—the gap was in the fine-tuning data. In the ChatGPT setting, the gap is in both the fine-tuning data *and* the pretrained model, and the latter may be the larger component. The paper's FLOPs-matched comparison (scaling model parameters from 1.5B to 7B to 13B vs. adding imitation data) is designed to tease apart these two factors.

**4. Limitations in evaluation practices.** A critical shortcoming the paper identifies is not in the models themselves but in **how they are evaluated**. The standard practice for evaluating instruction-following models involves pairwise comparisons by crowdworkers or by GPT-4. The paper demonstrates that these evaluations have a systematic blind spot:

- Crowdworkers without domain expertise cannot reliably distinguish stylistically convincing incorrect answers from factually correct ones.
- GPT-4 evaluations reproduce the same pattern ("our GPT-4 evaluations also showed the same trends as our crowdworker evaluations"—Section 4.4), suggesting LLM-based evaluation inherits human-like cognitive biases.
- Standard NLP benchmarks (MMLU, NQ, HumanEval) reveal gaps that pairwise evaluations miss because they directly test specific capabilities rather than asking for subjective quality judgments.

This is not merely a methodological observation—it explains the discrepancy between the encouraging results reported by Alpaca, Vicuna, etc., and the paper's more sober findings. The prior projects were not necessarily wrong in their reported metrics; those metrics were measuring the wrong thing. A model can score highly on "does this output look like ChatGPT's output?" while scoring poorly on "does this output contain correct information?" The paper argues that the former metric has been misleadingly interpreted as the latter.

**5. Defenses against imitation.** The paper briefly notes work on detecting or preventing model imitation (Krishna et al., 2020; Juuti et al., 2019; Szyller et al., 2019; Maini et al., 2021; Wallace et al., 2020; Orekondy et al., 2020) but observes that "existing methods often exhibit too severe of a tradeoff to be deployable in practice" (Section 6). This means that, for now, companies cannot reliably prevent imitation of their models—they must rely on the fact that imitation doesn't capture the most valuable aspects of their systems (base model capabilities) to maintain competitive advantage.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself not as proposing a new imitation method, but as **auditing an existing trend**. It is a critical analysis paper rather than a methods paper. This is reflected in its structure:

- **Section 2** defines model imitation rigorously and distinguishes local from broad-coverage imitation.
- **Section 3** describes the data collection process for building imitation datasets (both broad-coverage ShareGPT-Mix and task-specific NQ-Synthetic), establishing that the authors are using realistic, high-quality imitation data comparable to what prior projects used.
- **Section 4** presents the central empirical results: crowdworker/GPT-4 evaluations showing promise, automatic evaluations showing failure, and analysis showing imitation models learn style not content.
- **Sections 5-7** discuss implications, related work, and conclusions.

The paper's key strategic move is to conduct **both** human evaluations (replicating the methodology that led to optimistic claims in prior work) **and** targeted automatic evaluations (which prior work largely did not do). By showing that these two evaluation strategies yield divergent conclusions, the paper makes its central argument: the optimistic claims about imitation were artifacts of insufficient evaluation, not genuine capability transfer.

The authors are careful not to dismiss imitation entirely. They note legitimate use cases: inheriting safety behaviors (Figure 5 shows imitation models learn to abstain on toxic prompts), and subverting the need to annotate expensive fine-tuning data when one already has a strong base LM (Section 5). Their critique is specific to the claim that imitation can close the **raw capabilities gap** between weaker open-source and stronger proprietary models.

The paper also draws an explicit connection to the "finetuning as knowledge extraction" paradigm (citing Schulman, 2023; Gao, 2021; Goldberg, 2023), positioning itself within a broader rethinking of what fine-tuning can and cannot accomplish. This framing helps explain *why* imitation fails: if fine-tuning only extracts existing knowledge rather than injecting new knowledge, then fine-tuning on ChatGPT outputs cannot make a less-knowledgeable base model more knowledgeable. It can only change how the model presents what it already knows.

Finally, the paper raises the stakes by connecting its technical findings to strategic and policy questions: who will control the most powerful AI systems, what should the open-source community invest in, and what legal frameworks should govern imitation. These questions are not answered in the paper, but the empirical findings provide a factual basis for debating them.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

The paper's "system" is an **evaluation and analysis pipeline**—not a novel model but a methodology for rigorously testing whether fine-tuning a weaker open-source language model on outputs from a stronger proprietary model (ChatGPT) actually transfers meaningful capabilities. It solves the problem of **disentangling stylistic imitation from factual capability transfer** by constructing imitation models under carefully controlled conditions (varying base model size, data amount, and data scope) and then subjecting them to both human-preference evaluations (which prior work relied on) and targeted automatic benchmark evaluations (which measure specific capabilities like factual knowledge, coding, and reasoning). The shape of the solution is a **controlled experiment with two evaluation lenses that yield conflicting conclusions**, exposing the "false promise" of imitation.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The system has four major component families:

1. **Imitation Data Sources** — pipelines that collect input-output pairs from ChatGPT, either broadly (ShareGPT-Mix: user-shared conversations from the web) or narrowly (NQ-Synthetic: ChatGPT-generated Natural-Questions-like QA pairs). These produce the *imitation training datasets*.

2. **Base Language Models** — pretrained open-source LMs of varying sizes (GPT-2 1.5B, LLaMA 7B, LLaMA 13B) that serve as the *starting point* before imitation fine-tuning. Their raw capabilities represent the "floor" that imitation attempts to raise.

3. **Imitation Fine-Tuning Procedure** — a standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) process that trains the base LM on ChatGPT outputs using a next-token prediction objective. This produces the *imitation models* that are the subject of evaluation.

4. **Dual Evaluation Framework** — two parallel evaluation strategies applied to the same imitation models:
   - **Human/GPT-4 pairwise preference evaluation**: blind comparisons where raters (crowdworkers or GPT-4) judge whether the imitation model's output is better than, equal to, or worse than ChatGPT's output on a held-out set of prompts.
   - **Targeted automatic benchmark evaluation**: measuring exact-match accuracy or pass@1 on canonical NLP benchmarks (5-shot MMLU for broad knowledge, 3-shot Natural Questions for factual QA, 0-shot HumanEval for code generation).

Information flows as follows: imitation data is collected from ChatGPT → base LMs are fine-tuned on this data (with dataset size and base model size systematically varied) → the resulting imitation models are evaluated through *both* the pairwise preference pipeline (which yields "% ≥ ChatGPT" ratings) and the benchmark pipeline (which yields accuracy scores) → the two evaluation signals are compared, revealing that preference ratings rise while benchmark accuracy stays flat or declines, demonstrating that imitation transfers style but not factual substance.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the imitation data collection pipelines—both broad-coverage (ShareGPT-Mix) and task-specific (NQ-Synthetic)—because understanding what data the models are trained on is essential to interpreting what they do and do not learn.
- **Second**, the imitation fine-tuning procedure itself, including model configurations, training hyperparameters, and the explicit design choices (standard LM loss, special tokens, one-epoch training) that make the results comparable to prior imitation projects.
- **Third**, the dual evaluation framework—first the human/GPT-4 pairwise preference methodology (replicating prior work's approach), then the targeted automatic benchmark evaluation (the paper's novel contribution that reveals the failure mode), since the paper's core argument rests on the *divergence* between these two signals.
- **Fourth**, the analysis methodology that connects the evaluation results back to the central claim—specifically, how the paper isolates style learning from capability learning by comparing preference scores against benchmark accuracy as a function of imitation data quantity and base model size.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that imitation of proprietary LMs through fine-tuning on their outputs produces models that sound competent but remain factually weak, and that existing evaluation practices systematically conflate stylistic quality with factual correctness.

#### Imitation Data Collection: Broad-Coverage (ShareGPT-Mix)

The paper constructs a broad-coverage imitation dataset called **ShareGPT-Mix** by aggregating publicly available ChatGPT interactions from three sources:

**Source 1: ShareGPT (approximately 90K dialogues → 50K after filtering).** ShareGPT is a website where users post their ChatGPT conversations. The authors collected approximately 90,000 dialogues from this site. To maintain data quality, they applied two filtering steps: (a) **deduplication at the query level**—if multiple users posted conversations with the same user query, only one copy was retained, and (b) **non-English removal**—a language detector was applied to remove any non-English conversations. These filters reduced the dataset to approximately 50,000 examples. Each example consists of multiple turns of dialogue (user query → ChatGPT response → user follow-up → ChatGPT response, etc.), making this inherently multi-turn conversational data rather than single-instruction data.

**Source 2: HC3 (approximately 27K responses for 24K questions).** The Human-ChatGPT Comparison Corpus (Guo et al., 2023) is a publicly released dataset containing ChatGPT responses to approximately 24,000 questions. The authors used only the ChatGPT responses from the English portion of this corpus, yielding roughly 27,000 individual responses. Unlike ShareGPT, these are primarily single-turn QA interactions without extended dialogue history.

**Source 3: Discord ChatGPT Bots (approximately 10K examples).** The authors collected roughly 10,000 input-output examples from two public Discord channels—the r/ChatGPT server and the Turing AI server—where users interact with ChatGPT bots. These examples represent another source of varied, real-world ChatGPT usage.

**Combined dataset characteristics.** After merging these three sources, the authors assessed the resulting ShareGPT-Mix dataset for diversity and quality. They measured **query-level diversity** by computing the BLEU score similarity between each user query and its most similar neighbor in the dataset. The average maximum BLEU similarity was just **8%**, which is substantially lower than other instruction-tuning datasets such as Super-NaturalInstructions (Wang et al., 2022b), which has an average maximum BLEU of **61%** for a similarly sized set. Low BLEU similarity indicates that queries are not near-duplicates of each other—they cover genuinely diverse topics and phrasings.

The authors also manually reviewed 50 randomly sampled user queries and logged their semantic category. The distribution (Table 5 in the paper) is:

| Category | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Generation | 31% |
| General QA | 16% |
| Coding | 16% |
| Brainstorming | 8% |
| Chat | 6% |
| Summarization | 4% |
| Rewrite | 2% |
| Non-English | 14% |
| Invalid | 2% |

This confirms that ShareGPT-Mix spans diverse task categories, including a substantial fraction of coding tasks (16%) and multi-lingual conversations (14%). The authors note that the data is "generally of high quality" (Section 3). Qualitative examples in Appendix A (Figure 6) show conversations covering market size estimation for identity and access management, worldbuilding for a fictional Elven capital, and other complex, multi-turn interactions.

**Design rationale.** The decision to use *publicly posted* ChatGPT interactions rather than querying the API directly is strategic. It means the dataset reflects **real user behavior**—people asking ChatGPT about topics they actually care about—rather than experimenter-designed prompts. It also means the dataset can be collected "for free without ever having to interact with the company's API" (Section 3), which avoids rate limits, costs, and potential terms-of-service issues. However, a consequence is that the dataset may be biased toward the kinds of interactions users find interesting enough to share publicly (which could over-represent impressive ChatGPT successes or amusing failures).

#### Imitation Data Collection: Task-Specific (NQ-Synthetic)

In parallel with the broad-coverage dataset, the paper constructs a **task-specific imitation dataset** called **NQ-Synthetic**, designed specifically for the Natural Questions benchmark (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019a), which tests factual knowledge about Wikipedia entities.

**Construction procedure.** The pipeline uses an iterative generation approach inspired by Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2022a):

1. **Seed curation.** The authors manually selected 10 question-answer pairs from the Natural Questions validation set to serve as seeds. These pairs represent the desired distribution: questions are short, lowercased, and centered around Wikipedia-like entities; answers are concise (typically a few words). The seed examples (shown in Table 3 of the paper) include pairs like "who sang who wants to be a millionare in high society?" → "Frank Sinatra" and "when did the first harry potter movie come out?" → "2001."

2. **Iterative generation via ChatGPT prompting.** The authors then prompted ChatGPT to generate additional QA pairs that are "similar but distinct" from the seed examples. The prompting template (Table 4 in the paper) provides 5 randomly sampled seed QA pairs in-context and asks ChatGPT to generate new questions and answers with the same style. Specifically:

   > "I want you to generate a series of questions and answers. I want the answers to be concise, just a few words. The questions should be lowercased and centered around Wikipedia-like entities. For example,
   > Q: {question 1}
   > A: {answer 1}
   > ..."

3. **Scale and filtering.** The authors generated **6,000** total QA pairs using this procedure, with generation performed at **temperature 1.0** to encourage diversity. Duplicate question-answer pairs were discarded.

All examples are single-turn (no dialogue history), consistent with the format of the Natural Questions benchmark.

**Design rationale.** This targeted dataset serves as a **positive control** in the experimental design. The paper's central claim is that broad-coverage imitation fails to transfer real capabilities; the NQ-Synthetic experiment tests whether imitation *can* work when the training data is directly aligned with the evaluation task. If imitation on NQ-Synthetic did *not* improve NQ accuracy, that would suggest a fundamental problem with imitation as a technique. The fact that it *does* improve (Table 1: NQ accuracy rises from 17% to 22% for 7B, 20% to 27% for 13B) while broad-coverage imitation does not, provides a sharp contrast that isolates the role of data-task alignment.

#### Imitation Fine-Tuning Procedure

All imitation models are created by supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of pretrained base LMs on the collected ChatGPT outputs. The procedure is intentionally standard—the authors are not proposing a novel training method but rather using the same approach as prior imitation projects (Alpaca, Vicuna, etc.) to ensure that their findings reflect the imitation paradigm as commonly practiced.

**Base models.** The paper uses three decoder-only transformer models spanning different sizes and pretraining regimes:

- **GPT-2 1.5B** (Radford et al., 2019): a 1.5-billion-parameter model trained by OpenAI. Represents a smaller, older base model.
- **LLaMA 7B** (Touvron et al., 2023): a 7-billion-parameter model from Meta AI's LLaMA family, trained on a large corpus of public data. Represents a mid-size contemporary open-source model.
- **LLaMA 13B** (Touvron et al., 2023): a 13-billion-parameter model from the same family. Represents the largest open-source model available at the time of the study.

The authors explicitly note that they use "model scale as a proxy for base-model quality, however model quality could also improved by other factors such as the quality of pre-training data, architectural improvements, novel pre-training methods, etc." (Section 4.1, footnote 2). This is an important caveat: the 13B model might be better than the 7B not just because it's larger but because of differences in training data, architecture, or hyperparameters.

**Data preprocessing.** Conversations from ShareGPT-Mix (which are multi-turn dialogues) are **chunked into blocks of 2048 tokens**. The authors introduce **special tokens** to demarcate the beginning of each user query and each model output within the conversation. This token-level structuring allows the model to learn the distinction between user and assistant turns, which is essential for conversational behavior. Single-turn examples (from HC3, Discord, or NQ-Synthetic) are treated as conversations with one user turn and one assistant turn.

**Training objective.** The model is trained using the **standard language modeling loss** (next-token cross-entropy), but crucially, the loss is computed **only on the model output tokens**, not on the user query tokens. This means the model is trained to predict "given this user input, what would ChatGPT say?" but not "given this model output, what would the user ask next?" The authors follow the approach from Chung et al. (2022) and Chowdhery et al. (2022), who established that masking the instruction tokens during instruction tuning improves performance.

In formal terms, for a conversation with user tokens $u_1, ..., u_m$ and ChatGPT response tokens $r_1, ..., r_n$, the loss is:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} \log p_\theta(r_t \mid u_1, ..., u_m, r_1, ..., r_{t-1})$$

where $p_\theta$ is the probability assigned by the base LM (with parameters $\theta$) to the t-th response token, conditioned on all preceding user tokens and preceding response tokens.

**What it computes:** the average negative log-likelihood of the ChatGPT response tokens under the fine-tuned model. Each token's contribution is the log-probability the model assigned to the token that actually appeared in ChatGPT's output; these are summed and negated, then divided by the sequence length $n$. Lower loss means the model assigns higher probability to ChatGPT's actual responses.

**Why this form:** using standard LM loss (rather than, say, a ranking loss or a contrastive objective) makes the training identical to pre-training, which is the standard recipe for instruction tuning. The decision to mask user tokens is an established design choice from the instruction tuning literature (Chung et al., 2022) that prevents the model from wasting capacity on modeling the distribution of user queries (which is irrelevant to generating good responses) and focuses the parameter updates entirely on improving response quality.

**Training hyperparameters.** The specific configuration (quoted from Section 4.1):

- **Optimizer:** AdamW
- **Learning rate:** $2 \times 10^{-3}$ (i.e., 0.002)
- **Warmup:** 1000 steps of linear warm-up from learning rate 0 to the full learning rate
- **Batch size:** 32
- **Epochs:** 1 (each example is seen exactly once during training, as in Chowdhery et al., 2022 and Chung et al., 2022)
- **Gradient rescaling:** gradients are re-scaled by the magnitude of each weight (a technique also used in PaLM training, Chowdhery et al., 2022)
- **Hardware:** models trained in JAX using "a combination of fully shared data parallelism and tensor parallelism on TPUs hosted by Google Cloud or on a single Nvidia DGX server with 8 A100 GPUs"

**Training for one epoch only.** The decision to train for exactly one epoch (each training example seen once) is deliberate and follows the instruction tuning practices of FLAN (Chung et al., 2022) and PaLM (Chowdhery et al., 2022). The motivation is to avoid overfitting: with relatively small fine-tuning datasets (tens of thousands of examples) compared to pretraining corpora (trillions of tokens), multiple epochs risk the model memorizing specific ChatGPT outputs rather than learning generalizable instruction-following behavior. One epoch provides enough signal to learn the output format and style while minimizing memorization.

**Data scaling experiments.** To study how imitation model quality scales with imitation data quantity, the authors train models on **different-sized subsets of ShareGPT-Mix**, ranging from approximately 0.3 million tokens to 150 million tokens (as shown on the x-axis of Figures 1, 3, 4, and 5). The number of tokens is computed after chunking conversations into 2048-token blocks. This range corresponds to roughly 150 to 75,000 individual examples (since multi-turn conversations produce multiple blocks). By sweeping data quantity over two orders of magnitude, the authors can observe whether performance saturates, improves monotonically, or even degrades with more data.

**Model size scaling experiments.** In parallel with data scaling, the authors compare imitation models built on different base model sizes (1.5B, 7B, 13B). This creates a 2D experimental grid: data quantity × base model size. Figures 4 and 3 show the interplay: increasing base model size consistently improves performance across all evaluation metrics, while increasing imitation data quantity has flat or negative effects on benchmark accuracy. This contrast is the paper's central empirical finding.

---

#### Evaluation Framework: Human and GPT-4 Pairwise Preferences

The paper employs two distinct evaluation paradigms. The first replicates the methodology used by prior imitation projects (Alpaca, Vicuna, etc.) and is designed to measure *perceived output quality*. The second is the paper's novel contribution and measures *actual task capability*.

**Human evaluation via Amazon Mechanical Turk.** The authors conducted blind pairwise output comparisons using crowdworkers on Mechanical Turk. The procedure is:

1. **Evaluation prompts.** The authors hold out a set of 255 prompts from the evaluation process. Crucially, to mitigate any test-set leakage from the ShareGPT-Mix training data, they filtered out any evaluation prompt that had a BLEU score greater than **20%** with any example in the training set. They also removed non-English and coding-related prompts since "these cannot be reliably reviewed by crowd workers" (Section 4.1, footnote 3). This leaves 255 English-language, non-coding prompts for human evaluation.

2. **User interface.** Each rater is presented with a single task instruction (prompt) and the corresponding outputs from two unknown models—one of which is always ChatGPT (the reference) and one of which is one of the imitation models. The order of the two outputs is randomized (ChatGPT sometimes appears first, sometimes second). The raters select one of three options: (a) Output A is better, (b) Output B is better, or (c) the two outputs are equal in quality. Figure 7 in Appendix B shows the exact UI.

3. **Rater quality control.** The authors recruit workers who meet specific criteria: **≥95% approval rating** on Mechanical Turk, located in an **English-speaking country**, and with at least **100 HITs completed**. Approximately 70 unique crowdworkers participated.

4. **Collecting multiple ratings per example.** Each of the 255 evaluation examples is rated by **3 unique raters**. To produce a single preference for each example, the authors use **majority voting** across the 3 ratings. They then average the preference scores across all 255 examples to get an overall "% ≥ ChatGPT" metric, reporting "one standard deviation around the mean" (Section 4.1) as an uncertainty estimate.

5. **Compensation.** Evaluators are paid roughly **$15/hour** based on the average time taken to complete a task. In total, the authors spent approximately **$5000** on ratings experiments, including Mechanical Turk service fees.

**The "% ≥ ChatGPT" metric.** For each example, the score is determined as follows: if the majority of raters prefer the imitation model's output over ChatGPT's, that example scores 1; if the majority prefer ChatGPT, it scores 0; if the majority say "equal," it scores 0.5. Averaging across the 255 examples yields the fraction of prompts where the imitation model is rated as equal or better than ChatGPT. This is reported as a percentage.

**GPT-4-based evaluation.** As a complementary automatic evaluation, the authors follow the procedure from Vicuna (Chiang et al., 2023): they prompt GPT-4 with both outputs (one from the imitation model, one from ChatGPT) and ask GPT-4 to output a preference ranking—which output is better, or if they are equal. The same 255 evaluation prompts are used as in the human evaluation. GPT-4's preferences are scored identically to human preferences, producing a "% ≥ ChatGPT" metric.

**Key design choice: using ChatGPT as the reference model in all comparisons.** The pairwise evaluation always compares the imitation model against the *same model it imitated* (ChatGPT). This is a direct test of the imitation hypothesis: if imitation works perfectly, the imitation model should be indistinguishable from ChatGPT, and all ratings should be "equal." If it works partially, some fraction of outputs should be rated equal or better. This is a more demanding bar than comparing against the base LM (which would just measure improvement) or against a weaker baseline.

**Caveat acknowledged: the human evaluation difficulty problem.** Section 4.4 explicitly notes that "crowd workers without domain expertise or significant time investments can easily be deceived by stylistic components—answers that sound confident and correct are often spuriously chosen more often." This is not a flaw in the evaluation design per se—it's the central phenomenon the paper is studying. The human evaluation is intended to replicate prior work's methodology; the finding that human evaluators rate imitation models highly is the *replication*, not an error.

---

#### Evaluation Framework: Targeted Automatic Benchmarks

The second evaluation paradigm measures performance on canonical NLP benchmarks that directly test specific capabilities: factual knowledge, broad academic knowledge, and code generation ability. This is the evaluation that reveals the failure mode invisible to pairwise preference ratings.

**Benchmark 1: MMLU (5-shot).** The Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) consists of multiple-choice questions spanning 57 academic subjects (from law and medicine to philosophy and physics). The authors evaluate in the **5-shot setting**: for each question, the model is shown 5 example question-answer pairs (from the same subject) as in-context demonstrations before being asked to answer the target question. The model's predicted answer is compared to the ground-truth label; accuracy is reported as the percentage of questions answered correctly across all 57 subjects. MMLU tests the breadth of a model's academic and factual knowledge.

**Why MMLU?** MMLU questions cover subjects where ChatGPT's pretraining data likely contains substantially more relevant information than LLaMA's (due to OpenAI's proprietary data mix and larger pretraining scale). If imitation transfers knowledge, the imitation model should improve on MMLU relative to the base LM. If it only transfers style, MMLU accuracy should remain flat (since the base model's factual knowledge is unchanged) or even decline (if fine-tuning interferes with retrieval of pre-trained knowledge).

**Benchmark 2: Natural Questions (3-shot).** The Natural Questions benchmark (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019b) consists of real Google search queries paired with Wikipedia-based answers. The authors evaluate in the **3-shot setting**: 3 example questions with short-answer responses are provided as in-context demonstrations. The model is then asked a new question and must produce a short answer; the answer is scored using **exact match** against the ground-truth answer string. The paper reports standard exact-match accuracy (percentage of questions where the model's answer exactly matches the reference).

**Why Natural Questions?** NQ directly tests factual knowledge about real-world entities (Wikipedia subjects). It complements MMLU by focusing on short, factual answers rather than multiple-choice academic reasoning. If imitation transfers knowledge from ChatGPT's broader pretraining to the weaker base model, we would expect NQ accuracy to rise.

**Benchmark 3: HumanEval (0-shot).** The HumanEval benchmark (Chen et al., 2021) consists of 164 Python programming problems where the model must write a function that passes a set of hidden unit tests. The authors evaluate in the **0-shot setting**: the model receives the function signature and docstring describing the desired behavior, and must produce the complete function body. Performance is measured using **pass@1**: the fraction of problems where the model's first generated solution passes all unit tests. The paper reports pass@1 as a percentage.

**Why HumanEval?** Coding is one of the most practically important capabilities of modern LLMs and represents a different skill from factual knowledge. If imitation transfers ChatGPT's coding ability (which is substantially stronger than LLaMA's), pass@1 should improve. Moreover, the ShareGPT-Mix dataset contains approximately 16% coding examples, so there is relevant in-domain training data.

**Key result format.** For each benchmark, the authors report accuracy for (a) the base LM without any imitation fine-tuning, (b) the imitation model fine-tuned on varying amounts of ShareGPT-Mix data, and (c) ChatGPT itself (as the upper bound). These three data points appear on each subplot of Figure 4, with data quantity on the x-axis and accuracy on the y-axis.

#### Analysis Methodology: Disentangling Style from Substance

The core analytical move of the paper is the **comparison across evaluation methodologies**. Rather than reporting results from a single evaluation paradigm, the paper deliberately places the human/GPT-4 preference results *alongside* the automatic benchmark results and demonstrates their divergence.

**Design principle: replicate then critique.** The paper first replicates the evaluation methodology that led to optimistic claims in prior work (Alpaca, Vicuna, etc.). Figure 1 (left) and Figure 3 (left) show the "% ≥ ChatGPT" metric from crowdworkers and GPT-4 respectively, as a function of imitation data quantity. Both show that imitation models achieve competitive ratings (approximately 60-70% of outputs rated equal or better than ChatGPT), which *confirms* the earlier findings—if you stop here, imitation looks successful.

Then the paper applies the same models to canonical NLP benchmarks where the metric is factual correctness rather than perceived quality. Figure 4 (top) shows the same models' performance on MMLU, HumanEval, and Natural Questions. The three plots reveal flat or declining accuracy as imitation data increases, with the base LM (black line) often outperforming the imitation model (blue line). The gap between ChatGPT's accuracy (dashed line) and the imitation model remains essentially unchanged.

**The "style learning" analysis.** To directly demonstrate that imitation models learn style rather than content, the paper quantifies stylistic similarity between imitation models and ChatGPT as a function of imitation data quantity (Table 2). The metrics measured are:

1. **List usage concordance:** "If ChatGPT outputs a list, do we?" — the percentage of cases where the imitation model also outputs a list when ChatGPT does. This rises from 13% (base LLaMA-13B, which rarely produces lists) to 81% (150M tokens of imitation data), approaching ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT concordance of 83%.

2. **Summary paragraph concordance:** "If ChatGPT outputs a summary paragraph, do we?" — the percentage of cases where the imitation model also outputs a summary when ChatGPT does. This rises from 2% to 48%, approaching the ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT rate of 55%.

3. **Unigram overlap:** the average unigram intersection (percentage of words shared) between the imitation model's output and ChatGPT's output for the same prompt. This rises from 19.5% (base LLaMA) to 42.5% (150M tokens), with ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT overlap at 49.2%.

4. **Length correlation:** the Pearson correlation coefficient between the length of the imitation model's output and ChatGPT's output for the same prompt. This rises from -0.11 (essentially no correlation—LLaMA's output length is unrelated to ChatGPT's) to 0.62 (strongly correlated—the imitation model matches ChatGPT's verbosity). ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT correlation is 0.67.

5. **Authoritative tone:** the percentage of outputs rated as having an "authoritative tone" according to GPT-4 (presumably by prompting GPT-4 to classify the tone). This rises from 57% (base LLaMA) to 98% (150M tokens), matching ChatGPT at 98%.

All five metrics show the same pattern: substantial movement from the base LM toward ChatGPT's style as imitation data increases, with the 150M-token imitation model approaching the ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT reference values. Meanwhile, the automatic benchmark results (Figure 4, top) show no improvement—and in some cases degradation—on factual accuracy.

**Why this analysis is compelling.** The Table 2 metrics are not evaluation outcomes—they are **diagnostic measurements** that explain *why* the evaluation outcomes diverge. Crowdworkers see outputs that use lists when ChatGPT would, match ChatGPT's length and word choice, and sound authoritative. This drives their preference ratings up. But those ratings are measuring stylistic mimicry, not factual correctness. The automatic benchmarks bypass style entirely and measure only whether the answer is *right*, revealing that stylistic improvements have come at no factual benefit (or even at a cost, due to distribution shift from the conversational fine-tuning data interfering with the model's ability to retrieve pre-trained knowledge).

**The parameter-count vs. data-quantity comparison.** The paper's other key analytical move is contrasting the slope of improvement from adding imitation data against the slope from increasing base model size. Figure 4 (bottom) shows accuracy as a function of model parameters (1.5B → 7B → 13B) for the imitation models. Unlike the flat data-scaling curves, the parameter-scaling curves show clear upward trends on MMLU, HumanEval, and NQ. This directly supports the paper's conclusion: if you want a better model, spend your resources on building a better base LM, not on collecting more imitation data.

**Safety transfer analysis.** The paper also examines whether imitation transfers ChatGPT's safety properties (specifically, reduced toxicity). The authors evaluate imitation models on **RealToxicityPrompts** (Gehman et al., 2020), a benchmark for measuring toxic language generation. They report the **average non-toxicity score** according to the Perspective API (a score between 0 and 1 where higher values indicate less toxic text). Figure 5 shows that as imitation data increases, non-toxicity rises from approximately 0.86 (base LLaMA-13B) to approximately 0.91 (150M tokens), approaching ChatGPT at approximately 0.92. The interpretation (Section 4.4): the imitation model learns ChatGPT's behavior of *abstaining* from toxic responses rather than generating them. This is a style/persona transfer that the paper acknowledges as genuinely useful—unlike factual capability transfer, safety behavior *is* successfully transferred through imitation.

**The NQ-Synthetic positive control.** Table 1 provides the critical contrast that isolates data-task alignment as the key variable. The same base models (LLaMA 7B and 13B) are fine-tuned either on broad-coverage ShareGPT-Mix or on task-specific NQ-Synthetic, and evaluated on Natural Questions:

| Model | Imitation Data | NQ Accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| LLaMA 7B | – | 17% |
| LLaMA 7B | ShareGPT-Mix | 10% |
| LLaMA 7B | NQ-Synthetic | 22% |
| LLaMA 13B | – | 20% |
| LLaMA 13B | ShareGPT-Mix | 15% |
| LLaMA 13B | NQ-Synthetic | 27% |
| ChatGPT | – | 31% |

The ShareGPT-Mix models *degrade* NQ performance relative to the base LM (17% → 10% for 7B, 20% → 15% for 13B)—a performance regression attributed to distribution shift. The NQ-Synthetic models *improve* NQ performance (17% → 22%, 20% → 27%), demonstrating that imitation *can* work when the training data is aligned with the evaluation task. The gap to ChatGPT (31%) is not fully closed, but the improvement is substantial. This confirms the paper's nuanced position: imitation is not useless across the board; it is specifically ineffective for broad-coverage capability transfer, while task-specific imitation can be effective.

**Summary of design choices and their justifications.**

- **Using publicly posted ChatGPT interactions rather than API queries:** avoids costs and terms-of-service issues while capturing real user behavior; at the cost of potential selection bias in what users choose to share.
- **Training for one epoch:** standard in instruction tuning literature; prevents overfitting to small imitation datasets.
- **Loss only on model outputs (not user queries):** established practice from FLAN/PaLM; focuses model capacity on response quality.
- **Dual evaluation framework (human preferences + automatic benchmarks):** the paper's key methodological contribution; human evaluations replicate prior work, automatic evaluations reveal the failure mode, and the divergence between them is the central empirical finding.
- **Sweeping both data quantity and base model size:** creates a 2D experimental design that can distinguish the effects of "more imitation data" from "better base model," which is the core comparison the paper's conclusion rests on.
- **Task-specific (NQ-Synthetic) positive control:** demonstrates that imitation can work under favorable conditions, ruling out the interpretation that the authors' fine-tuning procedure is simply broken or that imitation is fundamentally impossible.
- **Style similarity quantification (Table 2):** provides mechanistic evidence for the style-vs-substance hypothesis by directly measuring convergent stylistic markers, going beyond correlation to explain the causal mechanism behind the evaluation divergence.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Style-vs.-Substance Diagnostic Framework

The paper's most fundamental contribution is not a new method but a **diagnostic concept**: the distinction between learning a model's *style* (fluency, structure, authoritative tone, length matching, list usage) and learning its *substance* (factual knowledge, reasoning ability, coding capability). Prior work on model imitation—Alpaca, Vicuna, Koala, GPT4All—had evaluated imitation models almost exclusively through pairwise preference comparisons (by crowdworkers or GPT-4), which conflate stylistic quality with factual correctness. The field's implicit assumption was that if an imitation model's outputs were rated as competitive with the target model, then imitation was successfully transferring capabilities.

This paper demonstrates that this assumption is **fundamentally invalid**. By constructing imitation models under controlled conditions (varying base model size and imitation data quantity) and evaluating them through *both* pairwise preferences and targeted automatic benchmarks (MMLU, Natural Questions, HumanEval), the authors reveal a systematic divergence: human and GPT-4 raters judge imitation model outputs as competitive with ChatGPT (Figure 1 left, Figure 3 left, ~70% rated equal or better), while the same models show no improvement—and in some cases degradation—on benchmark accuracy (Figure 4 top). The diagnostic measurements in Table 2 provide direct evidence for *why*: as imitation data increases, the models converge toward ChatGPT on five stylistic dimensions (list usage concordance rising from 13% to 81%, summary paragraph concordance from 2% to 48%, unigram overlap from 19.5% to 42.5%, length correlation from -0.11 to 0.62, authoritative tone from 57% to 98%), while benchmark accuracy remains flat.

This is a **fundamental shift** in how to evaluate imitation, not an incremental refinement. Prior evaluation practices implicitly treated "sounds like ChatGPT" as a valid proxy for "knows what ChatGPT knows." The paper demonstrates that this proxy is unreliable and provides a concrete methodology—parallel evaluation through style-sensitive and style-insensitive metrics—for detecting the failure. The implications extend beyond imitation: any evaluation methodology that relies on subjective quality judgments (human or LLM-based) may systematically overestimate capability transfer if the model has learned the surface patterns of competent outputs without the underlying competence. As the authors note, "imitation models actually embody some of the worst aspects of AI assistants: their answers sound confident but are less factual than ChatGPT" (Section 4.4). This insight recasts the optimistic narrative around imitation projects as an artifact of evaluation methodology rather than evidence of genuine capability transfer.

The qualitative example in Figure 2 makes this diagnostic concept concrete: the imitation model produces an answer about actor-critic algorithms that mirrors ChatGPT's well-structured, authoritative format but is substantively incorrect—confusing basic concepts about what the critic does, whether actor and critic are learned simultaneously, and how advantage estimation works. A reader without RL expertise would struggle to distinguish the correct from the incorrect answer, precisely because the surface form is so convincing.

### Innovation 2: Reframing Fine-Tuning as Knowledge Extraction, Not Knowledge Injection

The paper articulates and provides empirical support for a **theoretical reframing** of what fine-tuning accomplishes. Drawing on Schulman (2023), Gao (2021), and Goldberg (2023), the authors frame fine-tuning not as a mechanism for *injecting* new knowledge or capabilities into a model, but rather as a "lightweight method to train the model to extract its own knowledge" (Section 5). This reframing has deep implications for the imitation enterprise.

If fine-tuning only teaches a model to format and present knowledge it already possesses from pre-training, then fine-tuning a weaker base model on a stronger model's outputs cannot transfer the stronger model's knowledge—it can at best teach the weaker model to present its own more limited knowledge in a more polished format. The paper's empirical results are a direct test of this hypothesis, and they strongly support it: imitation models trained on broad-coverage ChatGPT outputs improve on style metrics (Table 2) but show no improvement on factual knowledge benchmarks (Figure 4 top, Table 1). The knowledge gap between LLaMA-13B and ChatGPT on Natural Questions (20% vs. 31%) remains essentially unchanged after imitation (15% after broad imitation, actually *worse* than the base model).

This reframing represents a **fundamental shift** from the implicit model underlying the imitation project. The Alpaca/Vicuna approach assumes—implicitly or explicitly—that fine-tuning on ChatGPT outputs transfers ChatGPT's capabilities to the weaker model through a form of distillation. If the knowledge-extraction view is correct, this assumption is systematically misguided. The knowledge that produces correct answers to factual questions, generates working code, and solves reasoning problems was acquired during pre-training and cannot be retroactively injected through lightweight fine-tuning—or at least, not through the modest amounts of imitation data used in these projects.

The paper provides a nuanced boundary condition: task-specific imitation *does* work when the training data is directly aligned with the evaluation task. The NQ-Synthetic experiment (Table 1) shows imitation improving NQ accuracy from 17% to 22% for LLaMA-7B and from 20% to 27% for LLaMA-13B. This suggests the knowledge-extraction view isn't absolute—fine-tuning can teach narrow factual patterns when the training data is dense in those patterns—but it's a strong constraint on the *breadth* of capability transfer. The paper's core negative result is that broad-coverage imitation, where the training data spans thousands of diverse topics at low density per topic, fails to transfer the target model's broad capabilities.

An important corollary of this reframing, noted in Section 5, is that during fine-tuning "you may even want to avoid introducing new knowledge (i.e., do not imitate better models), as you will otherwise be training the model to guess or hallucinate its answers, rather than actually doing the task as intended" (citing Schulman, 2023). In other words, fine-tuning on outputs from a more knowledgeable model may teach the weaker model to *produce answers that sound correct* without the underlying knowledge to actually be correct—exactly the failure mode the paper documents.

### Innovation 3: The Capabilities Gap as the Fundamental Bottleneck—Not Data Access

The paper reframes the open-source vs. proprietary AI competition as primarily a question of **base model capabilities**, not access to high-quality fine-tuning data. The dominant narrative in early 2023, crystallized by Patel and Ahmad (2023) ("Google 'We Have No Moat, and Neither Does OpenAI'"), held that open-source models were rapidly catching up to proprietary systems by fine-tuning on their outputs, and that the key competitive advantage—the "moat"—was eroding. The paper challenges this narrative at its root.

The critical evidence comes from the two-dimensional experimental design: sweeping both imitation data quantity and base model size. Figure 4 (bottom) shows that increasing base model parameters from 1.5B to 7B to 13B produces clear, consistent accuracy improvements on MMLU, HumanEval, and Natural Questions. Figure 4 (top) shows that increasing imitation data from 0.3M to 150M tokens produces flat or declining accuracy on the same benchmarks. The slope of improvement from scaling the base model is substantially steeper than the slope from adding imitation data—and on some benchmarks, the imitation data slope is actually negative. This is captured succinctly in Figure 1 (right): "the best way for the open-source community to improve models is by increasing these capabilities (e.g., via scaling, better pretraining data, etc.,) rather than fine-tuning on more and more imitation data."

This finding is a **fundamental reframing** of the strategic question. If the gap between open and closed models were primarily about fine-tuning data (ChatGPT has better instruction-following data than LLaMA), imitation would be a viable shortcut—collect ChatGPT outputs, fine-tune, and close the gap. The paper demonstrates that the gap is primarily about the *base model itself*: ChatGPT's underlying pretrained model is substantially more knowledgeable and capable than any available open-source model, and that gap cannot be cheaply bridged through fine-tuning. The practical implication is a reallocation of effort from imitation data collection (which is relatively cheap and accessible) to base model development (which requires enormous compute, data curation, and algorithmic advances—a fundamentally different and more difficult research program).

The paper is careful not to dismiss imitation entirely. Section 4.4 and Figure 5 show that imitation successfully transfers ChatGPT's safety properties (reduced toxicity via learning to abstain), and Section 5 acknowledges that "model imitation is highly effective in cases when one has a powerful base LM and is looking to subvert the need to annotate expensive finetuning data." This creates a nuanced picture: imitation is useful for transferring *behavioral patterns* (style, safety, persona) when the base model is already strong, but it is ineffective for transferring *raw capabilities* when there is a large capabilities gap.

### Innovation 4: Human and LLM-Based Evaluation Share a Systematic Blind Spot

The paper makes a **methodological contribution** to evaluation science by demonstrating that both human crowdworkers and GPT-4 exhibit the same systematic failure mode when evaluating language model outputs: they are highly sensitive to stylistic quality and largely insensitive to factual correctness when the two diverge. This is not presented as a hypothesis to be tested but rather emerges as an empirical finding: Figure 3 shows GPT-4 preference ratings tracking human preference ratings closely, both showing the same flat-or-rising trend with imitation data quantity that the automatic benchmarks reveal to be misleading.

This has significant implications beyond model imitation. LLM-based evaluation (using GPT-4 to judge other models' outputs) has become a widely adopted methodology, particularly following Vicuna's use of GPT-4 for evaluation (Chiang et al., 2023). The paper's finding that GPT-4 reproduces the same pattern as crowdworkers—rating stylistically convincing but factually incorrect outputs as competitive—suggests that LLM-based evaluation inherits the same style-over-substance bias as human evaluation. The authors note this explicitly: "our GPT-4 evaluations also showed the same trends as our crowdworker evaluations (albet with a slightly larger absolute preference for ChatGPT's outputs). While this suggests that GPT-4 may be a viable candidate to cheaply emulate human evaluations on some tasks, it also implies that LLMs may replicate some human-like cognitive biases" (Section 4.4).

This is a **fundamental insight** rather than an incremental finding because it challenges the validity of a widely-used evaluation methodology and identifies a specific, replicable failure mode. The practical implication is clear: pairwise preference evaluations—whether by humans or by LLMs—are insufficient on their own to measure genuine capability transfer. They must be complemented by targeted automatic benchmarks that directly test specific capabilities in ways that cannot be faked through stylistic mimicry. The paper does not claim to solve this problem—"the future of human evaluation remains unclear: how can we cheaply and quickly probe the utility of a powerful LLM?" (Section 7)—but it diagnoses the failure with concrete evidence and provides a template for detecting it (parallel evaluation through preference ratings and capability benchmarks).

### Innovation 5: Imitation as a Positive Control Experimental Design

The paper's inclusion of task-specific imitation (NQ-Synthetic) alongside broad-coverage imitation (ShareGPT-Mix) is not merely a robustness check—it represents a **methodological innovation** in how to critique an approach. Rather than arguing that imitation doesn't work in general, the paper constructs an experimental design where imitation is *known to work* (because training and evaluation distributions are aligned) and uses this as a positive control against which to interpret the broad-coverage results.

This design serves two functions. First, it rules out the interpretation that the authors' fine-tuning procedure is simply broken or that imitation is fundamentally impossible. The NQ-Synthetic results (Table 1: +5 percentage points for 7B, +7 for 13B) demonstrate that the training pipeline does transfer capabilities when the data-task alignment is favorable. The fact that broad-coverage imitation *doesn't* work under the same pipeline isolates the failure to the data regime, not the methodology.

Second, it sharpens the paper's central claim from "imitation doesn't work" to the more precise and actionable "broad-coverage imitation doesn't work for capability transfer because the imitation data lacks sufficient density on any one capability." This is a more useful claim: it tells future researchers that if they want imitation to transfer a specific capability, they should collect targeted data for that capability rather than hoping a broad dataset will cover it incidentally. It also explains *why* Alpaca and Vicuna appeared to improve instruction-following (the broad data *does* cover instruction-following patterns densely) while failing to improve factual knowledge (the broad data *does not* cover any particular set of facts densely).

This positive-control design is an **incremental innovation** in experimental methodology rather than a fundamental theoretical advance, but it substantially strengthens the paper's argument by preempting obvious alternative explanations. Without the positive control, skeptics could argue that the authors' fine-tuning hyperparameters were suboptimal, their data preprocessing was lossy, or their base models were unsuitable for fine-tuning. The NQ-Synthetic results make these objections implausible: the same pipeline works well when the task is locally supported in the data.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses multiple evaluation datasets to measure different aspects of model capability. For **broad-coverage imitation model evaluation**, the authors use a held-out set of **255 prompts** drawn from user-submitted conversations, filtered to remove any prompt with a BLEU score above 20% relative to any training example (to prevent test-set leakage) and to exclude non-English and coding-related prompts (since these cannot be reliably reviewed by crowdworkers). For **targeted automatic evaluation**, three canonical NLP benchmarks are used: **MMLU** (Hendrycks et al., 2021) comprising multiple-choice questions across 57 academic subjects, evaluated in the 5-shot setting; **Natural Questions** (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019b) consisting of real Google search queries paired with Wikipedia-based short answers, evaluated in the 3-shot setting with exact-match scoring; and **HumanEval** (Chen et al., 2021) containing 164 Python programming problems, evaluated in the 0-shot setting with pass@1. For **toxicity evaluation**, the paper uses **RealToxicityPrompts** (Gehman et al., 2020) with the Perspective API providing non-toxicity scores.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses three decoder-only transformer models spanning different scales and pretraining regimes: **GPT-2 1.5B** (Radford et al., 2019), **LLaMA 7B**, and **LLaMA 13B** (Touvron et al., 2023). The LLaMA models are chosen because they represent the strongest publicly available open-source base LMs at the time of the study—the same family used by Alpaca, Vicuna, and Koala—making the results directly comparable to prior imitation projects. GPT-2 1.5B provides a lower-capability baseline to test whether imitation effects depend on base model quality. The authors explicitly note they use "model scale as a proxy for base-model quality" while acknowledging that other factors (pretraining data quality, architecture, training methods) also contribute.

- **Metrics.** The paper employs two fundamentally different evaluation approaches that measure distinct constructs. **Pairwise preference evaluations** (both human and GPT-4) produce a **"% ≥ ChatGPT" metric**: for each prompt, the imitation model's output is compared blind against ChatGPT's output; if the majority of raters (3 per example for human evaluation) prefer the imitation model, that example scores 1; if they prefer ChatGPT, it scores 0; if they rate them equal, it scores 0.5. The average across all 255 evaluation examples is reported as a percentage, with the authors reporting one standard deviation around the mean. **Automatic benchmark evaluation** uses each dataset's standard metric: **accuracy** (exact-match-based for MMLU multiple-choice and Natural Questions short answers) and **pass@1** (fraction of HumanEval problems where the first generated solution passes all unit tests). For toxicity evaluation, the Perspective API's toxicity score (0–1 range, inverted so higher means less toxic) is reported as the **average non-toxicity score** across RealToxicityPrompts examples.

- **Baselines.** The paper establishes three tiers of baselines for comparison. The primary baseline is the **unmodified base LM** (GPT-2 1.5B, LLaMA 7B, LLaMA 13B) evaluated without any imitation fine-tuning. This answers: does imitation improve over the base model? The second baseline is **ChatGPT itself**, evaluated through the same prompts and benchmarks. This answers: how close does imitation get to the target? For pairwise preference evaluations, ChatGPT is always one of the two outputs being compared. For automatic benchmarks, ChatGPT's accuracy provides the upper bound. The third implicit baseline is the **prior imitation projects** (Alpaca, Vicuna, Koala), whose reported results—evaluated primarily through pairwise preferences and claiming near-parity with ChatGPT—serve as the status quo that the paper challenges. The paper does not directly re-evaluate Alpaca or Vicuna models, but its imitation models are constructed using substantially the same methodology (LLaMA base, ShareGPT-derived data, SFT on outputs) and evaluated using the same pairwise preference protocol, making the comparison valid.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use generation budget or FLOPs in the same sense as scaling-law papers, since it is not comparing search strategies under a fixed inference compute constraint. Instead, the relevant "compute" axis is **imitation data quantity**, measured in millions of tokens (ranging from approximately 0.3M to 150M tokens). This is the resource being invested to improve the model, analogous to the generation budget in test-time compute papers. The data quantity is computed after chunking multi-turn conversations into 2048-token blocks. The paper sweeps this quantity systematically to study scaling behavior. For base model comparisons, model size in billions of parameters (1.5B, 7B, 13B) serves as a proxy for pretraining compute investment. Training for one epoch on each dataset subset means the total optimization FLOPs scale linearly with data quantity times model size.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation in the traditional sense (the 255 evaluation prompts are a single fixed held-out set). For statistical reliability, each of the 255 human evaluation examples receives **3 independent ratings** from distinct crowdworkers, with majority voting used to determine the final preference per example. Averages are computed across the 255 examples. The authors report one standard deviation around the mean for crowdworker evaluation results. For automatic benchmarks, the standard evaluation protocols are followed (MMLU averaged across 57 subjects, Natural Questions exact match on the standard test set, HumanEval pass@1 across 164 problems). No confidence intervals or statistical significance tests are reported for the automatic benchmark results. The rater pool consists of approximately **70 unique crowdworkers** selected to meet quality thresholds: ≥95% Mechanical Turk approval rating, location in an English-speaking country, and at least 100 HITs completed.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Human and GPT-4 Pairwise Preference Evaluations Show Apparent Success

The paper's initial crowdworker evaluations replicate the optimistic findings of prior imitation projects. **Figure 1 (left)** shows that as the amount of imitation data increases from approximately 25M to 150M tokens, the fraction of imitation model outputs rated as equal or better than ChatGPT's outputs rises to approximately **70%**. The base LLaMA-13B model (without imitation) achieves substantially lower ratings (approximately 20–30%, estimated from the visible gap between the black and blue lines). This confirms the central observation from Alpaca, Vicuna, and similar projects: imitation fine-tuning dramatically improves perceived output quality as judged by human raters.

However, the scaling trend is notably flat beyond the initial data point. Figure 1 (left) shows the "% ≥ ChatGPT" metric hovering in the 65–75% range from roughly 25M tokens onward, with no clear upward trend as data quadruples or sextuples. The authors interpret this saturation as an early warning sign: if imitation were genuinely transferring capabilities, one would expect continued improvement with more data rather than a plateau at partial parity.

**Figure 3 (left)** shows the same trend using GPT-4 as the evaluator instead of crowdworkers, replicating the Vicuna evaluation protocol (Chiang et al., 2023). GPT-4's preference ratings follow essentially the same pattern as human ratings—relatively flat across data quantities, with the imitation model achieving approximately 30–40% "≥ ChatGPT" (somewhat lower absolute values than crowdworker ratings, as the authors note GPT-4 shows "a slightly larger absolute preference for ChatGPT's outputs"). The convergence between human and GPT-4 evaluations is itself a significant finding: it validates GPT-4 evaluation as tracking the same signal as human evaluation, but simultaneously demonstrates that both share the same limitation—sensitivity to stylistic quality rather than factual correctness.

**Figure 3 (right)** tests the effect of scaling base model size on GPT-4 preference ratings, holding imitation data constant. As model parameters increase from 1.5B to 7B to 13B, GPT-4's "% ≥ ChatGPT" rises steadily from near zero to approximately **45%**. This scaling curve shows clear improvement, in sharp contrast to the flat data-scaling curve in Figure 3 (left). The authors use this contrast to support their central argument: making the base model more capable improves perceived output quality substantially, while adding more imitation data to a fixed base model yields marginal or no improvement.

The divergence between human and GPT-4 absolute ratings (crowdworkers giving ~70% ≥ ChatGPT vs. GPT-4 giving ~30–40%) is noted but not deeply analyzed. It could reflect systematic differences in how humans and GPT-4 weight style versus substance, different implicit standards for "equal quality," or GPT-4 being better at detecting factual errors. The paper does not resolve which evaluator is more accurate; the key finding is that both exhibit the same *pattern* of flat data-scaling curves contrasted with rising model-scaling curves.

#### Targeted Automatic Benchmarks Expose the Capabilities Gap

**Figure 4 (top row)** presents the paper's central negative result: across three canonical NLP benchmarks (MMLU, Natural Questions, HumanEval), imitation models trained on broad-coverage ShareGPT-Mix data show **no improvement—and in some cases clear degradation—relative to the base LLaMA-13B model** as imitation data quantity increases from approximately 25M to 150M tokens.

**MMLU 5-shot (Figure 4, top-left).** The base LLaMA-13B achieves approximately **48%** accuracy (estimated from the black horizontal line). ChatGPT achieves approximately **68%** (dashed horizontal line). The imitation model (blue curve) starts near the base model's performance and actually **declines slightly** as data increases, ending around **45–46%** at 150M tokens. The gap between the imitation model and ChatGPT remains approximately **20 percentage points**, essentially unchanged from the gap between the base LLaMA and ChatGPT. Adding up to 150M tokens of imitation data closes none of this gap.

**HumanEval 0-shot (Figure 4, top-center).** The base LLaMA-13B achieves approximately **10–12%** pass@1. ChatGPT achieves approximately **65–70%** pass@1. The imitation model shows a modest initial improvement at low data quantities (rising to approximately 15–18%) but then **declines back toward base model performance** as data increases to 150M tokens, ending near 12–13%. The enormous gap between the imitation model and ChatGPT (approximately 50–55 percentage points) remains largely unchanged. This is particularly notable given that ShareGPT-Mix contains approximately 16% coding examples—even having in-domain training data at low density on coding does not transfer coding capability.

**Natural Questions 3-shot (Figure 4, top-right).** The base LLaMA-13B achieves approximately **20%** exact-match accuracy. ChatGPT achieves approximately **31%**. The imitation model shows a **clear performance regression**: accuracy drops from the base model's 20% to approximately **15%** at 25M tokens, and remains flat or slightly declining as data increases, ending around **13–14%** at 150M tokens. This is worse than doing nothing at all—fine-tuning on broad-coverage ChatGPT conversations actively degrades the model's ability to answer factual questions about Wikipedia entities. The authors attribute this to distribution shift: the conversational, instruction-following format of the ShareGPT-Mix data interferes with the model's ability to retrieve and output factual knowledge in the short-answer format required by Natural Questions.

**Figure 4 (bottom row)** shows the effect of increasing **base model size** (1.5B → 7B → 13B) while holding imitation data constant. Across all three benchmarks, larger base models produce imitation models with higher accuracy:

- **MMLU:** rises from approximately 25% (1.5B) to 35% (7B) to 45% (13B). ChatGPT at 68% remains substantially above all three.
- **HumanEval:** rises from near 0% (1.5B) to 5–8% (7B) to 12–13% (13B). ChatGPT at ~67% dwarfs all imitation models.
- **Natural Questions:** rises from approximately 5% (1.5B) to 10% (7B) to 13–15% (13B). ChatGPT at 31% remains substantially higher.

The key finding from the bottom row is the steepness of the scaling slope from base model parameters compared to the flatness from imitation data. A move from 7B to 13B parameters improves accuracy more substantially than a move from 25M to 150M tokens of imitation data. For MMLU, the parameter scaling produces roughly +10 percentage points, while data scaling produces -2 to +2 percentage points. This asymmetry is the empirical foundation for the paper's conclusion that "the higher leverage action for improving open-source LMs is to tackle the difficult challenge of developing better base LMs" rather than collecting more imitation data.

#### Task-Specific Imitation Succeeds Where Broad-Coverage Fails

**Table 1** presents the Natural Questions results for imitation models trained on two different data regimes, providing the critical positive control that isolates data-task alignment as the key variable:

| Model | Imitation Data | NQ Accuracy |
|---|---|---|
| LLaMA 7B | – | 17% |
| LLaMA 7B | ShareGPT-Mix | 10% |
| LLaMA 7B | NQ-Synthetic | 22% |
| LLaMA 13B | – | 20% |
| LLaMA 13B | ShareGPT-Mix | 15% |
| LLaMA 13B | NQ-Synthetic | 27% |
| ChatGPT | – | 31% |

The pattern is stark. **Broad-coverage imitation (ShareGPT-Mix) degrades NQ performance** for both model sizes: 17% → 10% for 7B, 20% → 15% for 13B—a relative degradation of approximately 25–40%. The 13B ShareGPT-Mix model (15%) is actually worse than the unfine-tuned 7B base model (17%), meaning the fine-tuning has destroyed enough of the base model's factual retrieval ability to offset the benefits of the larger model size.

**Task-specific imitation (NQ-Synthetic) improves NQ performance** for both model sizes: 17% → 22% for 7B (a +5 percentage point improvement), 20% → 27% for 13B (a +7 percentage point improvement). The 13B NQ-Synthetic model (27%) narrows the gap to ChatGPT (31%) to just 4 percentage points, compared to the base 13B gap of 11 points.

The task-specific results demonstrate that the authors' imitation pipeline (data collection, fine-tuning hyperparameters, evaluation protocol) is capable of transferring capabilities when the training data is aligned with the evaluation task. This rules out the interpretation that the broad-coverage failure is due to implementation issues—the pipeline works; it just doesn't work for the broad-coverage use case. The degradation from ShareGPT-Mix further shows that broad imitation data is not merely neutral (providing no benefit) but can be actively harmful through distribution shift, particularly for tasks that require short, factual answers rather than verbose, conversational responses.

#### Style Convergence Quantified

**Table 2** provides the mechanistic evidence for *why* crowdworkers rate imitation models highly despite flat benchmark accuracy. The table tracks five stylistic metrics as a function of imitation data quantity (0M, 20M, 80M, 150M tokens), with ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT comparisons ("ChatGPT #2") shown as a reference upper bound:

1. **If ChatGPT outputs a list, do we?** Truthfulness of list structure concordance: rises from 13% (base LLaMA-13B, which rarely outputs lists) through 50% (20M tokens), 67% (80M tokens), to 81% (150M tokens), approaching the ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT rate of 83%.

2. **If ChatGPT outputs a summary paragraph, do we?** Summary paragraph concordance: rises from 2% (base) through 40%, 42%, to 48% (150M), approaching the ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT rate of 55%.

3. **Unigram intersection with ChatGPT's output:** Average percentage of overlapping words between the imitation model's output and ChatGPT's output for the same prompt: rises from 19.5% (base) through 40.4%, 41.9%, to 42.5% (150M), approaching ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT at 49.2%.

4. **Pearson correlation in length with ChatGPT's output:** How strongly the imitation model matches ChatGPT's output length per prompt: rises from -0.11 (essentially no correlation—LLaMA's length is unrelated to ChatGPT's) through 0.51, 0.62, to 0.62 (150M), approaching ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT at 0.67.

5. **Outputs are in authoritative tone according to GPT-4:** Percentage of outputs rated as authoritative by GPT-4: rises from 57% (base) to 98–99% at all imitation data quantities, matching ChatGPT at 98%.

All five metrics show substantial convergence toward ChatGPT's style as imitation data increases, with the 150M-token imitation model approaching the ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT reference values in several cases. The most dramatic shifts occur between 0M and 20M tokens (e.g., list concordance from 13% to 50%, length correlation from -0.11 to 0.51), after which improvements level off—mirroring the saturation pattern seen in the human preference ratings (Figure 1 left). This suggests that the imitation model learns the basic stylistic patterns of ChatGPT quickly from a modest amount of data, and that additional data provides diminishing returns in stylistic mimicry while providing no benefits in factual accuracy.

The contrast between Table 2 and Figure 4 (top row) is the paper's central empirical demonstration: all the movement is on the style axis, none (or negative) on the substance axis.

#### Safety and Toxicity Transfer

**Figure 5** presents results on RealToxicityPrompts, measuring the average non-toxicity score (higher = less toxic) according to the Perspective API. The base LLaMA-13B achieves approximately **0.86**. As imitation data increases, non-toxicity rises steadily: reaching approximately 0.88 at ~25M tokens, 0.90 at ~75M tokens, and **~0.91** at 150M tokens. ChatGPT itself achieves approximately **0.92**, meaning the imitation model trained on 150M tokens has closed approximately 5/6 of the initial gap on this metric.

The mechanism, as the authors describe it (Section 4.4), is that the imitation model learns ChatGPT's behavior of *abstaining* from responding to prompts that could elicit toxic content, rather than reducing the toxicity of its substantive outputs. This is a legitimate success case for imitation: safety-related behaviors and refusal patterns are precisely the kind of stylistic/persona characteristic that fine-tuning can transfer. The contrast with factual knowledge benchmarks reinforces the paper's diagnosis—imitation transfers behavioral patterns but not underlying knowledge.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Data quantity scaling (Figures 1, 3, 4, 5):** The paper sweeps imitation data from approximately 0.3M to 150M tokens across all evaluation types, serving as a built-in ablation on whether "more imitation data helps." The consistent finding across all evaluation axes is that benefits saturate early (at roughly 20–50M tokens for style metrics, at near-zero for factual benchmarks), with additional data providing marginal or no improvement and sometimes causing regression. Figure 4 (top) shows MMLU, HumanEval, and NQ either flat or declining beyond the first data point.

**Base model size scaling (Figures 3, 4, bottom rows):** Sweeping GPT-2 1.5B → LLaMA 7B → LLaMA 13B tests whether imitation benefits depend on the underlying model's capability. The finding is clear: larger base models consistently produce better imitation models across all benchmarks, with substantially steeper improvement slopes than those obtained from increasing imitation data. Figure 4 (bottom-right) shows NQ accuracy rising from ~5% (1.5B) to ~10% (7B) to ~14% (15B), while increasing imitation data for the 13B model (Figure 4, top-right) shows a slight decline from ~15% to ~14%.

**Broad-coverage vs. task-specific imitation data (Table 1):** The most important ablation, testing whether imitation failure is inherent to the approach or specific to data-task misalignment. The NQ-Synthetic models demonstrate that imitation *can* work (+5 to +7 percentage points on NQ), ruling out the interpretation that the authors' fine-tuning procedure or base model choice is fundamentally broken. The ShareGPT-Mix models' degradation (-7 to -5 percentage points on NQ) demonstrates that broad-coverage data can actively harm specific capabilities even as it improves style.

**Human vs. GPT-4 evaluator agreement (Figures 1 left, 3 left):** Implicitly tests whether the observed evaluation blind spot is unique to human raters. GPT-4 evaluations track human evaluations closely (both show flat data-scaling curves for preference ratings), suggesting that LLM-based evaluation inherits the same style-over-substance bias. The paper notes that GPT-4 shows "a slightly larger absolute preference for ChatGPT's outputs" but the pattern is qualitatively identical.

**ChatGPT output style metrics (Table 2):** Quantifies what the model actually learns from imitation data. The five metrics (list concordance, summary paragraph concordance, unigram overlap, length correlation, authoritative tone) all show convergence toward ChatGPT's style, providing direct evidence for the style-learning hypothesis rather than merely inferring it from evaluation divergence. The comparison column "ChatGPT #2" (a second ChatGPT output compared against the first) provides a natural upper bound for how similar two independently generated ChatGPT outputs are.

**Toxicity transfer (Figure 5):** Tests whether imitation transfers safety properties distinct from knowledge transfer. The result (non-toxicity rises from 0.86 to 0.91) demonstrates that behavioral patterns learned from fine-tuning data (abstaining from toxic responses) transfer successfully, in contrast to factual knowledge which does not transfer from the same data. This supports the paper's nuanced position: imitation is not useless, but it transfers style, persona, and behavioral patterns rather than factual knowledge or reasoning capability.

**Relevance of coding examples in ShareGPT-Mix (HumanEval in Figure 4, top-center):** The ShareGPT-Mix dataset contains ~16% coding examples, yet the imitation model shows no sustained improvement on HumanEval pass@1. This is a finding about required data density: having coding examples scattered across a broad dataset at ~16% concentration is insufficient to transfer coding capability; the model would need a much higher density of coding-specific data to learn the capability. This absence of improvement cannot be attributed to the dataset containing no coding data—it does, just not enough.

**BLEU-based deduplication of evaluation prompts (Section 4.1):** The authors filter out evaluation prompts with BLEU >20% similarity to any training example. This is a robustness check against training set contamination inflating the human evaluation scores. The fact that preference ratings remain high (~70% ≥ ChatGPT) even after removing near-duplicate prompts suggests the stylistic mimicry is generalizing to unseen instructions rather than memorizing training examples.

**Multi-turn dialogue handling (training procedure, Section 4.1):** The chunking of multi-turn conversations into 2048-token blocks (rather than training on isolated single-turn pairs) tests whether the model benefits from dialog context. The paper does not run an explicit ablation (single-turn vs. multi-turn training), but the fact that the models learn conversational style (authoritative tone, list usage, length matching) from multi-turn data indicates the chunking approach is effective for style transfer.

**Missing ablations that would strengthen the paper:**

- **No experiment varying data diversity vs. data quantity independently:** Does 100M tokens from 100K unique prompts outperform 100M tokens from 10K prompts? The flat data-scaling curves could be due to hitting a diversity ceiling rather than a quantity ceiling—the paper cannot distinguish these because data quantity and diversity are confounded in the ShareGPT-Mix dataset.
- **No experiment mixing pretraining data with imitation data during fine-tuning:** The paper notes in Section 4.3 that "an open problem is whether these performance regressions can be mitigated using regularization or by mixing in pre-training data during fine-tuning." This is a natural ablation that would test whether the degradation on NQ (Table 1) is a catastrophic forgetting phenomenon that can be mitigated.
- **No experiment on imitation data from multiple proprietary models:** All imitation data comes from ChatGPT. It is possible that imitation from a different proprietary model (Claude, Bard) would show different patterns, or that ensembling outputs from multiple proprietary models would improve factual transfer.
- **No experiment on the amount of task-specific data required:** The NQ-Synthetic dataset uses 6,000 examples. How does NQ accuracy scale with NQ-Synthetic data quantity? Does it saturate like broad-coverage style learning, or continue improving? This would test whether task-specific imitation faces diminishing returns or can asymptotically approach the target model.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: "Imitation models are rated highly by crowdworkers but fail on automatic benchmarks."**  
This claim is **strongly supported** by the evidence. Figure 1 (left) and Figure 3 (left) show crowdworkers and GPT-4 rating imitation model outputs as competitive with ChatGPT (~70% and ~35% "≥ ChatGPT" respectively). Figure 4 (top) shows the same models perform no better—and sometimes worse—than the base LLaMA-13B on MMLU, HumanEval, and Natural Questions. The evaluation divergence is robust across three different benchmarks testing different capabilities (academic knowledge, factual QA, coding), two evaluator types (humans and GPT-4), and a range of data quantities (25M–150M tokens). The qualitative example in Figure 2 provides a concrete illustration: the imitation model produces an answer about actor-critic algorithms that is formatted identically to ChatGPT's but substantively incorrect. The only caveat is that the claim is demonstrated for a specific target model (ChatGPT), specific base models (LLaMA, GPT-2), and a specific imitation dataset (ShareGPT-Mix). The paper's title and abstract generalize to "proprietary LLMs" and "imitation" broadly, but the experiments only test one proprietary model and one data collection strategy. It is plausible—though not demonstrated—that imitating a different proprietary model (Claude, Bard) or using higher-quality imitation data (e.g., curated expert demonstrations rather than scraped user conversations) might show different patterns. The paper's claim about "evaluation blind spots" is therefore well-supported for the specific case tested; whether it generalizes to all proprietary LLMs and all imitation strategies is a reasonable extrapolation but not directly tested.

**Claim: "Imitation models learn style, not factuality."**  
This claim is **strongly supported**, and Table 2 is the critical piece of evidence. The five stylistic metrics show clear convergence toward ChatGPT's output patterns as imitation data increases (list concordance: 13% → 81%, approaching ChatGPT-vs-ChatGPT at 83%; summary paragraph concordance: 2% → 48% vs. 55% reference; unigram overlap: 19.5% → 42.5% vs. 49.2% reference; length correlation: -0.11 → 0.62 vs. 0.67 reference; authoritative tone: 57% → 98% vs. 98% reference). Meanwhile, factual accuracy on MMLU, NQ, and HumanEval is flat or declining. The evidence for the *factuality* side of the claim comes from the automatic benchmarks, and the conclusion that "imitation models have weak factuality" is relative—they are no more factual than the base LM, and substantially less factual than ChatGPT. However, the style metrics are measured on the same prompts used for pairwise evaluation (the 255 held-out prompts), while the factuality metrics come from a different set of benchmarks. The paper does not directly measure *both* style similarity and factual correctness on the *same* evaluation prompts—such a measurement would require expert annotation of factual correctness for the 255 evaluation prompts and would strengthen the causal link between style convergence and evaluator preference. The current evidence shows that style improves (on the evaluation prompts) while factuality does not improve (on separate benchmarks), which strongly implies but does not directly prove that evaluators are being deceived by style on those specific prompts.

**Claim: "Scaling base models is higher-leverage than scaling imitation data."**  
This claim is **supported with qualifications**. Figure 4 (bottom) shows clear improvements from scaling base model size (1.5B → 7B → 13B) on all three benchmarks. Figure 4 (top) shows flat or declining performance with increasing imitation data. The slopes are visually steeper for parameter scaling than for data scaling. However, the comparison is asymmetric in an important way: the base model size sweep covers a factor of approximately 8.7× in parameters (1.5B to 13B), while the data sweep covers a factor of approximately 500× in tokens (0.3M to 150M). The paper's conclusion that parameter scaling is "higher leverage" compares *these specific ranges*—it does not, and cannot, claim that any amount of imitation data would be less effective than any increase in model size. It is possible that at much larger data scales (billions of tokens, approaching pretraining scale), imitation data would start to improve factual accuracy. The paper acknowledges this boundary condition: "broadly matching ChatGPT using weaker base LMs such as LLaMA-13B would require a concerted effort to collect an extremely large and diverse imitation dataset that is far closer to the scale of pretraining. It is currently unclear whether such an effort is worth undertaking or feasible" (Section 4.3). The "higher leverage" claim is therefore a practical conclusion about where to invest effort *given current data collection capabilities*, not a fundamental law that imitation can never transfer knowledge.

**Claim: "There exists a substantial capabilities gap that cannot be bridged by fine-tuning on imitation data."**  
The strength of support depends on the interpretation of "cannot be bridged." The paper demonstrates that the gap **is not bridged** by the specific imitation approaches tested—broad-coverage SFT on 150M tokens of ShareGPT-Mix data. The gap between LLaMA-13B imitation model and ChatGPT on Natural Questions is approximately 18 percentage points (13% vs. 31%), on MMLU approximately 22 points (46% vs. 68%), and on HumanEval approximately 55 points (12% vs. 67%). These are large gaps that show no sign of closing with additional imitation data. The claim that this gap "cannot be bridged" with current methods is directly supported. The stronger claim that it can *only* be bridged by developing better base LMs (and that no imitation strategy could ever work) goes beyond the evidence. The NQ-Synthetic results demonstrate that imitation *can* close gaps when data is task-aligned, raising the possibility that a sufficiently large, diverse, and high-quality imitation dataset could bridge broader gaps. The paper's title calls imitation a "false promise"—this is best interpreted as a verdict on the specific promise made by Alpaca/Vicuna-style projects (that cheap imitation on modest data can achieve near-parity), which the evidence thoroughly refutes. The stronger interpretation (that imitation can *never* close the gap regardless of data scale or collection strategy) is a reasonable hypothesis that the paper's evidence is consistent with, but does not directly test.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

1. **No direct measurement of knowledge transfer on the same prompts used for preference evaluation.** The paper measures style convergence on the evaluation prompts (Table 2 metrics) and factual accuracy on separate benchmarks (Figure 4). This leaves open the logical possibility that the evaluation prompts happen to be "easy" in ways the benchmarks are not, and that the imitation model actually *is* more factual on those prompts but less factual on benchmarks. Directly annotating the factual correctness of the 255 evaluation prompt responses (for both the imitation model and ChatGPT) would close this gap. The paper's argument would be stronger if it could show: "on the specific prompts where crowdworkers rate the imitation model as equal to or better than ChatGPT, the imitation model's outputs contain X% more factual errors."

2. **Single proprietary model tested.** All imitation data comes from ChatGPT (the specific API version available in early 2023). The paper's conclusions refer to "proprietary LLMs" in general, but Claude, Bard, and GPT-4 might have different imitation characteristics—particularly if their style is harder to mimic or their knowledge is more deeply encoded. The paper acknowledges that "initial results suggest that these models have achieved near parity with proprietary models" from prior work, but only tests imitation of ChatGPT directly.

3. **Limited diversity of base models.** The paper uses GPT-2 1.5B and LLaMA 7B/13B. These are all autoregressive decoder-only transformer LMs from the same broad family. Different architectures (encoder-decoder models like FLAN-T5, or models with different pretraining objectives) might interact differently with imitation fine-tuning. In particular, encoder-decoder models might be less susceptible to the distribution-shift regressions observed on NQ, since they separate encoding and decoding.

4. **No experiment with RLHF or preference-based fine-tuning on imitation data.** The paper uses only supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on ChatGPT outputs. Prior work has shown that RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017) or constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) can improve models beyond what SFT alone achieves. The paper acknowledges this: "it may also be possible to use the target model to perform RLHF... to further improve results" (Section 5). An RLHF-based imitation approach—where the imitation model generates responses, ChatGPT scores them, and the model is updated via a reward signal—might transfer capabilities more effectively than SFT on static outputs. The paper's conclusions are strictly about SFT-based imitation, which is what the Alpaca/Vicuna/Koala projects used.

5. **Benchmark-specific evaluation limitations.** MMLU is multiple-choice, meaning the model only needs to select the correct option rather than generate a free-form answer. This tests recognition rather than recall, which might be less affected by the conversational fine-tuning's distribution shift than free-form QA. Natural Questions uses exact-match scoring, which is strict and may penalize the imitation model for producing verbose, conversational answers (which it learned from ShareGPT-Mix) even when the answers contain the correct information. HumanEval uses pass@1, which is a single-sample metric; the paper does not report pass@k for k > 1, which would measure whether the imitation model *can* generate the correct code at all (even if not as its first sample). These measurement choices could make the imitation model look worse than it actually is if its primary failure mode is formatting rather than knowledge.

6. **Small evaluation set for human evaluation.** The 255 prompts used for pairwise evaluation and style analysis (approximately 0.5% of the 50K training examples) is a modest sample. The statistical uncertainty around the ~70% "≥ ChatGPT" figure is not fully characterized beyond "one standard deviation." If the standard deviation is large relative to the differences between models (which is plausible with 255 prompts rated by 3 workers each), the flatness of the data scaling curve in Figure 1 (left) might partly reflect noise rather than genuine saturation. The paper does not report whether the pairwise preference differences between imitation models trained on 25M vs. 150M tokens are statistically significant.

7. **Data quality confound in data scaling experiments.** The data scaling sweep uses subsets of ShareGPT-Mix, but the paper does not describe how these subsets are selected. If they are selected chronologically (earlier-submitted ShareGPT conversations vs. later ones) or by source (HC3 vs. Discord vs. ShareGPT), the data subsets might differ in quality or topic distribution in ways that confound the scaling analysis. A cleaner ablation would randomly subsample the full dataset at different rates to isolate the effect of data quantity from potential data quality differences.

**Experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Factual annotation of evaluation prompt responses:** Hire domain experts to rate the factual correctness of the 255 evaluation prompt responses from both imitation models and ChatGPT. This would directly test whether evaluators prefer outputs that are stylistically similar but factually inferior, closing the inferential gap between the style metrics (Table 2) and the benchmark results (Figure 4).

- **Pass@k for HumanEval:** If the imitation model's coding weakness is primarily a formatting issue (it produces correct code embedded in verbose conversational responses), pass@10 might be substantially higher than pass@1. If pass@k shows the same flat data-scaling curve, the conclusion that coding capability does not transfer is strengthened.

- **Held-out task evaluation for NQ-Synthetic models:** The NQ-Synthetic model improves on NQ (Table 1). Does it also improve on *other* factual QA benchmarks (e.g., TriviaQA, WebQuestions), or does the improvement remain localized to NQ-style questions? If localized, it reinforces the paper's point about narrow transfer. If it generalizes, it suggests that task-specific imitation can transfer broader capabilities than expected.

- **Imitation data from a model other than ChatGPT:** Replicate the core experiment (imitation model trained on ShareGPT-Mix-style data, evaluated on benchmarks and human preferences) using Claude or GPT-4 as the target model. If the same pattern holds (style improves, factuality doesn't), the paper's conclusions generalize. If a different proprietary model produces different results, the phenomenon might be specific to ChatGPT's particular style-capability relationship.

- **Mixing pretraining data during imitation fine-tuning:** Train imitation models where the fine-tuning data is a mix of ShareGPT-Mix and generic pretraining text (e.g., C4, The Pile). This would test whether the performance regressions on NQ (Table 1) are catastrophic forgetting that can be mitigated by data mixing, or a more fundamental interference between conversational and factual-answer formats.

- **Continuous scaling to much larger imitation datasets:** If feasible, extend the data scaling curve to 1B+ tokens to test whether the flat lines in Figure 4 (top) eventually start rising. The paper's claim that "an unwieldy amount of imitation data" would be required is reasonable but extrapolates from a curve that shows no upward trend within the tested range. Data beyond the tested range is needed to confirm the shape of the extrapolation.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Single Proprietary Model as the Imitation Target

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire empirical case rests on imitating exactly one proprietary model: ChatGPT (the API version available in early 2023). The paper's title and abstract refer broadly to "proprietary LLMs," and the framing in Section 2 discusses the open-source vs. closed-source competition in general terms, citing multiple proprietary systems (ChatGPT, Bard, Claude). Yet every experiment—broad-coverage imitation (ShareGPT-Mix), task-specific imitation (NQ-Synthetic), and all evaluations—uses only ChatGPT as the target. The authors do not acknowledge this as a limitation explicitly, but the scope is clear from the experimental sections.

**The consequence.** The paper's central findings—that imitation transfers style but not factuality, that scaling base models is higher-leverage than scaling imitation data, and that imitation is a "false promise"—may be specific to ChatGPT rather than general properties of proprietary LLM imitation. Different proprietary models have different characteristics that could substantially alter imitation dynamics: Claude is trained with constitutional AI methods that might produce outputs with different stylistic signatures harder to mimic superficially; GPT-4 may have a larger capabilities gap from LLaMA-13B than ChatGPT does, making the style-vs.-substance divergence even starker or, alternatively, changing the threshold at which factual knowledge transfers; Bard may use different pretraining data with different coverage of the evaluation benchmarks. More subtly, the relationship between a model's style and its substance might differ across model families—a proprietary model whose style is *less* separable from its factual accuracy (e.g., one that uses more precise, less verbose language) might force imitation models to learn more substance to achieve style parity. The paper's policy and strategic implications—that companies can "establish and safeguard a competitive advantage by pre-training powerful base models" (Section 7)—depend on the assumption that all proprietary models share ChatGPT's property that their value comes primarily from base model capabilities rather than fine-tuning data. If another proprietary model's advantage derives largely from proprietary fine-tuning data (which might be imitatable), the strategic calculus changes.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly. The paper contains no experiments imitating Bard, Claude, GPT-4, or any proprietary system other than ChatGPT. The ShareGPT-Mix dataset is composed entirely of ChatGPT interactions (from ShareGPT, HC3 responses collected from ChatGPT, and Discord ChatGPT bots). The paper's "Related Work" section (Section 6) discusses model imitation in general terms and cites work on imitating various models, but the authors' own experimental results are confined to a single target. The paper's conclusions refer to "proprietary LMs" and "closed-source LMs" in the plural (e.g., Sections 1, 5, and 7), extrapolating from the single-target experiments without testing the extrapolation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the single-target-model limitation, does not argue that ChatGPT is representative of all proprietary systems, and does not suggest future work testing other proprietary models. The finding that GPT-4 evaluations track human evaluations (Figure 3) might be interpreted as indirect evidence that another proprietary model (GPT-4) processes imitation outputs similarly, but this is about evaluation methodology, not about imitation of different target models. A reader deciding whether to invest in or defend against imitation of a specific proprietary model (e.g., Claude in a corporate deployment) has no direct evidence from this paper about whether the results transfer.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Completely Unaccounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates imitation models by comparing their outputs to ChatGPT's, but it does not account for the cost of *creating* those imitation models—specifically, the cost of collecting the imitation training data from the proprietary API. For the ShareGPT-Mix dataset, the authors deliberately sidestep API costs by using publicly posted ChatGPT interactions rather than querying the API directly (Section 3: "we can collect a large, diverse, and generally high-quality dataset of examples for free without ever having to interact with the company's API"). For the NQ-Synthetic dataset, they *do* query ChatGPT's API 6,000 times to generate the training examples. For the human evaluation, they query ChatGPT 255 times to produce comparison outputs.

**The consequence.** The paper's framing of imitation as a "cheap" alternative to building better base models ("cheaply improve a weaker language model"—Abstract; "the shortcut of imitating proprietary systems"—Section 1) treats the data collection cost as negligible. This is a reasonable assumption for the specific data collection strategy used (scraping public posts), but it ignores two critical costs that would apply in realistic imitation scenarios. First, **the ShareGPT-Mix strategy is parasitic on ChatGPT's popularity**: the dataset exists only because millions of users voluntarily post their ChatGPT conversations publicly. This is not a strategy that generalizes to imitating a newly-released proprietary model with few users, a model used primarily in private enterprise settings, or a model whose terms of service prohibit reposting outputs. In those cases, the imitator must query the API directly, incurring per-token costs that scale linearly with the size of the imitation dataset. Second, **the NQ-Synthetic strategy requires crafting seed examples and running an iterative generation pipeline** for each target task. For an organization attempting to replicate ChatGPT's performance across hundreds of tasks (as broad-coverage imitation aims to do), the data collection effort—prompt engineering, seed curation, API query costs, filtering—becomes substantial. The paper does not model or report these costs, making the headline claims about imitation being "cheap" unquantified and potentially misleading. An organization deciding between imitation and base-model development would need a full cost comparison; the paper provides no data to support one.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides the amount of imitation data used (0.3M–150M tokens) and the number of training examples (approximately 50K from ShareGPT, 27K from HC3, 10K from Discord, 6K from NQ-Synthetic), but does not report any associated financial cost, API latency, or human effort for data collection and cleaning. The authors spent approximately $5,000 on Mechanical Turk evaluation, which is reported (Section 4.1, footnote 3), but the corresponding cost to query ChatGPT for the 255 evaluation prompts and the 6,000 NQ-Synthetic training examples is not mentioned.

**Mitigation status.** The paper implicitly acknowledges that data collection is a bottleneck by noting that the ShareGPT-Mix approach is opportunistic ("for free without ever having to interact with the company's API"—Section 3) and that broad-coverage imitation "would require a concerted effort to collect enormous imitation datasets" (Section 4.3). However, this is framed as a *data diversity and scale* challenge rather than a *cost* challenge. The paper does not quantify costs, does not propose cost-efficient data collection strategies, and does not compare the total cost of imitation (data collection + training) against the cost of alternative approaches (e.g., using a larger open-source model without imitation). The "cheap" characterization in the abstract and introduction therefore reflects the specific data collection strategy used in the paper rather than a property of model imitation in general.

### No Experiment with RLHF or Preference-Based Fine-Tuning on Imitation Data

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies exactly one imitation method: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on ChatGPT outputs using a standard next-token prediction loss. This is the method used by the prior imitation projects the paper critiques (Alpaca, Vicuna, Koala, GPT4All), and the authors deliberately replicate it to ensure their findings are directly comparable ("we train models to imitate ChatGPT while experimenting with different decisions... and conducting rigorous automatic and human evaluations"—Section 2). However, the broader space of possible imitation methods includes approaches that could transfer capabilities more effectively than SFT, such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) where the proprietary model provides reward signals, or reinforcement learning from AI feedback (RLAIF / constitutional AI) where the proprietary model critiques and refines the imitator's outputs.

**The consequence.** The paper's conclusion that model imitation is a "false promise" is technically a conclusion about *SFT-based* imitation specifically, not about all possible imitation strategies. An RLHF-based imitation pipeline might work differently because it provides a richer training signal: rather than learning to mimic ChatGPT's output distribution for a fixed set of prompts, the imitation model would generate its own responses, receive quality scores or critiques from ChatGPT, and be updated via a reward signal that could potentially distinguish stylistic quality from factual correctness (since factual errors would receive lower rewards). The paper acknowledges this possibility explicitly in Section 5: "it may also be possible to use the target model to perform RLHF or constitutional AI (OpenAI, 2022; Christiano et al., 2017; Bai et al., 2022) to further improve results." This is listed as a "potential confounder" to the paper's findings—the authors recognize it as a live possibility but do not test it. For practitioners, this means the paper's negative results apply to the most common current imitation approach (SFT on API outputs) but do not rule out more sophisticated imitation strategies that might be developed in the future. A company deciding whether to invest in imitation vs. base-model development needs to know whether the SFT failure mode extends to RLHF-based imitation, and the paper provides no evidence either way.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. All imitation models are trained exclusively with supervised fine-tuning (Section 4.1: "We fine-tune using standard LM losses on only the model outputs"). The paper contains no experiments with RLHF, DPO, or any preference-based or reward-based training on imitation data. The "Potential confounders" paragraph in Section 5 is the only discussion of this possibility, and it is a brief acknowledgment that these methods exist rather than an analysis of whether they would alter the conclusions.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged briefly in Section 5 as a "potential confounder" but not addressed experimentally. The paper does not suggest specific RLHF or constitutional AI imitation experiments for future work, does not discuss why these methods might or might not overcome the style-vs.-substance limitation, and does not qualify its conclusions to specify that they apply to SFT-based imitation only. Given that the paper's abstract and title make unqualified claims ("The False Promise of Imitating Proprietary LLMs," "model imitation is a false promise"), this is a significant overclaim relative to the evidence, which demonstrates SFT-based imitation's failure but does not test other imitation paradigms.

### Single Family of Base Models and Single Model Architecture

**The assumption or constraint.** All imitation experiments use decoder-only autoregressive transformer language models from a single architectural family: GPT-2 1.5B (Radford et al., 2019) and LLaMA 7B/13B (Touvron et al., 2023). The authors use model scale as a proxy for base model quality (Section 4.1: "We use model scale as a proxy for base-model quality, however model quality could also improved by other factors such as the quality of pre-training data, architectural improvements, novel pre-training methods, etc."—footnote 2). The paper does not test encoder-decoder architectures (e.g., FLAN-T5, T0), mixture-of-experts models, or models with different pretraining objectives (e.g., instruction-pretrained models, retrieval-augmented models).

**The consequence.** Different model architectures and pretraining strategies may interact differently with imitation fine-tuning, potentially changing the paper's central findings. Encoder-decoder models, for instance, have a natural separation between understanding the input (encoder) and generating the output (decoder), which might make them less susceptible to the distribution-shift regressions observed on Natural Questions (Table 1: LLaMA drops from 17% to 10% after broad imitation). The encoder could continue to extract factual knowledge from its pretraining even as the decoder learns conversational output patterns, whereas in a decoder-only model, all parameters are shared between knowledge retrieval and style generation. Additionally, models pretrained with different data mixtures might have different "gaps" to ChatGPT on various knowledge benchmarks—a base model pretrained primarily on Wikipedia might have a smaller factual knowledge gap (and thus less room for improvement or degradation from imitation) than LLaMA, whose pretraining data composition is not public but is known to include a broad web crawl. The paper's finding that "scaling base models is the highest leverage action" is demonstrated within the specific scaling trajectory of the LLaMA family (1.5B → 7B → 13B). It is possible that improvements from better pretraining data or architectural innovations would produce even steeper gains, consistent with the paper's conclusion, but also possible that certain architectural choices (e.g., retrieval augmentation) would make imitation more effective by allowing the model to ground its responses in retrieved evidence rather than relying solely on memorized knowledge.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper uses three models all from the same broad family: GPT-2 1.5B and LLaMA 7B/13B are all autoregressive decoder-only transformers with similar architectural principles (causal self-attention, standard transformer blocks). The paper provides no ablation across architectures. The base model size sweep (Figure 4, bottom row) compares models within the LLaMA family plus GPT-2, which differ in pretraining data, tokenizer, and training recipe as well as size, but the key comparison (scaling model size within the LLaMA family: 7B vs. 13B) holds architecture constant.

**Mitigation status.** The architecture limitation is not discussed explicitly. The paper's conclusion that improving base LMs is the highest-leverage action lists "scaling up models, improving pre-training data quality, improving pre-training, etc." as examples (Section 1, Figure 1 caption, Section 5), which implicitly acknowledges that model quality has multiple dimensions beyond parameter count. However, the paper does not discuss whether the specific failure modes observed (style-vs.-substance divergence, benchmark regressions from distribution shift) might be architecture-dependent, and does not suggest testing across model families as future work.

### No Direct Measurement of Factual Correctness on the Same Prompts Used for Preference Evaluation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's core argument—that imitation models are rated highly by crowdworkers because they mimic style while remaining factually weak—rests on a comparison between two different sets of evaluation data. The style measurements (Table 2) and human/GPT-4 preference ratings (Figures 1, 3) are conducted on a held-out set of **255 evaluation prompts**. The factual accuracy measurements (Figure 4, Table 1) are conducted on entirely **separate canonical NLP benchmarks** (MMLU, Natural Questions, HumanEval). The paper infers that crowdworkers are deceived by stylistic mimicry on the 255 evaluation prompts because style improves on those prompts (Table 2) while factuality does not improve on the benchmarks (Figure 4). But the paper never directly measures whether the imitation model's outputs on the 255 evaluation prompts are actually less factual than ChatGPT's outputs on those same prompts. The link between the two evaluation sets is correlational, not causal.

**The consequence.** The paper's central diagnostic claim is that crowdworkers rate imitation model outputs as competitive with ChatGPT because "they are adept at mimicking ChatGPT's style—they output confident and well-structured answers" while "their factuality is weak, and crowdworkers without domain expertise or significant time investments may miss these errors" (Section 4.4). But without measuring factual correctness on the actual prompts that crowdworkers evaluated, the paper cannot rule out an alternative explanation: that the 255 evaluation prompts happen to be *easier* than the benchmark questions, and that the imitation model *is* genuinely more factual on those prompts, not just stylistically similar. If the evaluation prompts skew toward conversational, open-ended tasks rather than factual QA (which is plausible, given that they are drawn from user-submitted ChatGPT conversations), it's possible that the imitation model's style improvements genuinely improve answer quality on those specific prompts, and that the failure to transfer on MMLU/NQ/HumanEval represents a separate phenomenon (task-specific capability not covered by the imitation data) rather than evidence that style deceived raters about factuality. The qualitative example in Figure 2 (the actor-critic explanation) provides a single compelling illustration but is not a systematic measurement.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides Table 2 (style metrics on the 255 evaluation prompts) and Figure 4 (accuracy on separate benchmarks) as parallel evidence. The qualitative example in Figure 2 comes from the imitation model's output on a question about actor-critic algorithms, but it's unclear whether this example is drawn from the 255 evaluation prompts or from a separate analysis. The paper does not report: the ground-truth factual correctness distribution of the 255 evaluation prompts, the factual error rate of the imitation model vs. ChatGPT on those prompts as judged by domain experts, or the correlation between a prompt's stylistic similarity and crowdworker preference at the per-prompt level. Without these measurements, the paper's causal chain—imitation improves style → evaluators rely on style → style improvements are conflated with capability improvements—is strong on the "imitation improves style" and "evaluators rate outputs highly" links, but weaker on the "evaluators would have rated them lower if they could detect factual errors" link.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the inferential gap between the two evaluation sets, does not attempt to measure factual correctness on the 255 evaluation prompts, and does not qualify its diagnostic claims to acknowledge that the style-deception mechanism is inferred rather than directly measured. Given that paying domain experts to annotate the 255 evaluation prompts for factual correctness would cost a few thousand dollars (comparable to the $5,000 already spent on Mechanical Turk ratings), this is a feasible experiment that would substantially strengthen the paper's central argument. The paper's call for future work—"the future of human evaluation remains unclear: how can we cheaply and quickly probe the utility of a powerful LLM?" (Section 7)—acknowledges the general problem of evaluation difficulty but does not propose the specific measurement needed to close the gap in their own argument.

### No Experiment on Scaling Imitation Data to Pretraining-Like Quantities

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper tests imitation data quantities ranging from approximately 0.3 million to 150 million tokens, and concludes that scaling imitation data provides little to no benefit on factual benchmarks, that the capabilities gap "can only be bridged using an unwieldy amount of imitation data" (Abstract), and that it is "unreasonable to expect that a small amount of imitation data (e.g., 1000x less data than pre-training) would enable one to bridge this gap" (Section 4.3). This conclusion rests on the extrapolation that the flat data scaling curves observed from 25M–150M tokens (Figure 4, top row) will remain flat even at much larger data scales.

**The consequence.** The paper cannot distinguish between two importantly different hypotheses: (1) imitation data is fundamentally incapable of transferring factual knowledge regardless of quantity (the strong "false promise" claim), or (2) imitation data *can* transfer factual knowledge, but requires quantities much larger than 150M tokens—perhaps approaching pretraining scale—to do so (the weaker "current datasets are insufficient" claim). The flatness within the 25M–150M range is consistent with both hypotheses, but the shape of the curve beyond 150M tokens could differ. If the curve remains flat through 1B+ tokens, Hypothesis 1 is supported. If it begins to rise, Hypothesis 2 is supported, and the paper's title ("The False Promise") would be overly strong for what is actually a data scaling limitation. The NQ-Synthetic results (Table 1) provide some evidence for Hypothesis 2: when imitation data is concentrated on a single task (6,000 NQ-like examples), factual accuracy improves. This suggests that density of task-relevant data matters more than total data quantity, and that scaling broad-coverage data to much larger quantities might eventually provide sufficient density on each evaluation task to transfer factual knowledge. The 150M-token dataset is approximately 1000× smaller than a typical pretraining corpus (LLaMA was trained on approximately 1 trillion tokens). If the required ratio for factual transfer is, say, 1:100 rather than 1:1000, a 1.5B-token broad imitation dataset might start to show factual improvements that are invisible at 150M tokens.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The data scaling curves in Figure 4 (top row) show flat or declining accuracy from approximately 25M to 150M tokens on all three benchmarks. The paper does not test any data quantity outside this range. Table 1 shows that the much smaller NQ-Synthetic dataset (6,000 examples, perhaps a few hundred thousand tokens) produces better NQ accuracy than the much larger ShareGPT-Mix dataset (150M tokens), demonstrating that data composition matters more than raw quantity—but this doesn't answer whether scaling broad-coverage data further would eventually compensate for low per-task density. The paper cites the scale disparity explicitly: "e.g., 1000x less data than pre-training" (Section 4.3).

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged in Section 4.3, but the acknowledgment is framed as a practical objection rather than an experimental limitation: "broadly matching ChatGPT using weaker base LMs such as LLaMA-13B would require a concerted effort to collect an extremely large and diverse imitation dataset that is far closer to the scale of pretraining. It is currently unclear whether such an effort is worth undertaking or feasible." The paper does not test whether such an effort *would succeed if undertaken*—it only shows that it hasn't been done and that smaller efforts fail. The "unclear whether such an effort is worth undertaking or feasible" phrasing is appropriately cautious, but the paper's stronger claims ("false promise," "can only be bridged using an unwieldy amount") treat the failure as more definitive than the evidence supports. A single data point beyond 150M tokens—even 500M tokens—would provide some indication of whether the flat curve is genuinely asymptotic or just flat in a local regime. The paper does not provide such a data point and does not discuss the practical or computational barriers to obtaining one. For a practitioner deciding whether to invest in larger-scale imitation data collection, the paper provides the useful information that 150M tokens is insufficient for factual transfer; it does not provide evidence about whether 1.5B tokens would be sufficient.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not introduce a new model, training method, or dataset. It causes a shift through **diagnosis and reframing** — by showing that the dominant evaluation paradigm for imitation models (pairwise preference ratings by humans or GPT-4) systematically conflates stylistic mimicry with factual capability transfer, and that the resulting optimistic claims about open-source models catching up to proprietary systems are artifacts of this measurement failure. This is not a paradigm shift in the Kuhnian sense (the paper does not propose a new theory of fine-tuning), but it is a **methodological corrective** with significant practical consequences for how the field evaluates and invests in model development.

The specific change is this: before this paper, the default narrative — driven by projects like Alpaca, Vicuna, Koala, and GPT4All, and amplified by industry analysis such as Patel and Ahmad (2023) — held that open-source models fine-tuned on proprietary model outputs were rapidly closing the capabilities gap, achieving "90% of ChatGPT quality" (Vicuna) and threatening the competitive moats of major AI companies. The evaluation evidence for these claims came almost entirely from pairwise preference comparisons. This paper demonstrates that the same evaluation methodology, applied to models constructed using the same recipe (LLaMA base, ShareGPT-derived data, SFT on outputs), produces the same encouraging numbers (~70% of outputs rated equal or better than ChatGPT by crowdworkers, Figure 1 left) — but that targeted automatic benchmarks measuring factual knowledge (MMLU, Natural Questions), coding ability (HumanEval), and broad academic reasoning tell a fundamentally different story: imitation models close **little to none** of the capabilities gap, and in some cases actively regress relative to their base models (Figure 4, top row; Table 1: NQ drops from 20% to 15% for LLaMA-13B after broad imitation).

The paper thus **recasts the optimistic narrative as an evaluation failure**, not a genuine capabilities advance. This is a reframing of the strategic question: the open-source vs. proprietary competition is not about access to high-quality fine-tuning data (which imitation can cheaply acquire), but about the raw capabilities of the base pretrained model — and that gap remains large even after imitation. The contrast in Figure 4 between the flat data-scaling curves (top row) and the rising parameter-scaling curves (bottom row) makes this reframing quantitative: moving from 7B to 13B parameters improves accuracy across benchmarks far more than scaling imitation data from 25M to 150M tokens. The slopes tell the strategic story.

The paper also **reconciles several tensions** in the prior literature:

- **Why do imitation models feel so much better than base models?** Because they learn ChatGPT's output style — list usage concordance rises from 13% to 81%, authoritative tone from 57% to 98%, length correlation from -0.11 to 0.62 (Table 2) — and these stylistic improvements dominate human quality judgments. The feeling of improvement is real, but it measures the wrong thing.
- **Why do some prior works find imitation works (e.g., Honovich et al., 2022; Hsieh et al., 2023) while this paper finds it doesn't?** Because "works" means different things in different contexts. Task-specific imitation (NQ-Synthetic, Table 1) demonstrably improves task accuracy — confirming the positive results from narrow-domain imitation studies. Broad-coverage imitation (ShareGPT-Mix) does not transfer factual capabilities, explaining why projects aiming for general-purpose parity (Alpaca, Vicuna) produce models that feel good but underperform on knowledge-intensive benchmarks.
- **Why does training on more imitation data sometimes hurt?** Because the fine-tuning distribution (conversational, verbose, multi-turn dialogue) differs substantially from the evaluation distribution for factual QA benchmarks (short exact-match answers). The regression on Natural Questions (20% → 15% for 13B, Table 1) is a concrete instance of catastrophic interference: the model learns to produce ChatGPT-style answers instead of retrieving concise factual responses. The paper's framing of fine-tuning as "knowledge extraction rather than knowledge injection" (Section 5, citing Schulman, 2023) provides a theoretical explanation: fine-tuning changes how the model presents what it knows, but in doing so can disrupt existing retrieval pathways.

**Research directions that become more attractive** after this paper:

- **Better base LMs.** The paper's central policy implication is that improving open-source base models — through scaling, better pretraining data, architectural innovations, or novel pretraining objectives — is the highest-leverage investment for closing the gap to proprietary systems. Every additional unit of effort spent on imitation data collection would be better spent on base model development (Figure 1, right; Figure 4, bottom row). This redirects attention from the relatively accessible task of scraping API outputs to the much harder and more expensive task of pretraining better foundation models.
- **Mechanistic understanding of fine-tuning.** The paper's reframing of fine-tuning as extraction rather than injection raises specific, testable questions: what gets overwritten during fine-tuning that causes benchmark regressions? Can we measure knowledge retention directly (e.g., through probing classifiers) before and after imitation? Are there fine-tuning methods (regularization, data mixing, parameter-efficient adaptation) that preserve factual knowledge while improving style and instruction-following? The paper notes that mixing pretraining data during fine-tuning is "an open problem" (Section 4.3).
- **Evaluation methodology for LMs.** The paper's demonstration that both human crowdworkers and GPT-4 exhibit the same style-over-substance bias in pairwise evaluations (Figure 3: both show flat data-scaling curves for preference ratings while automatic benchmarks show no factual improvement) is a methodological finding with implications beyond imitation. It challenges the validity of LLM-based evaluation (increasingly used as a cheap substitute for human evaluation since Vicuna popularized it) and raises the broader question: if pairwise preferences are systematically insensitive to factual errors, what evaluation protocols *can* reliably measure genuine capability transfer? The paper does not solve this, explicitly noting "the future of human evaluation remains unclear" (Section 7), but it provides a diagnostic template: run *both* preference evaluations and targeted capability benchmarks, and treat divergence between them as a red flag.

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Incremental scaling of broad-coverage imitation datasets.** The paper shows that increasing ShareGPT-Mix data from 25M to 150M tokens produces no improvement on MMLU, HumanEval, or NQ (and possible regression on NQ — Figure 4, top row). Unless there is a qualitative change in the data scaling curve at much larger quantities (which the paper does not test but reasonably argues would require implausibly large datasets), collecting more data of the same kind is a dead end for capability transfer. The NQ-Synthetic results (Table 1) suggest that high-density task-specific data works, but broad low-density data does not — so the lesson is not "collect more data" but "collect *different* data" (concentrated on target capabilities).
- **Claims of parity based solely on pairwise preference evaluations.** The paper provides a clear, replicable counterexample: models that score competitively on pairwise preferences (~70% "≥ ChatGPT") can simultaneously show no improvement on canonical benchmarks. Any future imitation project that reports only preference ratings should be met with the question: "What do the MMLU, NQ, and HumanEval numbers look like?" The paper effectively raises the evidence bar for claims of capability transfer through imitation.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Directly measure factual correctness on the same prompts used for preference evaluation.** The paper's central diagnostic — that crowdworkers rate imitation outputs highly because they mistake stylistic quality for factual correctness — is inferred from two separate evaluation sets: style metrics and preference ratings on 255 held-out prompts (Table 2, Figure 1 left), and factual accuracy on different benchmarks (Figure 4). The causal chain would be substantially strengthened by a single experiment: hire domain experts to annotate the factual correctness of both ChatGPT and imitation model outputs on the 255 evaluation prompts, then test whether prompts where the imitation model is rated equal-or-better by crowdworkers have higher factual error rates than prompts where it is rated worse. The paper's qualitative example in Figure 2 (the actor-critic explanation) provides a vivid single case; systematic annotation across all 255 prompts would quantify the effect. This is feasible within a similar budget to the existing Mechanical Turk evaluation (~$5,000) and would close the most significant inferential gap in the paper's argument. A strong follow-up would also measure whether GPT-4 evaluations track human factual error detection — or fail to, as the aggregate pattern in Figure 3 suggests.

**Test whether mixing pretraining data during imitation fine-tuning prevents benchmark regressions.** The paper documents that broad-coverage imitation degrades performance on Natural Questions (Table 1: 17% → 10% for 7B, 20% → 15% for 13B) and produces modest regressions on MMLU and HumanEval (Figure 4, top). The authors hypothesize that these regressions arise from "distribution shift and tension between the conversational-style fine-tuning data and the downstream benchmarks" and note that "an open problem is whether these performance regressions can be mitigated using regularization or by mixing in pre-training data during fine-tuning" (Section 4.3). A direct follow-up would replicate the ShareGPT-Mix training runs but mix the imitation data with a general pretraining corpus (e.g., C4, The Pile, or a held-out sample of LLaMA's own pretraining data) at varying ratios (10%, 25%, 50%, 75% pretraining data). The key measurement is whether NQ accuracy remains at or above the base model's performance while still achieving the stylistic improvements in Table 2. If a modest amount of pretraining data prevents catastrophic forgetting of factual knowledge, imitation becomes substantially more practical — the model keeps its existing knowledge while acquiring ChatGPT's conversational style. If not, the interference is more fundamental and may require architectural solutions (e.g., parameter-efficient fine-tuning that preserves the original model's knowledge retrieval pathways).

**Extend the data scaling curve beyond 150M tokens to test whether factual transfer eventually emerges.** The paper's flat data-scaling curves (Figure 4, top) cover 25M to 150M tokens of broad imitation data. The conclusion that scaling imitation data is ineffective rests partially on the extrapolation that these curves remain flat at larger scales. This is a reasonable prior — the curves show no upward trend, and the authors note that 150M tokens is already ~1000× less than pretraining scale — but it is an extrapolation, not a direct measurement. A strong follow-up (requiring substantial compute — training at 1B, 5B, or 10B tokens of imitation data) would test whether the curves remain flat or begin to rise. The key comparison: at what data scale, if any, does the LLaMA-13B imitation model match the LLaMA-13B base model's NQ accuracy again (recovering from the observed regression), and at what scale does it exceed the base model (demonstrating genuine knowledge transfer from ChatGPT outputs)? The paper's NQ-Synthetic result (6K task-specific examples improve accuracy) suggests that *density* of task-relevant data matters more than total quantity — scaling broad data to 1B tokens would test whether density eventually becomes sufficient simply through volume, or whether the signal from task-relevant facts is permanently diluted by irrelevant conversational data even at large scales.

**Test imitation across multiple proprietary target models to assess generality.** Every experiment in the paper imitates exactly one target model: ChatGPT (the API version available in early 2023). The paper's conclusions refer to "proprietary LLMs" in general, but the specific pattern — style transfers easily, factual knowledge does not — might depend on ChatGPT's particular training recipe, output style, or knowledge distribution. A direct extension would replicate the core experiment with other proprietary models as imitation targets: Claude (Anthropic), Gemini (Google), or GPT-4 (OpenAI). The key measurements are: (1) Does the style-vs.-substance divergence replicate across target models, or do some models' outputs contain denser factual signals that transfer through SFT? (2) Do different target models produce different regression patterns on benchmarks — e.g., does imitating a model trained with constitutional AI (Claude) produce less catastrophic forgetting because its outputs are more factually grounded and less stylistically verbose? (3) If GPT-4 is the target, does the larger imitation data requirement (since the capabilities gap is even larger) change the shape of the data scaling curve? This experiment would test whether the paper's findings are about imitation as a technique or about imitating ChatGPT specifically.

**Train an imitation model using RLHF with the proprietary model as a reward signal.** The paper only tests supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on static ChatGPT outputs. Section 5 acknowledges that "it may also be possible to use the target model to perform RLHF or constitutional AI (OpenAI, 2022; Christiano et al., 2017; Bai et al., 2022) to further improve results" and lists this as a potential confounder that could change the conclusions. A critical follow-up would test whether an RLHF-based imitation pipeline — where the imitation model generates candidate responses, the proprietary model scores them for quality (or provides preference judgments between pairs of outputs), and the imitation model is updated via a reward signal (e.g., PPO or DPO) — transfers factual knowledge more effectively than SFT. The hypothesis: RLHF provides a richer training signal because the model explores its own output space during training rather than being forced to mimic a fixed distribution, and the proprietary model's quality judgments might implicitly penalize factual errors (especially if prompts are chosen to test factual knowledge). The counter-hypothesis: RLHF still primarily optimizes for style (since the proprietary model's preferences, like human preferences, may be style-dominated), and the factual knowledge gap remains. This experiment directly addresses the scope of the paper's "false promise" claim: does it apply only to SFT-based imitation, or to all feasible imitation strategies?

**Develop and validate evaluation protocols that are sensitive to the style-vs.-substance distinction.** The paper demonstrates that existing evaluation methodologies (crowdworker pairwise preferences, GPT-4 pairwise preferences) fail to detect significant factual weaknesses in imitation models. It does not, however, propose a solution. A natural follow-up is to develop evaluation protocols that are specifically designed to be *insensitive* to style and *sensitive* to factual correctness. Concrete approaches: (1) targeted factual probes — curate a set of prompts where the correct answer is a single unambiguous fact (e.g., "What year did X happen?") and measure exact-match accuracy, where stylistic variation is irrelevant; (2) adversarial evaluation — deliberately prompt models in ways that invite confident-sounding but incorrect answers (e.g., asking about obscure or non-existent entities) and measure hallucination rates; (3) expert-in-the-loop rubrics — develop structured scoring protocols that require raters to separately judge style (fluency, structure, tone) and substance (factual accuracy, reasoning validity) rather than a single subjective quality rating; (4) calibrated LLM-based evaluation — test whether prompting GPT-4 to explicitly check factual claims in an output (by asking it to identify and verify specific assertions) produces ratings that better correlate with true factual correctness than unguided pairwise comparisons. The paper's finding that GPT-4 evaluations track human evaluations (Figure 3) suggests that naive LLM-as-judge does not solve the problem; structured evaluation protocols might.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Reallocating open-source development effort from imitation data collection to base model improvement.** The paper's most actionable practical finding — grounded in Figure 1 (right) and Figure 4 (bottom) — is that scaling the base model (from 7B to 13B parameters) improves benchmark accuracy substantially more than scaling imitation data (from 25M to 150M tokens). For an open-source AI lab deciding how to allocate a fixed budget: spending on compute for pretraining a larger or better base model yields higher returns on downstream capability than spending on API credits to collect more imitation data or on human annotators to curate broader imitation datasets. The specific numbers: moving from LLaMA-7B to LLaMA-13B improves NQ accuracy from ~10% to ~15% after ShareGPT-Mix imitation (Table 1), while adding ~125M more tokens of imitation data to the 13B model yields no improvement (Figure 4, top-right: the blue curve is flat or declining from 25M to 150M tokens). This is not a small reallocation — pretraining a 13B model costs millions of dollars, while collecting 150M tokens of ChatGPT outputs costs thousands — but the paper's evidence suggests the expensive investment is the one that actually moves the needle on capabilities.

**Flagging dangerous over-trust of stylistically convincing but factually weak models in user-facing deployments.** The paper demonstrates that imitation models "embody some of the worst aspects of AI assistants: their answers sound confident but are less factual than ChatGPT" (Section 4.4). The quantitative evidence: after 150M tokens of imitation data, the model's outputs match ChatGPT's authoritative tone 98% of the time (Table 2), while its factual accuracy on Natural Questions drops from 20% to 15% (Table 1). For organizations deploying imitation-based chatbots in high-stakes domains (medical advice, legal guidance, educational tutoring, technical support), this is a concrete warning: the model will *sound* authoritative and may receive positive user satisfaction ratings (as the ~70% "≥ ChatGPT" crowdworker preference scores would suggest), while actually giving incorrect information more frequently than the base model would have. The practical implication is that user satisfaction ratings or pairwise preference evaluations are insufficient quality control for such deployments; systematic factual accuracy testing on domain-relevant benchmarks must be part of the deployment pipeline. The paper provides a template: run both preference evaluations (to measure user-perceived quality) and targeted automatic evaluations (to measure actual accuracy), and monitor divergence between them.

**Task-specific imitation for narrow deployment scenarios where it demonstrably works.** The paper's NQ-Synthetic experiment (Table 1) shows that imitation *does* work when the training data is concentrated on the target task: 6,000 ChatGPT-generated Natural-Questions-like QA pairs improve factual QA accuracy from 17% to 22% for LLaMA-7B and from 20% to 27% for LLaMA-13B. For a practitioner with a specific, well-defined task (e.g., customer support for a product with a known set of FAQs, extracting structured fields from legal documents, answering factual questions about a specific knowledge base), the paper's evidence supports a targeted imitation strategy: identify the task, use ChatGPT (or another proprietary model) to generate a few thousand task-specific training examples, fine-tune a base model on those examples, and evaluate on a held-out task-specific test set. This is substantially more effective than training on a broad conversational dataset and hoping the task is covered incidentally. The caveat: the improvement is task-specific; the NQ-Synthetic model's gains likely do not transfer to other factual QA benchmarks (the paper does not test this, but the logic of narrow imitation implies it). The practical recipe is: imitation for narrow, well-defined tasks with task-aligned training data; base model scaling for broad capability improvement.

**Leveraging imitation for safety and persona transfer when the base model is already capable.** Figure 5 shows that imitation successfully transfers ChatGPT's refusal behavior on toxic prompts: non-toxicity score rises from 0.86 (base LLaMA-13B) to 0.91 (150M tokens of imitation data), approaching ChatGPT's 0.92. This is a genuinely practical use case: if an organization has access to a capable base model but lacks the resources to conduct its own RLHF safety training, fine-tuning on a modest amount of proprietary model outputs (with appropriate prompt coverage of edge cases) can induce similar refusal and safety behaviors. The key condition, which the paper makes clear, is that the base model must already possess the underlying capabilities — the safety transfer works because refusal behavior is a stylistic/persona pattern that fine-tuning can teach, not because the model is acquiring new knowledge. For a company deploying an open-source 13B model in a consumer-facing chatbot and wanting to reduce toxic outputs, a few tens of millions of tokens of ChatGPT conversation data might be a cost-effective way to induce safer behavior, even if it doesn't improve factual accuracy (which the company might not need if the chatbot's domain is narrow enough that the base model's knowledge is already sufficient).
