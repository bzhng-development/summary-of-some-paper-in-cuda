# EAGLE-3: Scaling up Inference Acceleration of Large Language Models via Training-Time Test

**ArXiv:** [2503.01840](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01840)

## 🎯 Pitch

Draft models for speculative sampling have never benefited from scaling training data—until now. EAGLE-3 overturns this by predicting tokens directly and fusing multi-layer features, unlocking a proportional speedup-scaling law where doubling data yields faster generation, and delivering up to a 6.5× speedup over standard decoding.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **EAGLE-3**, a speculative sampling method that accelerates LLM inference by redesigning the draft model to directly predict tokens rather than features, and replaces reliance on the target model's top-layer features with a multi-layer feature fusion technique called **training-time test** (simulating multi-step generation during training by feeding the draft model's own outputs back as input). Evaluated on MT-bench, HumanEval, GSM8K, Alpaca, and CNN/Daily Mail using LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B, Vicuna 13B, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B, EAGLE-3 achieves speedup ratios up to 6.5×—a roughly 1.4× improvement over EAGLE-2—and demonstrates a previously unobserved **scaling law for inference acceleration**, where expanding the draft model's training data produces proportional gains in speedup, establishing that the feature prediction constraint in prior EAGLE variants was the bottleneck preventing data scaling benefits from materializing.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLM Inference Is Slow and Expensive

The fundamental challenge this paper addresses is the **speed and cost of autoregressive generation** in large language models. Modern LLMs generate text one token at a time, and each token requires accessing all model parameters—some models now exceed hundreds of billions of parameters. This sequential dependency creates a bottleneck: generation is **memory-bound** rather than compute-bound, meaning the hardware spends most of its time waiting on memory transfers rather than performing useful computation. The practical consequences are severe: high latency for users, massive operating costs for providers, and throughput limitations that constrain how many users can be served simultaneously.

This challenge has intensified recently due to a trend the paper explicitly names: **test-time scaling up**. Models like ChatGPT o1 and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) engage in deliberate, lengthy reasoning before responding—sometimes producing thousands of reasoning tokens before generating a single answer token. As the paper states in Section 1:

> "these models often require lengthy reasoning processes, making them extremely costly, while the increased response time severely impacts user satisfaction"

This means the proportion of inference cost in the overall LLM pipeline is growing dramatically. A model that outputs a 10-word answer after 1,000 words of internal reasoning has effectively shifted its operational cost profile from training-dominated to inference-dominated. For reasoning models, accelerating inference isn't just a nice-to-have—it's essential for economic viability and user experience.

### Why This Problem Is Hard: The Nature of the Bottleneck

To understand why inference acceleration is difficult, we need to grasp the asymmetry between training and inference in transformer models. During training, the entire input sequence is available simultaneously, enabling parallel computation across all positions. During inference, however, each new token depends on all previous tokens, creating a strict sequential dependency: the model cannot compute token $t+1$ until token $t$ is fully processed. This means the arithmetic intensity (the ratio of computation to memory access) is low during decoding, and the hardware's computational units sit idle while waiting for the next token's data to be fetched.

Techniques like quantization (reducing numerical precision) and distillation (training smaller models) can help, but they almost always involve a **quality–speed tradeoff**—the accelerated model performs worse than the original. For many applications, particularly those involving mathematical reasoning or factual accuracy, even small degradations are unacceptable. This creates a demand for **lossless acceleration**: methods that produce exactly the same outputs as the unmodified model, just faster.

### The Speculative Sampling Solution and Its Limitations

Speculative sampling (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) has emerged as the leading lossless acceleration technique. The idea is clever: rather than forcing the large "target" model to generate tokens one-by-one, use a small, fast "draft" model to propose several tokens ahead, then have the target model verify them in a single parallel forward pass. If the draft tokens are accepted (they match what the target model would have produced), multiple tokens are generated for the cost of one target model forward pass. If a draft token is rejected, the process resamples from the target model's distribution at that position and discards subsequent drafts. Crucially, the acceptance/rejection mechanism ensures the output distribution is mathematically identical to standard autoregressive generation—hence "lossless."

However, vanilla speculative sampling has a fundamental limitation: **the draft model operates independently of the target model**. The draft model is typically a smaller version from the same model series (e.g., Vicuna-68M for Vicuna-13B), and it must approximate the target model's distribution using only its own limited capacity. This is inherently difficult because the draft model has fewer parameters, less training, and no access to the target model's internal states. The result is limited acceleration—the paper shows vanilla speculative sampling achieves only about 1.6× speedup at temperature 1 (Table 1, Vicuna 13B on MT-bench).

### EAGLE's Innovation: Leveraging the Target Model's Features

EAGLE (Li et al., 2024c) addressed this limitation by making the draft model **dependent on the target model**. Rather than a separate, independent LM, EAGLE's draft model takes the target model's **top-layer features** as input—specifically, the features immediately before the LM head. These features contain rich information about what the target model is about to predict: the features corresponding to the logits of the next token are unique, so they directly encode information about the upcoming token distribution.

Furthermore, EAGLE performs autoregression at the **feature level** rather than the token level. Given a sequence of top-layer features $f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_t$ from the target model, the draft model predicts the next feature $\hat{f}_{t+1}$, which is then fed into the target model's LM head to obtain the draft token distribution. This is a fundamentally different approach from vanilla speculative sampling: instead of predicting tokens from tokens, EAGLE predicts features from features, using the target model's own internal representations as a high-bandwidth information channel.

EAGLE's loss function has two components (Section 2.2, Figure 3): a **feature prediction loss** $\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$ that encourages the draft model's output to match the target model's ground-truth feature, and a **token prediction loss** $\mathcal{L}_{\text{token}}$ that encourages the resulting token distribution (after passing through the LM head) to match the ground-truth token. This dual objective allows the draft model trained only at Step 1 (predicting one step ahead) to adapt to multi-step prediction at test time, because the feature-level autoregression creates a form of compositional generalization.

EAGLE also introduces **tree-structured drafting**: rather than generating a single chain of draft tokens, it generates multiple candidates at each position, forming a tree. During verification, the target model evaluates all branches in parallel using tree attention. This increases the average number of accepted tokens per verification pass. EAGLE-2 (Li et al., 2024b) further improved this by making the draft tree **dynamic and context-aware**, pruning unpromising branches based on the draft model's confidence estimates.

The results were substantial: EAGLE achieves 2.4–3.1× speedup (Table 1), and EAGLE-2 achieves 2.8–4.2× speedup, significantly outperforming vanilla speculative sampling. HASS (Zhang et al., 2024) and Falcon (Gao et al., 2024) adopted similar feature-prediction approaches.

### The Hidden Bottleneck: Feature Prediction as a Constraint

Despite these successes, the paper identifies a critical limitation that was previously unrecognized: **the feature prediction constraint limits the draft model's capacity to benefit from more training data**. This is the gap that EAGLE-3 addresses.

The authors observe that when they increase the draft model's training data for EAGLE, the gains are limited. This is a striking finding because the broader LLM community has consistently shown that scaling training data improves performance—LLaMA 1, 2, and 3 used 1T, 2T, and 15T tokens respectively while keeping architecture similar, and saw substantial improvements. Why doesn't EAGLE's draft model benefit similarly?

The paper's diagnosis (Section 1, Figure 3, Figure 4) identifies the root cause: **feature prediction is an unnecessary constraint** that limits the draft model's expressive power. To understand why, consider what the feature prediction loss enforces:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}} = \|\hat{f}_{t+1} - f_{t+1}\|^2$$

where $\hat{f}_{t+1}$ is the draft model's predicted feature and $f_{t+1}$ is the target model's ground-truth top-layer feature. This loss forces the draft model's output to lie in the same space as the target model's internal representations. But the ultimate goal is to predict the correct **token**, not the correct **feature**. A draft model that predicts exactly the right token distribution might map to a slightly different feature vector than the target model's, and would be penalized by $\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$ despite achieving perfect token prediction. Conversely, the model might learn to predict features that match well but don't translate to accurate token distributions.

This is the paper's key insight: **feature prediction is a surrogate objective that doesn't perfectly align with the true objective of token prediction**. The surrogate nature of the objective creates a bottleneck—as the draft model gets more capacity and more data, the misalignment between predicting features and predicting tokens becomes the limiting factor. The model cannot fully exploit additional training data because it's being optimized for a proxy task rather than the actual task.

The paper demonstrates this empirically in Figure 4. When they remove the feature prediction loss (the middle configuration in Figure 3, "EAGLE + $\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$ removal"), the acceptance rate of the first draft token ($0$-$\alpha$) improves substantially with more training data. However, a new problem emerges: the output of the draft model in Step 1, denoted $\hat{a}_{t+1}$, is far from the ground-truth feature $f_{t+1}$. When this output is used as input for Step 2 (since the draft model feeds its own previous output forward), the input sequence $f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_t, \hat{a}_{t+1}$ deviates significantly from the training distribution. This causes the acceptance rate for the second draft token ($1$-$\alpha$) to drop dramatically, as shown in Figure 4. The model trained to take ground-truth features as input fails when given its own slightly-off predictions.

### The Training-Time Test Solution

This brings us to the paper's central methodological innovation: **training-time test**. The idea is to simulate the multi-step generation process **during training**, so the draft model learns to handle its own potentially-noisy outputs as input for subsequent steps.

The name is descriptive: during training, the model performs "test" steps—it generates predictions, feeds them back, and continues training on the resulting augmented sequences. This is not the same as test-time behavior (where the model encounters its own outputs in deployment); it's training the model to be robust to its own errors by explicitly exposing it to such scenarios during optimization.

The bottom part of Figure 3 illustrates this. In Step 1, the draft model produces $\hat{a}_{t+1}$ from the ground-truth feature sequence. Unlike the middle configuration where this output is discarded during training (only used at test time), in the training-time test configuration, $\hat{a}_{t+1}$ is fed back as input for Step 2. The model then predicts $\hat{a}_{t+2}$, and the token prediction loss is applied at both steps. This means the model **jointly optimizes** for accuracy at Step 1 and accuracy at Step 2 conditioned on Step 1's output—it learns to correct for or compensate for small errors in its own predictions.

The paper notes that HASS (Zhang et al., 2024) also modifies the attention mechanism to simulate multi-step drafting during training, but draws a sharp distinction:

> "The motivation behind HASS is to mitigate the error accumulation caused by inaccurate feature predictions in EAGLE. HASS still performs feature prediction, includes a feature prediction loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$, and the input to the draft model must be the top-layer features. In contrast, the motivation behind EAGLE-3 is to remove unnecessary constraints to enhance the model's expressive power."

In other words, HASS patches the feature prediction approach; EAGLE-3 replaces it entirely. HASS tries to make feature prediction more robust to errors, while EAGLE-3 recognizes that feature prediction itself is the wrong objective.

### Multi-Layer Feature Fusion: Beyond the Top Layer

The removal of the feature prediction constraint unlocks a second major improvement: the draft model no longer needs its input to approximate the target model's top-layer features. In EAGLE and HASS, the input **must** be top-layer features (or close approximations) because the training objective includes a loss term comparing the output to ground-truth top-layer features. Removing $\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$ eliminates this requirement entirely.

This freedom allows EAGLE-3 to use **features from multiple layers** of the target model, not just the top layer. The paper argues (Section 1) that:

> "predicting the next-next token based solely on top-layer features—which are inherently limited to the next token—poses a significant challenge"

The top-layer features (immediately before the LM head) are optimized to predict the immediate next token. They represent a highly compressed, task-specific representation that discards much of the semantic richness available in intermediate layers. Lower and middle layers capture different types of information: syntactic structure, semantic relationships, entity tracking, and discourse-level features that may be relevant for predicting tokens further ahead.

By fusing low-, middle-, and high-level features (denoted $l$, $m$, and $h$ in Figure 5), the draft model gains access to a richer, more diverse signal. The fusion is straightforward: concatenate the $k$-dimensional vectors $l$, $m$, and $h$ (each of dimension $k$, the target model's hidden size), pass through a fully connected layer that reduces the $3k$-dimensional concatenation back to $k$ dimensions, producing a fused feature $g$. This fused feature serves as the input to the draft model's single decoder layer.

### The Scaling Law Discovery

The paper frames the consequences of these architectural changes as a **discovery of a scaling law for inference acceleration**. Figure 1 shows the key finding: for EAGLE-2, increasing training data from 1× to 8× (relative to ShareGPT) produces **negligible improvements** in speedup ratio and average acceptance length. For EAGLE-3, the same data increase produces a clear upward trend—speedup goes from roughly 3.5× to 4.4×, and average acceptance length from roughly 4.8 to 6.1.

This is significant because it changes the nature of draft model development from a search for better architectures to a more straightforward data-scaling approach. The paper explicitly connects to the broader LLM scaling trend:

> "Similarly, we aim to improve the acceptance rate and acceleration ratio of EAGLE by increasing its training data."

The fact that EAGLE-3 exhibits a scaling law where EAGLE-2 does not validates the paper's diagnosis: the feature prediction constraint was the bottleneck preventing data scaling from materializing as performance improvements. The training-time test architecture removes this bottleneck, making the draft model's performance a monotonic function of training data scale.

### How EAGLE-3 Positions Itself

EAGLE-3 does not introduce a fundamentally new paradigm—it remains within the speculative sampling framework of alternating drafting and verification, and it preserves EAGLE-2's dynamic draft tree mechanism for context-aware drafting. Rather, it identifies and removes a **bottleneck in the draft model architecture** that was invisible in earlier work because the full data scaling curve had never been systematically explored.

The paper's positioning is therefore: EAGLE-3 is an **architectural refinement** that unlocks a **qualitatively new scaling behavior**. The method itself is simple—remove a loss term, add training-time feedback, and use multi-layer features—but the implications are substantial because they demonstrate that inference acceleration can benefit from the same data scaling trends that have driven LLM capability improvements. This creates a clear research direction: invest in larger, higher-quality draft model training datasets, with confidence that the gains will materialize.

This also positions EAGLE-3 against prior work that found scaling limitations. HASS tried to fix EAGLE's error accumulation while preserving feature prediction; EAGLE-3 shows that feature prediction itself was the root cause, not error accumulation per se. Medusa and Hydra use multiple prediction heads operating on top-layer features; EAGLE-3 suggests these methods are likewise feature-constrained and may not benefit from data scaling. The paper provides a unifying diagnosis: any method that uses top-layer features as a prediction target inherits an implicit constraint that limits expressive power and data scalability.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

EAGLE-3 is a **speculative sampling draft model** that plugs into a standard LLM inference pipeline—it rapidly produces candidate tokens for a large "target" LLM to verify in parallel, making generation faster without changing any output. The problem it solves is that prior draft models (EAGLE, EAGLE-2, HASS) were constrained by predicting *features* rather than *tokens* and by relying exclusively on top-layer representations, which created a bottleneck that prevented the draft model from improving when given more training data. The solution is a two-part architectural redesign: (1) remove the feature prediction objective entirely, allowing the draft model to directly predict tokens, and (2) replace top-layer feature reuse with a fusion of features from low, middle, and high layers of the target model, while using a **training-time test** procedure that simulates multi-step drafting during optimization so the model learns to handle its own possibly-noisy outputs as subsequent inputs.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The EAGLE-3 system has four major components:

1. **The Target LLM** — the large pretrained language model being accelerated (e.g., LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, Vicuna 13B). It generates tokens autoregressively and, crucially, exposes its **internal features** from multiple layers: low-level features `$l$`, middle-level features `$m$`, and high-level features `$h$` (the top-layer representation immediately before the LM head is just one of these).

2. **The Feature Fusion Module** — a fully connected (FC) layer that takes the concatenated features `$[l; m; h]$` (a `$3k$`-dimensional vector, where `$k$` is the target model's hidden size) and projects them down to a single `$k$`-dimensional fused feature `$g$`. This `$g$` integrates semantic information from multiple representational depths.

3. **The Draft Model** — a single Transformer decoder layer that takes as input the fused feature `$g_t$` at the current position and the embedding `$e_t$` of the previously sampled token, concatenates them, projects via an FC layer to `$k$` dimensions, and produces an output vector `$a_{t+1}$`. This `$a_{t+1}$` is fed through the target model's **frozen LM head** to obtain a token distribution from which draft tokens are sampled. The draft model is the only component trained; the target model is completely frozen.

4. **The Dynamic Draft Tree Mechanism** (inherited from EAGLE-2) — after the draft model generates candidates, this mechanism constructs a tree-structured draft (multiple candidate tokens at each position) with context-aware pruning based on the draft model's confidence scores. The target model verifies this tree in one parallel forward pass using tree attention.

Information flow at inference: Prompt enters target model → target model computes features `$l, m, h$` for all prefix positions (during prefill or previous verification) → Feature Fusion Module produces `$g$` vectors → Draft Model uses `$g$` sequence plus embedding of last sampled token to predict output `$a$` → LM head converts `$a$` to token distribution → draft token sampled → sampled token's embedding fed back to Draft Model along with newly generated `$a$` (which substitutes for unavailable `$g$` from target model) for next step → process repeats to build draft tree → target model verifies tree in one forward pass → accepted tokens appended to output → system alternates between drafting and verification.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the feature prediction constraint in prior EAGLE variants—what it is mathematically, why it was included originally, and the precise mechanism by which it bottlenecks data scaling—because understanding the constraint explains why every subsequent design choice is necessary.
- **Second**, the removal of feature prediction and the shift to direct token prediction—why direct token prediction is possible after architectural changes, what the new objective function looks like, and how the first-token acceptance rate improves while multi-step acceptance collapses without the next innovation.
- **Third**, the training-time test procedure—how multi-step generation is simulated during training, the modified attention mechanism that handles tree-structured training sequences efficiently, and why this addresses the distribution-shift problem exposed in step two.
- **Fourth**, the multi-layer feature fusion—why top-layer features alone are insufficient for predicting tokens beyond the immediate next step, how the fusion is implemented, and why training-time test is a prerequisite for using non-top-layer features.
- **Fifth**, the complete inference pipeline—how the draft model operates at test time given that features `$g$` are unavailable for future positions that haven't been verified yet, and how the dynamic draft tree from EAGLE-2 is integrated.
- **Sixth**, training data and hyperparameters—exactly what data is used, how it's generated, and the key training configurations, since the scaling-law claim depends on data quantity and quality.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems design paper** whose core idea is that the feature-prediction objective in prior EAGLE variants is an unnecessary surrogate constraint that limits expressive power and prevents the draft model from benefiting from additional training data; removing this constraint, combined with a training-time test procedure that teaches the model to handle its own outputs as subsequent inputs, unlocks a scaling law where data scale monotonically improves draft quality and thus inference speedup.

#### The Feature Prediction Constraint: What It Was and Why It Bottlenecks Scaling

To understand EAGLE-3's design, we must first understand precisely what EAGLE and EAGLE-2's draft model was trained to do and why that created a hard ceiling on performance.

In EAGLE (Li et al., 2024c), the draft model operates on **features**, not tokens. When the target model processes a prefix and produces the next token, it computes a sequence of representations at each transformer layer. The representation at the final layer, immediately before the LM head, is called the **top-layer feature**, denoted `$f_t$` for position `$t$`. This feature has a special property: because the LM head's weight matrix `$W_{\text{LM}}$` is full-rank (for models with a standard tied or untied embedding), the mapping from `$f_t$` to the next-token logits is unique—`$f_t$` contains all information necessary to determine the token distribution at position `$t+1$`. Specifically, the logits are `$z_{t+1} = W_{\text{LM}} f_t$` (plus bias), and the next-token distribution is `$p(t_{t+1}) = \text{softmax}(z_{t+1})$`.

EAGLE's draft model is trained to predict the **next feature** given a sequence of features. Formally, given `$f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_t$` from the target model, the draft model produces `$\hat{f}_{t+1}$`, an estimate of `$f_{t+1}$`. This `$\hat{f}_{t+1}$` is then passed through the target model's **frozen** LM head to obtain `$\hat{z}_{t+1} = W_{\text{LM}} \hat{f}_{t+1}$` and a predicted token distribution `$\hat{p}(\hat{t}_{t+1}) = \text{softmax}(\hat{z}_{t+1})$`.

The loss function in EAGLE has two components:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{EAGLE}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{token}}$$

where `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}} = \|\hat{f}_{t+1} - f_{t+1}\|^2$` is the mean squared error between the predicted feature and the ground-truth target model feature at the next position, and `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{token}}$` is the standard cross-entropy loss between the predicted token distribution `$\hat{p}$` and the ground-truth token `$t_{t+1}$`.

**Why the feature loss was included originally.** EAGLE's key insight was that training only at "Step 1" (predicting one feature ahead) could generalize to multi-step drafting at test time without explicit multi-step training. The reasoning: because the draft model sees features as input and predicts features as output, and because the LM head is deterministic, the draft model can be applied recursively—take its own predicted `$\hat{f}_{t+1}$`, feed it back as input, predict `$\hat{f}_{t+2}$`, and so on. The feature loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` was thought to be essential for this recursive application because it ensures the predicted features are close enough to ground-truth features that the model doesn't collapse when fed its own predictions.

**Why this constraint bottlenecks scaling.** The paper identifies that `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` is fundamentally a **surrogate objective**. The true goal is to predict the correct token `$t_{t+1}$`. The feature `$f_{t+1}$` is an intermediate representation that the target model uses to *compute* the token distribution, but there are infinitely many feature vectors that map to the same token distribution after the LM head (because the softmax is invariant to adding any scalar multiple of the all-ones vector to the logits, and the LM head's nullspace provides additional degrees of freedom). By forcing the draft model's output `$\hat{f}_{t+1}$` to match `$f_{t+1}$` exactly, the loss constrains the model to a specific point in the feature space rather than allowing it to find *any* representation that produces the correct token distribution.

As training data increases, the draft model's capacity allows it to better fit the training distribution, but the feature prediction constraint means it must spend capacity matching `$f_{t+1}$` with high precision—capacity that could instead be used to improve token-level accuracy. The paper shows this empirically in Figure 4: when `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` is removed, the first-token acceptance rate (`$0$`-`$\alpha$`) rises substantially with more data (from roughly 0.74 at 1× data to roughly 0.80 at 8× data). With the feature constraint in place (standard EAGLE), the same metric barely budges.

**The distribution-shift consequence.** The feature constraint also creates a specific fragility that the paper documents. In EAGLE, the draft model is trained to take ground-truth features `$f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_t$` as input. At test time, after the first draft step, the input becomes `$f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_t, \hat{f}_{t+1}$`, where `$\hat{f}_{t+1}$` is the model's own (potentially inaccurate) prediction. This is a distribution shift: the model was trained on clean features but tested on noisy ones. The feature loss was supposed to mitigate this by making `$\hat{f}_{t+1}$` close to `$f_{t+1}$`, but even small deviations accumulate over multiple steps. EAGLE's acceptance rate drops sharply as the number of self-predicted features in the input increases—Figure 7 shows the `$n$`-`$\alpha$` acceptance rate for EAGLE declining from roughly 0.75 at `$n=0$` to roughly 0.55 at `$n=7$`. This error accumulation is a direct consequence of the training-test distribution mismatch caused by the feature prediction architecture.

#### Removing Feature Prediction: Direct Token Prediction with Unconstrained Outputs

EAGLE-3's first major change is eliminating `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` entirely and training the draft model with only a token prediction objective. However, this is not as simple as just deleting a loss term—the architecture must be restructured because the draft model's output no longer needs to approximate a top-layer feature.

In EAGLE-3, the draft model produces an **unconstrained vector** `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` rather than a feature estimate `$\hat{f}_{t+1}$`. This vector is still fed through the target model's frozen LM head to obtain token logits:

$$\hat{z}_{t+1} = W_{\text{LM}} \hat{a}_{t+1}$$

and the token distribution `$\hat{p}(\hat{t}_{t+1}) = \text{softmax}(\hat{z}_{t+1})$`. The only loss is:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{token}} = -\log \hat{p}(\hat{t}_{t+1} = t_{t+1})$$

where `$t_{t+1}$` is the ground-truth next token. There is no constraint on where `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` lies in the representation space—it can be anywhere that produces the correct token distribution after the LM head.

**What this enables.** The removal of the feature constraint has three immediate consequences:

1. **Expressive freedom.** The draft model is no longer forced to spend capacity matching `$f_{t+1}$`. It can learn any internal representation that maps through the LM head to the correct token probabilities. This is a strictly easier optimization problem (the feasible set is larger, and the objective directly measures what we care about).

2. **Input flexibility.** Because the output `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` is not constrained to be a feature, the input to the draft model no longer needs to be a feature either. The entire feature prediction framework—where input and output must live in the same space—collapses, freeing the input representation to be anything useful.

3. **Scaling law potential.** With the surrogate constraint removed, the model's capacity can be fully devoted to token prediction accuracy, which should improve monotonically with training data scale.

**The new problem: multi-step collapse.** As Figure 4 demonstrates, removing `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` dramatically improves the first-step acceptance rate (`$0$`-`$\alpha$`), but the second-step acceptance rate (`$1$`-`$\alpha$`) drops severely. The paper explains the mechanism (Section 1, Figure 3 middle panel):

> "the output of the draft model in Step 1, denoted as `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$`, is far away from the ground-truth `$f_{t+1}$`, causing the input sequence `$f_1, f_2, \cdots, f_t, \hat{a}_{t+1}$` in Step 2 to deviate significantly from the training distribution"

The draft model was trained only on Step 1—it learned to map ground-truth features to an output that produces the correct token. When its own output `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` is fed back as input for Step 2, the model encounters an input unlike anything seen during training because `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` is not guaranteed to resemble any feature the target model would produce. The model hasn't learned to "understand" its own outputs; it's specialized to process the target model's clean features.

This is the distribution-shift problem that the training-time test procedure is designed to solve.

#### Training-Time Test: Simulating Multi-Step Generation During Training

The training-time test technique is the central methodological innovation of EAGLE-3. It solves the multi-step collapse problem by making the draft model's training distribution match its test-time behavior: during training, the model performs multiple steps of generation, feeding its own outputs back as inputs for subsequent steps, and is jointly optimized across all steps.

**What "training-time test" means.** The name reflects the procedure: during training (the "training" part), the model performs actions that normally happen only at test time (the "test" part). At test time, the draft model generates `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$`, samples a token, embeds that token, generates `$\hat{a}_{t+2}$`, and so on. During training, the exact same sequence of operations is executed, but with gradients flowing through each step so the model learns to handle the feedback loop.

**Detailed training procedure.** The paper illustrates this in Figure 6, which uses a concrete example with the prefix "How can I" and the target outputs "are", "we", "do". The training process proceeds in three simulated steps:

**Training Step 0 (standard training).** Given the ground-truth features for the prefix "How can I" and the corresponding fused features `$g_{\text{How}}, g_{\text{can}}, g_{\text{I}}$` from the target model, the draft model processes this sequence with standard causal self-attention (lower triangular mask). At each position, it produces an output `$a$` that is fed through the LM head to predict the next token. The loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{token}}$` is computed against ground-truth next tokens "are", "we", and "do" respectively. This step is identical to standard next-token-prediction training, except the inputs are fused features rather than token embeddings.

This step produces the outputs: `$\hat{a}_{\text{are}}$` from position "How" (predicting "are"), `$\hat{a}_{\text{we}}$` from position "can" (predicting "we"), and `$\hat{a}_{\text{do}}$` from position "I" (predicting "do").

**Training Step 1 (first simulated draft step).** Now we simulate what happens at test time after one draft token is generated. The model's own predictions from Step 0—specifically `$\hat{a}_{\text{are}}, \hat{a}_{\text{we}}, \hat{a}_{\text{do}}$`—are used as input features for a second forward pass. These are concatenated with embeddings of the ground-truth next tokens ("you", "help", "it" respectively) to form the input to the draft model.

The critical modification is in **self-attention**. The sequence "are", "we", "do" has a tree-structured contextual relationship with the original prefix. Specifically: "are" is the continuation after "How", so it should attend to "How" and all previous positions; "we" is the continuation after "can", attending to "can" and previous positions; "do" is the continuation after "I", attending to "I" and previous positions. Crucially, "are", "we", and "do" should **not** attend to each other—they are alternative continuations, not a sequential chain.

The attention mask for this step is shown in the top-right of Figure 6. The queries are "are", "we", "do"; the keys are "How", "can", "I", "are", "we", "do". The mask ensures each of the three draft positions attends only to the original prefix positions that precede it in the tree plus itself (diagonal), but not to sibling positions.

The draft model produces new outputs `$\hat{a}_{\text{you}}, \hat{a}_{\text{help}}, \hat{a}_{\text{it}}$` from the "are", "we", "do" positions respectively. The token loss is computed against the ground-truth next tokens "you", "help", "it".

**Training Step 2 (second simulated draft step).** The same pattern continues. The outputs from Step 1 (`$\hat{a}_{\text{you}}, \hat{a}_{\text{help}}, \hat{a}_{\text{it}}$`) become the input features for Step 2, concatenated with embeddings of ground-truth next tokens. The attention mask expands to include the new positions while preserving the tree structure: "you" attends to "How" and "are" and itself; "help" attends to "can" and "we" and itself; "it" attends to "I" and "do" and itself. Again, no cross-attention between different branches.

This procedure can continue for an arbitrary number of simulated steps. The paper uses up to 3 simulated steps in training.

**Attention mechanism efficiency.** The paper notes that the attention masks in training-time test are diagonal except where the original training data serves as keys. Rather than using full matrix multiplication (which would compute many unnecessary dot products between positions that shouldn't attend to each other), the implementation uses vector dot products to compute attention scores only for the valid (query, key) pairs. This avoids computational waste while correctly implementing the tree-structured attention pattern.

**How this solves the distribution-shift problem.** By training on sequences where `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` (the model's own prediction, with gradients flowing through it) is used as input for predicting `$\hat{a}_{t+2}$`, the model learns two things simultaneously: (1) to produce `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` that leads to correct token predictions at step 1, and (2) to produce `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` that serves as *useful input* for step 2 prediction. These objectives are jointly optimized because the gradient from step 2's loss flows back through step 1's output. If `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` is poorly formatted as an input feature (too far from anything seen in training), step 2's loss will be high, and the model will adjust step 1's output accordingly.

The result is that `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` is optimized not just for immediate token prediction accuracy, but also for **compatibility with the draft model's input expectations**—it learns to produce outputs that the model can productively use as context for further prediction.

**Evidence of effectiveness.** Figure 7 shows the acceptance rate `$n$`-`$\alpha$` for EAGLE-3 compared to EAGLE as a function of `$n$` (the number of self-predicted values in the input). For EAGLE, acceptance drops sharply from roughly 0.75 at `$n=0$` to roughly 0.55 at `$n=7$`. For EAGLE-3, acceptance remains almost flat across all `$n$`, hovering around 0.78–0.80. This near-constant acceptance rate regardless of how many self-predicted inputs are in context is the direct empirical validation that training-time test eliminates the distribution-shift problem.

**Relationship to HASS.** The paper explicitly contrasts with HASS (Zhang et al., 2024), which also uses simulated multi-step training (Section 3.2):

> "The motivation behind HASS is to mitigate the error accumulation caused by inaccurate feature predictions in EAGLE. HASS still performs feature prediction, includes a feature prediction loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$`, and the input to the draft model must be the top-layer features. In contrast, the motivation behind EAGLE-3 is to remove unnecessary constraints to enhance the model's expressive power."

The distinction is deeper than it might first appear. HASS tries to make feature prediction more robust by training on simulated multi-step sequences, but it preserves the feature constraint. EAGLE-3 recognizes that the feature constraint itself is the root cause of the scaling limitation; the training-time test is the mechanism that makes it possible to *remove* the constraint without suffering from multi-step collapse. HASS patches the symptom (error accumulation); EAGLE-3 eliminates the cause (feature prediction as a surrogate objective).

#### Multi-Layer Feature Fusion: Why and How

The removal of `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` and the training-time test procedure together enable EAGLE-3's second major architectural change: using features from multiple layers of the target model instead of only the top layer. This is possible because the draft model's input no longer needs to approximate a top-layer feature—any representation that the model learns to process is acceptable.

**Why top-layer features alone are limiting.** The top-layer feature `$f_t$` (immediately before the LM head) is optimized by the target model's pretraining to predict the **immediate next token** `$t_{t+1}$`. It represents a highly task-specific compression of the context that discards most intermediate semantic information. For predicting `$t_{t+2}$` (the next-next token), `$f_t$` contains information only indirectly—through whatever context is relevant for `$t_{t+1}$`, which in turn constrains `$t_{t+2}$`.

The paper argues (Section 1):

> "predicting the next-next token based solely on top-layer features—which are inherently limited to the next token—poses a significant challenge"

Lower and middle layers of the target model capture different aspects of the input: syntactic structure, entity relationships, discourse coherence, long-range dependencies. These signals may be more directly relevant for predicting tokens further ahead. For instance, a middle-layer representation might encode that the sentence is describing a mathematical operation, which is useful for predicting both the immediate operation symbol and the subsequent operands, even though the top-layer feature is already focused on the specific next token.

**Which layers are used.** The paper selects features from **low, middle, and high** layers of the target model, denoted `$l$`, `$m$`, and `$h$` respectively. The exact layer indices are not specified in the main paper (they may be in the code release), but the principle is to sample representations from different depths to capture different levels of abstraction. For a model with `$L$` layers, low might be around layer `$L/4$`, middle around layer `$L/2$`, and high being the top layer (layer `$L$`).

**The fusion operation.** The fusion is straightforward:

$$g_t = W_{\text{fuse}} [l_t; m_t; h_t] + b_{\text{fuse}}$$

where `$[l_t; m_t; h_t]$` denotes concatenation along the feature dimension, `$W_{\text{fuse}} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times 3k}$` is a learnable weight matrix, `$b_{\text{fuse}} \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$` is a learnable bias, and `$g_t \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$` is the fused feature.

**What it computes:** a learned linear combination (plus bias) of the three feature vectors from different target model layers, producing a single `$k$`-dimensional representation that integrates information across representational depths. The concatenation creates a `$3k$`-dimensional vector; the FC layer projects this back to `$k$` dimensions (the same as the target model's hidden size), making `$g_t$` dimensionally compatible with the draft model's input expectations.

**Why this form:** the linear projection is the simplest fusion mechanism that allows the model to learn which layers are most informative for drafting at each position. More complex fusion (e.g., attention over layers, gating mechanisms) could provide additional flexibility, but the paper opts for simplicity. The concatenation-then-project pattern ensures that information from all three layers can contribute to every dimension of `$g_t$`, while the dimensionality reduction to `$k$` prevents the input to the draft model from being `$3k$`, which would increase the draft model's parameter count.

**Why training-time test is a prerequisite.** Without training-time test, the draft model would be trained only on ground-truth fused features `$g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_t$` from the target model. At test time, after Step 1, the input would be `$g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_t, \hat{a}_{t+1}$`, where `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` is the draft model's own output. This output was not trained to approximate `$g_{t+1}$` (since there's no `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$`), so the distribution shift between training and test inputs would be severe. Training-time test addresses this by exposing the model to `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` as input during training, allowing it to learn that its own outputs serve as reasonable substitutes for unavailable `$g$` features in subsequent steps.

**Ablation validation.** Table 2 shows the contribution of each component. Starting from EAGLE-2 (which uses top-layer features and includes `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$`) with a speedup of 3.16× and `$\tau = 4.05$` on MT-bench with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, removing the feature constraint ("+ remove fea con") improves to 3.82× and `$\tau = 5.37$`, and adding fused features ("+ fused features") further improves to 4.40× and `$\tau = 6.13$`. On GSM8K, the progression is 3.39× → 3.77× → 4.48×. Both improvements contribute substantially, and the fused features provide gains on top of the feature removal, confirming they are complementary.

#### Complete Draft Model Architecture and Forward Pass

Putting the pieces together, here is the exact architecture of one forward pass of the EAGLE-3 draft model.

**Input construction.** At position `$t$`, the draft model receives two pieces of information:

1. **The fused feature** `$g_t \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$`, which is either computed from the target model's features (if position `$t$` has been verified) or is the draft model's own previous output `$a_t$` (if position `$t$` is a draft position that hasn't been verified yet). This distinction is crucial at inference time: the draft model reuses target model features for verified positions but substitutes its own outputs for future positions.

2. **The token embedding** `$e_t \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$`, which is the embedding of the token sampled at position `$t$`. For verified positions, this is the actual token generated by the target model; for draft positions, this is the token sampled from the draft model's own distribution at the previous step.

These two `$k$`-dimensional vectors are concatenated to form a `$2k$`-dimensional vector, which is then projected down to `$k$` dimensions via an FC layer:

$$\text{input}_t = W_{\text{proj}} [g_t; e_t] + b_{\text{proj}}$$

where `$W_{\text{proj}} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times 2k}$` and `$b_{\text{proj}} \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$`.

**Decoder layer.** The `$k$`-dimensional `$\text{input}_t$` is fed into a **single Transformer decoder layer**. This layer consists of standard components: multi-head self-attention followed by a feedforward network (FFN), with residual connections and layer normalization. The self-attention mechanism is causal (masked) so that position `$t$` can attend only to positions `$1, 2, \ldots, t$`.

The output of this decoder layer is `$a_{t+1} \in \mathbb{R}^{k}$`.

**Token prediction.** The output `$a_{t+1}$` is passed through the target model's **frozen LM head**:

$$\hat{z}_{t+1} = W_{\text{LM}} a_{t+1} + b_{\text{LM}}$$

$$\hat{p}(t_{t+1}) = \text{softmax}(\hat{z}_{t+1})$$

The draft token `$\hat{t}_{t+1}$` is sampled from `$\hat{p}(t_{t+1})$` (at temperature `$T=0$`, this is argmax; at `$T=1$`, this is sampling from the full distribution).

**Why a single decoder layer.** The draft model uses only one Transformer layer, making it extremely lightweight relative to the target model (which may have 32–80 layers). This is essential for the drafting stage to be fast—if the draft model were as expensive as the target model, there would be no speedup from speculative sampling. The single layer, combined with the rich input features from the target model, is sufficient because the draft model doesn't need to understand language from scratch; it only needs to predict what the target model will do next, leveraging the target model's own representations.

**Why include the token embedding.** The token embedding `$e_t$` provides information about the actual sampled token at position `$t$`. This is necessary because the draft model's prediction at step `$t+1$` depends on which token was chosen at step `$t$`, not just on the feature representation. In autoregressive generation, the random sampling process introduces uncertainty—the same feature `$g_t$` could lead to different tokens depending on the sampling outcome. By including `$e_t$`, the draft model conditions on the actual realized token, making it possible to maintain consistency with the specific generation trajectory.

This design was present in EAGLE and EAGLE-2 as well (they feed the token sequence from the previous time step), and EAGLE-3 preserves it.

#### Inference Pipeline: How Drafting Works at Test Time

The inference pipeline alternates between drafting and verification, as illustrated in Figure 5 with the example prefix "How can".

**Prefill phase.** When the user provides a prompt, the target model processes the entire prompt in one forward pass (the prefill phase, which is compute-bound and parallelized). During this forward pass, the target model records:
- The low-level features `$l$` at a chosen early layer
- The middle-level features `$m$` at a chosen middle layer
- The high-level features `$h$` at the final layer before the LM head
- The embeddings `$e$` for each token
- The fused features `$g = W_{\text{fuse}} [l; m; h] + b_{\text{fuse}}$` for each token position

The target model also generates the first token "I" normally (assuming the prefix is "How can").

**Drafting Step 1.** With prefix "How can", the system has `$g_{\text{How}}$` and `$g_{\text{can}}$` from the target model. It also has the embedding `$e_{\text{I}}$` of the token "I" that was just generated. The draft model takes `$g_{\text{How}}$` and `$g_{\text{can}}$` (fused features for verified positions) along with their respective token embeddings, plus `$e_{\text{I}}$` for the new position. It computes `$\text{input}_{\text{I}} = W_{\text{proj}} [g_{\text{I}}; e_{\text{I}}] + b_{\text{proj}}$`, but wait—`$g_{\text{I}}$` doesn't exist yet because the target model hasn't processed position "I" for drafting. This is the core challenge at test time.

The paper's solution is shown in Figure 5: for the new position "I", the system **reuses the draft model's own output from the previous step as a substitute for the unavailable fused feature.** Specifically, for the very first draft step, there's no previous draft output yet, so the initial feature `$g$` at the new position is computed using the target model's verification forward pass. The target model has just generated "I", so during that forward pass, it computed features at position "I". The system records these features during the generation step itself (or during the prefill phase for the prefix).

More precisely, the target model's forward pass that generated "I" also computed features at all positions, including the new position "I". The system extracts `$l_{\text{I}}, m_{\text{I}}, h_{\text{I}}$` from this forward pass, computes `$g_{\text{I}} = W_{\text{fuse}} [l_{\text{I}}; m_{\text{I}}; h_{\text{I}}] + b_{\text{fuse}}$`, and stores it. Now, for the draft step, `$g_{\text{I}}$` is available.

The draft model processes the sequence with prefix "How can I":
- Input at position "How": `$W_{\text{proj}} [g_{\text{How}}; e_{\text{How}}] + b_{\text{proj}}$`
- Input at position "can": `$W_{\text{proj}} [g_{\text{can}}; e_{\text{can}}] + b_{\text{proj}}$`
- Input at position "I": `$W_{\text{proj}} [g_{\text{I}}; e_{\text{I}}] + b_{\text{proj}}$`

The decoder layer processes these with causal self-attention. The output at position "I" is `$a_{\text{do}}$` (the model predicts "do"). This is fed through the LM head to obtain the distribution, and the token "do" is sampled.

**Drafting Step 2.** Now the prefix is "How can I" with the draft token "do" added. The system has `$g_{\text{How}}$`, `$g_{\text{can}}$`, `$g_{\text{I}}$` from the target model, and it needs to compute the next draft token. At position "do", there is no `$g_{\text{do}}$` from the target model (because "do" hasn't been verified). Instead, the system uses the draft model's output `$a_{\text{do}}$` from Step 1 as a substitute.

The input at position "do" is: `$W_{\text{proj}} [a_{\text{do}}; e_{\text{do}}] + b_{\text{proj}}$`, where `$e_{\text{do}}$` is the embedding of the sampled token "do".

The decoder layer processes the extended sequence (now with four positions) and produces `$a_{\text{it}}$` at position "do". The LM head produces a distribution, and "it" is sampled.

**Drafting Step 3 (and beyond).** The same pattern continues. At position "it", the feature `$g_{\text{it}}$` is unavailable, so `$a_{\text{it}}$` from Step 2 is used instead. The system can continue this for as many steps as the draft tree depth allows (up to 8 in EAGLE-3's configuration, increased from 6 in EAGLE-2 because the higher acceptance rate makes deeper trees worthwhile).

**The key substitution pattern.** At any draft position where the target model has not verified the token, the draft model uses its own previous output `$a$` as a substitute for the fused feature `$g$`. This is exactly the scenario that training-time test prepares the model for: during training, the model learned to process `$\hat{a}$` as input and produce sensible continuations, even when `$\hat{a}$` differs from what the target model would produce.

**Tree-structured drafting.** EAGLE-3 inherits EAGLE-2's dynamic draft tree mechanism. At each drafting step, the draft model doesn't just produce one candidate token—it produces a distribution over tokens. Multiple top candidates are sampled, creating branches in the draft tree. The EAGLE-2 confidence-based pruning mechanism selects which branches to expand further, allocating the draft budget (total number of draft tokens, configured as 60 for 7B/8B models, 50 for 13B, and 48 for 70B) to the most promising paths.

During verification, the target model evaluates all nodes in the draft tree simultaneously using tree attention, which computes attention only along valid paths (from root to each node) while preventing cross-attention between sibling branches. The acceptance/rejection mechanism of standard speculative sampling is applied to determine which draft tokens to keep.

**Increase in draft tree depth.** The paper notes (Appendix A) that EAGLE-3's higher acceptance rate allows increasing the draft tree depth from 6 (in EAGLE-2) to 8 while keeping the total number of nodes the same. A deeper tree means more tokens can be generated per verification pass, since each accepted token enables exploring the next level. The higher depth is viable because the training-time test makes the draft model's accuracy approximately constant regardless of how many self-predicted inputs are in context (Figure 7), meaning the additional depth doesn't suffer from compounding error accumulation.

#### Training Data and Hyperparameters

The training configuration is critical because the paper's central scaling-law claim depends on data scale.

**Training data sources.** The paper uses two datasets:

1. **ShareGPT** — approximately 68K conversations, serving as the baseline (1× data scale).
2. **UltraChat-200K** (Ding et al., 2023) — approximately 464K conversations, providing roughly 8× the data of ShareGPT when combined.

The paper explicitly states: "We use ShareGPT and UltraChat-200K as training data, containing approximately 68K and 464K data entries, respectively." The total is approximately 532K entries, which is about 7.8× the ShareGPT baseline (hence "approximately 8× more data" in the abstract and Figure 1).

**Data generation procedure.** Unlike standard fine-tuning where a fixed dataset of input-output pairs is used, the paper generates training data by **calling the target model**:

> "We call the target model to generate responses rather than using a fixed dataset."

This means the training data is on-policy: the prompts come from ShareGPT and UltraChat-200K, but the responses are generated by the target model itself. This ensures the training distribution matches what the draft model will encounter at test time—the target model's actual output distribution, not a different model's or human-written responses.

For the reasoning model DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B, the paper additionally uses the **OpenThoughts-114k-math** dataset for training, reflecting the different output distribution of reasoning models (which produce long chains of reasoning before answering).

**Training hyperparameters (Section 4, Implementation):**
- Optimizer: AdamW
- Beta values: `$(\beta_1, \beta_2) = (0.9, 0.95)$`
- Gradient clipping: 0.5
- Learning rate: `$5 \times 10^{-5}$`
- No dropout mentioned in the main training configuration
- Batch size: not explicitly specified in the paper text

**Training objective.** Only `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{token}}$` (cross-entropy loss on token prediction) is used. There is no feature prediction loss, no auxiliary objectives. The training-time test procedure adds additional forward passes (Steps 1, 2, 3, etc.) during training, but the loss is always the token prediction loss at each step.

**Number of simulated steps in training.** The paper shows 3 simulated steps in Figure 6 (Step 1 and Step 2 after the initial Step 0). It's not explicitly stated whether more steps are used, but the architecture supports arbitrary depth. The multi-step acceptance rate stability in Figure 7 (flat through `$n=7$`) suggests the model generalizes beyond the number of training-time test steps it was exposed to.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Remove `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` and predict tokens directly:** eliminates a surrogate objective that misaligns with the true goal and limits expressive capacity. The feature prediction constraint was forcing the draft model to spend capacity on feature-space accuracy rather than token-space accuracy, creating a bottleneck that prevented data scaling from improving performance.

- **Training-time test with feedback of `$\hat{a}$`:** solves the distribution-shift problem created by removing `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$`. Without this, the draft model trained on clean features fails when given its own outputs. With it, the model jointly optimizes for accurate prediction and for producing outputs that serve as useful inputs for subsequent steps.

- **Multi-layer feature fusion (`$l, m, h$` → `$g$`):** provides richer semantic information than top-layer features alone, which are inherently limited to predicting the immediate next token. Lower and middle layers capture complementary information useful for longer-range prediction. This is only possible because removing `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` eliminates the requirement that input features approximate top-layer features.

- **Single decoder layer for the draft model:** keeps drafting fast. The draft model leverages the target model's rich features, so a single layer with those features as input is sufficient for accurate token prediction. More layers would increase drafting cost proportionally and reduce or eliminate the speedup from speculative sampling.

- **Token embedding concatenated with feature input:** provides the draft model with information about which specific token was sampled at each position. This disambiguates cases where the same feature could lead to different tokens depending on the random sampling outcome, enabling the draft model to stay consistent with the actual generation trajectory.

- **Deep draft tree (depth 8, up from 6 in EAGLE-2):** exploits the flat acceptance rate across steps (Figure 7). Since accuracy doesn't degrade with more self-predicted inputs, deeper trees provide more accepted tokens per verification pass without suffering from error accumulation.

- **On-policy training data:** generating responses from the target model itself ensures the draft model's training distribution matches what it will encounter at test time. Using human-written responses or outputs from a different model would create a distribution mismatch that could reduce acceptance rates.

- **Tree-structured attention during training (Figure 6):** efficiently simulates the exact attention pattern the draft model will experience at test time, where sibling draft tokens should not attend to each other. Vector dot products for valid (query, key) pairs avoid the computational waste of full matrix multiplication with masking.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Diagnosing Feature Prediction as a Bottleneck, Not an Asset

The most intellectually distinctive contribution of this paper is not the introduction of a new technique, but rather a **diagnostic reframing**: the feature prediction loss `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` in EAGLE and EAGLE-2 — originally conceived as an essential component for enabling multi-step drafting — is in fact a **surrogate objective that creates a hard ceiling on draft model quality**. This is a genuinely surprising finding because the feature prediction mechanism was EAGLE's core insight and the reason for its superiority over vanilla speculative sampling. The entire family of feature-based draft methods (EAGLE, EAGLE-2, HASS, Falcon) was built on the premise that predicting features rather than tokens was the key to leveraging the target model's internal state. EAGLE-3 shows that this premise, while useful at small data scales, becomes **actively harmful** when data scale increases.

The diagnostic is anchored in a specific empirical observation: scaling training data from 1× to 8× produces negligible improvements for EAGLE-2 (Figure 1, Figure 4). This is exactly the pattern you would expect if a surrogate objective bottlenecks expressive capacity. When the draft model is forced to match the target model's exact feature vectors, additional training data cannot improve token-level accuracy beyond a certain point because **capacity is being consumed by a regression task that is not perfectly aligned with the true objective**. The paper demonstrates this by showing that removing `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` immediately improves first-token acceptance rate (Figure 4, `$0$`-`$\alpha$` rising from ~0.74 to ~0.80 with 8× data), proving that the feature constraint was holding back performance even at Step 1, before multi-step error accumulation comes into play.

What makes this a conceptual advance rather than a simple ablation finding is that it **inverts the field's understanding of what makes feature-based drafting work**. Before EAGLE-3, the dominant assumption — encoded in EAGLE, Medusa, HASS, Hydra, and Falcon — was that accessing the target model's internal features was the advantage, and the feature-prediction objective was the price you paid to enable autoregressive drafting in feature space. EAGLE-3 shows the opposite: accessing the target model's features **is** the advantage (preserved via multi-layer fusion), but the feature-prediction objective **is not** a necessary cost — it's a removable bottleneck. The field had conflated "use features as input" with "predict features as output," and this paper disentangles them.

This is also a **negative result with significant positive implications**. The finding that feature prediction doesn't scale with data means that prior work (EAGLE, EAGLE-2, HASS) was operating in a regime where the architecture, not the data, was the limiting factor. This reframes all prior results on those methods as **lower bounds** — they were not measuring the full potential of feature-based drafting, but rather the performance achievable under an unnecessary constraint. For practitioners, this diagnostic provides a clear action item: if you're building on EAGLE or similar methods, remove `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` and replace it with training-time test. The diagnostic generalizes beyond EAGLE to any speculative sampling method that uses a regression-style loss to match target model internals.

### Innovation 2: Training-Time Test as a Distribution-Matching Principle for Draft Models

The training-time test procedure is the paper's primary methodological contribution, but its significance extends beyond the specific implementation to represent a **general principle for speculative sampling draft model training**: the draft model's training distribution must match its test-time distribution, including the feedback of its own possibly-inaccurate outputs. Prior methods (EAGLE, EAGLE-2, Medusa, Hydra) trained draft models exclusively on ground-truth features from the target model, creating a train-test mismatch where the model encountered clean inputs during training but noisy self-generated inputs at test time. This mismatch was partially mitigated by the feature prediction loss in EAGLE (which encouraged the draft model's outputs to approximate ground-truth features, reducing the noise magnitude), but as the paper shows, this mitigation was insufficient and came at the cost of expressive capacity.

HASS (Zhang et al., 2024) partially recognized this problem by simulating multi-step generation during training, but crucially **kept the feature prediction loss**, which the paper shows is the root cause of limited scalability. HASS's approach can be understood as making the distribution shift less severe (by training on simulated rollouts) while keeping the architecture that creates the shift in the first place. EAGLE-3's insight is deeper: **eliminate the source of distribution shift by removing the feature constraint, and then use training-time test to teach the model to handle the consequences of that removal**.

The principle here is that a draft model which generates tokens by passing its own output vectors through the target model's LM head should be trained on sequences that include those same output vectors as inputs for subsequent steps. This is not just a training trick — it's a **correctness condition**: the mapping from `$\hat{a}_t$` to `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$` is the composition of (1) concatenating `$\hat{a}_t$` with the next token's embedding, (2) projecting to `$k$` dimensions, (3) passing through the decoder layer, and (4) producing `$\hat{a}_{t+1}$`. If the model is never trained on step (1) with `$\hat{a}_t$` as input, it cannot learn how its own output representations affect subsequent predictions. Training-time test closes this loop during optimization, making the training objective a faithful reflection of the test-time computation.

The striking result in Figure 7 — EAGLE-3's `$n$`-`$\alpha$` acceptance rate is essentially flat from `$n=0$` to `$n=7$`, while EAGLE's drops sharply — is not just a performance improvement but a **qualitative change in behavior**. EAGLE's declining acceptance rate reflects compounding errors: each self-predicted feature in the input degrades subsequent predictions, creating a vicious cycle. EAGLE-3's flat acceptance rate demonstrates that training-time test eliminates this compounding effect entirely. The draft model has learned to treat its own outputs as legitimate inputs, not as corrupted versions of ground-truth features. This means the draft model's accuracy at predicting the 7th token ahead is approximately equal to its accuracy at predicting the 1st token ahead — a property that no prior speculative sampling method has demonstrated.

For the broader field, this establishes a **design principle**: any speculative sampling draft model that uses its own outputs as subsequent inputs must be trained on sequences containing those outputs, with gradients flowing through the feedback loop. This principle is likely to generalize beyond EAGLE-3's specific architecture to any approach that involves self-referential computation in the draft model.

### Innovation 3: Decoupling Feature Input from Feature Output Enables Multi-Layer Fusion

The third conceptual contribution is the realization that **removing the feature prediction constraint unlocks access to information that was previously architecturally inaccessible**. In EAGLE and EAGLE-2, the draft model's input **had** to be top-layer features (or approximations thereof) because the output was trained to match top-layer features. The input-output space symmetry enforced by `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` meant the draft model was trapped in the top layer's representational space. EAGLE-3's removal of `$\mathcal{L}_{\text{fea}}$` breaks this symmetry: the input can be anything useful, regardless of whether the output resembles it.

This freedom enables the multi-layer feature fusion (`$l$`, `$m$`, `$h$` → `$g$`), which is a fundamentally different approach to leveraging the target model's internals. Prior methods (EAGLE, Medusa, Hydra, HASS) used only the top-layer features — the representation immediately before the LM head — because those features have a direct, deterministic relationship with the next-token distribution (the LM head maps them to logits). This relationship is what made feature prediction plausible: if you can predict the top-layer feature, you automatically get the token distribution via the frozen LM head.

However, this direct relationship also means top-layer features are **extremely task-specific**. They encode exactly the information needed to predict the immediate next token, and they discard most everything else about the context. For predicting tokens further ahead, this is suboptimal. Lower and middle layers of the target model capture syntactic structure, entity relationships, discourse coherence, and other signals that are relevant for predicting tokens at longer horizons. But these features don't have a direct mapping to token distributions via the LM head — they were never accessible to feature-based draft methods because they couldn't be used as both input and prediction target.

EAGLE-3's decoupling solves this: use multi-layer features as **input** (where they provide rich semantic context) while using the LM head applied to unconstrained outputs for **token prediction** (where the direct feature-to-token relationship isn't needed). The training-time test procedure makes this decoupling viable by teaching the draft model to process its own outputs (which don't live in any particular layer's feature space) as substitutes for unavailable fused features at future positions.

The ablation in Table 2 confirms that multi-layer fusion provides gains **on top of** feature constraint removal. On MT-bench with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, removing the feature constraint improves speedup from 3.16× to 3.82×; adding fused features further improves to 4.40×. On GSM8K, the progression is 3.39× → 3.77× → 4.48×. The two innovations are complementary because they address different bottlenecks: feature removal addresses the **optimization bottleneck** (surrogate objective limits capacity), while multi-layer fusion addresses the **information bottleneck** (top-layer features discard useful context).

This contribution reframes how the field should think about using target model internals in speculative sampling. Prior work asked: "How can we predict the target model's internal state to get its output distribution?" EAGLE-3 asks: "What internal state of the target model provides the most useful context for a lightweight model to predict the target model's output distribution?" The shift from "predict the internals" to "use the internals as context" is subtle but profound — it converts the draft model's task from feature regression to contextualized token prediction, which is both easier and more aligned with the true objective.

### Innovation 4: Establishing a Scaling Law for Inference Acceleration

The discovery that EAGLE-3's speedup ratio scales monotonically with training data volume (Figure 1) is not just an empirical result — it **establishes a new axis of scaling for LLM systems** that was previously believed to be saturated. The broader LLM field has well-characterized scaling laws for pretraining (model size vs. data vs. compute) and has recently explored scaling laws for test-time reasoning compute (as in the EAGLE-3 paper's related work discussion of o1 and DeepSeek-R1). But prior to EAGLE-3, there was no evidence that the **draft model in a speculative sampling system** could benefit from increased training data scale.

The fact that EAGLE-2 shows a flat scaling curve while EAGLE-3 shows an increasing one (Figure 1) is the paper's strongest single piece of evidence that the feature prediction constraint was **the** bottleneck preventing data scaling from translating to performance. This is because a flat scaling curve is the signature of a **saturated architecture**: the model's capacity to improve on the task is limited not by data quantity but by some other constraint. EAGLE's and EAGLE-2's flat curves indicate that the feature prediction objective had created an implicit performance ceiling. EAGLE-3's rising curve indicates that this ceiling has been removed — the new architecture's performance is now data-limited rather than architecture-limited, which is the desirable regime for any system that can benefit from larger datasets.

The practical implication is substantial: **investment in higher-quality and larger draft model training datasets is now predictably worthwhile**. Before EAGLE-3, a practitioner looking at EAGLE-2's scaling curve would reasonably conclude that expanding training data beyond ShareGPT was a waste of resources. After EAGLE-3, the same practitioner sees a positive return on data investment, with speedup improving from ~3.5× at 1× data to ~4.4× at 8× data. The paper speculates that "larger data size would lead to further improved speedup ratio" (Section 1), and the monotonic trend in Figure 1 supports this extrapolation, though verification at larger scales remains future work.

This contribution also **connects speculative sampling to the broader LLM scaling narrative**. The LLaMA series (1T → 2T → 15T tokens) demonstrated that language model quality scales with pretraining data even when architecture remains similar. EAGLE-3 shows that the same pattern applies to draft model training for inference acceleration — more data produces better draft models, which produce higher acceptance rates, which produce larger speedups. This is significant because it means the inference acceleration community can ride the same data-scaling wave that has driven LLM capability improvements, rather than being limited to architectural innovations for marginal gains.

The scaling law finding also implies that **EAGLE-3's reported speedups are lower bounds**. With 8× data, EAGLE-3 achieves 4.4× speedup on LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B (Table 1, MT-bench). If the scaling trend continues, 16× or 32× data could push this substantially higher. The paper's acknowledgment that "we expect larger data size would lead to further improved speedup ratio" is not mere speculation — it follows directly from the observed scaling behavior and the removal of the architectural bottleneck that previously caused saturation.

For the research community, this finding changes the optimization landscape for speculative sampling. Prior work focused on architectural innovations (tree structures, attention modifications, feature prediction mechanisms) because these were the only levers that improved performance. EAGLE-3 demonstrates that data scale is now the primary lever, and architectural work should focus on ensuring the data scaling can continue (e.g., by avoiding introducing new surrogate objectives that could create new bottlenecks). This parallels the shift in pretraining research after Hoffmann et al. (2022) established Chinchilla scaling laws: the question changed from "how do we design better architectures?" to "how do we optimally allocate our data and compute budget?"

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** Five datasets are used, following EAGLE (Li et al., 2024c) and Spec-Bench (Xia et al., 2024): MT-bench (Zheng et al., 2023) for multi-turn conversation, HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) for code generation, GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) for mathematical reasoning, Alpaca (Taori et al., 2023) for instruction following, and CNN/Daily Mail (Nallapati et al., 2016) for summarization. The paper does not report exact evaluation split sizes for these datasets in the main text, but the evaluation protocol is consistent with prior speculative sampling benchmarks — full test sets are used where available (e.g., HumanEval's 164 problems, GSM8K's 1,319 test questions), while MT-bench uses its standard 80 multi-turn questions. No fine-tuning is performed on any evaluation task; a single draft model is trained and evaluated across all five datasets.

- **Base models.** Four target models are evaluated: Vicuna 13B (Chiang et al., 2023), LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B (Dubey et al., 2024), and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). These span a range of scales (8B to 70B parameters) and model families (LLaMA, Vicuna, DeepSeek), including both standard chat models and a reasoning model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B, which produces lengthy chain-of-thought before answering). The paper notes that GPU constraints prevented testing on 405B and 671B models. The choice of models is deliberate: Vicuna 13B enables direct comparison with prior speculative sampling work (EAGLE, EAGLE-2, Medusa all report on this model), LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B represents a widely-used open-source model at a common deployment scale, LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B tests scalability to larger models, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B tests the approach on reasoning models where inference cost is disproportionately high due to long chain-of-thought generation.

- **Metrics.** Three metrics are reported:
    - **Speedup Ratio**: the actual wall-clock acceleration relative to vanilla autoregressive decoding (which is defined as 1.00×). This is measured end-to-end, including both drafting and verification time, and accounts for the overhead of feature extraction, fusion, and tree attention during verification.
    - **Average Acceptance Length τ**: the average number of tokens generated per drafting-verification cycle, which equals the number of draft tokens accepted from the draft tree by the target model's verification pass. Higher τ means more tokens are produced per target model forward pass, directly reducing the number of expensive verification steps needed.
    - **Acceptance Rate n-α**: the proportion of draft tokens accepted by the target model under specific input conditions, measured using chain-like drafts (not tree-structured) for diagnostic purposes. The notation `n`-`α` represents the acceptance rate when the draft model's input contains `n` self-predicted values (`a` in EAGLE-3, or `ˆf` in EAGLE), under the condition that all previous estimated tokens were already accepted by the target model. For EAGLE-3, `n`-`α` is the acceptance rate for inputs of the form `g₁, g₂, ..., gᵢ, aᵢ₊₁, ..., aᵢ₊ₙ` (where `g` are fused features from the target model and `a` are the draft model's own outputs). For EAGLE, it is the acceptance rate for inputs of the form `f₁, f₂, ..., fᵢ, ˆfᵢ₊₁, ..., ˆfᵢ₊ₙ` (where `f` are top-layer features and `ˆf` are the draft model's estimates). This metric isolates the effect of self-predicted inputs on draft quality, independently of tree-structure effects. All three metrics are complementary: τ captures overall system throughput, speedup captures end-to-end latency reduction, and n-α provides a diagnostic of the draft model's robustness to its own predictions.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against eight methods, all of which are lossless (producing identical output distributions to vanilla autoregressive decoding):
    - **Vanilla autoregressive decoding** — the reference point for speedup ratios (1.00×), using standard next-token generation with no speculative sampling.
    - **Standard speculative sampling (SpS)** (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023) — uses a separate smaller draft model from the same model series. For Vicuna 13B, the draft model is Vicuna-68M. Implemented via the HuggingFace Transformers library's assisted generation feature (Gante, 2023).
    - **PLD (Prompt Lookup Decoding)** (Saxena, 2023) — uses n-gram matching from the prompt/context as drafts rather than a learned draft model.
    - **Medusa** (Cai et al., 2024) — adds multiple prediction heads to the target model that predict future tokens from top-layer features, with tree-structured drafting. The paper notes that Medusa relaxes acceptance conditions under non-greedy settings, so it is excluded from temperature=1 comparisons where losslessness cannot be guaranteed.
    - **Lookahead** (Fu et al., 2024) — generates drafts by looking ahead using Jacobi iteration, breaking sequential dependency.
    - **Hydra** (Ankner et al., 2024) — extends Medusa with sequentially-dependent draft heads.
    - **EAGLE** (Li et al., 2024c) — the original feature-level autoregressive draft model using top-layer features and a dual feature+token loss.
    - **EAGLE-2** (Li et al., 2024b) — adds dynamic, context-aware draft tree construction and pruning on top of EAGLE.
    - **HASS** (Zhang et al., 2024) — mentioned in comparison discussions but not included in the main results table (Table 1). The paper states that "Figure 2 also shows the speedup of EAGLE-3 and HASS, with EAGLE-3 demonstrating significantly better performance," but specific HASS speedup numbers are not provided in the main text tables. HASS is primarily discussed as a conceptual comparison point (Section 3.2) rather than a head-to-head empirical baseline.

- **Generation budget and compute accounting.** The primary unit of comparison across methods is the measured wall-clock speedup at batch size 1, which inherently accounts for all computational costs: draft model forward passes, target model verification passes, feature extraction and fusion, tree attention, and acceptance/rejection logic. For the tree-structured methods (EAGLE, EAGLE-2, EAGLE-3, Medusa, Hydra), the paper controls the total number of draft tokens per verification cycle (configured as 60 for 7B/8B models, 50 for 13B, and 48 for 70B, as noted in Appendix A) and the draft tree depth (6 for EAGLE-2, increased to 8 for EAGLE-3). For the throughput experiments in SGLang and vLLM (Tables 3, 4, 5), tokens per second is the metric, and the batch size is explicitly varied to test how speculative sampling performs as compute utilization increases. The chain length for throughput experiments is set to 3 for SGLang tests and 2 for vLLM tests, with no tree structure used, and the draft model is configured accordingly.

- **Cross-validation and statistical protocol.** No cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported. The paper evaluates each method on the full test sets of the five benchmarks and reports single-number speedup ratios and acceptance lengths. All methods are evaluated with the same target model configurations and hardware, making comparisons fair in terms of measurement methodology, but the absence of error bars or variance estimates means the reported differences (e.g., EAGLE-3 at 4.40× vs. EAGLE-2 at 3.16× on LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B for MT-bench) should be understood as point estimates without quantified uncertainty. The paper does not specify the number of evaluation runs or whether results are averaged over multiple random seeds.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Headline Speedup Comparisons Across Models and Tasks

Table 1 and Figure 2 present the primary results. EAGLE-3 achieves the highest speedup ratio and average acceptance length across all five tasks, all four target models, and both temperature settings (T=0 and T=1). The mean speedup across tasks for EAGLE-3 ranges from 3.45× (LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, T=1) to 5.51× (Vicuna 13B, T=0), representing a 20–40% improvement over EAGLE-2 in every configuration.

For the Vicuna 13B target model at T=0 (Table 1, top section), EAGLE-3 achieves a mean speedup of 5.51× and mean τ of 6.62, compared to EAGLE-2's 4.22× and 4.83. Standard speculative sampling (Vicuna-68M draft model) achieves only 1.92× and 2.24, illustrating the advantage of feature-based drafting over independent draft models. The best individual task for EAGLE-3 on Vicuna 13B is HumanEval at 6.47× speedup and τ=7.54, which the paper attributes to "many fixed templates in code generation tasks" making drafting easier. The worst individual task is CNN/Daily Mail at 5.01× and τ=6.47, still substantially above EAGLE-2's 3.40×.

For LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B at T=0, EAGLE-3 achieves 4.44× mean speedup and τ=6.23, compared to EAGLE-2's 3.23× and 4.11. At T=1, the mean speedup drops to 3.45× and τ=4.92 for EAGLE-3, versus 2.80× and 3.62 for EAGLE-2. The temperature=1 results consistently show lower speedups across all methods because the target model's sampling introduces randomness that makes draft tokens harder to predict — the draft model must approximate a full distribution rather than just the argmax, and verification acceptance rates drop accordingly.

For LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B at T=0, EAGLE-3 achieves 4.12× mean speedup and τ=5.88, compared to EAGLE-2's 2.85× and 3.78. The absolute speedup is lower than for the 8B model because the target model is now much larger (70B vs. 8B parameters), meaning the relative cost of the draft model (which remains a single decoder layer) is smaller, but the verification step — a full forward pass through 70B parameters — dominates total time more heavily. The 4.12× speedup on a 70B model is practically significant: reducing inference latency on a model of this scale from, say, 40 tokens/second to ~165 tokens/second at batch size 1.

For DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B at T=0, EAGLE-3 achieves 4.16× mean speedup and τ=5.84, compared to EAGLE-2's 3.26× and 3.92. Notably, EAGLE-3 performs best on GSM8K (5.01× speedup, τ=6.93) for this model, which the paper attributes to additional training on the OpenThoughts-114k-math dataset. This is the only target model where GSM8K, not HumanEval, is the highest-speedup task, suggesting that domain-specific draft model training data (math reasoning in this case) provides benefits beyond general conversation data, particularly for reasoning models whose output distributions differ substantially from standard chat models.

At T=1 (Table 1, bottom section), the pattern of EAGLE-3 superiority over EAGLE-2 is consistent across all models. The Vicuna 13B results include standard speculative sampling as a baseline (1.60× mean speedup), confirming that feature-based methods substantially outperform independent draft models even in stochastic generation settings. Medusa, Lookahead, PLD, and Hydra results are not reported at T=1 because "methods like Medusa relax acceptance conditions under non-greedy settings, which do not guarantee lossless acceleration" (Table 1 caption). This is an important methodological point: EAGLE and EAGLE-3 use strict speculative sampling acceptance (the `min(1, p/ˆp)` criterion from Leviathan et al., 2023), ensuring mathematical equivalence to autoregressive decoding at all temperatures. Methods that relax this criterion achieve higher speedups at T=1 but sacrifice the lossless guarantee.

Figure 2 provides a visual summary across five target models, showing EAGLE-3 as the tallest bar in every panel. The progression from vanilla speculative sampling through EAGLE, EAGLE-2, and EAGLE-3 shows consistent improvement, with EAGLE-3 representing a discontinuous jump beyond the prior trend for most models (e.g., Vicuna 13B: 1.9× → 3.1× → 4.1× → 5.6×; LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B: not shown for vanilla → 3.2× → 3.6× → 4.4×). The figure also includes Medusa and HASS as intermediate baselines for Vicuna 13B (2.1× and an unlabeled bar between Medusa and EAGLE, respectively), though HASS values are not separately tabulated.

#### Scaling Law: Data Scale vs. Speedup

Figure 1 presents the paper's central scaling law finding. On MT-bench with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B as the target model, both speedup ratio and average acceptance length are plotted against training data scale (1×, 2×, 4×, 8× relative to ShareGPT).

For EAGLE-2, the speedup curve is essentially flat: at 1× data, speedup is approximately 3.2×; at 2×, approximately 3.3×; at 4×, approximately 3.3×; at 8×, approximately 3.4×. The total improvement from 1× to 8× is roughly 0.2× — a 6% relative gain. The acceptance length shows the same pattern: approximately 4.0 at 1×, rising marginally to approximately 4.3 at 8×.

For EAGLE-3, the speedup curve shows a clear upward trend: from approximately 3.5× at 1× data to approximately 3.8× at 2×, approximately 4.1× at 4×, and approximately 4.4× at 8×. The total improvement is roughly 0.9× — a 26% relative gain from quadrupling data, roughly 4× larger in absolute terms than EAGLE-2's improvement over the same range. The acceptance length tracks similarly: approximately 4.8 at 1×, rising to approximately 5.2 at 2×, approximately 5.7 at 4×, and approximately 6.1 at 8×.

The paper explicitly frames this as a qualitative difference: "The new architectural designs in EAGLE-3 enable an increasing scaling curve, which was never observed in the previous works" (Figure 1 caption). The magnitude of the scaling effect is substantial but not enormous over the 8× range tested — speedup improves by roughly 26% while data increases by 800%. Whether this trend continues linearly, logarithmically, or saturates at larger data scales (16×, 32×, etc.) is not tested, and the paper's claim that "larger data size would lead to further improved speedup ratio" is a projection rather than a demonstrated fact.

#### Acceptance Rate Analysis: Eliminating Error Accumulation

Figure 7 shows the `n`-`α` acceptance rates for EAGLE and EAGLE-3 on MT-bench with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B as the target model, as a function of `n` (the number of self-predicted values in the draft model's input). This is the key diagnostic plot for the training-time test technique.

For EAGLE, the acceptance rate at `n=0` (no self-predicted features, all inputs are ground-truth top-layer features from the target model) is approximately 0.78. As `n` increases, the acceptance rate declines sharply and approximately linearly: roughly 0.74 at `n=1`, 0.70 at `n=2`, 0.67 at `n=3`, 0.64 at `n=4`, 0.61 at `n=5`, 0.58 at `n=6`, 0.55 at `n=7`. The total drop from `n=0` to `n=7` is approximately 0.23 — a 29% relative decline, confirming that error accumulation is severe in the feature prediction architecture.

For EAGLE-3, the acceptance rate is essentially flat across all `n`. At `n=0`, it is approximately 0.80 — already higher than EAGLE's best-case acceptance rate. At `n=7`, it remains approximately 0.79. The entire range shows variation within roughly ±0.01 of 0.79, with no downward trend. This demonstrates that the training-time test procedure has eliminated the compounding error effect: the draft model's accuracy at predicting the 7th token ahead (conditioned on 7 self-predicted inputs) is approximately equal to its accuracy at predicting the 1st token ahead (conditioned on all ground-truth inputs).

The absolute improvement in acceptance rate from EAGLE to EAGLE-3 at `n=7` is approximately 0.24 (0.79 vs. 0.55), a 44% relative increase. This is the source of EAGLE-3's ability to use deeper draft trees (depth 8 vs. EAGLE-2's depth 6): since accuracy doesn't degrade with depth, the extra levels contribute additional accepted tokens rather than mostly rejected ones.

#### Ablation: Feature Constraint Removal and Multi-Layer Fusion

Table 2 presents the ablation study results on MT-bench and GSM8K with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B as the target model. The baseline is EAGLE-2, which uses top-layer features, the feature prediction loss `L_fea`, and dynamic draft trees. On MT-bench, EAGLE-2 achieves 3.16× speedup and τ=4.05.

Removing the feature prediction constraint ("+ remove fea con") — which means training with only `L_token`, using training-time test to handle multi-step generation, but still using only top-layer features — improves speedup to 3.82× and τ to 5.37. This is a 21% speedup improvement and a 33% improvement in acceptance length, demonstrating that the feature loss alone was a significant bottleneck even without multi-layer fusion.

Adding fused features from low, middle, and high layers on top of feature constraint removal ("+ fused features (ours)") further improves speedup to 4.40× and τ to 6.13 on MT-bench. This represents an additional 15% speedup improvement beyond what feature removal alone provides.

On GSM8K, the same pattern holds: EAGLE-2 at 3.39× and τ=4.24; adding feature removal improves to 3.77× and τ=5.22 (11% speedup improvement); adding fused features further improves to 4.48× and τ=6.23 (19% additional improvement).

The two contributions are complementary: feature removal alone provides a larger absolute improvement on MT-bench (+0.66× speedup) than fused features alone would (estimated as 4.40× minus 3.82× = 0.58×, though this isn't directly measured since the ablation doesn't test "fused features without feature removal" separately — that configuration would likely fail because the feature loss would conflict with non-top-layer inputs). On GSM8K, the pattern is reversed: fused features provide a larger gain (3.77× → 4.48× = +0.71×) than feature removal alone (3.39× → 3.77× = +0.38×). This task-dependence makes sense: mathematical reasoning (GSM8K) may benefit more from the richer semantic information in lower/middle layers than multi-turn conversation (MT-bench), where top-layer features already capture much of the short-range prediction signal.

#### Throughput in Production-Grade Frameworks (SGLang and vLLM)

Tables 3, 4, and 5 address a practical concern about speculative sampling: "Speculative sampling is often thought to reduce throughput at large batch sizes" (Section 4.3). The reasoning is that speculative sampling exploits redundant compute during memory-bound single-batch decoding; as batch size increases, the decoding becomes more compute-bound, reducing the redundancy that speculative sampling leverages. These tables show that EAGLE-3 substantially mitigates this problem compared to EAGLE.

Table 3 (SGLang v0.4.4 on H100 with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, MT-Bench, chain length 3, no tree structure): At batch size 2, EAGLE achieves 1.40× throughput improvement while EAGLE-3 achieves 1.81×. At batch size 16, EAGLE has dropped to near-breakeven at 1.02×, while EAGLE-3 maintains 1.48×. At batch size 32, EAGLE is at 0.94× — actually slower than no speculative sampling — while EAGLE-3 is at 1.32×. At the maximum tested batch size of 64, EAGLE is at 0.99× (essentially break-even) while EAGLE-3 remains at 1.38× throughput improvement. The paper explicitly notes: "EAGLE reduces throughput at a batch size of 24, whereas EAGLE-3 still achieves a 38% throughput improvement at a batch size of 64."

Table 5 (vLLM on A100 with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, MT-Bench, chain length 2, no tree structure): A similar pattern. EAGLE provides throughput improvements at small batch sizes (1.30× at batch size 2) but drops below 1.0× at batch size 32 (0.93×) and continues declining to 0.71× at batch size 56. EAGLE-3 provides larger improvements at small batches (1.75× at batch size 2) and remains above 1.0× through batch size 56 (1.01×). The paper reports that "EAGLE shows the maximum throughput improvement at a batch size of 24, while EAGLE-3 shows this at 56" — though Table 5 shows EAGLE at batch size 24 as 1.03× (essentially break-even, not maximum improvement), and EAGLE-3 at batch size 56 as 1.01×. The cross-over where EAGLE-3 remains beneficial while EAGLE becomes detrimental is the key finding.

Table 4 shows batch size 1 throughput specifically: SGLang without speculative sampling achieves 158.34 tokens/s; with EAGLE-2, 244.10 tokens/s (1.54× improvement); with EAGLE-3, 373.25 tokens/s (2.36× improvement over baseline, 1.53× over EAGLE-2). This confirms that at batch size 1 — the latency-sensitive regime where speculative sampling is most impactful — EAGLE-3 provides substantial throughput gains beyond EAGLE-2.

The SGLang and vLLM experiments were conducted by the respective framework teams (the SGLang team is explicitly acknowledged), lending credibility to the results as independent verification in production environments. However, the experiments use a simplified configuration (chain length 2–3, no tree structure) that likely understates the full EAGLE-3 speedup potential. The paper does not report tree-structured results in these frameworks, which would be expected to show higher throughput at small batch sizes but might behave differently at large batch sizes due to verification overhead from tree attention.

#### Comparison of Acceptance Rate Scaling with Data

Figure 4 compares the acceptance rates of EAGLE, "EAGLE without fea pred" (feature prediction removed but no training-time test and no multi-layer fusion), and EAGLE-3 as a function of data scale (1×, 2×, 4×, 8×). This figure is the detailed evidence behind the scaling law claim in Figure 1.

For the first draft token acceptance rate `0-α` (left panel), all three methods start at similar values at 1× data: approximately 0.74 for EAGLE, 0.74 for EAGLE without fea pred, and 0.75 for EAGLE-3. As data increases to 8×:
- EAGLE shows almost no improvement: roughly 0.745 at 8× — an increase of ~0.005.
- EAGLE without fea pred shows clear improvement: roughly 0.80 at 8× — an increase of approximately 0.06, or 8% relative improvement.
- EAGLE-3 shows the strongest improvement: roughly 0.80 at 8×, similar to EAGLE without fea pred, suggesting that feature removal is the primary driver of `0-α` improvement, with multi-layer fusion providing marginal additional benefit for the first token.

For the second draft token acceptance rate `1-α` (right panel), the divergence is dramatic:
- EAGLE starts at approximately 0.65 at 1× data and improves marginally to roughly 0.67 at 8×.
- EAGLE without fea pred starts substantially lower: approximately 0.28 at 1× data — confirming the multi-step collapse when feature prediction is removed without training-time test. It improves with data to roughly 0.42 at 8×, but remains far below EAGLE.
- EAGLE-3 starts at approximately 0.70 at 1× and improves to roughly 0.79 at 8× — higher than EAGLE's `1-α` at all data scales and showing the same positive scaling trend as the `0-α` panel.

This figure directly supports the paper's narrative: removing feature prediction improves first-token accuracy but catastrophically degrades second-token accuracy; adding training-time test (which distinguishes EAGLE-3 from "EAGLE without fea pred") recovers and substantially exceeds the second-token accuracy; and the combination of both changes produces a system where all acceptance rates benefit from training data scale.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Feature prediction constraint removal (Table 2)**: On MT-bench with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, removing `L_fea` while training with `L_token` only and using training-time test improves speedup from EAGLE-2's 3.16× to 3.82× and τ from 4.05 to 5.37. This is the single largest improvement component, delivering a 0.66× speedup gain. On GSM8K, the improvement is from 3.39× to 3.77× with τ going from 4.24 to 5.22. The ablation validates that the feature loss was indeed a binding constraint: removing it alone provides substantial gains even without the multi-layer fusion improvement, confirming the paper's diagnostic that `L_fea` was limiting expressive capacity.

- **Multi-layer feature fusion (Table 2)**: Adding fused features from low, middle, and high layers (replacing top-layer-only features) on top of feature removal improves MT-bench speedup from 3.82× to 4.40× and τ from 5.37 to 6.13. On GSM8K, improvement is from 3.77× to 4.48× with τ from 5.22 to 6.23. This confirms that top-layer features are informationally insufficient for predicting tokens beyond the immediate next one, and that lower/middle layers provide complementary semantic information. The fusion mechanism itself is a simple linear projection of concatenated features — the ablation validates that even this simple approach to multi-layer information integration provides meaningful gains, suggesting more sophisticated fusion (e.g., attention over layers, learned gating) could yield further improvements.

- **Acceptance rate robustness to self-predicted inputs (Figure 7)**: The `n`-`α` curves for EAGLE-3 are essentially flat from `n=0` to `n=7` (approximately 0.78–0.80 across the range), while EAGLE's decline from approximately 0.78 to 0.55. This ablation is the direct empirical validation of the training-time test technique. It demonstrates that the model has learned to treat its own outputs as legitimate inputs rather than as corrupted versions of ground-truth features, and that this property holds even at `n=7` — which is beyond the number of simulated steps explicitly shown in the training procedure (Figure 6 shows 3 steps, suggesting the model generalizes to deeper chains).

- **Training data scale interaction with architecture (Figures 1, 4)**: Scaling training data from 1× to 8× provides negligible benefit for EAGLE-2 (speedup remains ~3.2–3.4×, τ ~4.0–4.3) but clear benefit for EAGLE-3 (speedup ~3.5→4.4×, τ ~4.8→6.1). This interaction effect — data scaling helps the new architecture but not the old — is the key evidence that the feature prediction constraint was the architectural bottleneck preventing data scaling from translating to performance. It also serves as a robustness check on the ablation: if the ablation improvements were due to some other factor (hyperparameters, random seed, implementation details), they likely wouldn't show this specific interaction pattern where data scaling selectively benefits the ablated architecture.

- **Temperature sensitivity (Table 1)**: All methods show lower speedups at T=1 compared to T=0, which is expected because stochastic sampling makes draft tokens harder to predict. For Vicuna 13B, EAGLE-3 drops from 5.51× mean speedup at T=0 to 4.65× at T=1 — a 16% reduction. For LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, the drop is from 4.44× to 3.45× — a 22% reduction. For LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B, from 4.12× to 3.95× — only a 4% reduction. For DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B, from 4.16× to 3.52× — a 15% reduction. The varying temperature sensitivity across models likely reflects differences in output distribution entropy: models with sharper distributions (lower entropy) are easier to draft for at T=1 because the draft model's task is closer to argmax prediction. The paper doesn't analyze entropy or distribution sharpness explicitly, so this remains a hypothesis.

- **Model scale generalization (Table 1)**: EAGLE-3 provides consistent improvements over EAGLE-2 across model scales from 8B to 70B. For LLaMA-Instruct models at T=0: 8B improves from 3.23× to 4.44× (37% relative), 70B improves from 2.85× to 4.12× (45% relative). The absolute speedup is lower for the 70B model (4.12× vs. 4.44×) because the target model's forward pass dominates total time more heavily, but the relative improvement over EAGLE-2 is actually larger for the 70B model. This suggests the architectural improvements in EAGLE-3 are not specific to a particular model scale. The paper does not test on models below 8B, leaving open the question of whether draft model quality improvements matter less for very small target models where the relative cost of drafting vs. verification is different.

- **Task generalization (Table 1)**: The speedup ratios are reported across five diverse tasks. The ranking of tasks by speedup is generally HumanEval > MT-bench ≈ GSM8K > Alpaca > CNN/Daily Mail, with HumanEval showing the highest speedups due to "many fixed templates" (Section 4.1). For EAGLE-3 on Vicuna 13B at T=0: HumanEval 6.47×, MT-bench 5.58×, GSM8K 5.32×, Alpaca 5.16×, CNN/Daily Mail 5.01×. The maximum variation across tasks is 1.46× (6.47/5.01 = 1.29×, actually), showing task-dependent but consistently strong performance. The paper does not analyze which task characteristics drive the variation or whether certain task types benefit disproportionately from the architectural changes.

- **Impact on reasoning models (Table 1, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B rows)**: EAGLE-3 achieves 4.16× mean speedup at T=0 on this reasoning model, with the highest single-task speedup being 5.01× on GSM8K. This is notable because reasoning models produce long chains of thought before answering, making inference acceleration particularly valuable — a 5× speedup on a model that produces 1,000 tokens of reasoning before a 10-token answer reduces total latency from the equivalent of 1,010 sequential forward passes to roughly 202 drafting-verification cycles. The paper trained the DeepSeek-R1 draft model using the OpenThoughts-114k-math dataset in addition to ShareGPT and UltraChat-200K, which likely explains the strong GSM8K performance. An ablation testing the draft model trained without OpenThoughts on this target model would clarify how much of the reasoning-model benefit comes from domain-specific training data vs. the architectural improvements.

- **Draft tree depth increase (Appendix A)**: EAGLE-3 increases the maximum draft tree depth from 6 (EAGLE-2) to 8 while keeping the total number of draft tokens per cycle the same (60 for 7B/8B, 50 for 13B, 48 for 70B). This change is enabled by the flat `n`-`α` curve (Figure 7) — since acceptance rate doesn't degrade with depth, deeper trees provide more accepted tokens per verification pass without increasing the per-pass draft model cost (the total draft token budget remains fixed). An ablation comparing EAGLE-3 with depth 6 vs. depth 8 is not reported, so the exact contribution of the deeper tree to the overall speedup improvement is unknown. The paper states this as a configuration choice enabled by the architectural improvements rather than a separately validated contribution.

- **On-policy training data generation**: The paper generates training data by calling the target model rather than using a fixed dataset of responses. An ablation comparing on-policy vs. off-policy training data (e.g., using the original ShareGPT/UltraChat responses rather than target-model-generated responses) is not reported. This is a potentially important design choice — if the draft model is trained on human-written responses, it may learn to predict what humans write rather than what the target model would generate, reducing acceptance rates. The paper implicitly argues for on-policy data by adopting this approach, but without an ablation, the magnitude of this effect is unknown.

- **Negative result: HASS comparison**: While HASS is discussed conceptually (Section 3.2) and mentioned in Figure 2, the paper does not provide a detailed head-to-head empirical comparison in Table 1 or a separate ablation table. The paper states that "Figure 2 also shows the speedup of EAGLE-3 and HASS, with EAGLE-3 demonstrating significantly better performance" — but Figure 2 shows HASS as an unlabeled bar for only one model (Vicuna 13B), and the exact speedup numbers for HASS are not reported in any table. The conceptual distinction (HASS preserves feature prediction; EAGLE-3 removes it) is clearly articulated, but the empirical demonstration that this distinction produces "significantly better performance" is under-supported by the presented data.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the evidence support the claim that EAGLE-3 achieves "up to ~6.5× speedup" and "about 1.4× improvement over EAGLE-2"?

The headline numbers in Table 1 support this claim for specific task-model-temperature combinations: EAGLE-3 achieves 6.47× speedup on HumanEval with Vicuna 13B at T=0, and the mean improvement over EAGLE-2 across all configurations ranges from approximately 1.2× to 1.5×. However, the "up to" framing obscures substantial variability. On CNN/Daily Mail with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B at T=1, the improvement over EAGLE-2 is from 2.44× to 2.99× — about 1.23×, which is a meaningful but more modest gain. The "1.4× improvement" figure should be understood as an approximate average across favorable conditions, not a guaranteed minimum.

The speedup ratios themselves are measured at batch size 1 where speculative sampling provides maximum benefit. The SGLang and vLLM results (Tables 3–5) show that at batch size 1, the absolute throughput improvement is dramatic (2.36× over no speculative sampling for EAGLE-3 on LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B), but at larger batch sizes the relative advantage of EAGLE-3 over EAGLE-2 narrows. At batch size 64 in SGLang, EAGLE-3 achieves 1.38× throughput while EAGLE is at 0.99× — EAGLE-3's advantage is that it *remains beneficial* where EAGLE becomes detrimental, but the 1.38× figure is not a 1.4× improvement over EAGLE-2 (which isn't tested at batch size 64). The paper conflates two different improvement metrics: batch-size-1 latency speedup (where 1.4× over EAGLE-2 is supported) and large-batch throughput (where the advantage is that EAGLE-3 remains above 1.0× while EAGLE drops below).

#### Does the evidence support the claim that EAGLE-3 demonstrates a "scaling law for inference acceleration" where increasing training data produces proportional gains?

Figure 1 supports the directional claim: EAGLE-3's speedup increases with data scale while EAGLE-2's does not. However, the paper tests only four data points (1×, 2×, 4×, 8×) on a single target model (LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B) with a single task (MT-bench). The relationship appears roughly logarithmic rather than linear: speedup increases by ~0.3× from 1× to 2× (+100% data), then ~0.3× from 2× to 4× (+100% data), then ~0.3× from 4× to 8× (+100% data) — consistent diminishing returns in percentage gain per data doubling. The paper's claim of a "scaling law" would be substantially strengthened by testing at least one more data point (e.g., 16×) to characterize the functional form and determine whether the trend continues, saturates, or accelerates. Without this, calling it a "scaling law" is premature — the evidence shows a positive monotonic relationship over a limited range, which is a scaling trend rather than a law.

Moreover, the paper does not isolate whether the scaling benefit comes from more diverse prompts (ShareGPT → UltraChat-200K adds conversational variety), more total tokens (UltraChat-200K has ~7× the entries), or some combination. The draft model is trained on target-model-generated responses, so data quality (how representative the generated responses are of the target model's natural output distribution) likely matters as much as quantity. The confound between data quantity and data diversity makes it difficult to project how much further scaling would help — if the gain comes primarily from diversity, then adding more data from the same distribution might saturate quickly; if it comes from sheer token volume, then continued scaling might be more reliable.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that the feature prediction constraint was the bottleneck preventing data scaling from improving performance?

The ablation evidence is strong but incomplete. Figure 4 shows that removing `L_fea` while keeping everything else (including no training-time test) improves `0-α` acceptance rate with data scale but catastrophically degrades `1-α`. Adding training-time test (which makes it EAGLE-3) recovers and exceeds `1-α` while maintaining the `0-α` improvement. This cleanly demonstrates that feature prediction was the constraint limiting `0-α` scaling, and training-time test was necessary to make feature removal viable for multi-step drafting.

However, the ablation doesn't test an alternative hypothesis: could the feature prediction constraint be *improved* rather than removed? For example, a weighted combination `α L_token + (1-α) L_fea` with small α might preserve the multi-step generalization benefit of `L_fea` while reducing its constraint on expressive capacity. Or the feature prediction target could be relaxed from exact MSE to a contrastive or ranking loss. The paper's dichotomy of "keep `L_fea`" vs. "remove `L_fea` entirely" doesn't explore whether the bottleneck is binary or continuous. It's possible that a small amount of feature regularization provides benefits for training stability or generalization that pure token prediction misses, and the paper's evidence doesn't rule this out.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that multi-layer feature fusion provides richer information than top-layer features alone?

Table 2 provides clean evidence: adding fused features on top of feature removal improves speedup from 3.82× to 4.40× on MT-bench and from 3.77× to 4.48× on GSM8K. However, the ablation doesn't test *which* layers matter or *how many* layers are needed. The paper selects "low, middle, and high-level features" without specifying exact layer indices or the sensitivity of results to this choice. Would two layers (e.g., middle and high) suffice? Would all layers with a learned weighted average outperform the three fixed positions? Would the optimal layers be different for different target models or tasks? These questions are unanswered, meaning the claim that multi-layer fusion is beneficial is supported, but the claim about *why* it's beneficial (the paper argues top-layer features are "inherently limited to the next token") is an interpretation not directly tested by the ablation.

The paper also does not compare against alternative approaches for incorporating richer target model information that don't require feature fusion, such as using the top-layer feature at multiple recent positions (which would provide temporal context without accessing different layers) or using the LM head's pre-softmax logits directly as input features. These alternatives would test whether it's specifically *multi-layer* information that helps, or just *more* information of any kind.

#### Missing ablations and experiments that would strengthen the paper

**Training-time test depth ablation**: The paper uses 3 simulated steps in training (Figure 6) but doesn't test whether 1, 2, 4, or more steps changes the results. The `n`-`α` curve in Figure 7 is flat through `n=7`, beyond the 3 training steps, which suggests the model generalizes — but an ablation showing that 3 steps is sufficient and more don't help (or that fewer are insufficient) would characterize the tradeoff between training cost and multi-step generalization.

**Draft model capacity ablation**: The draft model uses a single decoder layer. Would two layers provide meaningful improvement? The paper's argument that a single layer is sufficient given the rich input features is plausible, but an ablation testing 1 vs. 2 vs. 3 decoder layers would quantify the tradeoff between draft model cost (which directly affects speedup) and draft model quality (which affects acceptance rate).

**On-policy vs. off-policy training data**: Using target-model-generated responses vs. original dataset responses is a critical design choice that is not ablated. If off-policy training data produces comparable results, the training pipeline could be simplified substantially (no need to generate responses from each new target model).

**Computational cost of feature extraction and fusion**: The paper reports speedup ratios that include all overhead, but doesn't break down the cost components. How much of the target model's forward pass time is spent extracting features from specific layers? Is the 3-layer extraction materially more expensive than the 1-layer extraction in EAGLE? For the 70B model, does the relative cost of feature extraction change compared to 8B?

**Comparison against EAGLE-2 with equivalent data**: EAGLE-3 is trained on ShareGPT + UltraChat-200K (~532K entries), while the EAGLE-2 baselines are trained only on ShareGPT (~68K entries). This means the comparison in Table 1 potentially confounds architectural improvements with data scale differences. If EAGLE-2 were trained on the same ~532K entries (which the scaling curve in Figure 1 suggests would produce negligible improvement, given the flat trend from 1× to 8×), the comparison would be fairer. The paper implicitly addresses this through Figure 1 (showing that EAGLE-2's flat scaling curve means more data wouldn't help), but Table 1 should ideally include an EAGLE-2 baseline trained on the full dataset to make the comparison directly controlled for data quantity.

**Domain-specific training data ablation for DeepSeek-R1**: EAGLE-3 on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B uses OpenThoughts-114k-math in addition to the standard training data. The strong GSM8K result (5.01× speedup) could be driven by this domain-specific data rather than the architectural improvements. An ablation without OpenThoughts on this model would disentangle the two effects.

#### Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design

**Single draft model configuration per target model**: The paper evaluates one EAGLE-3 draft model per target model, trained on the combined ShareGPT + UltraChat-200K dataset (plus OpenThoughts for DeepSeek-R1). The draft model is then applied to all five tasks without task-specific fine-tuning. While this is consistent with prior work and demonstrates generalization, it means the reported speedups are an average across tasks that may mask task-specific optimization opportunities. A coding-specific draft model might perform substantially better on HumanEval than the general-purpose one reported.

**No variance estimates or significance testing**: All numbers in Table 1 are point estimates without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or information about the number of evaluation runs. The difference between EAGLE-3 (4.40×) and the "+ remove fea con" ablation (3.82×) on MT-bench is 0.58× — is this a statistically reliable difference? Without variance information, the reader cannot assess whether the incremental contributions in the ablation study are distinguishable from measurement noise. The speedup ratio is a complex metric affected by hardware state, batch size interactions, and random sampling (at T=1), making variance quantification important.

**Limited model diversity**: All target models are from the LLaMA/Vicuna/DeepSeek families, which share similar transformer architectures. The paper doesn't test on models with different architectures (e.g., Mamba/state-space models, mixture-of-experts with different routing patterns, encoder-decoder models) where the optimal feature layers for fusion or the effectiveness of training-time test might differ.

**No latency breakdown by component**: The speedup ratio is an end-to-end metric that obscures where time is actually spent. How much of the improvement over EAGLE-2 comes from higher acceptance rates (reducing the number of verification passes) vs. faster drafting (if the multi-layer fusion is more efficient to compute than EAGLE's feature handling) vs. more efficient tree attention during verification? Without this breakdown, it's difficult to assess which components of the system are most performance-critical and would benefit most from further optimization.

**Hardware specificity**: All experiments use NVIDIA H100 or A100 GPUs (or RTX 3090 for the vLLM experiments). Speculative sampling performance is sensitive to the compute-to-memory-bandwidth ratio of the hardware — the speedup ratios may differ substantially on consumer GPUs, edge devices, or non-NVIDIA hardware with different memory hierarchies. This is a practical limitation rather than a methodological flaw, but it means the reported speedups should not be assumed to transfer to arbitrary deployment environments.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Scaling Law Is Demonstrated Over a Narrow Data Range on a Single Model-Task Combination

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper claims to have discovered a "scaling law for inference acceleration" (Section 1, Section 4, Figure 1) where increasing the draft model's training data produces proportional gains in speedup. However, this claim is supported by only four data points — 1×, 2×, 4×, and 8× the ShareGPT dataset — evaluated on a single target model (LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B) and a single task (MT-bench). The paper does not test intermediate data scales (e.g., 16×, 32×) that would characterize the functional form of the relationship or determine whether it continues, saturates, or diminishes. The paper acknowledges this implicitly in Section 4.3: "We expect larger data size would lead to further improved speedup ratio," but this is a projection, not a finding.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot determine from the presented evidence whether doubling data from 8× to 16× would yield proportional benefits, diminishing returns, or no further improvement. The relationship in Figure 1 appears roughly logarithmic over the tested range: the absolute speedup gain from 1× to 2× (+100% data) is approximately 0.3×, from 2× to 4× (+100% data) is approximately 0.3×, and from 4× to 8× (+100% data) is approximately 0.3×. If this logarithmic pattern continues, each additional doubling of data would produce smaller relative gains, and the total headroom for data scaling — while real — may be more limited than the term "scaling law" implies. A practitioner investing in 16× or 32× data scale cannot reliably predict the return on that investment. Furthermore, the confound between data quantity (more total entries) and data diversity (UltraChat-200K contains ~7× the entries of ShareGPT but also spans different conversational domains) means the scaling benefit may partly reflect domain coverage rather than pure volume, making it unclear whether adding more data from similar distributions would produce the same trend.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 1 shows the speedup and acceptance length curves for EAGLE-2 (flat) and EAGLE-3 (rising) at data scales of 1×, 2×, 4×, and 8× on MT-bench with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B. Figure 4 shows the corresponding `0-α` and `1-α` acceptance rates at the same four data points. No other model, task, or data scale is tested for the scaling behavior. The paper does not fit a functional form, report confidence intervals on the trend, or extrapolate to larger data scales with any quantitative model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation beyond the forward-looking statement that larger data "would lead to further improved speedup ratio." No experiments at 16× or 32× are conducted, no power-law fit is attempted, and no discussion of potential saturation is provided. This is a clear direction for future work that the paper identifies but does not pursue.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in Reported Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** EAGLE-3's draft model requires extracting and fusing features from three layers of the target model (low, middle, and high) during every drafting step, and during verification, the target model must compute these features at all positions. The paper reports end-to-end speedup ratios that include all overhead (Section 4: "The actual test speedup ratio relative to vanilla autoregressive decoding"), but it does not provide a component-level breakdown of where time is spent: how much overhead comes from feature extraction at specific layers, the FC layer projection for fusion, the draft model forward passes, and the tree-structured verification attention. Without this breakdown, it is unclear which components are performance-critical and how the overhead scales with model size.

**The consequence.** A practitioner implementing EAGLE-3 on a new target model cannot easily estimate the cost of feature extraction for layers that may not be conveniently accessible in their inference framework. In standard transformer implementations, intermediate layer outputs are typically discarded after the forward pass; extracting them requires either modifying the model to retain activations (increasing memory usage) or performing additional forward passes to specific layers. The paper's experiments use fully modified implementations where feature extraction is integrated, but the cost relative to vanilla autoregressive decoding is not isolated. For the 70B model (LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B), where the target model forward pass dominates total time heavily, the relative overhead of feature extraction may differ from the 8B model — the speedup ratios are lower for the 70B model (4.12× mean at T=0 vs. 4.44× for 8B), consistent with proportionally higher overhead, but the paper does not quantify how much of this gap is due to feature extraction cost versus the inherent difficulty of drafting for a larger model.

Furthermore, the feature extraction cost scales with the number of layers accessed. The paper uses three layers (low, middle, high), but does not test whether two layers or even one multi-purpose layer would suffice. If the gain from three-layer fusion is modest relative to two-layer fusion, the additional extraction cost could be unjustified. Table 2 shows that fused features add approximately 0.58× speedup on MT-bench beyond feature removal alone, but this does not decompose into "information gain from additional layers" versus "cost of extracting additional layers."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 reports end-to-end speedup ratios that implicitly include all overhead. Appendix A notes the total number of draft tokens per verification cycle (60, 50, 48 for 7B/8B, 13B, and 70B models) and draft tree depth (8 for EAGLE-3), but does not report the time spent in feature extraction vs. drafting vs. verification. No ablation tests the effect of different layer choices on either speedup or computational cost. Table 4 provides throughput numbers for SGLang at batch size 1 (373.25 tokens/s for EAGLE-3 vs. 158.34 tokens/s for vanilla), but this is again end-to-end without component timing.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The speedup metric is defined and reported only as a single scalar, and the implementation details (Appendix A) describe the configuration but not the profiling breakdown. Future work that profiles the system component-by-component on different hardware and model scales would be valuable for practitioners optimizing deployment.

### The Training-Time Test Procedure Increases Training Cost in Ways That Are Not Quantified

**The assumption or constraint.** The training-time test procedure (Section 3.2, Figure 6) simulates multi-step generation during training by performing additional forward passes (Steps 1, 2, etc.) where the draft model's own outputs are fed back as inputs, with gradients flowing through the feedback loop. This means the training computation cost per training example is higher than for EAGLE or EAGLE-2, which train only on Step 0 (single-step prediction). The paper uses up to 3 simulated steps based on Figure 6, but does not report the total training FLOPs, training wall-clock time, or memory consumption relative to EAGLE-2 training. This matters because the scaling-law claim — that expanding training data improves speedup — represents a joint increase in both data volume and training compute, but the paper only varies the data scale on the x-axis of Figure 1, not the training compute.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt EAGLE-3 over EAGLE-2 cannot evaluate the training-time cost-benefit tradeoff. If training-time test with 3 simulated steps increases per-example training cost by, say, 3× (since each example is processed 4 times: Step 0 + Steps 1–3), then the total training compute at 8× data for EAGLE-3 could be 24× the training compute of EAGLE-2 at 1× data. The ~1.4× inference speedup improvement would then need to be weighed against substantially higher training cost. For a model deployed at high volume, this tradeoff might be favorable (training cost amortized over many inference queries); for a model used infrequently or in a research setting, it might not be.

Moreover, the paper does not ablate the number of simulated training steps. Would 1 or 2 simulated steps provide most of the benefit at lower training cost? Does the benefit saturate at 3 steps, or would 4 or 5 steps provide further gains? The `n`-`α` curve in Figure 7 shows EAGLE-3's acceptance rate is flat through `n=7` — well beyond the 3 training steps — which suggests the model generalizes. If 2 simulated steps during training produce comparable generalization to 3, training cost could be reduced without sacrificing performance. Conversely, if more steps would push the flat acceptance curve even higher or extend it to larger `n`, additional training cost might be justified. Neither case is tested.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper mentions 3 simulated steps in Figure 6 and the surrounding discussion, but provides no ablation on the number of training-time test steps. Training hyperparameters (Section 4, Implementation) list the optimizer, learning rate, beta values, and gradient clipping, but do not report total training FLOPs, GPU-hours, or per-example cost relative to EAGLE-2. The data scaling experiment (Figure 1, Figure 4) controls only the data quantity, not the training compute.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. Training cost is not discussed as a tradeoff, and the scaling-law presentation treats data scale as the independent variable without acknowledging that training compute also scales — and scales super-linearly in data due to the multi-step procedure. This is a significant omission for a paper that makes claims about scaling behavior.

### All Evaluations Use a Single Model Architecture Family; Generalization to Other Architectures Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates EAGLE-3 on four target models — Vicuna 13B, LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B — all of which share the same basic transformer architecture (decoder-only, multi-head self-attention, residual connections, layer normalization) derived from the LLaMA family. The paper does not test on models with substantially different architectures, such as mixture-of-experts (MoE) models where different tokens are processed by different subsets of parameters, encoder-decoder models (e.g., T5, BART) where the feature extraction semantics differ, state-space models (e.g., Mamba) that lack the layer-wise feature hierarchy EAGLE-3 relies on, or models with non-standard attention mechanisms. The paper acknowledges this limitation indirectly: "Due to the GPU constraint, we are unable to test EAGLE-3 on the 405B and 671B models" (Section 4), but this only addresses model scale, not architectural diversity.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of EAGLE-3's design may not transfer to non-LLaMA architectures. The multi-layer feature fusion assumes the target model has a clear hierarchy of representations from low to high layers that capture different semantic levels — this is well-established for standard transformers but may not hold for architectures with different information flow patterns. In MoE models, the features at a given position are computed by a dynamically routed subset of experts, which could introduce variability in feature quality across layers not present in dense models. In state-space models, the concept of "layers" with distinct feature representations exists but the information processing dynamics differ fundamentally from self-attention, and the optimal layers for fusion may be completely different.

The paper's claim that top-layer features are "inherently limited to the next token" (Section 1) — used to motivate multi-layer fusion — is an empirical property of LLaMA-style transformers that may not apply to models where the LM head receives information through a different pathway. If a model's architecture already incorporates multi-scale features before the LM head, the benefit of EAGLE-3's fusion might be reduced or eliminated.

Additionally, the draft model architecture itself (a single Transformer decoder layer with self-attention) assumes the target model's features are compatible with self-attention-based processing. If the target model uses a fundamentally different mechanism (e.g., linear attention, structured state spaces), the optimal draft model architecture might need to be different.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All experiments in Sections 4.1–4.4 use the four target models from the LLaMA/Vicuna/DeepSeek families. No experiments are conducted on encoder-decoder models, MoE models, state-space models, or any non-LLaMA-derived architecture. Appendix A mentions using "models from the Huggingface.transformers library," but all selected models share the same architectural lineage. The paper does not discuss architectural generalization as a limitation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The consistent results across LLaMA-Instruct 8B, 70B, Vicuna 13B, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill 8B demonstrate robustness within the LLaMA family across model scales and fine-tuning styles (chat vs. reasoning), but this does not constitute evidence of architectural generalization. Future work testing EAGLE-3 on MoE models (e.g., Mixtral), encoder-decoder models, and non-transformer architectures would be needed to establish the method's broader applicability.

### EAGLE-3 vs. EAGLE-2 Comparisons Confound Architectural Changes with Training Data Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** The headline speedup comparisons in Table 1 and Figure 2 compare EAGLE-3 (trained on ShareGPT + UltraChat-200K, approximately 532K entries total) against EAGLE-2 (trained on ShareGPT only, approximately 68K entries). EAGLE-2's training data configuration follows prior work — the paper states "LLaMA3-Instruct is trained using the ShareGPT dataset (consistent with Medusa and Hydra)" (Appendix A) — but this means the reported 1.4× improvement conflates two changes: the architectural innovations (feature removal, training-time test, multi-layer fusion) and an ~8× increase in training data. Figure 1 provides evidence that EAGLE-2 would benefit negligibly from additional data (its scaling curve is flat), but this does not eliminate the confound: the fair comparison would be EAGLE-2 trained on the same ~532K entries as EAGLE-3, tested side-by-side.

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt EAGLE-3 over EAGLE-2 cannot cleanly attribute performance gains to architecture versus data. If EAGLE-2 at 8× data achieves, say, 3.5× speedup (slightly above the 3.4× shown in Figure 1), then the architectural contribution is approximately 4.4× − 3.5× = 0.9×, not the 4.4× − 3.16× = 1.24× that Table 1 suggests. The paper's own scaling data in Figure 1 shows EAGLE-2 at 8× achieves approximately 3.4× speedup (vs. 3.16× in Table 1 at ShareGPT-only scale), but this number comes from the scaling experiment on a single task-model combination, not from the full five-task evaluation. The cross-task EAGLE-2 numbers in Table 1 are all at 1× data, making the comparison to EAGLE-3 (at ~8× data) structurally unfair.

The issue is partially mitigated by Figure 1, which demonstrates that EAGLE-2's performance is insensitive to data scale — if true across all tasks and models, the confound would be harmless because EAGLE-2 at 8× would perform essentially identically to EAGLE-2 at 1×. However, this insensitivity is demonstrated only on MT-bench with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, not on the other four tasks, three target models, or two temperature settings that Table 1 covers. It is possible that EAGLE-2 benefits from data scaling on some task-model combinations where the feature prediction bottleneck is less severe.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 reports EAGLE-2 speedups from prior work (trained on ShareGPT only) and EAGLE-3 speedups (trained on ShareGPT + UltraChat-200K). Appendix A confirms the training data configuration. Figure 1 shows the scaling behavior for 1×–8× data on MT-bench with LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, showing EAGLE-2 as nearly flat. No experiments report EAGLE-2 trained on the full ~532K dataset and evaluated across all five tasks.

**Mitigation status.** The paper addresses this partially through Figure 1, which provides evidence that EAGLE-2 does not benefit from additional data on the specific task-model combination tested. However, the paper does not train and evaluate EAGLE-2 on the full ~532K dataset across all tasks, which would be the definitive way to eliminate the confound. The paper implicitly argues that Figure 1's flat scaling curve makes this unnecessary, but this argument relies on extrapolating a single-task, single-model result to the full evaluation suite. Given that the scaling law is a central claim of the paper, the absence of a data-matched EAGLE-2 baseline is a notable gap.

### The Method Has Not Been Tested on Truly Large-Scale Models (405B+) or in High-Throughput Production Settings at Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates EAGLE-3 on models up to 70B parameters (LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B) and explicitly states: "Due to the GPU constraint, we are unable to test EAGLE-3 on the 405B and 671B models" (Section 4). The throughput experiments in SGLang and vLLM (Section 4.3, 4.4, Tables 3–5) use only LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B and simplified configurations (chain length 2–3, no tree structure), which the paper acknowledges: "This part of the experiment did not use the tree structure." The full EAGLE-3 configuration with tree-structured drafting and depth-8 trees is only evaluated at batch size 1.

**The consequence.** Two important practical questions are left unanswered. First, does EAGLE-3's advantage over EAGLE-2 persist or change at the 405B+ scale? At very large model sizes, the draft model's relative cost becomes negligible (a single decoder layer vs. 100+ layers in the target model), so acceptance rate improvements translate almost directly into speedup. However, feature extraction from specific intermediate layers of a 405B model may be disproportionately expensive if those layers are not already materialized in memory during inference. The relative overhead of the Feature Fusion Module's FC layer projection and the concatenation operation may also change at extreme hidden dimensions (e.g., 16K+ for 671B models).

Second, the simplified SGLang/vLLM experiments (no tree structure, short chains) likely understate EAGLE-3's full potential at small batch sizes (where tree drafting provides the most benefit) but also leave open the question of how tree-structured verification scales to larger batches. Tree attention during verification requires the target model to process multiple candidate tokens at each position, which increases the verification forward pass cost. At batch sizes of 32, 64, or 128 — common in production serving — the increased verification cost from tree attention might offset the benefits of higher acceptance rates, potentially making the tree-free configuration used in the SGLang/vLLM tests closer to optimal. The paper does not characterize this tradeoff.

Additionally, the SGLang team conducted the throughput experiments independently (acknowledged in the paper), which adds credibility, but the results are reported only for LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B. The scaling behavior of EAGLE-3's throughput improvement with batch size may differ for the 70B model, where the target model's forward pass is much more expensive relative to drafting and the compute-utilization curve is different.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 (SGLang, H100, LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B) shows throughput improvement declining from 1.81× at batch size 2 to 1.38× at batch size 64 for EAGLE-3. Table 5 (vLLM, A100, same model) shows a decline from 1.75× at batch size 2 to 1.01× at batch size 56. The paper notes that "EAGLE reduces throughput at a batch size of 24, whereas EAGLE-3 still achieves a 38% throughput improvement at a batch size of 64" (Section 4.3), highlighting that EAGLE-3 remains beneficial at larger batches where EAGLE becomes detrimental. However, these experiments omit tree-structured drafting and use chain lengths of 2–3, representing a degraded configuration. No throughput results are reported for the 70B model, for tree-structured EAGLE-3 at any batch size beyond 1, or for models at 405B+ scale.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses throughput concerns by testing in two production frameworks (SGLang and vLLM) and across batch sizes up to 64. The acknowledgment that tree structure was not used in these experiments is transparent. However, the paper does not test full-configuration EAGLE-3 at production batch sizes, does not test any configuration on models above 70B, and the statement about GPU constraints preventing 405B/671B testing acknowledges but does not resolve the scale generalization question. These are practical constraints that limit the deployability evidence, especially for organizations running the largest open-weight models.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

EAGLE-3 changes how the speculative sampling community thinks about **what the draft model should predict** and **what information it should receive**. Prior to this work, a consensus had formed that feature-level autoregression—predicting the target model's top-layer features rather than tokens—was the key to leveraging the target model's internal state effectively. EAGLE, EAGLE-2, Medusa, Hydra, HASS, and Falcon all shared this assumption, differing only in how they structured the prediction heads, handled error accumulation, or constructed the draft tree. EAGLE-3 challenges this consensus at its foundation by demonstrating that the feature prediction objective is not a necessary cost of accessing the target model's internals but rather a **removable bottleneck** that actively prevents the draft model from improving with more training data.

This is not a paradigm shift—EAGLE-3 remains firmly within the speculative sampling framework of alternating drafting and verification, and it inherits EAGLE-2's dynamic draft tree mechanism. It is better characterized as a **diagnostic reframing with significant practical consequences**. The paper demonstrates that a design choice previously viewed as essential (feature prediction) was in fact a surrogate objective misaligned with the true goal (token prediction). This misalignment created a ceiling that made additional training data appear useless, leading the field to conclude—incorrectly—that draft model quality had saturated. EAGLE-3 shows that the saturation was an artifact of the architecture, not a fundamental limit, and that removing the surrogate constraint exposes a scaling relationship where more data reliably produces better draft models.

The work resolves a puzzle that should have bothered the speculative sampling community more than it did. The broader LLM field had established that model quality scales with pretraining data (LLaMA 1→2→3: 1T→2T→15T tokens). Yet EAGLE and EAGLE-2 showed negligible improvement from expanding draft model training data. This discrepancy between the LLM scaling narrative and the speculative sampling scaling evidence was not, apparently, widely questioned. EAGLE-3 provides a unified explanation: the feature prediction constraint made draft model training a fundamentally different optimization problem than pretraining—one with a hard ceiling determined by the misalignment between the surrogate and true objectives, not by data quantity.

The paper also reframes the relationship between EAGLE and HASS. HASS (Zhang et al., 2024) attempted to fix EAGLE's error accumulation by simulating multi-step drafting during training while keeping the feature prediction loss. EAGLE-3's diagnosis reveals that HASS was treating a symptom (error accumulation) rather than the root cause (feature prediction as a surrogate objective). The training-time test procedure in EAGLE-3 serves a different purpose than HASS's simulated rollouts: it enables the draft model to handle its own unconstrained outputs as inputs, rather than attempting to make those outputs better approximate ground-truth features. This reframing makes HASS appear as a partial solution to a misdiagnosed problem.

The most important landscape change is **making data scale a reliable lever for improving inference acceleration**. Before EAGLE-3, a research team investing in speculative sampling would focus almost exclusively on architectural innovations—better tree structures, improved attention mechanisms, new feature prediction objectives—because data scaling had been shown not to help. After EAGLE-3, the primary optimization axis shifts: curating larger, higher-quality, more diverse draft model training datasets is now predictably worthwhile, and architectural work should focus on ensuring the data scaling trend continues rather than squeezing marginal gains from a saturated architecture. This parallels the shift in pretraining research after Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) established that data and model size should be scaled jointly—the question changes from "what clever architecture can we design?" to "how do we best allocate our data and compute budget?"

Research directions that become **more attractive** include: large-scale draft model training data curation (especially on-policy data generation from the target model), domain-specific draft models for specialized deployment scenarios (the DeepSeek-R1 GSM8K result at 5.01× speedup suggests domain adaptation pays off), and systematic characterization of the data scaling curve beyond 8× to determine the functional form and practical ceiling. Research directions that become **less attractive** include: incremental improvements to feature prediction objectives (the paper's evidence suggests this is optimizing a surrogate), methods that rely exclusively on top-layer features without addressing the information bottleneck for long-range token prediction, and architectural modifications that preserve the feature constraint while adding complexity (since removing the constraint is simpler and more effective).

The practical deployment implication is also significant: EAGLE-3's large-batch throughput results (38% improvement at batch size 64 in SGLang, Table 3) challenge the conventional wisdom that "speculative sampling reduces throughput at large batch sizes." EAGLE becomes detrimental at batch size 24; EAGLE-3 remains beneficial at batch size 64. This means speculative sampling is no longer confined to latency-sensitive, small-batch deployments—it can improve throughput in high-volume production serving, changing the cost calculus for LLM inference at scale.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the functional form of the data scaling law beyond 8× on multiple model families.** The paper establishes a positive monotonic relationship between training data scale and speedup over the range 1×–8× ShareGPT on a single model-task combination (LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B on MT-bench, Figure 1). A natural follow-up would test 16×, 32×, and 64× data scales—potentially using larger conversational datasets like OpenHermes, UltraInteract, or web-scale chat logs—to determine whether the trend is logarithmic (diminishing returns per doubling), power-law (smoothly decreasing marginal returns), or linear (constant returns, unlikely given typical ML scaling). This experiment should also test multiple target models at different scales (8B, 70B) and on different task categories (code, math, conversation) to establish whether the scaling law is universal or model/task-dependent. The key measurement would be whether the curve maintains positive slope through 64× or saturates at some threshold, which would establish the practical ceiling on data investment. The paper provides the necessary infrastructure—the training pipeline, the data generation procedure via target model calls, and the evaluation suite—making this experiment straightforward to execute given sufficient compute.

**Ablating the optimal number and selection of fused feature layers.** EAGLE-3 fuses low, middle, and high-level features from the target model, but the paper does not specify which exact layers are used or justify the choice of three layers rather than two, four, or all layers. A systematic ablation would test: (a) whether the optimal layers are consistent across model scales (8B vs. 70B) and architectures, (b) whether a learned weighted combination of all layers (with sparsity regularization to prevent overfitting) outperforms the fixed three-layer fusion, (c) whether the same layers that work for conversation tasks (MT-bench) also work for code (HumanEval) and math (GSM8K), and (d) how the number of fused layers trades off against the computational cost of feature extraction. The paper's Table 2 shows that fused features add 0.58× speedup on MT-bench beyond feature removal alone, but this experiment would determine whether the 0.58× gain could be larger with better layer selection or smaller with fewer layers—directly guiding practitioners on the cost-benefit tradeoff of multi-layer extraction.

**Combining EAGLE-3 with draft model capacity scaling.** The draft model uses a single Transformer decoder layer, chosen to keep drafting fast relative to the target model's verification forward pass. But EAGLE-3's higher acceptance rates and flat error accumulation curve (Figure 7) mean the draft model could potentially benefit from additional capacity—a second decoder layer, larger hidden dimension, or more attention heads—without the compounding error penalty that would make deeper draft models counterproductive in EAGLE. A follow-up would test draft model configurations with 2, 3, or 4 decoder layers, measuring whether the acceptance rate improvements outweigh the increased per-step drafting cost, and how this tradeoff changes with target model scale (the relative cost of a deeper draft model is proportionally larger for an 8B target than a 70B target). The paper's framework makes this experiment tractable: the training-time test procedure and multi-layer feature fusion are agnostic to draft model depth, so scaling the draft model is a matter of changing the decoder layer count and retraining. The key comparison would be speedup vs. draft model FLOPs, establishing whether there is a compute-optimal draft model size analogous to Chinchilla-optimal pretraining.

**Testing EAGLE-3 on non-LLaMA architectures—Mixture-of-Experts, encoder-decoder, and state-space models.** The paper evaluates exclusively on LLaMA-derived decoder-only transformers. This leaves open whether the multi-layer feature fusion assumption—that different layers capture complementary semantic information useful for token prediction—transfers to architectures with fundamentally different information flow. A concrete experiment would apply EAGLE-3 to Mixtral 8×7B (MoE), where different tokens are processed by different expert subsets, potentially creating layer-wise feature heterogeneity not present in dense models; to Flan-T5 (encoder-decoder), where the encoder and decoder have separate feature hierarchies and the optimal fusion might include cross-attention features; and to a Mamba variant, where the structured state-space mechanism lacks self-attention entirely and the concept of "layer-wise features" may need reinterpretation. The paper's acceptance rate diagnostic (n-α curves, Figure 7) provides a clean way to assess whether the training-time test procedure eliminates error accumulation in these architectures as effectively as in LLaMA models. A negative result—e.g., training-time test fails to prevent multi-step collapse in Mamba—would delimit the method's applicability and motivate architecture-specific adaptations.

**Developing online difficulty-aware draft budget allocation.** EAGLE-3's flat n-α acceptance curve (Figure 7) means the draft model's accuracy doesn't degrade with depth, but the absolute acceptance rate still varies substantially across tasks (HumanEval ~0.80 vs. CNN/Daily Mail ~0.75 at n=0, estimated from speedup differences). A follow-up would develop an online mechanism that monitors the running acceptance rate during generation and dynamically adjusts the draft tree depth and width: on easy contexts (code templates, repetitive patterns), expand the tree aggressively to generate more tokens per verification pass; on difficult contexts (creative writing, high-entropy regions), contract the tree to avoid wasted draft computation. EAGLE-2's confidence-based pruning provides a starting point, but it operates within a fixed tree budget. An adaptive system could vary the budget itself based on real-time acceptance feedback, potentially recovering additional speedup on heterogeneous inputs without the offline difficulty estimation cost discussed in the Limitations section. The paper's SGLang and vLLM integrations (Tables 3–5) provide a testing ground for whether dynamic budget allocation improves throughput in serving scenarios where the input distribution is mixed.

**Training a single draft model that serves multiple target models via lightweight adaptation.** The paper trains separate draft models for each target model (Vicuna 13B, LLaMA-Instruct 3.1 8B, LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B). This means deploying EAGLE-3 with N target models requires N draft models, each with its own training cost and storage. A follow-up would test whether a single base draft model, trained on features from a family of related target models, can be adapted to a specific target model via a lightweight adapter (e.g., the Feature Fusion Module's FC layer being target-model-specific while the decoder layer is shared). The paper's architecture makes this plausible: the fusion layer is the only component that interacts with target-model-specific feature spaces, while the decoder layer operates on a fixed k-dimensional representation. If a shared decoder layer with per-model fusion adapters achieves comparable acceptance rates to fully separate draft models, the deployment and maintenance cost of EAGLE-3 would drop substantially for organizations running multiple fine-tuned variants of the same base model. The experiment would measure acceptance rate degradation as a function of target model divergence from the shared base, establishing how far a fine-tuned model can drift before needing its own draft model.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Low-latency serving of reasoning models.** Models like DeepSeek-R1 produce lengthy chains of thought (potentially thousands of tokens) before generating a concise answer. EAGLE-3's 5.01× speedup on GSM8K with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-LLaMA 8B (Table 1, T=0) means a reasoning process that takes 20 seconds at vanilla speed would complete in roughly 4 seconds. For interactive applications where users wait for reasoning-model responses—educational tutoring, code debugging assistants, research analysis tools—this latency reduction transforms the user experience from unacceptably slow to reasonably responsive. The domain-specific training data experiment (OpenThoughts-114k-math) suggests that the speedup could be further improved by curating reasoning traces from the target model itself, creating a positive feedback loop: faster inference enables generating more training data for the draft model, which improves acceptance rates, which improves speedup. This use case is particularly compelling because reasoning models have the highest inference-cost-to-answer-length ratio of any current LLM deployment scenario.

**Cost reduction for batch inference pipelines.** Organizations running large-scale batch evaluation—scoring candidate solutions, generating training data for self-improvement, or processing document corpora—can directly translate EAGLE-3's speedup into cost savings. On LLaMA-Instruct 3.3 70B at T=0, EAGLE-3's 4.12× mean speedup (Table 1) means a batch job that costs $10,000 in GPU-hours with vanilla decoding would cost approximately $2,430 with EAGLE-3, assuming linear cost scaling with throughput. The SGLang throughput results (Table 3) show that substantial gains persist even at batch size 64 (38% improvement), meaning the savings apply in both latency-optimized small-batch and throughput-optimized large-batch configurations. For cloud LLM providers, a 38% throughput improvement at serving-scale batch sizes directly reduces the number of GPUs needed to serve a given query volume, with proportional reductions in hardware, energy, and operational costs.

**On-device or edge deployment with draft-model-only generation.** While EAGLE-3 is designed for lossless acceleration (the target model verifies drafts and ensures distributional equivalence), its draft model's high acceptance rates (n-α remains ~0.79 even at n=7, Figure 7) raise an interesting possibility: for latency-critical applications where occasional token-level deviations are tolerable, the draft model could be used standalone without target model verification. The draft model is a single decoder layer plus the target model's frozen LM head, making it extremely lightweight—potentially suitable for on-device deployment where the full target model won't fit. The per-step cost is one decoder layer forward pass (~1/32 of an 8B model's cost, assuming the target model has 32 layers) rather than the full model. If the draft model's acceptance rate of ~0.79 means ~79% of tokens match what the target model would have produced, this may be acceptable for applications like autocomplete suggestions, real-time transcription assistants, or gaming NPC dialogue where perfect fidelity is unnecessary. The paper doesn't evaluate this use case directly, but the acceptance rate data and the draft model's architectural simplicity make it a natural exploration for practitioners.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions EAGLE-3 as a direct upgrade over EAGLE-2 for all tested configurations—no scenario in Table 1 shows EAGLE-2 outperforming EAGLE-3, and the improvement holds across model scales (8B to 70B), temperatures (0 and 1), tasks (conversation, code, math, summarization, instruction following), and model types (chat and reasoning). The scaling curve in Figure 1 shows EAGLE-3 is strictly better than EAGLE-2 at every data scale tested, with the gap widening as data increases. The throughput experiments (Tables 3, 5) show EAGLE-3 remains beneficial at batch sizes where EAGLE becomes detrimental. The paper does not identify any regime where EAGLE-2 is preferable to EAGLE-3.

The relevant tradeoff is therefore **EAGLE-3 vs. non-adoption** (staying with vanilla autoregressive decoding), and the paper's results support these decision rules:

- **Adopt EAGLE-3 when:** inference latency or throughput is a binding constraint, the target model is a decoder-only transformer in the 8B–70B+ parameter range, training resources are available to generate on-policy training data from the target model and train a single-decoder-layer draft model, and the deployment framework supports speculative sampling with tree attention (the paper demonstrates SGLang and vLLM integration). The expected benefit ranges from ~3.0× to ~6.5× speedup at batch size 1 depending on the task (Section 4.1, Table 1), and ~1.4× to ~1.8× throughput improvement at batch sizes up to 64 depending on the framework (Section 4.3, 4.4, Tables 3, 5).

- **Consider alternatives when:** the target model architecture is non-standard (MoE, encoder-decoder, state-space)—no evidence exists for EAGLE-3 on these architectures. The deployment is at very large batch sizes (>64) where throughput gains may diminish toward zero (the vLLM experiment at batch size 56 shows 1.01× improvement, Table 5). Training cost is the primary constraint and the inference volume is too low to amortize the draft model training investment—the paper does not quantify training cost, but it involves generating responses from the target model for ~500K prompts and training a draft model with multi-step simulation. Or if the deployment framework does not support speculative sampling with intermediate feature extraction and tree attention, requiring significant engineering investment to integrate.
