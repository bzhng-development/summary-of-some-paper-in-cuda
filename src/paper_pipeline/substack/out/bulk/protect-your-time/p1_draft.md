Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (Staff Engineer who originally built the compute efficiency workstream that drove his promo, writing on protecting time for career-growing projects):

**Understanding Impact as a Filter**

Before you can protect your time, you need a working framework for evaluating work against its true cost. Ryan weighs every task along two axes: **benefit** ("What is the business impact? What metrics would it move and how important are they?") and **cost** ("For most software engineering, the main cost is developer time—so the question is how long will this take to finish?"). He recommends leaning on your manager and tech lead to calibrate this sense early, discussing prioritization openly while you build the skill. The emotional hurdle—feeling bad about deprioritizing—diminishes once you internalize that your manager and company *want* you to have more impact, so protecting your time is "good for everyone." He's blunt about the ceiling: "You can't say no to everything," but explaining *why* work isn't impactful should buy back slack.

**The ~20% Commitment Strategy**

The core story is a specific Senior Engineer (IC5) maneuver that crystallizes the whole philosophy. His manager tapped him to lead a workstream inside a larger partnership—he was the best fit, couldn't refuse, and assessed it as "impactful but not a home run." He then went to his mentor (who happened to be the lead of the overall partnership) with a proposition: lead the workstream with the *least possible time investment*. The mentor agreed. Ryan consulted across all the sub-projects and kept the initiative healthy, but deliberately avoided time-intensive work like coding. This let him carry a formal leadership obligation on roughly **20% of his time**, freeing the other 80% for a far more impactful workstream—the compute efficiency effort that ultimately got him promoted to Staff (IC6).

Two principles are embedded in that story. First, **you can't always drop your regular duties, but you can manage your level of investment**—and the key is communicating clearly during planning so everyone's expectations are aligned on *how* you'll show up. Second, **impact genuinely matters above all else** in a healthy culture: his mentor had every incentive to want more of Ryan's time on the broader partnership but sided with the lower-investment model because the raw impact calculus made sense.

**The Seniority Gradient on Protecting Time**

As engineers advance, the need to protect time intensifies. Ryan observes that the most senior ICs he knows "are often hard to reach unless you're working on something that is a priority for them"—not out of meanness, but out of necessity. He cites a Distinguished Engineer (IC9) who literally negotiated his way *off* his team's oncall rotation. But there's a balance that same IC9 crossed: "he was also known as a jerk to some since he was too stingy with his time." Ryan's counterweight is explicit—helping other engineers *is* impactful, and that's one of the reasons he carves out time to write this newsletter weekly. The meta-lesson is that the protection playbook scales, but the social cost of over-protecting rises in parallel.
