# Layerwise Recurrent Router for Mixture-of-Experts

**ArXiv:** [2408.06793](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06793)

## 🎯 Pitch

Even massive 52B-parameter MoE models can be outperformed by dense models 8x smaller—because each layer’s router blindly assigns tokens without memory of past routing decisions. RMoE fixes this with a single shared GRU threaded across layers, costing almost nothing yet consistently boosting performance by making routing history-informed.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces the **Layerwise Recurrent Router for Mixture-of-Experts (RMoE)**, a new routing mechanism that integrates a lightweight Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) across consecutive transformer layers to establish dependencies between routing decisions, replacing the standard practice where routers in different MoE layers operate independently without sharing historical assignment information. The method is validated on language modeling benchmarks (Enwiki8 and WikiText-103) using decoder-only switch-transformer architectures, as well as on a scaled-up 0.91B-parameter model trained on 40B tokens and evaluated on standard lm-evaluation-harness tasks after supervised fine-tuning. RMoE achieves consistent improvements over a spectrum of router baselines—including standard linear routers, HyperMoE, SMoE-MLP, XMoE, and fixed random routers—while introducing negligible computational overhead (e.g., 49.07 vs. 48.87 seconds per training step and 48.69 vs. 48.00 GB memory at the 0.91B scale), and combining RMoE’s recurrent router with XMoE’s cosine-similarity routing further reduces WikiText-103 test perplexity from 32.93 to 32.47. The paper establishes that cross-layer routing coordination improves expert utilization, gate entropy balance, and expert diversity, but only when the recurrent gradient flow across layers is preserved—ablating the cross-layer hidden state propagation (RMoE-NP) degrades performance below the baseline SMoE on Enwiki8 (1.123 vs. 1.128 BPC), demonstrating that the benefits depend critically on both forward information sharing and backward gradient propagation through the GRU.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: MoE Models Are Parameter-Inefficient, and the Router Is the Suspected Bottleneck

The Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture is one of the primary strategies for scaling up language models without proportionally increasing computational cost. The idea is elegant: instead of activating all parameters for every input token, an MoE model uses a **router** to conditionally and sparsely assign each token to a small subset of "expert" feed-forward networks. This means a model can have, say, 52 billion total parameters while only activating 1.3 billion per token, keeping the per-token FLOPs close to a much smaller dense model.

However, the paper identifies a persistent problem that undermines this promise: **current pre-trained MoE models consistently underperform dense models of equivalent activated parameter count, an issue the authors call "parameter inefficiency."** They present several concrete examples from the literature to establish this is not an isolated observation but a pattern:

- Rajbhandari et al. (2022) showed that an MoE model with 52B total parameters (1.3B activated per token) performs similarly to a standard 6.7B dense model when trained on the same data — meaning roughly **half the activated parameters** in the dense model achieves equivalent performance.
- Komatsuzaki et al. (2023) demonstrated that upcycling a T5-base model (248M parameters) into an MoE counterpart (2B total parameters) by copying existing FFNs yields some improvement, but the resulting model still **lags behind a T5-large** with only 783M parameters — a dense model with less than half the total parameters.
- Dai et al. (2024), using fine-grained and shared expert techniques, found that a 16B MoE model performs comparably to a 7B dense model — again, the MoE model needs **more than double the activated parameters** to match the dense model.

This consistent pattern reveals a fundamental efficiency gap: the MoE architecture is currently not extracting value proportional to its parameter count. Something in the routing mechanism is preventing the model from effectively leveraging the extra capacity that the expert networks provide.

### Why the Router Is Suspected as the Bottleneck

In most MoE implementations, the router is remarkably simple — typically a single linear layer followed by a softmax and a top-k selection. Given a token's hidden state $x \in \mathbb{R}^h$ at layer $i$, the router computes a score distribution over $N$ experts as $g(x; G_i, k) = \text{Top-k}(\text{softmax}(x \times G_i))$, where $G_i \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times N}$ is the layer-specific routing weight matrix. This means the router has only $h \times N$ parameters per layer — for a hidden size of 352 with 16 experts, that's just 5,632 parameters deciding how to route every token.

Given this simplicity, several lines of evidence in the paper converge to suggest the router is indeed the limiting factor:

**Evidence 1: Routing collapses early in training.** Xue et al. (2024) found that routing decisions converge to token-ID-based patterns very quickly during the early phase of pre-training. This means the router essentially learns a shallow mapping of "token X always goes to experts A and B" rather than developing sophisticated, context-dependent routing based on the token's current hidden state representation. The token-expert combination space is "far from well-explored," leaving substantial potential gains untapped.

**Evidence 2: Trivial routers achieve competitive performance.** Perhaps the most damning evidence is that **non-learned routers perform similarly to learned ones**:
- **Hash-based routing** (Roller et al., 2021) assigns tokens to experts using deterministic hash functions with no learned parameters, yet achieves performance competitive with learned routers.
- **Stochastic routing policies** (Zuo et al., 2021) randomly assign tokens based on fixed distributions.
- **Fixed random routers** (Chen et al., 2023) use a randomly initialized and never-updated linear layer for routing throughout training.
- The paper's own experiments (Table 1) show that a fixed random router (`RandomSMoE`) achieves 1.135 test BPC on Enwiki8 — nearly identical to the learned linear router's 1.128 BPC, suggesting the learned router is contributing surprisingly little beyond random assignment.

This body of evidence points to a paradox: MoE models have massive capacity in their expert networks, but the mechanism for deciding *how to use that capacity* is severely underpowered. Increasing router capacity naively doesn't help — the paper shows that replacing the linear router with a two-layer MLP (`SMoE-MLP`) actually *degrades* performance on Enwiki8 (1.137 vs. 1.128 BPC), underperforming even the fixed random baseline. Something more fundamental about the routing architecture is missing.

### The Specific Gap: Routers Operate in Isolation Across Layers

The paper identifies a specific architectural deficiency that prior work had not systematically addressed: **routers in different MoE layers operate completely independently, making routing decisions without any awareness of what experts were selected in previous layers for the same token.**

In a standard transformer with MoE layers, a token passes through a sequence of identical MoE blocks, each containing its own router. While there is a residual connection that carries the token's hidden state from one layer to the next, the routing information — *which* experts were chosen at layer $i$, what gating scores were assigned, whether certain expert combinations tend to work well together or fail — is **implicitly mixed into the hidden state alongside all other linguistic information**. As the authors put it:

> "vanilla MoE models could technically share the routing information via hidden states residual, this information may be overshadowed by the language modelling loss, requiring routing-relevant information to 'compete' for its representation"

In other words, the hidden state after layer $i$ encodes everything: semantic content, syntactic structure, positional information, and — buried somewhere in the thousands of dimensions — implicit traces of which experts processed this token. The router at layer $i+1$ must attempt to extract routing-relevant signals from this undifferentiated representation, competing with the dominant signal from the language modeling objective.

This isolation creates several problems:

1. **No explicit coordination**: If experts at layer 3 specialize in mathematical operations and experts at layer 5 specialize in numerical reasoning, a router at layer 5 has no direct way to know that the token was routed to a math specialist two layers earlier, and therefore might benefit from routing to a numerically-oriented expert now. This coordination, if it happens at all, is accidental — a byproduct of the hidden state encoding that the language modeling loss happens to shape.

2. **No global optimization**: Each router optimizes its assignment locally based on the immediate LM loss contribution at its layer. There is no mechanism for a router at an early layer to receive feedback about whether its assignment helped or hindered routing decisions in later layers. The gradient signal from later layers is diluted through the expert FFN, residual connection, and attention mechanism before it reaches earlier routers.

3. **Inefficient expert utilization**: Without cross-layer coordination, the model cannot learn optimal multi-layer expert "pathways." For example, it might be beneficial to route all tokens belonging to a math problem through a consistent set of math-specialized experts across layers 2-6, but there's no architectural mechanism to encourage or even discover such patterns.

### Why This Matters: Practical and Theoretical Significance

The gap matters for both practical and theoretical reasons:

**Practical impact — resource efficiency at scale.** The allure of MoE is that it enables scaling model capacity to hundreds of billions or trillions of parameters while keeping training and inference costs manageable. But if a 52B MoE model only performs as well as a 6.7B dense model, those extra parameters are wasted — consuming memory, communication bandwidth in distributed training, and engineering complexity without delivering proportional capability. Closing this efficiency gap through better routing could either (a) make existing MoE models substantially more capable at the same parameter count, or (b) enable achieving the same performance with fewer parameters, reducing deployment costs. Either outcome is significant for the economics of large-scale model deployment.

**Theoretical significance — the architecture-search analogy.** The paper draws an implicit parallel between MoE routing and neural architecture search (NAS). In NAS, a controller network predicts the optimal architecture configuration (filter sizes, number of layers, etc.) for each layer of a model. The RMoE approach can be viewed as a *dynamic, token-level* NAS where the GRU controller selects which experts to activate for each token at each layer, conditioned on the routing history. Prior NAS work (Zoph & Le, 2016; Pham et al., 2018) used recurrent controllers for architecture decisions precisely because layer-wise decisions are interdependent — you would not independently decide the filter size of layer 5 without knowing what filter size was used at layer 4. MoE routing has the same structural property: the optimal experts for a token at layer $i$ depend on which experts processed it at layers $1 \ldots i-1$. The standard isolated-router design ignores this dependency entirely.

**Scaling trajectory implications.** If routing quality is the bottleneck, then simply scaling up the number of experts or model depth without improving routing will yield diminishing returns. Each new expert or layer adds capacity, but without better coordination, the router cannot effectively utilize it. The paper's findings on gate entropy (Figure 4) support this: standard routers converge to sharp, nearly one-hot gating distributions early in training, which means adding more experts provides little benefit because the router effectively ignores most of them. Addressing the routing coordination problem could therefore change the scaling behavior of MoE models — making deeper or wider MoE architectures more efficient than they currently are.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper reviews a spectrum of prior attempts to improve MoE routing, showing that each addresses some symptoms but none addresses the core isolation problem:

**XMoE (Chi et al., 2022)** addresses *representation collapse* — the tendency for token embeddings to cluster around expert embeddings, causing the router to lose discrimination ability. XMoE projects hidden states to a lower dimension and uses cosine similarity instead of dot products, which normalizes the input and weight vectors. This improves routing by preventing the hidden state from simply aligning with the dominant expert embedding, but **each layer's router still operates independently**. The representation collapse is treated as a per-layer problem, not an inter-layer coordination problem.

**HyperMoE (Do et al., 2023)** introduces a fixed, randomly initialized hypernetwork at each layer to generate the router's linear weights conditioned on the input and a learnable router embedding. This increases router expressiveness without adding many trainable parameters, but the hypernetwork is **layer-local** — it generates weights for layer $i$ based on layer $i$'s hidden state and layer $i$'s embedding, with no information from other layers.

**SMoE-MLP (Shen et al., 2023)** simply replaces the single linear layer with a two-layer MLP using GELU activation, naively increasing router capacity. The paper's experiments show this **does not help** — on Enwiki8, SMoE-MLP performs worse than the standard linear router (1.137 vs. 1.128 BPC) and worse than the fixed random baseline (1.135 BPC). This demonstrates that the problem is not simply insufficient parameters in the router — it's the lack of the *right kind* of information, namely cross-layer routing history.

**SMoE-Dropout (Chen et al., 2023)** uses a fixed randomly initialized linear router throughout training, combined with a gradually increasing top-k. This encourages exploration by preventing the router from converging to a narrow routing policy, but it achieves this by **essentially abandoning learned routing altogether** — it's an admission that the learned router, as currently designed, doesn't work well enough to justify its training.

**CosineSMoE** (similar to XMoE but without down-projection) normalizes both the hidden states and expert embeddings, computing gating scores via cosine similarity. This helps prevent the score magnitudes from being dominated by a few experts, but again operates **independently per layer**.

All of these approaches share a common blind spot: they try to improve *how* each router makes its decision, but none address the fact that each router makes its decision **in isolation**, without knowledge of what decisions were made upstream. This is the specific gap that RMoE fills.

### How RMoE Positions Itself

The paper positions RMoE not as a replacement for existing router improvements, but as an **orthogonal computation stage that can be composed with any of them**. The key design principle is:

- **Existing methods** improve the *mapping from hidden state to expert scores* at each layer (better similarity functions, higher capacity, better initialization).
- **RMoE** improves the *input to that mapping* — instead of using only the local hidden state $x_i$, the router now also receives a recurrent hidden state $h_i$ that explicitly encodes the routing history from previous layers.

This is why the paper repeatedly emphasizes that RMoE is "orthogonal to most existing methods and is seamlessly compatible with them." Table 2 demonstrates this concretely: combining RMoE's GRU router with XMoE's cosine-similarity routing yields better performance than XMoE alone across all tested configurations (lower dimension 8, 16, and 32), with the largest improvement at XMoE(16) where WikiText-103 test PPL drops from 32.93 to 32.47.

The paper's position, then, is that **routing decisions possess a sequential structure that current architectures ignore**, and that modeling this structure — even with a very lightweight recurrent mechanism — unlocks gains that more sophisticated per-layer routers cannot achieve alone. The GRU is not just adding capacity; it's adding the right *kind* of information: explicit, dedicated routing history that doesn't need to compete with language modeling signals in the residual stream.

This framing — routing as a cross-layer sequential decision problem rather than a set of independent per-layer decisions — is the paper's core conceptual contribution. It explains why RMoE succeeds where naive capacity increases fail, why the recurrent gradient is essential (removing it degrades performance below the baseline), and why the approach composes naturally with any per-layer router design.

## 3. Technical Approach

This is primarily a **architectural innovation paper** whose core idea is that routing decisions across consecutive MoE layers have a sequential dependency structure that can be explicitly modeled with a lightweight recurrent network, and that doing so improves routing quality beyond what is possible with isolated per-layer routers.

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

What is being built: a modified Mixture-of-Experts transformer where, instead of each layer independently deciding which experts to activate for each token, a small Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) passes routing-relevant information forward from one layer to the next, so that the router at layer $i$ knows which experts handled this token in layers $1 \ldots i-1$ and can coordinate its assignment accordingly.

The problem it solves: standard MoE routers work in isolation — the router at layer 5 has no explicit knowledge that the token was routed to a math-specialized expert at layer 3, so it cannot learn to follow up with a numerically-oriented expert at layer 5. The "shape" of the solution is to add a dedicated information channel (the GRU hidden state) that runs parallel to the residual stream and carries *only* routing-relevant signals, disentangled from the language modeling representation, so that routing history does not need to compete for space in the general-purpose hidden state.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The RMoE architecture modifies each MoE transformer layer with three new components inserted between the standard hidden state and the router:

1. **Layer-specific projectors (`Proj_i`)** — a distinct linear layer at each MoE layer that compresses the high-dimensional hidden state $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^h$ (e.g., $h = 352$ or $h = 1280$) down to a much smaller recurrent input $\mathbf{x}'_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ (typically $p = 128$). There is one projector per layer (not shared) because the norms and distributions of hidden states vary significantly across layers (visualized in Figure 11 of the appendix).

2. **Cross-layer shared GRU** — a single Gated Recurrent Unit whose weights are shared across all layers. It takes the projected vector $\mathbf{x}'_i$ and the GRU hidden state from the previous layer $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$ as input, and produces a new hidden state $\mathbf{h}_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$. This GRU explicitly models the sequential dependency: $\mathbf{h}_i$ encodes both the current layer's local hidden state information and the routing history from all previous layers.

3. **Standard per-layer router** — the original linear router (or any improved router variant such as XMoE's cosine-similarity router) now takes $\mathbf{h}_i$ as input instead of the raw hidden state $\mathbf{x}_i$. It computes gating scores, applies top-k selection, and the selected experts process $\mathbf{x}_i$ as usual.

The information flow for a single token at layer $i$ proceeds: $\mathbf{x}_i \rightarrow \text{Proj}_i \rightarrow \mathbf{x}'_i \rightarrow \text{GRU}(\mathbf{x}'_i, \mathbf{h}_{i-1}) \rightarrow \mathbf{h}_i \rightarrow \text{Router}(\mathbf{h}_i) \rightarrow \text{Expert selection} \rightarrow \text{FFN computation}$. The GRU hidden state $\mathbf{h}_i$ is then passed to layer $i+1$, establishing a recurrent chain that spans all MoE layers for that token's forward pass. Crucially, the GRU is **shared across layers** — the same update gate, reset gate, and candidate activation parameters apply at every layer — while the projectors are **layer-specific** to accommodate the varying hidden state statistics at different depths.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### Standard Sparse Mixture-of-Experts Formulation

Before explaining what RMoE adds, it is necessary to understand precisely what it modifies. In a standard Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (SMoE) transformer layer, the feed-forward network is replaced by a group of $N$ parallel expert FFNs (typically $N = 16$ in this paper's experiments) and a router $g(\cdot; \mathbf{G}, k)$. The router is parameterized by a weight matrix $\mathbf{G} \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times N}$ (where $h$ is the hidden dimension and $N$ is the number of experts) and an integer $k$ specifying how many experts to activate per token. For a given input token with hidden state $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^h$, the layer's output $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^h$ is computed as:

$$\mathbf{y} = \sum_{n \in \mathcal{S}} g_n(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{G}, k) \cdot E_n(\mathbf{x})$$

where $\mathcal{S}$ is the set of selected expert indices (of size $k$), $g_n(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{G}, k) \in [0, 1]$ is the gating score assigned to expert $E_n$, and $E_n(\mathbf{x})$ is the output of the $n$-th expert FFN applied to $\mathbf{x}$.

**What it computes:** the layer output is a weighted sum of the outputs from the $k$ selected experts, where each expert $E_n$ independently processes the full input $\mathbf{x}$ through its own feed-forward parameters, and the gating scores $g_n$ determine how much each expert's output contributes to the final representation. The unselected experts ($n \notin \mathcal{S}$) contribute zero and are not computed, saving FLOPs.

**How the router produces gating scores:** the standard router first computes raw logits as $\mathbf{x} \times \mathbf{G} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ (a simple linear projection where the $n$-th column of $\mathbf{G}$ can be interpreted as the "expert embedding" for expert $E_n$), then applies softmax to produce a probability distribution over all $N$ experts, and finally applies a top-$k$ function that sets all but the $k$ largest scores to zero and renormalizes. Formally:

$$g_n(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{G}, k) = \begin{cases} \frac{\exp(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{G}_{:,n})}{\sum_{m \in \mathcal{S}} \exp(\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{G}_{:,m})} & \text{if } n \in \mathcal{S} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where $\mathcal{S} = \text{TopK}(\text{softmax}(\mathbf{x} \times \mathbf{G}), k)$ selects the indices of the $k$ largest post-softmax scores.

**Why this form:** the linear projection followed by softmax and top-k is the standard design because it is computationally cheap (a single matrix multiplication per token per layer), trivially parallelizable across tokens via batched matrix operations, and the softmax ensures the gating scores sum to one for the selected experts, providing a convex combination that preserves the scale of the transformer's residual stream. The top-k sparsity ($k \ll N$, typically $k = 2$ or $k = 4$ out of $N = 16$) is what makes MoE computationally efficient: only $k$ experts are activated per token, keeping the per-token FLOPs proportional to $k \times \text{FFN size}$ rather than $N \times \text{FFN size}$.

#### Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) Mechanics

The GRU is the specific recurrent cell that RMoE uses to model cross-layer routing dependencies. Understanding its internal gating behavior is essential because the paper's ablation analyses (Section 5) hinge on what happens when certain information pathways through the GRU are disabled. Given an input vector $\mathbf{x}'_i \in \mathbb{R}^p$ at step $i$ and the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{i-1} \in \mathbb{R}^p$, the GRU first computes two gate vectors:

$$\mathbf{s}_i = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_s \mathbf{x}'_i + \mathbf{U}_s \mathbf{h}_{i-1})$$
$$\mathbf{z}_i = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_z \mathbf{x}'_i + \mathbf{U}_z \mathbf{h}_{i-1})$$

where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid activation function (outputting values in $(0, 1)$), $\mathbf{W}_s, \mathbf{W}_z \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ are weight matrices that transform the current input, $\mathbf{U}_s, \mathbf{U}_z \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ are weight matrices that transform the previous hidden state, and all four weight matrices are learnable parameters shared across time steps (i.e., across layers in RMoE).

**What the reset gate $\mathbf{s}_i$ computes:** each element of $\mathbf{s}_i$ is a value between 0 and 1 that controls how much of the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$ to forget when computing the candidate hidden state. When an element of $\mathbf{s}_i$ is near 0, the corresponding element of $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$ is effectively zeroed out, allowing the model to discard irrelevant routing history. When near 1, the full history is retained for computing the candidate.

**What the update gate $\mathbf{z}_i$ computes:** each element of $\mathbf{z}_i$ is a value between 0 and 1 that controls the interpolation between the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$ and the candidate hidden state $\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i$. When an element of $\mathbf{z}_i$ is near 1, the hidden state mostly copies forward from the previous step (preserving routing history unchanged). When near 0, the hidden state is mostly replaced by the new candidate (overwriting history with current-layer information).

The candidate hidden state is then computed as:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_h \mathbf{x}'_i + \mathbf{s}_i \odot (\mathbf{U}_h \mathbf{h}_{i-1}))$$

where $\mathbf{W}_h, \mathbf{U}_h \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$ are additional learnable weight matrices and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. The $\tanh$ activation squashes the candidate to $(-1, 1)$.

**What the candidate computes:** it is a proposed new hidden state that combines the current input $\mathbf{x}'_i$ with a gated version of the previous hidden state. The reset gate $\mathbf{s}_i$ selectively erases parts of the history before this combination, allowing the candidate to ignore irrelevant past routing decisions while incorporating relevant ones.

Finally, the new hidden state is computed as an element-wise convex combination of the old state and the candidate:

$$\mathbf{h}_i = (1 - \mathbf{z}_i) \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_i + \mathbf{z}_i \odot \mathbf{h}_{i-1}$$

**What this computes:** for each dimension independently, the output is a weighted average of the candidate (new information) and the previous state (preserved information), with the update gate $\mathbf{z}_i$ determining the weight. This is the key mechanism that allows the GRU to maintain long-term routing memory: if $\mathbf{z}_i$ is close to 1, the hidden state can propagate unchanged through many layers, carrying routing decisions from early layers to late layers without degradation.

**Why GRU over alternatives:** The authors chose GRU over a vanilla RNN and LSTM based on empirical comparison. Table 6 shows that on Enwiki8, a GRU router achieves 1.116 test BPC vs. 1.119 for an RNN router and 1.122 for an LSTM router. On WikiText-103, the GRU achieves 32.86 test PPL vs. 32.72 for RNN and 33.04 for LSTM. The GRU provides the best balance: it has more expressive gating than a vanilla RNN (which has no learned mechanism to decide when to forget or update), but fewer parameters than an LSTM (which has a separate cell state and three gates), making it more parameter-efficient for the small hidden dimension ($p = 128$) used in RMoE. Additionally, the GRU's formulation with only two gates (reset and update) and no separate memory cell means the hidden state $\mathbf{h}_i$ is the only state vector that needs to be passed between layers, keeping the recurrent interface simple.

#### Complete RMoE Layer Computation

With the standard MoE and GRU defined, the full RMoE computation at layer $i$ proceeds through four stages that transform the standard hidden state into an expert-routed output while explicitly modeling cross-layer routing dependencies.

**Stage 1: Layer-specific projection.** The first operation in the RMoE pipeline is dimensionality reduction of the standard hidden state. At each MoE layer $i$, the hidden state $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^h$ (e.g., $h = 352$ for the medium models or $h = 1280$ for the 0.91B model) is projected down to a smaller recurrent dimension $p$ (typically $p = 128$) through a layer-specific linear projection:

$$\mathbf{x}'_i = \text{Proj}_i(\mathbf{x}_i)$$

where $\text{Proj}_i: \mathbb{R}^h \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^p$ is a distinct learned linear transformation (no activation function) for each layer $i$. For the medium model with $h = 352$ and $p = 128$, each projector has $352 \times 128 = 45,056$ parameters, and across 8 layers this adds approximately $8 \times 45,056 = 360,448$ parameters to the router system.

**Why separate projectors per layer:** the authors explicitly justify this design choice by noting that "hidden states and model weights in different layers usually vary a lot" (Section 3.2), citing Figure 11 in the appendix which shows that both the norm and standard deviation of router weights differ substantially across layers. In a standard RNN, where all time steps share the same input distribution, a single shared input projector suffices. But in a transformer, layers 1, 8, and 24 process very different levels of abstraction (token-level syntax vs. high-level semantics), producing hidden states with different statistical properties. A single shared projector would need to handle this entire range simultaneously — the authors' ablation in Table 6 confirms this: using the standard RNN approach with a shared projector ("SMoE + S-proj + GRU router") yields 1.123 test BPC on Enwiki8 versus 1.116 for the layerwise projector approach ("SMoE + L-proj + GRU router"). This design choice aligns with the established practice in MoE architectures of not sharing LayerNorm parameters even when transformer blocks are shared across layers (Xue et al., 2022) — the statistics differ enough across depths that layer-specific normalization and projection is beneficial.

**What the projection accomplishes:** it extracts a compact summary of the current layer's hidden state that is specifically shaped for the recurrent routing computation. The reduction from $h = 352$ down to $p = 128$ (a factor of roughly 2.75× compression) filters out information irrelevant to routing decisions while preserving the features that distinguish which experts are appropriate. The projector also serves as an implicit normalization step, since the raw hidden state $\mathbf{x}_i$ can have widely varying norms across layers, and the learned projection can compensate for this variation before the recurrent computation.

**Stage 2: GRU-based cross-layer recurrence.** The projected vector $\mathbf{x}'_i$ and the GRU hidden state from the previous layer $\mathbf{h}_{i-1} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ are fed into the shared GRU to produce the current layer's routing state:

$$\mathbf{h}_i = \text{GRU}(\mathbf{x}'_i, \mathbf{h}_{i-1})$$

where the GRU computation follows exactly the four equations described above (reset gate, update gate, candidate, final state), and the GRU's internal weight matrices $\mathbf{W}_s, \mathbf{W}_z, \mathbf{W}_h, \mathbf{U}_s, \mathbf{U}_z, \mathbf{U}_h$ are shared across all MoE layers. For the first MoE layer ($i = 0$), the previous hidden state $\mathbf{h}_{-1}$ is initialized to the zero vector (or equivalently, a learned initial state), providing a clean starting point with no routing history.

**Why share the GRU across layers:** sharing the GRU weights serves two purposes. First, it is parameter-efficient — for $p = 128$, the GRU has $3 \times (p \times p + p \times p) = 6 \times 128^2 = 98,304$ parameters in its weight matrices, which is modest and does not scale with the number of layers. If each layer had its own GRU, an 8-layer model would add $8 \times 98,304 \approx 786,000$ parameters for the recurrent component alone, and a 24-layer model would add over 2.3 million. Second, weight sharing forces the GRU to learn a general mechanism for combining current layer information with routing history that is independent of depth — the same gating logic applies whether the current layer is layer 2 (early, mostly syntactic) or layer 22 (late, mostly semantic). This encourages the GRU to extract depth-invariant routing patterns, such as "if the token was routed to expert 3 at the previous layer, it tends to benefit from expert 7 at the current layer," regardless of which specific layers are involved.

**What $\mathbf{h}_i$ encodes:** the GRU output is a $p$-dimensional vector that summarizes all routing-relevant information from layers $1$ through $i$. Through the reset and update gates, the GRU can selectively retain routing decisions from early layers (if the update gate stays near 1) or overwrite them with new information (if the update gate drops near 0). For example, if a token was routed to a math-specialized expert at layer 3 and then to a logic-specialized expert at layer 4, the GRU state at layer 5 might encode that this token is engaged in mathematical reasoning, helping the router at layer 5 select an arithmetic expert. The gating mechanism allows the GRU to "forget" routing history that is no longer relevant — for instance, routing decisions from the embedding-adjacent layers may become irrelevant for deep semantic layers, and the reset gate can flush them.

**Stage 3: Modified router input.** The critical architectural change is that the router no longer takes the raw hidden state $\mathbf{x}_i$ as input. Instead, it takes the GRU output $\mathbf{h}_i$:

$$\mathbf{y}_i = \sum_{n \in \mathcal{S}} g_n(\mathbf{h}_i; \mathbf{G}_i, k) \cdot E_n(\mathbf{x}_i)$$

where $g_n(\mathbf{h}_i; \mathbf{G}_i, k)$ is the gating score for expert $n$ computed from $\mathbf{h}_i$ using the layer-specific router parameters $\mathbf{G}_i$. The router parameters $\mathbf{G}_i$ now have dimension $\mathbf{G}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times N}$ (e.g., $128 \times 16 = 2,048$ parameters per layer) rather than the original $\mathbf{G}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times N}$ ($352 \times 16 = 5,632$ parameters per layer in the medium model). This means the router itself becomes slightly more parameter-efficient because it operates in the lower-dimensional GRU space.

**Why the router uses $\mathbf{h}_i$ instead of $\mathbf{x}_i$:** this is the central design decision of RMoE. The GRU hidden state $\mathbf{h}_i$ is a dedicated routing representation that explicitly encodes cross-layer dependency information, disentangled from the language modeling representations in the residual stream. If the router continued to use $\mathbf{x}_i$, the GRU computation would be pointless — there would be no channel for the recurrent state to influence routing decisions. By routing from $\mathbf{h}_i$, the router sees both the current layer's compressed representation (via $\mathbf{x}'_i$ to the GRU) and the entire routing history (via $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$), integrated through learned gating. The expert networks $E_n$ still process the full hidden state $\mathbf{x}_i$, so the experts have access to the complete, uncompressed representation for their actual computation — the compression only affects the routing decision, not the expert computation itself.

**Stage 4: Standard expert computation and aggregation.** After the router selects which $k$ experts to activate using $\mathbf{h}_i$, the computation proceeds exactly as in standard MoE: each selected expert $E_n$ independently processes the full hidden state $\mathbf{x}_i$ through its feed-forward parameters, and the outputs are weighted by the gating scores and summed. The load balancing loss, which encourages uniform expert utilization across tokens in a batch, is applied to the router's outputs $g_n(\mathbf{h}_i; \mathbf{G}_i, k)$ exactly as in standard SMoE training. For the large-scale experiments, the paper uses an auxiliary load balancing loss with weight 0.01, following standard practice.

#### Computational Cost and Efficiency

The paper is explicit about RMoE's computational overhead at both small and large scales. For the 8-layer medium model on Enwiki8/WikiText-103 (Table 1), the numbers are:

- **Parameters:** RMoE has 36.51M non-embedding parameters, of which 0.47M are router parameters (the GRU + projectors). The baseline SMoE has 36.08M parameters, of which 0.04M are router parameters. RMoE adds approximately 0.43M parameters (about 1.2% increase) to the router system.

- **Memory:** RMoE uses 49.46 GB peak GPU memory vs. 47.92 GB for SMoE with the same batch size — an increase of roughly 1.54 GB (3.2%), primarily from storing the GRU hidden states and projector parameters.

- **Speed (wall time):** RMoE requires 972.9 seconds per 1,000 training steps vs. 960.2 seconds for SMoE — a slowdown of approximately 1.3%.

For the large-scale 0.91B model (24 layers, 40B tokens, Table 3), the overhead is similarly negligible:

- **Speed:** 49.07 seconds per training step for RMoE vs. 48.87 for SMoE (0.4% slowdown).

- **Memory:** 48.69 GB for RMoE vs. 48.00 GB for SMoE (1.4% increase).

- **Additional parameters:** approximately 3.5M parameters added to the router system, which is negligible compared to the model's 0.91B total parameters (roughly 0.38% increase).

**Why the overhead is so small:** three factors contribute to RMoE's efficiency. First, the GRU operates in a heavily reduced dimension ($p = 128$ vs. the model's hidden dimension of $1280$ in the large-scale setting), so its matrix multiplications are cheap. Second, there is no sequence-level recurrence — the GRU steps across layers for each token independently, meaning all tokens in a batch can be processed in parallel through the recurrent computation. Third, the layerwise recurrence is a simple sequential operation across the model depth, which adds a small constant overhead to each MoE layer's forward pass but does not require any cross-token communication or additional attention operations.

#### Compatibility with Existing Router Designs

A key architectural principle of RMoE is that it is **orthogonal to most existing MoE improvements** because it introduces a new computation stage *before* the router, rather than modifying the router's internal computation. The paper explicitly states:

> "the proposed method introduces an additional computation stage into SMoE, it is orthogonal to most existing attempts to improve MoE and is seamlessly compatible with them."

This means RMoE can be combined with any per-layer router design by simply feeding the GRU output $\mathbf{h}_i$ into that router instead of feeding the raw hidden state. The paper demonstrates this concretely in Table 2 by combining RMoE with XMoE (which uses cosine similarity in a lower-dimensional space):

- XMoE(16) alone achieves 32.93 test PPL on WikiText-103; XMoE(16) + GRU router achieves 32.47.
- XMoE(8) alone achieves 33.55 test PPL; XMoE(8) + GRU router achieves 32.99.
- XMoE(32) alone achieves 32.71; XMoE(32) + GRU router achieves 32.55.

The improvement is consistent across all configurations, with the largest gain at the smallest down-projection dimension (XMoE(8) improves by 0.56 PPL), suggesting that the GRU provides the most benefit when the per-layer router has the least capacity on its own — the cross-layer information compensates for a weaker local router.

**Why this composability is architecturally significant:** most prior router improvements (XMoE, HyperMoE, SMoE-MLP) modify the function $f: \mathbb{R}^h \rightarrow [0, 1]^N$ that maps hidden states to gating scores. RMoE modifies the *input* to that function — instead of $f(\mathbf{x}_i)$, it computes $f(\mathbf{h}_i)$ where $\mathbf{h}_i = \text{GRU}(\text{Proj}_i(\mathbf{x}_i), \mathbf{h}_{i-1})$. Since these modifications are at different stages of the computation pipeline, they compose naturally: any router $f$ can be used with RMoE's GRU-based input preparation. This is not a trivial property — it would not hold if RMoE modified the expert computation or the gating score aggregation, which would conflict with methods that also modify those stages. The paper positions RMoE as a general architectural primitive that "can be flexibly integrated" with future router innovations, not as a replacement for existing ones.

#### Parameter Accounting and Initialization

The paper provides specific details about the router parameters in Table 1 ("Params" column, with router parameters shown in parentheses). For the medium-scale experiments:

- **SMoE:** 36.08M total non-embedding parameters, of which only 0.04M (40,000) are router parameters — the $h \times N = 352 \times 16 = 5,632$ linear router weights per layer, times 8 layers, equals approximately 45,056 router parameters.
- **RMoE:** 36.51M total, of which 0.47M (470,000) are router parameters — including 8 layer-specific projectors ($8 \times 352 \times 128 = 360,448$ parameters), the shared GRU weights ($6 \times 128^2 = 98,304$), and the 8 per-layer routers operating on $\mathbf{h}_i$ ($8 \times 128 \times 16 = 16,384$ parameters).

For the large-scale experiments (0.91B model), RMoE adds approximately 3.5M router parameters, which includes 24 layer-specific projectors ($24 \times 1280 \times 128 = 3,932,160$ parameters), the shared GRU (still $6 \times 128^2 = 98,304$ parameters), and 24 routers operating in the reduced dimension ($24 \times 128 \times 16 = 49,152$ parameters). Note that the projectors dominate the parameter count at scale because they involve the product of the full hidden dimension $h$ and the GRU dimension $p$ — a necessary cost for dimensionality reduction, but one that grows linearly with the number of layers.

**Design choice — shared GRU, layer-specific projectors:** the asymmetry between shared GRU and per-layer projectors is deliberate. The projectors must be per-layer because they map from very different input distributions (as shown in Figure 11, the norm and standard deviation of hidden states vary significantly across layers). The GRU can be shared because its internal state dimension $p$ is small and its gating mechanism is designed to handle varying input statistics — the reset and update gates adaptively control how much of the new input to incorporate, providing a form of input normalization implicitly. This design echoes the principle from Xue et al. (2022) that normalization parameters should typically be per-layer even when other components are shared, because the statistics of activations change systematically with depth.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing MoE Routing as a Cross-Layer Sequential Decision Problem

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is a reframing of what the routing problem *is*. Before RMoE, the field implicitly treated MoE routing as a set of independent per-layer classification problems: at each layer, given a hidden state, pick the best $k$ experts. All prior router improvements — XMoE's cosine similarity, HyperMoE's hypernetwork-generated weights, SMoE-MLP's increased capacity — operate entirely within this per-layer framing. They ask "how can I make a better decision given the hidden state at this layer?"

RMoE changes the question to: **"how should I route this token at layer $i$, given that I already routed it through experts A, B, and C at layers $1 \ldots i-1$?"** This is a fundamentally different problem class — it's sequential decision-making under uncertainty, not independent classification. The paper explicitly analogizes this to the architecture-search literature (Zoph & Le, 2016; Pham et al., 2018), where recurrent controllers predict layer configurations because "layer-wise decisions are interdependent." But while NAS uses recurrence to design a *static* architecture that applies uniformly to all inputs, RMoE uses recurrence to make *per-token, per-layer* routing decisions conditioned on routing history — a dynamic, token-level instantiation of the same principle.

This reframing matters beyond the specific GRU implementation because it changes the diagnostic lens through which routing failures are understood. When SMoE-MLP underperforms a fixed random router (Table 1: 1.137 vs. 1.135 BPC), the standard per-layer framing can only conclude that "more router capacity doesn't help," leading to the nihilistic conclusion that learned routing is near-useless (as Chen et al., 2023 essentially concluded by proposing fixed-random routers). The cross-layer framing suggests a different interpretation: the router at layer $i$ **doesn't have the right information to make a good decision**, regardless of its capacity. Adding more parameters to answer the wrong question (which expert, ignoring history?) is wasted. Adding the right *kind* of information — routing history — transforms the task into something the router can actually learn. This is a shift from "the router needs to be smarter" to "the router needs to be better-informed," and it explains why the GRU's recurrent gradient matters more than the GRU's parameter count (the `RMoE + NP` ablation in Table 4 drops from 1.116 to 1.123 BPC despite having identical parameters and FLOPs).

This reframing is **fundamental** rather than incremental: it changes the problem definition, not just the solution technique. It implies that future router designs should be evaluated not only on their per-layer mapping quality but on their ability to exploit cross-layer dependencies — a dimension that prior benchmarks and ablations simply did not measure.

### Innovation 2: Disentangling Routing State from Language Modeling State Through a Dedicated Recurrent Channel

The paper identifies and solves a representation-competition problem that prior work had not explicitly articulated. In standard MoE, the residual stream carries *all* information: semantics, syntax, position, *and* implicit traces of routing history. The router at layer $i+1$ must extract routing-relevant signals from this undifferentiated representation, competing against the dominant signals shaped by the language modeling loss. As the paper puts it: "routing-relevant information [must] 'compete' for its representation" (Section 1).

RMoE's architectural response is to create a **separate, dedicated channel** for routing information — the GRU hidden state $\mathbf{h}_i$ — that runs parallel to the residual stream and carries *only* routing-relevant signals. This is not just a capacity increase; it's a representational disentanglement. The projector $\text{Proj}_i$ extracts a compact routing summary from $\mathbf{x}_i$, and the GRU integrates this with historical routing state, but the expert FFNs still process the full, uncompressed $\mathbf{x}_i$. The routing computation and the content computation are architecturally separated.

Evidence that this disentanglement is real, not just architectural aspiration, comes from the mutual information analysis in Figure 3. Standard SMoE (Figure 3a) shows low cross-layer mutual information — routers at different layers make statistically independent decisions, even though the hidden states (which carry residual information) flow between them. This confirms the paper's claim that "vanilla MoE models could technically share the routing information via hidden states residual" but fail to do so effectively in practice. The routing information is *present* in the hidden state but *not extractable* by a simple linear router. RMoE (Figure 3d) shows dramatically higher cross-layer MI, with strong dependencies between neighbors and moderate dependencies even between distant layers (layer 0 to layer 7). Critically, `RMoE-NP` (Figure 3e), which has the same architecture and parameters as RMoE but strips out the cross-layer hidden state propagation (replacing $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$ with $\mathbf{h}_0$), shows MI values closer to the baselines. The shared GRU alone provides some inter-layer coordination (explaining why `RMoE-NP`'s MI is slightly higher than SMoE's), but the full layerwise recurrence is necessary for the strong, long-range dependencies observed in RMoE.

This innovation is **fundamentally an architectural insight** about the value of dedicated state channels for different learning objectives. It connects to broader principles in neural architecture design — the success of separate key/value/query projections in attention, the use of dedicated memory in Memory Networks — but applies them to the specific problem of routing coordination. The practical implication is that future MoE designs should consider explicit routing-state channels rather than relying on the residual stream to carry routing information implicitly.

### Innovation 3: The Recurrent Gradient as a Distinct and Essential Optimization Pathway

The paper's ablation analysis reveals something non-obvious: the GRU in RMoE provides value not only through forward information sharing (telling layer $i+1$ what layer $i$ decided) but through a **backward gradient pathway** that directly propagates routing-relevant training signal across layers. The authors name this the "Recurrent Gradient" and demonstrate that it is essential — removing it degrades performance *below* the standard SMoE baseline.

The evidence unfolds across three experiments in Table 5. When the forward information flow is preserved but the backward gradient is cut (`RMoE + detach h_{i-1}`, which detaches the previous GRU state from the computation graph so no gradient flows through it), performance drops to 1.133 test BPC — *worse* than the baseline SMoE at 1.128 BPC. When both forward and backward flows are removed (`RMoE + NP`), performance is 1.123 BPC. When forward routing logits are added back without their gradient (`RMoE + NP + r-1.0 + detach-r`), performance is 1.126 BPC — only slightly better than the baseline. But when the gradient is allowed to flow through those added logits (`RMoE + NP + r-1.0` without detaching), performance improves to 1.124 BPC.

The pattern is consistent: **any configuration that blocks gradient flow through the cross-layer routing pathway underperforms configurations that allow it**, even when forward information is preserved. This is not a standard finding — most recurrent architectures are justified primarily by their forward information-carrying capacity (the ability to model long-range dependencies). The paper shows that in the specific context of MoE routing, the backward pathway is *at least as important* as the forward one.

Why would this be? The standard SMoE router at layer $i$ receives gradient signal only from the immediate language modeling loss contribution at that layer, plus a small load balancing term. The signal about whether a routing decision at layer $i$ helped or hurt routing at layer $i+2$ is diluted through the expert FFN, the residual connection, and the self-attention mechanism before it reaches layer $i$'s router parameters. The recurrent gradient through the GRU provides a **direct, low-latency pathway** for later layers to communicate routing-relevant feedback to earlier layers, bypassing the noisy, diluted path through the main model. This is analogous to the role of residual connections in training deep networks (He et al., 2020 is cited for the parallel insight about attention score residuals), but applied to the cross-layer routing optimization problem.

This finding is **diagnostically significant**: it identifies a specific optimization pathology (insufficient gradient flow to early routers) that would not be obvious from forward-pass analysis alone, and provides a concrete mechanism (recurrent gradient via GRU) to address it. It implies that future router designs should be evaluated not just on their forward-pass routing quality but on the gradient pathways they create for training.

### Innovation 4: Gate Entropy as a Diagnostic for Router Exploration-Exploitation Balance

Section 6 introduces a novel diagnostic lens for evaluating router behavior: the **distribution of gate entropy across tokens**. Gate entropy, defined as $-\sum_n g_n \ln g_n$ where $g_n$ are the post-softmax gating scores, measures how evenly the router distributes probability mass across experts. High entropy means the router is uncertain (spreading mass across many experts, encouraging exploration). Low entropy means the router has converged to sharp preferences (concentrating mass on a few experts, exploitation).

This is more than a visualization — it's a diagnostic framework that reveals *why* different router designs succeed or fail. Figure 4 shows distinct entropy signatures for each method:
- **RandomMoE** shows a single peak at very high entropy values (~2.0-2.5) — the router is maximally explorative but never exploits, explaining why it achieves competitive but not optimal performance (it uses experts evenly but without learned specialization).
- **SMoE and HyperMoE** show a sharp peak at near-zero entropy — the router has converged to near-deterministic assignments, meaning the top-2 selection effectively degrades to top-1, and the second expert receives almost no gradient signal. This premature exploitation explains why these methods plateau early.
- **XMoE and CosineMoE** show intermediate entropy distributions, consistent with their better performance — the normalization inherent in cosine similarity prevents the gating scores from collapsing.
- **RMoE** shows a distinctive **bimodal distribution**: a peak at moderate-to-high entropy for most tokens, plus a small tail at low entropy for a minority. This suggests the router learns to be confident when routing history strongly indicates a particular expert path, but remains exploratory when uncertainty is high. The cross-layer information from the GRU enables this adaptivity — the router knows *when* it has enough context to commit.

The insight extends to the `RMoE-NP` variants (Figure 4, right panel): without cross-layer state propagation, the entropy distribution collapses toward the SMoE-like low-entropy peak. Adding logit residuals (`NP + r-0.5`, `NP + r-1.0`) shifts the entropy higher but produces a narrow, high-entropy peak — the router is uniformly uncertain rather than adaptively balancing exploration and exploitation. Only the full RMoE achieves the bimodal distribution that correlates with best performance.

This diagnostic framework is **transferable**: it provides a tool that future MoE researchers can use to evaluate whether their router designs are striking the right balance between exploration and exploitation, independent of downstream task metrics. It also explains **why** the recurrent gradient is important — without it, the router cannot learn *when* to be confident, so it collapses to either always-confident (low entropy) or always-uncertain (high entropy), both of which are suboptimal.

The entropy analysis is complemented by the Inner/Outer Balance metrics (Table 8), which quantify the same phenomenon from a different angle: RMoE achieves a Top-1/Top-2 ratio of 2.02 (meaning the second expert genuinely contributes) versus 34.68 for SMoE (the first expert dominates completely). This is a **fundamental improvement in expert utilization**, not just a metric gain — it means the extra capacity from top-k routing is actually being used, which directly addresses the parameter inefficiency problem that motivated the paper.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

**Dataset.** The paper evaluates on two standard language modeling benchmarks: **Enwiki8** (character-level language modeling, with Bits-Per-Character (BPC) as the evaluation metric) and **WikiText-103** (word-level language modeling, with Perplexity (PPL) as the evaluation metric). Both datasets use their default train-validation-test splits, with test performance reported from the best validation checkpoints. For large-scale pre-training experiments, the authors use a proprietary multilingual corpus spanning Wikipedia, finance, and legal texts, trained for 20B or 40B tokens. After supervised fine-tuning on the Alpaca dataset (52,000 instruction-response pairs), the paper evaluates on five tasks from the `lm-evaluation-harness`: ARC-Easy (accuracy), HellaSwag (accuracy-normalized), PIQA (accuracy-normalized), SciQ (accuracy), and LAMBADA (accuracy).

**Base model(s).** The medium-scale experiments use an 8-layer decoder-only switch-transformer architecture with post-norm, a hidden size of 352, 8 attention heads, 16 experts (top-2 selected), and expert hidden size of 352. For large-scale experiments, the model scales to 24 layers with a hidden size of 1280, 20 attention heads, 16 fine-grained experts (top-4 selected, following DeepSeek-MoE design with shared experts), SwiGLU activations, Rotary Embeddings, and RMSNorm — yielding approximately 0.53B activated parameters out of 0.91B total. Further scaling experiments use a 15B total / 2.7B activated parameter model. The medium-scale model was chosen to match CompeteSMoE (Pham et al., 2024) configurations for controlled comparison; the large-scale models were chosen to validate that RMoE benefits persist under realistic training budgets.

**Metrics.** For language modeling: **Bits-Per-Character (BPC)** on Enwiki8 (lower is better), computed as the negative log-likelihood in bits per character; **Perplexity (PPL)** on WikiText-103 (lower is better), computed as the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood per word. For large-scale evaluation: per-task accuracy or accuracy-normalized metrics as reported by `lm-evaluation-harness`, with the paper reporting an unweighted average across the five tasks as a summary statistic. Additionally, the paper uses several diagnostic metrics not directly measuring performance but characterizing router behavior: **gate entropy** ($-\sum_n g_n \ln g_n$), **Inner Balance** (ratio of top-1 gating score to top-2 gating score), **Outer Balance** (sum of selected gating scores), and **Mutual Information** between routing distributions across layers (discretized into 100 bins and computed via `sklearn.metrics.mutual_info_score`).

**Baselines.** The paper compares against six router designs, all within the same decoder-only switch-transformer architecture: (1) **SMoE** — standard switch-transformers with a linear router (Fedus et al., 2022); (2) **HyperMoE** (Do et al., 2023) — a fixed, randomly initialized hypernetwork at each layer generates the router's linear weights; (3) **SMoE-MLP** (Shen et al., 2023) — replaces the linear router with a two-layer MLP using GELU activation; (4) **RandomSMoE** — a fixed randomly initialized linear router that is never updated during training (inspired by SMoE-Dropout from Chen et al., 2023); (5) **XMoE** (Chi et al., 2022) — projects hidden states to a lower dimension and uses cosine similarity with low-dimensional expert embeddings; (6) **CosineSMoE** — cosine-similarity routing without down-projection (similar to XMoE). For the large-scale experiments, the primary comparison is against SMoE and SMoE-MLP, with additional comparisons at the 15B/2.7B scale in Appendix A.3.

**Generation budget / compute accounting.** For the medium-scale experiments, all models are trained for 80,000 steps with identical batch size (48), learning rate (0.0007), warmup (4000 steps), and optimizer (Adam). The paper reports **peak GPU memory** (GB) with identical batch-size configurations and **training speed** (seconds per 1,000 training steps) to quantify computational overhead. The "generation budget" concept from the paper overview does not apply here — this is a training-time comparison, not an inference-time scaling study. For the large-scale experiments, training cost is reported as seconds per training step and GPU memory on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, with all models trained on the same number of tokens (20B or 40B) for fair comparison.

**Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the medium-scale experiments, the paper reports test performance at the **best validation checkpoint** (standard practice). For the large-scale experiments, the paper evaluates pre-trained checkpoints at 20B and 40B tokens on downstream tasks without task-specific fine-tuning, following the standard pre-training evaluation protocol. For supervised fine-tuning, all models undergo identical SFT on Alpaca (3 epochs, learning rate 2e-5, batch size 128, cosine schedule) and are evaluated on `lm-evaluation-harness` tasks. The paper reports results under both "freeze router" and "unfreeze router" SFT settings, following the recommendation from Zoph et al. (2022) that freezing routers during fine-tuning can improve stability. No k-fold cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported — results are from single training runs.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Medium-Scale Language Modeling (Table 1)

The primary comparison uses 8-layer models with 16 experts (top-2) trained on Enwiki8 and WikiText-103. RMoE achieves the **best performance across both validation and test sets on both benchmarks** while introducing negligible computational overhead.

**Headline numbers:** On Enwiki8, RMoE achieves **1.116 test BPC** versus 1.128 for SMoE — an improvement of 0.012 BPC, or roughly a 1.1% relative reduction. On WikiText-103, RMoE achieves **32.867 test PPL** versus 33.061 for SMoE — an improvement of 0.194 PPL. These gains are achieved with 36.51M parameters (of which 0.47M are router parameters) versus 36.08M for SMoE (0.04M router parameters), using 49.46 GB peak GPU memory (vs. 47.92 GB for SMoE) and 972.9 seconds per 1,000 training steps (vs. 960.2 for SMoE) — roughly 1.3% training time overhead and 3.2% memory overhead.

**Comparison to other baselines (Table 1):**

- **SMoE-MLP** performs *worse* than standard SMoE on Enwiki8 (1.137 vs. 1.128 test BPC) and only marginally better on WikiText-103 (33.142 vs. 33.061 test PPL), despite having 36.79M parameters (0.75M router parameters). This demonstrates that naively increasing router capacity does not help.
- **RandomSMoE** (fixed random router) achieves 1.135 test BPC — nearly identical to the learned SMoE router at 1.128 BPC, confirming that the standard learned router contributes surprisingly little beyond random assignment. On WikiText-103, RandomSMoE underperforms more clearly (33.410 vs. 33.061 PPL), suggesting the word-level task benefits more from learned routing than the character-level task.
- **HyperMoE** performs worst among learned routers on Enwiki8 (1.139 test BPC) and WikiText-103 (33.374 test PPL), despite having the most router parameters (12.4M, though many are unlearnable hypernetwork weights).
- **CosineMoE and XMoE** perform competitively with SMoE. CosineMoE achieves 1.122 test BPC and 33.047 test PPL; XMoE achieves 1.125 test BPC and 32.926 test PPL. XMoE's better performance on WikiText-103 is notable — it is the second-best method on that benchmark, consistent with its design goal of preventing representation collapse.
- **RMoE outperforms all methods** on both benchmarks, with the largest margin against SMoE on WikiText-103 (0.194 PPL improvement) and against CosineMoE on Enwiki8 (0.006 BPC improvement).

The relative ordering on WikiText-103 test PPL is: RMoE (32.867) < XMoE (32.926) < CosineMoE (33.047) < SMoE (33.061) < SMoE-MLP (33.142) < HyperMoE (33.374) < RandomSMoE (33.410). The 0.543 PPL gap between RMoE and RandomSMoE represents a substantial improvement, confirming that learned routing with cross-layer coordination substantially outperforms both unlearned routing and sophisticated per-layer routing in isolation.

#### Compatibility with Existing Router Designs (Table 2)

Table 2 demonstrates that RMoE's recurrent router can be composed with XMoE to yield consistent improvements across all XMoE configurations. The experiment tests XMoE with three lower-dimensional projection sizes (8, 16, and 32) both with and without the GRU router.

**Headline numbers:** Adding the GRU router to XMoE reduces test BPC and PPL across all configurations. The largest improvement occurs at the smallest XMoE dimension:

- **XMoE(8) + GRU router:** test BPC drops from 1.132 to 1.124 (0.008 improvement); test PPL drops from 33.55 to 32.99 (0.56 improvement).
- **XMoE(16) + GRU router:** test BPC drops from 1.125 to 1.119 (0.006 improvement); test PPL drops from 32.93 to 32.47 (0.46 improvement).
- **XMoE(32) + GRU router:** test BPC drops from 1.114 to 1.112 (0.002 improvement); test PPL drops from 32.71 to 32.55 (0.16 improvement).

The pattern is clear: **the GRU provides the largest absolute benefit when the per-layer router has the least capacity** (smallest XMoE dimension). When XMoE already operates in a 32-dimensional space, the per-layer router can capture much of the needed routing information on its own; the cross-layer information adds modest value. When XMoE compresses to only 8 dimensions, the per-layer representation is severely constrained, and the GRU's cross-layer history becomes crucial. This supports the paper's claim that RMoE and per-layer router improvements are complementary — the GRU compensates when per-layer routing is weak, and both can be deployed together for maximum gain.

Additionally, the XMoE(32) baseline achieves 1.114 test BPC — better than the standard RMoE at 1.116 BPC — but XMoE(32) + GRU improves this to 1.112, showing that even strong per-layer routers benefit from cross-layer information. The gap closes as per-layer routing improves, but never reverses sign.

#### Large-Scale Pre-Training (Table 3, Main Text)

The paper scales RMoE to a 0.91B parameter model (24 layers, 1280 hidden size, 16 fine-grained experts with top-4 selection) trained on 20B and 40B tokens, with and without supervised fine-tuning on Alpaca. Table 3 reports results across five `lm-evaluation-harness` tasks. All RMoE variants use GRU with $p = 128$ unless otherwise noted.

**Pre-training results (no fine-tuning):**

At 20B tokens:
- **SMoE average across 5 tasks:** 47.63%
- **RMoE average:** 49.20% — a **+1.57 percentage point improvement** (3.3% relative). The largest single-task gain is on LAMBADA (19.13% vs. 14.61%, a +4.52 point gain), while ARC-Easy is essentially tied (47.01% vs. 47.14%).

At 40B tokens:
- **SMoE average:** 54.26%
- **RMoE average:** 55.31% — a **+1.05 percentage point improvement** (1.9% relative). The largest gain is again on LAMBADA (32.58% vs. 26.74%, +5.84 points), while SciQ is nearly tied (83.6% vs. 83.4%).

The improvement from 20B to 40B tokens benefits both models substantially (SMoE gains +6.63 points, RMoE gains +6.11 points), but RMoE maintains its advantage at both scales. The narrowing gap from +1.57 to +1.05 points with more training suggests that cross-layer routing information is most valuable during early training when per-layer routers have had less time to develop effective routing policies — this aligns with the intuition that the GRU helps router exploration in early training stages before per-layer routers converge to sharp assignments.

**Supervised fine-tuning results (Table 3):**

After SFT on Alpaca:
- **SMoE (20B tokens) + sft:** average 48.97% (with router frozen: 49.11%). The frozen router improves slightly over unfrozen (49.11% vs. 48.97%), supporting the finding from Zoph et al. (2022) that router freezing helps during fine-tuning.
- **RMoE (20B tokens) + sft:** average 51.20% (with GRU and router frozen: 51.24%). The improvement over SMoE grows to **+2.13 points** (from +1.57 pre-SFT to +2.13 post-SFT for the best freeze configuration). This suggests that RMoE's better routing during pre-training produces better representations that transfer more effectively during fine-tuning.
- **SMoE (40B tokens) + sft:** average 56.13%. With router frozen: 55.88% — notably, freezing helps *less* at 40B than at 20B, possibly because the router is more stable after longer training.
- **RMoE (40B tokens) + sft:** average 57.15%. With GRU and router frozen: 57.08%. The improvement over SMoE is **+1.02 points** (consistent with the 20B improvement). RMoE's best SFT configuration at 40B achieves the highest average across all models and training regimes at this scale.

**Key observation on freezing patterns:** For SMoE, freezing only the router (but not the experts) during SFT yields the best results at 20B (49.11%). For RMoE, freezing both the GRU *and* the router (51.24%) slightly outperforms freezing only the router (51.20%) at 20B. At 40B, the patterns are similar but differences narrow — all freeze configurations for RMoE cluster around 57.08-57.15%. This suggests that RMoE's routing is relatively stable after sufficient pre-training and does not require careful freeze strategy tuning.

**Computational cost at scale (Table 3):** The overhead is minimal. At the 0.91B scale, SMoE trains at 48.87 seconds/step with 48.00 GB GPU memory; RMoE trains at 49.07 seconds/step with 48.69 GB — a 0.4% slowdown and 1.4% memory increase. RMoE adds approximately 3.5M parameters to the router system, negligible relative to the 0.91B total.

#### Further Scaling: 15B/2.7B Models (Appendix A.3, Tables 9 and 10)

The paper validates RMoE at larger scale using 15B total / 2.7B activated parameter models with fine-grained experts and shared experts (following DeepSeek-MoE design). Two training budgets are tested: 120B tokens (Table 9) and 400B tokens (Table 10), with evaluations at intermediate checkpoints.

**120B token training (Table 9):**

At 80B tokens, the gap between RMoE and SMoE is already visible: RMoE achieves 27.14% on GSM8K vs. 24.18% for SMoE (+2.96 points), and 47.74% on MMLU vs. 46.24% (+1.50 points). SMoE-MLP performs similarly to or worse than SMoE on most metrics (GSM8K: 23.58% vs. 24.18%; MMLU: 46.47% vs. 46.24%), confirming again that naive router capacity increases do not help.

At 120B tokens, RMoE achieves:
- **HellaSwag:** 72.36% vs. 72.03% for SMoE (+0.33 points)
- **MMLU:** 54.02% vs. 52.79% (+1.23 points)
- **GSM8K:** 36.13% vs. 34.80% (+1.33 points)
- **Avg PPL:** 6.425 vs. 6.447 (-0.022, lower is better)

The improvements on reasoning-heavy tasks (MMLU, GSM8K) are more substantial than on commonsense reasoning (HellaSwag), suggesting cross-layer routing coordination particularly benefits multi-step reasoning where consistent expert pathways across layers are valuable.

**400B token training (Table 10):**

At 200B tokens: RMoE (70.00% HellaSwag, 52.21% MMLU) vs. SMoE (69.48%, 49.96%). The MMLU gap of +2.25 points is the largest single-metric improvement at this checkpoint.

At 400B tokens: RMoE (76.72% HellaSwag, 60.60% MMLU, 52.99% GSM8K, 5.620 Avg PPL) vs. SMoE (76.39%, 59.54%, 52.16%, 5.685). The improvements are consistently positive but modest at this scale:
- HellaSwag: +0.33 points
- MMLU: +1.06 points
- GSM8K: +0.83 points
- Avg PPL: -0.065

**Key finding at scale:** RMoE's advantage persists but narrows with more training tokens. At 80B tokens, GSM8K shows a +2.96 point gap; at 120B, +1.33; at 400B, +0.83. This pattern echoes the finding from Table 3 where the gap narrowed from 20B to 40B tokens, and suggests that **cross-layer routing information is most valuable during early-to-mid training**, when per-layer routers have not yet converged to effective local policies. Once training is sufficiently long, even isolated routers can discover good assignments through extended gradient-based optimization, reducing but not eliminating the benefit of explicit cross-layer coordination.

**SMoE-MLP comparison at scale:** Across both Tables 9 and 10, SMoE-MLP (MLP router) never convincingly outperforms SMoE and frequently underperforms it. At 400B tokens, SMoE-MLP achieves 59.96% MMLU vs. 59.54% for SMoE (+0.42), but 51.71% GSM8K vs. 52.16% for SMoE (-0.45), and 5.709 Avg PPL vs. 5.685 (-0.024, worse). This is a consistent finding across all scales: **increasing router capacity through a deeper MLP does not reliably improve routing quality**, underscoring that the problem is informational (what the router knows) rather than representational (how the router computes).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

#### RMoE Architecture Component Ablations (Table 4 — Medium Scale)

**Layerwise recurrence vs. additional parameters:** Table 4 compares SMoE, SMoE + MLP (larger router, no recurrence), RMoE + NP (GRU present but no cross-layer state propagation — the GRU input is always $\mathbf{h}_0$ rather than $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$), and full RMoE. The results isolate the contribution of recurrence from the contribution of additional parameters. On Enwiki8 test BPC, full RMoE achieves 1.116; RMoE + NP achieves 1.123 — *worse than both SMoE (1.128) and SMoE + MLP (1.183 for the small model variant, 1.137 for medium)*. Despite having identical parameters and FLOPs to full RMoE, removing cross-layer state propagation degrades performance below the baseline. This demonstrates that the recurrent pathway, not the GRU's parameters or computation, is the active ingredient.

On the small model variant (fewer parameters overall), SMoE achieves 1.184 BPC, SMoE + MLP achieves 1.183 (no improvement from larger router), RMoE + NP achieves 1.196 (worse than SMoE), and RMoE achieves 1.183 (matching SMoE + MLP despite the NP variant's degradation). The fact that full RMoE recovers from the NP degradation to match or exceed baselines while using the same parameter count as NP is strong evidence that the layerwise recurrence is both necessary and sufficient for the gains.

#### Recurrent Gradient Ablations (Table 5 — Medium Scale)

This set of ablations is the most technically revealing in the paper, systematically disentangling forward information flow from backward gradient flow through the GRU. All experiments use the 8-layer medium model on Enwiki8.

**Detaching the recurrent gradient (`RMoE + detach h_{i-1}`):** By detaching $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$ in Eq. 5, the GRU at layer $i$ still receives the forward value of $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$ (so it can condition on routing history), but no gradient flows backward through this connection (so later layers cannot send routing-relevant training signal to earlier layers via the GRU pathway). The result: **1.133 test BPC, worse than SMoE at 1.128 BPC** and dramatically worse than full RMoE at 1.116. This is a striking negative result — preserving forward information alone, without the backward gradient, is *harmful*. The model has access to routing history but cannot learn to use it effectively because the optimization signal is cut off. The router at layer $i$ receives gradient only through the diluted path (via expert FFNs, residuals, attention), and the direct path through the GRU is blocked.

**Adding routing logit residuals without gradient (`RMoE + NP + r-α + detach-r`):** These experiments test whether providing forward routing information through an alternative pathway (gating score residuals) can substitute for the GRU recurrence, and whether the gradient through those residuals matters. The gating score for expert $n$ at layer $i$ becomes $g_n(\mathbf{h}_i; \mathbf{G}_i, k) + \alpha g_n(\mathbf{h}_{i-1}; \mathbf{G}_{i-1}, k)$.

Without detaching (gradient flows through the residual connection): `RMoE + NP + r-0.5` achieves 1.124 test BPC, and `RMoE + NP + r-1.0` achieves 1.124 — identical performance, both slightly worse than `RMoE + NP` alone at 1.123. The forward routing information through residuals provides no clear benefit.

With detaching (gradient blocked on the residual): `RMoE + NP + r-0.5 + detach-r` achieves **1.133 test BPC** — substantially worse, identical in magnitude to `RMoE + detach h_{i-1}`. `RMoE + NP + r-1.0 + detach-r` achieves 1.126 BPC — still worse than the non-detached versions. The gradient through the cross-layer connection, whether through GRU or through logit residuals, is the critical factor.

**Interpretation across the full ablation matrix (Table 5, sorted from best to worst):**

| Configuration | Test BPC | Forward Info? | Backward Gradient? |
|---|---|---|---|
| RMoE (full) | 1.116 | Yes (GRU) | Yes (GRU) |
| RMoE + NP | 1.123 | No | No (no cross-layer path) |
| RMoE + NP + r-1.0 | 1.124 | Yes (residual) | Yes (residual) |
| RMoE + NP + r-0.5 | 1.124 | Yes (residual) | Yes (residual) |
| RMoE + NP + r-1.0 + detach-r | 1.126 | Yes (residual) | No (residual detached) |
| SMoE (baseline) | 1.128 | No (standard) | No (standard) |
| RMoE + detach h_{i-1} | 1.133 | Yes (GRU) | No (GRU detached) |
| RMoE + NP + r-0.5 + detach-r | 1.133 | Yes (residual) | No (residual detached) |

The pattern is unambiguous: configurations with backward gradient flow through the cross-layer connection (rows 1, 3, 4) consistently outperform configurations without it, regardless of whether forward information is present. Configurations without backward gradient flow (rows 2, 5, 7, 8) cluster near or below the SMoE baseline. The best configuration (full RMoE, 1.116) has both forward information and backward gradient.

#### Model Depth Scaling (Figure 2 — Medium Scale, Enwiki8)

Figure 2 tests RMoE and its variants across model depths of 6, 12, 18, 24, and 32 layers to assess whether the recurrent mechanism's benefits increase with depth. The key findings:

- **RMoE consistently outperforms SMoE at all depths**, with the gap widening from approximately 0.003 BPC at 6 layers to approximately 0.010 BPC at 24 layers, then slightly narrowing at 32 layers. This supports the hypothesis that deeper models benefit more from cross-layer routing coordination because the routing history spans more layers and the optimization problem for isolated routers becomes harder.
- **RMoE-NP (no cross-layer state) underperforms SMoE at all depths**, and the gap *widens* with depth. At 6 layers, RMoE-NP and SMoE are nearly tied; at 32 layers, RMoE-NP is approximately 0.015 BPC worse than SMoE. This suggests that adding a GRU without cross-layer recurrence becomes increasingly harmful in deeper models — the stateless GRU acts as a bottleneck without providing the coordination benefit.
- **RMoE-NP-r0.5 (logit residuals with gradient) outperforms RMoE-NP but underperforms RMoE at all depths**. The gap between RMoE-NP-r0.5 and RMoE widens with depth, suggesting that direct logit residuals do not scale as well as GRU-mediated recurrence for deep models. This is consistent with the mutual information analysis (Figure 3f vs. 3d) which showed that logit residuals improve neighbor-layer MI but fail to maintain long-range dependencies.

**Specific figures from the text:** At 12 layers, SMoE test BPC is approximately 1.140; RMoE is approximately 1.135; RMoE-NP is approximately 1.145. At 24 layers, SMoE is approximately 1.128; RMoE is approximately 1.118; RMoE-NP is approximately 1.140. At 32 layers, SMoE is approximately 1.122; RMoE is approximately 1.115; RMoE-NP is approximately 1.132. These are approximate values read from the log-scale figure and should be confirmed against the paper's exact numbers if available.

#### Recurrent Unit Design Space (Tables 6 and 7)

**Medium scale (Table 6):** The paper compares GRU, vanilla RNN, and LSTM as the recurrent cell, and compares layerwise projectors vs. shared projector.

- **GRU vs. RNN vs. LSTM:** On Enwiki8 test BPC, GRU achieves 1.116, RNN achieves 1.119, LSTM achieves 1.122. On WikiText-103, GRU achieves 32.86 test PPL, RNN achieves 32.72, LSTM achieves 33.04. The ordering is mixed — RNN slightly edges out GRU on WikiText-103 — but GRU provides the best average across both benchmarks and has the most consistent behavior. LSTM underperforms both, likely because its additional parameters (input, forget, output gates plus separate cell state) are inefficient at the small hidden dimension ($p = 128$) used here, leading to overfitting or optimization difficulty.

- **Layerwise projector vs. shared projector:** Using a layerwise projector (standard RMoE) achieves 1.116 test BPC and 32.86 test PPL; using a single shared projector ("S-proj") achieves 1.123 test BPC and 33.02 test PPL. The degradation of 0.007 BPC and 0.16 PPL confirms that hidden state statistics vary sufficiently across layers to warrant per-layer projection. This is consistent with Figure 11 (Appendix), which shows router weight norms and standard deviations varying systematically with depth.

**Large scale (Table 7):** The paper further ablates recurrent cell type and hidden dimension at the 0.91B scale with 20B tokens:

- **GRU variants ($p = 128, 256, 512$):** Averaged across five downstream tasks (pre-training only, no SFT), GRU($p=128$) achieves 49.20%, GRU($p=256$) achieves 49.08%, GRU($p=512$) achieves 49.19%. The medium-sized GRU ($p=128$) performs best, with the larger dimension ($p=256$) slightly worse and $p=512$ recovering but still below $p=128$. After SFT, GRU($p=128$) achieves 51.32%, outperforming $p=256$ (50.04%) and $p=512$ (50.02%) by larger margins. This suggests that a moderate recurrent dimension extracts sufficient routing signal without overfitting — larger GRU states may memorize spurious routing correlations that do not transfer to downstream tasks.

- **RNN vs. GRU at scale ($p=256$):** RNN achieves 47.92% pre-training average (below SMoE at 47.63%) and 50.44% after SFT. The simpler RNN provides less benefit than GRU, consistent with the medium-scale findings — gating mechanisms are important for selectively retaining and discarding routing history.

**Full results in Appendix Table 12:** The per-task breakdowns (Appendix A.5, Table 12) reveal that the GRU($p=512$) model achieves the highest LAMBADA score at 20B tokens pre-training (25.00% vs. 19.13% for GRU($p=128$) and 14.61% for SMoE), but underperforms on other tasks (ARC-E: 47.77%, HellaSwag: 35.39%, PIQA: 64.80%), leading to a lower average. This heterogeneity suggests that different tasks benefit differently from routing history — LAMBADA, which requires long-range context understanding, may particularly benefit from cross-layer coordination, while tasks requiring commonsense reasoning (PIQA) or basic knowledge (ARC-E) are less sensitive to routing quality.

#### RMoE Variant Design Choices (Tables 6, 7, 12 — Full Breakdown)

**Layerwise projectors are consistently better than shared projectors at medium scale** (Table 6): the shared projector variant degrades Enwiki8 from 1.116 to 1.123 BPC and WikiText-103 from 32.86 to 33.02 PPL. This aligns with the observation in Figure 11 that hidden state statistics vary substantially across layers, supporting the design principle of per-layer projection.

**Freezing strategies during SFT (Table 12, Appendix A.5):** The paper explores several freezing configurations for RMoE during SFT: (a) freeze nothing, (b) freeze router only, (c) freeze router and GRU. For RMoE GRU($p=128$) at 20B tokens: freeze-nothing achieves 51.20% average, freeze-router achieves 51.32%, freeze-router-and-GRU achieves 51.24%. The differences are small, suggesting RMoE's routing is robust to SFT updates — the routing learned during pre-training transfers well without requiring careful freezing.

For RMoE GRU($p=256$) at 20B tokens: freeze-nothing achieves 50.04%, freeze-router achieves 49.94%, freeze-router-and-GRU achieves 49.91%. The results are again tightly clustered, but note that the larger GRU underperforms the smaller one across all freeze configurations, confirming that $p=128$ is the better design choice at this scale.

**Summary of design choices validated by ablations:**

1. **Layerwise projectors** are superior to shared projectors (Table 6, Figure 11).
2. **GRU** is the best recurrent cell among GRU, RNN, LSTM (Table 6, Table 7), though RNN is competitive on WikiText-103.
3. **$p = 128$** is the optimal GRU hidden dimension at the tested scales (Table 7), outperforming $p = 256$ and $p = 512$.
4. **Cross-layer hidden state propagation** is essential — removing it (RMoE-NP) degrades performance below SMoE (Tables 4, 5; Figure 2).
5. **Recurrent gradient flow** is essential — blocking gradient through the cross-layer connection (detaching $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$) degrades performance below SMoE (Table 5).
6. **Naive logit residuals** cannot substitute for learned GRU recurrence (Table 5, Figure 2).
7. **Freezing strategy during SFT** has minimal impact on RMoE performance (Table 12).

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the experiments support the central claim that RMoE improves MoE parameter efficiency?

The paper's primary claim is that adding cross-layer routing coordination via a GRU improves the performance of MoE language models with negligible computational overhead. The experimental evidence broadly supports this claim, but with important boundary conditions:

**What is demonstrated:**

The paper shows consistent improvement across three model scales (medium 8-layer, large 24-layer 0.91B, very large 15B/2.7B), across multiple datasets and tasks (Enwiki8, WikiText-103, ARC-Easy, HellaSwag, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval, domain PPL), across both pre-training and SFT paradigms, and across two training regimes (small-scale "from scratch" and large-scale continued pre-training). The improvements are not large in absolute terms — typically 0.01-0.02 BPC on Enwiki8, 0.2-0.5 PPL on WikiText-103, 1-2 percentage points on downstream tasks — but they are remarkably consistent, appearing in essentially every comparison where RMoE is pitted against SMoE under matched training conditions.

**What is NOT demonstrated:**

**1. Parameter efficiency relative to dense models is not evaluated.** The paper motivates RMoE by citing inefficiencies in prior work: a 52B MoE matching a 6.7B dense model, a 2B MoE matching a 783M dense model, a 16B MoE matching a 7B dense model. However, the paper never compares RMoE against dense models of equivalent compute or activated parameter count. The experiments only compare against other MoE router designs. The claim that RMoE "addresses parameter inefficiency" (Section 1) is therefore a claim about relative improvement within the MoE paradigm, not a claim that RMoE closes the gap to dense models. Whether the 0.01-0.02 BPC improvement on Enwiki8 or the 1-2 point improvement on downstream tasks would meaningfully change the 52B-MoE-vs-6.7B-dense comparison is unknown and untested. This is a significant gap in the evaluation, especially since the paper's motivation frames parameter inefficiency as the core problem.

**2. The largest RMoE models are not compared against comparably-sized dense models.** The 15B/2.7B experiments (Tables 9, 10) compare RMoE against SMoE and SMoE-MLP, but provide no comparison to a ~2.7B dense model trained on the same data. Without this comparison, it is impossible to assess whether RMoE's routing improvements actually close the efficiency gap that motivated the work.

**3. The number of training tokens is relatively modest.** The 0.91B models are trained on only 20B or 40B tokens — roughly 22-44 tokens per parameter. By modern standards (e.g., Chinchilla-optimal training suggests ~20 tokens per parameter, but many practical models train on far more), these models are significantly undertrained. The 15B/2.7B models trained on 400B tokens (~27 tokens per activated parameter, ~27 tokens per total parameter for 15B) are closer to reasonable budgets but still modest. Whether RMoE's benefits persist or diminish at truly large training scales (trillions of tokens) is unknown. The trend of narrowing gaps with more training (GSM8K gap: +2.96 at 80B tokens → +1.33 at 120B → +0.83 at 400B, from Tables 9 and 10) suggests RMoE's advantage may continue to shrink at larger scales, though it has not disappeared by 400B tokens.

**4. Single model family and architecture.** All experiments use decoder-only transformer architectures with post-norm (medium scale) or Llama-style modifications (large scale). The medium-scale architecture is quite specific (8 layers, hidden size 352). Whether the findings transfer to encoder-decoder architectures, much larger models (100B+), different attention mechanisms, or different expert configurations (e.g., many more experts per layer) is not tested. The paper claims RMoE is "orthogonal to most existing MoE designs," but this claim is tested only by combining with XMoE (Table 2) — which modifies the router's similarity function, not the expert structure.

#### Do the ablations genuinely isolate the effect of cross-layer recurrence?

The ablation design in Tables 4, 5, 6, and Figure 2 is one of the paper's strengths. The key comparisons are well-constructed:

- **SMoE vs. SMoE + MLP vs. RMoE + NP vs. RMoE** (Table 4): This 2×2 design varies two factors independently — router capacity (SMoE vs. MLP) and cross-layer recurrence (NP vs. full RMoE) — allowing isolation of each factor's contribution. The result that RMoE + NP underperforms SMoE despite having more parameters essentially proves that the recurrent pathway is necessary.

- **Detaching $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$** (Table 5): By blocking only the backward gradient while preserving forward information, this ablation cleanly separates the forward and backward contributions of the recurrence. The result that detaching is worse than SMoE (1.133 vs. 1.128 BPC) is genuinely surprising and informative — it shows that forward routing information without the ability to optimize its use is actually harmful.

**However, there are missing ablations that would strengthen the case:**

**1. No ablation on the projectors' necessity.** The paper could have tested whether the projectors are needed at all by feeding $\mathbf{x}_i$ directly into the GRU (with a larger GRU input dimension matching $h$). This would isolate whether the dimensionality reduction from $h$ to $p$ is beneficial or merely a computational convenience. The paper's justification — that hidden state statistics vary across layers — would be more convincing if a no-projector version performed worse.

**2. No ablation on GRU weight sharing across layers.** The paper states that the GRU is shared across layers, but never tests a per-layer GRU variant. It argues (convincingly) that sharing reduces parameters and encourages learning depth-invariant patterns, but without an ablation, we cannot know whether per-layer GRUs would perform even better at the cost of additional parameters.

**3. No ablation on the GRU hidden state dimension relative to model size.** The paper tests $p = 128, 256, 512$ and finds $p = 128$ optimal, but does not test whether the optimal $p$ scales with model hidden dimension. It is plausible that a model with $h = 1280$ benefits from a different $p$ than one with $h = 352$, but this is not explored.

**4. No comparison to simply concatenating previous-layer gating scores.** A trivial way to provide cross-layer routing information would be to concatenate the gating scores from layer $i-1$ to the hidden state at layer $i$ before the router. This would provide forward routing information without any recurrence and would serve as a simpler baseline than RMoE. The `RMoE + NP + r-α` experiments partially address this by adding logit residuals, but this is not the same as augmenting the router input.

#### Are the diagnostic analyses (gate entropy, MI, expert similarity) convincingly linked to performance?

The paper devotes significant space to analyzing *why* RMoE works, measuring gate entropy (Figures 4, 8, 9, 10), cross-layer mutual information (Figures 3, 6, 7), inner/outer balance (Table 8), router gradient norms (Table 11), and expert similarity (Figures 5, 13). These analyses are rich and informative, but the causal link to downstream performance is mostly correlational:

**Gate entropy (Figure 4):** The paper argues that RMoE's bimodal gate entropy distribution (some tokens high entropy, some low) represents a better exploration-exploitation balance than SMoE's sharp low-entropy peak. However, RandomMoE shows very high entropy and underperforms RMoE on WikiText-103 (33.410 vs. 32.867 PPL). CosineMoE shows intermediate entropy and performs competitively (33.047 PPL). The correlation between entropy distribution shape and performance is suggestive but not strictly monotonic. The paper would be strengthened by an experiment that artificially adjusts gate entropy (e.g., via temperature scaling) during training to test whether moderate entropy *causes* better performance or merely *correlates* with it.

**Cross-layer MI (Figure 3):** RMoE shows higher MI than all baselines, and RMoE-NP shows lower MI than RMoE, consistent with the performance ordering. However, the `RMoE-NP + r-1.0` variant (Figure 3f) shows MI values between RMoE and RMoE-NP *on the diagonal and near-diagonal*, but its performance (Table 5) is worse than RMoE-NP when the gradient is detached and similar when it is not. The MI analysis convincingly shows that RMoE increases cross-layer coordination, but does not establish that this coordination *directly causes* the performance improvement — it could be a side effect of better training dynamics rather than a mechanism.

**Expert similarity (Figure 5):** RMoE shows lower expert similarity than SMoE, which the paper interprets as evidence of greater expert specialization. This is plausible and consistent with the performance results, but the magnitude of the similarity difference is small (the medians overlap substantially in Figure 5), and it is unclear whether this degree of similarity reduction is sufficient to explain the performance gap.

**Router gradient analysis (Table 11):** This is the most causally direct analysis. The paper shows that the linear router's gradient is dominated by the load balancing loss in early training (grad from LB loss = 0.433 at step 0.1k vs. 0.625 from LM loss), while the RNN router's gradient is more balanced. The drop ratio data (fewer tokens dropped by the RNN router in early training) corroborates this. This analysis directly supports the paper's narrative that RMoE helps router optimization by controlling the influence of the LB loss, but it is only presented for the 15B/120B setting and only for one comparison (linear vs. RNN router). It would be strengthened by showing the same analysis for SMoE vs. GRU across more training runs and scales.

#### What experiments would strengthen the paper?

**1. Comparison against dense models of equivalent activated parameters.** The paper's motivation is MoE parameter inefficiency relative to dense models. Without this comparison, the absolute magnitude of RMoE's improvement is hard to contextualize. Even a single comparison (e.g., 0.91B MoE vs. ~0.5B dense at 40B tokens) would establish whether RMoE moves the needle on the efficiency gap.

**2. Scaling laws analysis.** Given the narrowing gap with more training tokens (GSM8K: +2.96 at 80B → +0.83 at 400B), it would be valuable to extrapolate whether RMoE's benefit asymptotes to zero or stabilizes at some positive value. Training runs at multiple token budgets with power-law fits would answer this.

**3. Inference-time routing analysis.** The paper focuses entirely on training-time benefits. Does RMoE affect inference-time routing behavior differently from SMoE? Are the cross-layer dependencies stable across different inputs from the same domain? An analysis of routing consistency (whether the same token routed through the same experts gets consistent cross-layer assignments) would illuminate whether the learned routing patterns are robust at deployment.

**4. Broader model family validation.** Testing RMoE on encoder-decoder architectures or non-transformer backbones would establish whether the cross-layer coordination principle generalizes beyond the specific transformer-MoE combination tested.

**5. Training from diverse random seeds.** All reported results are from single training runs. Without error bars or multi-seed results, we cannot assess whether RMoE's 0.01 BPC improvement on Enwiki8 or 0.2 PPL improvement on WikiText-103 exceeds run-to-run variance. Given the modest absolute gains, this is a non-trivial concern.

#### Summary of evidence strength

The paper provides **strong evidence that adding cross-layer recurrence to MoE routers consistently, if modestly, improves performance across a range of scales and tasks**, and that this improvement depends on both forward information sharing and backward gradient flow through the recurrent connection. The ablation design is rigorous within its scope, and the diagnostic analyses provide plausible mechanistic explanations for the observed gains.

However, the paper does not demonstrate that these improvements address the parameter inefficiency problem that motivated the work, because no comparison against dense baselines is provided. The absolute magnitude of improvements is small, narrowing with increased training, and reported from single runs without statistical quantification. The paper's strongest contributions are the diagnostic framework (gate entropy, MI, recurrent gradient analysis) and the architectural principle of disentangling routing state into a dedicated recurrent channel — the performance gains themselves are real but incremental, and their practical significance at production scale remains to be demonstrated.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Parameter Inefficiency Claim Is Motivated but Never Tested Against Dense Baselines

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's core motivation — articulated repeatedly in the introduction — is that MoE models suffer from "parameter inefficiency": a 52B MoE model performs similarly to a 6.7B dense model (Rajbhandari et al., 2022), a 2B MoE lags behind a 783M dense model (Komatsuzaki et al., 2023), and a 16B MoE is comparable to a 7B dense model (Dai et al., 2024; Bi et al., 2024). The paper positions RMoE as addressing this problem: "To alleviate this issue, we introduce the Layerwise Recurrent Router." The implicit claim is that better routing through cross-layer coordination closes some portion of this efficiency gap.

**The consequence.** The paper never compares RMoE against a dense model of equivalent activated parameters, equivalent total parameters, or equivalent training compute. Every experiment in Section 4 and Appendix A.3 compares RMoE only against other MoE router variants. The +0.012 BPC improvement on Enwiki8, the +0.194 PPL improvement on WikiText-103, the +1-2 percentage point improvements on downstream tasks — none of these are placed in the context of the parameter-efficiency gap that motivated the work. A practitioner deciding whether RMoE makes MoE models competitive with dense counterparts has no evidence either way. Given that the initial motivation cited inefficiencies where MoE models required 3-8× more total parameters to match dense performance, it is entirely possible that RMoE's improvements, while consistent, are too small to meaningfully shift this ratio.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper contains zero experiments comparing RMoE against dense baselines. The words "dense model," "dense baseline," or "standard model" do not appear in the experimental sections. The only comparisons are among MoE router variants (SMoE, HyperMoE, SMoE-MLP, RandomSMoE, XMoE, CosineSMoE).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper never acknowledges this as a missing evaluation. The motivation section introduces a problem (parameter inefficiency relative to dense models) that the experimental section does not test against. A reader coming from the introduction expects evidence that RMoE narrows the MoE-to-dense efficiency gap; they leave the paper with evidence only that RMoE improves over other MoE routers. The two are not equivalent.

---

### RMoE's Benefit Narrowes with Increased Training, and the Asymptotic Behavior Is Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates RMoE at relatively modest training budgets: 20B and 40B tokens for the 0.91B model in Table 3, and 80B, 100B, 120B tokens (Table 9) and 200B, 280B, 400B tokens (Table 10) for the 15B/2.7B model. These are not trivially small budgets, but they are far below the trillion-token regimes used for production-scale LLMs. Embedded in the results is a consistent but unremarked pattern: **RMoE's advantage over SMoE shrinks as training progresses.**

**The consequence.** If the shrinking trend continues, RMoE's benefit at production-scale training budgets (trillions of tokens) could asymptote to zero or become negligible. The evidence for this trend is consistent across three separate training trajectories:

- **Table 3 (0.91B model, downstream average):** SMoE vs. RMoE pre-training gap narrows from +1.57 points at 20B tokens to +1.05 points at 40B tokens. Post-SFT, the gap narrows from +2.13 to +1.02.

- **Table 9 (15B/2.7B model, MMLU):** Gap at 80B tokens is +1.50 points (47.74% vs. 46.24%); at 100B tokens, +1.13 points (51.74% vs. 50.61%); at 120B tokens, +1.23 points (54.02% vs. 52.79%).

- **Table 9 (GSM8K):** +2.96 points at 80B tokens (27.14% vs. 24.18%); +2.20 points at 100B (32.98% vs. 30.78%); +1.33 points at 120B (36.13% vs. 34.80%).

- **Table 10 (GSM8K at 400B scale):** Gap narrows from +2.25 points on MMLU at 200B (52.21% vs. 49.96%) to +0.83 points on GSM8K at 400B (52.99% vs. 52.16% — a nearly negligible 1.6% relative improvement).

The pattern is not perfectly monotonic (MMLU at 120B tokens actually widens slightly), but the overall trajectory is clear: RMoE provides the largest benefit during early-to-mid training, with diminishing returns as training extends. This makes mechanistic sense — early in training, per-layer routers have not yet converged to effective local policies, so cross-layer coordination provides substantial guidance. As training continues, even isolated routers eventually discover good assignments through extended gradient-based optimization, reducing the marginal value of explicit coordination.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The trend is visible across all training trajectories described above but is never explicitly discussed, quantified, or extrapolated. The paper presents each data point as a standalone result without analyzing the temporal dynamics of RMoE's benefit. No scaling law analysis (fitting power-law curves to extrapolate asymptotic behavior) is attempted.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the shrinking trend, does not discuss its implications for production-scale training, and does not attempt to predict whether benefits stabilize at some positive asymptotic value or decay to zero. This is a significant omission for practitioners deciding whether to adopt RMoE for large-scale training — the strongest evidence of benefit comes from the smallest training budgets, which is the least representative regime for production deployments.

---

### Difficulty Estimation Is Free, but No Analogous Mechanism Exists for RMoE at Inference Time

**The assumption or constraint.** RMoE introduces a sequential computation across layers (the GRU hidden state $\mathbf{h}_i$ depends on $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$), which means the routing computation at layer $i$ cannot begin until the routing computation at layer $i-1$ has completed. In training, this is hidden by the fact that all layers process in sequence anyway, so the added sequential dependency along the routing pathway adds negligible overhead (0.4% slowdown, as reported in Table 3).

**The consequence.** At inference time, particularly in deployment scenarios that pipeline transformer layers across devices or use layer-parallel inference strategies, the sequential GRU dependency may become a bottleneck that the paper's training-time speed measurements do not capture. Standard MoE inference can, in principle, pipeline layer $i+1$'s attention computation while layer $i$'s expert computation is still running, because there is no explicit dependency between the routing decisions at different layers. RMoE introduces a hard dependency: layer $i+1$'s router cannot compute until layer $i$'s GRU state is available, which means the GRU computation must be serialized across layers. For batched inference with large expert counts where expert computation dominates, this may be negligible. For latency-sensitive single-query inference (e.g., interactive assistants) or for deployments that interleave computation across layers, the sequential bottleneck could be meaningful.

Additionally, the paper notes (Section 3.2) that "there is no sequence-level recurrence" in RMoE — the GRU operates across layers independently for each token, so all tokens in a batch can be processed in parallel. This is true, but it only eliminates the cross-token sequential dependency. The cross-layer sequential dependency remains: for a single token, the GRU state must be computed serially from layer 0 to layer $L-1$. In a 24-layer model with $p = 128$, this adds $24 \times O(p^2) = 24 \times 16,384 \approx 393,000$ sequential operations on the critical path, which is small relative to the attention and FFN computations but is strictly serialized where the standard router has no such serialization.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports only training-time wall-clock measurements (seconds per 1,000 training steps in Table 1; seconds per training step in Table 3). No inference latency measurements, throughput measurements, or analysis of the GRU's impact on the critical path at inference time are provided. The paper's claim that RMoE "doesn't introduce sequence-level recurrence and can be efficiently implemented" (Figure 1 caption) is correct as stated but addresses only cross-token parallelism, not cross-layer serialization.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss inference-time implications, does not report inference benchmarks, and does not analyze whether the GRU computation can be fused with other operations to hide latency. For deployment-focused practitioners, this is a meaningful gap — the decision to adopt RMoE depends not only on training overhead but on whether it introduces unacceptable inference latency in the target deployment scenario.

---

### Single Model Family and Task Domain; No Evidence of Generalization Beyond Decoder-Only Transformers on Language Modeling

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses decoder-only transformer architectures. The medium-scale experiments (Tables 1, 2, 4, 5, 6) use a standard post-norm switch-transformer. The large-scale experiments (Tables 3, 7, 9, 10, 12) use a Llama-style architecture with Rotary Embeddings, SwiGLU activations, RMSNorm, and fine-grained experts following DeepSeek-MoE. While this demonstrates RMoE works across architectural variants within the decoder-only transformer family, it provides no evidence about encoder-decoder architectures, encoder-only models, vision transformers, or other backbone architectures where MoE routing is also used.

The task domain is similarly narrow. The medium-scale experiments evaluate solely on language modeling (Enwiki8 and WikiText-103). The large-scale experiments add downstream evaluation (ARC-Easy, HellaSwag, PIQA, SciQ, LAMBADA, MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval) but only in the context of language model pre-training followed by SFT. The paper does not evaluate RMoE on tasks where cross-layer routing coordination might be most valuable — multi-step reasoning, code generation requiring consistent variable tracking across layers, or multi-modal processing where different modalities might benefit from coordinated expert pathways.

**The consequence.** The paper claims RMoE is "orthogonal to most existing attempts to improve MoE and is seamlessly compatible with them" (Section 3.2, abstract, conclusion). The composability claim is technically validated by the XMoE combination experiment (Table 2), which shows RMoE works with a different similarity function. But the claim of broad applicability across MoE architectures is not validated — the paper tests exactly one architectural backbone (decoder-only transformer) with two stylistic variants (post-norm switch-transformer and Llama-style). A practitioner using MoE in an encoder-decoder model (e.g., T5-based MoE), a vision transformer, or a multi-modal architecture has no direct evidence that RMoE transfers.

Additionally, the paper's abstract and introduction frame RMoE as a general solution for MoE models broadly, not just for language modeling. But all evaluations are on language tasks. Whether cross-layer routing coordination is beneficial for vision tasks (where layers process increasingly abstract visual features), speech processing, or multi-modal fusion is untested. The mechanism might transfer — the principle of disentangling routing state applies to any deep MoE model — but the magnitude of benefit could vary substantially by domain.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None beyond language modeling on decoder-only architectures. The paper cites MoE applications in vision-language models (Lin et al., 2024) and code (Ding et al., 2024) in passing but does not evaluate on these domains.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper makes no claims about domain generality and does not discuss this as a limitation. The consistent title and abstract framing ("Mixture-of-Experts" without qualification) implies broader applicability than is demonstrated. For researchers in non-language MoE domains, the paper provides a promising architectural idea but no empirical evidence that it works in their setting.

### The Difficulty of Isolating Causal Mechanisms from Correlational Diagnostics

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 6 ("Observations") devotes substantial space to diagnostic analyses: mutual information between routing distributions (Figure 3), gate entropy distributions (Figure 4, Appendix Figures 8-10), inner/outer balance metrics (Table 8), expert similarity (Figure 5, Appendix Figure 13), and router gradient norms (Table 11). These analyses are presented as explanations for *why* RMoE works: higher cross-layer MI enables better coordination, moderate gate entropy balances exploration and exploitation, lower expert similarity indicates greater specialization, and better gradient balance prevents load-balancing loss from dominating early training.

**The consequence.** The causal direction of these relationships is not established. It is possible that some or all of these diagnostics are *effects* of better performance (or better training dynamics) rather than *causes* of it. For example:

- **Gate entropy (Figure 4):** RMoE shows a bimodal distribution (some tokens high entropy, some low) while SMoE shows a sharp low-entropy peak. The paper interprets this as "RMoE can achieve a better balance between exploration and exploitation." But the bimodal distribution could simply be a consequence of the GRU receiving two different types of input (tokens where cross-layer history is informative vs. uninformative), not an actively learned exploration strategy. The RandomMoE baseline shows uniformly high entropy and underperforms RMoE, while CosineMoE shows intermediate entropy and performs competitively — the mapping from entropy distribution to performance is not monotonic.

- **Cross-layer MI (Figure 3):** RMoE shows higher MI than baselines. The paper attributes performance gains to this "effective cross-layer information sharing." But higher MI could be a byproduct of the GRU's architecture (which by design couples routing decisions across layers) rather than the mechanism driving performance. The `RMoE + detach h_{i-1}` variant, which has identical forward-pass MI to full RMoE but no gradient flow, performs worse than SMoE (Table 5), demonstrating that forward information alone is insufficient. This suggests MI is not the causal mechanism — the backward gradient pathway is — yet the paper primarily analyzes forward-pass diagnostics.

- **Expert similarity (Figure 5):** RMoE shows slightly lower expert similarity than SMoE, which "indicates that RMoE encourages more diverse experts." The effect size is small — medians overlap substantially — and it is equally plausible that more diverse experts emerge from better training dynamics rather than causing better performance.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges no causal uncertainty in interpreting these diagnostics. The language is uniformly causal: "RMoE enables moderate flat gating scores" (Section 6), "Layerwise recurrence reduces the negative effect of load balance constraint" (Section 6), "Layerwise recurrence encourages expert diversity" (Section 6). The gradient analysis in Table 11 is the only diagnostic with a plausible causal mechanism directly grounded in optimization dynamics, and it is presented only for one scale (15B/120B) and one comparison (linear vs. RNN router), not for the primary RMoE configuration.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. No causal intervention experiments are performed — for example, artificially manipulating gate entropy during training (via temperature scaling) to test whether moderate entropy causes better performance, or directly controlling expert similarity through regularization to test whether diversity drives gains. The analyses are informative and well-designed as correlational diagnostics, but the paper presents them as mechanistic explanations without appropriate caveats. This is a limitation of the *interpretation* of results rather than the architecture itself, but it matters for researchers trying to understand which aspects of RMoE to prioritize in future designs. If gate entropy is not causal, then designing routers to achieve bimodal entropy distributions will not reliably produce gains.

---

### Modest Absolute Improvements Reported from Single Training Runs Without Statistical Quantification

**The assumption or constraint.** All performance numbers in the paper are from single training runs. There are no error bars, confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multi-seed results reported for any experiment. For the medium-scale experiments on Enwiki8 and WikiText-103 (Tables 1, 2, 4, 5, 6), each result is a single number from a single training run. For the large-scale experiments (Tables 3, 7, 9, 10, 12), each checkpoint evaluation is from a single pre-training run. The paper does not mention using multiple random seeds, does not report variance across runs, and does not discuss whether the observed differences exceed run-to-run noise.

**The consequence.** The absolute magnitude of RMoE's improvements is modest in several key comparisons. On Enwiki8 test BPC (Table 1), RMoE achieves 1.116 vs. SMoE's 1.128 — a difference of 0.012 BPC, or roughly 1.1% relative improvement. On WikiText-103 test PPL (Table 1), RMoE achieves 32.867 vs. SMoE's 33.061 — a difference of 0.194 PPL, or roughly 0.6% relative improvement. On the downstream task average at 40B tokens (Table 3), RMoE achieves 55.31% vs. SMoE's 54.26% — a difference of 1.05 percentage points. On MMLU at 400B tokens (Table 10), RMoE achieves 60.60% vs. SMoE's 59.54% — a difference of 1.06 percentage points.

Without any quantification of variance, it is impossible to assess whether a 0.012 BPC difference or a 1.06 percentage point difference is statistically reliable or could arise from different random initializations, data orderings, or hardware nondeterminism. The Enwiki8 gap, in particular, is small — equivalent to a 1.1% reduction in bits per character, which for a model at this scale could plausibly fall within run-to-run variance. The paper's training runs are relatively short (80,000 steps for the medium models), and the smaller models (8 layers, hidden size 352) may exhibit non-trivial training variance that single-run reporting cannot capture.

The most direct evidence that this matters comes from within the paper itself: in Table 1, RandomSMoE (fixed random router) achieves 1.135 test BPC, which is only 0.007 BPC worse than the learned SMoE router at 1.128 BPC. If a completely non-learned router with fixed random weights can achieve performance within 0.007 BPC of a learned router, then the difference between two learned routers at 0.012 BPC (RMoE vs. SMoE) must be interpreted cautiously. The "signal" of learned routing (SMoE vs. RandomSMoE) is comparable in magnitude to the "signal" of improved routing (RMoE vs. SMoE), and both are measured without variance estimates.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports single numbers without variance estimates for all performance metrics. The training configuration sections (Appendix A.2) do not mention multiple seeds or run averaging.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. This is standard practice in many MoE papers — single-run reporting is common due to the computational cost of multi-seed experiments, especially at large scale. However, given the modest absolute improvements, the lack of statistical quantification weakens confidence that RMoE's gains would replicate reliably. For the small-scale experiments (Enwiki8/WikiText-103, ~20 GPU-hours per run), multi-seed experiments would be computationally feasible and would substantially strengthen the evidence. The paper neither performs these experiments nor acknowledges their absence.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **conceptual reframing** of MoE routing as a cross-layer sequential decision problem rather than a collection of independent per-layer classifications, and provides architectural infrastructure (the dedicated GRU channel) to operationalize that reframing. The magnitude is best characterized as a **targeted architectural insight** rather than a paradigm shift — it does not replace or upend the standard MoE formulation, but it identifies a specific, previously unaddressed coordination failure and provides a lightweight mechanism to correct it.

The reframing matters because it changes the diagnostic lens through which routing failures are understood. Before this work, when SMoE-MLP underperformed a fixed random router (Table 1: 1.137 vs. 1.135 BPC), the natural conclusion was that learned routing is near-useless — hence the emergence of hash-based routing (Roller et al., 2021) and fixed-random routers (Chen et al., 2023) as serious alternatives. RMoE offers a different interpretation: the router at layer $i$ lacks the **right information** (routing history), not the right capacity. When that information is provided through the GRU hidden state, routing becomes reliably beneficial (RMoE at 1.116 BPC vs. RandomSMoE at 1.135 BPC). This shifts the research question from "how do we make routers smarter?" to "how do we ensure routers are well-informed?" — a more productive framing because it directs attention to information architecture rather than capacity scaling.

The paper also resolves a latent tension in the MoE literature. On one hand, learned routers demonstrably do *something* — when ablated (RandomSMoE), WikiText-103 performance degrades by 0.35 PPL (33.41 vs. 33.06). On the other hand, that *something* is surprisingly weak — replacing a learned linear router with a more powerful MLP degrades performance (SMoE-MLP at 33.14 PPL). This creates a paradox: routing matters, but router capacity doesn't help. RMoE resolves this by showing that the missing ingredient is **coordination across layers**, not capacity within layers. The SMoE-MLP router at layer $i$ makes a more sophisticated decision from the same impoverished information (only $\mathbf{x}_i$), which is like installing a more powerful engine without upgrading the navigation system — it can execute more complex computations but still lacks the map of where it has been.

Practically, the paper provides a diagnostic toolkit — gate entropy distributions (Figure 4), cross-layer mutual information (Figure 3), inner/outer balance metrics (Table 8), and recurrent gradient analysis (Table 5) — that researchers can use to evaluate future router designs regardless of whether they adopt RMoE's specific GRU mechanism. This shifts the evaluation standard from "does it improve perplexity?" to "does it improve *how* routing works?" — a maturation of the field toward mechanism-aware evaluation.

The paper makes certain research directions **more attractive**. Improving per-layer router expressiveness (HyperMoE, SMoE-MLP) now looks like a dead end unless accompanied by better routing information — the paper's consistent finding that SMoE-MLP never convincingly outperforms SMoE (Tables 1, 9, 10) establishes a ceiling on what capacity-alone approaches can achieve. Conversely, **information-architectural approaches** — dedicated channels for routing state, explicit cross-layer communication mechanisms, recurrent or attention-based routing coordinators — now look more promising. The paper also strengthens the case for **diagnostic-first router development**: rather than designing new routers and evaluating only on downstream metrics, future work should analyze gate entropy, expert utilization balance, and cross-layer coordination as first-class evaluation targets.

Directions that become **less attractive** include purely random or hash-based routing as a replacement for learned routing. While fixed-random routers achieve competitive performance in isolation (RandomSMoE at 1.135 BPC vs. SMoE at 1.128 BPC in Table 1), the fact that adding routing history (RMoE) pushes performance to 1.116 BPC demonstrates that routing *can* add value beyond random assignment — when properly coordinated. Fixed-random approaches abandon this value rather than capturing it.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Directly measuring whether RMoE closes the parameter-efficiency gap against dense models.** The paper's primary motivation is that MoE models are parameter-inefficient relative to dense models (52B MoE matching 6.7B dense, 2B MoE lagging 783M dense, 16B MoE comparable to 7B dense). Yet the paper never compares RMoE against any dense baseline. A direct follow-up would train three configurations on identical data: a dense model with ~X activated parameters, an SMoE model with ~X activated parameters (and $N \times X$ total), and an RMoE model with ~X activated parameters, then compare performance at matched training compute. Based on the paper's results, RMoE might narrow the efficiency gap by a few percentage points on downstream tasks, but whether this meaningfully changes the 52B-vs-6.7B ratio is an open empirical question. A negative result (RMoE only recovers a small fraction of the gap) would indicate that routing coordination is a real but minor bottleneck — the larger efficiency problem lies elsewhere (expert initialization, load balancing interference, training instability).

**Training RMoE at Chinchilla-optimal scales to determine whether cross-layer routing benefits plateau or persist.** The paper shows a consistent pattern of narrowing RMoE-SMoE gaps with increased training: +1.57 points downstream average at 20B tokens → +1.05 at 40B (Table 3); +2.96 GSM8K at 80B → +1.33 at 120B (Table 9); +2.25 MMLU at 200B → more modest improvements at 400B (Table 10). This suggests RMoE's benefit may asymptote toward zero or a small positive value. A scaling law study training identical SMoE and RMoE configurations at 5-10 logarithmically spaced token budgets (e.g., 1B, 3B, 10B, 30B, 100B, 300B tokens) and fitting power-law curves to the performance gap would answer this definitively. If the gap stabilizes at some positive value (e.g., 0.5-1% relative improvement), RMoE is worth adopting at scale. If it decays toward zero, RMoE is primarily a training-acceleration technique (reaching a given performance with fewer tokens) rather than an asymptotic improvement. The paper's ablation showing that the recurrent gradient is essential (Table 5) suggests the benefit might persist — the gradient pathway provides a permanent optimization advantage, not just a transient one — but this is unproven without scaling data.

**Causal intervention experiments to validate mechanistic claims.** The paper presents extensive correlational diagnostics (gate entropy, mutual information, expert similarity) but no experiments that causally manipulate these factors. A strong follow-up would test whether gate entropy *causes* better performance or merely correlates with it. For example: take a trained SMoE model, apply temperature scaling to its router softmax during continued training to artificially modulate gate entropy, and measure whether forcing intermediate entropy (similar to RMoE's distribution in Figure 4) improves performance relative to the baseline. If yes, gate entropy is a reliable design target for future routers. If no, the entropy pattern is a side effect of good routing rather than a mechanism. Similarly, one could directly penalize expert similarity (via a cosine-similarity regularization loss on expert weight matrices) during training to test whether increased expert diversity — which RMoE shows modestly in Figure 5 — drives performance gains. A negative result on either intervention would not invalidate RMoE but would refine our understanding of *which* of RMoE's many effects (cross-layer information, recurrent gradient, entropy modulation, expert diversity, load-balance gradient control) are causally responsible for the gains, enabling more targeted future designs.

**Combining RMoE with sequence-level recurrence.** The paper emphasizes that RMoE introduces only layerwise recurrence (across layers for each token independently), not sequence-level recurrence (across tokens in the input). An obvious extension is to add a second recurrent dimension that propagates routing state across token positions within a sequence. This would allow the router at token position $t$ to condition on which experts handled tokens at positions $1 \ldots t-1$, creating full 2D (layer × position) routing coordination. The architectural challenge is that this introduces a sequential dependency across token positions, breaking the parallelism that makes transformers efficient. But for applications where routing quality matters more than throughput (e.g., high-stakes single-query reasoning), the tradeoff might be worthwhile. A concrete experiment: take the 0.91B RMoE model, add a token-level GRU (or lightweight Transformer) that processes the sequence of GRU hidden states $\mathbf{h}_i^{t=1}, \mathbf{h}_i^{t=2}, \ldots$ within each layer $i$ before the router, and measure whether multi-step reasoning tasks (GSM8K, MMLU) benefit disproportionately from this additional coordination dimension.

**Domain-transfer experiments to test generality.** The paper evaluates exclusively on language. A direct transfer test would apply RMoE to vision MoE architectures (e.g., MoE-ViT for image classification) or multi-modal MoE (e.g., MoE-LLaVA-style vision-language models where different modalities might benefit from coordinated routing). The hypothesis: in vision transformers, lower layers process edges and textures while higher layers process objects and scenes — cross-layer routing coordination might help maintain consistent feature-processing pathways across this abstraction hierarchy, analogous to how it helps language models maintain consistent reasoning pathways. A negative result (RMoE provides no benefit for vision) would suggest that the sequential dependency RMoE exploits is specific to the hierarchical, compositional nature of language processing, not a universal property of deep networks. A positive result would establish RMoE as a domain-general architectural primitive.

**Adaptive routing-history gating.** The paper's GRU uses fixed learned gates (reset and update) that apply uniformly to all tokens and all layers. An extension would make the gating behavior adaptive: at layer $i$, an auxiliary network could examine the current hidden state $\mathbf{x}_i$ and the routing history $\mathbf{h}_{i-1}$ and decide whether to (a) trust the history and route accordingly, (b) ignore the history and route from scratch, or (c) partially incorporate the history with a dynamically adjusted update gate. This would address a limitation implicit in the paper's results: RMoE improves routing on average but cannot *know* when routing history is misleading (e.g., after a token's semantic role changes mid-sequence, previous routing decisions become irrelevant). The fixed GRU must learn a static compromise between preserving and discarding history, while an adaptive gating mechanism could condition this decision on the token's current state. The experiment: replace the GRU with a hypernetwork that takes $[\mathbf{x}'_i; \mathbf{h}_{i-1}]$ as input and outputs both the standard GRU gates *and* a learned "history relevance" score that modulates the effective update rate. Measure whether this improves performance on tasks requiring context-dependent routing shifts, such as multi-turn dialogue or code generation with changing variable scopes.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient MoE pre-training with faster convergence.** The paper's results (Table 3, Tables 9-10) consistently show RMoE providing the largest relative benefit during early training stages. At 20B tokens, RMoE achieves 49.20% downstream average vs. SMoE's 47.63% (+1.57 points); the gap narrows to +1.05 points at 40B tokens. At 80B tokens, GSM8K shows a +2.96 point gap (27.14% vs. 24.18%), narrowing to +1.33 at 120B tokens. This pattern suggests RMoE functions as a **training accelerator**: it reaches a given performance level with fewer tokens than standard SMoE. For organizations running expensive large-scale MoE pre-training, adopting RMoE could mean reducing the total training budget by an amount proportional to the early-training gap. Concretely, if RMoE at 20B tokens matches SMoE at ~25B tokens (a 20% token reduction), that translates to 20% less GPU time, energy, and cost for the same downstream performance. The paper does not directly quantify this token-efficiency multiplier — a practical follow-up would measure iso-performance token counts — but the trend across all three training trajectories is clear enough to motivate deployment in resource-constrained training scenarios.

**Improved expert utilization in deployed MoE inference servers.** A persistent practical challenge in MoE inference is expert load imbalance: some experts receive disproportionately many tokens, causing straggler computation and underutilized hardware. While load balancing losses mitigate this during training, deployment-time input distributions can shift, causing imbalance to re-emerge. RMoE's demonstrated effect on router behavior — more balanced inner/outer gating scores (Table 8: Top-1/Top-2 ratio of 2.02 for RMoE vs. 34.68 for SMoE) and more moderate gate entropy (Figure 4) — suggests that the router makes softer, less extreme decisions when informed by cross-layer history. This softer routing means no single expert dominates any layer, which should translate to more uniform expert utilization at inference time. While the paper's expert frequency analysis (Appendix Figure 12) shows RMoE actually *slightly increases* expert imbalance relative to SMoE in the medium-scale setting, the large-scale gradient analysis (Table 11) shows RMoE's router receives more balanced LM-vs-LB gradients, suggesting the imbalance dynamic may differ at scale. A deployment team could adopt RMoE and expect reduced tail latency from better-balanced expert queues, though direct inference-time imbalance measurements would be needed to confirm this.

**Stronger base models for fine-tuning on specialized domains.** The paper shows that RMoE's pre-training benefits persist and sometimes amplify after supervised fine-tuning. At 20B tokens, the SMoE-to-RMoE gap grows from +1.57 points pre-SFT to +2.13 points post-SFT (Table 3, best freeze configurations). At 40B tokens, +1.05 pre-SFT becomes +1.02 post-SFT (stable). This pattern — where routing improvements during pre-training produce representations that transfer more effectively during fine-tuning — is particularly valuable for the common deployment pattern of pre-training a large MoE model once and fine-tuning it for many downstream tasks. The cross-layer routing coordination learned during pre-training creates expert pathways that are more semantically coherent, and these pathways remain beneficial even when the fine-tuning data distribution differs from pre-training. A practical implication: organizations maintaining a single base MoE model fine-tuned for many internal applications (code generation, document summarization, customer support) would see compounding returns from RMoE — the routing improvement is paid for once during pre-training and yields dividends across all fine-tuned variants. The paper's finding that freezing the GRU and router during SFT works as well as or better than unfreezing (Table 12: 51.24% freeze vs. 51.20% unfreeze for GRU($p=128$) at 20B) means this benefit comes without additional SFT complexity — the routing learned during pre-training transfers directly.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions RMoE as an orthogonal addition compatible with existing router designs (Section 3.2, validated in Table 2 with XMoE), not as a replacement that forces a tradeoff against them. Since RMoE can be combined with any per-layer router without conflict, the decision is not "RMoE vs. alternative router design" but rather "add RMoE or don't." The paper's experimental evidence suggests the following decision rule:

- **Add RMoE when** your MoE model is deep (12+ layers) and you care about absolute performance improvements at modest training budgets. The recurrent gradient pathway (Table 5) and depth-scaling results (Figure 2) show RMoE's benefit is most pronounced in deeper networks and during early-to-mid training, where per-layer routers have not yet converged to effective local policies. The 0.4% training time overhead and 1.4% memory increase (Table 3) make the cost negligible relative to potential gains.

- **Deprioritize RMoE when** your primary bottleneck is inference latency in a single-query, interactive setting where the cross-layer sequential GRU dependency may add unacceptable latency to the critical path (not measured in the paper), or when training at extreme scale (trillions of tokens) where the paper's results suggest benefits may asymptote to small values. However, the paper provides no inference latency measurements, so this is a precautionary assessment based on architectural reasoning, not empirical evidence from the paper itself.

The paper does not articulate tradeoffs against specific named alternatives because RMoE is composable with all of them. The ablation in Table 6 comparing GRU vs. RNN vs. LSTM suggests preferring GRU with $p=128$ as the recurrent cell and dimension, but this is an internal design choice within RMoE, not a decision between competing frameworks.
