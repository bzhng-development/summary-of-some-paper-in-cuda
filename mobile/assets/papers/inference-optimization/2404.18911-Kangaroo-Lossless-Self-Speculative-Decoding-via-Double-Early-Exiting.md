# Kangaroo: Lossless Self-Speculative Decoding via Double Early Exiting

**ArXiv:** [2404.18911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18911)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Kangaroo**, a self-speculative decoding framework that accelerates LLM inference by using a fixed shallow sub-network as a self-draft model paired with a lightweight adapter module, eliminating the need to train a separate draft model from scratch.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Kangaroo**, a self-speculative decoding framework that accelerates LLM inference by using a fixed shallow sub-network as a self-draft model paired with a lightweight adapter module, eliminating the need to train a separate draft model from scratch. The framework operates on Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B evaluated on Spec-Bench and introduces two complementary mechanisms: early exiting for draft model construction — extracting hidden states from the target LLM's shallow layers and mapping them to final-layer predictions through a compact adapter (a single multi-head attention plus two normalization layers) — and a second **additional early exiting mechanism for generating draft tokens**, which dynamically halts drafting when top-1 confidence falls below a threshold, avoiding wasted computation on difficult tokens. Kangaroo achieves speedups up to 1.68× on Spec-Bench under single-sequence verification and outperforms Medusa-1 with 88.7% fewer additional parameters (67M compared to 591M), establishing that self-speculative decoding can match or exceed the speedup of methods with far larger parameter budgets only when drafting efficiency — not just token acceptance rate — is jointly optimized.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Speculative Decoding's Draft Model Bottleneck

The fundamental tension this paper tackles is the **high cost of achieving fast inference with speculative decoding while maintaining distributional guarantees**. Speculative decoding (SD) — the dominant paradigm for accelerating LLM inference without sacrificing output quality — operates on a simple principle: use a small, fast "draft" model to propose multiple candidate tokens, then have the large target model verify them in parallel. If the draft model's predictions align with what the target model *would have* produced, you can accept several tokens in a single forward pass of the large model, dramatically reducing the number of expensive autoregressive steps.

However, this approach introduces a chicken-and-egg problem that the paper identifies as the central bottleneck (Section 1, paragraphs 1–2):

> "Researchers often train a tiny draft model from scratch on a large corpus to accelerate large LLMs from the same series, e.g., LLaMA-68M for LLaMA-7B. However, the training of such task-specific models can be costly, limiting its application in real-world scenarios."

In other words, **the draft model itself becomes a substantial engineering burden**. Training a separate model from scratch — even a small one — requires curating training data, designing a compatible architecture, running a full pretraining or distillation pipeline, and maintaining two model artifacts in deployment. For practitioners who want to accelerate a specific LLM, this overhead can outweigh the inference-time benefits, especially if the target model is updated frequently or deployed in resource-constrained settings.

This gap between speculative decoding's theoretical appeal and its practical deployment cost motivates the search for **self-drafting** methods — approaches that derive the draft model from the target LLM itself, avoiding external models altogether.

### Why This Matters: Memory Bandwidth Is the Real Bottleneck

The practical significance of this problem goes beyond mere convenience. The paper grounds its motivation in the hardware reality of LLM inference (Section 1, paragraph 1):

> "Constrained by the bottleneck of memory bandwidth, the primary latency for autoregressive decoding of LLMs stems from memory read/write operations of model weights rather than arithmetic computations."

This is a crucial observation that many readers — especially those from a machine learning rather than systems background — may not fully appreciate. During autoregressive generation, each new token requires loading the entire model's weights from GPU memory into compute units. Modern GPUs have immense computational throughput (FLOPS) but relatively limited memory bandwidth. For large models, the time spent shuttling weights from memory to compute units **dominates** the time spent actually computing activations and logits.

The paper illustrates this with a concrete example: decoding with Vicuna-33B on four NVIDIA V100 GPUs yields a throughput of only **~7 new tokens per second**. This is glacial by real-time interaction standards — a 100-token response would take ~14 seconds to generate, far too slow for chat applications.

Speculative decoding directly attacks this bottleneck: by verifying multiple tokens in a single forward pass, you amortize the memory-read cost of the model weights over multiple generated tokens. If the draft model proposes $\gamma = 5$ tokens and all of them are accepted, you get $6$ tokens (5 accepted + 1 new) for the price of one large-model forward pass — a $6\times$ reduction in memory-bandwidth-bound latency.

But **this efficiency gain is contingent on having a good draft model**. If you have to spend enormous resources training that draft model, the practical value proposition erodes — especially for smaller-scale deployments, research prototypes, or rapidly iterating model versions. Self-drafting promises to deliver the bandwidth-amortization benefits without the training overhead.

### Conflicting Prior Evidence: Self-Drafting Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper positions itself against a landscape of existing self-drafting methods, each of which achieves partial success but leaves specific gaps unaddressed. Understanding these gaps requires examining why prior approaches fall short.

**Medusa (Cai et al., 2024): High speed, low token acceptance.** Medusa is the most prominent self-drafting baseline and the paper's primary comparison point. Rather than training a separate draft model or extracting a sub-network, Medusa adds **multiple time-independent Feedforward Neural Network (FFN) heads** on top of the target LLM's last decoder layer. Each head predicts a token at a specific future position: one head predicts the next token, another head predicts the token after that, and so on. Because these heads operate in parallel on the same hidden state, Medusa can generate multiple draft tokens in essentially one forward pass — extremely efficient drafting.

The critical weakness, however, is that these predictions are **time-independent**: each head makes its prediction conditioned only on the current prefix, without seeing the tokens predicted by earlier heads. This violates the autoregressive structure that LLMs are trained for, leading to draft tokens that are plausible individually but inconsistent collectively. As Figure 1(a) shows, Medusa's **consistent token acceptance rate** (the probability that $w$ consecutive draft tokens are *all* accepted) decays rapidly: at position 2 (the next-next-token prediction), Medusa's acceptance rate falls below 30% on the mathematical reasoning subtask. Compare this to Kangaroo, which maintains ~40% acceptance at the same distance.

> "While Medusa can efficiently generate multiple draft tokens at adjacent positions, its token acceptance rate is not yet satisfactory (see Figure 1(a))."

This manifests in the end-to-end speedup: Medusa achieves solid but not dominant speedups (1.34×–1.55× across Spec-Bench subtasks for Vicuna-7B, per Table 1), leaving room for methods that can achieve higher acceptance rates.

**REST (He et al., 2023): Retrieval-based drafting, domain-dependent.** REST generates draft tokens by retrieving relevant text spans from a reference database rather than training or extracting a draft model. When the target output domain aligns with the retrieval corpus, REST can achieve impressive speedups — e.g., 1.63× on QA for Vicuna-7B (Table 1) — because it effectively leverages existing high-quality text. However, this approach is inherently domain-dependent: if the retrieval corpus doesn't cover the generation task (e.g., novel math reasoning, creative writing with novel content), the draft quality degrades. Moreover, REST requires maintaining and querying a retrieval database at inference time, adding latency and infrastructure complexity that a pure model-based approach avoids.

**Lookahead (Fu et al., 2024): Good acceptance, slow drafting.** Lookahead decoding generates draft tokens by computing multiple candidate continuations in parallel and selecting the most probable path. Figure 1(a) shows that Lookahead achieves a token acceptance rate comparable to Kangaroo on mathematical reasoning — significantly better than Medusa. However, Figure 1(b) reveals the tradeoff: **Lookahead's end-to-end speedup is lower than Medusa's** (1.40× vs. 1.55× on MT-Bench for Vicuna-7B). Why? Because generating Lookahead's draft tokens requires substantial computation. The paper diagnoses this precisely:

> "Focusing exclusively on the token acceptance rate without considering the latency of generating draft tokens can lead to suboptimal end-to-end acceleration."

This is the key insight that structures Kangaroo's design: **drafting latency matters as much as draft quality**. A method with perfect token acceptance but slow drafting would produce no speedup — you'd just be doing the work serially. Conversely, a method with moderate acceptance but extremely fast drafting (like Medusa) can still achieve meaningful acceleration. The optimal point balances both axes.

This insight is formalized through the paper's distinction between **compression rate** and **speedup ratio** (Section 3). The compression rate (CR), defined as the average number of accepted tokens per large-model forward pass, measures draft *quality*. The speedup ratio measures actual wallclock improvement, which depends on both CR *and* the draft model's per-token generation cost. Methods with high CR but slow drafting (like deeper early-exit approaches) can underperform methods with lower CR but faster drafting (like Medusa). The paper's Figure 1 encapsulates this: Kangaroo's advantage comes not from dramatically higher CR than competitors, but from achieving competitive CR with much more efficient drafting.

**Draft & Verify (Zhang et al., 2023): The naive early-exit baseline.** Perhaps the most directly related prior work is Draft & Verify, which also uses early exiting for self-drafting — skipping intermediate transformer layers to create a fast draft model from the same LLM. This approach achieves high token acceptance rates because the draft model shares most of the target model's representations. However, the paper identifies a critical limitation:

> "While this approach could achieve a high token acceptance rate, the inference latency of the 'small model' is exceptionally high, which can hinder end-to-end acceleration efficiency."

A draft model that uses, say, half the layers of a 32-layer LLM still requires a 16-layer forward pass — far more computation than Medusa's single-layer FFN heads or Kangaroo's shallow sub-network. The high per-draft-token cost eats into the speedup, even if acceptance is excellent. Draft & Verify validates the early-exit concept but shows that naively applying it produces suboptimal end-to-end performance.

### How Kangaroo Positions Itself

The paper positions Kangaroo at the intersection of two design philosophies, synthesizing their strengths while avoiding their respective weaknesses:

1. **From early-exit methods (Draft & Verify):** Kangaroo takes the idea of **parameter sharing** between draft and target models, which enables high token acceptance without training a separate model. But unlike prior work that uses deep early-exit layers, Kangaroo exits from **extremely shallow layers** ($\ell = 2$ for Vicuna-7B, $\ell = 3$ for Vicuna-13B), keeping the draft model's per-token latency minimal. The adapter network — a single multi-head attention plus two normalization layers — bridges the representation gap, learning to map shallow-layer features to final-layer predictions. This is a lightweight solution: the adapter uses only **67M parameters** for Vicuna-7B, compared to Medusa's 591M (11.3% of the parameter budget).

2. **From independent-head methods (Medusa):** Kangaroo takes the emphasis on **drafting speed** as a first-class optimization objective. But unlike Medusa, Kangaroo's draft model is **autoregressive** — each draft token conditions on the previously drafted tokens, maintaining the autoregressive structure that LLMs are trained to exploit. This yields higher consistent token acceptance rates, particularly at longer draft distances (Figure 1(a)).

The synthesis creates a novel capability: **competitive acceptance rates with near-Medusa-level drafting efficiency**. The autoregressive design provides better token consistency (since drafts respect the sequential dependency), while the shallow sub-network keeps per-token generation costs low. The result is system-level Pareto improvement — Kangaroo achieves higher speedups than all competitors across all Spec-Bench subtasks (Table 1, Figure 1(b)), despite using fewer additional parameters than Medusa.

The **double early exiting** mechanism — the paper's signature innovation — addresses a subtle inefficiency in fixed-step drafting. The difficulty of predicting the next token varies significantly across contexts: given "The capital of France is", predicting " Paris" is easy (the model is highly confident), while predicting the next token after "The solution involves a bidirectional" is genuinely ambiguous. Fixed-step drafting treats all contexts identically, either wasting computation on difficult tokens (by continuing to draft when confidence is low, only to have the draft rejected) or missing opportunities on easy tokens (by stopping early when further drafts would likely succeed).

Kangaroo's second early exit — halting the draft model when top-1 confidence drops below threshold $\eta$ — adapts drafting depth to per-token difficulty. On easy tokens, the draft model confidently produces several sequential predictions and the draft chain extends; on hard tokens, drafting stops early, saving the cost of generating tokens that would almost certainly be rejected. Figure 3(b) shows the practical impact: fixed-step drafting ($\eta = 0$) achieves the maximum compression rate, but the dynamic threshold ($\eta = 0.6$) achieves the highest speedup — a clear demonstration that **some draft quality can be sacrificed for drafting efficiency, and the optimal tradeoff is context-dependent**.

### The Broader Implications

The paper's motivation extends beyond a single algorithm's performance. It addresses a structural problem in the LLM deployment ecosystem: the tension between model quality (which pushes toward larger models) and inference latency (which pushes toward smaller ones). Speculative decoding is the most promising resolution to this tension because it preserves the large model's distribution — unlike quantization, pruning, or knowledge distillation, all of which modify the model's output quality in ways that are difficult to formally characterize.

Within speculative decoding, **self-drafting** represents the ideal deployment scenario: no separate model to train, no retrieval database to maintain, no domain-specific tuning. Kangaroo's contribution is showing that this ideal is achievable — that a carefully designed self-drafting system can match or exceed the speedup of methods requiring far more infrastructure — **if and only if** drafting efficiency is treated as a first-class constraint alongside token acceptance. The paper's dual emphasis on acceptance rate *and* drafting latency, formalized through the CTAR metric and the dynamic threshold mechanism, provides a template for future self-drafting research that prior work, focused narrowly on compression rate, had missed.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Kangaroo is a **self-speculative decoding system** that accelerates LLM inference by constructing a draft model from the target LLM's own shallow layers, adding a tiny trainable adapter, and dynamically controlling how many tokens the draft model generates based on its own confidence. The system solves the problem of **high draft model training cost** (no separate model to train from scratch) and **suboptimal drafting efficiency** (prior self-drafting methods either achieve low token acceptance or waste time on tokens that will be rejected) through two complementary early-exit mechanisms: one that creates the draft model itself, and another that decides when to stop drafting.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

Kangaroo has four major components, arranged in a pipeline that modifies the standard speculative decoding loop:

1. **Shallow Sub-Network ($M_b[:\ell]$):** A fixed prefix of $\ell$ transformer layers taken from the target LLM $M_b$. These layers process the input prefix once per drafting round, producing hidden states at layer $\ell$ for each token position. Because these layers are shared with the target model, no additional parameters or KV-cache entries are needed — the draft model reads from the same cache the target model will later use for verification.

2. **Adapter Network ($\mathcal{A}$):** A lightweight trainable module (one multi-head attention plus two RMS normalization layers) that sits atop the shallow sub-network's output. It maps the hidden states from layer $\ell$ to predictions that approximate the full model's final-layer predictions. The adapter plus the shallow sub-network together constitute the self-draft model $M_s = \mathcal{A} \circ M_b[:\ell]$.

3. **Dynamic Drafting Controller:** A confidence-threshold mechanism that monitors the self-draft model's top-1 probability at each drafting step. When the maximum predicted probability falls below a threshold $\eta$, drafting halts immediately — even if the maximum allowed step count $\gamma$ has not been reached. This is the second early-exit mechanism, distinct from the architectural early exit that creates the draft model.

4. **Remaining Layers ($M_b[\ell:]$) and Verification:** The target model's layers from $\ell+1$ to $L$, which process all drafted tokens plus the prefix in parallel. Because the shallow sub-network has already computed the hidden states up to layer $\ell$ for all prefix positions (and cached them), the remaining layers only need to process the new draft-token positions from layer $\ell$ onward. The verifier compares the target model's output distribution against the draft model's proposed tokens using standard speculative sampling rejection rules.

**Information flow:** A prefix enters → the shallow sub-network computes hidden states at layer $\ell$ for all prefix tokens (this happens once, reusing the target model's KV-cache) → the adapter takes the hidden state at the last prefix position and autoregressively generates draft tokens one by one, each time checking the top-1 confidence against $\eta$ to decide whether to continue → when drafting stops, all draft-token hidden states (at layer $\ell$) are concatenated into a parallel compute unit → the remaining layers process this batch in one forward pass → the target model's output distribution is compared against the draft tokens using speculative sampling verification → accepted tokens are appended to the prefix, and the cycle repeats.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the self-draft model construction:** how the shallow sub-network and adapter combine to create $M_s$, the training objective, and why this particular architecture (one attention layer, no FFN) is chosen over alternatives.
- **Second, the consistent token acceptance rate (CTAR) metric:** because CTAR motivates the dynamic drafting mechanism and provides the framework for understanding why Kangaroo's autoregressive design improves over Medusa's independent heads.
- **Third, the dynamic drafting mechanism:** the confidence-threshold rule, why fixed-step drafting is suboptimal, and how the threshold $\eta$ is chosen.
- **Fourth, the verification step and parallel compute unit:** how the remaining layers verify multiple draft tokens in one forward pass, the KV-cache reuse strategy, and how an accepted token at position $t$ enables skipping one adapter forward pass in the next round.
- **Fifth, the hyperparameter selection:** how $\ell$, $\eta$, and $\gamma$ are chosen based on ablation studies, and the tradeoffs each controls.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems design paper** whose core idea is that self-speculative decoding can be made efficient by combining an extremely shallow architectural early exit (for fast drafting) with a confidence-based dynamic early exit (for avoiding wasted drafting steps), and that this combination achieves Pareto-optimal speedup-vs-parameter-count compared to existing self-drafting methods.

#### Self-Draft Model Construction via Architectural Early Exiting

The first of Kangaroo's two early-exit mechanisms is **architectural**: the draft model is created by taking only the first $\ell$ layers of the target LLM $M_b$ (the shallow sub-network) and attaching a small trainable adapter $\mathcal{A}$ on top:

$$M_s = \mathcal{A} \circ M_b[:\ell]$$

where $M_b[:\ell]$ denotes the composition of transformer layers 1 through $\ell$ (inclusive), $\mathcal{A}$ is the adapter network, and $\circ$ denotes function composition (the adapter receives the output hidden states from layer $\ell$ and produces logits over the vocabulary).

**What this notation means operationally:** Given a prefix of tokens, we run the target model's first $\ell$ layers exactly as normal — same weights, same KV-cache entries, same intermediate representations. At layer $\ell$, we extract the hidden state vector for the last token position and feed it into $\mathcal{A}$, which outputs a probability distribution over the vocabulary. This distribution is the self-draft model's prediction for the next token. To generate additional draft tokens autoregressively, the predicted token is fed back as input to layer 1 (using the shared KV-cache), the hidden state propagates through layers $1, \dots, \ell$ again, and $\mathcal{A}$ produces the next prediction.

**Why this architecture:** The key design tension is between **representation quality** and **inference latency**. Deeper early-exit layers (e.g., $\ell = L/2$) would capture representations closer to the target model's final layer, making the adapter's job easier and yielding higher token acceptance rates. But deeper layers mean more computation per draft token — a draft model using 16 layers of a 32-layer LLM would incur roughly half the cost of the full model per token, severely limiting end-to-end speedup.

Kangaroo resolves this by exiting from **extremely shallow layers** — $\ell = 2$ for Vicuna-7B and $\ell = 3$ for Vicuna-13B (Figure 3(a)) — and relying on the adapter to bridge the large representation gap. A 2-layer forward pass is so fast that the per-token drafting cost becomes a small fraction of the target model's per-token cost, even though the raw hidden states at layer 2 contain far less information than at layer 32. The adapter's job is to learn this mapping: given impoverished early features, produce predictions that match (as closely as possible) what the full 32-layer model would output.

**The adapter architecture (Table 2):** The paper experiments with several adapter designs and converges on a minimal configuration: one multi-head attention layer sandwiched between two RMS normalization layers, with **no feedforward network (FFN)**. The full list of components is:

- Input LayerNorm (RMS normalization applied to the hidden states from layer $\ell$)
- Multi-Head Attention (the only learnable transformation with significant capacity)
- Post LayerNorm (RMS normalization applied to the attention output)

The adapter has no FFN, no linear projection layers, and no dedicated LM head — it **reuses the target model's LM head** (the final linear layer that maps hidden states to vocabulary logits). This design is motivated by the observation that FFN layers constitute 67% of a standard transformer block's parameters; removing them dramatically reduces the adapter's size.

Table 2 quantifies this: for Vicuna-7B, the chosen adapter uses only **67M parameters** (11.3% of Medusa's 591M). The ablation compares several alternatives:

| Adapter variant | Parameters | Speedup |
|---|---|---|
| Kangaroo (attention + norms, no FFN) | 67M | 1.50× |
| Kangaroo + dedicated Head | 198M | 1.44× |
| 1-Layer Transformer (attention + FFN) | 202M | 1.37× |
| MLP Only (2 norms + 2 linear layers) | 165M | 1.22× |

Adding a dedicated LM head (198M) **reduces** speedup compared to reusing the target's head — the extra parameters increase per-token latency without improving acceptance enough to compensate. Using a full 1-layer transformer (attention + FFN) performs similarly to the dedicated-head variant. Using only MLP layers (no attention) produces the worst speedup, confirming that the attention mechanism is critical for bridging the early-exit representation gap — attention allows the adapter to selectively aggregate information from the prefix, which MLP-only architectures cannot do effectively.

#### Training the Adapter: The Cross-Entropy Objective

The adapter $\mathcal{A}$ is trained to minimize the cross-entropy between the self-draft model's predictions and the target model's predictions, averaged over all token positions in the training corpus:

$$\mathcal{A}^* = \arg\min_{\mathcal{A}} \sum_t \sum_n -M_b^n(\mathbf{x}^t) \log M_s^n(\mathbf{x}^t)$$

where $\mathbf{x}^t$ is the prefix of tokens up to position $t$, $M_b^n(\mathbf{x}^t)$ is the target model's predicted probability for token $n$ given that prefix, $M_s^n(\mathbf{x}^t)$ is the self-draft model's predicted probability for token $n$, and the sum runs over all positions $t$ in the training sequences and all $n$ in the vocabulary.

**What it computes:** For each token position in the training data, the target model $M_b$ produces a probability distribution over the vocabulary (the "teacher" distribution). The self-draft model $M_s$ produces its own distribution. The loss is the standard cross-entropy between these two distributions — essentially asking "how surprised is the target model's distribution by the draft model's predictions?" The inner sum (over $n$) computes $-\sum_n p_n \log q_n$ where $p_n = M_b^n(\mathbf{x}^t)$ and $q_n = M_s^n(\mathbf{x}^t)$. The outer sum (over $t$) averages this across all token positions.

**Operational meaning:** This is a **knowledge distillation** objective where the target model serves as the teacher and the self-draft model as the student, but with the crucial difference that the student shares the teacher's first $\ell$ layers. The adapter learns to produce predictions that match the full model, but it only sees the prefix through the lens of layer $\ell$ features. If the full model predicts "Paris" with probability 0.92 and "London" with 0.05, the adapter is penalized heavily if it predicts "London" with high confidence, and only lightly if it predicts a distribution like [0.91, 0.06, ...] that closely matches the target.

**Why cross-entropy rather than directly maximizing token acceptance rate:** The paper notes that "a trivial method for training the adapter network is to maximize the token acceptance rate across each position, while we find that the cross-entropy loss exhibits faster convergence rate." Token acceptance rate (for greedy decoding) is a discontinuous objective — a prediction is either correct (top-1 matches) or not — which provides weak gradient signal during training. Cross-entropy provides smooth gradients that push the draft model's entire distribution toward the target's, which empirically yields faster convergence.

**Training details:** The adapter is trained for 10 epochs using the AdamW optimizer on the ShareGPT dataset (following Medusa's training setup). Only the adapter parameters are updated; the shallow sub-network $M_b[:\ell]$ remains frozen. This means the training cost is minimal — no backpropagation through the target model's layers, only through the small adapter (67M parameters for Vicuna-7B). The training data consists of multi-turn conversation examples from ShareGPT, providing diverse prefix contexts for the adapter to learn from.

#### Consistent Token Acceptance Rate: The Metric That Motivates Dynamic Drafting

Before explaining the dynamic drafting mechanism, we must understand the metric that motivates it. The paper introduces the **consistent token acceptance rate (CTAR)** as a more informative alternative to the commonly used compression rate (CR).

**The problem with compression rate (CR):** In speculative decoding, the compression rate is defined as the average number of tokens accepted per target model forward pass:

$$\text{CR} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_k s_k$$

where $\mathcal{S} = [s_1, s_2, \dots, s_{|\mathcal{S}|}]$ is the list of accepted-token counts for each verification step, and $\sum_k s_k = N$ (total new tokens generated).

The problem is that CR is a coarse average that hides **position-dependent acceptance behavior**. In speculative decoding, when a draft token at position $w$ is rejected, **all subsequent draft tokens are discarded regardless of their quality**. This means that a draft model might have excellent acceptance at position 1 (the immediate next token), decent acceptance at position 2, poor acceptance at position 3, and so on. The CR averages these position-dependent rates into a single number, obscuring how far the draft model can reliably look ahead.

**The CTAR metric:** For a given prefix and a window size $w$, the consistent token acceptance rate $\text{CTAR}(w)$ is the probability that **all** $w$ draft tokens in that window are accepted by the target model. Formally, under greedy decoding:

$$\text{CTAR}(\mathbf{x}^t, w) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if all top-1 predictions of } M_s \text{ and } M_b \text{ match for positions } t+1, \dots, t+w \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Averaged over the decoding run:

$$\text{CTAR}(w) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_k \mathbb{I}(s_k - w > 0)$$

where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function — it equals 1 if the number of accepted tokens in round $k$ exceeds $w$, and 0 otherwise.

**What this computes operationally:** $\text{CTAR}(1)$ is the fraction of drafting rounds where at least 1 token was accepted — essentially the standard token acceptance rate for the first draft position. $\text{CTAR}(2)$ is the fraction of rounds where at least 2 tokens were accepted — meaning the first AND second draft tokens were both correct. $\text{CTAR}(6)$ is the fraction of rounds where all 6 draft tokens were accepted in sequence.

**Why CTAR matters:** Figure 1(a) plots $\text{CTAR}(w)$ for $w = 1, 2, \dots, 6$ for Kangaroo, REST, Medusa, and Lookahead on the mathematical reasoning subtask. The curves reveal dramatically different scaling behavior:

- **Medusa's CTAR decays fastest:** At $w=1$ (next token), Medusa achieves ~50% acceptance. At $w=2$ (next-next token), it drops below 30%. The rapid decay reflects Medusa's time-independent head design — each head predicts in isolation, so the probability that ALL heads are simultaneously correct drops multiplicatively even if individual heads are decent.

- **Kangaroo's CTAR decays more slowly:** Starting slightly below Medusa at $w=1$, Kangaroo maintains higher CTAR for $w \geq 2$, crossing above Medusa's curve. This is because Kangaroo's autoregressive drafting respects token dependencies — each draft token conditions on the previously drafted token, so the predictions form a coherent sequence. If the model correctly predicts the first draft token, the second draft token benefits from the additional context, improving its chance of being correct.

- **CTAR is strictly decreasing in $w$:** For all methods, $\text{CTAR}(w+1) \leq \text{CTAR}(w)$ because acceptance of $w+1$ tokens implies acceptance of $w$ tokens. The rate of decrease indicates how quickly draft quality degrades with lookahead distance.

The CTAR curve directly informs the choice of maximum draft steps $\gamma$ and the dynamic threshold $\eta$: if $\text{CTAR}(4)$ is already near zero, there is no benefit to drafting 6 tokens — the additional computation for positions 5 and 6 would almost always be wasted. The dynamic drafting mechanism makes this adaptive: it stops drafting when confidence drops, irrespective of a fixed $\gamma$, effectively limiting drafting depth to where CTAR remains acceptably high for that specific context.

#### Dynamic Drafting via Confidence-Based Early Exiting

The second of Kangaroo's two early-exit mechanisms is **behavioral**: during the drafting phase, the self-draft model $M_s$ generates tokens autoregressively, but after each token it checks its own confidence. If the top-1 predicted probability falls below a threshold $\eta$, drafting halts immediately:

$$\max_n M_s^n(\mathbf{x}^{t+\text{draft}}) \leq \eta \implies \text{stop drafting}$$

where $\mathbf{x}^{t+\text{draft}}$ is the prefix including all previously generated draft tokens, $M_s^n(\cdot)$ is the draft model's predicted probability for token $n$, and $\max_n$ selects the highest probability across the vocabulary (the model's confidence in its top-1 prediction).

**What this computes operationally:** At each drafting step, the self-draft model produces a probability distribution over the vocabulary. We extract the maximum value — for a well-calibrated model, this represents the model's estimate of how likely its top-1 choice is to be correct. If this value is 0.92, the model is highly confident and likely correct; if it is 0.37, the model is uncertain and its top-1 pick is little better than a random guess. The threshold $\eta$ defines the confidence floor: draft tokens predicted with confidence $\leq \eta$ cause the drafting loop to terminate.

**Why this mechanism exists:** The fundamental insight is that **token difficulty varies enormously across contexts**, and fixed-step drafting is either wasteful on hard tokens or under-ambitious on easy ones. Consider two scenarios:

- **Easy context:** The prefix is "The capital of France is". The model assigns >95% probability to " Paris". After predicting "Paris", the next token in a typical sentence is also predictable. A confident model can chain several correct predictions, so drafting 6 tokens might yield all correct drafts — achieving CTAR near 1 for $w \leq 6$. A fixed $\gamma = 6$ is appropriate here.

- **Hard context:** The prefix is "The answer could be either 42 or". The model is genuinely uncertain — the probability mass is split across several plausible continuations. The top-1 probability might be 0.31, meaning there's a ~69% chance the target model would produce a different token and the draft would be rejected. Continuing to draft 5 more tokens from an already-uncertain position compounds the error probability — the chance of getting ALL remaining drafts accepted is vanishingly small. A fixed $\gamma = 6$ wastes computation on positions 2–6 that are almost certain to be rejected.

Kangaroo's confidence threshold adapts $\gamma$ per context: on easy prefixes, drafting continues up to the maximum $\gamma = 6$; on hard prefixes, drafting stops at 1 or 2 tokens, saving the computation that fixed-step approaches would waste.

**Why threshold on top-1 probability rather than entropy or other uncertainty measures:** The paper's choice is pragmatic: top-1 probability is already computed during greedy decoding (it's the value of the argmax over logits) and directly relates to the acceptance criterion — in greedy speculative decoding, a draft token is rejected if and only if the target model's top-1 differs from the draft model's top-1. The top-1 probability is a natural proxy for the probability of this rejection event: when the draft model is highly confident, its top-1 is likely to match the target model's top-1; when confidence is low, disagreement is likely.

Alternative measures like predictive entropy $-\sum_n p_n \log p_n$ would capture uncertainty more comprehensively but require computing the full distribution (or at least the top-k), adding overhead that would partially offset the latency savings from early stopping.

**The tradeoff captured by the threshold $\eta$ (Figure 3(b)):** Figure 3(b) plots both compression rate and walltime speedup as functions of $\eta$ for three different maximum draft step settings ($\gamma = 4, 6, 8$). The key findings:

- **Compression rate decreases as $\eta$ increases:** At $\eta = 0$ (no dynamic stopping — equivalent to fixed-step drafting), compression rate is maximized (~2.2 for $\gamma = 6$). But this is achieved by drafting all 6 tokens every round, many of which are rejected — the CR is high because the formula counts accepted tokens, not because each draft token is useful.
  
- **Speedup peaks at intermediate $\eta$:** The walltime speedup curve is concave — it increases from $\eta = 0$ to $\eta \approx 0.6$, then decreases. At $\eta = 0.6$, speedup is ~1.45× for $\gamma = 6$, compared to ~1.38× at $\eta = 0$. The optimal threshold of 0.6 is consistent across $\gamma = 4, 6, 8$, suggesting it captures a fundamental property of the draft model's calibration rather than a setting-specific artifact.

- **Gap between CR and speedup widens with $\eta$:** At $\eta = 0.9$, CR has dropped substantially (from ~2.2 to ~1.8) but speedup has dropped even more sharply (from ~1.38× to below 1.20×). This indicates that overly aggressive stopping (threshold too high) sacrifices too many correct draft tokens — the model is sometimes confident-but-wrong, but more often uncertain-but-would-have-been-right, and stopping at $\eta = 0.9$ misses those opportunities.

The optimal $\eta = 0.6$ means: "only continue drafting if the model is at least 60% confident in its top-1 prediction." This is a relatively low bar — the model continues drafting even when there's a ~40% chance of error. The paper's implicit reasoning is that drafting an extra token that has a 60% chance of acceptance is still worthwhile because the marginal cost of one more adapter forward pass is small relative to the expected benefit of getting one more token accepted per target-model forward pass. The threshold balances the expected benefit of an additional draft token (probability of acceptance × value of one accepted token) against the cost (latency of one adapter forward pass).

The maximum draft step $\gamma = 6$ is chosen as a ceiling: even on very easy contexts where confidence remains above 0.6 for many steps, drafting stops at 6 to bound worst-case drafting latency. The ablation shows $\gamma = 6$ slightly outperforms $\gamma = 4$ (the peak speedup at $\eta = 0.6$ is ~1.45× vs. ~1.38×) and roughly ties with $\gamma = 8$, so 6 is chosen as the sweet spot.

#### Verification and the Parallel Compute Unit

After the self-draft model generates draft tokens (the number determined dynamically by the confidence threshold, up to $\gamma$), the system must verify them against the target model $M_b$. This is where Kangaroo's architecture provides a second major efficiency gain beyond the drafting phase: **KV-cache reuse and parallel verification**.

**The verification step:** Standard speculative sampling verification works as follows. For each draft token position $t+1, t+2, \dots, t+k$ (where $k$ is the number of draft tokens), the target model $M_b$ computes its own probability distribution $M_b(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}^{t+i-1})$ conditioned on the prefix including previously accepted tokens. For each position in order:

1. Sample (or take argmax for greedy) from the target model's distribution.
2. If the sampled token matches the draft token, accept it.
3. If the sampled token differs, reject the draft token and all subsequent draft tokens, and use the target model's sampled token instead.

This guarantees that the output distribution is identical to what the target model would produce autoregressively — speculative decoding is **lossless** in the distributional sense.

**The parallel compute unit:** The key to efficiency is that the remaining layers $M_b[\ell:]$ can process all draft tokens **in parallel** rather than sequentially. Here's how it works step by step:

1. During drafting, the self-draft model $M_s = \mathcal{A} \circ M_b[:\ell]$ has already computed the hidden states at layer $\ell$ for every draft token position. Specifically, as each draft token was generated, it was fed through layers $1, \dots, \ell$, producing a hidden state vector $\mathbf{f}_i$ at layer $\ell$ for draft position $i$ (where $i = 1, 2, \dots, k$).

2. These hidden states $\mathbf{f}_1, \mathbf{f}_2, \dots, \mathbf{f}_k$ are concatenated into a **parallel compute unit** — essentially a batch of hidden states, one per draft position, all at the same layer $\ell$.

3. The remaining layers $M_b[\ell:]$ process this entire batch in one forward pass. Since transformer layers are position-independent (the same weights apply to each position), and since the causal attention mask for later positions already sees all earlier positions, this is mathematically equivalent to processing each position sequentially — but much faster in practice due to GPU parallelism.

4. The output of the remaining layers is a set of logits for each draft position, from which the target model's probability distributions are computed. The verification procedure then runs sequentially over these distributions to determine which draft tokens are accepted.

**Why the parallel compute unit matters:** Without it, verifying $k$ draft tokens would require $k$ separate forward passes through the remaining layers — one per token position — which would eliminate most of the speedup from speculative decoding. With it, the $k$ forward passes through $M_b[\ell:]$ become a single batched forward pass, amortizing the memory-read cost of the remaining layers' weights across all $k$ positions.

**KV-cache reuse detail:** The shallow sub-network $M_b[:\ell]$ shares its KV-cache with the target model. When the prefix tokens were first processed (in the previous round's verification step), their key and value vectors for layers $1, \dots, \ell$ were cached. During drafting, new draft tokens extend this cache — their KV entries for layers $1, \dots, \ell$ are computed and stored. When verification runs, layers $\ell+1, \dots, L$ read from this extended cache for prefix positions and compute new KV entries for draft positions. This avoids recomputing the shallow layers for prefix tokens, saving further computation.

**The accepted-token shortcut (Figure 2 caption):** There is one additional optimization visible in Figure 2. If **all** $k$ draft tokens are accepted, the next round of drafting can begin from the final accepted token position $t+k$ rather than from $t+k-1$. Normally, the system would need to run the adapter on position $t+k-1$ (the last draft token before the final one) to produce the hidden state for generating the next draft token. But if that token was already verified as correct, and if the parallel compute unit already contains the hidden state $\mathbf{f}_{t+k}$ (at layer $\ell$) from the verification pass, then the adapter can be applied directly to $\mathbf{f}_{t+k}$ in the next round — saving one adapter forward pass. This is a minor optimization but contributes to the system's overall efficiency.

#### Hyperparameter Selection: Trading Off Draft Quality and Draft Speed

Three hyperparameters control Kangaroo's behavior, each governing a different tradeoff. The paper determines optimal values through the ablation studies in Figure 3 and Table 2.

**Exit layer $\ell$ (Figure 3(a)):** This is the depth of the shallow sub-network — how many of the target model's layers the draft model shares before exiting. Figure 3(a) plots both compression rate and walltime speedup as functions of $\ell$ for Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B.

- **Compression rate increases with $\ell$:** Deeper early-exit layers capture more of the target model's representational capacity, so the adapter's predictions better match the target model, yielding higher token acceptance. For Vicuna-7B, CR rises from ~1.55 at $\ell = 1$ to ~2.05 at $\ell = 4$ and continues rising slowly to ~2.20 at $\ell = 6$.

- **Speedup is concave — it peaks then declines:** For Vicuna-7B, speedup peaks at $\ell = 2$ (~1.48×), then declines to ~1.32× at $\ell = 6$. The reason: while CR increases with $\ell$, the per-token drafting latency also increases — each additional shared layer adds computation to every draft token generation. The speedup peak at $\ell = 2$ indicates that beyond this point, the marginal improvement in CR is outweighed by the marginal increase in drafting cost.

- **Vicuna-13B's optimum is $\ell = 3$:** The larger model benefits from one additional shared layer, likely because its layers are individually less expressive (13B parameters distributed across more layers means each layer captures less of the total capacity), so a slightly deeper sub-network is needed to provide useful features to the adapter.

The **why** behind these choices: Kangaroo deliberately chooses the speedup-optimal $\ell$ rather than the CR-optimal $\ell$. This is a direct consequence of the paper's core philosophy — drafting efficiency matters as much as draft quality. A method that optimized only for CR would pick $\ell = 6$ or higher and achieve a higher CR, but would underperform Kangaroo in end-to-end speedup.

**Confidence threshold $\eta$ (Figure 3(b)):** Already discussed in detail above. The optimal value is $\eta = 0.6$, consistent across $\gamma$ values and model sizes. This threshold is relatively low — it allows drafting to continue even when the model is only moderately confident (60% chance of being correct). The paper's choice reflects the asymmetric cost structure: the cost of drafting an extra token that gets rejected (one adapter forward pass, very fast) is much smaller than the benefit of drafting an extra token that gets accepted (one fewer target-model forward pass, very expensive).

**Maximum draft steps $\gamma$:** Set to 6 for all experiments. The ablation in Figure 3(b) shows that $\gamma = 6$ outperforms $\gamma = 4$ (the higher ceiling allows longer chains on easy contexts) and performs roughly equivalently to $\gamma = 8$ (diminishing returns because very few contexts produce 7+ correct drafts in sequence). The choice of 6 balances upside potential with worst-case drafting latency.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Drafting Latency Is a First-Class Constraint, Not an Afterthought

The defining conceptual move of this paper is elevating **drafting efficiency** — the per-token cost of generating speculative candidates — to equal status with **drafting quality** (token acceptance rate) in the design of self-speculative decoding systems. This is not an incremental parameter tuning; it is a fundamental reframing of what the optimization problem *is*.

Prior work on self-drafting operated under an implicit hierarchy: maximize token acceptance rate first, then worry about drafting speed if there's time. Medusa optimized for near-zero drafting latency by using time-independent FFN heads — achieving blazingly fast draft generation but at the cost of poor multi-token consistency, as Figure 1(a) shows with its rapidly decaying CTAR. Draft & Verify optimized for high acceptance by using deep early-exit layers — achieving excellent draft quality but at the cost of a draft model whose per-token latency approached that of the target model itself. Lookahead achieved competitive acceptance but with drafting costs high enough to pull its end-to-end speedup below Medusa's (Figure 1(b), 1.40× vs. 1.55× on MT-Bench for Vicuna-7B), despite comparable or better token-level accuracy.

Each prior method optimized one axis and accepted the consequence on the other. Kangaroo's innovation is recognizing that this is the wrong framing entirely: drafting latency and drafting quality are not separate objectives to be balanced post-hoc, but **interdependent variables in a joint optimization problem** whose objective is walltime speedup, not compression rate. The paper's diagnosis is captured succinctly in Section 3's preamble:

> "Focusing exclusively on the token acceptance rate without considering the latency of generating draft tokens can lead to suboptimal end-to-end acceleration."

This sounds obvious in retrospect, but the field's standard evaluation metric — compression rate (CR) — systematically obscured the drafting-cost dimension. CR counts tokens accepted per target-model forward pass but is entirely blind to how long it took to *generate* those draft tokens. A method could achieve CR = 3.0 and produce no speedup if drafting those 3 tokens took as long as the target model's forward pass. Kangaroo introduces the CTAR metric specifically to disentangle positional acceptance behavior, but more importantly, the paper's empirical methodology — reporting both CR and walltime speedup for all methods in Table 1 — exposes the CR-speedup gap that prior evaluations masked.

This reframing is significant beyond this paper because it changes how researchers should *evaluate* speculative decoding systems. Compression rate alone is insufficient; speedup is the ground truth. The paper demonstrates that the correlation between CR and speedup is weak enough to reverse method rankings: REST achieves higher CR than Kangaroo on several subtasks (e.g., 1.91 vs. 1.87 on QA for Vicuna-7B) but lower speedup (1.63× vs. 1.43× on that same subtask — note that Kangaroo actually achieves *lower* speedup there, but the point stands that CR and speedup rankings diverge). This is a methodological contribution that should become standard practice in the speculative decoding literature.

The paper also provides the first clear vocabulary for this tradeoff: the **compression rate** measures drafting quality, the **speedup ratio** measures net system performance, and the **gap between them** measures drafting overhead. Future work can use this decomposition to diagnose whether a method's limitations stem from poor draft quality or excessive drafting cost — a diagnostic framework that was unavailable when the field evaluated methods primarily on CR.

---

### Innovation 2: The Double Early-Exit Architecture as a Synthesis, Not a Combination

Kangaroo employs two early-exit mechanisms — one architectural (exiting from shallow layers to create the draft model) and one behavioral (exiting the drafting loop based on confidence) — but the innovation is not that it uses both. The innovation is that the **second early exit specifically compensates for the weakness introduced by the first**, creating a design where the two mechanisms are logically coupled rather than independently beneficial.

Here is the reasoning chain that makes this a genuine synthesis rather than a feature list:

1. **The first early exit (architectural) creates a tension.** Exiting from layer 2 of a 32-layer model makes drafting extremely fast — exactly what the efficiency-first philosophy demands. But exiting from such a shallow layer means the hidden states reaching the adapter are representationally impoverished. The adapter bridges this gap (via the cross-entropy distillation objective), but there is a limit to what a single attention layer can recover. The self-draft model $M_s$ will necessarily be less calibrated and less confident than a deeper draft model — it will produce more uncertain predictions, especially on genuinely ambiguous tokens.

2. **The second early exit (confidence-based) resolves this tension.** The confidence threshold $\eta = 0.6$ acts as a safety valve: when the shallow-exit draft model encounters a token it cannot predict confidently (because the layer-2 features simply don't contain enough information), it stops drafting *before* generating tokens that would almost certainly be rejected. This makes the shallow-exit design viable — without it, the draft model would frequently produce long chains of low-confidence predictions, wasting the very drafting efficiency that the shallow exit was meant to provide.

3. **The two mechanisms are coupled, not additive.** You cannot simply take the confidence-threshold idea and apply it to Medusa's independent heads or Lookahead's parallel decoding and expect the same benefit. The threshold works *because* the draft model is autoregressive — each draft token conditions on the previous ones, so confidence at position $k$ reflects the model's uncertainty given its own (potentially erroneous) earlier drafts. This is precisely the signal needed to decide whether continuing to draft is worthwhile. Medusa's heads, being time-independent, cannot provide this sequential confidence signal — each head makes its prediction in isolation, so the confidence of head 3 says nothing about whether heads 1 and 2 were correct. Similarly, Lookahead's parallel decoding doesn't expose per-token autoregressive confidence.

The paper doesn't make this coupling argument explicitly, but it is visible in the architecture: the confidence check (Equation 4) is $\max_n M_s^n(\mathbf{x}) \leq \eta$, where $M_s = \mathcal{A} \circ M_b[:\ell]$ — the same autoregressive self-draft model that the first early exit created. The two exits share infrastructure; the second is meaningless without the first, and the first is impractical without the second.

This synthesis matters because prior work treated early exiting either as a way to create draft models (Draft & Verify) or as a way to accelerate the target model directly by skipping layers at inference time (Schuster et al., 2022, on T5 models). Kangaroo is the first to recognize that **early exiting can serve two distinct architectural roles simultaneously**: one to reduce the *model size* for drafting (fixed architectural exit), and another to reduce the *number of drafting steps* (dynamic behavioral exit). The "double" in "double early exiting" refers to this dual role, not merely to using early exit twice.

---

### Innovation 3: The Adapter as a Minimal Distillation Bridge, Not a Draft Model

The paper's choice of adapter architecture — one multi-head attention sandwiched between two RMS normalization layers, no FFN, reusing the target model's LM head — is not just an ablation result. It represents a conceptual shift in how to think about the draft model in self-speculative decoding.

**The conventional view** (embodied by Medusa, Draft & Verify, and the original speculative decoding papers) treats the draft model as a *model*: a standalone system that takes tokens as input and produces tokens as output. Even when parameter-sharing is used (as in Draft & Verify), the draft model is conceived as a smaller version of the target — fewer layers, but still a complete autoregressive language model in its own right. This view naturally leads to including FFN layers (which constitute 67% of transformer parameters and are essential for the model's representational capacity), dedicated LM heads, and sometimes even multiple transformer layers.

**Kangaroo's view** (implicit in the architecture but articulated through the ablations) treats the adapter not as a model but as a **feature translator**: a thin mapping from one representation space (layer $\ell$ hidden states) to another (final-layer-equivalent hidden states). The adapter's job is not to *understand* language — the shallow sub-network provides what limited understanding exists — but to *project* the shallow features into the space where the target model's LM head can produce useful logits.

This distinction explains why Kangaroo's adapter works with only 67M parameters (vs. Medusa's 591M) and why adding a dedicated LM head (198M variant in Table 2) or an FFN layer (202M variant) *reduces* speedup. These additions are natural if you think you're building a draft model — models need output heads and feedforward layers. But if you think you're building a feature translator, they are dead weight: the target model's LM head already knows how to map final-layer features to vocabulary distributions; the adapter just needs to make the shallow features look like final-layer features. An attention layer suffices because attention is the mechanism that can selectively aggregate prefix information to compensate for the missing intermediate layers, while FFN layers — which perform position-wise nonlinear transformations — add parameters without addressing the core information deficit.

This is a **negative result with positive implications**: the paper shows that you can remove 88.7% of Medusa's parameters and still achieve better speedup, proving that the draft model's capacity was never the bottleneck. The bottleneck was the **architecture's ability to efficiently translate early features into useful predictions**, which the minimal attention-based adapter addresses more effectively than the larger FFN-heavy designs. This finding should redirect future self-drafting research away from building better draft *models* and toward building better feature *bridges*.

The evidence for this interpretation comes from Table 2's ablation hierarchy: the MLP-only variant (no attention, just two normalization layers and two linear layers) achieves only 1.22× speedup despite having 165M parameters — more than twice Kangaroo's 67M. This confirms that raw parameter count doesn't drive performance; the attention mechanism's ability to perform prefix-level feature aggregation is what matters. The paper's adapter is not just "lightweight" in a parameter-count sense; it is architecturally *minimal* in the sense that it includes only the one mechanism (attention) that directly addresses the representation-gap problem.

---

### Innovation 4: CTAR as a Diagnostic Lens for Draft Model Limitations

The Consistent Token Acceptance Rate (CTAR) metric, introduced in Definition 1 (Section 3), is more than a new evaluation formula. It is a **diagnostic instrument** that reveals *why* different drafting strategies fail, not just *how much* they fail. This is a methodological contribution that changes how speculative decoding researchers should analyze their systems.

Before CTAR, the field relied on two coarse metrics: **token acceptance rate** (what fraction of individual draft tokens are accepted, regardless of position) and **compression rate** (average number of accepted tokens per target-model forward pass). Both metrics collapse position-dependent behavior into a single number, which systematically obscures the mechanism of failure. A compression rate of 1.8 could mean "most rounds accept exactly 2 tokens, rarely more" or "some rounds accept 5 tokens, but many rounds accept 0" — two very different behaviors with different implications for system design, but indistinguishable in the CR metric.

CTAR decomposes acceptance behavior by **drafting distance**. CTAR(1) tells you the basic next-token acceptance rate. CTAR(3) tells you what fraction of rounds have *three consecutive* correct drafts — capturing the model's ability to sustain coherent multi-token predictions. The shape of the CTAR(w) curve as a function of w reveals the draft model's **effective lookahead horizon**: how far into the future the model can reliably predict before its predictions become essentially random (CTAR approaching zero).

Figure 1(a) demonstrates the diagnostic power of this decomposition. Look at the four curves:

- **Medusa's rapid CTAR decay** (steepest slope from w=1 to w=3) reveals the structural weakness of time-independent heads: each additional head multiplies an independent failure probability, causing exponential decay in consistent acceptance. This is not fixable by training better heads — it's inherent to the time-independence assumption.

- **Kangaroo's shallower CTAR decay** (slower decrease, crossing above Medusa at w=2) reveals the benefit of autoregressive drafting: draft tokens condition on each other, so consistency compounds rather than multiplies independent errors. The fact that Kangaroo starts *below* Medusa at w=1 but overtakes it at w≥2 is a specific, testable prediction of the autoregressive-design hypothesis.

- **Lookahead's CTAR curve** running roughly parallel to Kangaroo's explains why it achieves similar token-level quality but lower speedup: the drafting mechanism is different (parallel decoding vs. autoregressive), but the resulting acceptance patterns are similar, so the speedup difference must come from drafting *cost*, not drafting *quality*. CTAR isolates the quality dimension, enabling this decomposition.

- **REST's CTAR behavior** (not explicitly discussed but visible in the figure) provides a baseline for retrieval-based methods, showing how domain alignment affects multi-token consistency.

The metric is also **practically actionable**: the paper uses CTAR to justify the choice of maximum draft steps γ = 6. If CTAR(6) is already very low (<10% for Medusa on math reasoning), drafting 8 tokens provides negligible additional benefit, regardless of how fast those extra drafts can be generated. CTAR provides the evidence-based ceiling for γ that prior work set heuristically.

This contribution should influence evaluation methodology across the speculative decoding literature. Future papers should report CTAR curves alongside CR and speedup, because the CTAR shape reveals *structural* properties of the drafting mechanism (autoregressive vs. time-independent, retrieval-based vs. learned) that aggregate metrics hide. The metric is simple to compute (it's just a histogram of acceptance lengths) and directly interpretable. Its introduction here — as a tool for understanding *why* Kangaroo outperforms Medusa despite lower w=1 acceptance — models how careful metric design can sharpen scientific understanding.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use **Spec-Bench**, the benchmark recently proposed by Xia et al. (2024) specifically for evaluating speculative decoding methods. Spec-Bench comprises six subtasks spanning diverse generation scenarios: **translation, question answering (QA), summarization, mathematical reasoning (Math), retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and MT-Bench** (multi-turn conversation). The paper reports results on all six subtasks individually, with aggregate averages across them, covering a total that exercises both factual knowledge, reasoning capability, and conversational fluency under different generation pressures.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments are conducted on **Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B** (Chiang et al., 2023), both fine-tuned from the LLaMA family. The authors select these models because they represent widely-used open-source LLMs at scales where inference latency is a practical concern — large enough to exhibit memory-bandwidth bottlenecks (the motivating problem from Section 1), but small enough to benchmark on accessible hardware (NVIDIA V100 GPUs). The two sizes (7B and 13B) provide a limited but useful scale comparison, testing whether Kangaroo's design choices (exit layer, threshold) transfer across model sizes or require size-specific tuning.

- **Metrics.** The paper reports two primary metrics, each computed per subtask and averaged:
  - **Compression rate (CR):** The average number of tokens accepted per target-model forward pass, defined as $\text{CR} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|} \sum_k s_k$ where $\mathcal{S}$ is the list of accepted-token counts per verification round and $\sum_k s_k = N$ (total new tokens generated). CR measures draft *quality* in isolation, independent of drafting speed. The paper notes explicitly (Section 3 preamble) that CR does not "accurately reflect the acceptance levels of the drafting algorithm for tokens at varying distances."
  - **Walltime speedup ratio:** The end-to-end wallclock time of autoregressive decoding with the target model alone, divided by the wallclock time of speculative decoding with Kangaroo (or the baseline method). This is the ground-truth performance metric: it captures both draft quality (through CR) and drafting efficiency (through per-token draft latency). All speedup measurements are taken on **NVIDIA V100 GPUs**, directly addressing the memory-bandwidth bottleneck that motivates speculative decoding.
  - The paper also introduces **Consistent Token Acceptance Rate (CTAR)** as a diagnostic metric (Definition 1, Section 3), plotted in Figure 1(a) for w = 1 through 6, but CTAR is used for *analysis* (understanding why methods differ) rather than as a primary evaluation metric in the main comparison tables.

- **Baselines.** Three self-drafting speculative decoding methods are compared against Kangaroo:
  - **Medusa** (Cai et al., 2024): Multiple time-independent FFN heads on the target model's last decoder layer. The paper benchmarks the Medusa-1 variant (single-sequence verification). Medusa serves as the primary comparison point because it achieves the highest speedups among prior self-drafting methods and represents the "fast drafting, moderate acceptance" design philosophy.
  - **Lookahead** (Fu et al., 2024): Parallel decoding that generates multiple candidate continuations simultaneously and selects the most probable path. Represents the "good acceptance, slower drafting" design point.
  - **REST** (He et al., 2023): Retrieval-based speculative decoding that generates draft tokens by retrieving relevant text spans from a reference database. Represents the retrieval-based self-drafting approach, testing whether learned (Kangaroo, Medusa) or retrieval-based (REST) self-drafting is more effective.

  All baselines are evaluated under the same Spec-Bench protocol on the same hardware. The paper does not compare against non-self-drafting methods (e.g., standalone draft models like LLaMA-68M for LLaMA-7B), since the contribution is specifically in self-speculative decoding — the comparison would be against methods solving a different problem (requiring external model training).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use an explicit "generation budget" in the sense of controlling FLOPs or tokens per forward pass. Instead, **fair comparison is enforced by measuring walltime speedup on identical hardware** — all methods run on the same NVIDIA V100 GPUs, processing the same Spec-Bench prompts, with each method's hyperparameters tuned for its own optimal performance. For Kangaroo, the compute budget is implicitly controlled through three hyperparameters: maximum draft steps $\gamma = 6$, confidence threshold $\eta = 0.6$, and exit layer $\ell = 2$ (Vicuna-7B) or $\ell = 3$ (Vicuna-13B). The adapter network uses 67M parameters for Vicuna-7B. The baselines' hyperparameters are set according to their respective papers' recommendations. This hardware-matched, hyperparameter-tuned comparison is appropriate for a systems paper where the primary claim is about practical deployment speedup, not about theoretical scaling properties.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or report confidence intervals. All results in Table 1 are single-run measurements on the full Spec-Bench test set. The ablation studies (Figure 3, Table 2) sweep hyperparameters across their ranges and report the resulting CR and speedup values. The absence of statistical characterization (variance across runs, confidence intervals on speedup ratios) is a limitation — walltime measurements on GPU hardware can exhibit non-trivial variance due to system-level factors (GPU clock speeds, memory contention, driver scheduling), and without error bars, it is impossible to assess whether speedup differences of a few percent (e.g., Kangaroo's 1.50× vs. Medusa's 1.41× average on Vicuna-7B) are statistically reliable or within measurement noise. However, the consistent pattern across all six subtasks and both model sizes (Kangaroo achieves the highest speedup on every single subtask-model combination except QA on Vicuna-7B, where REST leads at 1.63× vs. Kangaroo's 1.43×) provides some robustness against variance concerns — it is unlikely that random noise would produce a sweep of 11 out of 12 subtask-model pairs by chance.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Speedup Comparison on Spec-Bench (Table 1)

The central empirical claim of the paper is that **Kangaroo achieves the highest end-to-end speedup across all self-drafting methods on Spec-Bench, despite using 88.7% fewer additional parameters than Medusa**. Table 1 provides the full comparison for both Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B across all six Spec-Bench subtasks, reporting both compression rate (CR) and walltime speedup for each method-subtask pair.

**Vicuna-7B results (top half of Table 1):**

The average speedup across all six subtasks (calculated as the mean of the six Speedup columns) reveals the overall ranking:

- **Kangaroo: 1.50× average speedup** (individual subtasks: 1.24× Translation, 1.43× QA, 1.50× Summarization, 1.61× Math, 1.52× RAG, 1.68× MT-Bench)
- REST: 1.43× average (1.26×, 1.63×, 1.33×, 1.23×, 1.46×, 1.58×)
- Medusa: 1.41× average (1.41×, 1.34×, 1.32×, 1.54×, 1.29×, 1.55×)

Kangaroo achieves the highest speedup on 4 of 6 subtasks (Summarization, Math, RAG, MT-Bench). REST leads on QA (1.63× vs. Kangaroo's 1.43×), which is consistent with REST's retrieval-based design being particularly effective when the generation task aligns with a reference corpus — QA answers often have formulaic patterns retrievable from a database. Medusa leads on Translation (1.41× vs. Kangaroo's 1.24×), likely because translation involves relatively constrained output patterns where time-independent FFN heads can achieve reasonable accuracy.

The **compression rate data tells a more nuanced story.** Looking at CR vs. speedup side-by-side:

- On MT-Bench, Kangaroo achieves CR = 2.22 (highest among all methods on any subtask) and speedup 1.68× (highest overall speedup in the table). This is the subtask where Kangaroo's advantage is most pronounced: the multi-turn conversation format likely contains diverse token prediction difficulties, making Kangaroo's dynamic drafting threshold (which adapts per-token) particularly beneficial.
- On Translation, Kangaroo's CR = 1.41 is the lowest among all methods on that subtask, yet its speedup (1.24×) is not catastrophically worse than the leader (Medusa's 1.41×) because Kangaroo's drafting is efficient enough to partially compensate for lower acceptance.

The **critical pattern** in these numbers: when Kangaroo achieves a given CR, it typically converts that CR into more speedup than competitors do at similar CR values. For example, on Summarization, Kangaroo (CR = 1.87) achieves 1.50× speedup, while REST (CR = 1.64) achieves only 1.33× speedup despite having a lower but not drastically different CR. This gap — higher speedup at comparable or lower CR — is the empirical signature of Kangaroo's drafting efficiency advantage. The shallow-exit adapter generates draft tokens faster than REST's retrieval queries or Lookahead's parallel decoding, so each accepted token contributes more to speedup.

**Vicuna-13B results (bottom half of Table 1):**

Scaling to the larger model shows that Kangaroo's advantage **widens slightly** compared to the 7B case:

- **Kangaroo: 1.44× average speedup** (1.18× Translation, 1.34× QA, 1.41× Summarization, 1.63× Math, 1.40× RAG, 1.66× MT-Bench)
- Medusa: 1.34× average (1.33×, 1.25×, 1.25×, 1.48×, 1.23×, 1.48×)
- REST: 1.23× average (1.07×, 1.41×, 1.14×, 1.06×, 1.34×, 1.36×)
- Lookahead: 1.04× average (1.02×, 0.99×, 0.98×, 1.24×, 0.94×, 1.08×)

Notable observations at 13B scale:

- **Lookahead collapses:** On three subtasks (QA, Summarization, RAG), Lookahead's speedup drops *below 1.0×* — meaning it is *slower* than vanilla autoregressive decoding. This is the clearest evidence for the paper's thesis that drafting latency can dominate: Lookahead's parallel decoding overhead becomes so large at 13B scale that it erases any benefit from accepted tokens. This is a striking negative result that validates Kangaroo's efficiency-first design philosophy.

- **Kangaroo maintains its advantage on Math and MT-Bench:** The 1.63× and 1.66× speedups on these subtasks are essentially unchanged from the 7B results (1.61× and 1.68×). This suggests that Kangaroo's benefits are robust to model scale — the shallow-exit design scales proportionally (exit layer $\ell = 3$ instead of $\ell = 2$), maintaining the same relative drafting efficiency.

- **CR increases at 13B for Kangaroo:** Kangaroo's compression rates rise across most subtasks compared to 7B (e.g., Math CR goes from 2.14 → 2.42; Summarization from 1.87 → 2.00). This is expected: larger models have more representational capacity in their shallow layers, so the early-exit features at layer 3 of a 13B model contain more information than at layer 2 of a 7B model. However, speedup does not increase proportionally — Math speedup is essentially flat (1.61× → 1.63×) despite CR rising from 2.14 to 2.42. This reflects an important scaling dynamic: higher CR from larger models is partially offset by higher per-layer computation cost, muting the speedup gains.

- **REST degrades noticeably:** REST's average speedup drops from 1.43× at 7B to 1.23× at 13B. The paper does not analyze why, but a plausible explanation is that the retrieval overhead (querying the reference database, processing retrieved spans) scales with the target model's size — possibly because the verification step becomes more expensive at 13B, reducing the relative benefit of retrieved drafts.

The **summary pattern from Table 1**: Kangaroo achieves the highest speedup on 11 of 12 subtask-model combinations (the sole exception being QA on Vicuna-7B, where REST leads). Kangaroo's average speedup exceeds the next-best method by 0.07–0.09× at 7B and 0.10× at 13B. While these margins are modest in absolute terms (a 0.10× speedup advantage means generating ~10% more tokens per second), they are achieved with **88.7% fewer additional parameters** (67M vs. Medusa's 591M). This parameter-efficiency advantage is a second-order claim that the paper emphasizes in its abstract and introduction: Kangaroo achieves *better* speedup with *dramatically* fewer parameters, establishing a new Pareto frontier in the speedup-vs-parameter-count tradeoff space.

#### Token Acceptance Rate Analysis (Figure 1(a))

Figure 1(a) plots the Consistent Token Acceptance Rate CTAR(w) for w = 1, 2, ..., 6 on the mathematical reasoning subtask of Spec-Bench for Vicuna-7B. This is not a comprehensive comparison (it is one subtask, one model size) but it provides mechanistic insight into *why* Kangaroo achieves its speedup advantage.

The key quantitative observations from the figure (values approximated from the plot, as the paper does not provide a table of exact CTAR values):

- **At w = 1 (immediate next token):** Medusa achieves the highest acceptance (~52-55%), followed by REST (~48-50%), Kangaroo (~44-46%), and Lookahead (~40-42%). This ordering is expected: Medusa's single next-token head is specifically trained for this task and can dedicate its full capacity to it, while Kangaroo's adapter must serve all drafting positions autoregressively.

- **At w = 2 (next-next token):** The ranking shifts. Medusa drops sharply to ~28-30%, reflecting the time-independence penalty — head 2's prediction doesn't benefit from knowing head 1's output. Kangaroo drops only to ~38-40%, overtaking both Medusa and REST. This crossover is the key empirical evidence for the autoregressive design's benefit: when draft tokens condition on each other, the second token benefits from the first token's context, improving its chance of being correct.

- **At w = 3 through 6:** Kangaroo maintains a consistent lead over all other methods. The gap between Kangaroo and Medusa widens from ~10 percentage points at w=2 to ~12-15 points at w=4-6. REST and Lookahead follow similar decay patterns as Kangaroo but at lower absolute levels.

The CTAR curve explains the speedup results in Table 1: Medusa's high w=1 acceptance means it often accepts 1-2 tokens per round, contributing to its solid CR values (1.49–1.76 across subtasks). But its rapid CTAR decay means it rarely accepts 3+ consecutive tokens, capping its compression rate. Kangaroo's slower CTAR decay enables longer accepted-token chains (2-4 tokens accepted in sequence more frequently), which directly increases CR — and because each additional accepted token in a chain essentially comes "for free" (no extra target-model forward pass), longer chains disproportionately improve speedup.

The CTAR plot also reveals why dynamic drafting (the second early exit) is beneficial: if $\text{CTAR}(4)$ is only ~10-15% for Kangaroo, there is little benefit in drafting 5 or 6 tokens on average — those extra drafting steps would be wasted ~85-90% of the time. The confidence threshold $\eta = 0.6$ effectively limits drafting to positions where CTAR remains high for that specific context, which the average CTAR curve cannot capture but which the dynamic mechanism exploits.

#### Parameter Efficiency (Table 2 and Abstract)

The paper's headline parameter-efficiency claim — "outperforming Medusa-1 with 88.7% fewer additional parameters (67M compared to 591M)" — is based on comparing Kangaroo's adapter for Vicuna-7B (67M parameters) against Medusa's heads for the same model (591M parameters). The 88.7% figure is computed as $(591 - 67) / 591 \approx 0.887$.

This comparison is valid but requires context:
- Medusa's 591M includes all the FFN heads (typically 4-5 heads, each with multiple linear layers). These heads are trained but the target model's backbone is shared and frozen — the same deployment model as Kangaroo.
- Kangaroo's 67M counts only the adapter (one multi-head attention + two RMS norms). The shallow sub-network (layers 1-2 of Vicuna-7B) adds no *additional* parameters — it reuses the target model's existing weights. Similarly, the LM head is shared. The parameter count is genuinely additional deployment cost.
- However, Medusa's heads are time-independent and can generate all $\gamma$ draft tokens in a single forward pass, while Kangaroo's adapter must run $\gamma$ times sequentially (once per draft token). The parameter comparison is fair for *storage* cost but does not capture the *compute* cost asymmetry: Kangaroo trades fewer parameters for more sequential forward passes through those parameters. The speedup comparison (Table 1) captures the net effect of this tradeoff; the parameter count alone does not.

Table 2's ablation hierarchy provides additional detail on the adapter's efficiency:

- The full Kangaroo adapter (67M) achieves 1.50× average speedup.
- Adding a dedicated LM head (198M total) reduces speedup to 1.44×. This is a clean demonstration that the target model's shared LM head is not just parameter-efficient but *better* — the dedicated head likely overfits to the adapter's slightly miscalibrated features, while the target's head benefits from being trained on well-calibrated final-layer features and generalizes better when fed adapter-transformed features.
- A full 1-layer transformer (attention + FFN, 202M) achieves 1.37×. The FFN adds 135M parameters over the base adapter but provides negative marginal benefit for speedup — its per-token computation cost outweighs any acceptance-rate improvement.
- MLP-only (no attention, 165M) achieves only 1.22×. This is the critical ablation: removing attention while keeping substantial parameters causes a large speedup drop, confirming that attention's prefix-aggregation capability — not raw capacity — is the active ingredient.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Depth of shallow sub-network (exit layer $\ell$):** Figure 3(a) sweeps $\ell$ from 1 to 15 (essentially the full model depth) for both Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B, measuring average CR and speedup across all Spec-Bench subtasks. For Vicuna-7B, CR increases monotonically with $\ell$ (from ~1.55 at $\ell=1$ to ~3.25 at $\ell=15$), but speedup peaks at $\ell=2$ (~1.48×) and then declines to ~1.22× at $\ell=15$. For Vicuna-13B, the speedup peak shifts to $\ell=3$ (~1.42×), with speedup declining to ~1.05× at $\ell=15$ — nearly no speedup when using half the model as the drafter. The key non-obvious finding: the **speedup-optimal $\ell$ is far shallower than the CR-optimal $\ell$**, and the gap widens with model size (at 13B, using $\ell=15$ yields CR > 3 but speedup near 1.0×). This empirically validates the paper's central thesis that deeper early exits, despite better draft quality, are self-defeating for end-to-end speedup due to drafting latency. The practical implication is that self-drafting designers should optimize for speedup directly, not for CR — a lesson prior work (Draft & Verify) failed to internalize.

**Confidence threshold $\eta$ (dynamic vs. fixed-step drafting):** Figure 3(b) sweeps $\eta$ from 0.1 to 0.9 for three $\gamma$ values (4, 6, 8) on Vicuna-7B, measuring both CR and speedup. The fixed-step baseline ($\eta = 0$, meaning the threshold is never triggered and drafting always continues to $\gamma$) achieves the maximum CR (~2.2 for $\gamma=6$) but suboptimal speedup (~1.38×). The speedup-optimal $\eta$ is 0.6, consistent across all three $\gamma$ values, achieving ~1.45× for $\gamma=6$. At $\eta=0.6$, the CR has dropped only modestly (from ~2.2 to ~2.1 for $\gamma=6$), indicating that the threshold prunes primarily the lowest-confidence drafts that would almost certainly be rejected — it sacrifices little draft quality for meaningful latency savings. At $\eta=0.9$, both CR and speedup collapse (CR ~1.8, speedup <1.20×), showing that overly aggressive stopping discards too many correct drafts. The robustness of the optimal $\eta$ across $\gamma$ values is notable — it suggests that 0.6 captures a fundamental calibration property of the draft model rather than an artifact of a particular $\gamma$, increasing confidence that the value would transfer to other contexts.

**Adapter architecture components (Table 2):** As discussed in the main results, the ablation of adapter architectures demonstrates that attention is necessary (MLP-only achieves only 1.22×), FFN is unnecessary and harmful (adding FFN drops speedup from 1.50× to 1.37×), and a dedicated LM head is counterproductive (1.44× vs. 1.50×). The non-obvious finding is the **inverse relationship between parameter count and speedup** among the attention-containing variants: the 67M Kangaroo adapter > 198M Kangaroo+Head > 202M 1-Layer Transformer. This is strong evidence that overparameterization of the draft model is not just wasteful but actively harmful — extra parameters increase per-token latency without commensurate acceptance-rate improvements, reducing net speedup. This finding challenges the intuition (implicit in Medusa's 591M design) that more draft-model capacity is always better for speculative decoding.

**Maximum draft steps $\gamma$:** Not ablated as a standalone variable, but Figure 3(b) shows three $\gamma$ values (4, 6, 8) with their speedup curves as functions of $\eta$. At the optimal $\eta=0.6$, $\gamma=6$ achieves ~1.45× speedup, $\gamma=4$ achieves ~1.38×, and $\gamma=8$ achieves ~1.45× — essentially tied with $\gamma=6$. The paper chooses $\gamma=6$ as the sweet spot: $\gamma=4$ leaves speedup on the table (the draft model can sometimes sustain 5-6 correct drafts, and not allowing those chains reduces CR), while $\gamma=8$ provides no additional benefit (CTAR(7) and CTAR(8) are near zero, so the extra drafting steps are almost never productive). The fact that $\gamma=6$ and $\gamma=8$ tie confirms that the dynamic threshold already prunes unproductive long chains — with $\eta=0.6$, the draft model rarely reaches even 6 steps before confidence drops below threshold, so raising the ceiling to 8 changes nothing. This implies that the exact choice of $\gamma$ is not critical as long as it exceeds the draft model's effective lookahead horizon under the chosen $\eta$.

**Model size scaling (7B vs. 13B):** The paper does not ablate model size systematically (only two sizes are tested), but the comparison between Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B results in Table 1 and Figure 3 reveals scaling behavior. Key observations: (1) The optimal exit layer shifts from 2 to 3 when going from 7B to 13B, suggesting a weak scaling relationship — roughly, $\ell_{\text{opt}}$ grows sublinearly with depth. (2) Speedup does not increase with model size despite higher CR (Math CR: 2.14 → 2.42, but speedup: 1.61× → 1.63×), because the per-layer computation cost scales with model size, offsetting the CR gains. (3) Lookahead collapses at 13B (speedup drops below 1.0× on multiple subtasks) while Kangaroo remains robust, demonstrating that drafting efficiency becomes *more* important — not less — as model size increases, since the absolute latency of draft-token generation grows with model width. This scaling behavior is a meaningful robustness check: Kangaroo's design principles (shallow exit, lightweight adapter, dynamic stopping) are not tuned to a single scale but transfer with minimal adjustment (only $\ell$ changes).

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "Kangaroo achieves speedups up to 1.68× on Spec-Bench"

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 1 reports a maximum speedup of 1.68× on the MT-Bench subtask for Vicuna-7B, and 1.66× for Vicuna-13B on the same subtask. The "up to 1.68×" figure is genuinely achieved. However, the average speedup across subtasks is 1.50× for Vicuna-7B and 1.44× for Vicuna-13B — the headline "up to" number overstates typical performance by 12-16%. This is standard practice in systems papers but readers should calibrate: Kangaroo's expected speedup in a randomly chosen deployment scenario is likely ~1.4-1.5×, not 1.68×.

**Missing evidence:** The paper reports only Spec-Bench results. There are no results on standard LLM benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K) or on open-ended generation tasks without reference answers. Spec-Bench covers diverse tasks but is relatively new and not yet widely validated as representative of real-world LLM usage patterns. The absence of results on other benchmarks makes it difficult to assess whether the reported speedups generalize beyond Spec-Bench's particular task distribution.

**Hardware specificity caveat:** All speedup measurements are on NVIDIA V100 GPUs. The V100 is a relatively old architecture (2017) with different memory bandwidth characteristics than modern GPUs (A100, H100). Since speculative decoding's benefit is directly tied to the memory-bandwidth-to-compute ratio, speedup ratios measured on V100s may not transfer to newer hardware where the bottleneck is less severe or differently structured. The paper would be strengthened by results on at least one modern GPU architecture.

#### Claim 2: "Outperforms Medusa-1 with 88.7% fewer additional parameters"

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 1 confirms that Kangaroo achieves higher speedup than Medusa on 11 of 12 subtask-model pairs, and Table 2 confirms Kangaroo's adapter uses 67M parameters vs. Medusa's 591M. The 88.7% figure is arithmetically correct. However, this claim conflates two distinct advantages — higher speedup AND fewer parameters — that should be evaluated separately.

**Conflated comparison:** The claim would be equally true if Kangaroo achieved the *same* speedup as Medusa with 88.7% fewer parameters, or if it achieved higher speedup with the *same* parameters. The fact that it achieves *both* simultaneously makes the result stronger, but the paper does not run the intermediate experiment that would isolate the parameter-efficiency effect: what speedup would Kangaroo achieve with a parameter-matched adapter (e.g., 591M)? The ablation in Table 2 suggests that *adding* parameters to Kangaroo reduces speedup (198M and 202M variants underperform 67M), so a 591M Kangaroo variant might perform worse, not better. But this counterintuitive result — that more parameters actively harm speedup — is not explored in depth. It may be specific to the adapter architectures tested; a differently designed high-capacity adapter might improve acceptance enough to justify its cost. Without exploring this design space, the parameter-efficiency claim is empirically valid but its mechanism is under-explained.

**Parameter counting methodology:** Kangaroo's 67M counts the adapter only. Medusa's 591M counts the FFN heads. Neither counts the shared backbone parameters (which are identical and cancel out in a comparison). This is a fair counting methodology, but it does not account for the *compute* asymmetry: Medusa's heads run once per drafting round (generating all $\gamma$ draft tokens in parallel from a single hidden state), while Kangaroo's 67M adapter runs up to $\gamma$ times sequentially (once per draft token). The parameter count advantage understates Kangaroo's *compute* disadvantage — the adapter executes more forward passes with fewer parameters. The speedup comparison in Table 1 captures the net effect, but the "88.7% fewer parameters" framing should not be misinterpreted as "88.7% less computation" — it is not.

#### Claim 3: "Double early exiting enables efficient self-drafting"

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 3 provides evidence for each early-exit mechanism individually: Figure 3(a) shows the speedup benefit of shallow architectural exiting (speedup peaks at $\ell=2$ for 7B), and Figure 3(b) shows the speedup benefit of dynamic confidence-based exiting (speedup peaks at $\eta=0.6$, outperforming $\eta=0$ fixed-step drafting). However, there is **no ablation that isolates the interaction between the two exits**. Specifically, the paper does not test:

- What speedup would dynamic drafting ($\eta=0.6$) achieve with a *deeper* architectural exit (e.g., $\ell=6$)? If dynamic drafting is genuinely coupled to shallow exiting (as argued in Innovation 2 of the Key Insights section), then the benefit of $\eta=0.6$ should diminish at deeper $\ell$ because the draft model would be more confident per token and would rarely trigger the threshold — making dynamic drafting unnecessary. This interaction is not tested.
- What speedup would fixed-step drafting ($\eta=0$) achieve at the optimal $\ell=2$? Figure 3(b) tests $\eta=0$ only at the default $\ell$ (presumably $\ell=2$), but does not show the full $\eta \times \ell$ grid. It is possible that at $\ell=4$, fixed-step drafting would outperform dynamic drafting because the higher-quality draft model at layer 4 rarely needs early stopping.

Without this interaction ablation, the "double" early-exit claim — that two coupled mechanisms are better than either alone — is not experimentally verified. The paper demonstrates that *each* mechanism independently improves speedup, but does not demonstrate that they are synergistic rather than additive. An additive model (independent benefits that sum) would still produce the observed results; a synergistic model (benefits that multiply because the second mechanism compensates for the first's weakness) would produce a larger-than-additive effect, which is not tested.

#### Claim 4: "Dynamic drafting avoids unnecessary costs on more difficult tokens"

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 3(b) shows that $\eta=0.6$ achieves higher speedup than $\eta=0$ (fixed-step), which is consistent with the claim. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence that the *mechanism* by which $\eta=0.6$ improves speedup is "avoiding difficult tokens." Alternative explanations are possible: perhaps $\eta=0.6$ simply reduces the average number of draft tokens per round (from ~6 to ~4.5), and the speedup improvement comes from the fact that shorter drafting phases reduce *verification* latency (fewer tokens to verify in parallel) rather than from selectively pruning difficult tokens. The paper does not measure per-token difficulty or show that low-confidence tokens are indeed more likely to be rejected — which would be straightforward to verify by plotting rejection rate as a function of draft-model confidence. Without this analysis, the claim about the *mechanism* of improvement is speculative, even though the *effect* (better speedup with $\eta=0.6$) is empirically demonstrated.

#### Genuine Weaknesses

**Absence of statistical characterization.** All results in Table 1 and Figure 3 are single-point measurements. Walltime speedup on GPUs is subject to measurement noise from system-level factors (GPU boost clock variability, memory contention from other processes, CPU-GPU synchronization jitter). Differences of 0.05× between methods (e.g., Kangaroo's 1.50× vs. REST's 1.43× average on Vicuna-7B) may not be statistically significant. The paper would be strengthened by reporting standard deviations across multiple runs, or at minimum noting how many runs were averaged. The consistent pattern across subtasks (Kangaroo leads on 11/12) provides some informal robustness, but formal statistical treatment is standard in systems benchmarking and its absence is a weakness.

**Single GPU architecture.** All experiments on V100 GPUs. The memory-bandwidth bottleneck that motivates speculative decoding is less severe on newer architectures (A100 has ~2× the memory bandwidth of V100; H100 has ~3×). Speedup ratios are likely to be lower on newer hardware because the baseline autoregressive decoding is faster (less bandwidth-bound), reducing the headroom for speculative decoding to improve upon. The paper's speedup claims should be interpreted as V100-specific; results on A100 or H100 could be substantially different.

**No comparison against non-self-drafting speculative decoding.** The paper compares only against self-drafting methods (Medusa, Lookahead, REST). It does not compare against the original speculative decoding paradigm with a separately trained draft model (e.g., a distilled LLaMA-68M for LLaMA-7B, or the LLaMA-160M draft model from the SpecInfer paper). While the paper's stated contribution is self-speculative decoding, a comparison against the "oracle" of a purpose-trained draft model would contextualize how much performance is sacrificed by the self-drafting constraint. If a 68M standalone draft model achieves 2.0× speedup on the same hardware, then Kangaroo's 1.5× represents a meaningful speedup gap that practitioners must weigh against the training-cost savings. The paper does not provide this context.

**Limited model diversity.** Only Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B are tested. Vicuna is a chat-fine-tuned LLaMA variant; results may not generalize to base LLaMA models, non-LLaMA architectures (Mistral, Falcon, Qwen), or encoder-decoder models (T5, Flan). The adapter architecture (RMS norm, multi-head attention) assumes a specific transformer variant; different normalization schemes or attention mechanisms might require adapter redesign. The paper's claims are empirically grounded only for the LLaMA/Vicuna lineage.

**Training data specificity.** The adapter is trained on ShareGPT following Medusa's setup. ShareGPT consists of ChatGPT conversations, which may bias the draft model toward conversational patterns. The Spec-Bench subtasks include translation, summarization, and mathematical reasoning — domains that are underrepresented in ShareGPT. If the adapter were trained on a more diverse corpus, its token acceptance rates on non-conversational subtasks might improve. The paper does not ablate training data, so the sensitivity of results to data distribution is unknown.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

1. **Full $\ell \times \eta$ interaction grid:** Testing all combinations of exit layer (1-6) and confidence threshold (0.0-1.0) would reveal whether the two early-exit mechanisms are synergistic or additive, and would identify the globally optimal $(\ell, \eta)$ pair rather than optimizing them sequentially (first $\ell$ at fixed $\eta=0$, then $\eta$ at fixed $\ell=2$). Sequential optimization can miss interactions.

2. **Rejection-rate-by-confidence analysis:** Plotting the probability of draft rejection as a function of the draft model's top-1 confidence would directly validate the mechanism behind dynamic drafting. If low-confidence drafts are indeed rejected at a much higher rate, the threshold mechanism is well-justified. If the relationship is weak, the benefit of $\eta>0$ must come from another source (e.g., reduced verification latency from fewer draft tokens).

3. **Hardware sensitivity:** Results on at least one modern GPU architecture (A100 or H100) would establish whether Kangaroo's speedup ratios are architecture-specific or broadly applicable. Given the rapid hardware evolution in LLM deployment, single-architecture results have limited shelf life.

4. **Comparison against trained draft models:** Including a baseline with a separately trained small draft model (e.g., a 68M-160M parameter standalone model) would contextualize the cost-performance tradeoff between self-drafting and traditional speculative decoding.

5. **Breakdown of latency components:** A timing breakdown showing where time is spent (shallow sub-network forward pass, adapter forward passes, remaining-layers verification, KV-cache operations) would help readers understand which components dominate latency and where future optimization effort should focus. Currently, only end-to-end speedup is reported, which is a black-box measurement.

6. **Statistical error characterization:** Reporting mean and standard deviation of speedup across at least 5 independent runs per configuration would allow readers to assess whether the reported differences between methods are statistically reliable. This is especially important given the small absolute margins between methods (e.g., 1.50× vs. 1.43× average).

Despite these limitations, the experimental section provides consistent, multi-subtask evidence that Kangaroo achieves higher speedup than existing self-drafting methods on the tested hardware configuration. The ablation studies systematically validate the key design choices (shallow exit layer, attention-only adapter, confidence threshold). The weaknesses are primarily in *generality* (single hardware, single model family, single benchmark suite) and *mechanistic depth* (lack of interaction ablations, no rejection-rate analysis), not in the internal validity of the reported comparisons.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Excluded from All Headline Speedup Numbers

**The assumption or constraint**

The paper's central claimed benefit — speedups up to 1.68× on Spec-Bench — is measured *after* the optimal hyperparameters (exit layer $\ell$, confidence threshold $\eta$, maximum draft steps $\gamma$) have been determined through ablation studies. These hyperparameters are selected based on a grid search that requires running Kangaroo across the full Spec-Bench suite at every candidate setting (Figure 3 sweeps $\ell$ from 1 to 15 and $\eta$ from 0.1 to 0.9, each configuration benchmarking all six subtasks). This grid search cost is substantial — it requires evaluating approximately 15 × 9 = 135 configurations per model size, each running full speculative decoding across six subtasks — and none of this tuning overhead is amortized into the reported speedup ratios.

The paper does not acknowledge this directly in the limitations discussion, but the ablation methodology in Section 4.2 makes the cost explicit: "As shown in Figure 3(a), we set $\ell = 2$ for Vicuna-7B and $\ell = 3$ for Vicuna-13B" — these choices are the *output* of the grid search, not *inputs* derived from a cheap heuristic. Similarly, "For Kangaroo, we set $\gamma = 6$ and $\eta = 0.6$" — these values were found by sweeping and observing the speedup curves in Figure 3(b).

**The consequence**

A practitioner deploying Kangaroo on a new model family (e.g., Mistral, Qwen, or a custom fine-tuned LLaMA variant) cannot simply use $\ell = 2$, $\eta = 0.6$, $\gamma = 6$ and expect optimal speedup. The paper provides **no transferable guidance for hyperparameter selection** — no scaling law relating model depth to optimal exit layer, no calibration analysis showing whether $\eta = 0.6$ generalizes across model families, no cheap proxy for the grid search. The practitioner must either (a) accept potentially suboptimal hyperparameters (if, say, the optimal $\ell$ for their model is 4 rather than 2, speedup could drop substantially — Figure 3(a) shows Vicuna-7B speedup declining from ~1.48× at $\ell=2$ to ~1.32× at $\ell=6$), or (b) conduct their own grid search, the cost of which may exceed the inference savings from Kangaroo in low-to-moderate volume deployments.

This is the same structural problem that the Difficulty Estimation limitation creates in the reference example paper — a key enabler of the method (hyperparameter tuning) incurs a cost that is excluded from the headline benefit calculation, and no cheap approximation is provided.

**What evidence exists in the paper**

Figure 3 directly quantifies the sensitivity. For Vicuna-7B, choosing $\ell=1$ instead of $\ell=2$ drops speedup from ~1.48× to ~1.40×; choosing $\ell=4$ drops it to ~1.44×; choosing $\ell=6$ drops it to ~1.32×. For Vicuna-13B, the speedup peak at $\ell=3$ (~1.42×) declines to ~1.30× at $\ell=2$ and ~1.20× at $\ell=6$. Similarly, Figure 3(b) shows that choosing $\eta=0.3$ instead of $\eta=0.6$ drops speedup from ~1.45× to ~1.42× for $\gamma=6$; choosing $\eta=0.9$ drops speedup below 1.20×. These are meaningful differences — a practitioner who guesses $\ell=4$, $\eta=0.3$ for Vicuna-7B would achieve ~1.44× speedup (down from the optimal 1.50× reported in Table 1), representing a ~4% relative performance loss from hyperparameter mis-specification. The gap widens for models where the optimal settings differ more from the paper's recommendations.

**Mitigation status**

The paper does not address this limitation at all. There is no discussion of hyperparameter transferability, no proposed heuristic for selecting $\ell$ based on model depth or architecture, and no analysis of whether $\eta=0.6$ is a universal calibration property or specific to Vicuna's training. The paper does not suggest future work on cheap hyperparameter selection. A practitioner reading this paper is left with optimal hyperparameters for exactly two model checkpoints (Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B) and no methodology for extending to their own models without repeating the full ablation.

The situation is worse than it appears because the difficulty estimation limitation in the reference example paper was at least explicitly acknowledged by the authors. Here, the hyperparameter tuning cost is simply not discussed as a limitation — the ablation methodology is presented as a *contribution* (showing what the optimal settings are) without acknowledging that finding those settings is itself a deployment barrier.

### 6.2 Single Hardware Architecture: All Speedups Measured on NVIDIA V100 GPUs Only

**The assumption or constraint**

All walltime speedup measurements in Table 1, Figure 3, and throughout the paper are taken on NVIDIA V100 GPUs. The paper states this explicitly in Section 4.1: "All models are evaluated on NVIDIA V100 GPUs." The V100 (released 2017, 900 GB/s memory bandwidth with NVLink, 15.7 TFLOPS FP32) represents a specific point in the compute-vs-bandwidth tradeoff space that is substantially different from modern deployment hardware (A100: 2,039 GB/s, 19.5 TFLOPS; H100: 3,350 GB/s, 67 TFLOPS). Since speculative decoding's benefit is directly tied to the **ratio of memory-bandwidth-bound time to compute-bound time** — you can only amortize the weight-loading cost if weight loading dominates the forward pass — the speedup ratios measured on V100s embed an implicit assumption about this hardware ratio that does not transfer across GPU generations.

**The consequence**

On newer GPUs with higher memory bandwidth, the baseline autoregressive decoding is **faster** (less bandwidth-bound, can push closer to compute saturation), which means speculative decoding has **less headroom** to improve upon. The paper motivates its work with a V100-specific observation: "decoding with Vicuna-33B on four NVIDIA V100 GPUs yields a throughput of only seven new tokens per second" (Section 1). But on an A100, the same model would have higher baseline throughput, and the relative benefit of Kangaroo's drafting-efficient design would likely compress.

Conversely, it is also possible that Kangaroo's advantage over methods like Lookahead (which is even more bandwidth-sensitive due to heavy drafting computation) **increases** on newer hardware — but we cannot know because no measurements exist. The paper's speedup claims (1.50× average for Vicuna-7B, 1.44× for Vicuna-13B) are V100-specific point estimates that may not rank-order the same way on A100 or H100 hardware.

This is a practical limitation because the GPU landscape is shifting rapidly. Most production LLM inference in 2024 runs on A100 or H100 GPUs; V100 deployments are increasingly legacy. A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt Kangaroo for their A100-based inference pipeline cannot directly use the paper's speedup numbers and must either extrapolate (with unknown error) or benchmark themselves.

**What evidence exists in the paper**

The paper provides **no multi-GPU measurements and no analysis of hardware sensitivity.** Every speedup number in Table 1 and Figure 3 is on V100 hardware. The paper does not discuss why V100 was chosen, whether results are expected to generalize, or what architectural properties of V100s make the speedup ratios what they are. The V100's memory bandwidth and compute characteristics are never cited or analyzed.

The closest the paper comes to a hardware discussion is the motivating observation in Section 1 about memory bandwidth being the bottleneck. But this observation is true of all GPU architectures (the ratio just varies), and the paper does not analyze how the bandwidth-to-compute ratio of the V100 shapes the specific speedup numbers reported.

**Mitigation status**

The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation and does not suggest multi-GPU evaluation as future work. The hardware specificity is treated as an incidental detail of the experimental setup rather than a consequential constraint on result generality. There is no discussion of expected speedup scaling with GPU generation, no comparison to theoretical speedup bounds under different bandwidth assumptions, and no mention of the V100's relevance (or lack thereof) to contemporary deployment environments.

Given how frequently speculative decoding papers report speedup numbers that depend sensitively on hardware configuration (batch size, GPU model, memory bandwidth, CPU-GPU transfer overhead), the absence of hardware sensitivity analysis is a significant gap. A skeptical reader could reasonably ask: were the V100s chosen because they show the largest speedup ratios? Would Kangaroo's advantage over Medusa shrink or grow on A100? The paper provides no evidence either way.

### 6.3 Single Model Family and Architecture: Only Vicuna (LLaMA-Based) Tested

**The assumption or constraint**

All experiments are conducted on **Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B** — both fine-tuned variants of LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023). The adapter architecture assumes RMS normalization (matching LLaMA's use of RMSNorm rather than LayerNorm), a specific attention implementation, and a specific transformer layer structure. The paper's design choices — particularly the architecture of the adapter (one multi-head attention, two RMS normalization layers) and the decision to share the LM head — are validated only within this architectural family.

The paper implicitly assumes that its findings transfer to other model architectures, stating in Section 4.1 that experiments are on Vicuna models without discussing why other architectures were excluded or whether the adapter design is architecture-agnostic.

**The consequence**

At minimum, the following aspects of Kangaroo's design may not transfer:

- **Exit layer position:** Figure 3(a) shows the optimal $\ell$ differs between Vicuna-7B ($\ell=2$) and Vicuna-13B ($\ell=3$). These models differ in both depth (32 layers) and width — but across LLaMA variants, layer count and hidden dimension both scale. For a model with different depth-to-width scaling (e.g., Mistral-7B with 32 layers but different width; Falcon-7B with 32 layers but multi-query attention; Qwen-7B with different normalization), the optimal exit layer could be substantially different, and the shallow exit at $\ell=2$ might not capture enough representation capacity.

- **Adapter architecture:** The adapter uses RMS normalization because LLaMA models use RMSNorm. For models using standard LayerNorm (e.g., older GPT variants), the adapter's normalization layers would need architectural adjustment. The paper's ablation showing that attention is critical (MLP-only achieves only 1.22× speedup) may also be architecture-specific — models with different attention mechanisms (grouped-query attention, multi-query attention, sliding window attention) might require different adapter designs to efficiently aggregate prefix information.

- **LM head sharing:** The paper shows that reusing the target model's LM head is better than training a dedicated head (1.50× vs. 1.44×, Table 2). But this result depends on the target model's LM head being well-calibrated for adapter-transformed features. For models where the final layer applies additional transformations before the LM head (e.g., models with final LayerNorm after the last transformer block), the adapter's output might not align well with the LM head's expected input distribution, potentially requiring a dedicated head or an additional normalization layer.

- **KV-cache sharing:** The self-draft model shares the KV-cache with the target model's shallow layers. This assumes that the target model uses standard multi-head attention with compatible KV-cache structures. Models with non-standard attention patterns (e.g., models using FlashAttention variants with different cache layouts, or encoder-decoder architectures) would require KV-cache sharing logic to be redesigned.

The paper's claims about Kangaroo's generality — "a novel self-speculative decoding framework" that "outperforms Medusa-1 with 88.7% fewer additional parameters" — are presented without qualification about model family scope. A practitioner using a non-LLaMA architecture cannot assume the reported speedups or the optimal hyperparameters apply.

**What evidence exists in the paper**

The paper provides **zero evidence of architectural generality.** The only models tested are Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B. There are no experiments on base LLaMA (without chat fine-tuning), no experiments on other LLaMA-derived models (e.g., Alpaca, LLaMA-2-Chat), and no experiments on non-LLaMA architectures (Mistral, Falcon, Qwen, GPT-NeoX, MPT, Phi). The adapter design choices are informed only by ablations on Vicuna-7B (Table 2); there is no equivalent ablation for Vicuna-13B or any other model to show robustness.

The paper does compare Vicuna-7B and Vicuna-13B (two LLaMA variants of different scale), but this is a within-family comparison. Moving from 7B to 13B required only changing $\ell$ from 2 to 3; the adapter architecture, training procedure, and hyperparameters ($\eta=0.6$, $\gamma=6$) transferred without modification. This weakens rather than strengthens the generality claim — it shows sensitivity to model scale even within the same architecture, implying that cross-architecture sensitivity could be larger.

**Mitigation status**

The paper does not acknowledge model-family specificity as a limitation. The abstract and introduction present Kangaroo as a general framework without qualifying the scope of empirical validation. Section 4.1 states "We conduct experiments on Vicuna models with size of 7B and 13B" as a factual description without discussing why these models were chosen or whether results should generalize. The conclusion similarly makes unqualified claims: "Kangaroo achieves speedups up to 1.7× on Spec-Bench, outperforming Medusa-1 with 88.7% fewer additional parameters."

The paper does not suggest future work on architectural generalization. Given that the adapter design is specifically tailored to LLaMA's architectural choices (RMSNorm, standard MHA), extending to other architectures is non-trivial and would require design iteration that the paper does not acknowledge as needed.

### 6.4 Single Benchmark Suite: Only Spec-Bench Evaluated, No Task Diversity Analysis

**The assumption or constraint**

All speedup measurements are on **Spec-Bench** (Xia et al., 2024), a recently proposed benchmark specifically designed for evaluating speculative decoding. The paper does not evaluate Kangaroo on any other benchmark, generation task, or real-world deployment scenario. Spec-Bench covers six subtasks (translation, QA, summarization, mathematical reasoning, RAG, and MT-Bench multi-turn conversation), which provides diversity within the benchmark, but the paper does not analyze whether Spec-Bench's task distribution is representative of typical LLM deployment workloads.

The implicit assumption is that speedup on Spec-Bench predicts speedup in production — that the benchmark's mixture of task types, output lengths, and generation difficulties captures the token-level prediction challenges that determine speculative decoding performance in practice.

**The consequence**

Kangaroo's effectiveness depends critically on the **predictability of token sequences** — how often can the self-draft model correctly anticipate the target model's output? This, in turn, depends on task characteristics that Spec-Bench may or may not capture:

- **Output entropy:** Tasks with highly predictable outputs (structured data extraction, template filling, code completion of boilerplate) would likely show higher speedups because the draft model's confidence remains above $\eta=0.6$ for many consecutive tokens, enabling longer draft chains. Tasks with high-entropy outputs (creative writing, open-ended dialogue, brainstorming) would likely show lower speedups because confidence drops quickly and drafting stops early. Spec-Bench includes MT-Bench (conversation) and summarization (moderate entropy), but does not include the extremes of this spectrum.

- **Output length:** Kangaroo's speedup compounds across long sequences — the benefit of each accepted draft token accumulates over many decoding steps. On very short generation tasks (single-word answers, yes/no responses, very short translations), the overhead of the adapter's drafting phase might dominate, and speedup could drop below 1.0×. Spec-Bench does not analyze speedup as a function of output length.

- **Domain shift from training data:** The adapter is trained on ShareGPT (conversation data). If deployed on a task far from this distribution (e.g., code generation, mathematical proofs, non-English text), the draft model's predictions may be poorly calibrated, causing the confidence threshold to either stop drafting too early (if the model is uncertain on out-of-distribution tokens) or continue drafting incorrectly (if the model is overconfident on unfamiliar patterns). Spec-Bench includes translation and math reasoning, which are somewhat out-of-distribution for ShareGPT, but does not include the full range of tasks seen in production.

- **Interaction with decoding parameters:** All experiments use greedy decoding (implied by the use of top-1 confidence for the threshold). Under temperature sampling, nucleus sampling, or beam search, the verification procedure changes (speculative sampling with temperature requires different acceptance criteria), and the draft model's confidence threshold may need retuning. The paper's speedup claims apply only to greedy decoding.

Without task diversity analysis, a practitioner cannot estimate Kangaroo's expected speedup for their specific workload. The "up to 1.68×" headline number may be unrepresentative of typical performance if the practitioner's task distribution differs from Spec-Bench's mixture.

**What evidence exists in the paper**

Table 1 provides per-subtask speedups, which is the paper's strongest evidence for task sensitivity. The speedup varies substantially across subtasks for Vicuna-7B:

- Highest: MT-Bench (1.68×), Math (1.61×), RAG (1.52×)
- Middle: Summarization (1.50×), QA (1.43×)
- Lowest: Translation (1.24×)

The range from 1.24× to 1.68× — a 35% relative difference — demonstrates that Kangaroo's performance is **strongly task-dependent**. The paper does not analyze *why* Translation shows lower speedup. Looking at the CR for Translation (1.41, the lowest across all subtasks for Kangaroo), the low speedup appears to be driven by low draft quality, not drafting inefficiency — the self-draft model simply struggles to predict tokens on translation tasks. This is consistent with the adapter being trained on ShareGPT conversations (English-centric, conversational), where translation patterns are underrepresented. But the paper does not make this connection or discuss task-specific limitations.

The paper also does not report speedup as a function of output length or sequence position. CTAR(w) in Figure 1(a) shows acceptance decay with draft distance, but this is position *within a drafting round*, not position within the overall output sequence. Early tokens in a generation (where context is rich and confident) may show different drafting behavior than later tokens (where the model has already committed to a direction and might be more predictable, or conversely might be generating tail content that is harder to predict).

**Mitigation status**

The paper does not discuss task diversity or benchmark representativeness as a limitation. Spec-Bench is treated as a sufficient evaluation suite, and the per-subtask breakdown in Table 1 is presented as evidence of robustness (showing Kangaroo leads on most subtasks) rather than as a window into task-dependent limitations. The paper does not analyze why Translation speedup is low, does not discuss whether Spec-Bench covers the task types practitioners care about, and does not provide guidance for estimating speedup on novel tasks.

The absence of standard LLM benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K, etc.) means there is no evidence about Kangaroo's speedup on tasks where LLM quality is typically evaluated. A practitioner who cares about both generation quality AND speed (as most do) cannot assess from this paper whether Kangaroo's speedup holds on the task distribution they actually evaluate.

### 6.5 No Analysis of Correctness Preservation or Distributional Fidelity

**The assumption or constraint**

Speculative decoding is theoretically **lossless** — the target model's original output distribution is preserved exactly, regardless of the draft model's quality. The verification procedure (speculative sampling) guarantees this by sampling from a modified distribution that corrects for the draft model's biases. The paper invokes this guarantee in its abstract: "maintaining a consistent sampling distribution."

However, this guarantee assumes that the draft model and target model operate on identical inputs and produce distributions over identical token spaces. Kangaroo's architecture introduces a subtle deviation: the self-draft model $M_s = \mathcal{A} \circ M_b[:\ell]$ processes tokens through the shallow sub-network and adapter, while the target model $M_b$ processes them through the full depth. During the verification phase, the remaining layers $M_b[\ell:]$ receive hidden states that were computed by the shallow sub-network during drafting — hidden states at layer $\ell$ for draft tokens. These hidden states were produced by the self-draft model's autoregressive loop, which differs from how the target model would have produced them (since the target model would have processed each token through all $\ell$ layers with full-depth representations of the prefix).

The losslessness guarantee depends on the verification step having access to $M_b$'s true probability distribution $M_b(\cdot \mid \mathbf{x}^{t+i-1})$ for each draft position — the distribution the target model *would have produced* if run autoregressively. If the hidden states at layer $\ell$ for draft tokens differ from what the target model would have computed (because the shallow sub-network's representations are different from what the full model would produce at layer $\ell$), then the remaining layers $M_b[\ell:]$ are computing their output based on subtly different inputs than in true autoregressive decoding. This could, in principle, cause the verification distribution to deviate from the target model's true autoregressive distribution.

**The consequence**

If distributional fidelity is not preserved, then Kangaroo is not truly a drop-in replacement for autoregressive decoding — the outputs might differ in quality, factual accuracy, or stylistic properties from what the target model would produce on its own. The paper provides **no empirical verification of distributional equivalence**: no comparison of output quality metrics (perplexity, BLEU, ROUGE, human evaluation) between Kangaroo and vanilla autoregressive decoding, no analysis of whether accepted token distributions match, and no sanity check that running Kangaroo on a fixed set of prompts produces identical (or statistically indistinguishable) outputs from greedy autoregressive decoding.

For greedy decoding (which the paper uses), the speculative sampling verification reduces to: accept a draft token if and only if the target model's top-1 prediction matches the draft token. This is a deterministic comparison — if the target model's top-1 differs from the draft token, the draft is rejected. If Kangaroo's verification step produces even slightly different logits (due to the hidden-state issue described above), the top-1 token could differ, causing rejection of a token that *would have been accepted* under true autoregressive decoding, or (worse) acceptance of a token that *would have been rejected*. This would manifest as output differences between Kangaroo and vanilla decoding.

Even if the hidden-state mismatch is negligible in practice (because the shallow sub-network's representations at layer $\ell$ are close enough to what the full model would produce, or because the remaining layers are robust to small input perturbations), the paper provides no evidence for this assumption. In the absence of such evidence, a practitioner deploying Kangaroo in a correctness-critical application (e.g., mathematical reasoning, code generation, factual QA) cannot be confident that the accelerated model produces the same answers as the original.

**What evidence exists in the paper**

**None.** The paper does not report:

- Any comparison of Kangaroo's outputs to vanilla autoregressive outputs on the same prompts
- Any quality metric (accuracy, perplexity, BLEU, ROUGE, human preference) for Kangaroo-generated text vs. baseline-generated text
- Any analysis of whether the hidden states at layer $\ell$ from the shallow sub-network match those that the full model would produce
- Any discussion of the theoretical conditions under which the verification step preserves the target distribution given the shared-parameter architecture

The compression rate metric (Table 1) indirectly measures token-level agreement between draft and target models, but this is draft-model quality, not output fidelity. The speedup metric measures wallclock improvement, not equivalence of outputs. The CTAR metric measures acceptance rates, not whether accepted tokens lead to the same final outputs as autoregressive decoding.

The abstract's claim of "maintaining a consistent sampling distribution" is a theoretical invocation of speculative decoding's guarantees, not an empirical verification that Kangaroo achieves them. Given Kangaroo's architectural deviation from standard speculative decoding (shared shallow layers, adapter-based draft model), this theoretical guarantee does not automatically transfer — it requires the additional assumption that the verification step's inputs match what true autoregressive decoding would produce, which the paper neither states nor verifies.

**Mitigation status**

The paper does not acknowledge this as a concern. It relies entirely on the theoretical losslessness guarantee of speculative sampling without discussing whether Kangaroo's architecture satisfies the conditions for that guarantee. There is no mention of output quality evaluation in the experiments section, no comparison of generated text, and no discussion of distributional fidelity in the limitations or future work sections.

This is arguably the most significant gap in the paper from a deployment perspective. A practitioner choosing between Kangaroo and Medusa (which also claims losslessness) cannot assess from this paper whether either method actually preserves output quality — they must take the theoretical guarantee on faith. For Medusa, prior work and community testing has established empirical losslessness (outputs match vanilla decoding) across many prompts; Kangaroo, being newer and architecturally different, lacks this community validation, and the paper does not provide it.

A simple experiment would resolve this: run Kangaroo and vanilla autoregressive decoding on the same set of prompts with the same random seed (for temperature sampling) or deterministically (for greedy decoding), and report the fraction of prompts where outputs are bitwise-identical and the fraction where outputs differ in semantically meaningful ways. The absence of this experiment is a significant omission.

### 6.6 The Speedup Advantage Is Modest in Absolute Terms and May Not Justify Deployment Complexity in All Settings

**The assumption or constraint**

Kangaroo achieves speedups of 1.24×–1.68× across Spec-Bench subtasks for Vicuna-7B, with an average of 1.50×. For Vicuna-13B, the range is 1.18×–1.66×, averaging 1.44×. The paper frames these as substantial improvements — they beat all existing self-drafting methods and represent "up to 1.68×" acceleration.

However, these speedups are **modest in absolute terms.** A 1.5× speedup means that a generation that previously took 10 seconds now takes 6.7 seconds — a meaningful but not transformative improvement. A 1.24× speedup (Translation, Vicuna-7B) means 10 seconds becomes 8.1 seconds. Whether these speedups justify the deployment complexity of Kangaroo depends on the practitioner's scale, latency requirements, and alternative optimization strategies.

The paper assumes that any speedup is worthwhile, but does not analyze the **cost of adoption**: training the adapter (requiring access to ShareGPT-like data, 10 epochs of training with AdamW, hyperparameter tuning), modifying the inference stack to support the parallel compute unit and KV-cache sharing between shallow and deep layers, implementing the confidence-threshold logic, and maintaining two model artifacts (the adapter weights plus the target model). For a 1.5× speedup, this overhead may be justified at very large scale (millions of queries per day, each query producing hundreds of tokens) but not at moderate scale (thousands of queries per day). The paper provides no break-even analysis.

**The consequence**

A practitioner with moderate inference volume might find that alternative acceleration strategies — such as switching to a faster GPU (which can yield >2× speedup without any code changes), using quantization (which can yield 2-4× speedup with minimal quality loss), or simply using a smaller model — provide better cost-performance tradeoffs than adopting Kangaroo. The paper does not compare Kangaroo against these alternatives.

More importantly, the speedup is **task-dependent** and can be quite low (1.18×–1.24×) on tasks that are important in practice, like translation. A practitioner whose workload is dominated by translation tasks would see only a 1.24× speedup from Kangaroo on Vicuna-7B — which may not justify the engineering effort. The paper does not analyze which task characteristics predict high vs. low speedup, so a practitioner cannot estimate their expected benefit without benchmarking on their own workload.

Additionally, the speedup assumes **batch-size-1 inference** (single sequence generation at a time). In batched inference settings (serving multiple users simultaneously), the memory-bandwidth bottleneck is partially mitigated because the batch amortizes weight loading across multiple sequences. Speculative decoding's benefit typically decreases with batch size, and Kangaroo's speedup in batched settings may be lower than the reported single-sequence numbers. The paper does not evaluate batch-size scaling.

**What evidence exists in the paper**

Table 1 shows the speedup range across subtasks. The lowest speedup is 1.18× (Vicuna-13B, Translation) and 1.15× (Vicuna-7B, Lookahead on Translation), demonstrating that some task-method combinations yield marginal acceleration. The paper does not discuss what level of speedup is practically meaningful or analyze adoption costs.

The parameter-efficiency claim (67M vs. Medusa's 591M) partially addresses the deployment complexity concern by showing that Kangaroo's added model artifact is small. But the parameter count is only one component of deployment complexity; the inference stack modifications (parallel compute unit, KV-cache sharing, confidence threshold logic) represent engineering effort that the parameter count does not capture. And the training cost (10 epochs on ShareGPT, GPU hours for adapter training) is not quantified.

Figure 3 quantifies the sensitivity of speedup to hyperparameter choices, but does not provide a cost model for hyperparameter tuning. The ablation methodology described in Section 4.2 implies that finding the optimal $\ell$, $\eta$, and $\gamma$ for a given model requires running full Spec-Bench evaluations across a grid of settings — a computationally expensive process that itself consumes inference compute, potentially offsetting the speedup savings for low-to-moderate volume deployments.

**Mitigation status**

The paper does not discuss the practical significance of the absolute speedup magnitude, does not compare against non-speculative-decoding acceleration strategies (quantization, better hardware, smaller models), and does not provide adoption-cost analysis. The conclusion frames the 1.68× speedup as a success without discussing the deployment scenarios where a 1.2–1.7× speedup justifies the engineering investment.

The paper also does not evaluate batched inference, leaving open the question of whether Kangaroo's speedup persists at batch sizes > 1. Given that many production inference systems use continuous batching, this is a significant practical gap. The assumption of single-sequence inference is implicit in the speculative decoding setup (draft tokens are verified in one forward pass of the target model, but that forward pass processes a single sequence's worth of draft tokens, not a batch of sequences) and is not stated or discussed as a limitation.
