Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman piece (a reflection on unlearning Leetcode-style cleverness in professional code):

**The misalignment of Leetcode and professional craftsmanship**

Ryan opens with a moment of personal frustration—a timer going off after 30 minutes of grinding on a Leetcode problem, followed by the humbling experience of opening the solutions page to find "a single line of code. So clever it'd take me longer to understand than to come up with my own solution." Years of writing code at Meta have since inverted his reaction to that kind of cleverness. What once impressed him now signals something closer to poor craftsmanship: "complex code feels like poor craftsmanship. It doesn't feel clever to me, it feels sloppy."

**Simplicity as a social act—and a selfish one**

The case for simple code runs through several layers. Writing fancy code to impress people is inconsiderate—"you're just making them do more work to seem smart." It's also practically self-defeating: if teammates don't understand your code, "they're more likely to break it. So you're creating more work for yourself too." Simple code breaks less and therefore lasts longer, which means it will be read "often hundreds of times more than it's written." The return on readability compounds. And you are one of those future readers: "Even if you're the only one who reads your code, you should still write simple code. You'll understand it easier later."

**When complexity is unavoidable**

He concedes that not all logic can be made simple—"some logic is inherently complex." The fallback is not clever one-liners but **clear comments for future readers**. The final test is an act of perspective-taking: "Next time you prepare your code for review, read it from your teammates' perspective. Make it so clear that even engineers without context can understand it."
