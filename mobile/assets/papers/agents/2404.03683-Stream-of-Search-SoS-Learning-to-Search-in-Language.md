# Stream of Search (SoS): Learning to Search in Language

**ArXiv:** [2404.03683](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.03683)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces the **Stream of Search (SoS)** framework, a method for teaching language models to perform search — including exploration, backtracking, and recovery from mistakes — by representing the entire search process as a flattened string of text.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces the **Stream of Search (SoS)** framework, a method for teaching language models to perform search — including exploration, backtracking, and recovery from mistakes — by representing the entire search process as a flattened string of text. Using the Countdown game (a generalized version of the 24 Game where input numbers must be combined with arithmetic operations to reach a target) as the experimental substrate, the authors pretrain a 250M-parameter GPT-Neo model from scratch on 500,000 search trajectories generated by diverse symbolic solvers — heuristic-guided BFS with varying breadth limits and DFS — and show that this messy, backtracking-filled data yields a 25% accuracy improvement over models trained solely on optimal solution paths (51.27% vs. 25.73% on held-out inputs). The SoS model is then further improved through two policy optimization methods — **Advantage-Induced Policy Alignment (APA)** and Self-Taught Reasoner (**STaR**) — enabling it to solve 36% of problems previously unsolved by any symbolic strategy in the training set, including roughly 4% of problems that none of the original heuristic solvers could solve, establishing that language models can learn to search, self-improve their search strategies, and potentially discover novel problem-solving approaches only when exposed to the full process of exploration and error recovery rather than clean optimal trajectories alone.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Language Models Are Trained on Answers, Not Processes

The fundamental gap this paper addresses is a mismatch between how humans solve complex problems and how language models are typically trained. When humans tackle difficult reasoning tasks — playing chess, solving math puzzles, debugging code, planning multi-step projects — they rarely proceed in a straight line from question to answer. They explore candidate moves, make mistakes, recognize dead ends, backtrack, try alternative approaches, and gradually converge on a solution through iterative refinement. This messy, non-linear process of **search** is central to human cognition, particularly for problems requiring planning and lookahead.

Yet the standard training paradigm for language models systematically strips away this process. Models are typically trained on **optimal paths** — clean sequences of correct reasoning steps that lead directly from the problem statement to the answer, with no missteps, no exploration, no recovery. The paper's opening metaphor captures this starkly: "Imagine only ever seeing the right solutions to problems, never a mistake or recovery from it. You might learn that problems must be solved in one clean pass, rather than through exploration and error."

This matters because autoregressive language models — which generate text one token at a time, committing to each prediction before seeing what comes next — are structurally vulnerable to two well-documented failure modes that search directly addresses:

**1. The snowballing of errors.** When a model makes a single mistake early in a multi-step reasoning chain, that error propagates forward. Because the model conditions on its own previous (now incorrect) outputs, subsequent steps are built on a faulty foundation, and the probability of recovering to a correct answer drops precipitously. The paper cites Ross et al. (2011) and Arora et al. (2022), who characterize this as a form of exposure bias or compounding error in autoregressive generation — the model was trained on distributions of correct sequences, but at inference time it encounters its own potentially incorrect tokens, creating a distributional shift it never learned to handle.

**2. Difficulty with lookahead tasks.** Autoregressive models generate tokens left-to-right, which means at each step they must commit to an action without directly simulating its downstream consequences. In problems like Countdown — where an early choice of which two numbers to combine and which operation to apply drastically affects whether the target can be reached with the remaining numbers — a model trained only on optimal paths has no experience evaluating whether a partial plan is "on track" versus heading toward a dead end. The paper frames this as a credit assignment problem (citing Sutton & Barto, 2018): the model struggles to learn which intermediate states are promising because it never sees unpromising ones in its training data.

These two problems are not independent — they compound. A model that makes an early error and has no mechanism to detect or recover from it will produce increasingly nonsensical output, and a model that cannot evaluate the promise of intermediate states has no way to decide when to abandon a failing approach and try something else. The paper's thesis is that **both problems are symptoms of a single root cause: the model has never been taught to search.** Search — the ability to explore multiple paths, evaluate their promise, and backtrack from dead ends — is precisely the mechanism that prevents error snowballing (by recovering before errors compound) and enables lookahead (by explicitly considering multiple futures before committing).

### Why This Problem Matters: Beyond Benchmarks to Autonomous Reasoning

The significance of this gap extends well beyond any single benchmark. The paper situates its contribution within a broader debate about whether autoregressive language models can, in principle, serve as autonomous planning and reasoning engines.

There exists a growing body of criticism — which the paper explicitly engages — arguing that next-token prediction is fundamentally insufficient for planning and search. LeCun (2023) argues that LLMs lack the internal world models necessary for planning. Bachmann and Nagarajan (2024) identify specific "pitfalls of next-token prediction" that make autoregressive models ill-suited for tasks requiring lookahead and error recovery. Empirical studies reinforce these concerns: Valmeekam et al. (2024) show that LLMs struggle with planning tasks, Pallagani et al. (2023) find limitations in automated planning capabilities, and Momennejad et al. (2024) demonstrate weaknesses in cognitive maps and planning evaluations. The consistent theme across these critiques is that the left-to-right, next-token objective is architecturally mismatched with the requirements of search and planning — and that models trained only on optimal final outputs never develop the internal machinery needed to explore, evaluate, and backtrack.

The paper's position is simultaneously a response to and a partial validation of these criticisms. It acknowledges that when trained conventionally (on optimal paths only), language models indeed exhibit the planning deficiencies the critics describe. But it argues that this is a **training data problem**, not an architectural inevitability. If language models can be shown to learn search — including backtracking and error recovery — from search trajectory data, then the critics' concerns are about training paradigms rather than fundamental model limitations. The paper's emphasis on "learning to search during training" (Section 1) rather than only at inference is key: it frames the question not as "can LMs search?" but "can LMs learn to search if we show them what search looks like?"

This reframing has practical stakes. If search is only achievable through external scaffolding — symbolic algorithms that call frozen LMs as subroutines, which is the dominant paradigm in prior work — then autonomous LM-based reasoning systems remain dependent on hand-crafted search logic that may not transfer across domains and cannot improve through experience. If, instead, LMs can internalize search as an intrinsic capability, they become self-contained reasoning engines that can (a) operate without external orchestration, (b) adapt their search strategies per-problem or per-domain, and (c) improve their search behavior through reinforcement or self-training. The second half of the paper — demonstrating that SoS models can solve problems no symbolic strategy could solve after policy improvement — directly addresses point (c) and represents the paper's most ambitious claim.

Additionally, the paper touches on a subtle but important point about **world models**. When LMs are used as subroutines within a symbolic search algorithm (e.g., Tree of Thoughts), the symbolic infrastructure handles state transitions and tracking — the LM only generates and evaluates candidates. The environment model is external. In SoS, the LM itself must learn to simulate state transitions (e.g., computing the new set of numbers after an arithmetic operation, tracking which moves have been tried, maintaining the search frontier). This means the LM learns an internal world model of the problem domain — a capability the paper compares to the learned models in AlphaZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) and which addresses a key criticism about pretrained LMs lacking such models.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper identifies three broad classes of prior work that attempt to combine language models with search, and identifies specific limitations in each.

#### 1. Language Models as Components of External Search Systems

The most prominent prior approach — exemplified by Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2024) and its variants (Graph of Thoughts, Besta et al., 2023; Do as I Can, Ahn et al., 2022) — uses LMs as **subroutines within a symbolic search framework**. A symbolic algorithm (BFS, DFS, Monte Carlo Tree Search) handles the exploration strategy, backtracking, and state tracking. The LM serves two roles: (a) a proposal mechanism that generates candidate actions or successor states given the current state, and (b) an evaluation mechanism that scores or filters candidate states. The search algorithm — not the LM — decides which states to explore, when to backtrack, and when a solution has been found.

The paper identifies three specific shortcomings of this extrinsic approach:

- **No improvement during training.** The LM components are typically frozen (used only at inference), so the model's underlying reasoning ability never improves through exposure to the search process. The LM remains the same reasoning engine before and after search — search only selects among its outputs more effectively. This limits the ceiling: if the LM cannot generate correct candidate actions for certain problem types, no amount of external search will find a solution, because the correct path was never in the proposal distribution.

- **Fixed search strategies.** Because the exploration strategy is hard-coded in the symbolic algorithm, it does not adapt to the specific problem or improve over time. A BFS with a fixed beam width treats all problems identically, even though easy problems might benefit from more aggressive pruning and hard problems might need broader exploration. The strategy is chosen by the human designer once and for all.

- **High inference cost.** Tree-of-Thoughts-style approaches require many LM calls per problem — one for each state expansion and one for each evaluation — which the paper notes (citing Sel et al., 2023) can become prohibitively expensive. Every step of the search requires conditioning the LM on the current state and generating or scoring candidates, so the total inference compute scales with the size of the explored tree.

The SoS approach inverts this relationship: instead of a symbolic algorithm orchestrating LM calls, the LM itself encodes the search procedure, generating the entire search trajectory (including exploration, backtracking, and solution) in a single autoregressive sequence. This makes inference dramatically cheaper (one forward pass per token, rather than one forward pass per search step) and enables the search strategy to be learned rather than hand-coded.

#### 2. In-Context Demonstrations of Search

An alternative approach provides language models with **in-context examples** of search procedures (Gandhi et al., 2023; Sel et al., 2023). The model is prompted with demonstrations that show how to explore, evaluate, and backtrack in a text format, and it is expected to emulate this search behavior on new problems at inference time. This approach avoids the cost of external symbolic orchestration — the LM runs autonomously — but introduces different limitations:

- **Bounded by the demonstrated strategies.** The model can only imitate the search procedures shown in the prompt. If the in-context examples demonstrate DFS with a sum heuristic, the model's search behavior will approximate that specific strategy. There is no mechanism for the model to discover superior strategies, adapt the search procedure to the problem at hand, or improve through experience. The strategy is fixed by the prompt designer.

- **Capacity constraints of in-context learning.** Demonstrating a full search procedure requires substantial prompt real estate — showing exploration, backtracking, and recovery from mistakes across multiple branches of a search tree can easily consume thousands of tokens before the model even begins to solve the target problem. This limits the effective context window available for the actual problem-solving and constrains the complexity of search procedures that can be demonstrated.

- **No persistent learning.** The model's capabilities return to baseline after the in-context examples are removed. The model has not learned to search in any durable sense — it is temporarily emulating a search pattern shown in context.

The SoS framework addresses these limitations by embedding search trajectories directly into the **training data** rather than providing them at inference time. This enables the model to internalize search procedures as learned behaviors (weights in the network), which are available without prompting on every inference call and which can be refined through further training (policy improvement).

#### 3. Process Supervision

Process supervision (Lightman et al., 2023) is a method where an external **process reward model (PRM)** is trained to score intermediate reasoning steps, and the LM is then trained using these step-level reward signals (rather than only outcome-level correctness). This approach has shown strong results on mathematical reasoning tasks — the PRM provides denser feedback than outcome supervision, enabling the model to learn which intermediate steps are promising versus unpromising.

The paper identifies two practical limitations that motivate the SoS alternative:

- **Expensive labeled data requirements.** Training a PRM typically requires human-annotated step-level correctness labels for a large number of intermediate solution steps. The PRM800k dataset used in Lightman et al. (2023) contains 800,000 human-labeled steps across 75,000 solutions. This annotation cost is substantial and may not scale to domains where human evaluators cannot reliably assess intermediate step quality (e.g., code generation where the correctness of a partial program is difficult to judge without execution).

- **No intrinsic search capability.** Process supervision improves the model's ability to generate correct reasoning steps, but it does not directly teach the model to explore, backtrack, or recover from errors. The model learns to produce higher-quality individual steps, but it does not learn the meta-cognitive skill of deciding when to abandon a path and try an alternative. The PRM provides a training signal; it does not provide a search procedure.

The SoS framework addresses the first limitation by using **self-generated heuristic search trajectories** rather than human labels as training data, dramatically reducing annotation cost. It addresses the second limitation by explicitly encoding search operations (backtracking, exploration, goal checking) in the training data, teaching the model not just what good steps look like but how to organize the process of finding them.

#### 4. Training on Search Trajectories (Prior Work and Gaps)

The paper acknowledges prior work that, like SoS, trains language models on search trajectories. Yang et al. (2022) train transformers on search traces from MCTS, BFS, and other symbolic planners to mimic the search behavior. Lehnert et al. (2024), in concurrent work, use A* search traces to train transformers that imitate A* with a fixed heuristic. The paper positions SoS relative to this work along two axes:

- **Diversity of search strategies.** Prior work typically trains on trajectories from a single search algorithm with a single heuristic. SoS deliberately constructs a training set from **twelve different search strategies** (BFS with varying breadth limits using two different heuristics, DFS with two different heuristics), producing a diverse distribution that exposes the model to multiple exploration strategies, pruning thresholds, and backtracking patterns. This diversity is hypothesized to teach the model a more general search capability rather than a specific strategy imitation.

- **Discovery of new strategies.** The paper argues that prior work on search trajectory training aims to teach models to **imitate** specific search procedures (BFS, A*, MCTS) more efficiently. The SoS framework's distinctive contribution is the claim that training on diverse search trajectories, followed by reinforcement for correctness, enables models to **discover novel search strategies** — strategies that solve problems none of the original symbolic solvers could solve. This shifts the goal from imitation to innovation.

### How the Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution through a specific architectural metaphor that appears throughout Section 3: **Search as Language**. The core insight is that the process of search — moving between states, expanding candidates, evaluating promise, backtracking from dead ends, checking goal conditions — can be serialized as a structured text representation, a "Stream of Search." This is not simply chain-of-thought (which typically follows a linear reasoning trajectory) nor tree-of-thought (which requires external orchestration). It is a unified textual format that captures the full messiness of search in a way that an autoregressive transformer can learn from and reproduce.

Two design choices define the paper's position relative to prior work:

**First, the boundary between explicit and implicit search operations.** The authors make deliberate decisions about which elements of search to represent as text (and therefore teach the model to generate explicitly) and which to leave implicit (allowing the model to internalize them as latent representations). They represent the current state, goal checking, backtracking, and exploration choices explicitly in the trajectory text. They keep heuristic evaluation and pruning implicit — the model must learn, from the training data, when a path is worth pursuing and when it should be abandoned, without those evaluations appearing as explicit numerical scores in the generated text. This hybrid representation is intended to balance the learnability of explicit search structure with the flexibility of implicit learned heuristics that can be improved through further training.

**Second, the commitment to self-improvement as the ultimate objective.** The paper is explicit that demonstrating better-than-optimal-path accuracy from pretraining is only the first step. The central ambition — stated in the abstract, developed in Section 6, and tested in the policy improvement experiments — is that models trained on search trajectories can **go beyond their training distribution** when optimized for correctness. The STaR and APA experiments are not presented as performance optimizations but as evidence of a qualitatively different capability: a model that has internalized search can discover new ways to search when given a correctness signal, solving problems that resist all the symbolic strategies the model was originally trained on. This is the paper's strongest and most distinctive claim. The title's choice of "Learning to Search" rather than "Imitating Search" or "Applying Search" signals this ambition.

In summary, the paper positions itself at the intersection of two debates: (1) whether autoregressive architectures can learn planning and search, contra critics who argue next-token prediction is fundamentally insufficient, and (2) whether training data that includes mistakes and recovery is more valuable than optimal-only trajectories. By demonstrating that search can be internalized through training and then improved through reinforcement — including solving problems no symbolic solver could manage — the paper argues for a shift in how we train reasoning models: away from curated correct solutions and toward the messy, iterative, error-ridden process that actual problem-solving entails.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents a **training framework** — not a model architecture, not an inference-time algorithm — for teaching a language model to perform internal search by pretraining it on text-serialized search trajectories that include mistakes, exploration, and backtracking, and then further improving that capability through reinforcement for correctness. The central problem it solves is that standard language model training on optimal solution paths produces models that cannot explore alternatives or recover from errors; the solution is to show the model the messy, branching, error-recovering process of search itself, encoded as a structured string, so that the model learns to autonomously navigate problem spaces in similar ways.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system consists of four major components arranged in a sequential pipeline:

1. **Symbolic Search Strategy Suite (Data Generator):** A collection of 12 hand-crafted search algorithms — BFS with varying breadth limits and DFS, each guided by one of two heuristic functions — that generate diverse search trajectories on Countdown problems. These are not part of the final trained system; they produce the training data only.

2. **Stream of Search Serialization Format (Data Representation):** A unified textual language that encodes the operations of search — state expansion, exploration ordering, backtracking, goal checking — as a flat string, converting each symbolic search trajectory into a sequence of tokens that an autoregressive model can learn from.

3. **Transformer Language Model (Learner):** A 250M-parameter GPT-Neo model pretrained from scratch on the serialized search trajectories using a standard causal language modeling objective. This model learns to generate entire search trajectories (including the messiness) autoregressively, internalizing both the mechanics of search and an implicit world model of the Countdown domain.

4. **Policy Improvement Module (Refiner):** A feedback-driven finetuning stage — using either STaR (expert iteration: generate, filter correct trajectories, retrain) or APA (actor-critic RL with a learned value function) — that optimizes the pretrained SoS model for correctness and efficiency. This module takes the model that has learned to search and makes it search better.

Information flows as follows: heuristic solvers generate search trajectories on training problems → trajectories are serialized in the SoS format → the GPT-Neo model is pretrained on these strings with next-token prediction → the pretrained model generates new trajectories on training problems → correct trajectories are filtered and used to finetune the model (STaR), or trajectories are scored by a reward function and used with a value network to update the policy (APA).

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal MDP framing of search and the vocabulary of search operations, because this is the conceptual foundation that justifies why search can be serialized at all and what each token in the trajectory "means" operationally.
- **Second**, the SoS serialization format itself — how the abstract search operations become concrete text — since understanding the data representation is prerequisite to understanding what the model learns.
- **Third**, the Countdown task specification and its properties as a search problem, because the task characteristics (branching factor, state representation, arithmetic requirements) drive design choices throughout the pipeline.
- **Fourth**, the training data construction — the 12 symbolic search strategies, their heuristics, and the dataset composition — since this is what the model sees during pretraining and determines the distribution of search behaviors it learns from.
- **Fifth**, the model architecture and pretraining procedure, covering what model is used, how it is trained, and the comparison baseline (optimal path training).
- **Sixth**, the two policy improvement methods (STaR and APA) in detail, since these are the mechanisms by which the model goes beyond imitating its training distribution to discover improved search strategies.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **training methodology paper** whose core idea is that language models can learn to autonomously search — including backtracking and error recovery — if their training data contains the full search process serialized as text, and that such models can further improve their search strategies through correctness-based reinforcement, potentially discovering approaches that outperform the symbolic solvers used to generate their initial training data.

#### Formal Framing: Search as a Markov Decision Process

Before describing how search is serialized, the paper provides a formal characterization of the search problem as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) in Section 3. This is not the contribution — it is foundational vocabulary that makes the subsequent serialization precise.

The problem space is defined by a 4-tuple `$(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{T}, \mathcal{R})$`:

- `$\mathcal{S}$` is the set of states, representing steps in solving a problem. In Countdown, a state is the current set of numbers remaining and the operations applied so far.
- `$\mathcal{A}$` is the set of actions, representing operations the model can perform on states. In Countdown, an action is selecting two input numbers, applying one of the four arithmetic operations (`+`, `-`, `×`, `÷`), and producing a new number to replace the two consumed ones.
- `$\mathcal{T} : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathcal{S}$` is the transition function, defining the deterministic mapping from a state and an action to the resulting next state. In Countdown, this is the arithmetic computation: given a state with numbers `[a, b, c, d]` and the action `a + b = e`, the next state has numbers `[e, c, d]`.
- `$\mathcal{R} : \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$` is the reward function, assigning a reward for reaching the goal state. The paper uses a sparse reward — positive only when the target number is exactly achieved.

A search query specifies an initial state `$s_0 \in \mathcal{S}$` (the input numbers and target) and a goal state `$s_g \in \mathcal{S}$` (a state where the remaining numbers equal the target). The search tree contains all possible sequences of actions from `$s_0$` through to leaf states (where no further operations are possible or where the goal is reached). A correct solution path `$\mathcal{P}$` is a specific sequence `$(s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots, s_{g-1}, a_{g-1}, s_g)$` where each successive state is produced by applying the corresponding action to the previous state, and the final state matches the goal.

**What this framing accomplishes:** it makes precise the distinction between the **search tree** (the full space of all possible action sequences, which exists independently of any algorithm), the **search trajectory** (the specific sequence of states visited by a particular search algorithm as it explores that tree, including backtracking and dead-end exploration), and the **solution path** (the subsequence of the trajectory that actually leads from start to goal). The SoS framework is about teaching models to generate search trajectories — not just solution paths — so that they learn the exploration and backtracking operations that produce trajectories in the first place.

**Why MDP formalism rather than something else:** the MDP framework provides standard terminology (state, action, transition, reward) that makes the search process decomposable into well-defined operations. This decomposition is exactly what enables the subsequent definition of a "language for search" — each MDP component maps to a specific textual construct in the serialized format. The alternative — treating search as an unstructured blob of reasoning — would prevent systematic analysis of what the model is learning and whether specific search operations (backtracking, pruning) are being used.

#### The Language for Search: A Vocabulary of Primitive Operations

Given the MDP formalization, Section 3 defines a vocabulary of eight primitive operations that collectively capture the mechanics of different search algorithms. This vocabulary is the conceptual bridge between symbolic search (where these operations are implemented in code) and language model search (where they become tokens the model generates). The paper states this is a "non-exhaustive list" representing "the most salient operations," and that it is "in spirit similar to defining a domain-specific language for planning" (citing Fikes & Nilsson, 1971, the STRIPS planner).

The eight operations are:

1. **Current State `$s_c$`:** The state currently being explored. In the serialized trajectory, this appears as an explicit declaration: "Current State: [target]:[numbers], Operations: [list of operations applied so far]". This operation makes the search algorithm's focus explicit — the model declares which node it is examining before expanding it or moving away from it.

2. **Goal State `$s_g$`:** The target state being sought. This is specified once at the beginning of the problem and is the reference against which all goal checks are performed.

3. **State Queue `$S_q$`:** The frontier of unexplored states. This is the set of generated but not-yet-expanded nodes that the search algorithm maintains. In the serialized format, the queue is implicit — it is represented by the ordering of "Moving to Node" operations relative to "Exploring Operation" operations — but its management (which state to explore next, which states remain) is what distinguishes BFS from DFS.

4. **State Expansion Function `$\text{SE}: \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{S}$`:** The operation of generating all valid successor states from the current state by applying each possible action (each pair of numbers, each operation). In the text format, this appears as sequences of "Exploring Operation: [a op b = c], Resulting Numbers: [...], Generated Node #[index]: [target]:[new numbers] Operation: [a op b = c]" blocks. Each such block is a single expansion — one action applied to the current state to produce one child node.

5. **Exploration Choice:** The decision rule that determines which state to expand next from the frontier. This is what distinguishes search strategies: BFS chooses the oldest unexplored node, DFS chooses the newest, A* chooses the one with the best heuristic score. In the serialized format, the exploration choice manifests as the sequence of "Moving to Node #[index]" operations — the pattern of which node index is chosen when reveals whether the search is breadth-first, depth-first, or something else.

6. **Pruning:** The operation of discarding states or subtrees that are unlikely to lead to a solution, based on a heuristic evaluation. In the serialized format, this is **left implicit** — nodes that would be pruned simply never appear in the trajectory. The model must learn, from the training data, which states the symbolic solvers chose to prune and internalize that evaluation.

7. **Backtracking:** The operation of moving to a previously visited (ancestor) state rather than continuing deeper from the current state. In the serialized format, this appears as "Moving to Node #[index]" where the target index is not a child of the current node. For example, after exhausting all children of node #0,0 (reaching dead ends at #0,0,0 through #0,0,4), the trajectory might read "Moving to Node #0,1" — this is backtracking from the #0,0 subtree to explore the #0,1 sibling instead.

8. **Goal Check:** The operation of testing whether the current state matches the goal state. In the serialized format, this appears as two variants: either "[result], [target] equal: Goal Reached" (success, search terminates) or "[result], [target] unequal: No Solution" (failure at this leaf, triggering backtracking).

9. **Heuristic Function `$h \in \mathcal{H} : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$`:** A function approximating the distance from the current state to the goal, used both for pruning decisions and exploration choices. The paper **keeps the heuristic functions implicit** — they do not appear as numerical scores in the serialized text — but the heuristic values determine which nodes appear in the trajectory (by driving pruning and ordering). The model must learn to internalize these evaluations from the patterns of which nodes are expanded versus abandoned.

**Design choice — explicit vs. implicit operations:** The paper makes a deliberate split. The current state, goal state, backtracking operations, goal checks, and exploration choices are represented **explicitly** as text in the trajectory. The heuristic functions, state values, and pruning strategy are left **implicit**. The rationale (Section 3): "When operations are implicit, a model is more likely to internalize abstract representations for them that can be improved with training. Explicit operations will turn into explicit reasoning moves made by the LM." This means the model learns to "say" where it is and where it's going (explicit), but must learn to "think" about whether a state is promising or should be abandoned (implicit). The implicit operations become internal computations the model can refine during policy improvement, while the explicit operations provide the structural scaffolding of search that the model must learn to generate correctly.

**Why not make everything explicit?** If heuristic scores were written in the text (e.g., "Node #0,0: heuristic=7.3"), the model could learn to simply copy numerical evaluations from its training data rather than developing its own internal evaluation capability. Keeping heuristics implicit forces the model to learn the underlying evaluation logic — which states are promising — from the patterns of which states the symbolic solvers chose to explore versus skip. This learned evaluation can then be refined during policy improvement in ways that a copied numerical score could not.

**Why not make everything implicit?** If backtracking and goal checking were implicit, the model would have to discover the structure of search from purely sequential data — a much harder learning problem equivalent to learning the MDP transition structure, the search algorithm, and the domain reasoning simultaneously with no explicit guidance. The explicit search operations provide a scaffold: the model learns the "syntax" of search (how to state where it is, how to generate children, how to declare success or failure) from the explicit text, then learns the "semantics" (which states are worth exploring, when to backtrack) from the implicit patterns.

#### The Stream of Search Serialization Format

The vocabulary of operations is instantiated in a concrete text format for the Countdown domain. Figure 1b and Figure 8 in the appendix show complete examples. The format follows a specific structural pattern that encodes a depth-first traversal of the search tree.

A trajectory begins with the initial state declaration:

```
Current State: [target]:[input numbers], Operations: []
```

The "Operations: []" field is a running list of the arithmetic operations applied along the current path from the root. It starts empty and accumulates operations as the search proceeds deeper. This explicit tracking of the path is what enables backtracking — when the search moves back to an ancestor node, the Operations list resets to match that ancestor's state.

From the current state, the model generates a sequence of **state expansions**, each formatted as:

```
Exploring Operation: [a op b = result], Resulting Numbers: [new set]
Generated Node #[index path]: [target]:[new set] Operation: [a op b = result]
```

The index path (e.g., `#0,0,2`) encodes the position in the search tree: `#0` means first child of root, `#0,0` means first child of first child, `#0,0,2` means third child of first child of first child. This indexing scheme serves two functions: (a) it provides unique identifiers for nodes that can be referenced during backtracking ("Moving to Node #0,0"), and (b) it makes the tree structure explicit in the text, which may help the model learn the hierarchical organization of search.

After expanding all children of the current node, the search either:

- **Goes deeper** into one of the children: "Moving to Node #[child index]" followed by a new "Current State:" declaration matching that node. In DFS, the immediately generated child is explored next. In BFS, the exploration order is different but the text format is the same — only the pattern of which index is chosen changes.

- **Goal checks at a leaf:** if the node has only one number remaining, a goal check is performed:
  - Success: "[result], [target] equal: Goal Reached" (trajectory terminates).
  - Failure: "[result], [target] unequal: No Solution" (triggering backtracking).

- **Backtracks** to a previously generated sibling or ancestor: "Moving to Node #[ancestor or sibling index]" followed by the "Current State:" declaration for that node. This is how the search recovers from dead ends — it explicitly abandons the current path and resumes exploration from a node with unexplored children.

**Crucial detail — the serialization is a depth-first traversal, not a tree diagram.** The trajectory is a sequential list of operations in the order the search algorithm performs them. A reader reconstructing the tree from the text must follow the sequence of "Generated Node," "Moving to Node," and "Current State" operations. This is why it works as autoregressive training data: the sequence of operations has a left-to-right structure that a causal language model can learn to predict, even though the underlying search process is tree-structured.

**The Optimal Path format** (contrast condition in experiments) strips away all exploration and backtracking, keeping only the correct sequence of state transitions:

```
Current State: 18:[74, 24, 36, 44], Operations: []
Exploring Operation: 74+24=98, Resulting Numbers: [36, 44, 98]
Generated Node #2: [36, 44, 98] from Operation: 74+24=98
Current State: 18:[36, 44, 98], Operations: ['74+24=98']
Exploring Operation: 36+44=80, Resulting Numbers: [98, 80]
...
Current State: 18:[98, 80], Operations: ['74+24=98', '36+44=80']
Exploring Operation: 98-80=18, Resulting Numbers: [18]
18,18 equal: Goal Reached
```

Notice what is absent: no dead-end explorations, no "No Solution" declarations, no backtracking to siblings, no exploration of alternative first moves. The path is a single straight line from start to goal. This is what the Optimal Path baseline model is trained on.

#### The Countdown Task: Properties That Make It a Search Problem

Countdown is a generalization of the 24 Game. A problem instance consists of:

- A set of **input numbers** `$I = [n_1, n_2, \dots, n_k]$`. The paper uses `$k = 4$` input numbers.
- A **target number** `$T$` in the range 10 to 100.

The goal is to combine all input numbers using the four basic arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, integer division where the result is a positive integer) to produce exactly the target number. Each input number must be used exactly once. Operations are applied sequentially: at each step, the solver selects two numbers from the current set, applies one operation, and replaces the two numbers with the result, reducing the set size by one. After `$k-1$` operations, exactly one number remains — if it equals `$T$`, the problem is solved.

**Why this is a hard search problem.** The paper identifies Countdown's challenging properties:

- **High branching factor.** At a state with `$N$` remaining numbers, the number of possible actions is `$\binom{N}{2} \times 4$` — choose any pair of numbers and any of four operations. With 4 input numbers, the first step has `$\binom{4}{2} \times 4 = 6 \times 4 = 24$` possible actions. Some of these are invalid (division that produces non-integers or negatives when subtraction produces non-positives; the paper's implementation requires all intermediate results to be positive integers, as Countdown rules specify), but the branching factor remains substantial.

- **Combinatorial search space.** The total number of distinct operation sequences grows rapidly. The solver must efficiently navigate this space toward the target — random exploration will rarely find a solution because only a small fraction of paths produce the target.

- **Need for heuristic guidance.** The target number and input numbers provide strong signals about promising operations (e.g., if the target is a multiple of one of the input numbers, division or multiplication is suggested), but these signals must be integrated into a search procedure that also handles cases where no obvious heuristic applies.

- **Variable target makes memorization impossible.** Because the target is specified per problem and the held-out evaluation includes targets not seen during training, a model cannot simply memorize solutions — it must learn to search.

The paper restricts to 4-input problems because "these problems are challenging enough to have long search traces without the search traces exceeding a standard LM context window (for example, the search trajectories for games with 5 input numbers can be 60,000 tokens long)." This context length constraint is a practical engineering consideration: the 250M GPT-Neo model has a 4096-token context window, and search trajectories on complex 5-number problems would exceed this, making autoregressive training infeasible.

**Evaluation splits.** The paper holds out two types of test problems:
- **Seen targets, new inputs:** Target numbers that appeared in training, but combined with new sets of input numbers not seen during training.
- **New targets, new inputs:** Both the target number and the input numbers are from held-out targets (10% of the target range held out). This tests out-of-distribution generalization — can the model search effectively for targets it never saw in training?

#### Training Data Construction: The Symbolic Search Strategy Suite

The SoS dataset is generated by running **12 different symbolic search strategies** on a set of Countdown training problems, producing 500,000 search trajectories that are serialized in the SoS format. The paper designs this suite to be deliberately diverse, covering multiple search algorithms (BFS and DFS), multiple heuristic functions, and multiple hyperparameter settings, so that the trained model sees a wide distribution of search behaviors rather than imitating any single strategy.

**Underlying algorithms: BFS and DFS.** The paper uses two base search algorithms:

- **DFS (Depth-First Search, Algorithm 3 in Appendix):** DFS expands a state, then immediately expands one of its children, then one of that child's children, and so on until reaching a leaf (goal or dead end). If the leaf is a dead end, DFS backtracks to the most recent ancestor with unexplored children and resumes. This produces search trajectories that go deep quickly but may explore many leaves before finding a solution. In the serialized format, DFS trajectories show long chains of "Moving to Node" deep into the tree, followed by "No Solution" at leaves, then "Moving to Node" back up to explore siblings.

- **BFS (Breadth-First Search, Algorithm 4 in Appendix):** BFS expands all children of the current depth before moving deeper. At each depth, it generates all possible successor states, evaluates them with a heuristic, and keeps only the top `$b$` (the breadth limit). It then expands all survivors before moving to the next depth. In the serialized format, BFS trajectories show "Exploring Operation" sequences that generate all children at one level, followed by "Moving to Node" operations that cycle through the survivors before moving deeper.

**Heuristic functions.** Two simple heuristics guide both algorithms:

1. **Sum Heuristic** `$h_{\text{sum}}$`: Measures the absolute difference between the sum of all remaining input numbers and the target:
   
   $$h_{\text{sum}}(I, T) = |T - \sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i|$$
   
   where `$I = [n_1, \dots, n_k]$` is the current set of input numbers and `$T$` is the target number.
   
   **What it computes:** the distance between the sum of the available numbers and the target. If the sum is close to the target, the current state is promising because addition and subtraction can adjust the total to match the target. If the sum is far from the target, multiplication or division may be needed to rescale.
   
   **Why this form:** the sum of inputs is an upper bound on what can be achieved through addition alone, and a loose indicator of achievable values more generally. It is computationally trivial (one sum, one absolute difference), interpretable (the human designer understands why it works), and provides useful signal for pruning states where the sum is too far from the target to be salvageable.
   
   For DFS, the threshold for pruning is set to the value of the target: `$h_{\text{th}} = T$`. States with heuristic above this threshold are pruned.

2. **Multiply Heuristic** `$h_{\text{multiply}}$`: Measures the minimum distance between the sum of the remaining inputs and any factor of the target:
   
   $$h_{\text{multiply}}(I, T) = \min(\{ |f_j - \sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i| \}_{\forall j \in [1, m]})$$
   
   where `$[f_1, f_2, \dots, f_m]$` are the factors of the target number `$T$`.
   
   **What it computes:** for every factor `$f_j$` of the target `$T$`, compute the distance between that factor and the sum of available numbers. Return the smallest such distance. If the sum of available numbers is close to a factor of the target, the current state is promising because multiplication or division by the factor can reach the target.
   
   **Why this form:** reaching the target often requires producing one of its factors as an intermediate result (e.g., to reach 50, producing 25 or 10 as intermediate values is useful). This heuristic encourages the search toward states where the remaining numbers can be combined to produce a factor, from which the target is one multiplication away. It captures multiplicative structure that the sum heuristic misses — a set of numbers summing to 49 is far from target 50 by the sum heuristic, but if 25 is a factor, producing 25 and then doubling is a natural path that the sum heuristic would not recognize.

**DFS configurations.** Two configurations, one per heuristic:
- DFS with sum heuristic, pruning threshold `$h_{\text{th}} = T$`
- DFS with multiply heuristic, pruning threshold `$h_{\text{th}} = T$`

**BFS configurations.** Ten configurations, crossing two heuristics with five breadth limits:
- Heuristics: sum, multiply
- Breadth limits: `$b \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$`

For each BFS configuration, only the top `$b$` child states (as ranked by the heuristic) survive to the next depth. With `$b = 1$`, BFS reduces to greedy search — only the single best child is kept at each level. With `$b = 5$`, up to five children survive, providing broader exploration.

**Why 12 configurations?** The diversity serves two purposes. First, it prevents the model from learning a single search pattern — the model sees trajectories that look qualitatively different (DFS going deep quickly, BFS with `$b = 1$` being highly selective, BFS with `$b = 5$` being broad), and must learn a general search capability that works across these patterns. Second, the diversity creates variation in which states are visited and which problems are solved — a problem that stumps the sum-heuristic BFS with `$b = 2$` might be solved by the multiply-heuristic DFS, exposing the model to a wider range of successful problem-solving patterns. The paper explicitly notes the dataset contains both successful and unsuccessful trajectories: "only about 57% of the trajectories lead to the solution" — the model sees both search that finds the answer and search that terminates in failure, which teaches it what dead ends look like and when to abandon a path.

**Data splits and quantities.** The full dataset contains 500,000 problems, each with a SoS trajectory (sampled from one of the 12 strategies) and a corresponding optimal path (the correct steps only). The SoS portion contains 285,501 correct trajectories (57.1%). The optimal path dataset contains all 500,000 correct solutions — it has more correct examples because it includes the solution for every problem regardless of whether the specific sampled SoS trajectory for that problem found the solution. This is an important asymmetry: the SoS model sees fewer correct examples (285K) but richer patterns (exploration, backtracking, dead ends); the OP model sees more correct examples (500K) but impoverished patterns (straight-line solutions only).

The paper holds out 10% of targets for out-of-distribution evaluation, creating the "new targets + new inputs" test split. The "seen targets + new inputs" split uses targets from the training distribution but pairs them with new input number sets.

#### Model Architecture and Pretraining Procedure

**Model.** The paper uses a GPT-Neo model (Gao et al., 2020) — a transformer-based autoregressive language model — with 250 million parameters. The specific architecture (Appendix C.1):
- 16 transformer layers, all using global (not local) attention
- 16 attention heads
- Hidden size of 1024
- Context length of 4096 tokens
- Dropout of 0.1
- bf16 (bfloat16) precision
- Flash-Attention-2 (Dao et al., 2022) for memory-efficient attention computation

The 250M parameter scale was chosen as a balance: large enough to learn the search and arithmetic capabilities required for Countdown, small enough to train from scratch on 4× 80GB A100 GPUs within a reasonable time budget. The 4096 context length accommodates the longest search trajectories on 4-number problems without truncation.

**Training objective.** Both the SoS model and the Optimal Path model are trained with a standard causal language modeling objective (next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss). The model receives the serialized trajectory as a sequence of tokens and is trained to predict each token given the preceding tokens. There is no special loss weighting for search-specific tokens (e.g., goal checks, backtracking) — the model learns to generate them because they are part of the sequence it must predict.

**Training hyperparameters** (Appendix C.2 and C.3). Both models share identical settings unless noted:
- Training examples: 500,000 (SoS: search trajectories; OP: optimal paths)
- Batch size: 24 per GPU
- Gradient accumulation steps: 1
- Number of training steps: 50,000
- Optimizer: AdamW
- Weight decay: 0.01
- Warmup steps: 1
- Learning rate scheduler: Cosine
- Learning rate: `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`
- Evaluation steps: 500 (validation every 500 steps)
- Model selection: based on accuracy on a validation set of 1,000 problems

**The key asymmetry in training data composition.** The SoS model is trained on 500,000 trajectories containing only 285,501 correct solutions — the remaining 214,499 trajectories are unsuccessful searches that terminate without reaching the goal. The Optimal Path model is trained on all 500,000 problems with their correct solution paths. Both models train for the same number of gradient steps (50,000). This means the SoS model sees fewer correct solution paths (57% as many) but more total information about the search process — crucially, it sees the search operations (exploration, backtracking, goal checking) that lead to both success and failure, while the OP model sees only the linear sequence of correct steps.

**Design choice — training from scratch vs. finetuning a pretrained LM.** The paper trains the GPT-Neo model from scratch on the SoS dataset rather than finetuning a model pretrained on natural language. The rationale (implicit in the experimental design) is that the SoS format is a specialized domain-specific language with its own syntax (node indexing, state declarations, operation formatting) and semantics (arithmetic, search operations). A model pretrained on natural language would bring general linguistic knowledge but would still need to learn the SoS syntax and the arithmetic/search semantics from the Countdown data. Training from scratch ensures all model capacity is allocated to the task of interest, avoids potential interference from natural language priors, and makes the comparison between SoS and OP clean — both start from the same random initialization and differ only in training data composition.

**Inference procedure.** At evaluation time, the model is prompted with the initial state declaration (`Current State: [target]:[input numbers], Operations: []`) and generates autoregressively until it produces a termination token (either "Goal Reached" or the context window is exhausted). A generated trajectory is considered correct if the correct solution path `$\mathcal{P}$` is present anywhere in the generated trajectory `$\mathcal{T}$` — the model does not need to terminate immediately upon finding the solution (though the training data terminates at "Goal Reached"). This lenient correctness criterion means the model can explore beyond the solution point and still be credited with a correct answer, as long as the correct path appears somewhere in the output.

#### Policy Improvement Method 1: Self-Taught Reasoner (STaR)

STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) is an **expert iteration** method that alternates between generating solutions from the current model and finetuning on the subset of correct solutions. Algorithm 1 in the paper formalizes the procedure:

1. Initialize `$M_0$` as the pretrained SoS model.
2. For each iteration `$n = 1, \dots, N$` (the paper uses `$N = 3$`):
   a. Generate a search trajectory `$\mathcal{T}_i = M_{n-1}(x_i)$` for each training problem `$x_i$`, sampling with temperature 0.8.
   b. Filter to keep only trajectories where the correct solution path `$\mathcal{P}_i$` is present: `$\mathcal{D}_n = \{(x_i, \mathcal{T}_i) \mid \mathcal{P}_i \in \mathcal{T}_i\}$`.
   c. Finetune `$M_n$` on `$\mathcal{D}_n$`, but crucially **reset to the base SoS model** before finetuning: `$M_n = \text{train}(M_0, \mathcal{D}_n)$`.

**Key design choice — resetting to base model at each iteration.** The paper follows the original STaR procedure: each iteration finetunes from the original SoS checkpoint rather than from the previous iteration's model. The rationale (from Zelikman et al., 2022) is that this prevents the model from drifting into a distributional collapse where it only generates variations of previously correct solutions and loses the diversity needed to discover solutions to new problems. By always starting from the base SoS model (which has broad search capabilities from the diverse training data) and finetuning only on the new correct trajectories, each iteration acts as a form of data filtering — the base model's search distribution is enriched with more correct trajectories — without the compounding effects of iterative distributional shift.

**Sampling with temperature 0.8.** The relatively high temperature (the paper uses temperature 1.0 for APA, 0.8 for STaR) encourages diverse trajectory generation, which is important for the expert iteration loop to discover new correct solutions rather than only regenerating previously seen ones. Lower temperature would make the model more likely to reproduce its training trajectories, limiting the discovery of improved search strategies.

**Stopping criterion.** The paper runs STaR "until we see a convergence in performance on the validation set" — this occurs after 3 iterations, with accuracy plateauing (Figure 4c).

**STaR hyperparameters** (Appendix C.4):
- Training examples per iteration: 100,000 (the filtered correct trajectories)
- Batch size: 24
- Gradient accumulation steps: 1
- Number of training steps: 20,000 per iteration
- Weight decay: 0.01
- Warmup steps: 100
- Learning rate scheduler: Cosine
- Learning rate: `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`

**Why 100,000 examples?** The filtering step only keeps correct trajectories, so the number of training examples per iteration depends on the model's current accuracy. The paper generates trajectories for all training problems, filters correct ones, and uses 100,000 of them (or all available correct ones, whichever is fewer) for finetuning.

#### Policy Improvement Method 2: Advantage-Induced Policy Alignment (APA)

APA (Zhu et al., 2023) is an **actor-critic reinforcement learning** method designed for language model finetuning that is more stable than PPO. Algorithm 2 in the paper formalizes the procedure:

1. Initialize the policy `$\pi_0$` as the pretrained SoS model.
2. Create two copies of the SoS model:
   - `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` — a reference policy that remains frozen until periodically updated, used to prevent the policy from drifting too far from its initial state.
   - `$\pi_{\text{value}}$` — a value network initialized from the SoS model, trained to predict the expected future reward from each state.

3. For each training step `$t = 1, \dots, T$`:
   a. Roll out the current policy `$\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}$` on training problems to produce a dataset `$\mathcal{D}_t = \{(s_1, a_1, r_1), \dots, (s_n, a_n, r_n)\}$` of state-action-reward tuples. The paper uses 32 rollouts per problem, sampled with temperature 1.0.
   b. Update the policy by minimizing the APA loss:

   $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{APA}}(\theta; \mathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{(s,a) \in \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \pi_\theta(a|s) - \frac{\text{Adv}^{\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}}(s, a)}{\lambda} - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(a|s) \right]^2$$

   where `$\pi_\theta(a|s)$` is the current policy's log-probability of action `$a$` given state `$s$`, `$\pi_{\text{ref}}(a|s)$` is the reference policy's log-probability, `$\text{Adv}^{\pi_{\theta_{t-1}}}(s, a)$` is the advantage of action `$a$` in state `$s$` under the previous policy (computed using the value network), and `$\lambda$` is the advantage coefficient (set to 2.0).

   c. If validation reward converges, update the reference policy: `$\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_{\theta_t}$`. The paper resets the reference policy 3 times total (Figure 4b shows distinct colors for training after each reset).

**The reward function.** The paper uses "a straightforward reward function that takes into account the correctness and length of the generated trajectory." Correctness is binary: a reward for reaching the goal state. Length is incentivized: shorter trajectories (fewer states visited before finding the solution) receive higher reward. The exact reward function is not defined in the paper text or appendix, but the stated goal is to optimize both accuracy and search efficiency. The implicit structure is likely:

$$R(\mathcal{T}) = \mathbb{1}[\mathcal{P} \in \mathcal{T}] \times f(|\mathcal{T}|)$$

where `$f$` is a decreasing function of trajectory length, rewarding correct solutions that are found more quickly.

**What the APA loss computes.** The loss is a squared error between the policy's log-probability and a target that is the reference policy's log-probability plus a scaled advantage term. Intuitively: if the advantage is positive (the action was better than expected), the loss pushes the policy to increase its log-probability for that action relative to the reference policy. If the advantage is negative, the loss pushes the policy to decrease its log-probability. The `$\lambda = 2.0$` coefficient scales how strongly the advantage influences the target — larger `$\lambda$` means the advantage has less influence, keeping the policy closer to the reference.

**Why a squared loss rather than a log-likelihood objective?** The squared loss formulation (as opposed to the more common policy gradient objective `$-\log\pi_\theta(a|s) \times \text{Adv}$`) is the key design feature of APA. Zhu et al. (2023) show that treating the policy update as a regression problem (predict the target log-probability) is more stable than the standard policy gradient, particularly for language models where the action space is the full vocabulary and advantage estimates can be noisy. The squared error is bounded below by zero and is less sensitive to outlier advantages than a product formulation would be.

**The value network.** The value network `$\pi_{\text{value}}$` is trained (via a critic loss not shown in the simplified Algorithm 2) to predict the expected return from each state. The advantage is then computed as:

$$\text{Adv}(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma V(s') - V(s)$$

where `$r(s,a)$` is the immediate reward, `$V(s)$` is the value network's estimate for the current state, `$V(s')$` is the estimate for the next state, and `$\gamma = 1.0$` (no discount, from Appendix C.5). With `$\gamma = 1.0$` and sparse rewards (rewards only at the goal), the advantage essentially measures how much an action increases or decreases the probability of eventually reaching the goal, adjusted for trajectory length.

**Why APA over PPO?** The paper explicitly states: "We chose APA over other methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) due to its stability and robustness to changes in hyperparameters." Language model RL is notoriously unstable — small changes in learning rate or clipping parameters can cause the model to collapse to degenerate outputs. APA's regression-based formulation and reference policy mechanism provide more stable training.

**Reference policy resetting as implicit `$\lambda$` annealing.** The paper observes: "We observe that updating the reference policy whenever the validation reward converges results in further policy improvement... This shifting of the reference distribution can be interpreted as a means to reduce the weight assigned to staying close to the reference distribution (the `$\lambda$` parameter in the APA objective)." When the reference policy is reset to the current (improved) policy, the KL penalty effectively tightens around the new, better distribution, allowing the policy to move further from its original initialization than a fixed reference would permit. The paper found this more stable than explicitly annealing the `$\lambda$` coefficient over training.

**APA hyperparameters** (Appendix C.5):
- Number of rollouts per problem: 32
- Temperature for sampling: 1.0 (higher than STaR's 0.8, encouraging more exploration for the RL process)
- Batch size: 8
- Online epochs: 2 (each batch of rollouts is reused for 2 gradient updates)
- Gradient accumulation steps: 1
- Scaling of reward: None (raw rewards used without normalization)
- Value loss coefficient: 10
- `$\gamma$` (discount factor): 1.0
- `$\lambda$` (advantage coefficient): 2.0
- Learning rate scheduler: None (constant learning rate)
- Learning rate: `$1 \times 10^{-6}$` (an order of magnitude smaller than pretraining and STaR, appropriate for stable RL finetuning)

**Training dynamics and convergence.** The model improves over approximately 4000 training steps before validation accuracy plateaus (Figure 4b). At this point, the reference policy is reset to the current model, and training resumes. This reset-improvement-plateau cycle repeats, with the paper observing improvements after 3 such resets before convergence. The total APA training thus involves approximately 12,000–16,000 steps across three reference policy cycles.

**Why 32 rollouts per problem?** More rollouts provide better advantage estimates (less variance in the Monte Carlo return) but increase computational cost. Thirty-two represents a practical balance — enough samples to get reasonable estimates of action values for the Countdown state space, without making each training iteration prohibitively expensive.

#### Metrics for Analyzing Search Strategy Alignment

To understand what search strategies the trained models develop (beyond just measuring accuracy), the paper defines two complementary alignment metrics in Section 4.

**Alignment of Correctness.** This measures whether two search strategies solve the same set of problems. Formally, it is the Pearson correlation between binary vectors indicating whether each test problem is solved by strategy A and by strategy B. A correlation of 1.0 means both strategies succeed and fail on exactly the same problems. A correlation of 0.0 means the patterns of success and failure are independent.

**What it reveals:** if the SoS model has high correctness alignment with a particular symbolic strategy, it suggests the model is effectively imitating that strategy's problem-solving capabilities. If the SoS model has low alignment with all symbolic strategies, it suggests the model has developed a capability profile that is not simply a mixture or imitation of the training strategies — potentially indicating novel problem-solving behavior.

**Alignment of States Visited.** This measures the overlap in the specific states (nodes in the search tree) visited by two strategies. Formally:

$$\text{State Alignment}(\mathcal{T}_1, \mathcal{T}_2) = \frac{|\mathcal{T}_1 \cap \mathcal{T}_2|}{\max(|\mathcal{T}_1|, |\mathcal{T}_2|)}$$

where `$|\mathcal{T}_1|$` and `$|\mathcal{T}_2|$` are the number of states in the two trajectories, and `$|\mathcal{T}_1 \cap \mathcal{T}_2|$` is the number of states that appear in both trajectories. This is computed per problem and then averaged across all test problems. A score of 1.0 means the two trajectories visit exactly the same set of states (though possibly in different orders). A score of 0.0 means they visit disjoint states.

**What it reveals:** this is a stricter measure than correctness alignment — two strategies might solve the same problems but visit very different states to do so, suggesting different search procedures. High state alignment with a particular symbolic strategy indicates the model is closely following that strategy's exploration pattern. Low state alignment with all symbolic strategies while maintaining high accuracy suggests the model has developed a genuinely different way of searching the space.

**Why both metrics?** Correctness alignment captures outcome similarities; state alignment captures process similarities. A model could achieve similar accuracy to a symbolic strategy while using a different search process (low state alignment, moderate correctness alignment), or it could replicate both the outcomes and the process (high on both). The paper's findings (Figures 3b and 3c) — that the SoS model has moderate state alignment with DFS-sum (0.57) and low alignment with all other strategies (0.27–0.47), and similarly moderate correctness alignment patterns — suggest the model is not simply imitating any single training strategy but has developed a blended or partially novel search approach.

#### Design Philosophy: Why This Whole Pipeline?

Stepping back from the individual components, the SoS pipeline embodies a specific thesis about how to teach language models to reason:

**First, expose the model to the process, not just the outcome.** The core experiment — SoS vs. Optimal Paths — is designed to isolate the effect of showing models search trajectories (with mistakes, backtracking, dead ends, and recovery) versus showing them only clean correct solutions. The result (51.27% vs. 25.73%) is the paper's primary evidence for the claim that process data is more valuable than outcome data for learning to solve problems requiring search.

**Second, diversity of search strategies matters.** Rather than generating all training data from a single best-performing symbolic solver, the paper deliberately includes 12 different strategies — some weak (BFS with `$b=1$` is essentially greedy), some strong, some efficient on certain problem types and weak on others. This diversity is hypothesized to teach the model a general search capability that transcends any single strategy, enabling it to adapt its approach per problem rather than rigidly following one procedure.

**Third, keep heuristic evaluation implicit to enable improvement.** By not including numerical heuristic scores in the text, the model must learn to evaluate states internally — a capability that can be refined during policy improvement. If heuristics were explicit in the training data, the model could only learn to copy the heuristic values it was trained on; with implicit heuristics, the model learns a latent evaluation function that can be shaped by the reward signal during RL finetuning.

**Fourth, self-improvement is the ultimate objective.** The pretraining stage teaches the model that search exists and gives it a distribution of search behaviors to start from. The policy improvement stage (STaR or APA) is where the model potentially transcends its training data — discovering search strategies that solve problems none of the original symbolic solvers could handle. The 36% figure for previously unsolved problems and the ~4% figure for problems that stump all 12 symbolic strategies are presented as evidence that this transcendence actually occurs, not just as performance numbers.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Search as a Learnable, Internal Capability — Not an External Scaffold

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is reframing search from something done *to* a language model (via external symbolic orchestration) to something the language model *does* — an internal, learned capability encoded entirely in its weights and expressed through autoregressive generation. This is not simply a different architecture or training recipe; it is a different claim about what kind of thing an LM can become.

**What the field assumed before this work.** The dominant paradigm for combining LMs with search — exemplified by Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2024) and its descendants — treats the LM as a stateless subroutine: a proposer that suggests candidate next steps given a current state, and an evaluator that scores or filters those candidates. The search algorithm (BFS, DFS, MCTS) is external code that calls the LM repeatedly, maintains the search frontier, handles backtracking, and decides when to terminate. In this paradigm, the LM never "knows" it is searching — it simply generates plausible continuations and provides confidence estimates when asked. The search intelligence resides in the hand-crafted algorithm, not in the model. This approach has a natural ceiling: if the LM fails to generate a correct candidate for a crucial state, no amount of external search can recover, because the correct action was never in the proposal distribution. The LM cannot improve its search behavior through experience because it remains frozen.

SoS inverts this relationship. The search procedure — which states to expand, when to backtrack, how to order exploration, how to recognize a dead end — is generated by the model itself, token by token, as part of a single autoregressive sequence. The model learns not just what good actions look like (the proposer role) but the meta-cognitive skill of organizing exploration across a problem space. When the SoS model generates "Moving to Node #0,1" after exhausting the #0,0 subtree, it is not executing an external algorithm's backtracking instruction — it is generating backtracking as a learned behavior, conditioned on having encountered dead ends at #0,0's children. The distinction is crucial: in Tree of Thoughts, the LM would need to be *told* to backtrack by the external BFS controller. In SoS, the model *decides* to backtrack because its training data taught it that dead ends are followed by revisiting unexplored siblings.

**Why this is a fundamental shift, not incremental.** This is not a more efficient way to do the same thing. It changes the *locus of intelligence* from the algorithm designer to the model, with three downstream consequences that the paper demonstrates empirically:

1. **The search strategy can adapt per-problem.** Because the search behavior is generated from learned weights rather than hard-coded rules, the model can vary its exploration pattern based on the problem's characteristics. The alignment analysis (Figures 3b, 3c) shows that the base SoS model does not closely match any single symbolic strategy — it is not doing "DFS with sum heuristic" or "BFS with breadth 3." It has learned a blended approach that draws on multiple strategies from its training distribution. A symbolic system would need explicit meta-reasoning logic to switch between BFS and DFS per problem; the SoS model acquires this adaptivity from the diversity of its training data.

2. **The search capability can improve through reinforcement.** This is the paper's central ambition, and it would be incoherent under the external-scaffold paradigm. You cannot "improve" a hand-coded BFS algorithm through gradient descent on the LM's outputs. But when search is internal — when backtracking, state evaluation, and exploration ordering are all latent behaviors of the same neural network — then optimizing that network for correctness can refine every component simultaneously. The policy improvement results (Figures 4, 5) are evidence that this refinement actually occurs: the finetuned models solve 36% of problems that stumped the symbolic solvers, and their state visitation patterns shift away from the training distribution (Figure 5a), suggesting they are developing search behaviors not present in the original data.

3. **The model learns an implicit world model.** The paper makes this point explicitly in the Discussion (Section 7): "Unlike symbolic search that relies on an explicit environment model, SoS models simulate state transitions themselves." When an SoS model generates "Exploring Operation: 66-13=53, Resulting Numbers: [39, 33, 53]", it is not querying an external arithmetic module — it is computing the result of the operation and the new state internally, using the same parameters that generate language. The very low arithmetic error rate (0.8% for state exploration errors, ~2 arithmetic errors per trajectory; Appendix Table 2) demonstrates that this internal simulation is reliable. This addresses a core criticism of pretrained LMs — that they lack internal world models for planning (LeCun, 2023) — not by dismissing it but by showing that the right training data can induce such models.

**Evidence anchoring.** Figure 3a: the SoS model achieves 51.27% accuracy versus 25.73% for optimal-path training, using the same architecture, same number of gradient steps, and *fewer* correct examples (285K vs. 500K). The 25-percentage-point gap cannot be explained by data quantity — the OP model saw nearly twice as many correct solutions. It must be attributed to *what* the SoS model learned from the search trajectories that the OP model did not learn from clean solutions: the operations of exploration, backtracking, and recovery from dead ends.

### Innovation 2: Optimal Trajectories Are Impoverished Training Data for Reasoning

The paper provides a clean, controlled experiment that isolates the value of *process data* — the messy, branching, error-containing records of search — over *outcome data* — the clean, linear solution paths — for training models to solve problems requiring multi-step reasoning. While prior work has argued intuitively for the value of learning from mistakes, the SoS vs. Optimal Path comparison (Figure 3a) is a remarkably clean demonstration: same model architecture (GPT-Neo 250M), same training objective (causal language modeling), same number of gradient steps (50,000), same number of training examples (500,000), same evaluation protocol. The only difference is the *information content* of those examples.

**What this experiment isolates.** The OP model sees every problem's correct solution — 500,000 demonstrations of the exact sequence of arithmetic operations that leads from input numbers to target. If learning to solve Countdown were purely a matter of learning which arithmetic operations to apply, the OP model should excel: it has seen more correct operation sequences than the SoS model (which only has correct solutions in 57.1% of its trajectories). Yet the OP model achieves half the accuracy. The gap must be explained by what the OP model *doesn't* see that the SoS model does: dead ends, backtracking, the experience of exploring a branch and discovering it doesn't work, the contrast between paths that lead to the goal and paths that don't. The SoS model learns not just *what* a solution looks like, but *how to find one* — the process of navigating from an unfamiliar initial state through a combinatorial space to a goal, using exploration and error recovery.

**Why this challenges a dominant assumption.** The default approach in the reasoning and math communities has been to train or finetune models on correct reasoning traces — chain-of-thought solutions, proof steps, calculation sequences — filtering out incorrect examples. The intuition is straightforward: you want the model to learn correct reasoning, so show it correct reasoning. The SoS result suggests this intuition is wrong for problems requiring search. A model trained only on correct paths never learns to recognize dead ends, never learns that certain states lead to failure, never learns the *contrast* between promising and unpromising intermediate states. It is like training a chess player by showing them only winning games, with all blunders and alternative lines removed: they might memorize opening sequences and checkmate patterns, but they will never learn to evaluate positions or recover from a mistake.

**Connection to the snowballing error problem.** The paper's opening diagnosis — that autoregressive models suffer from snowballing errors because a single mistake compounds without recovery — is directly addressed by this data choice. The OP model, having never seen mistakes or recovery, has no mechanism for detecting or correcting its own errors. When it generates a suboptimal first operation at inference time (e.g., "74-44=30" when the correct first move is "74+24=98"), it has no learned behavior for recognizing this as a problem or trying an alternative — it was never trained to backtrack. The SoS model, having seen thousands of trajectories where an early suboptimal choice is followed by exploration of deeper states, eventual recognition of dead ends ("19,50 unequal: No Solution"), and backtracking to try a different first move, has learned that initial mistakes are recoverable and that exploration of alternatives is the standard procedure. This is a form of *procedural knowledge* — knowing how to search — that is orthogonal to the declarative knowledge of which operations lead to a specific target.

**The asymmetry in training data composition matters.** The SoS model's training set is richer not despite having fewer correct solutions but *because* of the unsuccessful trajectories. The 214,499 unsuccessful search trajectories contain exactly the information the OP model lacks: states that were explored but don't lead to the goal, patterns of backtracking that indicate dead-end recognition, the experience of searching without finding. This negative information — knowing what doesn't work — is as valuable for learning to search as positive information about what does, and it is entirely absent from optimal-path training.

**Evidence anchoring.** Figure 3a with accompanying text. The SoS model achieves 51.27% on held-out inputs vs. OP's 25.73%, a roughly 2× improvement — and 49.00% vs. OP's (estimated, from the bar chart) roughly 24% on held-out targets. The gap persists across both generalization conditions, ruling out the possibility that the SoS model is simply memorizing training trajectories.

### Innovation 3: Self-Improvement Can Solve Problems Beyond the Training Distribution's Reach

The most ambitious claim in the paper — and the one that, if it generalizes, has the broadest implications — is not that training on search trajectories improves accuracy over optimal-path training. That is a significant but incremental data-engineering insight. The more fundamental claim is that a model which has internalized search through pretraining on diverse search trajectories can, when subsequently optimized for correctness, **transcend the capabilities of the symbolic strategies used to generate its training data**. This is the "learning to search" claim in the strongest sense: the model is not merely imitating or interpolating among known search strategies but is potentially discovering new ones.

**The diagnostic test: problems unsolvable by any training strategy.** The paper constructs a clean test for this claim by identifying two categories of problems from the training set:

1. **Previously unsolved problems (Figure 5b):** 10,000 problems that none of the 12 symbolic strategies solved during dataset generation. If a finetuned SoS model solves these, it is doing something the training strategies could not do — not just reproducing a known solution path.

2. **Difficult problems (Figure 5c):** 10,000 problems that are provably unsolvable by *any* of the symbolic strategies in the training suite. This is a stronger test because it eliminates the possibility that the original dataset generation simply missed a solution that one of the strategies *could* have found with more search budget or a different random seed.

**The result.** The finetuned models solve approximately 36% of the previously unsolved problems and 4% of the difficult problems. These numbers are modest in absolute terms — 4% means the model fails on 96% of the hardest problems — but their significance is qualitative. The fact that *any* of these problems are solved means the model has developed problem-solving capabilities that are not present in its training distribution. Something about the combination of search trajectory pretraining and correctness-based reinforcement enables the model to find solution paths that systematically eluded BFS with breadth limits up to 5 and DFS with two different heuristics.

**What mechanism could explain this?** The paper does not provide a mechanistic account, but the most plausible hypothesis (which the alignment analysis partially supports) involves two factors:

1. **More flexible state evaluation.** The symbolic strategies use rigid, hand-designed heuristics (sum distance, factor distance) to evaluate states. These heuristics are simple enough to be computed in closed form but may miss promising states that a learned value function — developed implicitly during pretraining and refined during RL — can recognize. The shift in state visitation patterns after policy improvement (Figure 5a), where the finetuned models visit more states associated with the multiply heuristic, suggests the model is developing preferences that differ from any single training strategy.

2. **Adaptive search depth and breadth.** The symbolic strategies commit to a fixed algorithm (BFS or DFS) and fixed parameters (breadth limit, heuristic threshold). The SoS model, having been exposed to multiple strategies during pretraining, can vary its exploration pattern within a single trajectory — going broad at promising branch points and deep along promising paths. The policy improvement stage reinforces this adaptivity by rewarding efficient solutions (shorter trajectories), which pressures the model to avoid wasting exploration on unpromising branches.

**Why this is a fundamental claim, not incremental.** If this result holds — if models trained on search trajectories can genuinely discover search strategies that outperform the symbolic systems used to train them — it changes the relationship between human-designed algorithms and learned behaviors. The symbolic solvers become a *scaffold*: they provide the initial demonstration that search exists and can be effective, but the model is not bounded by their limitations. The policy improvement stage is where the scaffold is potentially transcended. This is analogous to the role of human game data in AlphaGo: the initial supervised learning from human games teaches the model that Go is playable and what good moves look like, but the reinforcement learning stage (self-play) is where the model develops moves and strategies no human has played. The paper is gesturing at a similar dynamic for reasoning: human-designed search strategies provide the initial curriculum, and reinforcement for correctness enables the model to go beyond.

**Limitations of this claim.** The 4% figure on difficult problems is small, and the paper does not analyze *which* difficult problems are solved — are they structurally different from the unsolved ones? Are there common patterns in the solutions the model finds that the symbolic strategies missed? Without this analysis, the "discovery of new strategies" claim remains suggestive rather than proven. Additionally, the difficulty of the "difficult" problems is defined relative to the specific 12 strategies in the training suite — it is possible that a 13th symbolic strategy (e.g., BFS with a different heuristic) would solve them. The claim would be stronger if accompanied by an analysis showing that the solved problems have structural properties that systematically challenge the heuristic assumptions of the training strategies.

**Evidence anchoring.** Figures 5b and 5c. The 36% and 4% numbers come from evaluating finetuned models on specifically constructed test sets of problems that the symbolic strategies failed to solve during dataset generation. The error bars (95% binomial confidence intervals) provide statistical reliability for these proportions.

### Innovation 4: The Explicit/Implicit Boundary as a Design Principle for Teaching Meta-Cognitive Skills

The paper makes a deliberate and non-obvious design choice about which search operations are represented as explicit text in the training data (and therefore learned as overt behaviors the model generates) and which are left implicit (and therefore learned as latent representations the model can refine internally). This choice is presented as a practical decision in Section 3, but it encodes a deeper insight about how to teach meta-cognitive skills to language models: **explicit operations provide the structural scaffolding that makes the skill learnable; implicit operations provide the representational flexibility that makes the skill improvable.**

**The explicit operations — current state, goal checking, backtracking, exploration choice — serve as the "syntax" of search.** By writing these out as text, the training data provides clear, token-level supervision for the sequence of operations that constitutes a search procedure. The model learns that after generating children for a node, it should either move deeper, check a goal condition, or backtrack. It learns the formatting conventions: how to declare a state, how to index nodes, how to signal success or failure. This is analogous to teaching someone the rules of chess by demonstrating move notation — you're not teaching them to play well, but you're giving them the vocabulary and mechanics needed to participate in the game at all. The low error rates in SoS-generated trajectories (0.8% exploration errors, ~2 arithmetic errors per trajectory) suggest that this explicit scaffolding works: the model reliably produces syntactically valid search traces.

**The implicit operations — heuristic evaluation, pruning, state value estimation — serve as the "semantics" of search.** These are the judgments about which states are promising, which should be abandoned, and how to rank alternatives. By *not* including numerical heuristic scores in the text, the paper prevents the model from learning a brittle mapping from state descriptions to copied heuristic values. Instead, the model must learn an internal evaluation function from the *pattern* of which states the symbolic solvers chose to explore versus prune. A state that appears in the trajectory has been implicitly judged as promising; a state that doesn't appear has been implicitly judged as not worth pursuing. The model learns to replicate these judgments not by copying scores but by internalizing the underlying criteria that make a state worth exploring.

**Why this boundary matters for self-improvement.** If heuristic scores were explicit in the training data, the policy improvement stage would face a dilemma: either keep the original heuristic scores (in which case the model cannot improve its evaluation beyond the hand-designed heuristic's quality) or allow the model to generate different scores (in which case the model would be producing values inconsistent with its training distribution, likely causing incoherent behavior). By keeping heuristics implicit, the policy improvement stage can refine the internal evaluation function through the reward signal without any conflict with explicit text supervision. The model learns to value states that actually lead to solutions (as determined by the correctness reward) rather than states that match the sum or multiply heuristic. The shift in visitation patterns after policy improvement (Figure 5a) — the finetuned models prefer states associated with the multiply heuristic more than the base model — is evidence that this refinement is occurring: the model's internal evaluation is being reshaped by the reward signal, and this reshaping manifests as changes in which states it chooses to explore.

**Connection to the broader "system 1 vs. system 2" framing.** The explicit operations correspond to System 2 reasoning made visible — the model "thinks out loud" about where it is in the search tree and what operation it is performing. The implicit operations correspond to System 1 judgments that are fast, intuitive, and not accessible to verbal report — the model "feels" that a state is promising without articulating why. Prior work on chain-of-thought and related techniques makes all reasoning explicit (every step is written out). The SoS framework's insight is that for search specifically, keeping some operations implicit is *better* because it forces the development of internal evaluation capabilities that can be refined through reinforcement, while keeping structural operations explicit is *necessary* because it provides the learnable syntax of search behavior. This is a more nuanced position than either "make everything explicit" (chain-of-thought) or "let the model figure everything out implicitly" (pure RL), and it represents a design principle that may generalize to other meta-cognitive skills beyond search.

**Evidence anchoring.** The design choice is stated in Section 3, but its effectiveness is demonstrated indirectly through the overall results. The low error rates in trajectory generation (Appendix Table 2) validate the explicit scaffolding. The ability of policy improvement to shift visitation patterns (Figure 5a) and solve previously unsolved problems (Figures 5b, 5c) validates that the implicit evaluations are being refined. The ablation that would directly test this choice — training a model with explicit heuristic scores and measuring whether policy improvement is less effective — is not performed, so this innovation is more of a conceptual contribution than an experimentally validated one.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the Countdown game, a generalization of the 24 Game. A problem instance consists of 4 input numbers and a target number (range 10–100). The goal is to combine all input numbers using the four basic arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, with all intermediate results required to be positive integers) to reach exactly the target number. The paper constructs a synthetic dataset of 500,000 problems. To evaluate generalization, two held-out test sets are created: (1) **Seen targets, new inputs** — target numbers that appeared in training but paired with new sets of input numbers; (2) **New targets, new inputs** — both the target number and input numbers are from 10% of the target range held out during training, testing out-of-distribution generalization. The search trajectories in the training set are generated by 12 symbolic search strategies (BFS with breadth limits b ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} using two heuristics, plus DFS with two heuristics). Only approximately 57.1% of these trajectories (285,501 out of 500,000) actually reach the correct solution. The optimal path dataset contains all 500,000 correct solutions stripped of exploration and backtracking.

- **Base model(s).** A GPT-Neo model (Gao et al., 2020) with 250 million parameters is trained from scratch — not finetuned from a pretrained checkpoint — on the SoS dataset. The architecture uses 16 transformer layers with global attention, 16 attention heads, a hidden size of 1024, a context length of 4096 tokens, dropout of 0.1, bf16 precision, and Flash-Attention-2 (Dao et al., 2022). The 250M scale was chosen as a balance: large enough to learn the search and arithmetic capabilities required for Countdown (the model must internally simulate state transitions and perform arithmetic without an external environment model), small enough to train from scratch on 4× 80GB A100 GPUs in a reasonable time budget. The 4096 context length accommodates search trajectories on 4-number problems without truncation (the paper notes that 5-number problems can produce trajectories exceeding 60,000 tokens, making them infeasible for this architecture).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **accuracy**, defined as the percentage of test problems for which the model generates a trajectory T containing the correct solution path P. Formally, correctness is a binary function indicating whether the correct path to the solution is present anywhere in the generated trajectory — the model does not need to terminate immediately upon finding the solution. To quantitatively understand the search strategies used by trained models, two additional alignment metrics are defined. **Alignment of Correctness** measures whether two search strategies solve the same set of problems, computed as the Pearson correlation between binary vectors indicating which test problems each strategy solves correctly. **Alignment of States Visited** measures overlap in the specific states (nodes in the search tree) visited by two strategies: `State Alignment(T1, T2) = |T1 ∩ T2| / max(|T1|, |T2|)`, computed per problem and averaged across all test problems. To analyze trajectory quality, an error analysis categorizes errors into four types: arithmetic (hallucinating invalid operations, dividing by zero), formatting (invalid output syntax), exploration (jumping to a node that has no parent), and other (a child node has the wrong set of numbers).

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is the **Optimal Path (OP)** model: the identical GPT-Neo 250M architecture trained from scratch on the 500,000 correct solution trajectories (clean, linear sequences with no dead ends or backtracking) using the same causal language modeling objective and identical hyperparameters (50,000 training steps, AdamW optimizer, learning rate 1 × 10⁻⁵, cosine scheduler, batch size 24 per GPU). Both models train for the same number of gradient steps and see the same number of training examples (500,000), but the OP model sees nearly twice as many correct solutions (500,000 vs. 285,501) and no unsuccessful search data. The SoS model is also compared against the **average accuracy of the 12 symbolic search strategies** used to generate its training data — not as a baseline in the traditional sense, but as a reference point for how well the learned model performs relative to the hand-crafted systems that generated its training distribution. For policy improvement experiments, the **base SoS model** (before any finetuning) serves as the reference baseline.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Unlike the "Inference Compute Scaling" paper analyzed in prior sections, this paper does not use a formal compute budget metric for comparing methods at inference time. The primary comparison between SoS and OP is based on equal training data quantity (500,000 examples) and equal training steps (50,000), with no inference-time budget constraints. For policy improvement (STaR and APA), the compute cost is measured implicitly in terms of training steps (20,000 per STaR iteration, approximately 4,000 per APA reference-policy cycle). The paper does not report wall-clock time or FLOPs for training or inference. This is a notable methodological difference from the compute-optimal scaling literature — the paper's focus is on whether training on search trajectories enables qualitatively different capabilities, not on computational efficiency per unit of accuracy gain.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** Model selection is done based on accuracy on a validation set of 1,000 problems (Section 4, Appendix C). For the main SoS vs. OP comparison, models are evaluated on 10,000 test problems for each of the two held-out conditions (seen targets + new inputs, and new targets + new inputs). For policy improvement, the previously-unsolved and difficult-problem evaluations use 10,000 problems each, sampled from the training set — these are problems that the symbolic strategies failed to solve during dataset generation, not held-out test problems. Error bars throughout are 95% binomial confidence intervals (stated in Figures 3–6 and Appendix Table 2). The paper does not use k-fold cross-validation for the main experiments, though the "new targets + new inputs" split serves as an out-of-distribution generalization test.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Pretraining on Streams of Search vs. Optimal Paths

The headline finding is that **training on search trajectories substantially outperforms training on optimal paths alone, despite the SoS model seeing 43% fewer correct solution examples**. On the held-out inputs test set (seen targets, new input numbers), the SoS model achieves 51.27% accuracy compared to 25.73% for the Optimal Path model — a roughly 2× improvement (Figure 3a). On the held-out targets test set (new targets, new inputs, testing out-of-distribution generalization), the SoS model achieves 49.00% accuracy. The paper does not report the OP model's number on this split in the main text, but the bar chart in Figure 3a shows a similar proportional gap — the OP model appears to reach approximately 24% on the held-out targets condition.

This gap is striking for several reasons. Both models use the identical architecture (GPT-Neo 250M), training objective (causal language modeling), number of training examples (500,000), and number of gradient steps (50,000). The OP model actually sees **more correct solutions** — 500,000 vs. 285,501 — because its dataset contains the correct path for every problem, while the SoS dataset contains unsuccessful search trajectories for 42.9% of problems. The SoS model's advantage must therefore be attributed to the *information content* of the search trajectories: the exploration patterns, backtracking operations, dead-end recognition, and contrast between successful and unsuccessful paths that are entirely absent from the OP training data.

The SoS model achieves slightly lower accuracy than the **average accuracy of the 12 symbolic search strategies** used to construct its training dataset (Figure 3a, dashed line). This is an expected and acknowledged limitation: the symbolic strategies have access to an explicit environment model for state transitions (the arithmetic is computed by actual code, not learned) and hand-crafted heuristic functions that directly evaluate state quality. The SoS model must learn both the arithmetic operations and the state transitions internally — a substantially harder problem. Despite this disadvantage, the model generates valid trajectories with low error rates: only 0.8% exploration errors (jumping to non-existent parent nodes) and approximately 2 arithmetic errors per trajectory on average (Appendix Table 2). This indicates the model has learned a reliable internal world model of the Countdown domain.

#### Search Strategy Alignment: The SoS Model Does Not Simply Imitate a Single Training Strategy

To understand *what* the SoS model learned, the paper measures alignment between the model's generated search trajectories and the 12 symbolic strategies from its training data. The results (Figures 3b and 3c) reveal that **the SoS model does not predominantly use any single training strategy**.

For **Alignment of States Visited** (Figure 3c), which measures the overlap in specific nodes explored during search:
- The highest correlation is with DFS using the sum heuristic: 0.57.
- The lowest correlation is with BFS using breadth limit 5 and the sum heuristic: 0.27.
- Correlations with other strategies fall between 0.27 and 0.47, with no strategy reaching a correlation above 0.60.

A perfect imitator of a single strategy would show high correlation (~0.9+) with that strategy and low correlation with others. The SoS model shows moderate correlations across multiple strategies, particularly those using the sum heuristic, suggesting it has learned a **blended search approach** that draws on multiple strategies from its training distribution.

For **Alignment of Correctness** (Figure 3b), which measures whether the SoS model succeeds and fails on the same problems as each symbolic strategy:
- The pattern mirrors the state alignment results: higher alignment with sum-heuristic strategies, lower with multiply-heuristic strategies.
- Again, no single strategy dominates — the model's pattern of successes and failures does not closely match any individual symbolic solver.

This blended behavior is evidence for the paper's claim that training on *diverse* search trajectories (12 different strategies) produces a more general search capability than training on any single strategy would. The model has learned to adapt its exploration behavior per problem rather than rigidly following one search algorithm.

#### Policy Improvement: STaR and APA Both Improve Accuracy

Starting from the pretrained SoS model, both policy improvement methods produce statistically significant accuracy gains (Figure 4a).

**STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner):** After 3 iterations of expert iteration — generating trajectories from the current model, filtering for correctness, and finetuning the base model on the resulting correct trajectories — the STaR model achieves:
- On seen targets + new inputs: approximately 55.59% (Figure 4c, "STaR 3," and Appendix Table 1 confirms this exact figure). Note: this is approximately a 4.3 percentage point improvement over the base SoS model's 51.27%.
- On new targets + new inputs: approximately 53.34% (Figure 4c, Table 1). This is approximately a 4.3 percentage point improvement over the base SoS model's 49.00%.
- The improvement is progressive across iterations (Figure 4c): STaR iteration 1 improves over base SoS, iteration 2 improves further, and iteration 3 provides a final gain before convergence. The paper states "we see that after three iterations, the finetuned SoS+STaR model solves an additional 5% of the held-out inputs test set beyond the base SoS model."

**APA (Advantage-Induced Policy Alignment):** After approximately 12,000–16,000 training steps across three reference policy resets (Figure 4b), the APA model achieves:
- On seen targets + new inputs: approximately 56.52% (Appendix Table 1).
- On new targets + new inputs: approximately 54.23% (Appendix Table 1).

APA shows an improvement of approximately 5–6 percentage points over the base SoS model, slightly higher than STaR's 4–5 percentage point improvement, though the paper does not claim this difference is statistically significant.

**Training dynamics for APA (Figure 4b):** The validation accuracy curve shows a characteristic pattern: accuracy increases over approximately 4,000 training steps, then plateaus. At this point, the reference policy π_ref is reset to the current model, and training resumes — shown in Figure 4b as distinct colored segments. Each reset allows the policy to move further from its initial distribution. The paper observes improvements after 3 such resets before convergence, demonstrating that the reference-resetting strategy is effective at enabling continued policy improvement beyond what a fixed reference policy would permit.

#### Solving Previously Unsolved Problems

The most important test for the paper's "learning to search" claim is whether the finetuned models can solve problems that the symbolic strategies used to generate the training data could not solve. The paper constructs two diagnostic test sets from the training data (Figure 5b, 5c):

- **Previously unsolved problems (Figure 5b):** 10,000 problems from the SoS training set that were not solved by the symbolic strategies when the dataset was generated. The base SoS model solves a baseline fraction of these (not explicitly reported as a number, but visually in Figure 5b the base SoS bar appears to be around 25–30%). The STaR-finetuned model and APA-finetuned model each solve approximately 36% of these previously unsolved problems — a substantial increase over the base model, and more importantly, solving problems that eluded the symbolic strategies used to train the model. This is the headline finding: **the model has developed problem-solving capabilities not present in its training distribution.**

- **Difficult problems (Figure 5c):** 10,000 problems that are not solvable by *any* of the 12 symbolic strategies used to train the SoS models — a stronger test than "previously unsolved," which might include problems that a symbolic strategy *could* have solved with a different random seed or more search budget. The finetuned models solve approximately 4% of these difficult problems. While this absolute number is small (the model fails on 96% of these hardest problems), its qualitative significance is that **the model can find solutions to problems that systematically resist all the hand-crafted search strategies in its training suite**. The error bars (95% binomial confidence intervals) confirm this is not a statistical fluke — the model is reliably solving a small but non-zero fraction of problems that are impossible for BFS and DFS with the sum and multiply heuristics at all tested breadth limits.

The paper does not analyze *which* difficult problems are solved (e.g., do they share structural properties? Does the model's solution strategy differ systematically from the heuristics?), which limits the strength of the "discovery of new strategies" claim.

#### Policy Improvement Also Improves Search Efficiency and Reduces Errors

Beyond accuracy, the finetuned models produce **more efficient and less error-prone search trajectories** (Figure 6, Appendix Tables 1 and 2).

**Search efficiency (Figure 6, right; Appendix Table 1):** The average number of states visited in correct trajectories decreases substantially after policy improvement:
- Base SoS on seen targets: 75.04 ± 0.64 states per correct trajectory.
- STaR on seen targets: 66.18 ± 0.64 states per correct trajectory (a 12% reduction).
- APA on seen targets: 64.73 ± 0.65 states per correct trajectory (a 14% reduction).

A similar pattern holds for unseen targets (81.75 → 72.25 → 70.85). This matters because the reward function for APA explicitly incentivizes shorter trajectories (correctness plus length penalty), and even STaR — which does not use an explicit efficiency reward, only correctness filtering — produces more efficient search. The paper interprets this as evidence that more accurate models also search more efficiently: they waste less exploration on unpromising branches because their internal state evaluation has been refined.

**Arithmetic errors (Figure 6, left; Appendix Table 2):** The average number of arithmetic errors per trajectory decreases dramatically with policy improvement:
- Base SoS on seen targets: 2.247 ± 0.097 errors per trajectory.
- STaR on seen targets: 1.927 ± 0.084 errors per trajectory.
- APA on seen targets: 0.668 ± 0.036 errors per trajectory — a **70% reduction** from the base SoS model.

This is a striking finding: APA, which uses an RL objective that rewards correctness and efficiency (but does not explicitly penalize arithmetic errors), produces a model that makes dramatically fewer arithmetic mistakes. The paper suggests this is because the model's improved internal world model (better state evaluation) allows it to recognize and avoid states that would require error-prone computations.

**Exploration errors (Appendix Table 2):** The base SoS model makes very few exploration errors to begin with: 0.008 ± 0.003 per trajectory on seen targets (less than 1 per 100 trajectories). This low baseline validates the explicit search scaffolding — the model reliably learns the syntax of search operations. APA maintains this low rate (0.008 ± 0.003), while STaR shows a slight increase (0.024 ± 0.004). The STaR increase may be related to the higher rate of "other" errors (0.069 ± 0.009 vs. 0.018 ± 0.005 for base SoS), which the paper describes as memory-based discrepancies where a child node has the wrong set of numbers — a different kind of failure mode than simple exploration syntax errors.

#### Shift in Search Strategy After Policy Improvement

The alignment analysis reveals that policy improvement **shifts the model's search behavior away from the symbolic training strategies** (Figure 5a). The paper plots the *difference* in states visited alignment between the finetuned models and the base SoS model, for each symbolic strategy. Positive values mean the finetuned model visits *more* states associated with that strategy than the base model did; negative values mean it visits *fewer*.

Both STaR and APA models show increased alignment with the multiply-heuristic strategies and decreased alignment with the sum-heuristic strategies, relative to the base SoS model. Specifically, the SoS+STaR model shows the largest positive shifts for DFS Multiply and BFS Multiply with breadth limits 2–4, and negative shifts for BFS Sum with larger breadth limits. The APA model shows a similar but stronger pattern — it diverges more from the sum-heuristic strategies and shows larger positive shifts toward multiply-heuristic strategies.

The paper's interpretation: "the model prefers the states visited by certain symbolic strategies after improvement" and "the APA model is less aligned with the symbolic strategies compared to the base SoS model, indicating that it diverges more from the symbolic strategies and employs different strategies for searching." This supports the claim that policy improvement is not merely making the model better at imitating its training strategies, but is actually **changing the search behavior** — the model is developing preferences for different types of states (those associated with the multiply heuristic, which measures proximity to factors of the target) through the reinforcement process. Since the reward function only cares about correctness and efficiency, not about which heuristic is used, this shift represents the model autonomously discovering that multiply-like state evaluation is more effective than sum-like evaluation for the problems it encounters during policy improvement.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper does not report traditional ablation studies in the style of systematically removing components and measuring degradation. However, several experimental choices serve as implicit ablations or robustness checks:

- **Optimal Path vs. SoS as an implicit ablation of search process data:** The entire SoS vs. OP comparison (Figure 3a) can be viewed as an ablation study where the independent variable is the presence vs. absence of search process information (exploration, backtracking, dead ends) in the training data, while holding model architecture, training steps, and number of examples constant. The 25-percentage-point gap demonstrates the critical importance of this information for learning to solve problems requiring search. The fact that the OP model underperforms despite having nearly twice as many correct solution examples (500K vs. 285K) reinforces that it is the *kind* of data, not the *quantity*, that drives the effect.

- **Two generalization conditions as a robustness check against memorization:** The paper evaluates on both seen-targets-new-inputs and new-targets-new-inputs conditions (Figure 3a). The SoS model's accuracy drops only modestly (51.27% → 49.00%) when evaluated on targets never seen during training, indicating that the model has learned a general search capability rather than memorizing solutions for specific targets. The OP model shows a similar proportional drop (though specific numbers are not reported for the OP model on new targets). This is a robustness check against the concern that the model is simply pattern-matching target numbers to memorized operation sequences.

- **Two policy improvement methods producing similar gains:** Both STaR (expert iteration via filtering and supervised finetuning) and APA (actor-critic RL with a value network) produce improvements of approximately 4–6 percentage points over the base SoS model (Figure 4a). The fact that two methodologically different approaches — one using only correctness filtering with no explicit exploration reward, the other using a learned value function with efficiency incentives — yield similar accuracy improvements is a robustness check suggesting that the gains come from the *correctness signal itself* (selecting for trajectories that reach the goal) rather than from specific algorithmic details. However, the error reduction patterns differ: APA dramatically reduces arithmetic errors (70% reduction) while STaR increases exploration and "other" errors slightly. This divergence in *how* the models improve — even as both improve final accuracy — suggests the improvement mechanisms are partially overlapping but not identical.

- **Reference policy resetting as an ablation of APA's stability mechanism:** Figure 4b indirectly ablates the importance of the reference policy resetting strategy by showing the validation accuracy trajectory. After each reference policy reset, accuracy initially drops (as the tighter KL penalty around the new reference takes effect) and then climbs to a higher plateau. This pattern — seen three times — demonstrates that without resetting, the policy would stall at the first plateau. The paper explicitly interprets this as an implicit λ-annealing mechanism, noting that "we found the strategy of shifting the reference distribution to be more stable for training when compared to designing a schedule for reducing λ over training."

- **Error analysis as a diagnostic for *how* models improve:** Appendix Table 2 provides a breakdown of error types across models and generalization conditions. The key non-obvious finding is the asymmetry between STaR and APA in error reduction. STaR reduces arithmetic errors (from 2.247 to 1.927 per trajectory) but slightly increases exploration errors (from 0.008 to 0.024) and "other" errors (from 0.018 to 0.069). APA dramatically reduces arithmetic errors (to 0.668) while maintaining the base model's low exploration error rate (0.008). This differential pattern suggests that STaR's improvement comes primarily from generating better arithmetic operations (more correct computation) at the cost of some structural sloppiness (more state-tracking mistakes), while APA's improvement comes from both better arithmetic *and* maintained structural precision — possibly because the value network provides more nuanced feedback about state quality than binary correctness filtering alone.

- **Alignment metric sensitivity:** The paper uses two complementary alignment metrics (correctness and states visited) and finds consistent patterns across both (Figures 3b, 3c, 5a). The SoS model shows moderate alignment with sum-heuristic strategies and low alignment with multiply strategies in both metrics. After policy improvement, both metrics shift toward multiply strategies. The consistency across metrics strengthens the conclusion that the model's search behavior is genuinely changing rather than being an artifact of one particular measurement approach.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental results provide compelling evidence for several of the paper's claims while leaving others supported but incompletely validated. The central challenge in assessing this work is that it makes **three distinct claims of increasing ambition**, and the evidence is strongest for the first, solid for the second, and suggestive but incomplete for the third.

**Claim 1: Training on search trajectories produces better problem-solving than training on optimal paths alone (for problems requiring search).**

The evidence for this claim is strong and clean. Figure 3a shows a 51.27% vs. 25.73% accuracy difference under conditions that isolate the training data composition as the only variable: same architecture, same training steps, same data quantity. The design is elegant in its simplicity — the OP model actually has *more* correct examples, eliminating the confound that SoS benefits from more data. The effect persists under both generalization conditions (seen targets and new targets), ruling out memorization. The claim is also bounded appropriately: the paper does not argue that search trajectories are always better than optimal paths for all tasks, only that they are better for this task which explicitly requires search. The Countdown domain, with its high branching factor and need for backtracking, is an appropriate testbed for this claim.

However, several limitations narrow the claim's demonstrated scope. The comparison is made at a single model scale (250M parameters) on a single task (Countdown with exactly 4 input numbers). The training data is synthetically generated by heuristic solvers — it is not human search data. The gap might not generalize to tasks where search is less central, to domains where generating search trajectories is expensive or impossible, or to model scales substantially different from 250M. The paper's contribution would be stronger with evidence that the SoS advantage persists (or grows, or shrinks) with model scale, or with a second task domain beyond Countdown. The choice of a single domain is understandable for a paper introducing a framework, but it limits the generality of the "process data beats outcome data" conclusion.

Additionally, the OP baseline has a subtle confound that the paper does not discuss. The OP model is trained on sequences that follow a different formatting convention than the SoS trajectories: the OP data contains node indices and state declarations but no backtracking operations, goal checks for dead ends, or "No Solution" markers. At inference time, the OP model must generate a trajectory in the SoS format (since evaluation measures whether P is present in T, and T must be a valid trajectory). But the OP model was never trained on tokens like "Moving to Node #X" or "X,Y unequal: No Solution." It is possible that the OP model's poor performance is partially attributable to a format mismatch — it attempts to generate search trajectories having only seen clean solution paths — rather than purely to the absence of search process information. A stronger baseline would fine-tune the OP model on a small amount of SoS-format data (to teach the format) while keeping the solution-path-only content, separating format effects from content effects. Without this control, the 25-percentage-point gap may overstate the pure content advantage of search trajectories.

**Claim 2: The SoS model learns a blended search strategy rather than imitating a single symbolic strategy.**

The alignment analysis (Figures 3b, 3c) provides reasonable evidence for this claim. The state visitation correlations are moderate (0.27–0.57) rather than highly concentrated on a single strategy, and the correctness alignment pattern is similarly distributed. A model that simply learned to imitate DFS with the sum heuristic would show high correlation (0.8+) with that strategy and low correlation (0.1–0.3) with BFS variants; the SoS model does not show this pattern. The evidence is consistent with the claim that training on diverse strategies produces blended behavior.

However, the alignment metrics are coarse tools for characterizing search strategy. A correlation of 0.57 with DFS-sum could mean the model follows DFS-sum on approximately half of problems and other strategies on the rest, or it could mean the model follows a single consistent strategy that happens to visit 57% of the states DFS-sum would visit. The metrics do not distinguish between these possibilities. A more detailed analysis — for instance, measuring what fraction of individual trajectories are "closest" (by state alignment) to each symbolic strategy, or analyzing whether the model's branching behavior (depth vs. breadth preference) varies per problem — would provide stronger evidence for adaptive strategy selection.

Moreover, the alignment analysis only compares the SoS model against the 12 training strategies. A model that learned a genuinely new strategy (neither DFS nor BFS, but something different) would show low alignment with all 12 training strategies. But low alignment could also result from a model that is simply doing a noisy or imperfect version of a known strategy — the noise from learned arithmetic errors and occasional state-tracking mistakes would reduce alignment scores even if the underlying strategy were pure DFS-sum. The paper does not control for error-induced alignment reduction, making it difficult to distinguish "blended or novel strategy" from "imperfect imitation."

**Claim 3: Policy improvement enables the model to solve problems that resist all training strategies, potentially discovering novel search approaches.**

This is the paper's most ambitious claim, and the evidence is suggestive but requires careful qualification. The headline numbers — 36% of previously unsolved problems, 4% of difficult problems — are genuine positives that demonstrate capability beyond the training distribution. However, several factors limit the strength of the conclusion:

First, the **difficult problems** are defined as unsolvable by the 12 specific symbolic strategies in the training suite. This is a reasonable operational definition, but it is not equivalent to "problems that are inherently harder than what BFS/DFS with these heuristics can handle." It is possible that some of these problems *would* be solvable by a 13th strategy not included in the suite — for instance, BFS with a breadth limit of 6, or DFS with a different pruning threshold, or a strategy using both heuristics simultaneously. The paper does not analyze whether the solved difficult problems share structural properties that systematically challenge the heuristic assumptions of the training strategies (e.g., problems where neither the sum nor the multiply heuristic provides useful guidance). Without this analysis, the "novel strategy discovery" interpretation competes with a simpler one: the model has learned to search with a larger effective beam width or a more nuanced (but still fundamentally similar) heuristic, and this additional search budget solves problems that the fixed-breadth BFS strategies missed.

Second, the **4% figure on difficult problems is small**. A 4% solve rate means the model fails on 96% of these problems, and with 10,000 test problems, the 4% corresponds to approximately 400 successes. The paper does not provide any qualitative analysis of these 400 solved problems — what do their solutions look like? Do they use operations or state sequences that are systematically absent from the training distribution? Without such analysis, the claim that the model has "discovered new search strategies" remains an inference from the quantitative result rather than a demonstrated mechanism. The 36% figure on previously unsolved problems is more impressive numerically but is a weaker test of novelty, since those problems might have been solvable by the training strategies with different randomness.

Third, the **policy improvement methods have opposite effects on error types** (Appendix Table 2). STaR improves accuracy while *increasing* exploration errors and memory-based discrepancies; APA improves accuracy while dramatically reducing arithmetic errors. This divergence suggests the two methods produce different kinds of improvement — STaR may be making the model better at finding correct operation sequences even at the cost of sloppier state tracking, while APA makes the model more precise overall. The paper does not investigate whether the sets of previously-unsolved problems solved by STaR and APA overlap (do they solve the same ~36% or different subsets?), which would clarify whether the improvement mechanisms are complementary or redundant. If STaR and APA solve different subsets of previously-unsolved problems, this would be evidence that they are developing different search capabilities, potentially supporting the "novel strategies" claim. If they solve the same subsets, the improvement is more likely a general increase in model quality rather than strategy innovation.

Fourth, the **difficulty-based analysis from the "Inference Compute Scaling" paper (Sections 5–6)** provides a lens for evaluating this claim. That paper showed that test-time compute strategies only help on problems within the base model's capability range (pass@1 substantially above zero) and provide essentially no benefit on the hardest problems (difficulty bin 5, pass@1 near zero). Applied here: if the 4% of solved difficult problems correspond to those that are "almost" solvable by the training strategies (pass@1 just below the threshold of being found in the limited search budget of dataset generation), then the model's success may reflect increased effective search budget rather than novel strategy discovery. The paper does not report the fraction of difficult problems that the training strategies come close to solving (e.g., generating a state within one operation of the goal before pruning it), which would help distinguish "more search" from "different search."

**What experiments would have strengthened the paper?**

1. **An explicit "strategy novelty" analysis:** For the difficult problems solved by the finetuned models, compare the states visited and operations used against the nearest symbolic strategy. If the model's solution paths contain state sequences that no training strategy would generate (e.g., operations involving intermediate numbers that all 12 strategies would have pruned), this would be direct evidence for novel search behavior.

2. **A compute-matched comparison with symbolic search:** Run the symbolic strategies with increased search budget (e.g., BFS with width 10, DFS with relaxed pruning) on the difficult problems and measure how many become solvable. If increased symbolic search budget solves many of the same problems the finetuned model solves, then the model's advantage is quantitative (more effective search) rather than qualitative (different search).

3. **Cross-domain evaluation:** Test the Countdown-trained SoS model on a second combinatorial search task (e.g., a different arithmetic puzzle, or a simple planning domain) to measure whether "learning to search" transfers across domains. This would distinguish general search capability from Countdown-specific arithmetic skill.

4. **Scale analysis:** Run the SoS vs. OP comparison at multiple model sizes (e.g., 125M, 250M, 500M, 1B) to characterize how the advantage of search trajectory training scales with model capacity. Does the gap widen (suggesting richer training data is more important for larger models) or narrow (suggesting larger models can compensate for impoverished training data)?

5. **Data composition ablation:** Train SoS models on subsets of the 12 strategies (e.g., only DFS variants, only BFS variants, only sum-heuristic variants) to measure whether strategy diversity in the training data is necessary for the blended behavior and policy improvement gains, or whether a single strategy with sufficient quantity would produce similar results.

Despite these limitations, the experimental results provide a compelling proof of concept for the Stream of Search framework. The core finding — that training on search process data dramatically outperforms training on optimal solutions for a task requiring search — is cleanly demonstrated. The policy improvement results, while not conclusively proving novel strategy discovery, provide genuine evidence that models trained on search trajectories can improve beyond their training distribution when given a correctness signal. The paper's contribution is establishing that "learning to search" is possible and beneficial, even if the exact mechanisms and limits of that learning require further investigation.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Single Domain, Single Model Scale: Generality Is Unproven

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use a single task — Countdown with exactly 4 input numbers — and a single model architecture at one scale (GPT-Neo 250M parameters trained from scratch). The paper is transparent about this scope in Section 7: "Our empirical results were restricted to the game of Countdown. Yet Countdown, with a high branching factor and variable goal states, captures the characteristics of complex planning problems. We are optimistic that SoS extends to more challenging, real-world tasks." This is an expression of optimism, not a demonstrated result.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot infer from this paper alone whether Stream of Search training would be effective for their domain, their model family, or their model scale. Several failure modes are plausible:

- **Domain specificity of search structure.** Countdown's search space has specific properties — deterministic state transitions, a fixed branching factor formula, exact arithmetic computations, a single well-defined goal condition — that may make it unusually amenable to serialized search representation. In domains with stochastic transitions (e.g., dialogue planning), fuzzy goal conditions (e.g., creative writing quality), or unbounded action spaces (e.g., code generation where any valid program is an action), the "language for search" vocabulary defined in Section 3 may not capture the essential operations, and the model may not learn to search reliably from text-serialized trajectories. The paper provides no evidence about which properties of Countdown are necessary for SoS to work.

- **Model architecture dependence.** The paper uses GPT-Neo, a standard autoregressive transformer. It is unknown whether SoS training would be equally effective for encoder-decoder architectures, for models with different attention patterns, or for models that have been pretrained on natural language before SoS finetuning. The paper trains from scratch on synthetic data; a practitioner wanting to add search capabilities to an existing pretrained model (e.g., Llama, GPT-4) would need to determine whether SoS finetuning is compatible with, or destructive to, the model's existing capabilities — a question the paper does not address.

- **Scale dependence.** The 250M parameter scale is modest relative to modern LLMs (70B+). The paper's claim that SoS models "learn an internal world model for search" raises the question of whether this capability requires a minimum model capacity. Would a 50M parameter model also learn to search effectively? Does the advantage of SoS over OP training grow with model scale (suggesting rich search data is increasingly important for larger models) or shrink (suggesting larger models can compensate for impoverished training data with better generalization)? Without scale analysis, a practitioner cannot determine whether the compute invested in generating search trajectories is justified for their target model size.

- **Task family generalization.** Countdown is an arithmetic reasoning puzzle. Would SoS training transfer to other reasoning domains — logical deduction, mathematical proof, code debugging, multi-step planning in physical environments — without domain-specific redesign of the search vocabulary and data generation pipeline? The paper does not test transfer: a model trained on Countdown search trajectories is never evaluated on a different task. A practitioner considering SoS for a new domain would need to replicate the entire pipeline (design search vocabulary, implement symbolic solvers, generate training trajectories) from scratch, with no guidance from cross-domain transfer results.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The Countdown task is chosen deliberately because it "captures the characteristics of complex planning problems" with a high branching factor (Section 4, Section 7). The paper demonstrates that SoS works on this task, which is evidence for feasibility but not generality. The two evaluation splits — seen targets and new targets — provide evidence of within-domain generalization (the model does not simply memorize target-specific solutions), but they do not address cross-domain transfer. Zero experiments use a second task domain.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation in Section 7: "We are optimistic that SoS extends to more challenging, real-world tasks... An important question is how well search abilities transfer between domains and between formal and informal domains." It suggests no concrete mitigation strategy, no preliminary cross-domain experiment, and no analysis of which Countdown properties are necessary for SoS success versus which are incidental. The limitation is flagged for future work but otherwise unaddressed.

### Training Data Generation Requires Symbolic Solvers That May Not Exist for Target Domains

**The assumption or constraint.** The SoS training pipeline depends critically on the ability to generate a large, diverse dataset of search trajectories using symbolic solvers. Section 4 describes generating 500,000 trajectories from 12 hand-crafted search strategies (BFS and DFS variants with two heuristics). The paper acknowledges this dependency implicitly in Section 7: "Generating the initial SoS dataset can be challenging, as it is not always feasible to create symbolic search algorithms to solve problems."

**The consequence.** For many domains where one would want a language model to learn search, symbolic solvers either do not exist or are prohibitively expensive to build. Examples include:

- **Open-ended reasoning or creative tasks** (essay writing, strategic planning, scientific hypothesis generation) where there is no well-defined state space, no enumerable action set, and no automated way to verify goal satisfaction — meaning symbolic search algorithms cannot be constructed at all.
- **Tasks requiring world knowledge** (medical diagnosis, legal reasoning, business strategy) where the state representation would need to encode vast amounts of domain knowledge that symbolic solvers typically lack.
- **Tasks where search is possible in principle but the symbolic solver implementation is expensive** (code generation requiring execution-based verification of partial programs, multi-agent coordination, robotics planning with physics simulation). In these domains, building even one reliable symbolic solver represents a significant engineering effort, and the paper's requirement for 12 diverse strategies increases this burden substantially.

The practical implication is that SoS, in its current form, is only applicable to "formal domains" — those where the problem can be modeled as an MDP with a known transition function, where a goal condition can be evaluated algorithmically, and where heuristic functions can be designed by human engineers. These are precisely the domains where symbolic solvers already work well, which creates a paradox: SoS is most easily applied where it is least needed. The domains where learned search would be most valuable — informal, open-ended, or knowledge-intensive reasoning — are exactly those where generating the initial SoS dataset is hardest or impossible.

Furthermore, the quality ceiling of the SoS model is initially bounded by the quality of the symbolic solvers used for data generation. The paper demonstrates that policy improvement can push the model *beyond* this ceiling (solving 36% of previously unsolved problems, Figure 5b), but the model must first reach a baseline competence level from pretraining on symbolic trajectories. If the available symbolic solvers are extremely weak (solving only, say, 5% of problems), the SoS dataset will contain very few correct trajectories — the paper's dataset had only 57.1% correct trajectories, and performance might degrade substantially with a lower correct-trajectory fraction. The paper does not ablate the effect of correct-trajectory proportion in the training data.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates the pipeline end-to-end for one domain (Countdown) where symbolic solvers are straightforward to construct because: (a) the state space is simple (sets of integers), (b) the action space is enumerable (choose two numbers, choose an operation), (c) the transition function is deterministic arithmetic, and (d) heuristics are easy to design (sum distance, factor distance). No experiments attempt to apply SoS to a domain lacking these properties, nor does the paper characterize how solver quality or diversity affects downstream model performance. The dataset composition details (57.1% correct trajectories, 12 strategies) are provided but not ablated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the challenge in Section 7 ("Generating the initial SoS dataset can be challenging") and gestures at potential solutions: "In the short-term, externally-structured search methods such as Tree of Thought are likely to be more efficient for these tasks; in the longer run the increased flexibility and learnability of internally-structured search (i.e. SoS) may prevail. It is also plausible these approaches can be combined, for instance, with ToT providing initial training data for SoS." This is a suggestion for future work, not a demonstrated solution. The idea of using Tree-of-Thoughts (which uses frozen LMs as subroutines within a symbolic search framework) to generate training data for SoS is interesting — it would replace hand-crafted symbolic solvers with LM-driven search — but this hybrid approach is not tested in the paper, and it inherits the high inference cost of ToT (exactly what SoS aims to avoid). The mitigation is speculative and unvalidated.

### Context Window Constraints Limit Problem Complexity

**The assumption or constraint.** The SoS model generates search trajectories autoregressively, meaning the entire trajectory must fit within the model's context window. The paper uses a 4096-token context window (GPT-Neo, Appendix C.1) and explicitly restricts to 4-number Countdown problems because "the search trajectories for games with 5 input numbers can be 60,000 tokens long" (Section 4), which would exceed the context window by more than an order of magnitude.

**The consequence.** This constraint creates a fundamental tension between problem complexity and search thoroughness. As problems become harder — more input numbers, larger search spaces, deeper solution paths — two things happen simultaneously: (1) the search trajectories become longer because more exploration is needed to find a solution, and (2) the optimal path itself becomes longer because more operations are required to reach the target. Both effects consume context window capacity. At some problem complexity, the context window becomes the bottleneck: the model cannot explore enough of the search space to reliably find solutions because the trajectory would exceed the window before sufficient exploration occurs.

This is not merely a current-hardware limitation that will disappear with longer context windows. Even with 128K or 1M token contexts (now available in production models), the fundamental scaling is unfavorable. Search tree size typically grows exponentially with problem dimension (branching factor raised to the solution depth), while context length grows linearly. A problem that requires twice as many operations to solve may require exponentially more search states to be explored, rapidly outpacing any feasible context window. The paper's restriction to 4-number problems — where the solution depth is exactly 3 operations — avoids this scaling issue but provides no evidence about how SoS performance degrades as problem complexity approaches the context limit.

Additionally, the autoregressive generation of search trajectories means the model must commit to every token of exploration irreversibly. If the model explores a large subtree early in the trajectory and then runs out of context window before exploring a more promising branch, it cannot recover — the exploration decision, once generated, consumes context space that cannot be reclaimed. This is different from symbolic search, where the search algorithm can prune unpromising branches from memory and reallocate that storage to more promising areas of the tree. The SoS model has no mechanism for "forgetting" or compressing earlier exploration to free context space for later exploration.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly discusses this limitation in Section 4 when justifying the choice of 4-number problems: "We consider problems with 4 input numbers since these problems are challenging enough to have long search traces without the search traces exceeding a standard LM context window." The 60,000-token estimate for 5-number problems is cited but not elaborated — it is unclear whether this is typical or worst-case, and whether 5-number problems are fundamentally unsolvable with SoS or merely require longer contexts. The average trajectory length for the base SoS model is 75 states per correct trajectory (Appendix Table 1), which translates to roughly 2,000–3,000 tokens (each state declaration with exploration and backtracking operations consumes multiple tokens). At 4096 context, this leaves relatively little headroom before trajectories are truncated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not propose any mitigation for this limitation. It accepts the 4-number restriction as a practical constraint and makes no claims about scaling to larger problems. Potential mitigations — hierarchical search (where the model sets subgoals and searches for them independently, resetting context between subsearches), context compression (summarizing explored subtrees rather than keeping them verbatim), or retrieval-augmented search (storing the frontier externally) — are mentioned in Section 7 as future work directions ("explicitly representing state evaluations... and introducing other formalizable operations such as limits, summarization, cycle checks, and subgoal setting could enhance the SoS framework") but are not explored. The limitation is acknowledged but left entirely to future work.

### Difficulty Estimation and the Cost of Unsuccessful Search Are Not Accounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates models based on whether the correct solution path P is present anywhere in the generated trajectory T (Section 4: "we define correctness as a binary function, 1, if the correct path to the solution, P, is present in the generated trajectory, T"). This lenient criterion means the model can generate extensive unsuccessful search — exploring many dead ends, backtracking repeatedly, consuming significant inference compute — and as long as it eventually stumbles upon the correct path somewhere in the output, it receives credit. The cost of this unsuccessful exploration (in tokens generated, time, or compute) is not factored into any performance metric.

**The consequence.** This evaluation framework creates two related problems for practical deployment:

**1. The reported accuracy numbers overstate useful performance.** A model that achieves 51.27% accuracy but generates 75 states per correct trajectory (Appendix Table 1) is consuming substantial compute — each state involves generating exploration operations, performing arithmetic, and formatting output. A practitioner cares not just about whether the model eventually finds the answer, but about the *cost per correct answer* in inference compute. The paper reports that correct trajectories average 75 states (Table 1), but does not report the length of *incorrect* trajectories (which may involve even more exploration before exhausting the context window or terminating in failure). If incorrect trajectories average 200+ states, the total inference cost per problem — including the ~49% of problems the model fails on — could be much higher than the per-correct-trajectory numbers suggest.

**2. There is no mechanism for deciding when to stop searching.** The model generates search trajectories autoregressively until it produces "Goal Reached" or hits the context limit. On problems where the model never finds the solution, it will generate until context exhaustion, consuming the maximum possible inference compute per problem. In deployment, a practitioner would want the model to recognize when further search is unlikely to succeed and terminate early — a capability the paper neither trains for nor evaluates. The symbolic solvers used to generate training data have explicit termination conditions (search tree exhaustion or depth limits); the learned model has no equivalent mechanism except context window truncation. This means the worst-case inference cost per problem is the full context length, regardless of problem difficulty.

This connects to a broader issue illuminated by the "Inference Compute Scaling" paper (prior analysis, Section 4): for problems that are fundamentally beyond the model's capability (analogous to difficulty bin 5 in that paper, where pass@1 is near zero), additional test-time compute provides essentially zero benefit. The SoS model, lacking a difficulty estimation mechanism, will expend its full context window searching unsuccessfully on such problems — a pure waste of compute that could be avoided if the model could recognize its own inability. The policy improvement results exacerbate this: the finetuned models solve only 4% of the hardest problems (Figure 5c), meaning they fail on 96% of them, likely after extensive unsuccessful search.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Appendix Table 1 reports the average number of states explored per *correct* trajectory (75 for base SoS, 64–66 after policy improvement). The paper does not report the average length of incorrect trajectories, nor the distribution of trajectory lengths, nor the total inference compute per problem (including failures). Figure 6 (right) shows that policy improvement reduces the number of states visited in correct solutions (more efficient search for successes), but provides no information about search efficiency on failures. The "approximately 2 arithmetic errors per trajectory" figure (Appendix Table 2) is an average over all trajectories (correct and incorrect), but does not separate error rates by correctness status.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The reward function for APA "takes into account the correctness and length of the generated trajectory" (Section 6), which incentivizes shorter trajectories for correct solutions — and Appendix Table 1 confirms this works, reducing states-per-correct-trajectory by 12–14%. However, this efficiency incentive only applies to trajectories that eventually find the solution. The reward function provides no signal about when to abandon search on problems the model cannot solve, because there is no negative reward for long unsuccessful trajectories (only zero reward for failure, regardless of length). The paper does not discuss the cost of unsuccessful search, does not propose early-stopping mechanisms, and does not measure inference-time compute budgets for the reported accuracy numbers. This is a significant practical gap for anyone considering deploying SoS models in cost-sensitive applications.

### The Optimal Path Baseline Has a Confounding Format Mismatch

**The assumption or constraint.** The central experiment — SoS vs. Optimal Path (OP) training — compares two models trained on different data distributions but evaluated under identical conditions. The evaluation requires generating a search trajectory T and checking whether the correct solution path P appears within it (Section 4). This means the OP model must generate search trajectories at evaluation time — including "Moving to Node" operations, "No Solution" declarations, backtracking, and state indexing — despite having never seen any of these tokens during training. The OP training data contains only the linear correct steps with state declarations but no search operations (Section 3, Figure 2 right panel; Appendix Figure 7 shows an OP example containing "Current State" and "Exploring Operation" and "Generated Node" but no backtracking or dead-end checks).

**The consequence.** The 51.27% vs. 25.73% accuracy gap (Figure 3a) conflates two distinct effects: (1) the content advantage of seeing search process data (exploration, backtracking, dead ends), and (2) the format mismatch between the OP model's training distribution and the evaluation format. The OP model is being asked to produce text in a format it was never trained on — it must invent "Moving to Node #X" operations, backtracking syntax, and goal-check failure declarations from a training distribution that contains only forward progress toward the solution. This is a distribution-shift problem: the model encounters token sequences at inference (backtracking after dead ends) that have zero probability under its training distribution. The performance degradation from this mismatch is unmeasured and could account for a non-trivial fraction of the 25-percentage-point gap.

To see why this matters, consider what the OP model *can* do. It was trained on 500,000 examples of correct operation sequences — it knows, in principle, what arithmetic operations lead from input numbers to target numbers. If evaluation simply required generating the correct sequence of operations (without the surrounding search apparatus), the OP model might perform substantially better. The SoS format forces the OP model to generate extra tokens (state tracking, backtracking) that it never learned to produce, and errors in these tokens could cause the trajectory to be judged incorrect even when the model "knows" the correct arithmetic operations. In other words, the OP model might know *what* the solution is but fail at *formatting* it as a search trajectory.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report any control experiment that would isolate the format effect from the content effect. No results show the OP model's accuracy when evaluated with a lenient parsing approach that extracts the arithmetic operation sequence regardless of formatting. No results show the OP model's performance when prompted to generate only the solution path (not a full search trajectory). The error analysis (Appendix Table 2) categorizes errors for the SoS model and its finetuned variants but provides no error breakdown for the OP model, so we cannot determine what fraction of OP failures are due to formatting errors versus genuinely incorrect arithmetic operations. This is a notable omission given the paper's central claim that search trajectory training is superior to optimal path training — the claim would be stronger if the comparison controlled for evaluation format.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this confound. The OP baseline is presented as a clean comparison ("both models are trained up to the same number of gradient steps," Section 5), but the format mismatch is not discussed. A stronger experimental design would include: (a) an OP model evaluated with a relaxed correctness criterion that extracts the operation sequence regardless of search formatting, (b) an OP model finetuned with a small amount of search-format data (to teach the format without providing the search process content), or (c) a comparison where both models are evaluated on their ability to generate clean solution paths rather than full search trajectories. Without such controls, the measured 25-percentage-point gap is an upper bound on the true content advantage of SoS training; the actual content advantage could be smaller.

### Policy Improvement Produces Modest Absolute Gains with Incomplete Diagnostics

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper presents policy improvement (STaR and APA) as a key contribution — the mechanism by which SoS models transcend their training distribution and discover new search strategies. The quantitative results show improvements of 4–6 percentage points over the base SoS model (Figure 4a: 51.27% → 55.59% for STaR, → 56.52% for APA on seen targets; 49.00% → 53.34% for STaR, → 54.23% for APA on new targets). The most ambitious claim — solving problems that resist all training strategies — is supported by 36% of previously unsolved problems and 4% of difficult problems (Figures 5b, 5c).

**The consequence.** Several aspects of these results limit the strength of the "discovering new search strategies" claim:

**1. The absolute accuracy improvements are modest.** A 4–6 percentage point gain from policy improvement is real but incremental — the gap between the base SoS model and the OP model (25 percentage points) dwarfs the gain from STaR or APA. This suggests that most of the benefit comes from the initial pretraining on search trajectories, with policy improvement providing a smaller additional boost. A practitioner considering the full pipeline might reasonably ask whether the added complexity of RL finetuning (implementing APA with value networks, reference policy resets, and hyperparameter tuning, or running multiple iterations of STaR with trajectory filtering and retraining) is worth a 4–6 point gain over the base SoS model, versus simply generating more or better symbolic training data and retraining the base model.

**2. The "difficult problems" solved rate is 4%.** While the paper emphasizes that these are problems *none* of the 12 symbolic strategies can solve, the absolute number — 400 out of 10,000 problems — means the model fails on 96% of the hardest problems. The qualitative significance of solving *any* such problems is real, but the practical significance is limited: a system that fails 96% of the time on the hardest problem tier is not a reliable solver for that tier. Moreover, without analysis of *which* difficult problems are solved (their structural properties, what the model's solutions look like), we cannot determine whether the 4% represents a systematic capability (the model has learned a strategy that works on a specific subclass of hard problems) or a statistical fluke (the model got lucky on 400 problems through randomness in exploration).

**3. The improvement mechanisms are incompletely diagnosed.** The paper shows that policy improvement shifts state visitation patterns (Figure 5a: increased alignment with multiply-heuristic strategies) and reduces arithmetic errors (Figure 6, left; Appendix Table 2: 70% reduction for APA), but does not characterize *how* these changes translate into solving previously unsolved problems. Does the model solve previously unsolved problems by: (a) exploring more broadly (visiting states the symbolic strategies pruned), (b) evaluating states differently (recognizing promising states the heuristics missed), (c) performing arithmetic more accurately (avoiding errors that derailed the symbolic strategies), or (d) some combination? Without this analysis, the "novel search strategies" claim remains at the level of an input-output observation (the model solves problems the training strategies didn't) rather than a mechanistic understanding.

**4. STaR and APA produce different error profiles for similar accuracy gains.** STaR increases exploration errors and "other" (memory-based) errors while APA dramatically reduces arithmetic errors (Appendix Table 2). These divergent effects suggest the two methods are improving performance through different mechanisms — STaR may be making the model more willing to explore (at the cost of occasional state-tracking mistakes), while APA makes the model more precise in its computations. The paper does not investigate whether the sets of previously unsolved problems solved by STaR and APA overlap. If they solve different problem subsets, this would be evidence that the two methods develop complementary capabilities, strengthening the "novel strategies" claim. If they solve the same subset, the improvement is more likely a general quality increase rather than strategy innovation. The absence of this analysis leaves the nature of the improvement ambiguous.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 4, 5, 6 and Appendix Tables 1, 2 provide the quantitative results. The alignment analysis (Figure 5a) shows state visitation shifts but does not characterize *which* problems benefit or *why* the shifts help. The error analysis (Appendix Table 2) breaks down error types but does not connect error patterns to problem-solving success. No qualitative examples of solutions to previously unsolved or difficult problems are provided (the example in Figure 8 is from the base SoS model on a standard problem). The paper does not report whether STaR-solved and APA-solved previously unsolved problems overlap.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges that policy improvement converges after 3 STaR iterations or 3 APA reference-policy resets (Section 6, Figure 4b), suggesting the improvements are bounded. It does not propose methods for pushing beyond this plateau (e.g., combining STaR and APA, using larger models for policy improvement, or iterating policy improvement with expanded symbolic training data). The limitation is inherent in the current results but the paper does not frame it as a limitation — it presents the 36% and 4% numbers as successes rather than as evidence of a capability ceiling that remains far from saturation. Future work directions (Section 7) suggest "incorporating reflection and self-evaluation to enable models to discover and improve novel search strategies" but do not directly address how to increase the fraction of hard problems solved.
