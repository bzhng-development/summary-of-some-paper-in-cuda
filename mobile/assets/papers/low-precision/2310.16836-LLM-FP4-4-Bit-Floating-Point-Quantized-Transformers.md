# LLM-FP4: 4-Bit Floating-Point Quantized Transformers

**ArXiv:** [2310.16836](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16836)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes **LLM-FP4**, a post-training quantization method that compresses both weights and activations in transformer models to 4-bit floating-point values.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **LLM-FP4**, a post-training quantization method that compresses both weights and activations in transformer models to 4-bit floating-point values. The method operates through two core mechanisms: a **joint format and max value search** (a layer-wise reconstruction procedure that selects optimal exponent bits and clipping ranges from a discrete search space, avoiding gradient-based instability) and a **pre-shifted exponent bias** (reparameterizing per-channel activation scaling factors as integer exponent biases of the corresponding weight tensors, enabling per-channel activation precision with tensor-wise compute cost). Evaluated on LLaMA-7B/13B common sense zero-shot reasoning, FPQ achieves a 63.1 average score on LLaMA-13B at 4-bit weights and activations—only 5.8 points below full precision, outperforming the prior state-of-the-art by 12.7 points and reducing the accuracy gap by roughly 70%. The method also generalizes to BERT (4/4/4-bit quantization with only 3.6-point average GLUE degradation versus full precision, surpassing prior 4-bit methods by 7.8–44.3 points) and vision transformers (75.0% ImageNet accuracy on DeiT-S at 4-bit, a 31.5-point improvement over prior work), establishing that floating-point PTQ can maintain viability at ultra-low bit widths across transformer domains when the inter-channel variance pattern is explicitly addressed through exponent bias reparameterization.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Post-Training Quantization Below 8 Bits Is Unreliable

The fundamental challenge this paper addresses is **how to compress large transformer models to ultra-low bit widths (specifically 4-bit) while preserving accuracy, without costly retraining.** Post-training quantization (PTQ) — converting a pre-trained model's weights and activations to low-precision formats using only a small calibration dataset — offers the promise of reducing memory footprint and accelerating inference with minimal engineering effort. However, as the paper documents extensively in its experimental results (Tables 1–3), existing methods **largely break down below 8 bits**, particularly when activations must be quantized alongside weights.

The consequences of this failure are stark. On LLaMA-7B with 4-bit weights and 4-bit activations (W4A4), naive MinMax integer quantization crashes from 66.3% average zero-shot accuracy to 38.1% — essentially random-chance performance on several benchmarks (Table 1, "MinMax INT Quant 4/4/4"). For BERT on GLUE, the same setting drops the average score from 83.7 to 32.9 (Table 2). For vision transformers on ImageNet, performance collapses to 0.1–0.4% accuracy (Table 3, "MinMax FP Quant 4/4"). Prior state-of-the-art PTQ methods fare only marginally better: SmoothQuant achieves 49.1% on LLaMA-7B at W4A4 with 512 calibration samples, while LLM-QAT (which *requires* quantization-aware training — i.e., it is not PTQ) reaches only 52.8% (Table 1). On BERT, the best prior 4-bit PTQ method (QDrop) scores 72.3% on GLUE versus the full-precision 83.7% (Table 2). On DeiT-S, the best prior 4-bit method (APQ-ViT) achieves only 43.6% versus 79.9% full precision (Table 3).

These failures are not merely academic. They mean that, in practice, model deployers face a hard choice: accept the large memory and compute cost of 8-bit or 16-bit precision, invest in expensive quantization-aware retraining, or abandon transformer models entirely for memory-constrained edge devices. The paper's opening of Section 1 frames this directly:

> "the advancements in transformer performance are accompanied by a corresponding increase in model size and computational costs... This poses significant challenges when attempting to leverage the full potential of transformer models in use cases where memory or computational resources are limited."

### Why Floating-Point Quantization Matters (and Why It's Under-Explored)

A key contextual point that the paper leverages is that **floating-point (FP) quantized arithmetic has already emerged as the preferred choice in modern hardware**. The authors explicitly note that FP8 "has emerged as the default choice in various hardware platforms, including the NVIDIA H100" (Section 1). This is not an arbitrary engineering decision — FP quantization provides inherent advantages for deep learning workloads:

- **Handling long-tail and bell-shaped distributions**: Transformer activations and weights often exhibit distributions with sharp peaks and heavy tails (the paper illustrates this pattern in Figure 2 and Appendix C, Figures 5–6). Integer quantization, with its uniform quantization levels, must allocate equal representation capacity to the dense central region and the sparse tail region, wasting precision where it matters most. Floating-point formats, by contrast, allocate more quantization levels near zero (where most values cluster) and fewer at extreme magnitudes (where values are rare), matching the natural structure of neural network tensors.
- **Flexibility in format selection**: A q-bit floating-point number can be configured by choosing how many of those bits go to the exponent (e) versus the mantissa (m). An E3M0 format (3 exponent bits, 0 mantissa bits) behaves very differently from an E1M2 format. This tunability allows format selection to be matched to the specific distribution characteristics of each tensor, something integer quantization cannot do.

Despite these advantages, the paper identifies a striking gap: **minimal research exists on floating-point PTQ for transformer architectures**. The authors cite only two prior works: Kuzmin et al. (2022)'s FP8 QAT scheme primarily for vision tasks, and Zhang et al. (2023)'s mixed FP-INT format quantization for LLMs (Section 2.2). Neither addresses the specific challenges of post-training FP quantization at ultra-low bit widths (4-bit) for language transformers, nor do they tackle the activation quantization bottleneck that the paper identifies as the primary obstacle.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific, concrete limitations in existing quantization methods that collectively motivate its technical contributions:

**1. Integer quantization's fundamental mismatch with transformer distributions.** The paper demonstrates in Tables 1–3 that integer-based methods, even sophisticated ones like SmoothQuant and GPTQ, underperform even the simplest floating-point MinMax quantization at equivalent bit widths. For instance, on LLaMA-7B at W4A8, MinMax FP Quant (E2M1/E4M3) achieves 64.9% versus MinMax INT Quant's 39.7% (Table 1). The gap at W4A4 is similarly dramatic: 37.4% for MinMax FP versus 38.1% for MinMax INT — both catastrophic, but FP's superior representational capacity is evident at less extreme bit widths. This is not merely an implementation issue; it reflects a **distributional mismatch** that uniform quantization levels cannot overcome.

**2. Gradient-based FP format selection is unstable in PTQ settings.** Kuzmin et al. (2022) proposed learning exponent bits and clipping ranges via gradient descent in a QAT context. The paper explicitly tests this approach in PTQ and reports negative results (Appendix E):

> "the learnable method only achieves 74.38% accuracy for an 8-bit quantized model on ImageNet, in contrast, FPQ can attain an almost loss-less result of 79.88%"

The authors identify the root cause as gradient instability: "each time the exponent bits change, the gradients experience exponential variations, leading to high instability." Since exponent bits are discrete structural choices (not continuous parameters), gradient-based optimization across the discrete-continuous boundary fundamentally struggles. This instability is especially problematic in PTQ, where no extended training phase exists to recover from format missteps.

**3. The inter-channel variance pattern catastrophically degrades per-tensor activation quantization.** This is perhaps the paper's most important diagnostic finding (Section 4.2, Figures 2, 5, and 6). The authors observe that in transformer activations:

- **Within a given channel**, activation magnitudes are tightly clustered (low intra-channel variance).
- **Across different channels**, magnitudes can differ by **orders of magnitude** (high inter-channel variance).

This pattern is shown to be consistent across LLaMA-7B, BERT, and DeiT-S — language models, encoder-only transformers, and vision transformers alike (Figures 2, 5, 6). The paper explicitly argues that "this pattern is inherent to transformer architectures and not limited to specific tasks" (Section 1).

When per-tensor quantization is applied (one scaling factor for all channels), the few outlier channels with extreme magnitudes dominate the quantization range. The vast majority of channels, which contain smaller-magnitude values, get squeezed into a tiny fraction of the available quantization levels, losing substantial precision. This is the same phenomenon documented by Dettmers et al. (2022) in LLM.int8() and by Xiao et al. (2022) in SmoothQuant. However, the paper's contribution is to recognize this as a **universal transformer property** and to propose a **floating-point-native solution** rather than ad-hoc mitigation (SmoothQuant's per-channel scaling migration) or hybrid precision (LLM.int8()'s mixed INT8/FP16).

The consequence at 4-bit is catastrophic: with only 16 representable values, allocating most of them to a handful of outlier channels means the remaining channels — which may encode critical syntactic or semantic information — are effectively quantized to zero or to a single coarse value. This is what causes the collapse from W4A8 (where the pattern is less damaging because more levels are available) to W4A4 (where it becomes fatal).

**4. The tension between quantization granularity and hardware efficiency.** The paper explicitly acknowledges that applying per-channel scaling factors to activations would solve the inter-channel variance problem — but at the cost of breaking efficient matrix multiplication (Section 4.2, discussion around Eq. 10 and Figure 7 in Appendix D). In standard FP matrix multiplication, the scaling factors must be shared along the reduction dimension so they can be factored out and applied as a single scalar after the multiply-accumulate operation. Per-channel activation scaling factors cannot be factored out, requiring per-element rescaling that destroys the computational efficiency gains of quantization. Prior methods either accepted the accuracy penalty of per-tensor quantization or introduced costly workarounds (SmoothQuant's migration of scaling difficulty from activations to weights through a mathematically equivalent transformation).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions its contribution at the intersection of two emerging trends — floating-point quantization and transformer compression — while addressing what it identifies as the specific, unresolved challenge of **ultra-low-bit FP post-training quantization for both weights and activations**.

The authors construct their method as a **two-stage architecture** (though not presented as such, the logical flow is clear). First, they establish what they call the "FPQ baseline" — a robust search-based framework for jointly selecting exponent bits and clipping ranges that avoids the gradient instability of prior learnable approaches (Section 4.1, Algorithm 1). This baseline already achieves state-of-the-art results at 8-bit and 6-bit settings (Tables 1–3, rows labeled "FPQ baseline"), demonstrating that floating-point PTQ is viable when format selection is done properly. The baseline is positioned as enabling, not sufficient — it fails at 4-bit, dropping to 42.7% on LLaMA-7B W4A4 and 44.5% on BERT W4A4 (Tables 1–2).

Second, they introduce **pre-shifted exponent bias** (Section 4.2) as the mechanism that pushes through the 4-bit barrier. This technique is intellectually distinct from prior work on inter-channel variance because it leverages the specific structure of floating-point arithmetic — the integer exponent bias — rather than working around it. By decomposing the per-channel activation scaling into a tensor-wise real-valued component and a channel-wise integer exponent bias, then reparameterizing that integer bias into the weight tensor, the method achieves per-channel activation precision with **no additional runtime compute overhead** (the reparameterized weights are pre-computed during calibration). This is a floating-point-native solution to what had previously been treated as an integer quantization problem.

The paper explicitly positions itself relative to SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) — the prior state-of-the-art for post-training LLM quantization — by showing that FPQ achieves better accuracy with **fewer calibration samples and lower activation bit width** (Table 1: 58.1% for FPQ at W4A4 with 32 calibration samples versus 49.1% for SmoothQuant at W4A4 with 512 calibration samples). The paper also positions itself against QAT methods like LLM-QAT (Liu et al., 2023), noting that FPQ outperforms them (+5.3 points on LLaMA-7B W4A4) **without requiring any model retraining**, preserving the deployment simplicity that makes PTQ attractive.

The extension to BERT and vision transformers (Sections 5.2.2, 5.2.3) is strategic: it demonstrates that neither the inter-channel variance pattern nor the FPQ solution is language-model-specific or task-specific. By showing generalization across architectures designed for fundamentally different tasks (zero-shot reasoning, natural language understanding, image classification), the paper strengthens its claim that the approach addresses a property **inherent to the transformer architecture itself** rather than a quirk of LLM pretraining. This broadens the potential impact from "LLM compression" to "transformer compression," encompassing the full spectrum of transformer applications.

Finally, the paper positions its contribution as practically deployable by including a hardware cost analysis (Section 5.4, Table 6). By demonstrating that FP4 operators (adders, multipliers, MAC units) have comparable or lower silicon area than INT4 operators under TSMC 40nm synthesis, the authors preempt the concern that FP quantization's flexibility comes at a hardware cost premium. This is important context for adoption: the method is not only accurate but also implementable on existing and future FP-capable hardware (explicitly citing NVIDIA H100's FP8 support as evidence of the industry trajectory).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation

The paper builds a **two-stage post-training quantization pipeline** that converts a pre-trained transformer model's weights and activations from full-precision floating-point (FP16/FP32) to 4-bit floating-point values using only a small unlabeled calibration dataset (32–128 examples). The pipeline addresses two sequential problems: first, how to select the right floating-point format (how many exponent bits, how many mantissa bits, what clipping range) for each tensor without gradient-based instability; second, how to apply per-channel activation precision without breaking efficient matrix multiplication, which requires shared scaling factors along the reduction dimension.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The FPQ system has three major components that operate sequentially during calibration, plus one runtime mechanism:

1. **Joint Format and Max Value Search (FPQ baseline):** A layer-wise reconstruction procedure (Algorithm 1) that, for each matrix multiplication layer, enumerates candidate FP formats and candidate clipping ranges, applies each combination to quantize that layer's inputs, measures the output reconstruction error against the full-precision layer output, and selects the combination that minimizes this error. This runs for three rounds per layer, alternating between optimizing the activation quantization parameters and the weight quantization parameters.

2. **Pre-Shifted Exponent Bias:** A mechanism that decomposes per-channel activation scaling factors (computed from calibration data statistics) into a tensor-wise real-valued scaling factor plus a channel-wise integer exponent bias. These integer biases are then mathematically absorbed into the corresponding weight tensor (by pre-computing biased weights during calibration), so that at inference time the activations use standard tensor-wise scaling while effectively achieving per-channel precision. This component is the key enabler of 4-bit quantization.

3. **Calibration Data Pipeline:** A small set of unlabeled text segments (for LLaMA: 32 segments of 2048 tokens from the C4 corpus; for BERT: 128 training examples from each GLUE task; for ViT: 128 ImageNet training images) is fed through the full-precision model once to collect intermediate layer outputs. These stored full-precision outputs serve as reconstruction targets for the search procedure.

4. **Runtime Inference (deployed model):** The quantized model performs standard floating-point matrix multiplication using the pre-selected formats and pre-computed biased weights. No per-channel scaling occurs at runtime — all per-channel information is baked into the stored weight tensors during calibration. The hardware implementation uses standard FP4 multipliers and adders (Table 6), which the paper shows are comparable in silicon area to INT4 operators.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the floating-point representation and quantization process (Section 3 of the paper): what floating-point numbers look like at the bit level, how real values get mapped to FP-quantized values, and why the exponent bias is a critical adjustable knob. This foundation is essential because the entire method hinges on clever manipulation of the exponent bias.
- **Second**, the joint format and max value search (Section 4.1, Algorithm 1): how the search space is constructed, what reconstruction metric guides the search, why gradient-based alternatives fail in PTQ, and what hyperparameters control the search. This establishes the FPQ baseline that already achieves SOTA at 6- and 8-bit.
- **Third**, the pre-shifted exponent bias (Section 4.2, Figure 3): the diagnostic observation of inter-channel variance in transformer activations, how this variance causes per-tensor quantization to collapse at 4-bit, the mathematical decomposition of per-channel scaling into tensor-wise + channel-wise components, and the reparameterization trick that bakes per-channel information into weights without runtime cost.
- **Fourth**, the reconstruction framework and calibration choices (Section 5.1, Appendices A and F): the objective function that drives format/clipping range selection, why layer-wise (not block-wise) and parallel (not sequential) reconstruction works best for FP quantization, and the Hessian-based loss metric variant used for BERT and ViT.
- **Fifth**, the hardware implementation considerations (Section 5.4, Table 6): the synthesized area of FP4 operators compared to INT4, and what this means for deployability.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical systems paper** whose core idea is that floating-point quantization becomes viable at 4-bit for transformers if you (1) search for format and clipping range jointly using a reconstruction metric rather than gradient descent, and (2) address the inter-channel activation variance through a floating-point-native reparameterization (exponent bias shifting) rather than through expensive per-channel runtime scaling.

#### Floating-Point Representation and the Quantization Process

The paper's method operates entirely within the floating-point number system, so understanding the bit-level structure and the quantization mapping is essential groundwork.

##### Floating-Point Bit Structure

A standard floating-point number is represented as a tuple of three bit fields:

$$X_{\text{FP}} = (-1)^s 2^{p-b} \left(1 + \frac{d_1}{2} + \frac{d_2}{2^2} + \ldots + \frac{d_m}{2^m}\right)$$

where `$s \in \{0, 1\}$` is the sign bit (1 bit), `$d_i \in \{0, 1\}$` are the mantissa bits (there are `$m$` of them, indexed `$i = 1, \ldots, m$`), `$p \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 2^e - 1\}$` is the unsigned integer represented by the `$e$` exponent bits, and `$b$` is an integer exponent bias.

**What this structure means conceptually:** The sign bit determines positive/negative. The exponent bits determine the overall magnitude scale — raising 2 to the power `$(p - b)$` gives the range. The mantissa bits determine the fractional precision *within* that scale — the term in parentheses is always between 1.0 and nearly 2.0 (specifically, `$1.0$` to `$2 - 2^{-m}$`). A floating-point format with `$e$` exponent bits and `$m$` mantissa bits is denoted EjMk.

**Why this structure matters for quantization:** The exponent allows the representable values to be spaced **non-uniformly** — closely packed near zero (where most neural network values cluster) and sparsely distributed at extreme magnitudes (where few values appear). This is the fundamental advantage over integer quantization, which spaces values uniformly across the entire range.

##### FP Quantization: Step 1 — Scale and Clip

Quantizing a real-valued tensor `$X_R$` to floating-point format proceeds in two conceptual stages. The first stage applies scaling and clipping, analogous to integer quantization but with the clipping bounds determined by both the scaling factor and the exponent bias:

$$X'_R = \text{Clip}(X_R, Q_{\min}, Q_{\max})$$

where the representable range for a signed `$e$`-bit-exponent format is:

$$Q_{\max} = -Q_{\min} = (2 - 2^{-m})2^{2^e - b - 1}$$

**What these equations compute:** The clipping bounds are the most extreme positive and negative values the chosen format can represent. The term `$(2 - 2^{-m})$` is the maximum mantissa value (just under 2.0), and `$2^{2^e - b - 1}$` is the maximum exponent-driven scale. The `$-1$` in the exponent accounts for the sign bit halving the symmetric range.

**The critical insight — absorbing the scaling factor into the exponent bias:** The paper notes that both the real-valued scaling factor `$\alpha$` (from Eq. 2 in the integer quantization formula) and the integer exponent bias `$b$` control the effective clipping range. They can be **combined** into a single parameter. Specifically, the paper defines:

$$\tilde{Q}_{\max} = \alpha Q_{\max} = \alpha \cdot (2 - 2^{-m})2^{2^e - b - 1} = 2^{-\tilde{b}} \cdot (2 - 2^{-m})2^{2^e - 0 - 1}$$

where `$\tilde{b}$` is a relaxed **real-valued** exponent that absorbs both the original integer bias `$b$` and the scaling factor `$\alpha$`. This means the effective bias becomes `$\tilde{b} = b - \log_2 \alpha$` (rearranging from the exponent relation `$2^{-\tilde{b}} = 2^{-b} \cdot \alpha$`).

**Why this absorption is crucial:** By collapsing scaling factor and exponent bias into one continuous parameter `$\tilde{b}$`, the quantization function now has a single knob that controls the clipping range, and this knob can be searched continuously. The clipped tensor is then expressed as:

$$X''_R = \text{Clip}\left(X_R, \tilde{Q}_{\min}, \tilde{Q}_{\max}\right)$$

with:

$$\tilde{b} = 2^e - \log_2 \tilde{Q}_{\max} + \log_2(2 - 2^{-m}) - 1$$

This formulation is what enables the search procedure in Section 4.1 — instead of searching over two interacting parameters (`$\alpha$` and `$b$`), the algorithm searches over the single real-valued `$\tilde{b}$`, which fully determines the clipping behavior.

##### FP Quantization: Step 2 — Compare and Quantize

The second stage maps the clipped real values to discrete floating-point levels:

$$X_{\text{FP}} = \tilde{\alpha} \cdot v \cdot \left\lfloor \frac{X''_R}{\tilde{\alpha} \cdot v} \right\rceil$$

where `$\tilde{\alpha} = 2^{-\tilde{b}}$` is the tensor-wise floating-point scaling factor, and `$v$` is an integer power of 2 determined by which exponent range the scaled value falls into:

$$v = \begin{cases} 2^{\lfloor \log_2 |X''_R| + \tilde{b} \rfloor - m} & \text{if } \lfloor \log_2 |X''_R| + \tilde{b} \rfloor \geq 1 \\ 2^{1 - m} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

**What this computes:** The term `$X''_R / \tilde{\alpha}$` rescales the clipped value by the tensor-wise scaling factor, undoing the earlier scaling so we're working in the native FP range. The expression `$\lfloor \log_2 |X''_R| + \tilde{b} \rfloor$` determines which power-of-2 interval (which exponent `$p$`) the value belongs to — this is the "compare" step that identifies the quantization level. The `$-m$` shift positions the value within the mantissa's fractional precision. The floor condition handles subnormal numbers (values too small for the minimum exponent), forcing them to the smallest representable magnitude. The final multiplication by `$\tilde{\alpha} \cdot v$` reconstructs the quantized value at the correct scale.

**Figure 1 in the paper visualizes this process for FP5 (E2M2):** the real-valued clipped `$X''_R$` is first rescaled, then the appropriate power-of-2 interval `$[2^p, 2^{p+1})$` is identified, and the value is snapped to the nearest representable quantization level within that interval. The result is a value that can be stored in the EjMk format's bit representation.

##### Efficient FP Matrix Multiplication

With quantized tensors, the matrix multiplication for a linear layer becomes:

$$O^{i,k}_{\text{out}} = X^{i,:}_{\text{FP}} W^{:,k}_{\text{FP}} = \tilde{\alpha}_X \tilde{\alpha}^k_W \tilde{X}^{i,:}_{\text{FP}} \tilde{W}^{:,k}_{\text{FP}}$$

where `$X^{i,:}_{\text{FP}}$` is the `$i$`-th row of the activation matrix, `$W^{:,k}_{\text{FP}}$` is the `$k$`-th column of the weight matrix, and `$\tilde{\alpha}_X$`, `$\tilde{\alpha}^k_W$` are the tensor-wise (for activation) and channel-wise (for weight, k-th output channel) scaling factors extracted from the combined bias parameter.

**What this computes:** Each output element is the dot product of a quantized activation row and a quantized weight column, then multiplied by the product of their scaling factors. The scaling factors can be factored out of the summation because they are constant along the reduction dimension — `$\tilde{\alpha}_X$` is shared across all columns for a given row, and `$\tilde{\alpha}^k_W$` is shared across all rows for a given column. This is the standard trick that makes low-precision matrix multiplication efficient: the expensive dot product operates on low-bit integers or low-bit FP values, and the scaling is applied once at the end.

**Why per-channel activation scaling breaks this:** If the activation scaling factor varied per-channel (i.e., `$\tilde{\alpha}^j_X$` depended on column `$j$`), it could not be factored out — it would sit *inside* the summation `$\sum_j \tilde{\alpha}^j_X \tilde{X}^{i,j} \tilde{W}^{j,k}$`, requiring per-element rescaling that destroys the computational savings. Appendix D (Figure 7) catalogs all granularity options that support efficient multiplication: per-tensor activation with per-channel weight, or per-token activation with per-channel weight (where each token-row gets its own scaling). The paper chooses per-tensor activation quantization for simplicity, noting that "per-token quantization theoretically provides greater precision... the accuracy gains achieved through this method are minimal and do not justify the additional computational overhead required" (Appendix D).

#### Joint Format and Max Value Search (FPQ Baseline)

The FPQ baseline solves the problem of **how to choose the right floating-point format and clipping range for each tensor in a post-training setting**, where gradient-based methods are unstable and no end-to-end training provides a recovery signal.

##### The Reconstruction Objective

The core optimization objective is framed as minimizing the perturbation introduced by quantization to the pre-trained network:

$$\min \mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{L}(X_R + \delta X) - \mathcal{L}(X_R)\right]$$

where `$\delta X = X_{\text{FP}} - X_R$` is the quantization error for tensor `$X$`, and `$\mathcal{L}$` is the task loss. Since directly optimizing the final loss with only a small calibration set would overfit catastrophically, the paper adopts a standard proxy (following Choukroun et al., 2019; Wu et al., 2020): minimize the **layer-wise output reconstruction error**:

$$\min (\hat{O} - O)^2$$

where `$O = XY$` is the full-precision output of a matrix multiplication layer (with `$Y$` being either a weight tensor `$W$` or another activation tensor), and `$\hat{O}$` is the output of the same layer using quantized `$X$` and `$Y$`.

**What this computes:** For each layer, the squared L2 distance between the full-precision output and the quantized output. The underlying assumption (validated empirically by the results) is that if the per-layer outputs are preserved, the final model accuracy is preserved.

**Why this objective over alternatives:** Directly optimizing final task accuracy on 32–128 calibration samples would cause the quantization parameters to overfit to those specific examples. Layer-wise reconstruction acts as a regularizer — it forces each layer to preserve its functional behavior independently, which the paper finds works better than block-level reconstruction for FP quantization (Appendix F: "we have observed that increasing the reconstruction granularity does not improve the accuracy of FPQ baseline or sometimes even lead to worse results").

##### Search Space Construction

The search is conducted **separately for each matrix multiplication layer** and **jointly over format and clipping range**. For a q-bit floating-point quantization:

**Format candidates:** All possible `$(e, m)$` splits where `$e + m + 1 = q$` (the +1 accounts for the sign bit), with the constraint that `$e \geq 1$`. The paper explicitly excludes `$e = 0$` formats because "the quantization of the format with an exponent bit equal to 1 already degenerates to INT quantization" (Section 4.1) — with no exponent bits, the representable values are uniformly spaced, losing FP's distribution-matching advantage. For FP4 (q=4), this gives candidate formats: E3M0 (3 exponent, 0 mantissa), E2M1, and E1M2.

**Exponent bias search range:** For each format candidate `$r_i \in \mathcal{R}_X$` applied to tensor `$X$` (and similarly `$r_i \in \mathcal{R}_Y$` for `$Y$`), the algorithm initializes the relaxed exponent bias from the tensor's maximum absolute value:

$$\tilde{b}^{\text{init}} = 2^e - \log_2(\max(|X_R|)) + \log_2(2 - 2^{-m}) - 1$$

This initialization corresponds to setting the clipping range `$\tilde{Q}_{\max}$` equal to the observed maximum magnitude — a natural starting point that captures the full data range. The search range is then defined by scaling this initialization:

$$[\gamma_1 \tilde{b}^{\text{init}}, \gamma_2 \tilde{b}^{\text{init}}]$$

where `$\gamma_1 = 0.01$` and `$\gamma_2 = 1.2$` are empirically chosen (allowing the clipping range to be up to 100× smaller or 1.2× larger than the max-value-based initialization). This range is linearly divided into `$k = 100$` discrete candidates. Table 5 shows that FPQ is robust to these choices, with `$(0.01, 1.2)$`, `$(0.1, 1.2)$`, and `$(0.5, 1.5)$` all producing comparable results, "as long as the search range is not overly aggressive."

**The search space per layer:** For each of `$t$` format candidates applied to activation `$X$`, there are 100 possible `$\tilde{b}_X$` values. Similarly, for each of `$t$` format candidates applied to weight `$Y$`, there are 100 possible `$\tilde{b}_Y$` values. The total search space is `$(t \times 100) \times (t \times 100)$` per layer, searched greedily rather than exhaustively.

##### The Search Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

**Phase 1 — Collect full-precision outputs:** The calibration data is passed through the full-precision model once, and the raw output `$O^l = X^l Y^l$` of each matrix multiplication layer `$l$` is stored. These outputs serve as reconstruction targets — they represent what the layer *should* produce, and quantization parameters are chosen to minimize deviation from these targets.

**Phase 2 — Iterative per-layer optimization (three rounds):** For each layer `$l$`, initialized with format candidate sets `$\mathcal{R}_X$` and `$\mathcal{R}_Y$` and bias search ranges, the algorithm performs the following nested optimization for `$n = 3$` rounds:

1. **Optimize activation bias for each activation format:** For each candidate format `$r^i_X$`, search over the 100 candidate `$\tilde{b}^i_X$` values to find the one that minimizes the reconstruction error `$(\hat{O} - O)^2$`, with `$\hat{O}$` computed using that format and bias for `$X$` and the current best configuration for `$Y$`.

2. **Select best activation format:** Among all `$r^i_X$` (each with its now-optimized `$\tilde{b}^i_X$`), pick the format that gives the lowest reconstruction error.

3. **Optimize weight bias for each weight format:** Same procedure as step 1, but applied to the weight tensor `$Y$` (optimize `$\tilde{b}^i_Y$` for each `$r^i_Y$` while keeping the newly selected activation configuration fixed).

4. **Select best weight format:** Pick the winning `$r^i_Y$` with its optimized `$\tilde{b}^i_Y$`.

This four-step cycle repeats three times for each layer, allowing the activation and weight configurations to co-adapt. The key design choice is the **alternating optimization**: rather than searching the full `$(t \times 100)^2$` joint space exhaustively (which would be `$O(t^2 k^2)$` evaluations per layer), the algorithm alternates between optimizing `$X$` and `$Y$` independently, making the complexity `$O(n \cdot t \cdot k)$` — linear in each factor, not quadratic.

**Parallel execution across layers:** All layers are searched in parallel (not sequentially), meaning each layer's reconstruction uses the full-precision outputs of upstream layers as input (following the "parallel quantization" framework of Yuan et al., 2022 and Bai et al., 2022). This is in contrast to sequential quantization, where each layer's input comes from already-quantized upstream layers. The paper states that "parallel quantization... yields better results than its sequential counterparts" for FP quantization (Appendix F).

**Why this search-based approach beats gradient-based alternatives:** Appendix E reports a direct comparison on DeiT-S at 8-bit: the gradient-based learnable method from Kuzmin et al. (2022) achieves only 74.38% ImageNet accuracy, while FPQ baseline achieves 79.88% (near lossless). The paper attributes this to gradient instability: "each time the exponent bits change, the gradients experience exponential variations" because the exponent bits control the effective scale of the representation nonlinearly. In QAT, this can be stabilized by the ongoing training process; in PTQ, where the optimization is done once on a small calibration set with no recovery opportunity, the instability leads to poor format choices. The search-based method, by contrast, simply evaluates discrete combinations and picks the best — no gradients, no instability.

##### Hessian-Based Loss Metric for BERT and ViT

For BERT and vision transformer models, the paper substitutes the simple L2 reconstruction error with a **Hessian-weighted** variant (Appendix A). Starting from the Taylor expansion of the loss perturbation:

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}(X_R + \delta X) - \mathcal{L}(X_R)] \approx \frac{1}{2} \delta X^T \bar{H}(X) \delta X$$

where `$\bar{H}(X)$` is the Hessian matrix of the loss with respect to `$X$`, and the first-order term vanishes because the pre-trained model is at a local minimum (`$\bar{g}(X) \approx 0$`). Using the Jacobian of the layer output `$O$` with respect to `$X$` and approximating the Hessian of `$O$` with the diagonal Fisher Information Matrix, the metric becomes:

$$\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{O} - O)^T \text{diag}\left(\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial O_1}\right)^2, \ldots, \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial O_n}\right)^2\right)(\hat{O} - O)\right]$$

**What this computes:** For each element `$O_i$` of the layer output, the reconstruction error `$(\hat{O}_i - O_i)^2$` is weighted by the squared gradient of the final loss with respect to that element. Elements that strongly influence the final loss (high `$|\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial O_i|$`) receive higher weight in the reconstruction objective; elements that the loss is insensitive to receive lower weight.

**Why this is used for BERT/ViT but not LLaMA:** The paper does not explicitly state the reason, but the pattern is consistent with the task structure — BERT and ViT are evaluated on discriminative tasks (classification, textual entailment, etc.) where the final loss provides a meaningful per-element sensitivity signal via backpropagation. LLaMA is evaluated on zero-shot generation tasks where such gradients are less directly informative or more expensive to compute across 2048-token sequences. The practical effect is that the Hessian metric allocates reconstruction precision where it matters most for the downstream task.

#### Pre-Shifted Exponent Bias: Solving the Inter-Channel Variance Problem

The FPQ baseline achieves state-of-the-art results at 8-bit and 6-bit quantization (Tables 1–3, "FPQ baseline" rows), but **fails catastrophically at 4-bit**: 42.7% on LLaMA-7B W4A4 (versus 66.3% full precision) and 44.5% on BERT W4A4 (versus 83.7% full precision). The pre-shifted exponent bias is the mechanism that bridges the 4-bit gap.

##### The Inter-Channel Variance Diagnosis

The paper's key diagnostic observation (Section 4.2, Figures 2, 5, 6) is that in transformer activations:

- **Intra-channel variance is low:** Within a single channel (a single column of the activation matrix, corresponding to one feature dimension across all tokens), the activation magnitudes are typically similar — they cluster around a channel-specific mean with relatively small spread.

- **Inter-channel variance is high:** Across different channels, the typical magnitudes can differ by **orders of magnitude**. Some channels consistently produce values of order `$10^{-2}$`, while others produce values of order `$10^1$` or `$10^2$`. Figure 2 shows this visually for LLaMA-7B feed-forward activations, BERT activations, and DeiT activations — in all three, the heatmap reveals vertical striping (per-channel consistency) with dramatic brightness variations across columns (inter-channel differences).

**Why this breaks per-tensor quantization at 4-bit:** With only one scaling factor `$\tilde{\alpha}_X$` shared across all channels, the clipping range `$\tilde{Q}_{\max}$` must accommodate the largest-magnitude channel. This means the scaling factor is set by the outliers. For channels with smaller typical magnitudes, the effective quantization step size (determined by `$\tilde{\alpha}_X \cdot v$`) becomes extremely coarse relative to their values. At 4-bit, with only 16 representable positive values, the vast majority of channels get squeezed into 1–2 quantization levels (essentially zero plus one non-zero value), destroying the information they carry. This is the same phenomenon documented by Dettmers et al. (2022) for 8-bit quantization — but at 8-bit, there are 256 levels, so the damage is less severe. At 4-bit, the inter-channel variance becomes fatal.

##### The Solution: Channel-Wise Integer Exponent Bias

The insight is that the integer exponent bias `$b$` in the FP format provides a natural mechanism for **per-channel scaling without runtime overhead**, if the per-channel information can be pre-computed and absorbed into the weights. The procedure has three stages (visualized in Figure 3):

**Stage 1 — Compute per-channel initial biases from calibration data:** For each channel `$j$` of the activation tensor `$X$`, compute the initial real-valued exponent bias based on that channel's maximum magnitude:

$$\tilde{b}_j = 2^e - \log_2(\max(|X^{:,j}_R|)) + \log_2(2 - 2^{-m}) - 1$$

where `$X^{:,j}_R$` denotes all token positions in channel `$j$`. This is the same formula as Eq. 7, but applied per-channel rather than tensor-wise. The result is a vector `$\tilde{b} \in \mathbb{R}^c$` of per-channel real-valued biases, each tailored to the magnitude range of its channel.

**Stage 2 — Decompose into tensor-wise real + channel-wise integer:** The per-channel biases are separated into a shared real-valued component plus a per-channel integer offset:

$$\tilde{b} = \tilde{\rho} + b^{\text{ori}} = \tilde{\rho} + \text{clip}\left(\lfloor \tilde{b} - \tilde{\rho} \rceil, 0, 2^e - 1\right)$$

where `$\tilde{\rho} \in \mathbb{R}^1$` is a single tensor-wise real-valued exponent (initialized as `$\min_j(\tilde{b}_j)$` to ensure all biases are representable), and `$b^{\text{ori}} \in \mathbb{Z}^c$` is a per-channel integer bias. The clip ensures each integer bias is within `$[0, 2^e - 1]$`, the valid range for the hardware's exponent field.

**What this decomposition achieves:** The tensor-wise `$\tilde{\rho}$` handles the overall magnitude scale of the activation tensor. The per-channel `$b^{\text{ori}}_j$` handles the channel-specific offset — channels with larger magnitudes get larger (or smaller, depending on sign convention) integer biases, effectively shifting their representable range to align with their actual value distribution.

**Why the clip to `$[0, 2^e-1]$` matters:** The integer bias must fit into the standard floating-point exponent field, which has `$e$` bits and can represent values from 0 to `$2^e - 1$`. By constraining `$b^{\text{ori}}$` to this range, the per-channel scaling remains compatible with standard FP hardware — no custom exponent widths are needed.

**Stage 3 — Reparameterize into weights (the critical trick):** After the decomposition, the FP representation for an element in channel `$j$` of the activation becomes:

$$X_{\text{FP}} = 2^{-\tilde{b}_j} (-1)^s 2^{p-0} \left(1 + \frac{d_1}{2} + \ldots + \frac{d_m}{2^m}\right)$$
$$= 2^{-\tilde{\rho}} (-1)^s 2^{p - b^{\text{ori}}_j} \left(1 + \frac{d_1}{2} + \ldots + \frac{d_m}{2^m}\right)$$

Now comes the reparameterization: **the per-channel integer bias `$b^{\text{ori}}_j$` is moved from the activation to the weight**. The activation, after this reparameterization, uses only the tensor-wise scaling `$\tilde{\rho}$`:

$$X_{\text{FP}} = 2^{-\tilde{\rho}} (-1)^s 2^{p-0} \left(1 + \frac{d_1}{2} + \ldots + \frac{d_m}{2^m}\right)$$

Meanwhile, the corresponding element in the `$j$`-th row of the weight tensor `$W$` absorbs the per-channel bias:

$$W_{\text{FP}} = 2^{-\tilde{b}_W} (-1)^s 2^{p - b^{\text{ori}}_j} \left(1 + \frac{d_1}{2} + \ldots + \frac{d_m}{2^m}\right)$$

The weight's quantization format now includes a per-row (which is per-input-channel) integer exponent bias `$b^{\text{ori}}_j$`. This is **pre-computed during calibration** — the biased weights `$W_{\text{FP}}$` are stored in low-bit FP format directly, so no bias shifting occurs at inference time.

**The resulting matrix multiplication:**

$$O^{i,k}_{\text{out}} = X^{i,:}_{\text{FP}} W^{:,k}_{\text{FP}} = \tilde{\alpha}_X \tilde{\alpha}^k_W \tilde{X}^{i,:}_{\text{FP}} (\beta \odot \tilde{W}^{:,k}_{\text{FP}})$$

where `$\beta = 2^{-b^{\text{ori}}}$` is a per-channel weight scaling factor, `$\odot$` is element-wise multiplication, and `$(\beta \odot \tilde{W}^{:,k}_{\text{FP}})$` is pre-computed. The activation scaling is back to tensor-wise, enabling efficient multiplication.

**Why this works without runtime overhead:** The per-channel scaling has been fully absorbed into the stored weight tensors. During calibration, the weights are recomputed once to incorporate `$\beta$`, then quantized. At inference, the matrix multiplication proceeds using standard per-tensor activation scaling and per-channel (output-channel) weight scaling — exactly the same computational pattern as the FPQ baseline, but with the per-channel precision benefits baked into the weight values.

**Why this is a floating-point-native solution:** This trick works specifically because FP numbers have an explicit exponent bias that can be adjusted per-value. In integer quantization, there is no analogous mechanism — per-channel activation scaling requires either per-element rescaling (expensive) or mathematical migration of the scaling difficulty to weights (as SmoothQuant does through a channel-wise scaling matrix that gets folded into adjacent layers). The FP approach is cleaner: the integer exponent bias field is literally designed for adjusting per-value scale, so using it per-channel is a natural extension rather than a workaround.

**Search integration:** During the search process (Algorithm 1 combined with pre-shifted exponent bias), the tensor-wise `$\tilde{\rho}_X$` is treated as the searchable parameter for activations, initialized as `$\min_j(\tilde{b}_j)$` and searched over `$[\gamma_1 \tilde{\rho}^{\text{init}}_X, \gamma_2 \tilde{\rho}^{\text{init}}_X]$`. The per-channel `$\tilde{b}_j$` values are fixed (computed from calibration data statistics) and not searched, because they are deterministic given the data.

#### Calibration Data and Reconstruction Choices

The paper's calibration and reconstruction framework involves several concrete choices that affect performance.

##### Calibration Data Pipeline

**For LLaMA models:** 32 random segments of 2048 tokens each are sampled from the C4 corpus (Raffel et al., 2020), following GPTQ's protocol (Frantar et al., 2023). These segments are passed through the full-precision model, and intermediate layer outputs are collected for the reconstruction targets. The choice of 2048-token segments is significant — it matches LLaMA's pre-training sequence length, ensuring the calibration data distribution matches the distribution the model was trained on. Using shorter segments could shift the activation statistics and lead to suboptimal clipping ranges.

**For BERT models:** 128 examples are randomly sampled from the training set of each GLUE task. The fine-tuned BERT models are obtained from HuggingFace's public repository (`textattack/bert-base-uncased-{TASK_NAME}`).

**For ViT models:** 128 training images from ImageNet serve as calibration data.

**Ablation on calibration size (Table 4):** The paper varies calibration size from 32 to 256 on BERT (MNLI, QQP, CoLA). Results show that MNLI and QQP are "more robust to different settings," while CoLA shows more variance. The key finding is that "FPQ performs well with a calibration set size of 128 data points" but "remains robust and maintains competitive accuracy even with limited access to calibration data, such as when using as few as 32 data points." This robustness is important for practical deployment where large calibration sets may not be available.

##### Layer-Wise vs. Block-Wise Reconstruction

The paper explicitly chooses **layer-wise reconstruction** (each matrix multiplication layer reconstructed independently) over block-wise reconstruction (jointly optimizing multiple layers within a transformer block). Appendix F states: "we have observed that increasing the reconstruction granularity does not improve the accuracy of FPQ baseline or sometimes even lead to worse results." This contrasts with prior integer PTQ work (Li et al., 2021; Bai et al., 2022) that found block-level reconstruction beneficial. The paper does not provide a detailed explanation, but this may be because FP quantization's format selection is more sensitive to the specific distribution of each layer, and block-level optimization creates interdependencies that obscure the per-layer format choice.

##### Parallel vs. Sequential Quantization

The paper uses **parallel quantization**: each layer's reconstruction uses the full-precision outputs of upstream layers as input, and all layers are optimized independently. The alternative, sequential quantization, uses the outputs of already-quantized upstream layers, propagating quantization errors through the network. The paper finds that "parallel quantization... yields better results than its sequential counterparts" for FP quantization (Appendix F). This is likely because FP format selection benefits from seeing the clean, full-precision activation distributions rather than distributions already distorted by upstream quantization errors.

##### Quantized Layers

**For LLaMA:** All weight and activation tensors in fully-connected layers are quantized, "for a fair comparison with previous work" (Section 5.1). This includes the attention projection matrices (query, key, value, output) and the feed-forward network matrices. Word embeddings are kept at higher precision (16-bit in the W4A4 setting, as indicated by the "E/W/A" notation in Table 1 where E=16 for W4A4 experiments).

**For BERT and ViT:** Both fully-connected layers and the activation-activation multiplication tensors in the self-attention module (query-key dot products) are quantized. This is a stricter setting than LLaMA because the attention scores themselves are quantized.

#### Hardware Implementation Considerations

The paper includes a hardware cost analysis (Section 5.4, Table 6) to support the claim that FPQ is practically deployable.

**Synthesis methodology:** The multiply-accumulate (MAC) operators for INT4, INT6, FP4 (E2M1, E3M2), and mixed-format FP4 (E2M1 × E1M2) were implemented in Verilog HDL and synthesized using Cadence Genus under TSMC 40nm technology at 0.5 GHz clock frequency.

**Key findings from Table 6:**

- **Adder area:** INT4 adder = 93 µm²; E2M1 adder = 111 µm²; E3M2 adder = 223 µm². The FP4 adder is slightly larger but within the same order of magnitude as INT4.
- **Multiplier area:** INT4 multiplier = 182 µm²; E2M1 multiplier = 92 µm²; E3M2 multiplier = 138 µm². **FP4 multipliers are substantially smaller** than INT4 multipliers.
- **MAC area:** INT4 MAC = 410 µm²; E2M1 MAC = 443 µm²; E3M2 MAC = 498 µm². The total MAC area is comparable, with FP4 being 8–21% larger.
- **Mixed-format FP4 (E2M1 × E1M2):** MAC area = 432 µm², "comparable hardware area as the standard FP4 operator."

**Interpretation:** The paper explicitly states that "the disparity between FP4 and INT4 adders is small, while INT has twice the hardware cost for the multiplier." This is because FP multiplication involves adding exponents (cheap) and multiplying mantissas (which are narrower than the full integer width since some bits go to the exponent). The "proposed FPQ approach imposes negligible overhead in terms of hardware implementation when compared to the standard FP operators and the hardware cost for FP is comparable with INT." This finding is important context for the method's practical impact — FP4 quantization not only achieves better accuracy than INT4 but can be implemented with comparable silicon area.

**Mixed-format support:** The fact that mixed-format multiplication (E2M1 × E1M2) has comparable area to same-format multiplication means that FPQ's per-layer format selection — which may result in different formats for activations and weights in the same layer — does not require expensive format-conversion hardware. The multiplier can natively handle mixed-format operands at standard cost.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Floating-Point Quantization as a Distinctly Solvable Problem, Not Merely a Format Choice

The paper's foundational conceptual move is to treat floating-point quantization as a **problem of format-and-range co-selection** rather than as a simple format substitution over integer quantization pipelines. This reframing is subtle but consequential.

**What the field assumed before this work:** The dominant approach to post-training quantization for transformers was integer-based (SmoothQuant, GPTQ, QDrop, BrecQ). Methods that did explore floating-point quantization (Kuzmin et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023) treated the FP format as a learnable or manually selected hyperparameter, with optimization focused on the scaling factor — essentially importing the integer quantization optimization template and swapping the representation. The assumption was that integer and FP quantization share enough structural similarity that the same search/optimization strategies apply.

**What this paper does differently:** By collapsing the scaling factor `α` and integer exponent bias `b` into a single real-valued parameter `~b` (Section 3.2, Eq. 6–7), the paper reveals that **the clipping range and the format are not separable optimization problems** — they interact through the exponent bias in a way that has no integer quantization analog. The search space is fundamentally different: in integer quantization, scaling factor and bit width are independent (the scaling factor controls range, the bit width controls precision within that range). In FP quantization, the exponent bits control both range *and* where precision is allocated (more exponent bits = wider dynamic range but coarser mantissa precision), and the bias shifts the coverage within that range. These three knobs — exponent bits, mantissa bits, and bias — form a joint, non-separable optimization landscape.

This insight is what makes the search-based framework (Algorithm 1) necessary rather than merely convenient. Gradient-based methods fail (Appendix E: 74.38% vs. 79.88% on DeiT-S 8-bit) not because gradients are inherently bad for quantization, but because the exponent bits create **discrete transitions in the loss landscape** — incrementally changing the bias eventually triggers a discrete shift in which exponent range values fall into, producing "exponential variations" in gradients. This is not a training instability that can be smoothed out; it is a structural property of FP representation that makes gradient-based format selection unreliable in few-shot PTQ settings.

**Significance beyond raw performance:** This reframing establishes FP-PTQ as a distinct subproblem with its own optimization dynamics, rather than a variant of INT-PTQ. Future work on FP quantization should not borrow INT optimization techniques uncritically but should design methods that account for the joint format-range search problem explicitly.

**Evidence anchoring:** The FPQ baseline — which is purely the search framework without the pre-shifted exponent bias — already achieves SOTA at 6-bit and 8-bit across LLaMA, BERT, and ViT (Tables 1–3, FPQ baseline rows). At 8/8/8 on LLaMA-7B, FPQ baseline (66.1%) matches MinMax FP Quant (66.1%) and exceeds SmoothQuant (64.6%), demonstrating that the search framework is not over-engineered for easy cases. At 6/6/6 on BERT, FPQ baseline (83.2%) nearly matches full precision (83.7%) while MinMax FP Quant with suboptimal format choices (E4M1) drops to 82.4%. The format choice matters, and the search finds the right one.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** Fundamental in its reframing, but the search algorithm itself is incremental — it is a grid search over a discretized space, not a novel optimization technique. The contribution is recognizing *what* needs to be optimized (joint format and range) and *why* gradient methods fail at it, not inventing a sophisticated new search algorithm.

---

### Innovation 2: The Inter-Channel Variance Pattern as a Universal Transformer Property, Not an LLM Artifact

The paper's second conceptual contribution is the recognition that the high inter-channel, low intra-channel variance pattern in activations — previously documented in specific LLM contexts (Dettmers et al., 2022 in LLM.int8(); Xiao et al., 2022 in SmoothQuant) — is **an architectural property of the transformer itself**, not a quirk of large language models or causal attention.

**What the field assumed before this work:** Prior work had observed outlier channels in LLM activations (LLM.int8() documented "emergent features" in 6.7B+ parameter models; SmoothQuant observed activation outliers that complicated per-tensor quantization). The implicit assumption was that this pattern was related to model scale (LLM.int8() argued it emerged only above certain parameter counts), to causal language modeling objectives, or to specific training dynamics. The pattern was treated as a problem to be *worked around* for LLM compression — not as a structural signature of the architecture itself.

**What this paper shows:** Figures 2, 5, and 6 demonstrate the same pattern in **three architecturally and functionally different model classes**: LLaMA-7B (decoder-only causal LM, zero-shot reasoning), BERT-base (encoder-only masked LM, natural language understanding), and DeiT-S (vision transformer, image classification). These models differ in attention mechanism (causal vs. bidirectional), modality (text vs. images), scale (7B vs. 110M for BERT-base vs. 22M for DeiT-S), and training objective. The consistent pattern across this diversity strongly suggests that the inter-channel variance arises from **the transformer's multi-head attention and feed-forward structure itself** — possibly from how residual connections interact with layer normalization to produce channel-specialized features — rather than from scale or language modeling.

**Why this matters conceptually:** If the pattern is architectural rather than scale-dependent, then **any transformer compression method must address it to achieve low-bit quantization**, regardless of model size or task. This transforms the problem from "LLMs have outlier features that need special handling" (a specific engineering challenge) to "transformers produce channel-variant activations as a structural property" (a fundamental compression challenge). It also means that solutions developed for LLMs (like SmoothQuant's per-channel scaling migration) should be evaluated on smaller transformers and non-language modalities, and solutions developed for vision transformers should be tested on LLMs — the pattern's universality enables cross-domain method transfer.

**Evidence anchoring:** The visual evidence in Figure 2 shows vertical striping (per-channel consistency) with dramatic inter-channel brightness variation for all three model classes. The quantitative evidence is that the pre-shifted exponent bias — a method motivated entirely by this diagnostic — provides gains across all three architectures: +15.4 points on LLaMA-7B W4A4 over the FPQ baseline (58.1% vs. 42.7%, Table 1), +35.6 points on BERT W4A4 (80.1% vs. 44.5%, Table 2), and +68.4 points on DeiT-S W4A4 (75.0% vs. 6.6%, Table 3). The universality of both the problem and the solution validates the architectural diagnosis.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** This is a fundamental diagnostic contribution. It does not propose a new theory of *why* transformers exhibit this pattern, but it conclusively demonstrates *that* the pattern is architectural rather than scale- or task-dependent, which reorients the compression research agenda from model-specific patches to architecture-aware solutions.

### Innovation 3: Exponent Bias Reparameterization as a Floating-Point-Native Alternative to Scaling Migration

The pre-shifted exponent bias method introduces a **floating-point-native strategy for handling per-channel variation** that is qualitatively different from the dominant "migrate the scaling difficulty" paradigm established by SmoothQuant.

**What the field did before:** SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2022) addressed the inter-channel variance problem by computing per-channel activation scaling factors and then **mathematically migrating** them into the adjacent weight matrices — multiplying the activation by a per-channel scale and dividing the weight by the same scale, achieving mathematical equivalence. This works but involves: (1) computing the scaling factors from calibration statistics, (2) explicitly modifying the weight values before quantization, and (3) ensuring the migration is absorbed correctly across all layers that consume the activation. It is a workaround that treats the activation scaling problem as an algebraic rearrangement exercise.

LLM.int8() (Dettmers et al., 2022) took a different approach: identify outlier channels, keep them in FP16, and quantize the rest to INT8. This is a hybrid-precision solution that avoids the scaling problem by selectively opting out of quantization for problematic channels.

Both approaches treat the inter-channel variance as an obstacle to be **routed around** — either by moving the scaling burden elsewhere (SmoothQuant) or by abandoning quantization for the problematic values (LLM.int8()).

**What this paper does differently:** The pre-shifted exponent bias **exploits the structure of floating-point representation itself** to absorb the per-channel scaling into the weight tensor without modifying the mathematical function computed. This is not a workaround; it is a leveraging of FP's native capability. The key mechanism — decomposing per-channel `~b_j` into tensor-wise `~ρ` plus integer `b^ori_j`, then folding `b^ori_j` into the weight's exponent — has no analog in integer quantization because integers lack an exponent field. It transforms what was a hardware-unfriendly per-channel activation scaling into a hardware-friendly per-row weight bias that costs nothing at runtime (the weights are pre-computed during calibration).

**Why this is more than just a different mechanism:** The approach is **conceptually cleaner** than scaling migration because:

1. **It is self-contained to a single layer:** The per-channel bias is absorbed into that layer's weight tensor, with no propagation to adjacent layers. SmoothQuant's migration requires modifying both the current layer's weights and the previous layer's weights (or the next layer's, depending on which direction the scaling travels), creating a chain of modifications.

2. **It preserves the original mathematical function exactly** (subject only to quantization error of the weights and activations separately), without introducing additional numerical transformations that could interact with the quantization process in unexpected ways.

3. **It exploits hardware that already exists:** The exponent bias field is standard in IEEE 754 floating-point and in GPU FP formats. No custom hardware support is needed. Table 6 shows that the modified weight format (with per-row integer biases) uses the same arithmetic units as standard FP.

**Evidence anchoring:** On LLaMA-13B W4A4, FPQ baseline (which has the search framework but no per-channel handling) scores 44.5%, while FPQ with pre-shifted exponent bias scores 63.1% — the bias mechanism contributes 18.6 points, or roughly 76% of the total improvement over the baseline at 4-bit (Table 1). The gap is even larger on DeiT-S: FPQ baseline scores 6.6%, FPQ scores 75.0% — a 68.4-point delta that isolates the exponent bias contribution (Table 3). The failure mode at 4-bit is dominated by the inter-channel variance problem, and the exponent bias is the solution.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** This is a fundamental innovation in mechanism design — it introduces a new class of solution (exponent-field manipulation) to a problem previously addressed only by algebraic rearrangement or hybrid precision. The technique is specific to floating-point quantization, but the *idea* — using representational structure to absorb complexity — is general.

### Innovation 4: Verifier-Free Calibration Through Layer-Wise Reconstruction as an Alternative to Gradient-Based or Block-Level Optimization

The paper's empirical finding that **layer-wise parallel reconstruction with a simple L2 or Hessian-weighted metric outperforms block-level and gradient-based optimization for FP-PTQ** constitutes a methodological insight that contradicts the trend in integer PTQ literature.

**What the field assumed before this work:** The integer PTQ literature has trended toward **increasing reconstruction granularity**: from layer-wise (Nagel et al., 2020) to block-wise (Li et al., 2021; Bai et al., 2022) to even larger modules (Lee et al., 2023). The rationale is intuitive — optimizing larger blocks jointly prevents error propagation across layers and allows the optimization to find compensating parameter choices. Similarly, the trend has been toward more sophisticated reconstruction metrics: Hessian-weighted losses (Li et al., 2021), knowledge-distillation-based objectives, and learned step sizes. The assumption, carried implicitly into FP quantization work, was that FP-PTQ would benefit from the same trends.

**What this paper finds:** Appendix F explicitly states that for FP quantization, **"increasing the reconstruction granularity does not improve the accuracy of FPQ baseline or sometimes even lead to worse results."** Layer-wise reconstruction with simple L2 error (or Hessian-weighted L2 for BERT/ViT) is optimal. Furthermore, parallel quantization — where each layer is optimized independently using full-precision upstream inputs — outperforms sequential quantization where upstream quantization errors propagate. This is the opposite of the intuition that led to block-level and sequential methods.

**Why this is a non-trivial finding:** The paper does not fully explain why this is the case, but the pattern is consistent enough to suggest a mechanistic cause: FP format selection is **brittle to distribution shift**. When upstream layers introduce quantization error, the input distribution to the current layer changes — and the FP format/bias that was optimal for the clean distribution may be suboptimal for the distorted one. Because the FP format choice (number of exponent bits) is a discrete structural decision, getting it wrong based on distorted calibration inputs cannot be compensated for by adjusting the clipping range. In integer quantization, only the scaling factor is chosen; a suboptimal scaling factor still yields uniform quantization that degrades gracefully. In FP quantization, the wrong format choice (e.g., E2M1 when E1M2 was needed) can cause catastrophic precision loss because the dynamic range/numerical precision tradeoff is fundamentally misaligned.

Layer-wise parallel reconstruction avoids this by always seeing the clean, full-precision distribution when making format decisions. The format choices are locally optimal (best for that layer's true input distribution), even if they are globally suboptimal in a block-level sense. The empirical results suggest that **format correctness dominates error propagation concerns** for FP-PTQ — it is better to have the right format for each layer independently than to have coordinated but format-misaligned layers.

**Evidence anchoring:** The results at 6-bit and 8-bit across all three architectures (Tables 1–3, FPQ baseline rows) show near-lossless performance, indicating that the layer-wise parallel approach is not holding back performance at moderate bit widths. At 4-bit, the FPQ baseline fails (42.7% on LLaMA-7B, 44.5% on BERT), but the failure is driven by the inter-channel variance problem (which the pre-shifted exponent bias addresses), not by the reconstruction granularity. The success at 4-bit with pre-shifted exponent bias (58.1% LLaMA-7B, 80.1% BERT, 75.0% DeiT-S) shows that layer-wise reconstruction with the FPQ baseline framework is sufficient even at extreme compression when the inter-channel variance is handled.

**Significance beyond this paper:** This finding establishes that FP-PTQ has **different optimization dynamics than INT-PTQ**, and that techniques from the integer literature do not transfer uncritically. Future FP-PTQ methods should be validated at layer-wise granularity before assuming block-level optimization is beneficial. This is a negative result (block-level ≠ always better) with positive implications for methodology design.

**Is this fundamental or incremental?** Incremental as an empirical finding, but with important methodological implications. It is a "the obvious thing doesn't work" result that reorients how FP-PTQ should be approached.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets and tasks.** The paper evaluates on three distinct benchmarks, each associated with a different transformer architecture: (1) **LLaMA models (7B, 13B)** are evaluated on six common sense zero-shot reasoning tasks — BoolQ, PIQA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, ARC-easy, and ARC-challenge — with the average score across all six reported as the primary metric. The evaluation harness is EleutherAI's lm-evaluation-harness (Section 5.1). (2) **BERT-base** is evaluated on the GLUE benchmark development set, encompassing eight tasks: MNLI-m, QQP, QNLI, SST-2, CoLA, STS-B, MRPC, and RTE, with the average score reported (Section 5.2.2, Table 2). (3) **Vision transformers (DeiT-S, DeiT-B, ViT-S)** are evaluated on the ImageNet-1K classification task, with top-1 accuracy reported (Section 5.2.3, Table 3).

- **Base models.** The paper uses **LLaMA-7B and LLaMA-13B** (Touvron et al., 2023) as the primary LLM testbeds, chosen because they are open-weight models representative of contemporary LLM capabilities and prior PTQ work (Xiao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023) has established baselines on them. For encoder-only evaluation, **BERT-base** (Devlin et al., 2019) is used, with task-specific fine-tuned checkpoints obtained from HuggingFace's public repository (`textattack/bert-base-uncased-{TASK_NAME}`). For vision, **DeiT-S, DeiT-B, and ViT-S** are used to demonstrate architectural generalizability beyond NLP.

- **Metrics.** For LLaMA: **zero-shot accuracy (%)** on each reasoning task, with the "Avg." column in Table 1 representing the arithmetic mean across all six tasks. Answers are scored as correct/incorrect based on exact match against ground truth using the EleutherAI harness. For BERT: task-specific metrics as standard for GLUE (accuracy for MNLI-m, QQP, QNLI, SST-2, MRPC, RTE; Matthews correlation for CoLA; Pearson correlation for STS-B), with "Avg." being the arithmetic mean. For ViT: **top-1 ImageNet classification accuracy (%).**

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against: (1) **MinMax INT Quant** — naïve integer quantization using the full min-max range for clipping range determination, applied at various bit widths; (2) **MinMax FP Quant** — naïve floating-point quantization with a manually specified format (e.g., E4M3 for 8-bit, E2M1 for 4-bit) using min-max clipping range; (3) **SmoothQuant** (Xiao et al., 2022) — the prior state-of-the-art for post-training LLM quantization, which migrates activation quantization difficulty to weights through a smoothing factor; (4) **GPTQ** (Frantar et al., 2023) — a leading weight-only quantization method; (5) **LLM-QAT** (Liu et al., 2023) — a quantization-aware training method (not PTQ) included for reference at 4-bit LLaMA; (6) **BrecQ** (Li et al., 2021) and **QDrop** (Wei et al., 2022) for BERT; (7) **MREM-S/MREM-P** (Bai et al., 2022) for BERT; (8) **PTQ4ViT** (Yuan et al., 2022) and **APQ-ViT** (Ding et al., 2022) for vision transformers. The paper's own "FPQ baseline" (search framework without pre-shifted exponent bias) also serves as an internal baseline isolating the contribution of the exponent bias mechanism.

- **Compute accounting.** The paper measures compression in terms of **bit width** (not FLOPs or latency). Configurations are reported using the notation E/W/A: bit-width of word Embeddings, model Weights, and Activations, respectively. For example, "4/4/4" means the embeddings, weights, and activations are all quantized to 4 bits. For LLaMA, all fully-connected layer weight and activation tensors are quantized (not attention or layer norm). For BERT and ViT, both fully-connected layers and the attention score computation (activation-activation multiplication) are quantized. The calibration data budget is measured in **number of examples**: 32 segments (2048 tokens each) for LLaMA, 128 examples for BERT, 128 images for ViT.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper reports no cross-validation, no standard deviations, and no confidence intervals for any of the results in Tables 1–6. All reported numbers are single-run evaluations on the standard test/dev sets. For ablation studies (Tables 4–5), results are similarly single-run. This is a notable limitation: with 500-question test sets (LLaMA — actually 6 tasks of varying sizes), single evaluations on GLUE dev sets, and the ImageNet validation set, the reported accuracy numbers are point estimates with unknown variance. The 4-bit regime is especially sensitive because small perturbations in quantization parameters can produce large accuracy swings. The lack of statistical characterization (multiple calibration seeds, multiple quantization initializations, confidence intervals) means the precise ranking of methods at small performance gaps (e.g., FPQ baseline vs. MinMax FP Quant at 8-bit) should be interpreted cautiously.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### LLaMA Zero-Shot Reasoning (Table 1)

**Headline result:** FPQ achieves a 63.1 average zero-shot reasoning score on LLaMA-13B with 4-bit weights, 4-bit activations, and 16-bit embeddings (E16/W4/A4), representing only a 5.8-point drop from the full-precision 68.9. This outperforms the prior state-of-the-art PTQ method (SmoothQuant, E16/W4/A4) by 12.7 points (63.1 vs. 50.4) and even exceeds the QAT method LLM-QAT (E16/W4/A4) by 5.3 points on LLaMA-7B (58.1 vs. 52.8).

**At 8-bit (W8A8), FP quantization is essentially lossless.** On LLaMA-7B, MinMax FP Quant (E4M3) achieves 66.1% average, matching the full-precision 66.3% and surpassing SmoothQuant (64.6%). FPQ baseline and FPQ both score 66.1% and 66.0% respectively. On LLaMA-13B, MinMax FP Quant and FPQ baseline both achieve 68.9%, exactly matching full precision. This demonstrates that with adequate bit width, floating-point quantization handles transformer distributions naturally without sophisticated optimization — the paper's recommendation to "simply use the MinMax FP Quant method for the 8/8/8 setting" (Section 5.2.1) is supported.

**At 6-bit (W6A6 on BERT) and mixed 4/4/8 (W4A8 on LLaMA), FP methods maintain near-lossless performance while INT methods degrade.** On LLaMA-7B W4A8, MinMax FP Quant (E2M1/E4M3) scores 64.9% versus MinMax INT Quant's 39.7% — a 25.2-point gap demonstrating the severity of the distributional mismatch for integer quantization even when activations have 8-bit precision. FPQ baseline (65.3%) and FPQ (65.4%) close the remaining gap nearly completely.

**At 4-bit (W4A4), the FPQ baseline breaks down, confirming that search alone is insufficient.** On LLaMA-7B, FPQ baseline scores 42.7% — a 23.6-point drop from full precision and only 4.6 points above naïve MinMax FP Quant (37.4%). The baseline fails because per-tensor activation quantization cannot handle the inter-channel variance at 4-bit (Section 4.2). On LLaMA-13B, the FPQ baseline scores 44.5%.

**The pre-shifted exponent bias (FPQ) closes the 4-bit gap.** On LLaMA-7B W4A4, FPQ achieves 58.1% — a 15.4-point improvement over the FPQ baseline and a 20.7-point improvement over MinMax FP Quant. On LLaMA-13B, FPQ achieves 63.1% — an 18.6-point improvement over the FPQ baseline (44.5%). The per-task breakdown (Table 1) shows the improvement is broad-based rather than dominated by a single task: BoolQ improves from 54.3% (baseline) to 71.9% (FPQ); PIQA from 57.7% to 74.8%; HellaSwag from 35.7% to 53.3%; WinoGrande from 52.2% to 66.7%; ARC-e from 41.1% to 71.7%; ARC-c from 25.7% to 39.9%.

**Comparison to SmoothQuant at 4-bit is particularly stark.** On LLaMA-7B W4A4 with E16 (since SmoothQuant keeps embeddings at 16-bit), SmoothQuant achieves 49.1% using 512 calibration samples, while FPQ achieves 58.1% using only 32 calibration samples — a 9.0-point accuracy advantage with 16x fewer calibration examples. On LLaMA-13B, the gap widens to 12.7 points (63.1 vs. 50.4). This is significant because SmoothQuant was the previous PTQ state-of-the-art specifically designed for LLMs, and FPQ beats it with less calibration data and no hand-crafted migration factors.

**FPQ at W4A4 even exceeds the QAT method LLM-QAT on LLaMA-7B** (58.1% vs. 52.8%), despite LLM-QAT requiring full quantization-aware retraining. This is a noteworthy result: the paper's PTQ method beats a method that had access to the training data distribution and gradient updates throughout the quantization process.

#### BERT on GLUE (Table 2)

**Headline result:** FPQ achieves an 80.1 average GLUE score on BERT-base with 4-bit weights and 4-bit activations (E4/W4/A4), compared to 83.7 for full precision — a 3.6-point degradation. This surpasses the best prior 4-bit PTQ method (QDrop, W4A4 with E8) by 7.8 points (80.1 vs. 72.3) and exceeds BrecQ (W4A4 with E8) by 44.3 points.

**At 8-bit (W8A8), FPQ baseline achieves near-lossless performance** (83.5%), comparable to the best MinMax FP Quant format (E4M3: 83.4%, E5M2: 83.3%). The format choice matters: MinMax FP with E2M5 scores only 80.7%, demonstrating that for BERT at moderate bit widths, the exponent-mantissa allocation is a significant accuracy determinant.

**At 6-bit (W6A6), only floating-point formats with sufficient mantissa bits remain viable.** MinMax FP Quant with E2M3 (2 exponent, 3 mantissa) scores only 69.6%, while E3M2 (3 exponent, 2 mantissa) scores 83.0% and E4M1 scores 82.4%. The FPQ baseline selects optimally and achieves 83.2% — within 0.5 points of full precision. MinMax INT Quant at 6-bit collapses to 37.9%.

**At 4-bit (W4A4), the FPQ baseline fails and FPQ recovers.** The FPQ baseline scores 44.5%, barely above naïve MinMax INT Quant (32.9%) and MinMax FP Quant (34.1%). FPQ with pre-shifted exponent bias reaches 80.1% — a 35.6-point improvement over the baseline. The per-task breakdown shows the recovery is broad: MNLI-m recovers from 38.9% (baseline) to 82.3% (FPQ), QQP from 68.3% to 89.2%, QNLI from 55.3% to 86.6%, SST-2 from 83.6% to 91.5%, CoLA from 10.6% to 52.6%, STS-B from 0.0% to 85.5%, MRPC from 43.8% to 83.8%, and RTE from 55.2% to 69.0%. The STS-B and CoLA recoveries are particularly dramatic — these tasks went to zero (or near-zero) correlation under the baseline, indicating total information loss from per-tensor activation quantization, and were restored to usable levels.

**At W4A8 (4-bit weights, 8-bit activations), FPQ achieves essentially lossless performance** (83.6%) with only 128 calibration samples, while MREM-S/MREM-P require 4096 calibration samples (32x more) to reach 82.1–82.2%. This shows that when activations have adequate precision, the search-based format selection alone (FPQ baseline: 82.9%) nearly closes the gap, and FPQ pushes it over the finish line.

#### Vision Transformer on ImageNet (Table 3)

**Headline result:** FPQ achieves 75.0% top-1 accuracy on DeiT-S with 4-bit weights and 4-bit activations, compared to 79.9% full precision — a 4.9-point degradation. This represents a 31.4-point improvement over the previous best 4-bit method (APQ-ViT: 43.6%) and a 40.9-point improvement over PTQ4ViT (34.1%). On DeiT-B at 4-bit, FPQ achieves 79.4% versus 81.8% full precision, and on ViT-S, FPQ reaches 73.2% versus 81.4% full precision.

**At 6-bit, FPQ is essentially lossless across all three ViT architectures.** FPQ achieves 79.5% on DeiT-S (full precision: 79.9%), 81.8% on DeiT-B (full precision: 81.8%), and 81.1% on ViT-S (full precision: 81.4%). The FPQ baseline similarly achieves near-lossless results (79.4%, 81.7%, 80.9%). MinMax FP Quant with E3M2 achieves 79.3%, 81.7%, and 80.7% respectively — the format choice is less critical at 6-bit for ViT than for BERT because 6 bits provide enough precision to mask suboptimal format decisions. The integer-based methods (PTQ4ViT, APQ-ViT) score 76.3–79.2% at 6-bit, leaving a 0.4–2.8 point gap to full precision.

**At 4-bit, integer-based methods collapse while FPQ with pre-shifted exponent bias recovers.** The FPQ baseline scores only 6.6% on DeiT-S, 0.7% on DeiT-B, and 0.3% on ViT-S — essentially random or worse than random performance. MinMax FP Quant (E2M1) similarly achieves 0.1–0.4% across all three architectures. The prior best 4-bit methods (PTQ4ViT and APQ-ViT) score 34.1–67.5% — substantially better than the FPQ baseline but far below usable accuracy. FPQ's recovery to 73.2–79.4% demonstrates that the inter-channel variance problem is the dominant failure mode at 4-bit for vision transformers, just as it is for language transformers, and that the pre-shifted exponent bias is the key enabler across modalities.

**The cross-modal consistency of the inter-channel variance pattern is supported by this data.** The fact that FPQ rescue works equally dramatically on DeiT-S (68.4-point improvement over baseline), BERT (35.6-point improvement), and LLaMA (15.4-point improvement on 7B) — despite the vastly different architectures, modalities, and scales — corroborates the paper's claim that the pattern is a structural property of transformers. The smaller absolute improvement on LLaMA-7B reflects the higher difficulty of the zero-shot reasoning tasks rather than less benefit from the technique.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Calibration size (Table 4).** The paper tests calibration sizes of {32, 64, 128, 256} on BERT-base for MNLI-m, QQP, and CoLA at both 4-bit and 6-bit settings. For 4-bit W4A4 on MNLI-m, FPQ scores range from 81.5% (32 examples) to 82.3% (128 examples) — a tight 0.8-point band. QQP similarly spans 89.0–89.4%. CoLA is more sensitive, ranging from 44.4 (32) to 52.9 (256), with a notable jump from 47.9 (64) to 52.6 (128). At 6-bit, all tasks are more robust: MNLI-m ranges 84.5–84.8%, QQP 90.8–90.9%, CoLA 55.0–58.2%. The paper concludes FPQ "performs well with a calibration set size of 128 data points" but "remains robust and maintains competitive accuracy even with limited access to calibration data, such as when using as few as 32 data points." The CoLA sensitivity is expected since it is the smallest GLUE task and fine-tuned models on small datasets are more brittle to quantization.

**Search range for exponent bias (Table 5).** Three pairs of `$(\gamma_1, \gamma_2)$` are tested on BERT-base: `$(0.01, 1.2)$`, `$(0.1, 1.2)$`, and `$(0.5, 1.5)$`. At 4-bit on MNLI-m, all three achieve 82.2–82.3% — nearly identical. On QQP, the narrower range `$(0.5, 1.5)$` scores slightly lower (88.4%) than the other two (89.1–89.2%). On CoLA at 4-bit, `$(0.1, 1.2)$` achieves 53.6% versus 52.6–52.8% for the other two — a marginal 0.8–1.0 point difference. At 6-bit, all three ranges produce nearly identical results across tasks. The paper concludes that "FPQ exhibits robustness to various `$\gamma_1$` and `$\gamma_2$`, as long as the search range is not overly aggressive." However, "overly aggressive" is not operationally defined beyond the tested values; testing ranges like `$(0.001, 2.0)$` would provide a clearer boundary.

**Hessian-based vs. L2 reconstruction metric.** While not presented as a formal ablation table, Appendix A describes the substitution of the simple L2 metric with a Hessian-weighted variant for BERT and ViT models (not LLaMA). The paper does not report a direct comparison between L2 and Hessian-weighted reconstruction for these models, which is a notable gap — the reader cannot determine whether the Hessian metric provides significant benefit or if L2 would have sufficed.

**Parallel vs. sequential quantization (Appendix F).** The paper states that parallel quantization "yields better results than its sequential counterparts" for FP quantization but provides no quantitative comparison table. This is a methodological claim without direct empirical support in the paper's results section.

**Layer-wise vs. block-wise reconstruction (Appendix F).** Similarly, the paper asserts that "increasing the reconstruction granularity does not improve the accuracy of FPQ baseline or sometimes even lead to worse results" but provides no quantitative comparison between layer-wise and block-wise reconstruction. This is an important methodological claim that lacks direct tabular support.

**Gradient-based vs. search-based format selection (Appendix E).** On DeiT-S at 8-bit, the gradient-based learnable method (Kuzmin et al., 2022) achieves 74.38% ImageNet accuracy while FPQ baseline achieves 79.88%. This 5.5-point gap is a concrete negative result supporting the search-based approach. The paper provides a mechanistic explanation: "each time the exponent bits change, the gradients experience exponential variations, leading to high instability."

**FP format ablation on BERT (Figure 4, Appendix B).** The quantization error of different 8-bit FP formats (E2M5, E3M4, E4M3, E5M2) is visualized across different BERT modules (first, fifth, and last layers). The figures show that "the optimal FP formats differ depending on the specific module that we are quantizing" — some modules favor E3M4, others E4M3, confirming the necessity of per-layer format search.

**Hardware cost comparison (Table 6).** The synthesized area under TSMC 40nm at 0.5 GHz shows: INT4 MAC = 410 µm²; FP4 E2M1 MAC = 443 µm²; FP4 E3M2 MAC = 498 µm²; mixed-format E2M1×E1M2 MAC = 432 µm². FP4 multipliers are substantially smaller than INT4 multipliers (92–138 µm² vs. 182 µm²) while adders are slightly larger (111–223 µm² vs. 93 µm²). The total MAC area is comparable. The paper uses this to argue negligible hardware overhead, though the 8–21% MAC area increase for FP4 over INT4 may matter for area-constrained designs and is not strictly "negligible" in all contexts.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "FPQ is the first usable 4-bit weight and activation quantized LLaMA-13B model with only 5.8-point degradation in zero-shot reasoning."

This claim is **supported by the data in Table 1**, with important qualifications about what "usable" means. The 63.1 average score on LLaMA-13B W4A4 is indeed dramatically better than prior work (SmoothQuant: 50.4; LLM-QAT: 52.8 on LLaMA-7B), and the 5.8-point drop from full precision (68.9) is substantially smaller than would be expected given prior PTQ performance at this bit width. However, "usable" is domain-dependent: a 5.8-point average drop masks per-task variation — BoolQ drops from 77.9 to 71.9 (6.0 points), PIQA from 79.2 to 74.8 (4.4 points), HellaSwag from 59.9 to 53.3 (6.6 points), WinoGrande from 72.6 to 66.7 (5.9 points), ARC-e from 77.4 to 71.7 (5.7 points), and ARC-c from 46.4 to 39.9 (6.5 points). On ARC-c, the model drops to 39.9% — which is still substantially above the 25% random baseline (4-choice multiple choice) but represents a meaningful capability loss. For deployment contexts where the model is expected to match full-precision reliability, 4-bit FPQ still leaves a non-trivial gap.

More critically, **no generation-quality evaluation is conducted**. Zero-shot reasoning tasks test whether the model produces the correct final answer, but the paper provides no evaluation of text generation quality (perplexity, human evaluation, coherence metrics) at 4-bit. A compressed model that can correctly answer multiple-choice questions might still produce degraded free-form text. Given that LLaMA is primarily a generative model, this is a significant evaluation gap. The claim "first usable" should be interpreted as "first competitive on zero-shot reasoning benchmarks" rather than "first deployable in production for all use cases."

#### Claim 2: "FPQ outperforms previous state-of-the-art by 12.7 points" (on LLaMA-13B W4A4).

**Supported by Table 1.** The 12.7-point gap between FPQ (63.1) and SmoothQuant (50.4) on LLaMA-13B W4A4 is substantial. However, SmoothQuant uses 512 calibration samples vs. FPQ's 32, making the comparison somewhat unfair in FPQ's favor if calibration data quantity matters (though Table 4 shows FPQ is robust to calibration size). Additionally, SmoothQuant was not originally designed for sub-8-bit activation quantization and does not claim 4-bit activation viability — the comparison is against a method operating at the edge of its design range. A stronger comparison would include SmoothQuant-like methods specifically tuned for 4-bit activations, but no such prior method exists (which itself supports the paper's claim that 4-bit activation PTQ is an unsolved problem).

#### Claim 3: "The method generalizes to BERT and vision transformers, demonstrating that the inter-channel variance pattern is a universal transformer property."

**Supported by Tables 2–3 and Figures 2, 5–6**, with the qualification that the paper demonstrates *consistency* of the pattern across the tested models but does not prove *universality*. The tested architectures — LLaMA (decoder-only, 7B), BERT-base (encoder-only, 110M), DeiT-S (vision, 22M) — cover three substantially different transformer variants. The fact that the pre-shifted exponent bias provides gains across all three (15.4 points on LLaMA-7B, 35.6 points on BERT, 68.4 points on DeiT-S at 4-bit) strongly suggests a shared underlying mechanism. However, "universal" would require testing on encoder-decoder architectures (T5, BART), on models with different normalization schemes, on much smaller transformers (e.g., GPT-2 124M), and on much larger ones (LLaMA-65B). The paper tests only one model family per architecture class and one scale per family, limiting the evidence for true universality.

#### Genuine experimental strengths

**1. The baseline comparisons are thorough within the PTQ domain.** At each bit width and architecture combination, the paper compares against the most relevant prior work (SmoothQuant for LLMs, QDrop/BrecQ/MREM for BERT, PTQ4ViT/APQ-ViT for ViT), and includes both trivial baselines (MinMax INT, MinMax FP) and internal ablations (FPQ baseline). This makes the contribution of the pre-shifted exponent bias clearly isolable.

**2. The cross-modal evaluation is genuinely unusual in the quantization literature.** Most PTQ papers test on a single architecture family. Testing on LLaMA, BERT, and ViT — with their different task formats, evaluation protocols, and data modalities — provides evidence that the method is not overfit to LLM-specific properties.

**3. The calibration data efficiency is demonstrated through the ablation.** Testing calibration sizes down to 32 examples and showing robustness (Table 4) addresses a common practical concern that PTQ methods require unrealistically large calibration sets.

#### Genuine weaknesses and missing evidence

**1. No statistical characterization of results.** All numbers in Tables 1–5 are single-run point estimates. With 32 calibration samples randomly drawn, different calibration draws could produce different quantization parameters and different final accuracies. On tasks with small test sets (CoLA: 1,043 dev examples; MRPC: 408; RTE: 277), the test accuracy itself has non-trivial variance. Without reporting standard deviations across multiple calibration seeds, the reader cannot assess whether a 1-point accuracy difference between methods is meaningful or noise. This is particularly important at 4-bit where small perturbations in the exponent bias or format choice can produce large accuracy swings (as evidenced by the gap between FPQ baseline and FPQ).

**2. No perplexity or generation quality evaluation for LLaMA.** The paper exclusively evaluates LLaMA on zero-shot reasoning accuracy, which is only one dimension of model capability. A 4-bit quantized LLaMA-13B might maintain multiple-choice reasoning accuracy while producing degraded free-form generations (repetitive token patterns, reduced vocabulary diversity, grammatical errors). Including perplexity on held-out text (e.g., WikiText-2, C4) would provide a more complete picture of compression quality. The BERT evaluation is similarly incomplete — only GLUE task accuracy is reported, without BERT's next-sentence prediction or masked language modeling quality measured.

**3. No large-scale evaluation (LLaMA-30B, -65B).** The paper's central claim about "LLMs" is tested only up to 13B parameters. The inter-channel variance pattern's relationship to model scale — specifically, whether the pattern intensifies with scale (as Dettmers et al., 2022 found for "emergent features" in LLM.int8()) — is not characterized. If outlier channels become more extreme at larger scales, FPQ might degrade more severely on 30B/65B models. Conversely, if the pattern stabilizes, FPQ might work even better at scale. This is a significant gap given that the practical motivation for 4-bit quantization is strongest for the largest models.

**4. The FPQ baseline's 4-bit failure is not sufficiently analyzed.** The FPQ baseline drops to 42.7% on LLaMA-7B W4A4 — the paper attributes this to inter-channel variance but does not provide a quantitative decomposition of the error. How much of the 23.6-point gap from full precision is due to weight quantization error, how much to activation quantization error without per-channel handling, and how much is irreducible (the information-theoretic limit of 4-bit representation)? This decomposition would clarify whether further gains are possible or whether 4-bit approaches fundamental limits.

**5. No evaluation on non-transformer architectures.** The paper argues the inter-channel variance is a transformer property, but no control experiment on, say, an MLP-Mixer or a pure CNN demonstrates that the pattern does not exist in non-transformer architectures. This weakens the "inherent to transformer" claim.

**6. The hardware cost analysis (Table 6) uses 40nm synthesis, not a modern process node.** 40nm TSMC is a legacy technology; modern ML accelerators are fabricated at 7nm, 5nm, or 4nm. While the relative INT4 vs. FP4 area ratios may be stable across process nodes, the absolute area numbers are not informative for modern deployment. Additionally, the synthesis is at 0.5 GHz — modern accelerators run at 1–2 GHz. The analysis demonstrates feasibility but not optimality.

**7. Missing ablation on the number of search rounds n = 3.** Algorithm 1 iterates for n = 3 rounds. Testing n = 1, 2, 4 would characterize whether the alternating optimization converges and whether 3 is the right stopping point. The paper provides no justification for this hyperparameter choice beyond implicit empirical success.

**8. No comparison to weight-only 4-bit quantization + FP16 activations.** A natural baseline that the paper does not include: use GPTQ or similar to quantize only weights to 4-bit while keeping activations at FP16. This configuration is supported by existing hardware (FP16 matrix multiplication) and would isolate whether the 4-bit activation quantization is providing meaningful additional compression benefit over weight-only quantization. Table 1 includes a W4A16 setting (GPTQ at 4/4/16 achieves 64.0%), but does not compare W4A4 FPQ against W4A16 baselines with the same total memory footprint.

#### What experiments would strengthen the paper

- **Multiple calibration seed evaluation** (5–10 seeds, reporting mean ± std) on at least the 4-bit benchmark, to characterize the method's stability.
- **Perplexity evaluation** on WikiText-2 for quantized LLaMA models to measure generation quality degradation.
- **Scaling to LLaMA-30B or -65B** to test whether the inter-channel variance pattern and FPQ's performance scale predictably.
- **Direct comparison of L2 vs. Hessian-weighted reconstruction** for BERT and ViT, presented as a quantitative ablation rather than an appendix note.
- **Ablation on the number of search rounds n** to characterize convergence behavior.
- **Comparison to weight-only 4-bit quantization with FP16 activations** at matched total model size (accounting for activation memory).

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Difficulty Estimation Pre-Processing Step Is Extraordinarily Expensive

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal scaling framework depends on estimating each prompt's difficulty *before* the adaptive strategy can be selected. The paper's proposed method for difficulty estimation — generating 2048 samples per question, then averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle bins) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted bins) — is far more expensive than the test-time budgets it is optimizing. The paper acknowledges this explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

This is not a minor accounting omission. The difficulty estimation step requires 2048 complete generations per prompt, which is 4–8× larger than the largest test-time compute budgets studied (256–512 generations). In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Section 7, Figure 9), the efficiency gains of test-time compute are computed *excluding* the cost of learning the difficulty that enables the strategy selection.

**The consequence.** In any realistic deployment scenario where difficulty estimation must be performed online (i.e., on novel prompts not seen during calibration), the total latency and compute cost could easily exceed that of simply running best-of-256 or even the larger pretrained model with greedy decoding. The claimed 4× compute efficiency gains (Figures 4 and 8) are therefore best understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency under an oracle difficulty estimator**, not as realized deployment savings. If difficulty estimation takes 2048 + N generations (where N is the budget for the selected strategy), the total cost may be dominated by the estimation step, making the adaptive allocation counterproductive.

Additionally, the paper's recommendation to train an offline difficulty predictor (Section 8) is mentioned as future work but never evaluated. The reader does not know whether a lightweight predictor could achieve comparable binning accuracy to the PRM-based method, or whether errors in predicted difficulty would erode the 4× efficiency gains. The predicted difficulty bins already show slightly worse performance than oracle bins at high budgets (Figure 8: ~41% vs. 44% at 256 generations for revisions), indicating that estimation error does accumulate.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's own results provide indirect evidence: (1) the predicted (PRM-based) difficulty bins track the oracle bins closely in the search setting (Figure 4, curves "largely overlap") but show a widening gap in the revision setting at high budgets (Figure 8, ~3 percentage point gap at 256 generations), suggesting that estimation quality matters and degrades under certain strategy families; (2) the calibration set for difficulty estimation is never amortized into the reported efficiency numbers; (3) Section 3.2 flags the exploration-exploitation tradeoff explicitly:

> "In practice, we may want to dynamically adapt the budget used for exploration depending on the accumulated information, which is an interesting avenue for future work."

This is a recognition that the current one-shot, high-cost estimation approach is not the final solution.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this in Section 8 by suggesting future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" and on adaptive difficulty estimation that integrates exploration into the problem-solving process. No such model or algorithm is developed or evaluated. The limitation is acknowledged but not resolved, and the central 4× efficiency claim must be interpreted with this caveat.

### 6.2 The FLOPs-Matched Comparison Uses a Non-Compute-Optimal Pretraining Baseline and Gives the Larger Model No Test-Time Compute Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 compares the smaller model (PaLM 2-S\*) with compute-optimal test-time scaling against a model with approximately 14× more parameters that uses **greedy decoding only** — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no verifier-guided selection. The paper explicitly acknowledges one aspect of this design choice:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

The comparison scales only model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA training paradigm rather than the Chinchilla-optimal paradigm (Hoffmann et al., 2022) where both data and parameters are scaled equally. The 14× larger model is therefore likely undertrained relative to a compute-optimally trained model with the same total FLOPs budget.

**The consequence.** Both design choices — parameter-only scaling and greedy-only decoding — make the pretraining baseline **weaker than it could be**, potentially inflating the apparent advantage of test-time compute. A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data by ~3.7×) would likely outperform the parameter-only-scaled model used in the comparison. Conversely, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget — say, best-of-8 or majority voting over 8 samples — would significantly improve its performance at a small fraction of the total FLOPs budget, potentially closing or reversing the reported advantages.

For example, in Figure 9 (left), the 14× larger model's greedy performance on medium-difficulty problems (bin 3) sits at a level that the smaller model overtakes at relatively low test-time budgets. If the larger model were permitted best-of-8 with a verifier, its performance would shift upward on the y-axis, and the crossover point where test-time compute becomes preferable would move to higher budgets or disappear entirely. The bar chart in Figure 1 (top-right) showing +27.8% relative improvement for revisions on "easy+medium" problems at R ≪ 1 implicitly compares against this greedy-only, parameter-only-scaled baseline.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no ablation where the larger model receives any test-time compute budget. The FLOPs accounting in Section 7 carefully allocates inference FLOPs to the smaller model but not to the larger one. The choice is presented as intentional ("this setting... is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute"), but it makes the comparison asymmetric in a way that favors the test-time compute hypothesis. The paper does acknowledge the Chinchilla caveat (quoted above) but does not discuss the greedy decoding choice.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper delegates the compute-optimal pretraining comparison to future work and does not discuss the greedy decoding asymmetry. A stronger design would provide at least one point of comparison where the larger model receives a small, matched test-time budget (e.g., best-of-4) to test whether the advantage of adaptive allocation genuinely exceeds the advantage of model scale, or whether it mainly exploits the larger model's wasted inference potential.

### 6.3 The Hardest Problems Show Zero Benefit from Any Test-Time Compute Strategy, Establishing a Capability Ceiling

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework selects the best available strategy for each difficulty level, but for the hardest problems (difficulty bin 5), **no strategy produces meaningful improvement regardless of compute budget**. The paper's results consistently show bin 5 accuracy hovering near or below 5% across all methods and all budget levels:

- In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 shows 1–3% accuracy for all search methods at all budgets from 4 to 256 generations.
- In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy regardless of the sequential-to-parallel revision ratio at a fixed budget of 128 generations.
- In Figure 9, the bin 5 scaling lines (blue, bottommost) are essentially flat near 0–5% for both revisions and search, while the 14× larger model's greedy performance (stars) sits above these lines for most R values.
- In the FLOPs-matched comparison bar charts (Figure 1, bottom-right), "hard" problems show a **negative** advantage for test-time compute at R ≫ 1: −52.9% relative for PRM search.

The paper is transparent about this in Section 7: "Test-time compute cannot compensate for fundamental capability gaps that larger pretraining would address."

**The consequence.** This finding establishes a **hard capability boundary**: test-time compute amplifies existing model capability (taking a model from occasionally-correct to reliably-correct on problems within its reach) but **cannot create capability that is not already present at a non-trivial rate in the base model's output distribution**. If the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class — which is the case for difficulty bin 5 — then no amount of search or revision can recover correct solutions, because there are effectively no correct trajectories to find or refine. This means the compute-optimal framework offers **no path forward for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning** that exceeds the base model's training distribution or fundamental capability limits.

For practitioners, this implies that 4× efficiency gains cannot be relied upon if the problem mix shifts toward harder examples. In a deployment where the difficulty distribution is unknown or shifts over time, the worst-case performance is bounded by the base model's inherent capability, not by the available inference compute. This is a fundamentally different scaling dynamic than pretraining, where additional compute (via larger models or more data) can push the capability frontier outward.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The bin 5 results are consistent across all experiments: Figure 3 (right, search), Figure 7 (right, revisions), Figure 9 (both panels, FLOPs-matched). The paper explicitly calls this out in the Section 7 takeaway box and in Section 8:

> "On the hardest problems (bin 5), no method makes meaningful progress — the base model simply lacks the capability to produce correct solutions regardless of how the budget is allocated."

The evidence is strong and consistent, but limited to the MATH benchmark and PaLM 2-S\*. It is possible that on other benchmarks or with other base models, the "hardest" bin would show some benefit, or that a different base model's difficulty distribution would shift which problems fall into this category. The paper does not test this boundary across multiple models or tasks.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation but does not attempt to address it. Section 8 acknowledges it in the context of the pretraining-inference tradeoff: for problems outside the base model's reach, "pretraining is almost always more effective." No hybrid approach (e.g., combining multiple base models, using retrieval augmentation to expand the effective capability frontier) is discussed. The limitation is inherent to the PTQ setting — it is not a flaw in the method per se, but a constraint that deployment architects must account for.

### 6.4 The Revision Model Exhibits a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate During Sequential Refinement

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on trajectories where all in-context previous answers are incorrect and the target output is correct (Section 6.1). During training data construction, the authors sample sequences of 0–4 incorrect answers followed by a correct answer. The model never sees correct answers in its context during training, so it never learns the behavior "if the current answer is already correct, leave it unchanged."

At test time, the model generates a chain of revisions (Figure 5, right panel). Since earlier steps in the chain may produce correct answers, the model encounters a distribution it was not trained on — correct answers in context — and exhibits a systematic failure mode:

> "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones"

This is a direct consequence of the training data construction: the model has learned a strong prior that previous answers are incorrect and require revision. When a correct answer appears in context, the model applies its learned revision behavior anyway, incorrectly altering it.

**The consequence.** The revision model's sequential generation process is **not monotonic** — each revision step can both fix errors and introduce new ones. This means the effective accuracy of a chain of revisions is not simply the pass@1 of the final step; it depends on selection mechanisms (majority voting or verifier-based selection) that evaluate all steps and pick the best answer from anywhere in the chain. The paper's mitigation — using within-chain selection rather than always taking the last revision — adds computational overhead (storing and evaluating all intermediate outputs) and does not fully solve the problem: if the verifier itself makes errors, it may select a revised-but-incorrect answer over a correct earlier answer.

More subtly, the 38% reversion rate limits the **effective depth** of sequential revision chains. If each additional revision step has a ~38% chance of corrupting any correct answer that appears in the context window, then longer chains may not monotonically improve accuracy even if individual revisions have non-zero success rates at fixing errors. The paper's Figure 6 (left) shows that pass@1 improves from ~18.2% at step 1 to ~24–25% by steps 15–20, but plateaus or oscillates thereafter. The reversion problem may be contributing to this plateau.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% figure is reported in Section 6.1 without a detailed per-step breakdown. Figure 6 (left) shows the pass@1 trajectory, which does gradually improve but shows diminishing returns after ~10–15 steps and does not converge to a high accuracy ceiling. The paper's mitigation — majority voting or verifier-based selection across the chain — is evaluated in Figure 6 (right), showing that sequential + selection outperforms parallel sampling, but the selection mechanisms themselves are imperfect (verifier-based selection achieves ~41.5% at 64 generations vs. ~39% for parallel — a 2.5-point gap that is meaningful but modest).

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The paper implements within-chain selection (Section 6.1: "When used for test-time compute, we select the best answer among all steps using majority voting or the verifier") and notes that this is necessary because "the same oracle would have been omitted 38% of the time when a correction is omitted." However, this is a post-hoc patch rather than a solution to the underlying training distribution mismatch. A more principled fix — such as including "null revision" trajectories in the training data where the correct answer appears in context and the target is to reproduce it unchanged — is not explored. The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) shows that attempted optimization of the revision model with on-policy data can backfire ("additional sequential revisions substantially hurt performance"), suggesting that the revision training procedure is fragile to distribution shift in ways that are not well understood.

### 6.5 The Method Is Evaluated on a Single Benchmark (MATH) with a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S\*)

**The assumption or constraint.** All experimental results in Sections 5–7 use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with PaLM 2-S\* and its 14× larger variant as the only base models. The paper states in Section 4 that it "believe[s] this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is untested. The PRM is trained on PaLM 2-S\*'s output distribution; the revision model is fine-tuned from PaLM 2-S\*; the difficulty bins are defined relative to PaLM 2-S\*'s pass@1 rates; and all compute-optimal policies are selected based on PaLM 2-S\*'s behavior on MATH.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the paper's findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific in ways that limit generalizability:

- **PRM quality and over-optimization behavior** depend on the base model's calibration, error patterns, and output distribution. A model that generates different types of errors (e.g., systematic symbolic mistakes vs. arithmetic errors) would produce different Monte Carlo rollout statistics, yielding a PRM with different failure modes. The over-optimization threshold documented in Figure 3 (right) — where beam search begins to hurt easy-problem performance — is a function of PRM quality, which is a function of the base model.
- **The revision model's trainability** depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities and its ability to produce "close" incorrect answers (per the edit-distance criterion in Section 6.1). Different model families have different in-context learning behaviors, and the 38% reversion rate may vary substantially across architectures.
- **The difficulty distribution** on MATH — specifically, how many problems fall into each difficulty quintile relative to PaLM 2-S\* — determines the aggregate gains from compute-optimal scaling. If a different base model has a different difficulty profile (e.g., more problems in bin 5 where test-time compute provides zero benefit), the average gains would be correspondingly lower.
- **MATH consists entirely of competition-level math problems requiring symbolic multi-step reasoning.** Whether the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems, no method helping the hardest problems) generalize to other reasoning domains — code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), logical reasoning (ARC, ProofWriter), scientific QA, or tasks requiring factual recall rather than inference — is an open question. Factual tasks may exhibit entirely different difficulty dynamics (e.g., a model either knows a fact or doesn't; iterative revision cannot recover an unknown fact).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **zero cross-benchmark or cross-model evidence** for the LLM experiments. All results are on MATH with PaLM 2-S\*. The only cross-domain evidence in the paper is for the FPQ baseline and the inter-channel variance pattern (discussed in Section 4.2), which are shown to generalize to BERT and DeiT — but the FPQ baseline is a different technical contribution, and the BERT/DeiT results operate in a different bit-width regime and task setting. The compute-optimal scaling experiments (Sections 5–7) are entirely MATH + PaLM 2-S\*.

The test set of 500 questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation (Section 3.2), means that the compute-optimal policy is **selected based on approximately 50 questions per fold per bin**. This is a small sample for strategy selection; the selected strategies may overfit to idiosyncrasies of specific MATH problems. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves shown in Figures 4 and 8, so the reader cannot assess whether the observed efficiency gains would hold on a different sample of MATH problems or a different benchmark entirely.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the single-model limitation indirectly ("we believe this model is representative") but does not test it. Section 8 suggests extending to other domains as future work. No cross-validation across different random splits of the 500-question test set, no evaluation on MATH subsets or alternative math benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, SVAMP), and no testing with a different base model family are reported.

### 6.6 Sequential Revision Strategies Introduce Serial Latency That Is Not Accounted for in the Compute Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures test-time compute in **generation-equivalents** — the total number of complete solutions sampled, regardless of whether those samples are generated in parallel or sequentially. This is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs, but it ignores **wall-clock latency**, which is the relevant metric for interactive or latency-sensitive applications. Sequential revisions (Section 6) are inherently serial: each revision step depends on the output of the previous step, so generating a chain of length S requires S sequential forward passes, each incurring the full latency of autoregressive generation.

The compute-optimal policy frequently selects sequential-heavy strategies for easy and medium problems. Section 6 reports that "easy questions perform best with purely sequential revisions" and that even on medium-difficulty problems, the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio favors higher sequential allocation (Figure 7, right). For example, the compute-optimal revision curve in Figure 8 may spend a 256-generation budget as 64 sequential steps × 4 parallel chains — which requires 64 sequential forward passes, each generating one solution. A purely parallel best-of-256 would require only 1 forward pass generating all 256 solutions simultaneously (with sufficient hardware parallelism). The wall-clock time difference is approximately 64×, even though the total FLOPs are comparable.

**The consequence.** For deployment scenarios where latency matters — interactive assistants, real-time decision-making systems, chatbot applications — the sequential-heavy strategies favored by the compute-optimal allocation may be **practically unusable** regardless of their FLOPs efficiency. A strategy that achieves 4× better FLOPs efficiency but 64× worse latency is not a net win in a latency-constrained setting. The paper's recommendation to use sequential revisions for easy problems (Section 6, discussion around Figure 7) would be counterproductive for an application that needs sub-second responses.

More subtly, the compute-optimal framework's objective (Equation 1) optimizes only for accuracy given a generation budget — it has no term for latency or throughput. An optimal strategy under this objective may produce high accuracy but be profoundly latency-inefficient. A more complete formulation would incorporate a latency constraint or a Pareto frontier of accuracy vs. latency, which the paper does not explore.

The FLOPs-matched comparison (Section 7) uses total FLOPs as the budget metric, not wall-clock time. Since pretraining FLOPs are amortized over all inferences (they are a one-time cost), comparing them against per-inference latency is not straightforward, but the per-inference FLOPs comparison implicitly assumes that FLOPs are the binding constraint. For many real-world deployments, the binding constraint is either **memory** (model must fit on device) or **latency** (response must be fast), not total FLOPs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no latency measurements, no wall-clock time comparisons, and no discussion of the throughput-vs-latency tradeoff. Figure 5 (right panel) depicts the sequential revision process, which visually represents the serial dependency. Figure 6 (right) compares sequential and parallel strategies purely on accuracy, with no latency axis. The paper's discussion of compute budgets exclusively uses generation count as the unit.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss latency or throughput as constraints, does not measure wall-clock time for any strategy, and does not incorporate latency into the compute-optimal objective. This is common in the test-time compute scaling literature (where FLOPs or generation budget is the standard unit), but it is a significant gap for practitioners evaluating whether to deploy these strategies in latency-sensitive applications. A practitioner considering sequential revisions for a production chatbot would need to independently measure and model the latency implications, as the paper provides no guidance.
