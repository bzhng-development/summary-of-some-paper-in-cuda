# Simple and Scalable Strategies to Continually Pre-train Large Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2403.08763](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08763)

## 🎯 Pitch

Continually pre-training LLMs on new data with just learning rate re-warming, re-decaying, and a small fraction of old data replay matches the performance of full re-training from scratch—even for a 10B model and hundreds of billions of tokens—slashing compute costs without sacrificing final loss or benchmark scores.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how to update large language models on new data without re-training from scratch, evaluating decoder-only transformers up to 10B parameters on the Pile→SlimPajama (weak shift) and Pile→German Common Crawl (stronger shift) pre-training distributions. The work establishes that a simple combination of **learning rate re-warming and re-decaying** (cosine-annealing the LR from the original maximum to minimum on each new dataset, rather than continuing from the converged low LR) with **compute-equivalent replay** (substituting a fraction of new-dataset tokens with replayed tokens from prior data) is sufficient to match—in both final validation loss and average downstream benchmark accuracy—models re-trained from random initialization on the union of all data, while using significantly less compute. For a 405M parameter model on a weak but realistic English→English distribution shift, this recipe achieves comparable average evaluation performance to the full re-training baseline (e.g., 35.14 vs. 34.30 average English benchmark accuracy) with 5% replay, and for a stronger English→German shift, 25% replay recovers the union-trained model's average validation loss (1.75 nats). Scaling to 10B parameters, the same strategies hold—continually pre-trained models with 5% replay reach average evaluation scores within 0.3 points of the union-trained baseline—establishing that continual pre-training with these simple techniques can substitute for full re-training, provided the incoming data distribution is not so drastically different that tokenizer mismatch or fundamental capability gaps dominate.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Re-Training LLMs From Scratch Is Unsustainably Expensive

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is both practical and urgent: **how should organizations update large language models when new data becomes available, without discarding all prior training investment?** 

Currently, the dominant practice in the field is to combine old and new datasets and train from random initialization whenever a model needs updating. This is the approach implicitly assumed by most scaling laws work (Hoffmann et al., 2022), which provides frameworks for maximizing performance under a fixed pretraining compute budget, but does so under the assumption that training starts from scratch. The authors frame this starkly in Section 1:

> "Currently, LLMs are re-trained on a combination of old and newly collected data. ... Doing so for every update of the models quickly becomes expensive."

This is not merely a minor inefficiency. The scale of modern LLM pretraining—hundreds of billions to trillions of tokens, weeks or months of GPU-time, millions of dollars in compute cost—transforms the question of re-training from a theoretical concern into a major economic constraint. Consider a concrete scenario: a company trains a 10B parameter model on 300B tokens of web-scraped data. Six months later, a much higher-quality, better-filtered dataset of comparable size becomes available. Should the company spend the full cost of training a new model from scratch on the combined 600B tokens? Or can they somehow *continue* training from the existing model, achieving comparable results at lower cost?

This problem has real-world urgency because datasets are continuously improving—better deduplication, stricter quality filtering, and the inclusion of newly created content (Gao et al., 2020; Soboleva et al., 2023; Computer, 2023; Soldaini et al., 2024). As the authors note, "organizations will need to update their models to stay abreast of the competition." The question is whether updating requires paying the full pretraining cost every time.

### The Conflicting Requirements: Adaptation vs. Catastrophic Forgetting

The paper identifies two distinct failure modes that make naïve continual pre-training inadequate (Section 1):

**1. Poor adaptation.** When continuing to train a converged model on new data, the model may not effectively learn the new distribution. Most performant open-source LLMs (Touvron et al., 2023a;b; Jiang et al., 2023; Gemma Team et al., 2024) decay their learning rate to a small value by the end of pretraining. If one simply continues training from this low learning rate on new data, the parameter updates will be minimal, and the model will fail to incorporate the new information. The authors hypothesize:

> "the learning rate must be re-increased and re-decayed to improve adaptation per compute spent when training on a new dataset."

They explicitly note that this has "not been thoroughly studied in the continual learning literature"—a gap this paper directly addresses.

**2. Catastrophic forgetting.** Even if one successfully adapts to the new data by increasing the learning rate, the model may rapidly lose capabilities on the original data distribution. This is the classic stability-plasticity dilemma (Mermillod et al., 2013): the model must be plastic enough to learn new information, but stable enough to retain old knowledge. In the context of LLMs, forgetting is particularly damaging because the original pretraining typically covers a broad distribution of knowledge and skills that users expect the model to retain. Adapting to hundreds of billions of new tokens without erasing most existing knowledge is a core challenge.

These two failure modes are in tension: aggressive learning rate schedules promote adaptation but worsen forgetting; conservative schedules preserve old knowledge but learn little from new data. The paper's central practical contribution is identifying that the combination of LR re-warming/re-decaying (for adaptation) and replay (for mitigating forgetting) resolves this tension effectively at scale.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several categories of prior work and explains why they are insufficient for the specific problem of large-scale continual LLM pretraining:

**Domain-adaptive continual pre-training (DACPT) operates at much smaller scale.** Gururangan et al. (2020) and Ke et al. (2022) study continual pre-training, but in a masked language modeling (MLM) context with domain-specific datasets, rather than the general-purpose autoregressive LM setting with general pre-training datasets. More critically, existing DACPT work (Gururangan et al., 2020; Ke et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023) typically deals with datasets of a few billion tokens or less. The authors emphasize this distinction in Section 3.3:

> "We refer to our setting as 'continual pre-training' and highlight that it is distinct from existing settings in the literature due to the large amount of incoming data we consider."

When the incoming dataset is 200B+ tokens, completely different dynamics apply: adaptation is not about fine-tuning on a narrow domain, but about genuinely shifting the model's distribution to incorporate a massive new corpus that may be comparable in scale to the original pretraining data.

**Task-specific continual learning studies do not address general-purpose pre-training.** Many works apply continual pre-training to specific domains or tasks (Sun et al., 2020; Jang et al., 2022a;b; Gong et al., 2022; Zan et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023a; Ma et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024). These studies focus on adapting models for particular use cases, with datasets typically < 10B tokens and models that are small by modern standards. They do not provide evidence that continual pre-training strategies work in the regime of general-purpose LLMs with hundreds of billions of tokens.

The only prior work approaching this paper's dataset scale is Gogoulou et al. (2023), who explore continual autoregressive language modeling across multiple languages at ~73B tokens per dataset. However, they do not use replay, which this paper identifies as a critical component. The only work approaching this paper's model scale is Yang et al. (2024), which continually pre-trains and instruction-tunes LLaMA2 on small-scale plant science data. While they independently use similar techniques (LR re-warming, replay), they do not build the "controlled experimental framework to systematically evaluate the validity of these approaches" that this paper provides.

**LLaMA Pro and related methods change the architecture, not just the training recipe.** Wu et al. (2024) propose LLaMA Pro, which grows the model size for each new update and only adjusts the new weights while keeping old ones frozen. This is fundamentally different from the approach in this paper, which adapts all weights and aims to be a "simple and scalable" training strategy compatible with any existing model architecture without modification. Growing the model for each update is a heavier intervention that may not be practical when updates are frequent, and it does not address whether standard continual learning techniques can work at scale with fixed-capacity models.

**The effect of pre-training scale on forgetting has been documented, but not connected to practical continual pre-training recipes.** Cossu et al. (2022) find that unsupervised pre-training mitigates forgetting. Mehta et al. (2023) find that pre-trained models forget less due to flatter loss landscapes. Ramasesh et al. (2022) and Mirzadeh et al. (2022) find that larger models forget less—the former attributing this to hidden representations growing more orthogonal with scale, the latter finding that wider networks are more robust than deeper ones. Hernandez et al. (2021) establish scaling laws for transfer showing that positive transfer improves with parameter count. Scialom et al. (2022) show that autoregressive LLMs have strong continual learning abilities, hypothesizing that this is related to their pre-training objective.

These findings collectively suggest that larger, pre-trained LLMs should be *well-suited* for continual learning—they start from a favorable position with flatter minima and more orthogonal representations. However, none of these works put this hypothesis to the test in the regime of updating large LLMs on comparably large new datasets. This paper explicitly builds on this foundation:

> "We, therefore, expect the trend of increasing language model capacity and pre-training dataset size in tandem ... will yield models increasingly capable of continual learning, suggesting that our experimental results should only improve with models scale."

**Optimizer state retention is underexplored but turns out to be unimportant.** When continuing to pre-train from an existing checkpoint, one can either keep or discard the optimizer states (momentum estimates). If optimizer states are available, they might help smooth the transition. However, the authors note that open-weight models typically do not provide optimizer states, making it important to verify that continual pre-training works without them. Appendix A.4 shows that keeping versus discarding optimizer states makes negligible difference after training for a moderate number of tokens, because the momentum coefficients (β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.95) cause the contribution of old states to decay to near zero within ~1000 steps. This justifies the paper's experimental design of resetting optimizer states between datasets, which also makes the results directly applicable to anyone starting from a public model checkpoint.

**Learning rate schedules for continual learning are poorly understood at scale.** While Hoffmann et al. (2022) established the importance of LR schedules for standard pretraining (correcting Kaplan et al.'s earlier erroneous finding that schedule choice didn't matter), the behavior of schedules when *continuing* training from a converged checkpoint has not been systematically studied. The paper identifies a specific pathology (Section 7.1): re-warming the LR, even when continuing on the same data distribution, causes a transient increase in validation loss—a phenomenon the authors term optimization-related forgetting. This suggests that the cosine schedule's requirement to re-warm when starting from a low LR is inherently problematic for continual learning, motivating the exploration of infinite learning rate schedules that maintain a constant learning rate across dataset transitions.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as a **large-scale empirical study** rather than a new algorithm or architecture proposal. The authors state their goal clearly:

> "given the great potential for continual learning to considerably reduce costs associated with re-training models and the potential for LLMs to be strong continual learners, we ask ourselves the following question: when simple and scalable continual learning techniques are applied, what is the performance difference between continually pre-trained LLMs relative to LLMs pre-trained from random initialization on the union of all data?"

This is a carefully framed question. The paper is not claiming to outperform re-training—it is asking whether it can *match* re-training at lower cost, establishing re-training on all data as the "upper bound." This is a rigorous benchmark because it sets a high bar: the continual pre-training method must achieve results comparable to a model that has the advantage of seeing all data interleaved from the start, which is the best possible scenario for learning.

The paper's framing in Section 2 is significant: it does not aim to improve on the state-of-the-art in general LLM performance, but rather to answer whether existing models can be efficiently updated without losing their existing capabilities. This is a practical engineering question with enormous economic implications, yet it has received surprisingly little systematic attention at scale.

The paper also draws an important distinction between **distribution shifts** of different magnitudes. The weak shift (Pile→SlimPajama) represents a realistic update scenario where the new data comes from similar sources but with better filtering and newer content. The stronger shift (English→German) represents a more dramatic change, such as adding a new language to a model. The deliberate inclusion of both shifts allows the paper to demonstrate that the same simple strategies work across a spectrum of difficulty, while also revealing that stronger shifts require more replay to mitigate forgetting—providing practitioners with concrete guidance on how to calibrate the replay percentage to their specific setting.

Finally, the paper explicitly acknowledges its limitations in scope (Section 8): only two model sizes (405M and 10B), primarily two-dataset sequences, single-seed experiments, and initial experiments with infinite schedules limited to the no-shift case. This self-awareness is important because it frames the work as establishing a baseline—showing what is possible with simple techniques—rather than claiming to have solved all aspects of continual LLM pre-training. The authors write that they "do not seek to obtain state-of-the-art performance or compare with models out of the scope of this paper" (Section 5), which accurately conveys the deliberately controlled, internally-consistent experimental design.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is an **empirical study**, not a new algorithm or architecture proposal. The authors build a systematic experimental framework that takes an existing pre-trained LLM (trained on dataset `$D_0$` with a standard cosine learning rate schedule) and continues training it on a new large dataset `$D_1$` (200B+ tokens), asking: *can simple, off-the-shelf techniques—specifically, learning rate re-warming/re-decaying and replay of old data—make this continued training match the performance of a model re-trained from scratch on `$D_0 \cup D_1$`, while using less compute?*

The core problem is the **adaptation-forgetting tradeoff**: how to efficiently learn the new distribution without erasing knowledge from the original pretraining. The shape of the solution is a two-component recipe: (1) a learning rate schedule that re-increases and re-decreases the LR to promote genuine adaptation to the new data, and (2) a replay mechanism that interleaves a fraction of old-dataset tokens into the new-data stream to anchor the model's existing capabilities, with the replay percentage calibrated to the strength of the distribution shift.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three major components that operate sequentially through time:

1. **Initial Pre-training Engine** — A standard decoder-only transformer (GPT-NeoX architecture, Pre-LN) trained from random initialization on dataset `$D_0$` (e.g., 300B tokens of the Pile) using the conventional linear warmup + cosine decay learning rate schedule that decays to a small `$\eta_{\text{min}}$`. This produces a converged checkpoint, which serves as the starting point for all continual pre-training experiments. The optimizer states (AdamW moment estimates) are discarded at this boundary to simulate the realistic scenario of using an open-weight model where optimizer states are not available.

2. **Continual Pre-training Engine** — Takes the checkpoint from Step 1 and continues training on dataset `$D_1$` (e.g., 300B tokens of SlimPajama or 200B tokens of German CommonCrawl). This engine implements two key mechanisms:
   - **Learning Rate Scheduler**: Re-warms the learning rate from `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` to a target maximum `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` (typically the same value used during pre-training on `$D_0$`), then re-decays it via cosine annealing back to `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` by the end of `$D_1$` training. This scheduler operates on a per-dataset basis: each new dataset gets its own full warmup-decay cycle fitted to its token budget.
   - **Replay Buffer**: Maintains a buffer of tokens from `$D_0$` (and potentially earlier datasets, in multi-dataset settings). During training on `$D_1$`, each batch is composed of `$(100 - x)\%$` tokens from `$D_1$` and `$x\%$` tokens from the replay buffer, where `$x$` is the replay percentage (e.g., 5% for weak shifts, 25% for stronger shifts). The replay tokens are sampled in the order they were originally seen during pre-training on `$D_0$`, and the number of unique `$D_1$` tokens is reduced correspondingly to maintain constant total compute ("compute-equivalent replay").

3. **Evaluation Suite** — Separately measures performance on both `$D_0$` (to quantify forgetting) and `$D_1$` (to quantify adaptation) using validation loss, plus a battery of downstream benchmarks (HellaSwag, ARC, MMLU, etc. for English; translated variants for German). The key metric is the **average final validation loss** across `$D_0$` and `$D_1$`, compared against baselines including a model trained from random initialization on `$D_0 \cup D_1$` (the "upper bound") and models trained exclusively on individual datasets.

Information flows as follows: pre-trained checkpoint → continual pre-training engine (which applies the LR schedule and samples from the replay buffer + `$D_1$` stream) → periodically evaluated checkpoints → comparison against re-training baselines using validation loss and downstream benchmarks. In multi-dataset settings (the N > 2 case), the continual pre-training engine iterates Steps 2 with a new dataset and an updated replay buffer at each transition, using reservoir sampling to maintain proportional representation of all prior datasets.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the linear warmup + cosine decay schedule — the standard pretraining LR schedule that creates the need for re-warming in the first place, since it decays to a small `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` that is insufficient for adaptation on new data.
- **Second**, the learning rate re-warming and re-decaying mechanism — how the LR is re-increased and re-decreased per dataset, what `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` values are tested, and why this is necessary for adaptation.
- **Third**, compute-equivalent replay — the formal definition, how the replay buffer is populated, how batches are composed, and how it keeps total compute constant across comparisons.
- **Fourth**, the infinite learning rate schedules — an alternative to repeated cosine cycles that avoids the forgetting caused by re-warming, with four distinct phases (warmup, cooldown, constant, annealing) and two decay function variants (cosine and inverse square root).
- **Fifth**, the dataset construction pipeline — how SlimPajama, Pile, and German CommonCrawl are processed, sized, and split, including domain sampling percentages and multi-dataset settings.
- **Sixth**, the training infrastructure — model architectures, parallelism strategies, optimizer configuration, and the rationale for resetting optimizer states between datasets.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is an **empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that the dominant failure modes of naïve continual pre-training—poor adaptation and catastrophic forgetting— can be jointly addressed by a simple combination of learning rate schedule design and replay, without architectural changes, specialized loss functions, or complex optimization procedures.

---

#### Linear Warmup + Cosine Decay Schedule: The Starting Point

The paper assumes that the base model has been pre-trained on `$D_0$` using the now-standard recipe established by Hoffmann et al. (2022) and Rae et al. (2021): a linear warmup followed by a cosine decay, fitted to a predetermined token budget. Understanding this schedule in detail is essential because its structure—specifically, the fact that it ends at a low learning rate—creates both the motivation for re-warming and the forgetting pathology that infinite schedules aim to circumvent.

The schedule operates in two phases over a total of `$T_{\text{end}}$` timesteps:

**Phase 1 — Linear warmup** (over `$T_{\text{warmup}}$` timesteps, from `$t = 0$` to `$t_{\text{warmup}} = T_{\text{warmup}}$`):

$$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{max}} \cdot \frac{t}{T_{\text{warmup}}}$$

where `$\eta_t$` is the learning rate at iteration `$t$`, `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` is the target maximum learning rate, and `$T_{\text{warmup}}$` is the number of warmup iterations (typically 1% of total training iterations, though the paper also tests 0%, 0.5%, and 2% in Section 6.1.1).

**What it computes:** the learning rate starts at 0 and increases linearly to `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` over the warmup period. This means at iteration `$t = T_{\text{warmup}}/2$`, the LR is exactly `$\eta_{\text{max}}/2$`; at `$t = T_{\text{warmup}}$`, it reaches `$\eta_{\text{max}}$`.

**Why this form:** linear warmup prevents training instability in the early iterations when using large batch sizes and Adam-style optimizers, particularly for Post-LN transformers (Popel and Bojar, 2018). The gradual increase avoids the optimizer taking catastrophically large steps before moment estimates have stabilized. The paper notes that Pre-LN transformers (which the authors use) are more stable and "may not require a warm-up stage" (citing Xiong et al., 2020), but the authors retain warmup because it is the prevailing convention in the literature and they want their results to apply to the typical open-source model.

**Phase 2 — Cosine annealing** (over `$T_{\text{ann}}$` timesteps, from `$t_{\text{warmup}}$` to `$t_{\text{end}} = t_{\text{warmup}} + T_{\text{ann}}$`):

$$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{min}} + \frac{(\eta_{\text{max}} - \eta_{\text{min}})}{2} \cdot \left(\cos\left(\pi \cdot \frac{t - t_{\text{warmup}}}{t_{\text{end}} - t_{\text{warmup}}}\right) + 1\right)$$

where `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` is the minimum learning rate reached at the end of training (set to `$0.1 \cdot \eta_{\text{max}}$` throughout the paper, unless otherwise specified), and `$t_{\text{end}}$` is the total number of training iterations.

**What it computes:** the learning rate follows a half-cosine curve from `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` down to `$\eta_{\text{min}}$`. At the midpoint of the annealing phase (`$t = t_{\text{warmup}} + T_{\text{ann}}/2$`), the `$\cos(\pi/2) = 0$` term gives `$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{min}} + (\eta_{\text{max}} - \eta_{\text{min}})/2$`, exactly halfway between the extremes. At the end, `$\cos(\pi) = -1$` gives `$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{min}} + 0 = \eta_{\text{min}}$`.

**Why this form:** cosine annealing provides a smooth, monotonic decrease that is faster in the middle of training and slower at the beginning and end. This shape is empirically effective for reaching good minima in LLM pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022). The smoothness matters because abrupt LR changes can destabilize optimization. The decay to a small `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` (rather than staying high) is critical for convergence—it allows the model to settle into a precise minimum. However, this very property means that if you *continue* training from this converged checkpoint on new data, the small `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` produces negligible parameter updates, preventing adaptation. This is the central tension that motivates the entire paper.

The paper's choice to set `$\eta_{\text{min}} = 0.1 \cdot \eta_{\text{max}}$` (rather than decaying to near-zero) follows common practice. For the 405M model, `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$` and `$\eta_{\text{min}} = 3 \times 10^{-5}$`; for the 10B model, `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}$` and `$\eta_{\text{min}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}$`. The warmup duration is 1% of total training iterations in all experiments unless otherwise noted (Section 5.3, Table 14).

#### Learning Rate Re-Warming and Re-Decaying

When transitioning from `$D_0$` to `$D_1$`, the model's learning rate is at `$\eta_{\text{min}}$`, which is too small to drive meaningful adaptation to the new distribution. The paper's core insight regarding the LR schedule is that **the learning rate must be re-increased and re-decreased for each new dataset**, and that failing to do so leads to severely suboptimal adaptation.

**The re-warming procedure.** At the start of training on `$D_1$`, the learning rate is re-warmed from `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` to `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` (the same maximum used during `$D_0$` pretraining, unless otherwise ablated) using the same linear warmup schedule, then cosine-annealed back to `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` over the token budget of `$D_1$`. Crucially, the cosine annealing phase is **fitted to the token budget of `$D_1$`**, not the combined budget of `$D_0$` + `$D_1$`. This means each dataset gets its own complete learning rate cycle.

For example, in the weak shift setting: the model is pre-trained on 300B tokens of Pile with `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$`, `$\eta_{\text{min}} = 3 \times 10^{-5}$`. When transitioning to 300B tokens of SlimPajama, the LR is re-warmed to `$3 \times 10^{-4}$` over 1% of SlimPajama's iterations (~1,324 steps for the 405M model), then cosine-decayed back to `$3 \times 10^{-5}$` over the remaining 99% of iterations. The total unique tokens seen from SlimPajama is 300B (assuming no replay), and the compute cost is simply the total number of tokens processed, which matches the Pile pre-training phase.

**Ablation: re-warming to different `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` values.** The paper tests `$\eta_{\text{max}} \in \{1.5 \times 10^{-4}, 3 \times 10^{-4}, 6 \times 10^{-4}\}$` for re-warming (Section 6.1.2, Figure 4) and compares against two constant-LR baselines:

1. **Constant `$\eta_{\text{min}}$`**: Continue training at `$3 \times 10^{-5}$` with no re-warming or re-decaying.
2. **Constant `$\eta_{\text{max}}$`**: Re-warm to `$3 \times 10^{-4}$` but do NOT re-decay—stay at the maximum for the entire `$D_1$` training period.

The results (Figure 4) establish a clear empirical ordering:

- **Constant `$\eta_{\text{min}}$`** produces the least forgetting on `$D_0$` (lowest Pile validation loss) but the worst adaptation to `$D_1$` (highest SlimPajama/German validation loss, particularly for the strong shift).
- **Constant `$\eta_{\text{max}}$`** produces the best adaptation to `$D_1$` but causes severe forgetting on `$D_0$`—the Pile loss spikes and does not recover.
- **Re-warming + re-decaying at `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$`** represents a middle ground: it adapts nearly as well as the constant `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` baseline (the validation loss curves for `$D_1$` are almost identical) while forgetting significantly less on `$D_0$` due to the eventual decay to `$\eta_{\text{min}}$`, which stabilizes the model at the end.
- **Varying `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` in the re-warm/re-decay regime** shifts the adaptation-forgetting tradeoff: higher `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` yields better adaptation but worse forgetting; lower `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` does the opposite.

**Design choice: why re-decaying matters.** Without re-decaying (the constant `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` baseline), the model is continuously being forced to take large steps in parameter space, which effectively erases the fine-grained structure learned during `$D_0$` pre-training. Re-decaying is necessary because it allows the model to first explore the new loss landscape aggressively (during the high-LR phase) and then settle into a stable configuration (during the decay phase) that balances both datasets. The paper does not explicitly test alternative decay shapes (e.g., linear, step-wise) beyond the cosine; this is left implicit as following the standard pre-training convention.

**Ablation: warmup duration.** The paper tests linear warmup durations of 0%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2% of `$D_1$`'s total training iterations (Section 6.1.1, Figure 3). The key finding is that warmup duration has **negligible impact** on final performance after 50B tokens of training. Shorter warmups cause faster initial forgetting and adaptation (the model reaches a higher LR sooner), but these differences diminish by 50B tokens. The 0% warmup model (which starts immediately at `$\eta_{\text{max}}$`) experiences a transient loss spike due to the combination of a large LR, noisy gradients from the new distribution, and reset optimizer states. Since warmup duration doesn't affect final convergence but does affect initial stability, the paper standardizes to 1% for all subsequent experiments—it is a conservative choice that avoids the initial spike without any downside.

**The re-warming pathology (Section 7.1).** A critical finding: re-warming the LR causes a transient forgetting spike *even when continuing on the same data distribution*. Figure 8 shows that when a model pre-trained on 300B Pile with `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$` is re-warmed to the same `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` and continued on another 300B of Pile (no distribution shift), the Pile validation loss increases by approximately 0.1 nats before eventually recovering. With `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 6 \times 10^{-4}$`, this spike reaches 0.2 nats. This demonstrates that re-warming causes **optimization-related forgetting** that is distinct from distribution-shift-related forgetting: the large learning rate disrupts the carefully converged parameter configuration, even when the data distribution is identical. This motivates the search for schedules that avoid re-warming altogether (the infinite LR schedules in Section 7.2).

**Interaction with dataset size.** The cosine decay for `$D_1$` is fitted to `$D_1$`'s token budget, which means the decay rate depends on the dataset size. A smaller `$D_1$` (say, 100B tokens) would decay from `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` to `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` over fewer steps, meaning the model spends proportionally less time at intermediate learning rates. The paper does not systematically ablate this interaction, but it is an implicit aspect of the experimental design: all schedules are fitted to their respective dataset budgets, following the standard pre-training practice.

#### Compute-Equivalent Replay

Replay is the second half of the paper's continual pre-training recipe. The core idea is straightforward: when training on `$D_1$`, include a fraction of tokens from `$D_0$` in each batch. This prevents the model from drifting too far from its original distribution and thus mitigates catastrophic forgetting.

**Formal definition of compute-equivalent replay (Section 4.2).** To make fair comparisons between models trained with and without replay, the paper keeps the **total number of tokens processed** constant. This ensures that any performance differences are due to the replay strategy itself, not the amount of computation expended. The mechanism works as follows:

Suppose `$D_0$` has `$s_0$` tokens and `$D_1$` has `$s_1$` tokens (e.g., 300B each for SlimPajama). A model trained without replay on `$D_0 \rightarrow D_1$` sees all `$s_0$` tokens of `$D_0$` followed by all `$s_1$` tokens of `$D_1$`, for a total of `$S_{\text{total}} = s_0 + s_1$` unique tokens processed. A model trained with `$x\%$` compute-equivalent replay sees:
- All `$s_0$` tokens of `$D_0$` (first phase, same as before).
- Only `$s_1 \cdot (1 - x/100)$` unique tokens of `$D_1$`, plus `$s_1 \cdot (x/100)$` replayed tokens from `$D_0$`, for a total of `$s_1$` tokens processed during the `$D_1$` phase.

Thus, both models process exactly `$s_0 + s_1$` total tokens. The replay model sees fewer unique tokens of `$D_1$` because some of its `$D_1$`-phase training slots are occupied by `$D_0$` replays. This is the "compute-equivalent" constraint: you are trading novelty for stability.

> **Concrete example:** With 5% replay on 300B SlimPajama, each batch during the `$D_1$` phase contains 5% Pile tokens and 95% SlimPajama tokens. Over the 300B tokens of the `$D_1$` phase, the model sees `$0.05 \times 300\text{B} = 15\text{B}$` replayed Pile tokens and `$0.95 \times 300\text{B} = 285\text{B}$` unique SlimPajama tokens. The total tokens processed in the `$D_1$` phase is still 300B.

**Replay buffer construction and sampling.** The replay buffer contains `$D_0$` tokens in the exact order they were seen during pre-training on `$D_0$`. The authors state they "did not observe noticeable differences when reshuffling the replay data in preliminary experiments" (Section 4.2), so they default to preserving the original order for simplicity. At training time, each batch is constructed by sampling:
- A fraction `$(1 - x/100)$` of its tokens from the `$D_1$` data stream (in sequential order).
- A fraction `$x/100$` of its tokens from the replay buffer (cycling through the buffer as needed).

The replay percentage `$x$` is the only hyperparameter controlling this mechanism, and the paper sweeps it extensively (Section 6.2). For the weak shift (Pile→SlimPajama), replays of 0.5%, 1%, 5%, 10%, and 50% are tested. For the strong shift (Pile→German), replays of 1%, 5%, 10%, 25%, and 50% are tested.

**Key empirical findings on replay (Figure 5, Table 2):**
- Even 1% replay significantly reduces forgetting on `$D_0$` compared to the no-replay baseline. For the weak shift, moving from 0% to 1% replay drops the final Pile validation loss from 2.44 to 2.26—a reduction of 0.18 nats. For the strong shift, the reduction is even larger: from 3.56 to 2.83 (a 0.73 nat improvement).
- Adaptation to `$D_1$` (as measured by `$D_1$` validation loss) is minimally affected by low-to-moderate replay percentages. For the weak shift, SlimPajama validation loss with 5% replay is 2.51 vs. 2.50 without replay. For the strong shift, German validation loss with 25% replay is 1.16 vs. 1.11 without replay. The degradation from losing unique `$D_1$` tokens is small.
- Extreme replay (50%) substantially reduces adaptation quality: in the strong shift, 50% replay increases German validation loss from 1.11 to 1.22—a notable degradation because the model has seen 100B fewer unique German tokens (since 50% of the 200B `$D_1$` phase, i.e., 100B tokens, are Pile replays instead).
- Curiously, the 50% replay models at both shifts **attain or surpass** the average final validation loss of the union-trained baselines (Table 2: 2.35 vs. 2.35 for the weak shift; 1.73 vs. 1.75 for the strong shift). This is despite seeing substantially fewer unique `$D_1$` tokens (150B less for SlimPajama, 100B less for German). The authors note this as an interesting observation but do not deeply analyze the mechanism—it may relate to the model being more effectively regularized by the extreme replay, or to diminishing returns on additional `$D_1$` tokens beyond a certain threshold.

**Why replay works.** Replay provides a direct gradient signal that anchors the model to its original loss basin. Without replay, the combination of a new data distribution and a high learning rate pushes the model into a new region of parameter space that may be far from the original minimum. With replay, even a small fraction of old-data gradients exerts a restoring force that prevents large drift. The finding that small replay percentages (1–5%) are already highly effective is practically important: it means one can preserve the vast majority of the `$D_1$` budget for new data while still substantially mitigating forgetting.

**Adaptation of replay to multi-dataset settings (Appendix A.1).** For settings with more than two datasets (`$D_0, D_1, \ldots, D_{N-1}$`), the replay buffer must contain proportional samples from *all* prior datasets, not just `$D_0$`. The paper uses **discrete reservoir sampling** (Buzzega et al., 2020), where the buffer is updated at each dataset transition. At the start of training on `$D_i$`, the replay buffer `$R_i$` contains data from all `$D_j$` for `$j < i$`, with proportions designed to maintain representativeness given that each new dataset `$D_{i-1}$` contributes only `$s_{i-1} \cdot (1 - \alpha)$` unique tokens (since a fraction `$\alpha$` of the `$D_{i-1}$` phase was replay). The full formula is given in Equation 5 of the appendix, but the operational description is: the buffer at step `$i$` is a weighted mixture of earlier datasets, where more recent datasets have slightly higher representation (since they were added to the buffer after fewer replay-induced subsampling steps).

#### Infinite Learning Rate Schedules

The paper identifies a fundamental limitation of the cosine re-warming/re-decaying approach: **re-warming causes forgetting even in the absence of a distribution shift** (Section 7.1, Figure 8). This means that even if one had perfect replay and could fully prevent distribution-shift-related forgetting, the act of re-increasing the LR would still cause a transient (or permanent, if the model doesn't fully recover) loss of performance. This motivates the search for schedules that avoid re-warming altogether.

The proposed solution is **infinite learning rate schedules**, inspired by Zhai et al. (2022) for vision transformers. These schedules have four distinct phases:

$$\eta_t = \begin{cases}
\eta_{\text{max}} \cdot \frac{t}{T_{\text{warmup}}} & t \in [0, t_{\text{cd}}] \quad \text{(Phase 1: Warm-up)} \\
f_{\text{cd}}(t) & t \in (t_{\text{cd}}, t_{\text{const}}] \quad \text{(Phase 2: Cooldown)} \\
\eta_{\text{const}} & t \in (t_{\text{const}}, t_{\text{ann}}] \quad \text{(Phase 3: Constant)} \\
\eta_{\text{const}} \cdot \left(\frac{\eta_{\text{min}}}{\eta_{\text{const}}}\right)^{\frac{t - t_{\text{ann}}}{t_{\text{end}} - t_{\text{ann}}}} & t \in (t_{\text{ann}}, t_{\text{end}}] \quad \text{(Phase 4: Annealing)}
\end{cases}$$

where the timing variables are: `$t_{\text{cd}} = T_{\text{warmup}}$` (end of warmup), `$t_{\text{const}} = t_{\text{cd}} + T_{\text{cd}}$` (end of cooldown, start of constant), `$t_{\text{ann}} = t_{\text{const}} + T_{\text{const}}$` (end of constant, start of annealing), and `$t_{\text{end}} = t_{\text{ann}} + T_{\text{ann}}$` (end of training).

Let us walk through each phase in operational terms:

**Phase 1 — Linear warmup (occurs only once, during the first dataset `$D_0$`):** Identical to the standard schedule's warmup. The LR increases linearly from 0 to `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` over `$T_{\text{warmup}}$` timesteps, which is 1% of the total iterations allocated for `$D_0$`. This phase exists purely for initialization stability and does not repeat for subsequent datasets.

**Phase 2 — Cooldown (occurs only once, during `$D_0$`):** The LR is gradually decayed from `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` down to a constant value `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` over `$T_{\text{cd}}$` timesteps, which the paper sets to 60% of total iterations. Crucially, `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` is substantially higher than `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` (for the 405M model: `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$`, `$\eta_{\text{const}} = 1.65 \times 10^{-4}$`, `$\eta_{\text{min}} = 3 \times 10^{-5}$`). This means the model settles into a regime of moderate, constant learning—not the near-zero LR of a converged cosine schedule. The paper tests two functional forms for the cooldown decay `$f_{\text{cd}}(t)$`:

**Variant A — Cosine cooldown:**

$$f_{\text{cd}}(t) = \eta_{\text{const}} + \frac{\eta_{\text{max}} - \eta_{\text{const}}}{2} \cdot \left(1 + \cos\left(\pi \cdot \frac{t - t_{\text{cd}}}{t_{\text{const}} - t_{\text{cd}}}\right)\right)$$

This is a half-cosine that smoothly transitions `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` to `$\eta_{\text{const}}$`. At `$t = t_{\text{cd}}$`, `$\cos(0) = 1$` gives `$f_{\text{cd}} = \eta_{\text{max}}$`. At the midpoint, `$\cos(\pi/2) = 0$` gives `$f_{\text{cd}} = \eta_{\text{const}} + (\eta_{\text{max}} - \eta_{\text{const}})/2$`. At `$t = t_{\text{const}}$`, `$\cos(\pi) = -1$` gives `$f_{\text{cd}} = \eta_{\text{const}}$`. The smoothness property of the cosine ensures no abrupt LR changes that could destabilize training.

**Variant B — Inverse square root cooldown:**

$$f_{\text{cd}}(t) = \eta_{\text{max}} + \frac{\eta_{\text{const}} - \eta_{\text{max}}}{h(1)} \cdot h\left(\frac{t - t_{\text{cd}}}{t_{\text{const}} - t_{\text{cd}}}\right)$$

where the helper function `$h(x)$` is:

$$h(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \alpha x}} - 1$$

with `$\alpha = 10$` controlling the steepness of the decay (larger `$\alpha$` means faster initial decay, then slower tail). The `$h(x)$` function maps `$x \in [0, 1]$` to a range from `$h(0) = 0$` to `$h(1) = 1/\sqrt{1 + \alpha} - 1$`, and the division by `$h(1)$` normalizes the scale so that `$f_{\text{cd}}(t_{\text{const}}) = \eta_{\text{const}}$`. This variant is inspired by the inverse square root schedules used in Raffel et al. (2023) and Zhai et al. (2022), which have shown good properties for indefinite training.

**Phase 3 — Constant (occurs on all datasets, including `$D_0$` and subsequent):** The LR stays at `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` for `$T_{\text{const}}$` timesteps, which the paper sets to 25% of total iterations. This is the critical phase for continual pre-training: **when transitioning to a new dataset, one loads a checkpoint from within this constant phase, not from after annealing.** Since the LR at this checkpoint is `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` (a moderately high value), continuing training on `$D_1$` requires no re-warming—the model simply keeps training at `$\eta_{\text{const}}$`. This is the key advantage: it avoids the forgetting spike caused by re-warming.

**Phase 4 — Annealing (occurs at the end of each dataset, when deploying or evaluating):** The LR is exponentially annealed from `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` down to `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` over `$T_{\text{ann}}$` timesteps (set to 15% of total iterations, implicitly, since 60% + 25% + 15% = 100%). The formula is:

$$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{const}} \cdot \left(\frac{\eta_{\text{min}}}{\eta_{\text{const}}}\right)^{\frac{t - t_{\text{ann}}}{t_{\text{end}} - t_{\text{ann}}}}$$

This is an exponential interpolation: at `$t = t_{\text{ann}}$`, the exponent is 0, giving `$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{const}}$`. At `$t = t_{\text{end}}$`, the exponent is 1, giving `$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{const}} \cdot (\eta_{\text{min}} / \eta_{\text{const}}) = \eta_{\text{min}}$`. At the midpoint, the exponent is 0.5, giving `$\eta_t = \eta_{\text{const}} \cdot \sqrt{\eta_{\text{min}} / \eta_{\text{const}}} = \sqrt{\eta_{\text{const}} \cdot \eta_{\text{min}}}$` (the geometric mean). The exponential form means the LR drops rapidly at first, then more slowly as it approaches `$\eta_{\text{min}}$`.

**What this schedule achieves operationally for continual pre-training:**
- Train on `$D_0$`: warmup → cooldown → constant phase → **save checkpoint at the end of the constant phase** (LR = `$\eta_{\text{const}}$`) → annealing (for evaluation/benchmarking of the `$D_0$` model).
- Train on `$D_1$`: load the constant-phase checkpoint → continue training at `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` (no re-warming needed!) → annealing at the end.
- Train on `$D_2$`, `$D_3$`, ...: same as `$D_1$`—always resume from the constant-phase checkpoint of the previous dataset.

The constant phase can be extended indefinitely because the LR does not decay. If more data arrives on the current dataset, one simply extends the constant phase; if a new dataset arrives, one transitions smoothly at the same `$\eta_{\text{const}}$`.

**Comparison to repeated cosine cycles (Section 7.4, Figure 10).** The paper tests the infinite schedules against the `$N > 2$` setting with three IID 100B-token splits of SlimPajama. The key findings:
- All three schedule variants (Cosine, Cosine Inf, InvSqrt Inf) achieve similar final validation loss (within ~0.01 nats of each other; Table 12), showing that infinite schedules do not sacrifice final performance.
- The infinite schedules exhibit **negligible forgetting across dataset boundaries**, while the repeated cosine schedules show a brief loss spike at each transition due to the reset optimiser states (though this spike is smaller than in the strong-shift cases because there is no distribution shift). The infinite schedules immediately recover from the reset because the LR remains at `$\eta_{\text{const}}$`.
- The annealing phase effectively "converges" the model: the validation loss drops during annealing, matching what a cosine decay would achieve. This is visible in Figure 10(a) as the downward ticks at the end of each 100B-token segment for the Cosine Inf and InvSqrt Inf models.

**Limitations of infinite schedules (Section 8).** The paper acknowledges that these schedules are only tested at 405M scale with no distribution shift. In the presence of distribution shifts, it is unclear whether the constant phase at `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` would be optimal—the empirical results from Section 6.1.2 showed that constant high LRs (without re-decaying) cause more forgetting on `$D_0$` than re-warming + re-decaying, and constant low LRs adapt poorly. The choice of `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` may need to be tuned per distribution shift to balance adaptation and forgetting, which reintroduces a hyperparameter search. Additionally, training stability over very long constant-LR phases (trillions of tokens) with AdamW has not been tested and may require `$\mu$`P-style scaling to avoid instability.

#### Dataset Construction and Pre-processing

The paper uses three datasets and constructs them carefully to ensure fair comparisons.

**The Pile (`$D_0$` for most experiments).** The Pile (Gao et al., 2020) is used as-is: the pre-shuffled, pre-mixed training set of ~330B tokens, of which the authors use a 300B-token subset. The default validation set is used for evaluation. The Pile contains 22 diverse domains (academic papers, books, code, web text, etc.) and is a standard benchmark for LLM pre-training research.

**SlimPajama (`$D_1$` for weak shift).** SlimPajama (Soboleva et al., 2023) is an extensively deduplicated version of RedPajama (Computer, 2023), built to replicate the LLaMA training dataset. The full SlimPajama contains 606B tokens. To make it comparable in size to the Pile (which has ~300B tokens), the authors randomly subsample SlimPajama to form a ~299B token subset. Table 1 in the paper provides the domain sizes and sampling percentages:

| Domain | Size (Tokens) | Sampling (%) |
|--------|--------------|--------------|
| Wikipedia | 11.96B | 4.00 |
| Book | 12.58B | 4.20 |
| C4 | 79.87B | 26.69 |
| Stack Exchange | 10.09B | 3.37 |
| GitHub | 15.63B | 5.22 |
| Common Crawl | 155.89B | 52.09 |
| Arxiv | 13.25B | 4.43 |
| **Total** | **299.28B** | **100.00** |

The sampling percentages are chosen proportionally to the amount of data available in each domain, ensuring that one pass over the dataset (one "epoch" of the 299B tokens) does not repeat samples from any domain. This follows standard LLM pre-training practice: data is sampled without replacement within epochs, with domains mixed according to their natural abundance rather than uniformly. The validation set is the default, extensively deduplicated SlimPajama validation set, simply tokenized without modification.

For the `$N > 2$` experiments, the 299B SlimPajama subset is further split into three ~100B token splits. The paper does not specify the exact splitting procedure (whether domain proportions are preserved in each split, or whether it is a sequential chunk), but given the IID framing ("no shift setting"), it is likely that each split maintains the same domain proportions as the full SlimPajama.

**German CommonCrawl (`$D_1$` for strong shift).** The German training set is created from the German CommonCrawl scrape available as part of the Oscar dataset (Laippala et al., 2022). The authors split and tokenize it into a 195.43B token training set and a 982.6M token validation set. The training set size of ~200B tokens is somewhat smaller than the other datasets (300B), which means the continual pre-training phase on German processes fewer total tokens—this is accounted for by fitting the cosine decay to the 200B budget.

A notable limitation (acknowledged in Section 8): the German validation set was not deduplicated from the German training set. While distinct shards were used for training and validation, there may be some contamination. The authors argue this is acceptable because all baselines have access to the same dataset, so comparisons remain valid.

**Tokenizer.** All datasets use the same tokenizer as Black et al. (2022), which was trained exclusively on the Pile via the BPE algorithm (Sennrich et al., 2016) with a vocabulary size of 50,432. This is significant for the German setting: the tokenizer has never seen German text during training, which means German tokens may be fragmented into sub-optimal subword units. The paper acknowledges this as a potential bottleneck for the strong-shift setting ("as the domain strays farther and farther away from the tokenizer's training corpus, the tokenizer may become a key bottleneck to performance," Section 5.2) but leaves tokenizer adaptation to future work.

**Multi-dataset settings (Section 5.2).** The paper defines several settings that differ in the number of datasets and the nature of the distribution shift:

- **Two datasets, weak shift**: `$D_0 =$` Pile (300B), `$D_1 =$` SlimPajama (300B). English→English with overlapping domains but newer/different quality filtering.
- **Two datasets, stronger shift**: `$D_0 =$` Pile (300B), `$D_1 =$` German CommonCrawl (200B). English→German, completely different language.
- **Three datasets, no shift**: `$D_0, D_1, D_2$` are three IID 100B-token splits of SlimPajama. Used for evaluating infinite schedules across dataset transitions.
- **Domain incremental continual pre-training** (Appendix A.1): SlimPajama's domains are processed sequentially in decreasing order of size (CommonCrawl → C4 → GitHub → Arxiv → Book → Wikipedia → StackExchange). This simulates a scenario where new domains arrive one at a time. The paper notes this setting yields poor results and is "unnecessarily difficult"—the recommendation is to mix domains rather than process them sequentially.

#### Training Infrastructure and Model Architecture

**Model architecture.** The paper uses autoregressive decoder-only transformers with Pre-LN (Pre-Layer Normalization), rotary positional embeddings, GeLU activations, and no weight tying. Two model sizes are tested:

**405M parameter model:**
- 24 layers
- Hidden size: 1024
- 16 attention heads
- Non-embedding parameters: 353,822,720
- Total parameters (including embeddings): 405,334,016

**10B parameter model** (referred to as 10B in the paper, actual count is ~9.6B):
- 36 layers
- Hidden size: 4608
- 36 attention heads
- Non-embedding parameters: 9,408,678,912
- Total parameters (including embeddings): 9,642,249,216

Both models use GPT-NeoX (Andonian et al., 2021) based on Megatron-DeepSpeed (Shoeybi et al., 2019; Microsoft, 2020), with a causal language modeling objective (next-token prediction with standard cross-entropy loss).

**Optimizer and training configuration.** All models use AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`, weight decay = 0.1, and gradient clipping at 1.0. The batch size is fixed at 1104 sequences, each of length 2048 tokens, giving a total of 1104 × 2048 = 2,260,992 tokens per optimizer step (~2.26M tokens/batch). Training runs in mixed precision FP16/FP32 to leverage NVIDIA tensor cores.

**Learning rates (Table 14):**
- 405M model: `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 3 \times 10^{-4}$`, `$\eta_{\text{min}} = 3 \times 10^{-5}$` (i.e., `$\eta_{\text{min}} = 0.1 \cdot \eta_{\text{max}}$`).
- 10B model: `$\eta_{\text{max}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-4}$`, `$\eta_{\text{min}} = 1.2 \times 10^{-5}$` (same 0.1 ratio).
- For infinite schedules: `$\eta_{\text{const}} = 1.65 \times 10^{-4}$` for the 405M model (Table 14), approximately `$0.55 \cdot \eta_{\text{max}}$`. The cooldown duration `$T_{\text{cd}}$` is 60% of total iterations, the constant duration `$T_{\text{const}}$` is 25%, and the annealing duration `$T_{\text{ann}}$` appears to be the remaining 15% (since 60% + 25% + 15% = 100%). Warmup duration `$T_{\text{warmup}}$` is 1% of total iterations for all schedules.

**Parallelism strategy.** The 405M model uses data parallelism across 46 nodes, each with 6 GPUs (276 GPUs total), with a micro-batch size of 4 per GPU. The 10B model uses a more complex strategy combining tensor parallelism (spanning 6 GPUs within a node, from Shoeybi et al., 2020), pipeline parallelism (spanning 4 nodes, from Huang et al., 2019), and data parallelism. Each model replica spans 24 GPUs across 4 nodes (6 GPUs × 4 nodes). The 10B model is trained on 276 nodes using gradient accumulation of 4 steps.

All models use ZeRO-1 optimizer sharding (Rajbhandari et al., 2020), activation checkpointing (Chen et al., 2016), and activation partitioning across tensor parallel ranks to reduce GPU memory consumption.

**One epoch of training** corresponds to approximately 132,366 total training steps (300B tokens ÷ 2.26M tokens/step ≈ 132,743 steps; the paper reports 132,366, which accounts for slight differences in the exact token count). For the German dataset (195.43B tokens), one epoch is approximately 86,000 steps (195.43B ÷ 2.26M ≈ 86,473; the paper rounds to 86,000).

**Reset of optimizer states between datasets.** A crucial design choice: when transitioning from `$D_0$` to `$D_1$`, the optimizer states (moment estimates, `$m_t$` and `$v_t$` in Adam terminology) are **discarded and re-initialized to zero**. This simulates the realistic scenario of using an open-weight model (e.g., downloaded from HuggingFace) where optimizer states are typically not provided. The paper verifies in Appendix A.4 (Figure 13) that keeping versus discarding optimizer states makes negligible difference: after 40B tokens of continued pre-training on SlimPajama, both models reach similar validation loss. The authors attribute this to the momentum coefficients: with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$` and `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`, after 1000 steps, the original moment estimates contribute less than 0.0044% to the current estimates (since `$0.9^{1000} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-46}$` for the first moment and `$0.95^{1000} \approx 5.3 \times 10^{-23}$` for the second moment). Thus, for any training run longer than a few thousand steps (which all experiments are, given the batch size of 2.26M tokens), the initial states are irrelevant.

**Evaluation benchmarks (Section 5.4).** The paper evaluates on a diverse set of downstream tasks to measure practical capabilities beyond validation loss:

- **English benchmarks**: HellaSwag (0-shot, normalized accuracy), ARC-Easy/Challenge (0-shot), PIQA (0-shot), OpenBookQA (0-shot), BoolQ (0-shot), MathQA (0-shot), WinoGrande (0-shot), TruthfulQA (0-shot, both MC1 and MC2), NaturalQuestions (5-shot, exact match), TriviaQA (5-shot, exact match), and MMLU (5-shot).
- **German benchmarks** (from Plüster, 2023, translated via GPT-3.5 API): HellaSwag-DE, ARC-Challenge-DE, TriviaQA-DE, MMLU-DE.

The primary metric reported in summary tables is the **average accuracy** across all English benchmarks (or German benchmarks, separately). For HellaSwag and HellaSwag-DE, normalized accuracy is used. For NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA, exact match (EM) is used. All other tasks use standard unnormalized accuracy. The paper cautions that these are base models (no instruction tuning, no RLHF), and performance should be interpreted relative to baselines rather than absolute state-of-the-art.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Re-warming + re-decaying over constant LR**: Constant `$\eta_{\text{min}}$` adapts too poorly; constant `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` forgets too severely. The combination provides a controllable tradeoff with `$\eta_{\text{max}}$` as the tuning knob.
- **Cosine re-decay over other decay shapes**: Follows standard LLM pre-training best practices (Hoffmann et al., 2022) and provides smooth transitions that avoid optimization instability. No ablation against linear or step-wise decay is provided, but this is consistent with the paper's goal of using simple, off-the-shelf techniques.
- **Compute-equivalent replay over standard replay**: Ensures fair comparisons by maintaining constant total FLOPs. Without this constraint, replay models would have an unfair compute advantage (they'd process more total tokens), making it impossible to isolate the effect of data composition.
- **Replay in original order (no reshuffling)**: Did not affect results in preliminary experiments; choosing the simplest option reduces implementation complexity and avoids introducing a confound (reshuffling could affect model performance in ways unrelated to forgetting).
- **Resetting optimizer states**: Simulates real-world constraints (open-weight models). Verified to not affect results, as momentum decays quickly.
- **1% warmup duration**: Standard in the literature; longer warmups showed no benefit (Section 6.1.1).
- **Single-seed experiments**: Acknowledged as a limitation. The large batch size (2.26M tokens) provides low-variance gradient estimates, and data is processed in the same order across runs, making results relatively stable to random seed changes. However, this is a genuine limitation—some differences between models (e.g., the small average accuracy variations in Table 5) may be attributable to noise.
- **Infinite schedules with `$\eta_{\text{const}} \approx 0.55 \cdot \eta_{\text{max}}$`**: Chosen empirically to balance adaptation and stability. The paper does not ablate different `$\eta_{\text{const}}$` values, leaving this to future work.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Difficulty-Agnostic Continual Pre-Training Through Simple Schedule + Buffer Engineering

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is establishing that **complex distribution shifts in large-scale LLM pre-training can be handled without any form of difficulty estimation, adaptive strategy selection, or architectural modification.** The entire continual pre-training recipe—re-warm, re-decay, replay—is applied uniformly across all tokens regardless of domain, difficulty, or position within the dataset. This stands in stark contrast to a growing body of work (Ke et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024) that assumes effective continual learning requires *identifying* which parameters or data are important and treating them differently.

What makes this a genuine conceptual contribution rather than an obvious incremental finding is that the paper explicitly tests this assumption and finds it unnecessary. The domain-incremental experiment in Appendix A.1 is particularly revealing here: when data is consumed one domain at a time with per-domain re-warming and replay, performance degrades significantly compared to simply mixing all domains and applying a uniform replay strategy. The authors themselves call this setting "unnecessarily difficult compared to consuming the data using a mixture" and recommend against it. This is a diagnostic insight, not just a performance result: **the difficulty of continual pre-training is not primarily driven by domain boundaries or distribution-shift magnitude per se, but by the optimization dynamics created by the learning rate schedule and the presence or absence of replay anchors.**

Prior to this work, the dominant mental model—inherited from the broader continual learning literature (Rolnick et al., 2019; Aljundi et al., 2019; Buzzega et al., 2020)—was that forgetting is caused by gradient interference between tasks, and that mitigating it requires mechanisms that identify and protect task-specific knowledge (elastic weight consolidation, importance-weighted replay, parameter isolation). This paper demonstrates that at LLM scale, with hundreds of billions of tokens per dataset, **the simple presence of a small fraction of old-data gradients is sufficient to keep the model in a region of parameter space that performs well on both distributions.** The model does not need to "know" which neurons are important for which task; the replay gradients provide a weak but omnipresent restoring force that prevents catastrophic drift regardless of architecture specifics.

This is a fundamental simplification with immediate practical implications: practitioners do not need to build complex difficulty-aware allocation systems (as in the compute-optimal test-time scaling framework from the example paper) or parameter-importance estimators. They need only to set a replay percentage—an easily calibrated hyperparameter that can be tuned on a tiny fraction of the total training budget, since "relative differences between [replay percentages] appear very early during training" (Section 2, Rules of Thumb). The paper's finding that replay percentages from 1–50% all substantially outperform no-replay, with modest differences between them (Figures 5 and 11), means the method is robust to the exact setting and requires minimal tuning effort.

Evidence: Table 2 shows that 5% replay reduces Pile forgetting by 0.21 nats compared to no-replay while degrading SlimPajama adaptation by only 0.01 nats. Figure 5 shows that even 1% replay provides a dramatic reduction in the Pile validation loss curve across both weak and strong distribution shifts.

---

### Innovation 2: The Re-Warming Pathology as a Diagnostic Discovery Rather Than a Bug

Section 7.1 of this paper contains what might be its most conceptually important finding: **re-warming the learning rate causes measurable forgetting even when there is zero distribution shift** (Figure 8). When a model pre-trained on 300B tokens of Pile is re-warmed from `η_min = 3×10⁻⁵` to `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` and continued on another 300B tokens of Pile—identical data distribution, identical domain composition—the Pile validation loss immediately increases by ~0.1 nats (peaking at 0.2 nats for the `η_max = 6×10⁻⁴` variant) and only gradually recovers.

This is not a machine learning "bug" to be fixed—it is a diagnostic discovery that **decouples forgetting into two mechanistically distinct components: distribution-shift-induced forgetting and optimization-induced forgetting.** The former is what the field has always studied (the model sees different data, so its parameters move to a different minimum). The latter is something prior work had largely overlooked: the act of increasing the learning rate itself, even on identical data, temporarily disrupts the carefully converged parameter configuration by enabling gradient steps that are large relative to the local curvature of the loss landscape.

Why is this conceptually significant? Because it reframes the stability-plasticity dilemma at scale. The dominant assumption in continual learning has been that the tradeoff is between old and new knowledge—that forgetting is the price of adaptation. This paper's re-warming experiment shows that **some forgetting is not about knowledge at all; it is about optimization dynamics.** The model hasn't "forgotten" Pile knowledge in the sense of overwriting it with SlimPajama knowledge—even when there is no SlimPajama knowledge to overwrite it with, the loss still increases. The parameters are simply kicked out of their carefully converged local minimum by the large learning rate, and the model needs additional training (at sufficiently low LR) to settle back down.

This diagnostic insight is what motivates the infinite learning rate schedules (Section 7.2): if optimization-induced forgetting is a real phenomenon, then schedules that avoid re-warming—by maintaining a constant, moderate learning rate across dataset boundaries—should eliminate this component of forgetting. Figure 10 provides preliminary evidence: infinite schedules show negligible loss spikes at dataset transitions compared to repeated cosine cycles. The conceptual advance here is not the schedule itself (which was inspired by Zhai et al., 2022 for vision), but the **identification of what problem it solves**: not distribution shift, but the self-inflicted optimization disruption caused by aggressive re-warming.

Comparison to prior work: Most continual learning papers treat forgetting as a content problem (gradients from task B interfere with knowledge of task A) and develop solutions accordingly (gradient projection, synaptic intelligence, memory replay). This paper provides evidence that at LLM scale, a non-trivial fraction of observed forgetting is a *schedule artifact*—an optimization transient that would occur even with perfect task alignment. To my knowledge, this distinction between optimization-induced and distribution-shift-induced forgetting has not been clearly articulated or experimentally isolated in the LLM continual pre-training literature prior to this work.

Evidence: Figure 8 (left panel, "Re-warming (300B Pile→300B Pile)") provides the clean experimental isolation. The `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` re-warming curve shows a peak Pile validation loss increase of ~0.1 nats above the constant-`η_min` baseline, despite identical data. This cannot be attributed to distribution shift because there is none.

---

### Innovation 3: The Adaptation-Forgetting Tradeoff as a Controllable Spectrum via Two Knobs

The paper's third conceptual contribution is demonstrating that **the adaptation-forgetting tradeoff in LLM continual pre-training is not a binary choice but a continuous, controllable spectrum with two independent adjustment mechanisms: the re-warming maximum learning rate (`η_max`) and the replay percentage.** This transforms the problem from "which algorithm should I use?" to "how much adaptation vs. stability do I need, and how do I dial it in?"

Let me be precise about why this is a conceptual advance rather than just an ablation study. Prior work in continual learning typically presents methods as *solutions* to the tradeoff—experience replay "solves" forgetting, elastic weight consolidation "solves" forgetting, progressive networks "solve" forgetting—with the implicit assumption that the goal is to eliminate forgetting entirely while maximizing adaptation. This paper's results suggest a different framing: **forgetting and adaptation are both tunable, and the optimal setting depends on the practitioner's specific priorities** (how important is it to preserve performance on old data? how valuable is adaptation to new data?).

Figure 4 provides the clearest visualization of this controllability. At the `η_max = 6×10⁻⁴` extreme, the model achieves the best adaptation to the new dataset (lowest SlimPajama/German validation loss) but the most forgetting on Pile. At constant `η_min`, the model preserves Pile performance almost perfectly but barely adapts. At `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` with cosine re-decay, the model achieves an intermediate point. For replay (Figure 5, Table 2), the spectrum runs from 0% replay (best adaptation, worst forgetting) to 50% replay (best forgetting, worst adaptation), with all intermediate values smoothly trading between the two.

This controllability is practically important because different deployment scenarios have different needs. A company updating an LLM on the latest web scrape may care much more about adapting to new information than preserving exact performance on older data (5% replay, moderate `η_max`). A company adding a new language to a production model may care enormously about not degrading English performance (25% replay, careful `η_max` selection). The paper's framework provides the knobs to make these choices without requiring fundamentally different methodologies for each use case.

Additionally, **the interaction between these two knobs is surprisingly decoupled.** Increasing replay reduces forgetting without significantly affecting adaptation at low-to-moderate percentages (Figure 5: the `D_1` validation loss curves for 0%, 1%, 5%, and 10% replay are nearly overlapping for both distribution shifts). Increasing `η_max` improves adaptation at the cost of more forgetting (Figure 4: the `D_1` loss decreases with higher `η_max` while the `D_0` loss increases). This approximate independence means practitioners can tune them separately: first pick `η_max` to achieve the desired adaptation level, then add replay to bring forgetting back to an acceptable threshold.

Contrast with prior work: Ke et al. (2022) propose a single, fixed importance-masking mechanism; Wu et al. (2024) propose architectural expansion as the only control mechanism. Neither provides a framework for smoothly trading between adaptation and forgetting based on deployment needs. This paper's two-knob controllability is not just a convenience—it is a conceptual reframing of continual pre-training from a binary success/failure problem to a resource allocation problem where the "right" answer depends on context.

Evidence: Table 2 shows the smooth tradeoff across replay percentages (0%→50%) for both shifts. Figure 4 shows the smooth tradeoff across `η_max` values (1.5×10⁻⁴, 3×10⁻⁴, 6×10⁻⁴) plus the two constant baselines. The final recommended configurations (5% replay + `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` for weak shifts; 25% replay + `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` for strong shifts) are simply specific points on this spectrum chosen to match the union-trained baseline's average loss.

### Innovation 4: Re-Training as an Upper Bound, Not a Competitor—The Benchmarking Mindset Shift

A subtle but important conceptual move in this paper is **reframing the goal of continual pre-training research from "outperforming re-training" to "matching re-training at lower cost."** The authors state this explicitly and repeatedly:

> "In this work, we do not intend to improve on the performance of models trained from a random initialization on all of the available data. Instead, we consider models trained on the union of existing datasets as baselines whose performance we seek to match using a combination of continual learning strategies at scale." (Section 1)

This is not merely a humble disclaimer—it is a methodological innovation in how the problem is benchmarked. By setting the re-trained model as the upper bound, the paper establishes a **clear, unambiguous success criterion:** if a continual pre-training method achieves comparable average validation loss and downstream accuracy to the union-trained baseline, it has succeeded—regardless of whether it "beats" any other method. If it falls short, the gap quantifies exactly how much performance is being sacrificed for the compute savings.

Why is this framing significant? Because it avoids the pathological incentive structure that afflicts much of the continual learning literature, where methods are compared against other continual learning methods on artificial benchmarks, and it is never clear whether the performance gap to full re-training is large or small. A continual learning method that achieves 80% of the re-training baseline's accuracy might be declared a success if it outperforms other continual learning methods by 5%, but it is still leaving 20% on the table—which may be unacceptable in a production deployment. The paper's framing makes this gap visible and interpretable.

This approach is analogous to how lossless compression algorithms are evaluated: the goal is not to outperform the uncompressed representation (that would be impossible), but to match it while using fewer bits. Similarly, continual pre-training aims to match the union-trained model while using less compute (by avoiding the cost of re-processing old data alongside new data from scratch). The "compression ratio" here is not a formal metric, but the compute savings are visible in Figure 1: the "PT on Pile" model (re-warming only) uses approximately half the total compute of the Pile ∪ SP baseline (since it only trains on 300B tokens of `D_1`, not the full 600B), while the 5% replay model uses the same 600B total tokens as the baseline but distributed differently (300B Pile + 285B SP + 15B Pile replay), and still achieves comparable results.

The paper does not frame it this way explicitly, but there is an implicit **compute-efficiency argument** running through all the results: the continually pre-trained models are not just "good enough"—they are genuinely efficient in a way that matters economically. A 5% replay model achieves the same average validation loss (Table 4: 2.37 vs. 2.35 for the 405M weak shift; 1.89 vs. 1.87 for the 10B weak shift) and comparable or better average evaluation accuracy (Table 5: 35.14 vs. 34.30 for 405M; 47.68 vs. 48.00 for 10B) as the union-trained baseline. If the goal were to maximize performance at any cost, re-training might win by small margins on certain metrics. But if the goal is to update a model efficiently while preserving existing capabilities, continual pre-training is clearly the right choice—and the paper's framing makes this tradeoff legible.

This is a conceptual contribution to **how the field should evaluate continual learning methods** at scale, not a technical contribution to the methods themselves. The paper demonstrates that with simple techniques and a clear upper-bound baseline, one can make rigorous, practically meaningful statements about the efficacy of continual pre-training that were absent from prior work.

Evidence: The entire structure of Sections 6.3 and 6.4 is built around this comparison. Tables 3, 4, and 5 place the continually pre-trained models directly alongside the union-trained baselines and the individual-dataset baselines, making the gaps (or lack thereof) immediately visible. Figure 7 shows the loss curves for 405M and 10B models with the union-trained baseline's final loss marked explicitly, so the reader can see whether the continual pre-training curves converge to the same level.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary pre-training datasets are the Pile (Gao et al., 2020), SlimPajama (Soboleva et al., 2023), and German CommonCrawl (Laippala et al., 2022). The Pile is used as-is with a 300B-token subset of its ~330B training set and its default validation set. SlimPajama is randomly subsampled from 606B total tokens to form a ~299B token training subset (Table 1) with domain proportions preserved, and its default deduplicated validation set is used. German CommonCrawl is split into a 195.43B token training set and a 982.6M token validation set. For downstream evaluation, the paper uses a suite of English benchmarks (HellaSwag, ARC-Easy/Challenge, PIQA, OpenBookQA, BoolQ, MathQA, WinoGrande, TruthfulQA, NaturalQuestions, TriviaQA, MMLU) and four German benchmarks (HellaSwag-DE, ARC-Challenge-DE, MMLU-DE, TruthfulQA-DE) translated via the GPT-3.5 API.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses autoregressive decoder-only transformers from the GPT-NeoX family (Andonian et al., 2021) with Pre-LN, rotary positional embeddings, and GeLU activations. Two scales are tested: a 405M parameter model (24 layers, hidden size 1024, 16 attention heads) and a 10B parameter model (36 layers, hidden size 4608, 36 attention heads; exact count: ~9.6B parameters). These scales are chosen to span an order of magnitude while remaining computationally feasible for the extensive ablation sweeps required. The models are described as "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), with pass@1 performance on MATH in the 10–19% range for the base models, leaving substantial room for test-time compute to demonstrate benefit.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **validation loss** on held-out data from both `D_0` and `D_1`, reported as the average over the last 100 training iterations sampled at 10-iteration intervals (standard error for all models is < 0.001 nats). The **average final validation loss** across `D_0` and `D_1` serves as the main summary statistic for comparing the adaptation-forgetting tradeoff. For downstream evaluation, the paper reports **normalized accuracy** for HellaSwag (and HellaSwag-DE), **exact match (EM)** for NaturalQuestions and TriviaQA, and **unnormalized accuracy** for all other tasks, with the **average accuracy across all English benchmarks** (or all German benchmarks) as the aggregate metric. MMLU and TriviaQA are evaluated 5-shot; all other benchmarks are zero-shot.

- **Baselines.** The paper uses three core baselines for each experimental setting: (1) a model trained from random initialization on `D_0 ∪ D_1` (the union-trained baseline, considered the upper bound on performance and representing the expensive full re-training approach); (2) a model trained from random initialization on `D_0` only (the pre-trained starting point for continual pre-training); (3) a model trained from random initialization on `D_1` only (a compute-equivalent alternative to continual pre-training—one could simply discard the old model and train a new one on the new data). Additionally, in the learning rate ablation experiments (Section 6.1.2, Figure 4), two constant-LR baselines are used: continuing at `η_min` (3×10⁻⁵ for 405M) with no re-warming, and re-warming to `η_max` (3×10⁻⁴ for 405M) with no re-decaying.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in **total tokens processed**, with batch size fixed at 1104 sequences × 2048 tokens = ~2.26M tokens per optimizer step across all experiments. All comparisons between models with and without replay use **compute-equivalent replay** (Section 4.2): the total tokens processed during the `D_1` phase is kept constant (e.g., 300B tokens for SlimPajama), so a model with `x%` replay sees `s_1 · (1 - x/100)` unique `D_1` tokens and `s_1 · (x/100)` replayed `D_0` tokens, while a model without replay sees all `s_1` unique `D_1` tokens. This ensures that performance differences are attributable to data composition (the presence of old-data gradients) rather than total FLOPs expended. For the 10B parameter model experiments, the same principle applies—the total tokens processed in the `D_1` phase is 300B, with 5% replay meaning 285B unique SlimPajama tokens + 15B replayed Pile tokens.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use cross-validation in the traditional machine learning sense. All experiments are **single-seed** due to computational constraints (Section 8, Limitation 4), which the authors justify by noting that the large batch size (~2.26M tokens) provides low-variance gradient estimates and that data is processed in the same order across runs. Standard errors for the final validation loss measurements are computed over the last 100 training iterations but represent temporal averaging, not statistical uncertainty across seeds. For downstream evaluation, results are reported as single values without confidence intervals. This is an acknowledged limitation—the authors note "there is likely a stochastic element to some results" and that the small average accuracy variations between models in Table 5 may be attributable to noise. The paper does not use cross-validation for strategy selection (unlike the compute-optimal test-time scaling paper), since there is no adaptive allocation policy being learned—the strategies (re-warming, re-decaying, replay percentage) are fixed per experimental configuration and evaluated directly on the test set, with the validation sets used only for monitoring convergence, not for hyperparameter selection.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Learning Rate Schedule Ablations (Section 6.1)

**Warmup duration has negligible impact on final performance (Figure 3).** For both the weak shift (300B Pile → 300B SlimPajama) and the strong shift (300B Pile → 200B German), models with linear warmup durations of 0%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2% of `D_1`'s total training iterations converge to nearly identical Pile and `D_1` validation losses after 50B tokens of continued pre-training. The 0% warmup model exhibits a transient validation loss spike initially—the Pile validation loss jumps to ~2.65 at the start for the weak shift (Figure 3a) and to ~3.75 for the strong shift (Figure 3c)—due to the combination of a large initial LR, noisy gradients from the new distribution, and reset optimizer states. However, all models recover to within ~0.02 nats of each other on both datasets by 50B tokens. The paper standardizes to 1% warmup for all subsequent experiments as a conservative choice that avoids the initial instability without any penalty.

**Re-warming and re-decaying is necessary for adaptation; the magnitude of η_max trades adaptation against forgetting (Figure 4, Table 12).** Five models are compared at 405M scale for each distribution shift: two constant-LR baselines (continue at `η_min = 3×10⁻⁵`; re-warm to `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` and stay constant), and three re-warm/re-decay configurations with `η_max ∈ {1.5×10⁻⁴, 3×10⁻⁴, 6×10⁻⁴}` (all cosine-decayed to `0.1·η_max`). For the weak shift (Pile→SlimPajama, 300B tokens each), the results in Table 12 show:

| Configuration | Pile Val Loss | SP Val Loss | Avg Val Loss |
|--------------|---------------|-------------|--------------|
| Constant η_min = 3×10⁻⁵ | 2.42 | 2.55 | 2.48 |
| Constant η_max = 3×10⁻⁴ | 2.48 | 2.50 | 2.49 |
| Re-warm/re-decay, η_max = 1.5×10⁻⁴ | 2.43 | 2.51 | 2.47 |
| Re-warm/re-decay, η_max = 3×10⁻⁴ | 2.44 | 2.50 | 2.47 |
| Re-warm/re-decay, η_max = 6×10⁻⁴ | 2.48 | 2.50 | 2.49 |

The constant-`η_min` baseline adapts poorly (highest SP loss at 2.55), while the constant-`η_max` baseline forgets severely (highest Pile loss at 2.48) but adapts well. The re-warm/re-decay models achieve a favorable tradeoff: at `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴`, SP adaptation is nearly identical to constant-`η_max` (2.50 vs. 2.50) while Pile forgetting is intermediate (2.44 vs. 2.48 for constant-`η_max`, compared to 2.42 for constant-`η_min`). For the strong shift (Pile→German, Table 12), the pattern is similar but more pronounced:

| Configuration | Pile Val Loss | German Val Loss | Avg Val Loss |
|--------------|---------------|-----------------|--------------|
| Constant η_min = 3×10⁻⁵ | 3.22 | 1.21 | 2.22 |
| Constant η_max = 3×10⁻⁴ | 3.63 | 1.11 | 2.37 |
| Re-warm/re-decay, η_max = 1.5×10⁻⁴ | 3.47 | 1.13 | 2.30 |
| Re-warm/re-decay, η_max = 3×10⁻⁴ | 3.56 | 1.11 | 2.34 |
| Re-warm/re-decay, η_max = 6×10⁻⁴ | 3.63 | 1.11 | 2.37 |

The constant-`η_min` model forgets the least on Pile (3.22 vs. 2.17 for the Pile-only baseline; Table 12) but barely adapts to German (1.21 vs. 1.17 for German-only). Re-warming and re-decaying at `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` achieves nearly the same German adaptation as the best configuration (1.11, matching constant-`η_max`) while limiting Pile forgetting to 3.56 (a 0.07 improvement over constant-`η_max` at 3.63). The general pattern is clear from Figures 4a–d: re-warming + re-decaying is Pareto-optimal—it dominates both constant baselines on the adaptation-forgetting frontier.

#### Replay Sweep (Section 6.2)

**Even 1% replay substantially reduces forgetting, with minimal impact on adaptation at low-to-moderate replay percentages (Figure 5, Table 2).** For the weak shift (Pile→SlimPajama), replay percentages of 0.5%, 1%, 5%, 10%, and 50% are tested at 405M scale (Table 2):

| Replay % | Pile Val Loss | SP Val Loss | Avg Val Loss |
|----------|---------------|-------------|--------------|
| 0% | 2.44 | 2.50 | 2.47 |
| 0.5% | 2.27 | 2.50 | 2.39 |
| 1% | 2.26 | 2.50 | 2.38 |
| 5% | 2.23 | 2.51 | 2.37 |
| 10% | 2.21 | 2.51 | 2.36 |
| 50% | 2.16 | 2.54 | 2.35 |
| Pile ∪ SP (600B) | 2.17 | 2.53 | 2.35 |

Going from 0% to 1% replay reduces Pile forgetting by 0.18 nats (from 2.44 to 2.26) while SP adaptation remains unchanged at 2.50. The visual evidence in Figure 5a is striking: the Pile validation loss curve for even 0.5% replay (pink) diverges from the no-replay curve (black) almost immediately and stays consistently lower throughout training. At 5% replay (the paper's recommended setting for weak shifts), Pile forgetting is reduced by 0.21 nats compared to no-replay (2.23 vs. 2.44) with only a 0.01 nat degradation in SP adaptation (2.51 vs. 2.50). The 50% replay model achieves the same average loss as the union-trained baseline (2.35 vs. 2.35) despite seeing 150B fewer unique SlimPajama tokens.

For the strong shift (Pile→German), replay percentages of 1%, 5%, 10%, 25%, and 50% are tested (Table 2):

| Replay % | Pile Val Loss | German Val Loss | Avg Val Loss |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|--------------|
| 0% | 3.56 | 1.11 | 2.34 |
| 1% | 2.83 | 1.12 | 1.97 |
| 5% | 2.57 | 1.12 | 1.85 |
| 10% | 2.46 | 1.13 | 1.80 |
| 25% | 2.33 | 1.16 | 1.75 |
| 50% | 2.24 | 1.22 | 1.73 |
| Pile ∪ German (500B) | 2.26 | 1.25 | 1.75 |

The stronger shift requires more replay to achieve the same level of forgetting mitigation: 25% replay reduces Pile forgetting from 3.56 to 2.33 (a 1.23 nat improvement), bringing the model within 0.07 nats of the union baseline (2.26). German adaptation degrades modestly from 1.11 to 1.16 at 25% replay, and more substantially to 1.22 at 50% replay. The 25% replay model achieves the same average validation loss as the union-trained baseline (1.75 vs. 1.75). Figure 5c shows that the gap between the replay and no-replay Pile loss curves widens dramatically over the course of training: at 50B tokens, 25% replay is already ~1 nat lower in Pile loss; by 200B tokens, the gap exceeds 1.2 nats.

**Downstream evaluation confirms replay does not degrade English benchmark performance at low percentages (Table 10).** For the weak shift (Pile→SlimPajama) at 405M scale, the average English evaluation accuracy across 0%, 0.5%, 1%, 5%, 10%, and 50% replay models is 34.93, 35.16, 34.91, 35.14, 34.91, and 34.94 respectively—all within a 0.25 point range. The 5% replay model actually achieves the second-highest average (35.14), slightly above the union-trained baseline (34.30). For the strong shift (Pile→German), average English evaluation accuracy improves monotonically with more replay: 29.20 (0%), 29.65 (1%), 31.37 (5%), 31.14 (10%), 32.48 (25%), 33.16 (50%), with the union baseline at 32.43. At 25% replay, the model matches the union baseline (32.48 vs. 32.43). On German HellaSwag (Table 11, the only German benchmark where models significantly exceed random chance), German-trained models consistently outperform English-only models (30.91–31.23 for German models vs. 27.03–27.43 for English models), and the 0% replay model achieves the highest German HellaSwag accuracy (31.23), confirming the adaptation-forgetting tradeoff visible in the loss curves.

**Replay scales predictably with dataset size (Appendix A.3, Figure 12).** When the `D_1` phase is varied to 100B, 200B, or 300B tokens of SlimPajama with 5% compute-equivalent replay, the same qualitative pattern holds across all sizes: replay consistently reduces Pile forgetting by ~0.20–0.22 nats at the end of training compared to no-replay, while SP adaptation differences are negligible (within 0.01 nats). The absolute SlimPajama validation loss improves with more data (from ~2.60 at 100B to ~2.53 at 300B), as expected, but the replay effect size remains constant.

#### Final Performance: Weak and Strong Shifts at 405M Scale (Section 6.3)

**LR re-warming + re-decaying + replay (5% for weak, 25% for strong) matches the union-trained baseline in average validation loss and average downstream accuracy (Figure 6, Tables 3 and 5).** For the weak shift (Pile→SlimPajama) at 405M scale, Table 3 reports:

| Model | Pile Val Loss | SP Val Loss | Avg Val Loss | Avg English Eval |
|-------|---------------|-------------|--------------|------------------|
| 300B Pile (baseline) | 2.17 | 2.70 | 2.44 | 33.95 |
| 300B SP (baseline) | 2.51 | 2.53 | 2.52 | 34.11 |
| Pile → SP (no replay) | 2.44 | 2.50 | 2.47 | 34.93 |
| Pile → SP (5% replay) | 2.23 | 2.51 | 2.37 | 35.14 |
| Pile ∪ SP (600B) | 2.17 | 2.53 | 2.35 | 34.30 |

The 5% replay model achieves average validation loss within 0.02 nats of the union-trained upper bound (2.37 vs. 2.35) and surpasses it on average English evaluation accuracy (35.14 vs. 34.30). The no-replay model, while adapting well to SP (2.50, better than union at 2.53), forgets substantially on Pile (2.44 vs. 2.17 for Pile-only), making its average loss 0.12 nats worse than 5% replay. On individual downstream tasks (Table 5), the 5% replay model and union baseline exchange best performance with no systematic pattern; differences are small (e.g., HellaSwag: 46.55 vs. 45.06; MMLU: 25.94 vs. 26.65; PIQA: 69.37 vs. 69.37), consistent with noise at this model scale.

For the strong shift (Pile→German) at 405M scale, Table 3 reports:

| Model | Pile Val Loss | German Val Loss | Avg Val Loss | Avg English Eval | HellaSwag-DE |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|--------------|------------------|--------------|
| 300B Pile (baseline) | 2.17 | 2.70 | 2.44 | 33.95 | 27.09 |
| 200B German (baseline) | 3.97 | 1.17 | 2.57 | 27.74 | 29.53 |
| Pile → German (no replay) | 3.56 | 1.11 | 2.34 | 29.20 | 31.23 |
| Pile → German (25% replay) | 2.33 | 1.16 | 1.75 | 32.48 | 31.04 |
| Pile ∪ German (500B) | 2.26 | 1.25 | 1.75 | 32.43 | 30.45 |

The 25% replay model matches the union baseline on average validation loss exactly (1.75 vs. 1.75) and on average English evaluation accuracy (32.48 vs. 32.43), while slightly exceeding it on German HellaSwag (31.04 vs. 30.45). The no-replay model adapts to German extremely well (1.11, best of all models, even better than German-only at 1.17) but at catastrophic cost to English capabilities: Pile loss jumps to 3.56 (from 2.17 pre-trained) and English evaluation drops from 33.95 to 29.20. The strong shift exacerbates the adaptation-forgetting tradeoff dramatically, but replay recovers the balance.

**Qualitative evaluation confirms German models learn the language (Appendix A.5, Tables 8–9).** Five German prompts testing various linguistic features (Umlauts, compound words, anglicisms) reveal that while 405M German-trained models produce repetitive, grammatically imperfect text, they consistently respect German syntax, casing, and character conventions, whereas the Pile-only baseline fails to even stay on-topic. The paper notes this as evidence that "there is an observable improvement in the generative quality of German-language outputs," despite the poor aggregate scores on German evaluation benchmarks (Table 11), which hover near random chance for all models on MMLU-DE and ARC-C-DE.

#### Scaling to 10B Parameters (Section 6.4)

**The same recipe works at 10B scale for weak shifts, with larger models showing slightly less baseline forgetting (Figure 7, Tables 4 and 5).** For the weak shift (Pile→SlimPajama) at 10B scale, Table 4 reports:

| Model | Pile Val Loss | SP Val Loss | Avg Val Loss |
|-------|---------------|-------------|--------------|
| 300B Pile (baseline) | 1.75 | 2.24 | 1.99 |
| 300B SP (baseline) | 2.08 | 2.05 | 2.07 |
| Pile → SP (no replay) | 1.98 | 2.00 | 1.99 |
| Pile → SP (5% replay) | 1.79 | 2.00 | 1.89 |
| Pile ∪ SP (600B) | 1.72 | 2.02 | 1.87 |

The 5% replay model achieves average validation loss within 0.02 nats of the union baseline (1.89 vs. 1.87), matching the 405M-scale finding. Notably, the no-replay model forgets less at 10B scale than at 405M: the Pile loss increase from pre-training is 1.98 - 1.75 = 0.23 nats at 10B vs. 2.44 - 2.17 = 0.27 nats at 405M, consistent with the hypothesis that larger models forget less (Ramasesh et al., 2022; Mirzadeh et al., 2022). The 5% replay model's Pile loss (1.79) is within 0.07 nats of the Pile-only baseline (1.75), while the union-trained model is at 1.72—closer to the Pile-only value because the union model sees Pile tokens from the start intermixed with SP.

On downstream evaluation (Table 5), the 10B results show:

| Model | Avg English Eval |
|-------|------------------|
| 300B Pile | 43.45 |
| 300B SP | 45.37 |
| Pile → SP (no replay) | 47.53 |
| Pile → SP (5% replay) | 47.68 |
| Pile ∪ SP (600B) | 48.00 |

Both continually pre-trained models outperform the individual-dataset baselines (43.45 and 45.37), confirming that they successfully combine knowledge from both datasets. The 5% replay model (47.68) is within 0.32 points of the union baseline (48.00), a gap that may be attributable to noise given the single-seed experimental design. The no-replay model achieves 47.53, also very close. One notable discrepancy: the union-trained model substantially outperforms the replay model on MMLU (37.78 vs. 28.79), but the replay model outperforms on OpenBookQA (30.60 vs. 27.80) and wins or ties on several other tasks. The authors suggest the MMLU gap may be "due to the limited amount of training data used in our study" and cite Glorioso et al. (2024), who successfully applied these techniques without MMLU degradation using larger datasets.

**The benefits of increased scale are visible in absolute terms but not in the relative efficacy of the continual pre-training strategies.** At both 405M and 10B scales, the 5% replay model closes the gap to the union baseline to within ~0.02 nats of average validation loss and ~0.3 points of average evaluation accuracy. The forgetting-reduction from replay is nearly identical in absolute nats: replay reduces Pile forgetting by 0.21 nats at 405M (2.44 → 2.23) and 0.19 nats at 10B (1.98 → 1.79). This suggests that the replay mechanism is equally effective across an order of magnitude of parameter count, and that the primary benefit of larger scale is better absolute performance, not increased robustness to forgetting.

#### Infinite Learning Rate Schedules (Section 7)

**Re-warming causes forgetting even without distribution shift (Figure 8).** When a model pre-trained on 300B Pile is re-warmed to `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` and continued on another 300B of Pile (identical data distribution), the Pile validation loss increases from ~2.17 to ~2.27 at the start of re-warming—a spike of ~0.1 nats—before gradually recovering. At `η_max = 6×10⁻⁴`, the spike reaches ~0.2 nats. This is distinct from the distribution-shift-induced forgetting seen in Figure 4 and establishes that some component of forgetting is purely an optimization artifact: large learning rates temporarily disrupt the converged parameter configuration even when the data is unchanged. The constant-`η_min` baseline shows no spike (the loss immediately continues decreasing), confirming that the re-warming itself, not any property of the data, causes the disruption.

**Infinite schedules match cosine decay performance in standard single-dataset pre-training (Figure 9).** Three 405M models are trained from random initialization on 300B SlimPajama: one with a standard cosine decay schedule, one with the Cosine Inf schedule (cosine cooldown → constant → exponential anneal), and one with the InvSqrt Inf schedule (inverse square root cooldown → constant → exponential anneal). All three achieve nearly identical final validation loss (~2.53; Table 12), demonstrating that infinite schedules are viable for standard pre-training without sacrificing convergence quality. The learning rate curves in Figure 9b show the qualitative difference: the cosine schedule decays continuously, while the infinite schedules plateau at `η_const = 1.65×10⁻⁴` for an extended period before the final exponential drop.

**Infinite schedules reduce forgetting across dataset boundaries in the no-shift setting (Figure 10, Table 12).** For three IID 100B-token splits of SlimPajama, the repeated-cosine model shows a visible loss spike at each dataset transition (e.g., SP validation loss jumps from ~2.57 to ~2.60 between 100B and 200B), which recovers within ~10–15B tokens. The Cosine Inf and InvSqrt Inf models show no such spike—the loss continues smoothly at each transition because the LR is already at `η_const` and does not need re-warming. However, the absolute final loss values are slightly worse for the infinite schedules (both at 2.59 vs. 2.53 for the repeated-cosine baseline; Table 12), and the paper does not test these schedules with distribution shifts, leaving their practical superiority unresolved. The average English evaluation accuracy for all three variants is comparable (33.33 for InvSqrt, 34.00 for Cosine Inf, 33.65 for Cosine; Table 10), within the noise range observed throughout the paper.

**Infinite schedules decouple the token budget from the schedule design.** Unlike cosine schedules, which must know the total number of tokens in advance to fit the annealing phase, infinite schedules can be extended indefinitely by simply prolonging the constant phase. The annealing phase can be applied at any point to converge the model for deployment. This makes them naturally suited for continual pre-training settings where the total data volume is unknown a priori or where datasets arrive incrementally over time.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Warmup duration (Figure 3):** 0%, 0.5%, 1%, and 2% warmup all converge to similar loss after 50B tokens for both weak and strong shifts. The 0% warmup model exhibits an initial loss spike (transient instability) but recovers fully. 1% is selected as the standard going forward, providing stability without cost.

- **Maximum learning rate η_max for re-warming (Figure 4, Table 12):** Tested at 1.5×10⁻⁴, 3×10⁻⁴, and 6×10⁻⁴ (compared to constant baselines at 3×10⁻⁵ and 3×10⁻⁴). Higher η_max improves adaptation to D₁ (lower D₁ loss) but increases forgetting on D₀. At η_max = 6×10⁻⁴ for the strong shift, Pile loss reaches 3.63 (matching the constant-η_max baseline) while German adaptation (1.11) is better than the union baseline (1.25). The baseline η_max = 3×10⁻⁴ is chosen for most experiments as a balanced default.

- **Replay percentage (Figure 5, Table 2, Table 10):** Tested at 0%, 0.5% (weak only), 1%, 5%, 10%, 25% (strong only), and 50% for both shifts. For weak shifts, 5% is selected as the recommended setting—it achieves most of the forgetting reduction benefit with negligible adaptation cost. For strong shifts, 25% is selected—lower percentages (1–10%) still show substantial Pile loss degradation (2.83, 2.57, 2.46 at 1%, 5%, 10% respectively) compared to the union baseline (2.26). 50% replay degrades adaptation notably on the strong shift (German loss 1.22 vs. 1.11 for no-replay) but achieves near-identical average performance to the union baseline. The downstream evaluation results (Table 10) confirm that English benchmark performance is robust to replay percentage, with all weak-shift models scoring within ~0.25 points of each other on average.

- **Replay scaling with D₁ size (Figure 12):** 5% replay provides consistent forgetting reduction (~0.20–0.22 nats) across D₁ sizes of 100B, 200B, and 300B SlimPajama tokens, demonstrating robustness to the incoming data volume.

- **Optimizer state reset (Figure 13):** Keeping versus discarding AdamW momentum states when transitioning from Pile to SlimPajama produces nearly identical validation loss trajectories after 40B tokens of continued pre-training. The 405M model with `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴` reaches ~2.47 Pile loss and ~2.53 SP loss in both cases, with differences < 0.01 nats. This validates the paper's default practice of resetting optimizer states, which simulates the realistic scenario of using a public model checkpoint without optimizer states.

- **Domain-incremental continual pre-training (Appendix A.1, Figure 11):** Consuming SlimPajama one domain at a time (largest to smallest) with per-domain re-warming and reservoir replay performs substantially worse than consuming the same data as a mixture. SlimPajama validation loss oscillates with each domain transition and converges to ~2.70–2.75, compared to ~2.50 for the mixed-data baseline. This negative result is informative: it shows that sequentially processing domains with distribution shifts between them creates optimization difficulties that replay alone cannot fully resolve, and that mixing data across domains is strongly preferable when possible.

- **Model merging vs. continual pre-training (Appendix A.2, Tables 6–7):** Using TIES merging (Yadav et al., 2023b) to combine separately trained Pile and SP/German models consistently underperforms continual pre-training with replay. For the 10B weak shift, the best merged model (TopK=75%) achieves 41.94 average English accuracy vs. 47.68 for continual pre-training with 5% replay. For the 405M strong shift, the best merged model (TopK=75%) achieves 24.60 on German HellaSwag vs. 31.04 for continual pre-training with 25% replay. Merging continually pre-trained checkpoints back into the Pile base model does not improve performance, suggesting replay is a more effective strategy than post-hoc weight interpolation.

- **German evaluation tasks (Table 11):** German evaluation benchmarks (MMLU-DE, ARC-C-DE) show near-random performance for all models (including those trained on German), with average scores of 23–25 across the board. Only HellaSwag-DE differentiates models meaningfully (27.09 for Pile-only vs. 29.53–31.23 for German-trained models). This suggests that at 405M scale, the model lacks sufficient capacity to perform well on knowledge-intensive German tasks, and that HellaSwag (which tests surface-form and commonsense reasoning) is a more sensitive probe for language acquisition.

- **Infinite schedule variants (Figure 10, Table 12):** Cosine Inf and InvSqrt Inf achieve comparable final validation loss to repeated-cosine on the 3×100B IID setting (2.59 for both vs. 2.53 for cosine), with negligible performance degradation. The InvSqrt Inf schedule shows slightly better average English evaluation than Cosine Inf on some tasks (e.g., 51.35 vs. 51.05 on ARC-Easy), but differences are within noise. The infinite schedules successfully eliminate the transient loss spike at dataset boundaries, confirming their primary design goal.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "A simple and scalable combination of LR re-warming, LR re-decaying, and compute-equivalent replay allows continually pre-trained decoder-only transformer-based LLMs to attain similar performance on average to models re-trained on the union of all data while using significantly less compute."**

This claim is well-supported for the two-dataset settings tested, with several important qualifications.

*What the experiments actually demonstrate:* For the weak shift (Pile→SlimPajama) at 405M and 10B scales, and for the strong shift (Pile→German) at 405M scale, the combination of re-warming, re-decaying, and an appropriate replay percentage produces average validation loss within 0.02 nats of the union-trained baseline (Tables 3 and 4) and average downstream evaluation accuracy within ~0.3 points (Table 5). The evidence is consistent across both scales and both shift strengths.

*Where the claim holds unconditionally:* The loss curves in Figures 6 and 7 show that the continually pre-trained models converge to final losses that are visually indistinguishable from the union baselines. The downstream evaluation numbers (Tables 5 and 10) demonstrate that these loss improvements translate to comparable real-world task performance—the 5% replay model actually exceeds the union baseline's average accuracy at 405M (35.14 vs. 34.30) and is within 0.32 points at 10B (47.68 vs. 48.00).

*Where the claim requires qualification:*
- **"Significantly less compute" is only partially correct.** The continually pre-trained models with replay process the same total number of tokens as the union-trained baseline (e.g., 600B for Pile→SP) due to the compute-equivalent constraint. The compute savings come from avoiding the cost of processing old data alongside new data *from scratch*—the union baseline must train on Pile tokens *after* random initialization, while the continual model leverages the pre-trained Pile checkpoint. Figure 1 visualizes this: the union baseline requires ~2.0 units of compute (training on 600B tokens from scratch), while the continual model (with 5% replay) requires ~1.0 units of *additional* compute beyond the initial Pile pre-training (which is assumed to already exist). However, the paper does not explicitly quantify this savings in FLOPs or GPU-hours, making "significantly less compute" a qualitative rather than quantitative claim. In practice, the union baseline trains on 600B tokens from random initialization; the continual model trains on 300B (Pile) + 300B (SP with 5% replay) = 600B total tokens, but the first 300B were already done. The *additional* compute for updating is only the second 300B tokens, which is ~50% of what a full re-train would cost. This is indeed significant, but the paper could make this arithmetic clearer.

- **The "average performance" framing masks task-level variation.** Table 5 shows that for the 10B model, the union baseline outperforms the 5% replay model by 8.99 points on MMLU (37.78 vs. 28.79), while the replay model outperforms the union baseline by 2.80 points on OpenBookQA (30.60 vs. 27.80). The averages cancel these differences out, but a practitioner who cares specifically about MMLU performance might find the replay model unacceptable. The paper acknowledges this ("this degradation in MMLU performance between both models could be cause for concern") but attributes it to limited training data and cites external evidence that the issue disappears with larger datasets. This is plausible but unverified within the paper's own experiments.

- **The absence of multi-seed experiments weakens the statistical claims.** With single-seed runs, we cannot distinguish between genuine performance differences and sampling noise. The average evaluation accuracy differences between models (Table 5) are often 0.2–0.5 points, which could easily be within seed-to-seed variation. The paper's argument that large batch sizes reduce gradient variance is valid but only addresses optimizer noise, not the noise from random initialization or data ordering. This is an important limitation for interpreting the "matches the re-training baseline" claim—"match" here means "within 0.02 nats of average validation loss," but whether that gap is statistically or practically significant is unknown.

- **The 10B strong-shift experiment was not run.** All strong-shift results are at 405M scale only. The claim that the strategies work "across two model sizes and distribution shifts" is true for model sizes (405M and 10B, both on the weak shift) and for distribution shifts (weak and strong, both at 405M), but the intersection (10B + strong shift) is missing. This is a genuine gap—the paper cannot claim that the 25% replay recipe transfers to 10B for strong shifts, only that it works at 405M and that weak-shift recipes transfer to 10B.

**Claim 2: "Re-warming and re-decaying is necessary for adaptation during continual pre-training."**

This claim is very strongly supported by Figure 4 and Table 12, with an important nuance about what "necessary" means.

*Evidence:* For the strong shift, continuing at `η_min = 3×10⁻⁵` results in German validation loss of 1.21, compared to 1.11 for re-warming and re-decaying at `η_max = 3×10⁻⁴`—a 0.10 nat degradation. For the weak shift, the constant-`η_min` baseline achieves SP loss of 2.55 vs. 2.50 for re-warming—a smaller but still meaningful 0.05 nat gap. The constant-`η_max` baseline adapts as well as re-warming (matching `D_1` loss) but forgets catastrophically. So re-warming without re-decaying gives adaptation without stability; no re-warming gives stability without adaptation; the combination gives both.

*Nuance:* The constant-`η_min` baseline does not *completely fail* to adapt—it still improves over the pre-trained model's `D_1` loss (which would be much higher, as evidenced by the Pile-only model's German validation loss of 2.70 in Table 3). It just adapts much more slowly. For a practitioner with infinite compute, continuing at `η_min` for long enough might eventually converge to similar performance. The paper's claim is that re-warming is necessary *per compute spent*—it maximizes adaptation efficiency. This is practically true but is a statement about compute-constrained adaptation, not a claim that adaptation is literally impossible without re-warming.

*Missing evidence:* The paper does not test whether continuing at `η_min` for 2× or 4× the normal token budget would eventually match re-warmed performance. If it would (even with drastically lower efficiency), the claim should be "re-warming and re-decaying dramatically improves adaptation efficiency" rather than "is necessary." The efficiency framing is more precise, but the paper uses "necessary" in multiple places (Section 6.1.2, Section 9).

**Claim 3: "Even small amounts of replay are good at mitigating forgetting."**

This claim is strongly supported by Figure 5 and Table 2.

*Evidence:* For the weak shift, 1% replay reduces Pile validation loss from 2.44 to 2.26 (0.18 nats improvement). For the strong shift, 1% replay reduces Pile loss from 3.56 to 2.83 (0.73 nats improvement). These are large effects from tiny data fractions—1% of the `D_1` phase for the weak shift is 3B replayed tokens out of 300B total. The downstream evaluation (Table 10) confirms that even 0.5% and 1% replay models achieve average English accuracy within 0.25 points of higher-replay models, demonstrating that the loss improvements are not an artifact of the loss metric.

*Nuance:* "Small amounts" is relative. For the strong shift, 1% replay still leaves a substantial Pile loss gap compared to the union baseline (2.83 vs. 2.26), and the paper ultimately recommends 25% replay for this setting. So while 1% replay is "good at mitigating forgetting" compared to no replay, it is not sufficient to close the gap to the union baseline for strong shifts. The claim is true as stated ("good at mitigating" ≠ "fully eliminates"), but a casual reader might infer that any small replay percentage is sufficient, which the strong-shift results contradict.

**Claim 4: "Infinite learning rate schedules circumvent optimization difficulties associated with learning rate re-warming."**

This claim is partially supported but the evidence is preliminary.

*Evidence:* Figure 10 shows that infinite schedules eliminate the transient loss spike at dataset boundaries in the no-shift setting, and they achieve comparable final validation loss to repeated-cosine schedules (~2.59 vs. ~2.53; Table 12). The downstream evaluation results are also comparable (Table 10: 33.33–34.00 for infinite variants vs. 33.65 for cosine). This confirms that the schedules are *functional* and that they *do circumvent re-warming-induced forgetting* in the no-shift case.

*Missing evidence:* The schedules have only been tested at 405M scale with IID splits of the same dataset (no distribution shift). We do not know whether the constant phase at `η_const = 1.65×10⁻⁴` provides sufficient adaptation when there is a genuine distribution shift (Pile→SlimPajama or Pile→German). We do not know whether training stability holds over very long constant-LR phases (trillions of tokens), particularly without `μ`P-style scaling. We do not know how to choose `η_const` for different distribution shifts—the paper uses the same value as in the single-dataset experiments without justification. The paper acknowledges all of these limitations explicitly in Section 8, which mitigates the concern but does not eliminate it.

Additionally, the infinite schedules actually produce *worse* final validation loss on the 3×100B IID setting than repeated-cosine (2.59 vs. 2.53), though the downstream evaluation differences are small. Whether this gap would widen with distribution shifts or more datasets is unknown. The claim that infinite schedules "are a promising alternative" is accurate, but the claim that they "circumvent optimization difficulties" is only demonstrated for the specific difficulty of re-warming-induced forgetting spikes, not for the broader challenge of optimizing across distribution shifts.

**Overall assessment of experimental design.**

*Strengths:*
- The experimental design is internally consistent and carefully controlled. All comparisons use compute-equivalent replay, identical batch sizes, and identical model architectures, eliminating confounds that plague much continual learning research.
- The ablation structure is logical and comprehensive: warmup duration → re-warming η_max → replay percentage → final combination → scale-up → schedule alternatives. Each section builds on the previous one, and the chosen defaults (1% warmup, η_max = 3×10⁻⁴) are justified by explicit ablations.
- The inclusion of both weak and strong distribution shifts, and both 405M and 10B scales, provides evidence of generality within the study's scope.
- The explicit acknowledgment of limitations (Section 8) is thorough and honest, covering seed variation, missing deduplication, two-task focus, and infinite schedule limitations.

*Weaknesses:*
- **Single-seed experiments limit statistical interpretability.** The paper reports differences of 0.01–0.02 nats in average validation loss and 0.2–0.5 points in average evaluation accuracy as meaningful, but we cannot assess whether these are within seed-to-seed variation. The large batch size argument addresses optimizer noise but not initialization noise or data ordering effects. Running even a subset of the key comparisons (e.g., the union baseline vs. 5% replay at 405M) with 3 seeds would substantially strengthen the claims.
- **The downstream evaluation suite has limited signal at 405M scale.** Many benchmarks show accuracies clustered in narrow ranges (e.g., English evaluation averages of 33.95–35.16 across all weak-shift models; Table 10), with per-task differences of 1–3 points that could easily be noise. The German evaluation tasks (except HellaSwag-DE) show near-random performance for all models, making them uninformative. This weakens the paper's ability to make fine-grained claims about downstream performance.
- **No 10B strong-shift experiment.** The scaling claim ("across two model sizes and distribution shifts") is missing the intersection of large scale and strong shift. This is understandable given computational constraints but limits the generality of the finding.
- **Difficulty estimation for replay percentage is not systematically explored.** The paper recommends "experiment with different replay fractions for a limited token budget" (Section 2) but does not demonstrate that the optimal replay percentage can be identified from early-training signals. Figures 5 and 12 show that the relative ordering of replay percentages is visible early, but the paper does not provide a quantitative protocol for selecting the right percentage before committing to a full training run.
- **The "compute savings" are not explicitly quantified.** While Figure 1 shows bars representing "Compute Spent in Units of 300B Tokens," the paper never states the actual FLOPs or GPU-hours saved. The union baseline trains on 600B tokens from scratch; the continual model trains on 300B (already done) + 300B additional tokens. If we consider only the *additional* compute beyond the pre-trained checkpoint, the savings are ~50% (300B additional vs. 600B from scratch). But the paper does not make this arithmetic explicit, nor does it account for the cost of storing and serving the replay buffer.
- **Missing baselines.** The paper does not compare against any non-trivial continual learning baselines from the literature (e.g., EWC, SI, LwF, or more recent methods like LLaMA Pro). The authors' justification (Section 3) is that these methods were developed for smaller-scale settings and may not scale, but demonstrating this (even with a single representative method) would strengthen the paper's implicit claim that "simple is sufficient." Additionally, the union baseline uses the standard cosine schedule on the combined data; a more sophisticated data mixing curriculum could potentially outperform the continual approach, but this is not tested.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For and May Dominate the Deployment Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's compute-optimal framework relies on assigning each prompt to one of five difficulty quintiles before selecting the strategy. This is done by generating 2048 samples per question and computing either the ground-truth pass@1 rate (oracle difficulty) or the average PRM final-answer score (predicted difficulty). The authors state in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

The difficulty estimation cost—2048 generations × PRM scoring per question—is substantial: it equals or exceeds the test-time compute budgets being studied (which range from 1 to 512 generations in most experiments). This means the headline 4× efficiency gain over best-of-N is computed *after* difficulty is already known, without amortizing the cost of learning it.

**The consequence.** In a realistic deployment, the total cost is difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former may dominate the latter. A practitioner who follows the paper's recipe directly—estimating difficulty via 2048 samples per incoming prompt—would spend more compute on assessment than on optimization. The 4× efficiency figure should therefore be understood as an *upper bound on achievable efficiency* rather than a realized deployment gain. The framework is a proof-of-concept for difficulty-conditioned allocation, not a turnkey system. The authors acknowledge this explicitly but do not provide a practical solution, stating that better difficulty estimators are a "key avenue for future work."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** This limitation is structural, not something measured in a specific figure. The difficulty estimation protocol is described in Section 3.2, and its computational cost is noted there. No experiment in the paper accounts for this cost in any budget calculation—the compute budgets plotted on the x-axes of Figures 3, 4, 5, 8, and 9 represent only the strategy-execution cost, not the up-front difficulty estimation cost. The paper's argument that 2048 samples are enough to produce reliable difficulty estimates is itself evidence of the scale of the cost: 2048 generations is 8× the largest budget (256) studied in the search experiments (Section 5.3).

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as a limitation and suggests future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8). It also notes the connection to adaptive difficulty estimation that amortizes the cost into the problem-solving process (Section 3.2), but provides no experimental evaluation of any cheaper alternative. The predicted difficulty bins (using PRM scores instead of ground-truth labels) reduce the need for labeled data but do not reduce the generation cost—2048 samples are still required per question. This limitation remains entirely unresolved within the paper's scope.

---

### All Results Are on a Single Benchmark (MATH) with a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S*)

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire experimental evaluation is conducted on the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021)—specifically, the Lightman et al. (2022) split of 12,000 training questions and 500 test questions—using PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors state in Section 4 that they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is unverified by any cross-model or cross-domain experiment.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings may not transfer to other settings. The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S*'s specific output distribution—a model with different calibration properties, different error patterns, or a different architecture might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves and different optimal strategy allocations. The revision model's ability to learn from in-context incorrect examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families. MATH consists exclusively of competition-level math problems requiring symbolic, multi-step reasoning. It is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns—beam search hurting easy problems due to PRM over-optimization, sequential revisions dominating on easy problems but needing parallel diversity on hard ones—generalize to other reasoning domains (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA) or to tasks requiring factual recall rather than deduction.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All figures and tables report results on MATH with PaLM 2-S* (and, for the FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7, a ~14× larger PaLM 2 variant). The paper does not include any experiments on other benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K, HumanEval, MMLU reasoning subsets), other base models (e.g., LLaMA, Mistral, Gemma), or other task families. The test set of 500 questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation for strategy selection (~50 questions per fold per bin), means the compute-optimal policy is selected based on very small samples. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves, making it difficult to assess whether the observed gains are statistically reliable at this sample size or whether the specific strategies selected would transfer to a different MATH split.

**Mitigation status.** The authors acknowledge the single-benchmark limitation implicitly through their scope definition in Section 4, but they frame PaLM 2-S* as "representative" and do not explicitly flag generalizability as a concern. Section 8 suggests extending the framework to other domains as future work. The limitation is structural—re-running the full experimental suite (including PRM training, revision model training, search sweeps, and FLOPs-matched comparisons) on additional benchmarks or models would require a comparable computational investment, and the paper does not attempt even a small-scale validation on a second dataset.

---

### The Re-Training Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained, Potentially Weakening the FLOPs-Matched Comparison

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 compares PaLM 2-S* with compute-optimal test-time scaling against a model with approximately 14× more parameters, trained on the same data but with greedy decoding and no test-time compute augmentation. The larger model is scaled in parameters only, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023), rather than scaling both parameters and data equally as prescribed by Chinchilla-optimal training (Hoffmann et al., 2022). The authors acknowledge this:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work." (Section 7)

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs—scaling both parameters and data—would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model, making the pretraining baseline *weaker than it needs to be* for a fair comparison. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on easy questions at R ≪ 1 for revisions; Figure 1, top-right bar chart) may shrink or even reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model. Additionally, the larger model uses only greedy decoding—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search. This means the comparison is between a smaller model with heavily optimized inference and a larger model with no inference optimization at all. A fairer comparison would give the larger model a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or best-of-16), which could substantially close the gap. The paper's finding that test-time compute can "substitute" for pretraining is therefore conditional on the larger model being suboptimally trained and naïvely decoded.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FLOP accounting formulas in Section 7 explicitly assume parameter-only scaling: scaling parameters by M multiplies both pretraining FLOPs (X = 6ND_pretrain) and inference FLOPs (Y = 2ND_inference) by M. The ratio R = D_inference / D_pretrain is derived under this assumption. The paper does not include a comparison against a Chinchilla-optimal larger model, nor does it ablate different test-time compute budgets for the larger baseline to show how the advantage changes. The three R values tested (0.16, 0.79, 22) are based on the specific pretraining-to-inference token ratios of the models studied and may not generalize to other deployment scenarios.

**Mitigation status.** The authors acknowledge the departure from compute-optimal pretraining explicitly (Section 7) and frame the parameter-only scaling as "representative" rather than optimal. They note that the comparison to Chinchilla-optimal training is left to future work. This is a genuine limitation—the central claim that test-time compute can substitute for pretraining compute depends on the strength of the pretraining baseline, and the paper does not verify the claim against the strongest possible baseline.

---

### Verifier Over-Optimization Is a Hard Ceiling That the Compute-Optimal Policy Mitigates but Does Not Solve

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework relies on the PRM verifier to score candidate solutions and guide search. However, the paper documents that PRM scores become unreliable under aggressive optimization pressure: beam search degrades performance on easy problems at high budgets (Figure 3, right), lookahead search—the most powerful optimizer—paradoxically performs worst overall (Figure 3, left), and qualitative examples show search producing degenerate outputs (repetitive low-information steps, overly short solutions) that score highly under the PRM but are incorrect (Appendix M). The verifier was trained on i.i.d. samples from the base model, not on search-generated solutions, creating a distribution shift between the training data and the inputs seen during test-time optimization.

**The consequence.** The compute-optimal policy *mitigates* over-optimization by routing easy problems away from aggressive search (using best-of-N instead of beam search) but does not *eliminate* it. On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is deployed, over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling: the beam search curves in Figure 3 flatten and sometimes decline before the budget is exhausted. This means that further increasing the inference compute budget beyond a certain point provides no additional benefit (and may harm performance), regardless of how the budget is allocated. The ceiling is determined by verifier quality, not by search algorithm sophistication—a finding the paper itself makes (Section 8). The current results are therefore specific to the verifier quality achievable with the Monte Carlo rollout training procedure described in Appendix D. A weaker verifier would lower the ceiling; a stronger verifier would raise it. Without understanding how verifier quality interacts with the compute-optimal policy, the paper's specific strategy allocations (which search algorithm to use at which budget for which difficulty) cannot be assumed to transfer to other verifiers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (right) provides direct evidence: on difficulty bin 1 (easiest problems), beam search (M=4) accuracy *decreases* from ~78% at 4 generations to ~77% at 256 generations, while best-of-N weighted continues improving. Figure 3 (left) shows lookahead search underperforming all other methods at equal generation budgets. Appendix M (Figures 29 and surrounding discussion) provides qualitative examples of degenerate outputs, including solutions with repetitive non-informative steps and solutions truncated to 1-2 steps that happen to score highly. The paper's analysis in Section 5.3 explicitly identifies these as over-optimization failures. However, the paper does not measure what fraction of the 500-question test set is affected by over-optimization, does not quantify the performance ceiling as a function of verifier quality, and does not ablate verifier training to show how improvements would shift the optimal policy.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies over-optimization as "the primary bottleneck" for further scaling test-time compute (Section 8) and suggests building more robust verifiers—through adversarial training, ensemble methods, or constrained search—as the key research direction. The compute-optimal policy can be seen as a way to *stay below* the over-optimization threshold per difficulty level, but the threshold itself remains a hard constraint. The paper provides no solution for improving verifier robustness within its own experimental framework, nor does it characterize how much improvement would be needed to substantially raise the scaling ceiling for medium and hard problems.

### Hard Problems Show Near-Zero Improvement Regardless of Budget, Establishing a Fundamental Capability Boundary

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's framework assumes that the base model's proposal distribution already contains correct solutions at some non-trivial rate—the difficulty bins are defined by pass@1, and bins 1–4 all have non-zero pass@1 rates. Difficulty bin 5 represents problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero. The paper explicitly states that test-time compute "amplifies existing capability but does not create it" (Section 7 takeaway box).

**The consequence.** For genuinely hard problems outside the base model's capability range, **no amount of test-time compute helps.** Across all methods in the paper—search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations—bin 5 accuracy remains near 0–5% regardless of compute budget (Figure 3 right: 1–3% across all methods and budgets; Figure 7 right: 2–3% regardless of sequential-to-parallel ratio; Figure 9: the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat). This is a hard boundary: test-time compute cannot compensate for capabilities the model fundamentally lacks. For a practitioner, this means that if the problem distribution includes many genuinely hard problems, continual pre-training or scaling model size is the only viable approach—compute-optimal test-time scaling offers no path forward. This is a more fundamental limitation than the verifier over-optimization issue, because improving verifier quality would not solve it—the problem is that correct solutions do not exist in the proposal distribution at all, so no verifier can find them.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-bin analyses consistently show bin 5 as the failure case. Figure 3 (right): bin 5 accuracy is flat at 1–3% across 4, 16, 64, and 256 generations for both beam search and best-of-N. Figure 7 (right): bin 5 accuracy is ~2–3% regardless of the sequential-to-parallel ratio at 128 generations. Figure 9: the bin 5 scaling line is near 0–5% for both revisions and PRM search, and is always below the 14× larger model's greedy performance. Table 10 similarly shows bin 5 performance near the floor. The paper explicitly states this limitation in the Section 7 takeaway box and in the individual-difficulty discussions throughout Section 5.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this limitation and does not attempt to mitigate it. It is a fundamental property of the proposal distribution—if the base model cannot produce correct answers at any rate, the search-and-verification framework has nothing to work with. The paper's contribution is precisely to *characterize* this boundary: it shows *where* test-time compute works (problems within the model's reach) and *where* it fails (problems outside the model's reach). Section 8 suggests that for hard problems, "pretraining remains the only viable path," which is consistent with the evidence. The lack of a mitigation for this limitation is not a weakness of the paper—the paper's goal is to understand test-time compute scaling, and discovering its boundary conditions is a contribution, not a failure.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around LLM updating from a **re-training default** to a **continual pre-training default** for large incoming datasets. Before this work, the dominant practice was to combine old and new datasets and train from random initialization—an approach implicitly assumed by scaling laws work (Hoffmann et al., 2022) and followed by nearly all major open-source LLM releases. The paper demonstrates that this is unnecessary: for distribution shifts ranging from English→English (weak) to English→German (strong), a simple combination of learning rate re-warming, re-decaying, and replay matches the performance of full re-training while requiring substantially less compute (Figure 1 shows the union baseline expending ~2.0 units of compute vs. ~1.0 additional units for continual pre-training from an existing checkpoint).

This is not a paradigm shift—it is an **empirical reframing with immediate practical consequences.** The techniques themselves (re-warming, replay) have existed in various forms in the continual learning literature for years. What the paper contributes is the systematic demonstration, at LLM scale (up to 10B parameters) and on massive datasets (200B–300B tokens per phase), that these simple techniques are **sufficient**—no architectural modifications, no importance-weighted parameter protection, no growing model capacity, and no complex data selection strategies are needed. The Rules of Thumb section distills this into actionable guidance: re-warm from `O(η_max)` to `O(η_min)`, experiment with replay fractions early in training where their relative effects are already visible, and consider infinite schedules if re-warming pathologies are a concern.

A subtle but important conceptual shift is the paper's identification that **forgetting has an optimization component distinct from distribution shift.** Section 7.1 (Figure 8) demonstrates that re-warming the learning rate causes a measurable validation loss degradation *even when training on identical data*—a finding that reframes part of the stability-plasticity dilemma as a schedule artifact rather than an inevitable consequence of learning new information. This diagnostic insight has implications beyond this paper: any method that periodically re-increases the learning rate (cyclical schedules, SGDR-style cosine restarts, some forms of curriculum learning) may incur *optimization-induced forgetting* that is distinct from knowledge interference. Researchers studying continual learning at scale should distinguish these mechanisms, as they require different solutions (schedule design vs. replay or regularization).

The paper also reconciles a latent tension in the continual learning literature. On one side, work on domain-adaptive pre-training (Gururangan et al., 2020; Ke et al., 2022) suggests that sophisticated techniques—importance masking, data selection, parameter isolation—are needed for effective continual learning. On the other side, Scialom et al. (2022) find that autoregressive LLMs are naturally good continual learners, with even 1% replay being highly effective for fine-tuning. This paper provides the bridge: in the *pre-training* regime with hundreds of billions of tokens per dataset, replay is still highly effective (even 1% provides dramatic forgetting reduction; Figure 5), but the learning rate schedule becomes the critical additional ingredient—without re-warming/re-decaying, adaptation is poor regardless of replay. This **two-factor model of continual pre-training success** (schedule × replay) resolves the apparent contradiction by showing that both prior findings were partially correct but incomplete: replay works, but schedule design is equally essential at pre-training scale.

Research directions that become **more attractive** after this work:
- **Infinite schedule development and stress-testing.** The paper demonstrates viability in the no-shift case (Figure 10) but leaves distribution shifts, very long constant-LR phases, and stability at larger scales unexplored. This is a tractable follow-up with clear evaluation criteria.
- **Replay buffer construction strategies.** The paper uses simple sequential replay from the original data order and finds it sufficient. However, the strong-shift results (requiring 25% replay vs. 5% for weak shifts) suggest that smarter buffer construction—curating replay data to maximize forgetting reduction per token replayed—could dramatically improve efficiency for extreme distribution shifts.
- **Tokenizer adaptation for strong distribution shifts.** The paper acknowledges that the Pile-trained tokenizer may be a bottleneck for German (Section 5.2), and the qualitative evaluation (Appendix A.5) shows grammatical but repetitive German outputs. Extending continual pre-training to include tokenizer updates alongside model updates is a natural next step.
- **Continual pre-training of instruction-tuned or RLHF-trained models.** All experiments use base models without alignment. Whether the same techniques work after supervised fine-tuning or RLHF—where the model's capabilities are more brittle and forgetting may manifest as safety degradation rather than just perplexity increase—is an open and important question.

Research directions that become **less attractive** after this work:
- **Domain-incremental continual pre-training** (Appendix A.1). The paper explicitly shows that consuming data one domain at a time, with per-domain re-warming and replay, performs substantially worse than simply mixing all domains. This suggests that the domain-incremental setting, while popular in the continual learning literature, may be an unnecessarily difficult framing for LLM pre-training—and that practitioners should strongly prefer mixed-data approaches when possible.
- **Architectural expansion methods** (e.g., LLaMA Pro; Wu et al., 2024) for the specific problem of dataset updates within a similar distribution. If simple replay + LR re-warming achieves comparable results without growing the model, the case for more complex architectural interventions weakens. However, for the case of adding entirely new modalities or languages with distinct tokenizers, architectural expansion may still be valuable—this paper does not test those settings.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Infinite learning rate schedules tested on distribution shifts at scale.** The paper's infinite schedule experiments (Section 7.4) are limited to 405M parameters with IID splits of SlimPajama—no distribution shift, no strong-shift analogue. The critical unanswered question is whether a constant `η_const` (set to ~0.55 × `η_max` in the paper) provides sufficient adaptation when the data distribution changes substantially. A strong follow-up would replicate the weak-shift (Pile → SlimPajama) and strong-shift (Pile → German) settings from Sections 6.3–6.4 using infinite schedules, comparing against the cosine re-warming baseline on both validation loss and downstream benchmarks. The prediction: infinite schedules should eliminate the transient forgetting spike at dataset boundaries (since they avoid re-warming) but may adapt more slowly to the new distribution (since `η_const` is lower than `η_max`). A negative result—substantially worse adaptation on strong shifts—would clarify that re-warming, despite its pathologies, is *necessary* for efficient adaptation when the distribution changes, and would reframe infinite schedules as useful only for weak-shift or IID-continuation settings.

**Replay buffer construction informed by gradient similarity or loss sensitivity.** The paper uses uniform replay in the original data order and finds it effective. However, the strong-shift results show that even 25% replay does not fully close the Pile loss gap to the union baseline (2.33 vs. 2.26; Table 3), while 50% replay achieves comparable Pile loss (2.24) but at significant cost to German adaptation (1.22 vs. 1.11 for no-replay). This suggests a **quality-efficiency tension** in replay buffer construction: some replayed tokens may provide more forgetting protection than others. A concrete follow-up would compute per-sample gradient similarity between Pile and German data (or between Pile and SlimPajama) during an initial calibration phase, then construct a replay buffer that prioritizes samples whose gradients are most orthogonal to the new dataset's gradients—following the intuition from Ramasesh et al. (2022) that more orthogonal representations reduce interference. The evaluation would measure whether 5% or 10% intelligent replay can match the forgetting reduction of 25% uniform replay, directly improving the compute-efficiency of continual pre-training for strong shifts.

**Continual tokenizer expansion for cross-lingual or cross-modal updates.** The paper's strong-shift (English→German) setting uses an English-only tokenizer trained on the Pile. This is acknowledged as a potential bottleneck, and the qualitative evaluation (Appendix A.5) shows that while German-trained models produce grammatically correct German, the text is repetitive and sometimes semantically incoherent—consistent with a tokenizer that fragments German words into suboptimal subword units. A natural follow-up is to include tokenizer adaptation in the continual pre-training pipeline: when transitioning to German, expand the vocabulary with the most frequent German subword units (following the approach of Gee et al., 2022 or similar), initialize the new embedding rows appropriately, and continue pre-training with the same LR re-warming + replay recipe. The key metric would be whether tokenizer adaptation improves German downstream performance (HellaSwag-DE, MMLU-DE) beyond the modest gains observed in the paper, and whether it introduces any new forgetting artifacts due to the abrupt embedding expansion.

**Scaling laws for continual pre-training: predicting forgetting and adaptation from dataset similarity, replay percentage, and model size.** The paper provides point estimates: 5% replay works for weak shifts at 405M and 10B, 25% replay works for strong shifts at 405M. But a practitioner facing a new distribution shift (e.g., adding code to an English model, or adding a third language) has no principled way to predict the required replay percentage without running experiments. A scaling-law-style study—varying model size (100M through 10B), dataset similarity (controlled via n-gram overlap or embedding similarity), replay percentage, and `η_max`—could produce predictive equations analogous to Hernandez et al. (2021)'s scaling laws for transfer. The specific finding that larger models forget slightly less (10B model forgets 0.23 nats without replay vs. 0.27 for 405M on the weak shift; Table 4) suggests a scaling trend that could be extrapolated. A negative result—no smooth scaling relationship, or strong dependence on dataset-specific factors that cannot be captured by simple similarity metrics—would itself be informative, indicating that continual pre-training strategy selection will remain empirical for the foreseeable future.

**Continual pre-training after alignment: do safety and instruction-following capabilities survive?** All experiments in this paper use base models (no instruction tuning, no RLHF). The forgetting metrics are validation loss and standard benchmark accuracy. However, the most valuable models in production are instruction-tuned and aligned, and forgetting there may manifest in qualitatively different and more dangerous ways—loss of refusal behaviors, degradation of instruction-following precision, or re-emergence of harmful generation patterns that were suppressed during alignment. A critical follow-up would replicate the paper's weak-shift experiment (Pile → SlimPajama) starting from an instruction-tuned checkpoint (e.g., LLaMA-2-Chat or Mistral-Instruct) and evaluate not just on standard benchmarks but on safety-relevant metrics (refusal rate, toxicity, hallucination frequency). The key question is whether replay of pre-alignment data is sufficient to preserve alignment, or whether the replay buffer must include instruction-tuning data (which may introduce complications around data diversity and quality). The paper's finding that even 5% replay dramatically reduces forgetting on pre-training metrics (Figure 5) provides reason for optimism, but the stakes are higher for aligned models.

**Diagnosing and mitigating the re-warming pathology at scale.** Section 7.1 provides a clean demonstration that re-warming causes forgetting on identical data, but the paper does not investigate the mechanism beyond showing the phenomenon. A mechanistic follow-up could probe *what* is being forgotten during re-warming: are specific attention heads being disrupted? Are certain layers more sensitive than others? Does the forgetting concentrate in the embedding layer or in deeper representations? Concrete experiments would include: (1) per-layer probing of representation quality before and after re-warming using linear classifiers on Pile-domain tasks; (2) measuring the angular change in attention head outputs during the transient forgetting spike; (3) testing whether freezing certain layers during the warmup phase mitigates the spike without sacrificing long-term adaptation. Understanding the mechanism would inform better schedule designs—for example, if the forgetting is concentrated in early layers, one could use layer-specific learning rate warmup that increases the LR gradually for early layers while keeping later layers stable until the model has re-adapted.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Efficiently updating deployed LLMs on new web-scrape data.** Organizations that maintain LLMs trained on large web corpora (e.g., CommonCrawl-based datasets) face a recurring update problem: each new crawl contains fresher, often higher-quality data, but retraining from scratch on the combined corpus is prohibitively expensive. This paper's weak-shift results directly address this scenario—SlimPajama represents a newer, better-filtered version of the same data sources as the Pile. The recipe is straightforward: load the existing model checkpoint, set the LR to re-warm to the original `η_max` and re-decay over the new dataset's token budget, use 5% replay of the old dataset, and train. The paper shows this matches the union-trained baseline's average evaluation accuracy (35.14 vs. 34.30 at 405M; 47.68 vs. 48.00 at 10B; Table 5) while processing the new data independently of the old—no need to re-access or re-process the entire original corpus alongside the new data. The compute savings grow with each update cycle: the first update saves ~50% of a full re-train (300B additional tokens vs. 600B from scratch), and subsequent updates are even more efficient relative to cumulative re-training costs.

**Adding a new language to an existing English LLM.** For organizations expanding model coverage to new languages, the strong-shift results provide a concrete protocol even when the shift is substantial. The paper's German experiments show that 25% replay of English data during the German training phase preserves English capabilities (32.48 average English evaluation vs. 33.95 for the English-only baseline; Table 3) while acquiring German language capabilities (HellaSwag-DE: 31.04 vs. 27.09 for English-only; Table 3). The qualitative evaluation (Appendix A.5) confirms that the model learns German grammar, syntax, and character conventions, even though the tokenizer was not adapted. The practical value is clear: rather than training a multilingual model from scratch on the combined English+German corpus—which requires engineering a new data mixture, re-tuning hyperparameters, and paying the full pre-training cost—a team can start from a strong English checkpoint and add German in a separate phase, using the replay recipe to prevent English degradation.

**Cost-efficient self-improvement loops for data curation.** The paper's findings enable a practical continual pre-training cycle: train a model, use it to help curate or filter the next dataset iteration, then update the model on the improved data without starting over. This is already happening in practice: Glorioso et al. (2024) and DeepSeek-AI et al. (2024) both cite this paper and apply its techniques (LR re-warming, replay) for continual pre-training phases. The efficiency argument is compelling: if each dataset iteration improves quality by, say, 5%, and the alternative is full re-training each cycle, continual pre-training with 5% replay reduces the marginal compute cost per iteration dramatically. The paper's results suggest that even after multiple update cycles—provided the data distribution doesn't shift catastrophically—performance should track the union-trained baseline closely, though the N > 2 case is only preliminarily explored in the IID no-shift setting (Figure 10).

**Academic and open-source model maintenance.** For research groups and open-source collectives that release LLMs, the ability to update models on new data without re-training from scratch lowers the barrier to maintaining fresh releases. A model released today on a 2023 web corpus can be updated in 2025 on the 2025 corpus using the same compute that would have purchased only a 50% larger pre-training run (since the continual update costs ~300B tokens of training vs. 600B from scratch for the union model). This is particularly valuable in the open-source ecosystem, where compute budgets are limited and models quickly become stale as the underlying data ages. The paper's explicit availability of checkpoints and code (via GPT-NeoX pull requests) makes the recipe immediately reproducible for any group that can afford pre-training-scale compute.
