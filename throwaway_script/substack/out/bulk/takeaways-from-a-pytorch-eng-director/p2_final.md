Here are the takeaways from this John Myles White interview (Engineering Director on PyTorch at Meta/MSL who recently quit, offering candid takes on promo culture, big tech's shift, and how to build a career on engineering excellence):

**The PyTorch org as a counter-cultural pocket**

White describes the PyTorch team at Meta as an anomaly inside big tech—an org where engineering excellence was genuinely prioritized over promotion-driven behavior. Because of this, engineers were "almost always under leveled" relative to their peers elsewhere in the company. The trade-off was slower career progression, but the payoff was substantial: engineers from that org were more skilled and had better external career mobility because "people respected their levels more." Interviewers and hiring managers outside the org understood that a PyTorch E6 had done real, difficult systems work at a higher bar than the same title elsewhere. This became a deliberate, if implicit, strategy for talent—and an example of how choosing the right pocket of a large company can shield you from the worst incentive distortions.

White's own career mirrors this philosophy. His background is in statistics and programming languages (he did a PhD focused on statistical computing), and he joined Facebook specifically to work on the intersection of ML infrastructure and developer tools. He deliberately avoided chasing roles in product orgs where promo velocity was the primary currency. His philosophy: **your main power is where you choose to work**. Even when promotion is the system's default incentive, there are teams that care more about craft, and betting on those teams—even if your title lags—builds deeper, more portable expertise.

**Promo culture and its distortions**

White is blunt about how deeply promotion incentives warp engineering behavior. At Meta in particular, he observed that "promo culture became so strong that every single engineer he'd meet had the primary goal of promotion." This created a series of predictable pathologies. Engineers would ship software they planned to delete weeks later, solely because the launch itself satisfied a "promo bar" and the cleanup was someone else's problem. Projects would be made **detrimentally complex**—layering on unnecessary abstractions, introducing microservices where a simple library would do—because complexity signals "scope" in a promotion packet. White is clear: this produces genuinely bad software that exists only to fulfill career incentives, not user needs.

The worst version of this, he says, was orgs **cannibalizing each other by promising faster promotion paths**: a manager in one org would poach engineers from another by saying "come over here, you'll get your next level in two halves instead of four," draining stable teams and leaving abandoned work behind. White notes that none of this is malicious at the individual level—engineers were responding rationally to the system they were in. But the aggregate effect was enormous wasted effort. He draws a contrast with the PyTorch org, where promotion was slower but work was judged by whether the code and systems actually held up over time. In that environment, the kind of short-lived, scope-inflating behavior that dominated elsewhere simply didn't survive peer review.

**How employee power has weakened since COVID**

Ryan asks White about the shift he's observed in big tech's relationship with its engineers. White points to a clear before-and-after: pre-COVID, large tech companies cared deeply about retention, even when it came at employer expense. Perks were lavish, internal mobility was easy, and managers had strong incentives to keep their people happy because losing an engineer was expensive and culturally frowned upon. Post-COVID, and especially in the era of AI-assisted productivity, "the sense is there are too many software engineers, and maybe way too many if you count AI costs and productivity gains."

This shift has direct downstream effects on how management behaves. In reorgs, for instance, retention used to be a major constraint on bold decisions—leadership had to worry about who would quit if a team was restructured or a project was cancelled. Now, if retention doesn't particularly matter, management can make much more aggressive, employee-unfriendly calls without sweating the fallout. White sees this as a structural change, not a cyclical one: the power balance between individual contributors and the companies they work for has tilted decisively toward the employer, and he doesn't expect it to swing back soon.

**How to avoid playing the game**

Given these dynamics, White's advice for engineers is pragmatic rather than idealistic. You can't opt out of the incentives entirely, but you can **choose where you place yourself inside the machine**. The PyTorch org is his primary case study: a team that deliberately focused on engineering first, accepted slower promotions as a cost, and in return produced engineers who were more skilled and had careers that outlasted any single promo cycle. The first-order benefit was job satisfaction—people liked building things well—but the second-order benefit was real career resilience. When those engineers eventually left Meta, they had a reputation that their level meant something, which gave them leverage in the external market that many higher-titled peers from promo-optimized orgs lacked.

White is careful not to frame this as a morality tale. He doesn't think the promo-driven engineers are wrong; they're playing the game as it's presented to them. But he does think there's a quieter path that trades fast titles for durable craft, and that the choice between them is the most consequential decision an engineer makes inside a large company. Pick a team that values the thing you want to be valued for. The rest—title velocity, peer comparison, the anxiety of "falling behind"—becomes less relevant when your work justifies itself on different terms.

---

White's career philosophy orbits around a single insight: in a system where promo incentives dominate, **your main power is where you choose to work**. The PyTorch org was his proof that you can find pockets where craft still matters, even if it means being "almost always under leveled." The pathologies he names—**detrimentally complex** projects built for scope, and orgs **cannibalizing each other** over promo velocity—are downstream of a system that rewards signaling over substance. His bet, and his advice, is that opting out of that particular race produces not just better work, but a career that's more portable, more respected, and ultimately more resilient when the employer-employee power balance shifts against you.
