Here are the takeaways from this Adrien Friggeri interview (went from new grad to Principal Engineer / IC8 at Meta, originally started Bento, the internal notebook platform):

**First team at Facebook (Data Science)**

He was pre-allocated to the early "Data Science" team (~20 people) because he had a PhD in social network analysis. He stresses this was *old* data science—statisticians and computer scientists *building* the A/B testing platform and data pipelines ("at the end of the day we were just doing big counting"), not today's data scientists who are mostly *users* of those tools doing product analytics. (Around 2014 the "product analytics" role got renamed to "data scientist," ballooning from ~20-30 PhDs to ~500 people—an identity crisis the team moved past, because "the title was not the important part, the impact was.")

A formative moment: in his first one-on-one he pretentiously told his manager Cameron Marlow "I have a PhD, I'm not a software engineer," and Marlow corrected him—"software engineers have power at this company. You are a software engineer. Don't pigeonhole yourself into a category or an area that is not the most important job of the company." He's considered himself a software engineer ever since. His early work analyzed how *rumors* (memes, Snopes-type stuff, not fake news) spread on social networks, finding that **false rumors spread faster than true ones**—his explanation is they're more egregious and thus inherently more shareable/viral ("true things tend to be more boring than false things"). After ~3 papers and tweaking newsfeed reshare weights, his manager said "now go figure out something impactful for the company."

**Senior promo (IC5) via Instagram A/B testing**

He met Mike Krieger and started helping the tiny early Instagram team (~30 core people) add personalization to the Explore tab and measure growth/conversions. He saw Instagram needed A/B testing, but Krieger and Systrom said "we have bigger fish to fry right now" (mid-migration off AWS to Facebook infra). So he just built it anyway—roping in a PM (Jeff Canter) for air cover, doing "the dumbest, hackiest, probably very expensive thing": a custom Facebook API endpoint allow-listed on the Instagram app to log exposures over the internet from AWS, plus ~50-100 lines to hash users into buckets, connected to the existing data warehouse. He proved it with a server-side A/A test, found a mobile engineer to help, ran small experiments, and it scaled. Come performance season his manager said "congratulations, we’re promoting you to IC5"—and he had **no idea promotions even existed**. He picked the project not because it'd be successful but because he *needed* it for something else he wanted to build.

Ryan notes the standout *agency* this early—walking up to the CTO and random engineers. Adrien attributes it to Facebook's intensely bottoms-up, hackathon-heavy early culture (he did ~6 hackathons in his first six months). His biggest career *regret*: never officially joining the Instagram team (he stayed on Data Science as an embedded "consultant" doing 6-month-to-2-year stints, which gave him the rare luxury of only ~2 managers in his first few years). It's a "didn't buy Bitcoin in 2013" flavor of regret—peers who joined Instagram then hit IC7/8/9/10 far sooner—though he's careful to say he's happy where he landed; it's just "a road not taken."

**Senior Staff (IC7/TLM2) via Bento**

While roped into a project to count users of a Facebook property (~2015), he was frustrated constantly switching between a data-pipelining framework and the ML inference framework (FBLearner Flow). He built helper scripts so his code could live in one place, then realized he could kill the repetitive boilerplate—turning a 300-line canonical data pipeline into ~20 lines with an escape hatch for config. Hooked on making people's lives easier, he noticed data scientists/ML engineers wasted enormous time manually setting up their own tools (half on Jupyter, half on RStudio, following ten outdated wikis). He told his manager in February: "I'm gonna take two to three months to just hack on this. If it doesn't pan out by April, I'll find another project to save the half." That became **Bento**, the Jupyter-based notebook platform used across the company—starting as a dumb setup script but with a vision of deep integration (type "Bento" in a browser, get a notebook preloaded with company libraries; on-demand instances instead of maintained dev servers). He moved into a TLM (tech-lead-manager) role and built a team of 6-8, hiring people who were *already* building tooling on the side ("do you want this to be your full-time job?").

His **two-part adoption playbook** is a key insight (and notably *different* from the "convince people to switch" model): (1) **Ride the growth wave**—they targeted Bootcamp/DataCamp (onboarding) so every newcomer set up their dev environment via Bento; in a company doubling in size, you get growth from newcomers who don't know legacy systems, plus stragglers pulled along by network effects. (2) **Provide value/support to legacy-system users first**—they staffed the previously-unmonitored Jupyter support group, built trust, then gently suggested Bento. He also went to leadership (Alex Schultz, then head of analytics) who validated it as "a good first step" among many problems, and he reorged himself into Dev Infra to start a team dedicated to data-science/ML developer tools.

**Taking on performance risk**

He'd explicitly told his manager he'd deviate from the team's actual job (data consulting) to explore tooling, with a pre-agreed "if it doesn't pan out, well, I was expected to go explore this." His advice on risk-taking: the strategy is the same as ever—**get aligned with your hierarchy first** (you're evaluated on expectations, so align on them), **over-communicate**, and **be ready to hear no** (he's been told no on later ideas). The far riskier move is locking yourself in a room for three months and emerging with "I did this thing" when no one knew. He also notes the *consequences* of underperforming are more drastic now than a decade ago, even if the strategy is unchanged. On becoming a TLM: it wasn't a grand "I want to manage" plan—the team needed more people and a manager, and rather than hire someone above him with a different vision, he took it on himself (a "natural, not really thought-out" evolution).

**Leaving Meta (the startup that became a sabbatical)**

After ~7 years (his only employer post-PhD), with "a little bit of hubris" from Bento being used by 60% of all data people at the company, he left to build it externally ("if Facebook needed this in 2018, the world is going to need that in 2020"). But he realized he *wasn't an entrepreneur*: he loved going deep into technical problems, and being a founder means **delegating the hard stuff and scaling through others**—whenever something got interesting he'd have to hand it off and stay superficial, which he didn't want. He'd also underestimated how much his team of 6 had spent two years building on mature infrastructure. "I left the company to do a startup, and I ended up taking a sabbatical instead"—traveled the world, built prototypes, nothing crystallized.

**Boomerang #1, Clubhouse, Boomerang #2**

COVID hit, he impulsively bought a house in Denver, needed a stable job, and returned to Meta—drawn back specifically by a manager he respected (James Pierce). Seven months later a friend connected him to Clubhouse during its viral peak (late 2020/early 2021); he joined at ~10-11 people. He built their data stack, brought A/B testing (as he had at Instagram), and added ML to filter Clubhouse's spammy notifications. The culture was hands-on, hungry, talent-dense (ex-Coinbase, a16z, Facebook). Then he left Clubhouse, did a thorough job search (even seriously considered an Apple offer), and went *back* to Meta a second time—drawn by another respected past manager, Vincent Hardy, engineering director on **Ray-Ban Stories**, offering a chance to pivot away from being "the data guy" into product/hardware.

**Principal (IC8) on smart glasses**

He came back as an IC7 (not entrusted with a big role from the start). Post-launch Ray-Ban Stories had quality/growth issues, so he spent ~8-12 months on its growth story and data—*building trust* with the org. After a late-2022 reorg, he told his manager the whole reason he joined was to work on smart glasses, not be the data guy, and asked to move onto product work despite lacking hardware experience ("you've seen my track record of the last year. I've developed this product sense"). He gained scope over the next couple of years, eventually responsible for *all user experiences* on a specific (unreleased) device—coordinating across dozens of teams and ~35 PMs, doing architecture and milestone delivery on the experiences layer (AI, music, photo capture, companion app—everything except the OS/system layers owned by partners). Growing pains: the hardware *cadence* was hard (very long lead times, little user signal until internal dogfooding/fishfooding began).

His advice for **ramping in a new domain** mirrors ramping at a new company (the "BOSS" social-network-exploration strategy): talk to people, ask who else to talk to, **don't be afraid to ask dumb questions early** (everyone knows you're new—better a silly question now than confusion in six months), check out the code even if you won't work on it (he tweaked firmware just to understand it), and **pick a hard problem to solve** where you have a rough idea, then fill in the blanks—"that's where I learn."

**How the IC8 promo actually happened**

His promotions escalated in effort: IC5 was a *surprise*; IC6 just took asking his manager; IC7 took a couple of cycles/revs; IC8 was real work. He co-wrote the promo packet with a manager who'd never done an IC8 promo (he wrote a substantial chunk), and crucially **built consensus ahead of time**—going to multiple VPs early ("is there IC8 scope here? would you support this?") and identifying a D2 who'd be critical, asking him for a *monthly* check-in to course-correct, so that six months later the D2 was already bought in. The first cycle his manager didn't even formally submit—just "felt the water" so people had context; the next cycle, with the work plus pre-built support, it went through. The key lesson: **identify your champions and align everyone *before* the calibration room** so there's no debate there. But he's emphatic this **isn't just a social game**—you need the underlying impact *and* the consensus-building; consensus without real work is nothing.

**Creating vs. securing scope (and "stealing scope")**

Two models in his own career: Bento was scope he *created from scratch* without permission; the smart-glasses Uber-TL-for-experiences role was scope within a constrained structure. There *was* already an Uber TL for the whole program (Sung Yu), so Adrien identified the experiences/product/design area, built trust, and *took it off Sung Yu's plate*—running a tight ship there so Sung Yu could focus on the broader program and only coordinate through Adrien. The "TL for experiences" label came *post hoc*, after he'd done the work for ~a year, just so he could "explain who I am in a sentence." His strong stance: **"stealing scope" is the wrong frame**—taking something off someone's plate is only stealing if you *don't talk to them*. Over-communicate ("do you mind if I help with this?"), and it becomes win-win: the other person offloads work and broadens their own scope (and gets credit for growing you). He's done the reverse too—pushing *junior* engineers to take and own things ("it's gonna be good for you, and I also would love to be able to delegate this to you").

**Being a well-rounded ("product hybrid") engineer**

He attributes his unique opportunities to being well-rounded—strong software *and* data, plus deliberately developed product and design sense. Being able to have a deep product conversation with PM leaders one day and debug firmware the next is where he adds value. He cites Heinlein's "specialization is for insects" and builds little prototypes / designs his own circuit boards (not production-ready) precisely because being able to *make a thing people can play with* starts conversations with product leads.

**Maximizing your luck with people**

A through-line: his career is "a series of bumping into the right people"—someone almost always brought him along or was instrumental in a promo-driving project. His tips: (1) **None of it happens in a vacuum**—he had rough edges and strong opinions early that didn't serve him, but learned "we're all in this to succeed, and the best way to succeed is to help others succeed; a rising tide lifts all boats." Build relationships of trust and respect (not necessarily friendship) *without* expecting anything in return—be a good, trustworthy person and you'll reap the benefits eventually. (2) **Don't be afraid to ask**—he's *never* applied for a job on a website; every move came through someone he knew. (3) **Don't be afraid to hear no or get no reply** (people are busy). (4) **Maximize your "surface area for luck"**—take more shots, more land.

**Advice to his younger self**

"Be a good person. Invest in things that are fun. Invest in your relationship with your coworkers. It doesn't really matter at the end of the day who gets the credit—everyone is gonna get credit for something." Early on he was very credit-driven and impatient (pushing for the IC7 promo before he was ready, which then took a couple of halves). Be patient, stay curious, and enjoy it—"you're gonna have an amazing adventure."
