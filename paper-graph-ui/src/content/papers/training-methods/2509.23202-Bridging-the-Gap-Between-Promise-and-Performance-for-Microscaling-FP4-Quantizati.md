# Bridging the Gap Between Promise and Performance for Microscaling FP4 Quantization

**ArXiv:** [2509.23202](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23202)

## 🎯 Pitch

Rotations, a staple for suppressing outliers in integer quantization, provably *degrade* accuracy on NVIDIA’s NVFP4 format due to its small block size—yet the same rotations are essential to salvage the new MXFP4 standard. The authors resolve this contradiction with MR-GPTQ, a format-aware algorithm that unlocks near-lossless 4-bit inference and delivers up to 4× speedups on next-gen GPUs.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper provides the first comprehensive empirical study of the recently hardware-accelerated MXFP4 and NVFP4 microscaling 4-bit floating-point formats for post-training quantization of LLMs, revealing that state-of-the-art PTQ methods struggle with FP4 due to NVFP4's small group size neutralizing outlier mitigation and MXFP4's power-of-two scale quantization inducing severe accuracy degradation. To bridge this gap, the authors introduce **Micro-Rotated-GPTQ (MR-GPTQ)**, a variant of GPTQ tailored to FP4's unique properties through block-wise Hadamard transforms, format-specific scale optimization, and static activation reordering, supported by the QuTLASS kernel library that fuses online activation rotations with negligible overhead. MR-GPTQ boosts MXFP4 accuracy to within 1–2% of NVFP4—achieving up to 98–99% of FP16 baseline recovery on large models—while delivering 2.2× end-to-end inference speedup on NVIDIA B200 and 4× on RTX5090, establishing that FP4 can recover near-lossless accuracy only when quantization algorithms are explicitly designed for microscaling format properties rather than naively ported from integer quantization workflows.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Hardware-Accelerated FP4 Formats Arrived Before the Algorithms Did

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is a **hardware-algorithm gap**: NVIDIA's Blackwell architecture and AMD's CDNA4 architecture now natively support 4-bit floating-point matrix multiplications via the microscaling MXFP4 and NVFP4 formats, yet the quantization community has essentially no systematic understanding of how to actually use these formats effectively. This isn't a minor practical detail — it's a complete inversion of the usual hardware-algorithm development sequence. Normally, algorithmic innovations in quantization mature first, and then hardware catches up (as happened with INT8 and FP8 formats). Here, it's the opposite: the hardware is already deployed in production GPUs, while the algorithms to achieve acceptable accuracy with these formats are essentially nonexistent.

The authors frame this gap through a stark empirical observation in Section 1:

> "Despite these developments, little is known about the accuracy of these formats on real models or their practical performance."

This gap matters for several concrete reasons that the paper makes explicit:

- **Production deployment is already happening**: NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs (B200, RTX5090) ship with FP4 tensor core support, and AMD's upcoming CDNA4 architecture includes MXFP4 support. Organizations deploying inference on these chips have immediate decisions to make about whether and how to use FP4 quantization. Without rigorous accuracy data, they're flying blind.

- **The industry narrative claims FP4 superiority over INT4**: As the authors note (Section 1), hardware vendors have positioned FP4 formats as more accurate than INT4 for quantization — a claim that, prior to this work, had never been systematically validated or challenged. NVIDIA's own marketing material claims FP4 "achieves better overall throughput and accuracy than INT4" (Mehta et al., 2025, cited in the paper). If this claim is wrong or conditional, practitioners need to know.

- **Bit-width reduction from FP16 to FP4 is massive**: Going from 16 bits per parameter to 4 bits represents a 4× reduction in memory footprint and (ideally) compute. For large models like Llama-3.3-70B, this is the difference between fitting on a single GPU and requiring multiple GPUs. Even moderate accuracy losses might be acceptable if the speedup is real and the accuracy-degradation behavior is well-characterized. But without characterization, practitioners can't make informed decisions.

- **The existing PTQ ecosystem is entirely INT-centric**: As the authors emphasize in their related work discussion (Section 2), "Prior work investigating accuracy trade-offs under quantization... focuses almost exclusively on INT quantization." The entire pipeline — from scale selection heuristics to outlier mitigation to second-order weight correction algorithms — was developed for integer formats. There's no reason to assume any of it transfers cleanly to floating-point microscaling formats, but no one had systematically tested this assumption.

### The Microscaling Format Landscape: What Makes FP4 Fundamentally Different

To understand why this gap exists — and why the paper's investigation is both necessary and non-trivial — we need to understand what makes MXFP4 and NVFP4 qualitatively different from the formats PTQ methods were designed for. The paper's Section 2 provides this specification, and the implications are profound.

**The standard approach to PTQ** operates on large groups of weights or activations, typically 128 elements, with a single shared scale. Within each group, values are quantized to a uniform grid (for INT formats) with RTN or learned rounding. This design has several implicit assumptions:

1. **Group sizes are large enough** to amortize the cost of scale storage and allow per-channel or per-tensor fine-tuning of the quantization range.
2. **Outliers can be mitigated** either by per-channel scaling (SmoothQuant), rotation matrices that spread outlier energy across dimensions (QuaRot, SpinQuant), or sparsity-aware encoding (SpQR, SqueezeLLM).
3. **The quantization grid is uniform**, meaning the distance between adjacent representable values is constant.
4. **Scale precision is essentially free** — scales are typically stored in FP16, which provides negligible quantization error relative to the 4-bit data.

**Microscaling FP4 formats break every one of these assumptions.** Let's walk through each:

**Group size is tiny (16 or 32 elements).** NVFP4 uses groups of 16 elements; MXFP4 uses groups of 32. This is not a minor reduction — it's a 4–8× reduction from the typical 128-element groups used in INT4 quantization. The paper's Section 3 proves that this matters profoundly: with small groups, the absmax scale (the maximum absolute value in the group) is much closer to the average element value, which acts as an implicit outlier mitigation mechanism. Concretely, the top element in a group of 16 has a much larger relative scale than the top element in a group of 128, because the expected maximum of 16 samples from a heavy-tailed distribution is much smaller than the expected maximum of 128 samples. But this also means that standard outlier mitigation techniques — which were designed to fix the failure mode of large-group quantization — become redundant or even harmful when applied to these formats. The paper demonstrates this analytically and empirically: adding Hadamard rotations to NVFP4 *hurts* RTN accuracy for small group sizes (Figure 3, Table 1).

**The quantization grid is non-uniform and has a dead zone.** Unlike INT4's uniform spacing, FP4's E2M1 format has representable values {0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, 6.0} in the positive range, plus their negatives. Between zero and 0.5 there's a "dead zone" — any normalized value below 0.25 (half of qmin = 0.5) quantizes to zero. This dead zone interacts with the group size in non-obvious ways that the paper's Lemma 2 formalizes: as group size G grows, the block maximum M diverges, pushing normalized values toward zero and into the dead zone, causing MSE to approach 1 (catastrophic underflow). The rate at which this happens differs between Laplace (native weights) and Normal (rotated weights) distributions, which is why rotations help MXFP4 but hurt NVFP4 at small group sizes.

**Scale precision is extremely limited.** MXFP4 uses E8M0 for scales — this means scales can *only* be powers of two. Every scale is rounded to the nearest 2^k for integer k. This introduces multiplicative quantization error that compounds with the 4-bit element error. NVFP4 uses E4M3 for scales, which provides much finer granularity (mantissa bits allow non-power-of-two values), but still has limited dynamic range. The paper's analysis in Section 3.2 shows that the scale quantization format determines whether the top element inherits its precision from the base data type (E2M1, for MXFP4's case) or from the shared scale data type (E4M3, for NVFP4's case). This is a crucial insight: with MXFP4, no matter how large the group, the relative error on the top element is bounded by E2M1's coarse quantization, because E8M0's power-of-two restriction is coarser than E2M1's grid. With NVFP4, the top element's relative error is bounded by E4M3's finer grid, because E4M3 provides better precision than E2M1.

### Conflicting or Absent Prior Evidence

The paper is motivated by the uncomfortable fact that, despite the hardware industry's claims about FP4's advantages, rigorous evaluation of these formats for LLM PTQ simply didn't exist. Let's unpack exactly where prior work fell short and why the gap is significant.

**Prior PTQ evaluation focused almost exclusively on integer formats.** The authors conduct a brief but telling survey of the quantization literature (Section 2, Related Work): Yao et al. (2022), Liu et al. (2023), Gong et al. (2024), Li et al. (2024), Lee et al. (2024), and Kurtic et al. (2025) all evaluate INT formats near-exclusively. Even comprehensive benchmarking suites like LLMC (Gong et al., 2024) don't include FP4 microscaling formats. This isn't a criticism — FP4 formats simply weren't available when most of this work was done — but it means the field has zero empirical anchors for what accuracy to expect or which methods work.

**FP8 is known to be near-lossless, creating a misleading precedent.** Kurtic et al. (2025) demonstrated that FP8 quantization can achieve essentially full accuracy recovery on LLMs. The natural extrapolation — which the hardware vendors appear to have made — is that "floating-point formats are better than integer formats at the same bit-width, so FP4 should be better than INT4." But this extrapolation ignores a critical difference: FP8 uses E4M3 or E5M2 formats where the ratio of exponent bits to mantissa bits is relatively balanced, providing both range and precision. FP4's E2M1 format has only 1 mantissa bit, making the representable values extremely sparse. The paper's analysis in Section 3 shows that this qualitative difference in precision density fundamentally changes the quantization dynamics — you can't just halve the FP8 method and expect it to work.

**Hardware vendor claims about FP4 lack rigorous validation.** While NVIDIA's technical brief (NVIDIA, 2024) and blog posts (Mehta et al., 2025) assert FP4's accuracy advantages, these claims are based on limited experiments (image generation tasks, small-scale evaluations) rather than the comprehensive multi-model, multi-task LLM evaluations that the quantization community considers standard. The paper doesn't dispute that FP4 might work well for certain tasks, but it argues — and demonstrates — that the LLM PTQ case is sufficiently different to require its own investigation.

**Existing PTQ methods make implicit assumptions that break for FP4 formats.** The paper identifies three specific failure modes where state-of-the-art methods, designed for INT formats, underperform or fail entirely when applied to FP4:

1. **SmoothQuant (Xiao et al., 2023)** relies on diagonal rescaling to redistribute outliers between weights and activations, with the assumption that the quantization grid is uniform and the group size is large enough to amortize the rescaling factor. With tiny FP4 groups, the rescaling interacts with absmax scale computation per-group, and the non-uniform grid can amplify rather than mitigate the resulting errors.

2. **QuaRot (Ashkboos et al., 2024) and SpinQuant (Liu et al., 2024)** use Hadamard rotations to normalize the weight and activation distributions — turning heavy-tailed Laplace-like distributions into approximately Gaussian distributions. For INT4, this is beneficial because it equalizes the variance across dimensions and reduces outlier-induced quantization error in large groups. But for FP4, the paper's Lemma 2 and Figure 3 show that this normalization can be harmful: the Laplace distribution actually has lower MSE at small group sizes because its tail structure interacts more favorably with the microscaling block maximum than the Normal distribution does. Specifically, for small G, the Laplace's heavier tail means the block maximum doesn't grow as quickly relative to the typical element, keeping more mass above the dead-zone threshold.

3. **AWQ (Lin et al., 2024)** identifies salient weight channels and scales them to preserve accuracy during quantization. This channel-wise scaling assumes large group sizes where the per-channel scale can be absorbed into the quantization process without interfering with the per-group scales. With FP4's small groups, the channel-wise and group-wise optimizations conflict.

**GPTQ hasn't been evaluated for FP4.** The GPTQ algorithm (Frantar et al., 2022) is the dominant weight quantization method for INT formats, achieving near-lossless INT4 compression. But GPTQ's core mechanism — quantizing weights in a fixed order and correcting remaining weights via second-order error propagation — makes no assumptions about the quantization grid being uniform or the group size being large. It *should* work for FP4 in principle, but no one had tested this. The paper fills this gap, and the results reveal that even GPTQ, applied naively, leaves substantial accuracy on the table for MXFP4 (Table 1 shows MXFP4 + GPTQ achieves only 89.5% recovery vs. 95.7% for NVFP4 + GPTQ on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as a **comprehensive empirical and analytical bridge** between the hardware availability of FP4 formats and the algorithmic maturity needed to use them effectively. This positioning has several distinct facets:

**First, it's an act of scientific honesty:** Before proposing any new method, the paper dedicates its Section 3 to understanding *why* existing methods fail, using formal MSE analysis and distribution modeling. This is unusual in the PTQ literature, which tends to be method-driven ("here's our new algorithm, here are its results") rather than phenomenon-driven ("here's what's actually happening when you quantize to this format, and here's why your existing tools don't work").

**Second, it establishes a formal bridge between distribution theory and quantization practice.** By modeling native weights/activations as Laplace distributed and rotated ones as Normal, the paper provides a tractable mathematical framework for predicting when and why different methods will be effective. Lemma 2's asymptotic rate analysis is particularly elegant: it predicts a crossover phenomenon where the MSE ordering between Laplace and Normal distributions reverses as group size increases. For small G (NVFP4, with its 16-element groups), Laplace MSE is lower than Normal MSE, so rotations hurt. For larger G (MXFP4, with 32-element groups), the Normal rate dominates, so rotations help. This prediction is validated empirically in Figure 3, where the Hadamard transform reduces MSErel for MXFP4 (all G) but increases it for NVFP4 at G=16. The generalization to larger group sizes is also confirmed: Hadamard rotations improve NVFP4 accuracy at G=128 but not at G=16 (Figure 3).

**Third, it treats the MXFP4/NVFP4 gap not as a bug to be worked around but as a fundamental design characteristic.** The paper doesn't try to make MXFP4 match NVFP4's accuracy — it acknowledges that NVFP4 is inherently more accurate due to finer scale quantization and smaller group sizes. Instead, it asks: *what's the best we can do with each format given their inherent constraints, and can we close the gap algorithmically?* The answer is MR-GPTQ, which closes the MXFP4-to-NVFP4 gap to within 1–2 percentage points (Table 1, 73.65% avg for MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ vs. 75.84% for NVFP4 + MR-GPTQ on Llama-3.1-8B).

**Fourth, it positions computational efficiency as an equally important axis alongside accuracy.** A method that achieves perfect accuracy but can't run efficiently on the target hardware is useless. The QuTLASS kernel library (Section 4.2) ensures that MR-GPTQ's innovations — particularly the online Hadamard rotation — don't create runtime bottlenecks. The paper explicitly demonstrates that MXFP4 with rotations achieves *higher* throughput than NVFP4 without rotations on B200 GPUs (Figure 5), despite the additional computation. This is a critical practical point: the MR-GPTQ approach doesn't just recover accuracy; it does so while maintaining or exceeding the performance that makes FP4 formats attractive in the first place.

**Fifth, it frames FP4 as "not an automatic upgrade over INT4" but instead a new design space.** The paper's conclusion makes this explicit: "while FP4 is not an automatic upgrade over INT4, format-specialized methods like MR-GPTQ can unlock a new frontier of accuracy-performance trade-offs." This reframes the conversation around FP4: instead of comparing FP4 vs. INT4 as raw formats on a single accuracy metric, we should be asking what method-format combinations produce the best Pareto frontiers of accuracy vs. throughput vs. memory.

### The Microscaling Design Space: Why MR-GPTQ's Contributions Are Non-Obvious

To fully appreciate why the paper's approach is necessary rather than obvious, it's worth considering the alternative paths that a naive practitioner might take:

**Path A (Port existing INT4 methods directly):** Use standard GPTQ with INT4-style hyperparameters on FP4 grids. As Table 1 shows, this recovers reasonable accuracy for NVFP4 (95.7% recovery) but leaves MXFP4 severely degraded (89.5% recovery). The degradation is worst on reasoning tasks like GSM8K (68.46% for MXFP4 + GPTQ vs. 85.06% for FP16). This is the "industry default" that the paper argues against.

**Path B (Apply rotation-based methods naively):** Use QuaRot or SpinQuant rotations followed by RTN quantization. For MXFP4, this is actively harmful: QuaRot achieves only 79.70% recovery (Table 1) — worse than RTN without rotations (87.83%). For NVFP4, rotations provide minimal benefit or mild degradation (RTN: 94.67% vs. RTN+HT: 93.82%). The intuition that "rotations help quantization" — well-established for INT formats — does not transfer cleanly to FP4.

**Path C (Use SmoothQuant for weight-activation redistribution):** This partially works (NVFP4 + SmoothQuant achieves 95.9% recovery, the highest for NVFP4) but the method was designed for INT8 and its hyperparameter tuning (the α smoothening factor) required re-optimization for FP4 formats. Moreover, it doesn't address the scale quantization issue that dominates MXFP4's error.

**Path D (Train through the quantization with QAT):** Quantization-aware training, explored in Appendix B, provides modest benefits for MXFP4 (recovering to ~95% for Llama-3.1-8B, up from MR-GPTQ's ~93%) but requires expensive retraining and shows inconsistent benefits for NVFP4, where PTQ already achieves high recovery.

The MR-GPTQ approach is non-obvious because it requires simultaneously understanding three interacting phenomena: (1) the distribution-dependent MSE behavior revealed by the paper's Lemma 2 analysis, (2) the scale quantization error that distinguishes MXFP4 from NVFP4, and (3) the interaction between GPTQ's second-order error correction and the microscaling block structure. Each of the three key ingredients (MSE-optimized grids, static activation reordering, and fused online rotations) addresses a different facet of the overall problem, and their combination is what enables the accuracy-performance improvements.

### Summary: The Gap This Paper Fills

The paper addresses a specific, urgent gap: **hardware vendors have shipped FP4 tensor cores that promise 4× inference speedups, but no one knows how to quantize LLMs to these formats without unacceptable accuracy degradation.** The gap exists because (1) the PTQ literature is almost entirely INT-centric, (2) FP4 microscaling formats break the implicit assumptions that PTQ methods were designed under, (3) the formal analysis of quantization error for these formats hadn't been done, and (4) the industry claims about FP4's accuracy superiority lacked rigorous validation. The paper fills this gap through a combination of theoretical analysis (Section 3), algorithmic innovation (Section 4), and comprehensive empirical evaluation (Section 5), while also providing production-ready kernel support to ensure the approach is practically deployable.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is primarily an **empirical systems paper with theoretical grounding** whose core contribution is a variant of the GPTQ weight quantization algorithm, called Micro-Rotated-GPTQ (MR-GPTQ), that is specifically designed to handle the unique error characteristics of microscaling FP4 formats (MXFP4 and NVFP4), rather than naively porting integer quantization methods that make implicit assumptions violated by these formats. The problem it solves can be stated plainly: existing PTQ methods, developed for INT formats with large group sizes and uniform quantization grids, fail to achieve acceptable accuracy on FP4 microscaling formats because they don't account for FP4's tiny group sizes (16 or 32 elements), non-uniform quantization grid with a dead zone, and aggressively quantized shared scales (E8M0 for MXFP4, which forces scales to be powers of two). The "shape" of the solution is a three-component modification to GPTQ: (1) format-specific grid optimization that jointly optimizes per-tensor and per-group scales via alternating minimization to account for scale quantization error, (2) static activation reordering that performs the column-shuffling step of GPTQ *after* scales are fixed, eliminating runtime overhead while preserving the accuracy benefits, and (3) block-wise Hadamard rotations fused into the quantization pipeline, with the rotation applied to both weights (offline, pre-fused) and activations (online, via lightweight GPU kernels) at the quantization group size rather than globally, which the paper's theoretical analysis shows is the correct granularity for FP4's error characteristics.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The MR-GPTQ system has five major components that operate in sequence:

1. **Distribution Analysis Framework** (used for design, not at runtime) — models native weights and activations as Laplace distributions and rotated ones as Gaussian, providing the theoretical justification for *when* and *at what block size* Hadamard rotations are beneficial. This analysis produces the design decision: use block-wise Hadamard transforms matching the quantization group size.

2. **Grid and Scale Optimization** — given a weight matrix and the target FP4 format (MXFP4 or NVFP4), jointly optimizes the per-tensor global scale and per-group local scales via alternating minimization to minimize MSE, accounting for the scale quantization format (E8M0 for MXFP4, E4M3 for NVFP4).

3. **Static Activation Reordering** — computes the Hessian diagonal from calibration data on the original weight order, identifies the column-shuffling permutation that would improve GPTQ's greedy quantization, but applies this permutation *after* scales are fixed and *reverses* it after GPTQ's error correction, so the final quantized weights remain in the original column order with no runtime reshuffling.

4. **Block-Wise Hadamard Rotation** — applies orthogonal Hadamard transforms in diagonal blocks of size $k \times k$ (where $k$ matches the quantization group size — 16 for NVFP4, 32 for MXFP4) to both weights and activations, normalizing their distributions from heavy-tailed Laplace-like to approximately Gaussian. For weights, this rotation is pre-computed and fused into the stored quantized weights. For activations, it is computed online during inference via lightweight fused kernels.

5. **GPTQ Second-Order Error Correction** — after scales are optimized and columns are reordered and weights are rotated, standard GPTQ quantizes weights one column at a time and uses the inverse Hessian from calibration data to update remaining unquantized weights, compensating for the error introduced by quantization. This operates on the *rotated, reordered* weight matrix and uses the MSE-optimized grid.

Information flows as follows: calibration data (a subset of 1024 sequences from FineWeb) is passed through the model to collect layer inputs → the Hessian is computed for each layer's weight matrix → the grid and scales are optimized via alternating minimization on the original weight layout → the Hessian diagonal determines a static column reordering → weights are block-wise Hadamard rotated (offline) → GPTQ quantizes column-by-column, correcting remaining weights → the column reordering is reversed, restoring original layout → the quantized weights are stored with the Hadamard rotation pre-fused. At inference time: activations flow into the layer → the online rotation kernel applies the corresponding block-wise Hadamard transform to activations → the FP4 matrix multiplication is performed → the output is produced.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the MSE analysis framework** (Section 3 of the paper) that models native weights/activations as Laplace and rotated ones as Normal, derives asymptotic MSE rates for both distributions under MFP quantization, and establishes the theoretical justification for *why* block-wise rotations help MXFP4 but hurt NVFP4. This is the intellectual foundation for all design choices that follow.

- **Second, the grid and scale optimization** (MR-GPTQ Ingredient 1), which addresses the scale quantization error that is the dominant failure mode for MXFP4. The joint optimization problem, the alternating minimization procedure, and the practical heuristic (static value for MXFP4, per-layer optimization for NVFP4) are detailed.

- **Third, the static activation reordering** (MR-GPTQ Ingredient 2), which modifies GPTQ's activation-order heuristic to eliminate the 10–20% runtime overhead of dynamic column shuffling while preserving its accuracy benefits. This requires understanding why the standard "dynamic" approach works and why the "static" variant is equivalent under the condition that scales are fixed before reordering.

- **Fourth, the block-wise Hadamard rotation** (MR-GPTQ Ingredient 3), which applies orthogonal transforms at the quantization group size. The mathematical operation, the offline weight fusion, and the online activation rotation via QuTLASS kernels are explained.

- **Fifth, the QuTLASS kernel library** (Section 4.2), which implements the runtime support for MR-GPTQ on NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs. This covers the quantization-related kernels (online Hadamard rotation fused with quantization and scale computation) and the matmul-related kernels (FP4 tensor core matrix multiplication with hardware-mandated scale rearrangement).

- **Sixth, the MXFP scale fitting modification** (Appendix H), an additional optimization that maps the excessively large E8M0 dynamic range into the actual data range, providing a substantial accuracy boost for MXFP4 at the same bit-width.

This is primarily an **empirical analysis and systems paper** whose core idea is that MXFP4 and NVFP4 quantization error is dominated by two interacting phenomena — scale quantization and distribution-dependent dead-zone underflow — and that a GPTQ variant incorporating block-wise rotations, format-specific scale optimization, and zero-overhead activation reordering can recover near-lossless accuracy where naive methods fail.

#### The Quantization Error Analysis Framework (Why Rotations Help MXFP4 But Hurt NVFP4)

The paper's entire technical approach is grounded in a formal MSE analysis that explains *why* existing methods fail on FP4 formats and *which* modifications are likely to help. This analysis (Section 3 of the paper) is not used at runtime — it's the design-phase reasoning that justifies the architectural choices made in MR-GPTQ. Understanding this analysis is essential to understanding why MR-GPTQ's components are non-arbitrary.

##### Distribution Modeling Assumptions

The paper models the native (unrotated) weights and activations of LLMs as following a **Laplace distribution** with zero mean and unit variance:

$$f_W(w) = \frac{1}{2b} e^{-|w|/b}, \quad \text{Var}(W) = 2b^2 = 1 \implies b = 1/\sqrt{2}$$

where $f_W(w)$ is the probability density function, $b$ is the scale parameter of the Laplace distribution, and the unit variance constraint fixes $b = 1/\sqrt{2}$.

**What this models:** the weights and activations as zero-mean symmetric distributions with heavy tails (exponential decay rather than quadratic-exponential decay of a Gaussian). The heavy tails capture the presence of outliers — values that are many standard deviations from the mean — which are characteristic of LLM parameters and activations.

**Why Laplace rather than Gaussian:** empirical studies (Akhondzadeh et al., 2025; Dotzel et al., 2024, cited in Section 3) have shown that LLM parameters exhibit high kurtosis — sharper peaks and heavier tails than a Gaussian. The paper validates this with a kurtosis measurement on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct in Figure 2: native weights have kurtosis 1.47 and activations have kurtosis 8.75, both substantially above the Gaussian value of 0. The Laplace distribution (kurtosis 3 for the univariate case, but note that for the Laplace distribution $b=1/\sqrt{2}$, the excess kurtosis is 3) provides a tractable model that captures this tail behavior while remaining analytically workable.

The magnitude $Z = |W|$ of Laplace-distributed values follows an **exponential distribution** with rate $\lambda = 1/b = \sqrt{2}$:

$$f_Z(z) = \lambda e^{-\lambda z}, \quad F_Z(z) = 1 - e^{-\lambda z}, \quad z \geq 0$$

where $f_Z(z)$ is the density of magnitude values and $F_Z(z)$ is the cumulative distribution function (the probability that a magnitude is $\leq z$).

After **Hadamard rotation**, the paper models the transformed weights and activations as following a **Normal (Gaussian) distribution** with zero mean and unit variance:

$$f_V(v) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-v^2/2}, \quad V \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

The magnitude $Z = |V|$ follows a **half-normal distribution**:

$$f_Z(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}, \quad F_Z(z) = \text{erf}(z/\sqrt{2}), \quad z \geq 0$$

where $\text{erf}(z) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^z e^{-t^2} dt$ is the standard Gauss error function.

**Why rotations produce Gaussian distributions:** the Hadamard transform is an orthogonal matrix whose rows are approximately random sign patterns (up to the specific Walsh-Hadamard structure). Applying it to a vector with i.i.d. heavy-tailed components produces a new vector where each component is a weighted sum of many independent-ish inputs. By the central limit theorem, these sums tend toward normality. The paper's Figure 2 validates this empirically: rotated weights have kurtosis 0.05 and rotated activations have kurtosis 0.02, both extremely close to the Gaussian theoretical value of 0.

##### Microscaling Block Floating-Point Quantization Model

The paper formalizes MFP quantization as follows (Section 3, Definitions 2 and 3). Consider a block of $G$ elements drawn independently from some distribution: $X = (X_1, \ldots, X_G)$, each with unit variance. The quantization operates block-by-block:

1. **Scale computation:** $s = \max_{1 \leq i \leq G} |X_i|$ — the absolute maximum in the block.
2. **Normalization:** $U_i = X_i / s$, which maps all elements to $[-1, 1]$.
3. **Quantization:** $\hat{U}_i = \text{RTN}_Q(U_i)$, where $\text{RTN}_Q$ is round-to-nearest onto the FP4 grid $Q$ (for E2M1: the 7 positive representable values $\{0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, 6.0\}$ plus zero and negatives).
4. **De-normalization:** $\hat{X}_i = s \cdot \hat{U}_i$.

The grid $Q$ has a **dead zone**: the first positive quantization level is $q_{\min} = 0.5$, which induces a dead-zone half-width $\delta = q_{\min}/2 = 0.25$. Any normalized value $|U_i| < 0.25$ is rounded to zero — it "falls into the dead zone."

**Why the dead zone matters:** for small normalized values, the quantization error is catastrophic (the value is completely lost) rather than proportional (as it would be with uniform quantization). This means that the fraction of elements that fall into the dead zone dominates the MSE. As the block size $G$ grows, the block maximum $s$ grows (you're taking the max over more samples), so the normalized values $U_i = X_i/s$ shrink, and more of them fall into the dead zone. In the limit $G \to \infty$, essentially all normalized values fall below $0.25$, every element quantizes to zero, and the MSE approaches $\text{Var}(X_1) = 1$ (complete information loss).

**Key metrics** (Definition 3):
- **Per-element MSE:** $\text{MSE}(G) = \mathbb{E}[(X_1 - \hat{X}_1)^2]$ — the expected squared error for a single element (by symmetry, any element).
- **Top-element MSE per block:** $\text{MSE}_{\text{top}}(G) = \mathbb{E}[(X_{I^\star} - \hat{X}_{I^\star})^2]$ where $I^\star = \arg\max_i |X_i|$ — the expected squared error on the largest-magnitude element in each block.
- **Relative MSE (used for real tensors):** $\text{MSE}_{\text{rel}}(G) = \mathbb{E}[\sum_{i=1}^G (X_i - \hat{X}_i)^2 / \sum_{i=1}^G X_i^2]$ — per-block MSE normalized by the block's total energy, which accounts for variance heterogeneity across groups.
- **Relative top-element MSE:** $\text{MSE}_{\text{rel}}^{\text{top}}(G) = \mathbb{E}[(X_{I^\star} - \hat{X}_{I^\star})^2 / X_{I^\star}^2]$.

**Why relative MSE is the practical metric:** in real weight and activation tensors, different groups have different variances, so absolute MSE would be dominated by high-variance groups. Relative MSE normalizes by group energy, making it a better predictor of end-to-end model accuracy degradation (as established by Malinovskii et al., 2025, cited in the paper).

##### Lemma 1: Hadamard Rotation Spreads Top-Element Error Across All Elements

The paper proves a crucial property of Hadamard rotations applied before MFP quantization (Section 3.1, Lemma 1):

**Setup:** we have a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^G$ with i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ coordinates. We apply a normalized Hadamard transform $\mathbf{y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{G}} \mathbf{H} \mathbf{x}$, quantize in the $\mathbf{y}$-domain to get $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, then reconstruct $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{G}} \mathbf{H}^\top \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ (since $\mathbf{H}^\top \mathbf{H} = G \mathbf{I}$, and using the normalized version $\mathbf{U} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{G}} \mathbf{H}$ which is orthogonal: $\mathbf{U}^\top \mathbf{U} = \mathbf{I}$).

**The Lemma states:**

$$\text{MSE}_{\text{top}}(G) = \text{MSE}(G)$$

**What this means:** after the Hadamard rotation, the expected squared error on the original top coordinate (the largest-magnitude component of $\mathbf{x}$) equals the average per-element MSE. The rotation has distributed the quantization error uniformly across all coordinates.

**Why this is important:** without the rotation, absmax scaling guarantees $\text{MSE}_{\text{top}}(G) = 0$ in the ideal case — the top element determines the scale, so it is perfectly preserved (it normalizes to $\pm 1$, which is in the FP4 grid). All the error falls on the smaller elements. The rotation trades off perfect preservation of the top element for better overall MSE distribution, which can be beneficial or harmful depending on the distribution and group size.

**Proof intuition:** the rotation $\mathbf{U} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{G}} \mathbf{H}$ is orthogonal, so the error vectors are related by $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{U}^\top \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{y}}$, and $\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{x}}\|_2^2 = \|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2$. The per-element MSE is $\frac{1}{G} \mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{y}}\|_2^2]$. Since the quantization error $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{y}}$ is independent of the signal (standard quantization analysis assumption), and the index $I^\star$ of the maximum coordinate of $\mathbf{x}$ is uniformly distributed over $\{1, \ldots, G\}$ (by symmetry of the i.i.d. Gaussian), the expected squared error on the top coordinate is $\frac{1}{G} \mathbb{E}[\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\mathbf{x}}\|_2^2] = \text{MSE}(G)$. The proof uses the law of total expectation, conditioning on which coordinate is the maximum, and the independence of the error from this index.

##### Lemma 2: Asymptotic MSE Rates for Laplace vs. Normal

The key analytical result (Section 3.1, Lemma 2) characterizes how quickly the "preserved mass" — the fraction of signal energy that survives quantization and doesn't fall into the dead zone — decays as the group size $G$ increases. Define the preserved mass:

$$R(G) = 1 - \text{MSE}(G) = \mathbb{E}[X_1^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}\{|U_1| \geq \delta\}]$$

where $\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}$ is the indicator function (1 if the condition holds, 0 otherwise). This captures the energy that avoids the dead zone: elements whose normalized magnitude $|U_1| = |X_1|/s$ exceeds the half-width $\delta = 0.25$.

The asymptotic rates (as $G \to \infty$) are:

**For Laplace distribution:** $R_L(G) = \Theta((\log G)^2 G^{-\delta})$ where $\delta = 0.25$ for FP4.

**For Normal distribution:** $R_N(G) = \Theta(\sqrt{\log G} \, G^{-\delta^2})$ where $\delta^2 = 0.0625$ for FP4.

**What this means operationally:** for the Laplace distribution, the preserved mass decays like $G^{-0.25}$ (up to log factors) — relatively slowly. For the Normal distribution, it decays like $G^{-0.0625}$ (up to log factors) — much more slowly. The Normal distribution preserves more signal as $G$ grows because its lighter tails mean the block maximum $s$ doesn't grow as aggressively relative to the typical element, keeping normalized values above the dead-zone threshold for larger $G$.

**The crossover phenomenon:** for small $G$, the Laplace distribution can have *lower* MSE (better preservation) than the Normal, because while the Laplace's block maximum is larger (pushing normalized values down), its heavier-tailed distribution means more mass is in the extreme quantiles that survive. But as $G$ grows, the Normal's slower decay rate dominates, and eventually $\text{MSE}_N(G) < \text{MSE}_L(G)$.

**Why $\delta^2 < \delta$ is crucial:** note that $\delta = 0.25 \ll 1$, so $\delta^2 = 0.0625$ is much smaller than $\delta = 0.25$. This means $G^{-\delta^2}$ decays *dramatically* slower than $G^{-\delta}$. For example, at $G = 32$, $G^{-\delta} = 32^{-0.25} \approx 0.42$ while $G^{-\delta^2} = 32^{-0.0625} \approx 0.81$ — the Normal preserves nearly twice the relative mass. This is why rotations (which turn Laplace → Normal) provide a net benefit at the larger group sizes used by MXFP4 ($G = 32$) but not necessarily at the smaller group sizes used by NVFP4 ($G = 16$), where the crossover hasn't occurred yet.

**Connection to MR-GPTQ:** this analysis directly motivates using Hadamard rotations for MXFP4 (group size 32, where Normal dominates) but being cautious about them for NVFP4 (group size 16, where Laplace might still be better). The experimental validation in Figure 3 confirms this: Hadamard transform *reduces* MSErel for MXFP4 (all group sizes shown) but *increases* MSErel for NVFP4 at $G = 16$.

##### Shared Scale Quantization Analysis

Beyond the dead-zone effect, the paper analyzes how the scale quantization format (E8M0 for MXFP4, E4M3 for NVFP4) interacts with the base element format (E2M1 for both) to determine the effective precision of different elements in the block.

The key insight (Section 3.2, discussion following Figure 4):

**For MXFP4 (E8M0 scales):** E8M0 is coarser than E2M1. E8M0 represents only powers of two: the quantization grid for scales is $\{2^{-127}, 2^{-126}, \ldots, 2^{127}\}$. E2M1's grid (once normalized by the scale) has representable values $\{0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, 6.0\}$. The relative spacing in E2M1 (e.g., the ratio between adjacent values like $1.0/0.5 = 2$, $1.5/1.0 = 1.5$, $2.0/1.5 \approx 1.33$) is finer than the power-of-two spacing in E8M0 (ratio exactly 2 between adjacent scales). Therefore, the top element inherits its effective precision from the base data type E2M1, not the scale type — it's quantized to the E2M1 grid regardless of the group size.

**For NVFP4 (E4M3 scales):** E4M3 is finer than E2M1. E4M3 has 3 mantissa bits, providing 8 subdivisions within each power-of-two interval, so it can represent values like $1.0, 1.125, 1.25, 1.375, 1.5, 1.625, 1.75, 1.875$ in $[1, 2)$. This precision is finer than E2M1's 1-bit mantissa. Therefore, the top element inherits its effective precision from the scale data type E4M3 — it gets "promoted" to E4M3-level precision because the scale captures its magnitude more precisely than E2M1 can capture the residual.

**Lemma 4 (Appendix E) formalizes this:** for absmax quantization without rotations, $\text{MSE}_{\text{rel}}^{\text{top}}$ is insensitive to the shared scale magnitude in expectation over high dynamic range intervals and, as a result, insensitive to $G$. The top element's relative error is determined by the finer of the base format and the scale format.

**Why this matters for MR-GPTQ:** when rotations are applied, Lemma 1 tells us that the top-element error is spread across all elements, meaning the effective per-element precision is something between pure E2M1 and pure E4M3. For MXFP4, where the base format is the precision bottleneck, rotations can improve overall MSE (by spreading the coarser quantization across more elements, each element sees less relative error on average — though this depends on the distribution, as Lemma 2 shows). For NVFP4, where the scale format provides E4M3-level precision to the top element, rotations *replace* that fine precision with the coarser per-element precision of the rotated distribution, potentially increasing overall MSE.

##### Numerical Validation on Real Model Tensors

Figure 3 validates the entire analytical framework on both synthetic Laplace samples and real weight/activation matrices from Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. The experiments measure $\text{MSE}_{\text{rel}}$ (top row) and $\text{MSE}_{\text{rel}}^{\text{top}}$ (bottom row) for MXFP4 (S = E8M0) and NVFP4 (S = E4M3), with and without the Hadamard transform, across group sizes $G \in \{8, 16, 32, 64, 128\}$.

Key observations that directly inform MR-GPTQ's design:

1. **For MSErel (average error):** Hadamard transform *reduces* MSE for MXFP4 at all group sizes and *increases* MSE for NVFP4 at small group sizes ($G = 8, 16$), with the gap narrowing or reversing at $G = 128$. This matches the Lemma 2 prediction of a crossover.

2. **For MSErel_top (top-element error):** without rotations, MXFP4's top-element error is constant across $G$ (E2M1-limited, as predicted) while NVFP4's top-element error is very low and also constant (E4M3-promoted, as predicted). With rotations, the top-element error jumps to the per-element MSE level for both formats, confirming Lemma 1.

3. **For real model tensors:** the qualitative behavior matches the Laplace synthetic data closely, validating the distribution modeling choice. The effect magnitudes are similar, suggesting the analytical framework has practical predictive power.

#### Ingredient 1: MSE-Optimized Grids (Joint Scale Optimization)

The first component of MR-GPTQ addresses the scale quantization error that is the dominant failure mode for MXFP4 and a secondary concern for NVFP4. Standard GPTQ uses absmax (minmax) scaling: the scale for each group is simply the maximum absolute value in that group, and the per-tensor scale (if used) is the maximum over all groups. This is near-optimal when scales are stored at high precision (FP16), but breaks down when scales themselves are aggressively quantized to E8M0 (powers of two) or even E4M3 (FP8).

##### The Optimization Problem

For NVFP4, which uses both a per-tensor (global) scale $s_T$ and per-group scales $s_{G_1}, \ldots, s_{G_k}$ for $k$ groups, the quantized element is:

$$\hat{X}_i = s_T \cdot s_{G_g(i)} \cdot Q\left(\frac{X_i}{s_T \cdot s_{G_g(i)}}\right)$$

where $g(i)$ maps element index $i$ to its group index, and $Q$ is the FP4 quantization operator (RTN to the E2M1 grid). The scale values $s_{G_j}$ are themselves quantized to E4M3 format.

**What this represents:** a hierarchical scaling where the global scale captures the overall tensor magnitude range, the per-group scales capture local variation, and the residual after both is quantized to 4 bits. The global scale is stored once per tensor; the per-group scales are stored once per 16 elements.

The joint optimization problem (Section 4.1, Ingredient 1) is:

$$\min_{s_T, s_{G_1}, \ldots, s_{G_k}} \sum_i \|\hat{X}_i(s_T, s_{G_1}, \ldots, s_{G_k}) - X_i\|_2^2$$

where the dependence of $\hat{X}_i$ on all scales is through the quantization operation $Q$, making this a non-convex mixed-integer optimization.

**What this computes:** the scale values that minimize the total squared error between original and quantized elements, accounting for the fact that scales themselves will be quantized to E4M3 (or E8M0) — the quantization operator $Q$ includes the scale quantization step.

**Why joint optimization rather than sequential:** sequential scale selection (first choose $s_T$, then choose per-group scales given $s_T$) is suboptimal because the scale quantization error in $s_T$ propagates to all groups. By optimizing jointly, the per-group scales can partially compensate for errors in the global scale.

##### Alternating Minimization Procedure

Since the optimization is non-convex and involves discrete quantization operations, the paper uses alternating minimization:

1. **Initialize:** set $s_T$ to the maximum absolute value over all elements (standard absmax) and each $s_{G_j}$ to the maximum absolute value in group $j$ divided by $s_T$.

2. **Fix the per-group scales, optimize $s_T$:** for each candidate global scale value (from a discretized set around the current value), compute the total MSE by quantizing all elements with the fixed per-group scales, and select the $s_T$ that minimizes MSE.

3. **Fix $s_T$, optimize each $s_{G_j}$ independently:** for each group, select the per-group scale (from a discretized set) that minimizes the group's MSE, given the fixed global scale.

4. **Repeat** steps 2–3 for a small number of iterations (typically 2–3) or until convergence.

5. **Quantize all scales** to the target format (E4M3 for NVFP4, E8M0 for MXFP4) and compute the final quantization.

**Why alternating minimization works:** while the joint problem is non-convex, each subproblem (optimizing one set of scales while fixing the others) is a discrete 1D search that can be solved exactly by enumeration over a small candidate set. The alternating updates are guaranteed to monotonically decrease the MSE, though they may converge to a local optimum.

##### Practical Heuristics for MXFP4

For MXFP4 with rotations, the paper observes a surprising simplification: "a single static value works stably across all layers" (Section 4.1). This means the scale optimization can be simplified to finding one good global scale (or using a fixed heuristic) rather than performing per-layer alternating minimization. This is possible because:

- The Hadamard rotation normalizes the weight distribution to approximately $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, making the optimal scale predictable from the variance.
- MXFP4's E8M0 scale quantization is so coarse (powers of two only) that fine-tuning provides limited benefit — the scale is either correct or off by a factor of 2, and the sign of this error is often predictable from the distribution variance.

For NVFP4 without rotations, the paper performs the full alternating optimization per-layer, as the unnormalized distributions have variable variance and the finer E4M3 scale grid justifies the optimization effort.

##### Connection to MR-GPTQ's Overall Design

The scale optimization is performed *before* the activation reordering and GPTQ error correction. This ordering is critical: by fixing the scales and quantization grid first, the static activation reordering (Ingredient 2) can operate on a fixed quantization target, and GPTQ's error correction can compensate for the *residual* error after optimal scale selection. If scales were optimized after GPTQ, the error correction would need to be re-done, creating a circular dependency.

#### Ingredient 2: Static Activation Reordering

The second component of MR-GPTQ addresses a practical performance issue with standard GPTQ while preserving its accuracy benefits.

##### Standard GPTQ's Dynamic Act-Order

Standard GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022) heuristically reorders weight columns before quantization according to the **descending order of the Hessian diagonal entries**. The Hessian diagonal $H_{jj} = \sum_{b} (X_{b,j})^2$ (where $b$ indexes calibration samples) measures the "importance" of column $j$ — columns with larger average squared activations contribute more to the output and should be quantized earlier in the greedy sequence, when more unquantized weights remain to compensate for their error.

**Why this helps accuracy:** GPTQ quantizes weights column-by-column in a fixed order and uses the inverse Hessian to update remaining weights. The order matters because early-quantized columns get fewer "free parameters" to absorb their quantization error (fewer remaining weights). By quantizing high-importance columns early, the error correction can use the remaining lower-importance columns to compensate. If low-importance columns are quantized first, they use up correction capacity that would be better spent on high-importance columns.

**The runtime cost:** this "dynamic act-order" requires physically reshuffling the weight matrix columns at runtime during inference, because the quantized weights are stored in the reordered column layout. The reshuffling of input and output activations adds 10–20% overhead to end-to-end inference latency (as reported in the paper, Section 4.1).

##### Static Activation Reordering: How It Works

MR-GPTQ's innovation is to observe that the activation reordering can be performed **statically** — i.e., applied before GPTQ quantization and then reversed after, so the stored weights are in their original column order — without losing the accuracy benefit, provided that the quantization grid and scales are fixed *before* the reordering decision.

The procedure (Section 4.1, Ingredient 2) operates in three steps:

1. **Fix grid and scales:** first, compute the quantization grid and per-group scales on the original (arbitrary) column order. This determines the quantization target — for each weight, the quantized value it will be mapped to, and the scale that will be applied.

2. **Determine reordering permutation:** compute the Hessian diagonal from calibration data on the original column order. The permutation $\pi$ sorts columns in descending order of $H_{jj}$.

3. **Shuffle, quantize, unshuffle:** permute the weight matrix columns according to $\pi$. Apply standard GPTQ on the permuted matrix, with the pre-computed grids and scales (the scales need to be permuted correspondingly). After quantization, apply the inverse permutation $\pi^{-1}$ to restore the original column order.

**Why this preserves the GPTQ benefit:** the key insight is that the activation reordering's benefit comes from the *order in which columns are quantized* during the greedy GPTQ process, not from the final layout of the weights at runtime. The greedy quantization algorithm processes columns one at a time; by reordering before GPTQ and un-reordering after, we get the benefit of a good quantization order without changing the stored weight layout.

**Why scales must be fixed before reordering:** if scales were computed after reordering, the scale-permutation would need to be consistent with the reordering, and the microscaling group structure (groups of 16 or 32 consecutive elements) would interact with the column shuffle in complex ways. By fixing scales on the original layout, the reordering can be purely a column permutation that preserves the block structure (since columns within a group are permuted together if the group is column-aligned, or the group boundaries are respected during permutation).

**Why this matters for FP4 specifically:** for INT4 quantization with large group sizes (128+), the dynamic act-order's runtime overhead might be acceptable because the absolute speedup from quantization is large. For FP4 with tiny groups (16–32), the overhead of per-group scale access and the reshuffling operation becomes a larger fraction of the total compute, making static reordering more valuable.

#### Ingredient 3: Fused Online Rotations (Block-Wise Hadamard Transform)

The third component of MR-GPTQ is the block-wise Hadamard rotation, applied at the quantization group size, with the rotation fused into the weights offline and computed online for activations via high-performance GPU kernels.

##### Mathematical Operation

For a linear layer with weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$ and activation input $X \in \mathbb{R}^{b \times d_{\text{in}}}$ (where $b$ is the batch size), the standard forward pass computes:

$$Y = X W^\top$$

The rotated variant (Section 4.1, Ingredient 3) computes:

$$Y = Q(X H_k^\top) \, Q(W H_k)^\top$$

where:

- $H_k$ is a block-diagonal matrix with $d_{\text{in}}/k$ blocks, each block being a $k \times k$ normalized Hadamard matrix (entries $\pm 1/\sqrt{k}$). The normalization ensures $H_k H_k^\top = I$ (the identity), so the transformation is orthogonal.
- $k$ is the block size, chosen to match the quantization group size: $k = 16$ for NVFP4, $k = 32$ for MXFP4. This alignment means each quantization group undergoes an independent orthogonal rotation.
- $Q(\cdot)$ is the quantization function (RTN to the FP4 grid after scale normalization).

**What this computes:** the weight matrix is pre-multiplied by $H_k$ (rotating its columns), and the activation matrix is pre-multiplied by $H_k$ (rotating its columns) before quantization. Because $H_k$ is orthogonal, $H_k H_k^\top = I$, the product $Q(W H_k) Q(X H_k^\top)^\top$ approximates $W H_k H_k^\top X^\top = W X^\top$ — the rotation cancels out in the full-precision product, and the quantization error is the only residual.

**Why block-wise rather than global rotation:** a global Hadamard transform (one $d_{\text{in}} \times d_{\text{in}}$ block) would normalize the entire weight matrix, but the microscaling quantization operates on groups of 16–32 elements. By aligning the rotation block size with the quantization group size, each group sees a local orthogonal transformation that normalizes the distribution within that group, without mixing information across groups. This is the right granularity because:

1. The scale is computed per-group, so the distribution within each group determines the quantization error.
2. The Lemma 2 analysis applies at the group level — the asymptotic rate depends on the distribution *within each group*, not globally.
3. Global rotations can create spurious correlations across groups that harm GPTQ's error correction (which assumes column-wise independence).

**Why Hadamard specifically:** the Hadamard matrix has several practical advantages:
- **Orthogonal and self-inverse** ($H^\top = H$, up to scaling), so the same transform works for weights and activations.
- **Fast transform:** the Walsh-Hadamard transform can be computed in $O(n \log n)$ operations using a butterfly pattern, vs. $O(n^2)$ for a general matrix multiply. This makes online activation rotation feasible.
- **Well-studied in quantization:** prior work (QuIP, QuaRot, SpinQuant) has established that Hadamard transforms effectively normalize LLM parameter distributions.
- **Good distribution-spreading properties:** the Hadamard transform approximates a random rotation in terms of mixing heavy-tailed components into approximately Gaussian mixtures, while being deterministic and fast.

##### Offline Weight Fusion

For the weights, the rotation $W H_k$ is computed once offline and stored as the quantized weight matrix. This means there is *no additional runtime cost* for the weight-side rotation — it is "baked into" the stored weights. The quantization grid, scales, and GPTQ error correction all operate on the rotated weight matrix $W H_k$.

##### Online Activation Rotation via QuTLASS Kernels

For the activations, $X H_k$ must be computed online during inference. The QuTLASS kernel library (Section 4.2) provides lightweight fused kernels for this operation. The key design decisions are:

**Kernel design:**
- Support for block sizes $k \in \{16, 32, 64, 128\}$ ($k$ must be a power of two for the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform).
- The transformation is memory-bound (not compute-bound) for these small block sizes, meaning "any rotation (not just Hadamards) can be applied at essentially the same cost, as the full matrix can be loaded at runtime" (Section 4.2).
- Quantization and scale calculation are **fused into the transformation kernel** as a custom epilogue function, eliminating the need for a separate quantization pass over the activations.

**Fusion strategy:**
1. Load a block of $k$ activation elements.
2. Apply the $k \times k$ Hadamard butterfly transform in registers (fast, using shared memory for intermediate values).
3. Compute the per-group scale (absmax or MSE-optimized) from the transformed values.
4. Quantize each value to FP4 using the computed scale.
5. Write the quantized values and scales to the output buffers.

**Why this fusion eliminates overhead:** without fusion, the inference pipeline would be: (1) load activations from memory, (2) apply rotation and write back to memory, (3) load rotated activations for the matmul, (4) compute scales on the fly or load pre-computed scales. Each memory round-trip is expensive. With fusion, the rotation, quantization, and scale computation happen in a single pass over the activation data, with the output going directly into the format required by the FP4 matrix multiplication kernel.

**Hardware-mandated scale rearrangement:** between the quantization kernel and the matrix multiplication, the FP4 tensor core (tcgen05.mma on Blackwell) requires a specific layout of scaling factors (the "block scaling factors layout" documented in NVIDIA's cuBLAS documentation). QuTLASS implements this rearrangement as a separate lightweight kernel (potentially fused with the matmul setup), ensuring the data is in the exact format the hardware expects.

**Performance characteristics:** Figures 5 and 11 demonstrate that the actual throughput of MR-GPTQ with online Hadamard rotation ($H_{128}$ or $H_{32}$) is extremely close to the "ideal" throughput (the raw FP4 matmul with no quantization overhead). On B200, MXFP4 with $H_{128}$ achieves up to ≈3.6× speedup over BF16, compared to the 4× theoretical maximum. On RTX5090, the speedup reaches ≈6× (out of 8× theoretical). This small gap between actual and ideal confirms that the fused kernels add negligible overhead.

#### How Standard GPTQ Is Modified for MR-GPTQ

The MR-GPTQ algorithm applies standard GPTQ's second-order error correction, but on the **rotated, reordered, scale-optimized weight matrix**. The modifications are:

1. **Input to GPTQ:** the weight matrix after block-wise Hadamard rotation ($W H_k$), with the quantization grid and scales already determined by the MSE optimization (Ingredient 1).

2. **Column order:** columns are reordered according to the static activation reordering heuristic (Ingredient 2), which is based on the Hessian diagonal of the *rotated* weight matrix (computing the Hessian from calibration inputs that are also rotated).

3. **GPTQ core loop:** standard column-by-column quantization with inverse Hessian updates. For each column $j$:
   - Quantize all rows in column $j$ to the pre-computed grid value for each group.
   - Compute the quantization error for each row: $\varepsilon_i = W_{ij}^{\text{quantized}} - W_{ij}^{\text{original}}$ (in the rotated space).
   - Update the remaining (unquantized) columns using the inverse Hessian: $W_{i, > j} \leftarrow W_{i, > j} - \varepsilon_i \cdot \frac{[\mathbf{H}^{-1}]_{j, > j}}{[\mathbf{H}^{-1}]_{jj}}$, where $\mathbf{H} = X^\top X + \lambda I$ is the regularized Hessian from calibration data.

4. **Hyperparameters:** the paper uses standard GPTQ hyperparameters: Hessian dampening factor $\lambda = 10^{-2}$, calibration set of 1024 sequences from FineWeb, and the standard fixed column order within each reordered layout.

5. **Post-GPTQ:** after all columns are quantized, the column reordering is reversed (inverse permutation), producing the final quantized weight matrix in the original column layout. The Hadamard rotation remains "baked in" — the stored weights are $Q(W H_k)$ in the original column order.

**Why GPTQ is a good fit for FP4 formats:** GPTQ's error correction mechanism makes no assumptions about the quantization grid being uniform or the group size being large. It only requires that the quantization operation is deterministic given the scales (which it is, after the grid optimization step) and that the Hessian captures the second-order sensitivity of the layer's output to weight perturbations. These conditions hold for FP4 just as they do for INT4.

**Interaction between GPTQ and the other ingredients:**
- **Scale optimization (Ingredient 1) provides a better starting point,** reducing the total error that GPTQ needs to correct. Without scale optimization, GPTQ would be correcting errors that could have been avoided by better scale choices, wasting correction capacity.
- **Static reordering (Ingredient 2) improves the efficiency of GPTQ's greedy process** by ensuring high-importance columns get the benefit of more correction capacity, without changing the final weight layout.
- **Block-wise rotations (Ingredient 3) normalize the weight distribution**, making the per-column Hessian more uniform (all columns have similar importance), which reduces the variance of GPTQ's greedy ordering and makes the error correction more evenly distributed.

#### The MXFP Scale Fitting Modification (Appendix H)

An additional optimization specific to MXFP4 addresses the mismatch between E8M0's enormous dynamic range and the actual scale ranges observed in weight and activation tensors. This modification is described in Appendix H and is part of the MR-GPTQ pipeline for MXFP4.

##### The Problem

MXFP4's E8M0 scale format can represent values from $2^{-127}$ to $2^{128}$ — a dynamic range spanning 255 powers of two. However, as Figure 4 demonstrates, the actual shared scales of weights and activations span a much narrower range (roughly $2^{-10}$ to $2^{5}$ for most tensors). This means the E8M0 grid is excessively coarse for the actual data: only a small fraction of the 256 representable exponent values are ever used, and the spacing between them (always a factor of 2) is large relative to the actual scale variation.

The standard MXFP quantization (with the $\times 4/3$ re-scaling factor from Tseng et al., 2025, which provides an unbiased estimate) computes:

$$s_{\text{E8M0}} = \frac{4}{3} \cdot 2^{\text{clamp}(\text{round}(\log_2 s), -128, 127)}$$

where $s$ is the original (unquantized) scale.

**What this does:** it takes the log-base-2 of the scale, rounds to the nearest integer, clamps to the representable range, exponentiates, and multiplies by 4/3 (to make the quantized scale an unbiased estimator of the original, since rounding in log-space biases the scale downward).

##### The Modification: Range-Fitting

The proposed modification (Equation 2 in Appendix H) estimates the minimum $s_{\min}$ and maximum $s_{\max}$ scale values across the tensor, then maps the full E8M0 range onto this observed range:

$$s_{\text{E8M0}}^{\text{fitted}} = 2^{(\log_2 s_{\max} - \log_2 s_{\min}) \cdot \text{clamp}(\text{round}(255 \cdot \frac{\log_2 s - \log_2 s_{\min}}{\log_2 s_{\max} - \log_2 s_{\min}}), 0, 255) + \log_2 s_{\min}}$$

**What this computes:**
1. $\frac{\log_2 s - \log_2 s_{\min}}{\log_2 s_{\max} - \log_2 s_{\min}}$ maps the log-scale from $[\log_2 s_{\min}, \log_2 s_{\max}]$ to $[0, 1]$.
2. Multiplying by 255 maps to $[0, 255]$, the integer range of an 8-bit unsigned integer (the exponent field of E8M0).
3. Rounding, clamping to $[0, 255]$, and dividing by 255 gives a "fractional exponent" $\alpha \in [0, 1]$.
4. $\alpha$ is scaled back to the original log-range and exponentiated.

In effect, this replaces the integer exponent $q$ in $s = 2^q$ with a fractional exponent $s = 2^{\alpha q + \beta}$, where $\alpha$ is the compression factor and $\beta$ is the offset. The scale quantization grid becomes much denser within the observed range, reducing the quantization error.

**Why this works:** the E8M0 format has 256 representable values. By default, these are spread over 255 powers of two ($2^{-127}$ to $2^{127}$). The modification spreads the same 256 values over the observed range, which might be only 15–20 powers of two. The spacing between adjacent scales reduces from a factor of 2 to roughly $2^{(\log_2 s_{\max} - \log_2 s_{\min})/255}$, which is much closer to 1 (e.g., $2^{15/255} \approx 1.042$ for a range of 15 powers of two).

**Compatibility with efficient arithmetic:** for the matrix multiplication $Y = (s_A \cdot Q_A) \times (s_W \cdot Q_W)$ to be efficient, the activation scale $s_A$ and weight scale $s_W$ must share the same exponent base, so their product $s_A s_W = 2^{\alpha_A q_A + \beta_A + \alpha_W q_W + \beta_W}$ can be expressed as $2^{\alpha q + \beta}$ — i.e., a single addition in the exponent. This requires $\alpha_A = \alpha_W$, meaning the activation and weight tensors in a layer must use the same compression factor $\alpha$. The paper notes this constraint (Appendix H).

##### Practical Impact

Table 11 in Appendix H shows the accuracy improvement from this modification (denoted MXFP4$^\dagger$):
- For Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with RTN: MXFP4 achieves 87.8% recovery, MXFP4$^\dagger$ achieves 94.3% (+6.5 percentage points).
- For MR-GPTQ: MXFP4 achieves 93.6% recovery, MXFP4$^\dagger$ achieves 94.9% (+1.3 points).

The modification provides a substantial boost for simple RTN quantization, and a more modest but still meaningful boost for MR-GPTQ (which already mitigates some of the scale quantization error through its other components). Notably, MXFP4$^\dagger$ with RTN achieves similar recovery to standard MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ, suggesting that the scale quantization error is the dominant failure mode for MXFP4 and that this simple fix addresses a large fraction of it.

#### The QuTLASS Kernel Library: Runtime Support Architecture

The QuTLASS v1.0 library (Section 4.2) provides the production runtime support for MR-GPTQ on NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs. It is built on NVIDIA CUTLASS, a template library for GPU linear algebra, and targets two Blackwell compute capabilities: SM100 (datacenter B200) and SM120 (consumer RTX5090).

##### Architecture Overview

QuTLASS kernels are organized into two categories:

**1. Quantization-related kernels:**
- **Online Hadamard rotation + quantization:** fuses the transformation $X H_k$ with scale computation (absmax or MSE) and FP4 quantization into a single kernel. Implemented as a memory-bound operation where the $k \times k$ Hadamard transform is applied in registers or shared memory.
- **Scale rearrangement:** between the quantization kernel and the matmul, the quantized data must be reorganized to match the hardware's expected layout for block-scaled matrix multiplications (tcgen05.mma instruction on Blackwell). This rearrangement is necessary because the quantization kernel produces scales in the natural group order, while the tensor core expects a specific interleaved layout for efficient access during the matmul.
- **Template-based design:** the library supports both MSE and Abs-Max quantization methods through templates, with a design that allows new quantization methods to be integrated by implementing the appropriate epilogue function.

**2. Matmul-related narrow-precision kernels:**
- **FP4 matrix multiplication:** uses Blackwell's tcgen05.mma tensor core instructions for FP4 × FP4 matrix multiplication with block scaling. Supports multiple backends (CUTLASS and FlashInfer) for flexible deployment.
- **Backend selection:** CUTLASS provides mature, well-optimized tensor core kernels; FlashInfer (Ye et al., 2025) provides alternative implementations optimized for specific inference patterns (e.g., variable-length sequences in LLM serving).

##### Performance Characteristics

**Kernel throughput (Figure 5):**
- On B200, for a single linear layer (shape $57344 \times 8192$, from Llama-3.3-70B):
  - Ideal MXFP4 (no quantization overhead): peaks at ~5500 TFLOPs/s at batch size 1024.
  - Actual MXFP4 + $H_{128}$ (with all overheads): peaks at ~5000 TFLOPs/s, about 91% of ideal.
  - Ideal NVFP4: peaks at ~4500 TFLOPs/s (NVFP4 has slightly lower theoretical throughput than MXFP4 on B200, possibly due to the smaller group size requiring more scale accesses).
  - Actual NVFP4: peaks at ~4200 TFLOPs/s.
  - Torch BF16 baseline: peaks at ~1400 TFLOPs/s.
  - Speedup of actual MXFP4 over BF16: up to 3.6× (5000/1400).

- On RTX5090, for a single linear layer (shape $51200 \times 5120$, from Qwen3-32B):
  - Ideal W4A4 (no quantization): peaks at ~1200 TFLOPs/s.
  - Actual W4A4 + $H_{32}$: peaks at ~1050 TFLOPs/s, about 88% of ideal.
  - Actual W4A4 + $H_{128}$: similar throughput.
  - Speedup over BF16: up to 6× (1050/175, approximately, reading from Figure 11).

**End-to-end inference speedup (Figure 6):**
- Llama-3.3-70B with MXFP4 on single B200, vLLM integration:
  - Prompt/decode lengths 32/128.
  - Batch sizes from 32 to 8192.
  - Speedup over BF16: 1.6–2.2×, with higher speedups at larger batch sizes.
  - At batch size 32: ~1.6× speedup.
  - At batch size 8192: ~2.2× speedup.
  - On RTX5090: up to 4× end-to-end speedup (Appendix F).

**Why MXFP4 can outperform NVFP4:** the paper notes an interesting observation (Section 5, point 4): "MXFP4 outperforms NVFP4 on B200, with up to ≈15% higher throughput, despite their closely related numerical formats. Possible contributing factors include MXFP4's use of potentially more efficient power-of-two scales as well as larger group sizes, which could reduce overhead." Power-of-two scale multiplication is a bit-shift in hardware (just adding exponents), whereas E4M3 scale multiplication requires a full floating-point multiply-add. The larger group size (32 vs. 16) means fewer scales to load, compute, and store per element. These micro-architectural factors can outweigh the numerical format differences in practical throughput.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Why block-wise Hadamard at the group size rather than global rotation:** Lemma 2's asymptotic analysis predicts that the Normal distribution (post-rotation) dominates the Laplace distribution (pre-rotation) at the group-level $G$ for MXFP4's 32-element groups but not necessarily for NVFP4's 16-element groups. Global rotation would mix information across many groups, potentially creating correlations that harm GPTQ's per-column independence assumption, without providing additional MSE benefit beyond what block-wise rotation at the group size already achieves.

**Why MSE-optimized grids with alternating minimization rather than simple absmax:** scale quantization is the dominant failure mode for MXFP4 (E8M0 forces scales to powers of two, introducing multiplicative error). Joint optimization of global and per-group scales accounts for the scale quantization step in the error objective, producing scales that minimize actual quantization error rather than idealized (unquantized-scale) error.

**Why static activation reordering rather than dynamic:** the 10–20% runtime overhead of dynamic column shuffling is significant relative to the 2–4× speedup from FP4 quantization. Static reordering achieves the same accuracy benefit (since the benefit comes from the quantization order during GPTQ, not from the stored layout) with zero runtime cost, provided scales are fixed before reordering.

**Why GPTQ rather than other weight quantization methods:** GPTQ's second-order error correction is format-agnostic — it works with any fixed quantization grid. Methods like AWQ rely on per-channel scaling that conflicts with FP4's tiny per-group scales. Methods like SpQR rely on sparse-dense decomposition that assumes large groups. GPTQ's only assumption is that the quantization operation is deterministic, which holds for any pre-computed grid.

**Why fuse online rotation with quantization:** the activation rotation $X H_k$ is memory-bound (the $k \times k$ transform requires few FLOPs per element for small $k$). Fusing rotation, scale computation, and quantization into a single kernel eliminates multiple memory round-trips (load activations → rotate → store → load for matmul → compute scales → quantize), reducing the overhead from a potential bottleneck to negligible.

**Why Hadamard over other orthogonal transforms (DCT, DST, GSR):** Appendix K (Tables 13 and 14) sweeps over DCT, DST, Hadamard, and GSR with block sizes $\{16, 32, 64, 128, 256\}$ on Llama-3-8B for both NVFP4 and MXFP4. The results show that all transforms perform similarly (within ~1% average accuracy), with Hadamard providing slightly better results on average. The practical advantages of Hadamard (fast butterfly algorithm, self-inverse property, established use in quantization literature) make it the preferred choice, but the finding that "any rotation" works similarly for these block sizes is practically important — it means the specific orthogonal structure isn't critical, only the distribution-normalizing property.

**Why matching rotation block size to group size:** Tables 13 and 14 show that for MXFP4 (group size 32), Hadamard with block size 32, 64, or 128 all perform similarly (within ~0.5% average accuracy). For NVFP4 (group size 16), Hadamard with block size 16 provides slightly better results than larger blocks, consistent with the MSE analysis that predicts optimal behavior when the rotation and quantization granularity match.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Dead-Zone Asymptotic Analysis Transforms FP4 Quantization From Empirical Tuning Into a Predictable Design Space

The paper's most intellectually distinctive contribution is not MR-GPTQ itself but the **formal MSE framework** that makes MR-GPTQ's design choices *derivable rather than discovered*. Prior to this work, the PTQ literature treated quantization error as something you measure empirically after trying a method: you run SmoothQuant or QuaRot, you check the accuracy, and if it works, you keep it. There was no predictive theory that could tell you *before running the experiment* whether a Hadamard rotation would help or hurt a particular format.

What makes the paper's analysis genuinely novel — and not merely a post-hoc justification — is that it makes a **falsifiable, counter-intuitive prediction** that the empirical results then confirm. Lemma 2 predicts a **crossover phenomenon**: for small group sizes, the Laplace distribution (native weights) has lower MSE than the Normal distribution (rotated weights), but for large group sizes the ordering reverses. This isn't an incremental refinement of known behavior — it's a *qualitatively new* prediction about the interaction between distribution shape, group size, and dead-zone underflow. Most quantization researchers would have guessed (as the prior work on QuIP and QuaRot implicitly assumed) that Gaussianization always helps, because it reduces outlier magnitude. The paper shows this intuition is wrong for small group sizes, and it explains exactly why: the Laplace's heavier tail means the block maximum doesn't grow as aggressively relative to typical elements, keeping more mass above the dead-zone threshold at small G. Figure 3 validates this directly: Hadamard transform *increases* MSErel for NVFP4 at G=16 but *decreases* it for MXFP4 at G=32.

The significance of this contribution extends beyond the immediate FP4 results. It establishes that **microscaling formats have a fundamentally different error landscape than traditional uniform quantization**, and that navigating this landscape requires understanding the interaction between three variables — distribution shape, group size, and scale precision — that were previously treated independently. This is a conceptual advance in how we think about quantization error, analogous to how the Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) transformed pretraining from empirical hill-climbing to a predictable tradeoff space. The paper doesn't claim this level of generality, but the framework it introduces — dead-zone asymptotic analysis coupled with distribution modeling — is transferable to any future microscaling format with different group sizes or element precisions.

The **scale quantization analysis** (Section 3.2, Figure 4) provides a second falsifiable prediction that proved decisive for MR-GPTQ's design: the insight that the effective precision of the top element in a block is determined by the *finer* of the base data type and the scale data type. For MXFP4, this means the top element is stuck at E2M1 precision regardless of group size (because E8M0 is coarser than E2M1), which explains *why* MXFP4 underperforms NVFP4 so dramatically — NVFP4's E4M3 scales promote the top element to effective FP8 precision. This isn't obvious from looking at the format specifications; you have to reason through the quantization pipeline to see it. Prior work treated scale formats as implementation details; this paper elevates them to first-class determinants of accuracy.

### Innovation 2: The Discovery That Existing PTQ Methods Make Implicit Assumptions Violated by FP4 — And That "Better Methods" Can Underperform RTN

A striking pattern in Table 1 is how many state-of-the-art methods **underperform simple round-to-nearest** on FP4 formats. QuaRot — a leading rotation-based method that achieves strong results on INT4 — scores 79.70% recovery on MXFP4, versus 87.83% for RTN without rotations. SpinQuant, which adds learned rotations, achieves 88.00% — barely better than doing nothing. For NVFP4, adding Hadamard rotations to RTN *reduces* recovery from 94.67% to 93.82%. These are not marginal differences; they represent catastrophic failures of methods that the field considers standard tools.

The intellectual contribution here is not the empirical observation itself — it's the **diagnostic framing** of *why* these failures occur. The paper identifies that the entire PTQ ecosystem — SmoothQuant's diagonal rescaling, QuaRot's global rotations, AWQ's channel-wise scaling — was designed under assumptions that FP4 formats explicitly violate: large group sizes (128+ elements), uniform quantization grids, and high-precision scales. When you apply these methods to formats with 16-element groups, non-uniform FP4 grids with dead zones, and power-of-two scales, the mechanisms that provide gains in the INT setting become either redundant (the small group size already mitigates outliers, so SmoothQuant adds nothing) or actively harmful (rotations spread the top element's E4M3 precision across all elements in NVFP4, degrading the one element that was well-preserved).

This is significant beyond the specific FP4 results because it reveals a **hidden coupling between method design and format properties** that the field had not articulated. The PTQ literature has largely treated quantization formats as interchangeable — you design a method for "4-bit quantization" and evaluate it on INT4, expecting similar behavior on FP4. The paper demonstrates that this assumption is false in both directions: methods that help INT4 can hurt FP4, and methods that help FP4 (like MR-GPTQ's block-wise rotations at the group size) are non-obvious from the INT4 literature. This is a **fundamental reframing** of how we should think about quantization algorithm design: not as format-agnostic error compensation, but as format-specific optimization that must account for the interaction between grid structure, scale precision, and group geometry.

The fact that **GPTQ works reasonably well across all formats** (95.7% for NVFP4, 89.5% for MXFP4) while more sophisticated methods fail is itself an important negative result with design implications. GPTQ's only assumption is that the quantization operation is deterministic — it makes no claims about group sizes, grid uniformity, or scale precision. The methods that fail make stronger assumptions that turn out to be violated. This suggests a principle: for novel formats, prefer methods with minimal format-specific assumptions until the error landscape is understood.

### Innovation 3: Block-Wise Rotations at the Quantization Group Size as a New Granularity for Distribution Normalization

Prior rotation-based quantization methods — QuIP, QuIP#, QuaRot, SpinQuant — all apply **global** orthogonal transforms to the entire weight matrix before quantization. The logic is that a single rotation can normalize the distribution across all dimensions simultaneously, and the rotation can be fused into adjacent operations (e.g., attention projections) to avoid runtime overhead. This global approach works well for INT formats with large group sizes, where the primary concern is cross-channel outlier variance.

MR-GPTQ introduces a different granularity: **block-wise rotations at the quantization group size** (16 for NVFP4, 32 for MXFP4). This isn't a minor parameter change — it represents a fundamentally different design philosophy about *what* the rotation is trying to achieve. Global rotations aim to equalize variance across all channels, making per-channel or per-tensor quantization more uniform. Block-wise rotations at the group size aim to normalize the distribution *within each quantization group*, which is the relevant unit for microscaling formats where each group gets its own scale and dead zone. The distinction matters because global rotations can create cross-group correlations that are invisible to per-group absmax scaling but visible to the dead-zone effect; block-wise rotations avoid this by ensuring each group's distribution is independently normalized.

The empirical support for this design choice is in Appendix K (Tables 13, 14) and the MSE analysis (Figure 3). The finding that Hadamard rotations with block size 32 or 128 perform similarly for MXFP4 (group size 32) but that block size 16 is optimal for NVFP4 (group size 16) confirms that **matching the rotation granularity to the quantization granularity** is what matters, not using the largest possible rotation. This is a new design principle for microscaling formats: the rotation block size should equal the quantization group size, not the matrix dimension.

The practical significance of this innovation is amplified by the QuTLASS kernel demonstration that **block-wise rotations can be supported with negligible overhead**. Prior work on global rotations (QuaRot) justified the global approach partly by the difficulty of efficiently computing per-block transforms at runtime. QuTLASS shows that for block sizes ≤ 128, the rotation is memory-bound, meaning "any rotation" costs essentially the same regardless of the specific orthogonal matrix used, and the cost is small relative to the FP4 matmul itself. This removes the efficiency argument for global rotations and opens the design space for granularity-matched transforms.

### Innovation 4: Format-Specialized Quantization as a Meta-Principle — And the Finding That MXFP4 Can Approach NVFP4 Accuracy Through Algorithm Alone

The paper's title promises to "bridge the gap between promise and performance," and the most practically impactful finding is quantitative: **MR-GPTQ closes the MXFP4-to-NVFP4 accuracy gap from ~7 percentage points to 1–2 percentage points** (Table 1, comparing MXFP4 + RTN at 87.83% recovery vs. MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ at 93.31%, with NVFP4 + MR-GPTQ at 96.08%). On large models (Llama-3.3-70B, Table 6), MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ achieves 98.4% recovery, within 0.7 points of NVFP4 + GPTQ (99.1%).

This is significant not primarily as a metric gain — though 5+ percentage points on challenging benchmarks like GSM8K (67.85% → 75.70% for MXFP4, Table 1) is substantial — but as a **demonstration that algorithmic innovation can compensate for format inferiority**. MXFP4 is objectively worse than NVFP4 on every dimension: coarser scale quantization (powers of two), larger group size (32 vs. 16), and higher dead-zone underflow risk. The hardware industry's implicit message was that these format differences represent a fixed accuracy hierarchy: if you want accuracy, use NVFP4; if you want efficiency, use MXFP4. The paper shows this hierarchy is not fixed — it is partially **algorithmically addressable**. MR-GPTQ's three components target the specific failure modes that make MXFP4 worse (scale quantization error via grid optimization, dead-zone underflow via distribution normalization at the group size, and GPTQ error propagation via static reordering), and the combination recovers most of the gap.

This finding has direct economic implications. MXFP4 requires 4.25 bits per element versus NVFP4's 4.5 bits, which translates to a ~6% memory reduction. More importantly, Figure 5 shows that MXFP4 achieves *higher* throughput than NVFP4 on B200 GPUs — up to ~15% higher — due to power-of-two scale arithmetic and fewer scale accesses. If MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ achieves comparable accuracy to NVFP4, then the economically rational choice is MXFP4: better performance (throughput) at lower memory cost with essentially equivalent accuracy. The paper doesn't claim MR-GPTQ makes MXFP4 *equal* to NVFP4 — the 1–2 point gap remains — but it makes the choice between formats a genuine engineering tradeoff rather than an obvious decision in NVFP4's favor.

The **MXFP scale fitting** modification in Appendix H reinforces this principle. By simply remapping E8M0's excessive dynamic range onto the actual observed scale range, RTN accuracy on MXFP4 improves by 6.5 percentage points (Table 11). This isn't a sophisticated algorithmic contribution — it's a straightforward observation that the format specification was designed for generality (covering 255 powers of two) rather than optimality for LLM weight distributions. The fact that such a simple fix provides such a large gain highlights how much low-hanging fruit exists in format-specialized algorithm design, and how much accuracy was being left on the table by treating FP4 formats as interchangeable with INT4.

### Innovation 5: The "Integer Microscaling" Thought Experiment Reveals That Format Choice, Not Just Bit-Width, Determines the Optimal Algorithm

The paper's analysis of hypothetical **NVINT4 and MXINT4** formats (Appendix D, Tables 1–10) is a methodological contribution disguised as an ablation. These formats don't exist in hardware — they're constructed by the authors to answer a specific counterfactual: *what if microscaling had been implemented with integer grids instead of floating-point grids?*

The results are instructive in ways that challenge conventional wisdom. NVINT4 with MR-GPTQ achieves 97.12% recovery on Llama-3.1-8B — the highest of any format, including NVFP4 (96.08%) and INT4 (94.71% with RTN+HT). This suggests that **the microscaling structure (small groups with shared scales) is more important than the choice between INT and FP element types**, at least for the group sizes used here. The combination of INT4's uniform grid (no dead zone) with NVFP4's fine E4M3 scales produces a format that avoids both the dead-zone underflow of FP4 and the coarse-scale quantization of standard INT4 with FP16 scales.

More provocatively, Figure 10 shows that Hadamard rotations **help NVINT4** (reducing MSErel) while they hurt NVFP4 at G=16. This is the exact prediction from the paper's theoretical framework: NVINT4 has no dead zone (the uniform INT grid has no gap between zero and the first representable value), so the Lemma 2 analysis doesn't apply — the dominant error mechanism is different. For INT formats, rotations help by equalizing variance and reducing outlier-induced clipping; for FP formats, rotations can hurt by increasing dead-zone underflow at small group sizes. The fact that the same analytical framework correctly predicts opposite behavior for INT and FP microscaling formats is strong evidence that the framework captures something real about the underlying error dynamics.

This thought experiment is significant because it **reframes the FP4 vs. INT4 debate** that the paper's introduction sets up. The hardware vendors' claim that "FP4 is more accurate than INT4" is revealed to be not a property of floating-point representation per se, but an artifact of the specific format designs: NVFP4's E4M3 scales + FP4 elements happen to work better than standard INT4 with FP16 scales, but NVINT4 with E4M3 scales + INT4 elements would work even better. The insight is that **scale precision, group size, and grid structure are the primary determinants of accuracy, not the choice between floating-point and integer arithmetic**. This has implications for future hardware design: if microscaling INT4 could be supported with the same hardware efficiency as microscaling FP4, it might be the optimal format for LLM inference — but this format doesn't exist in current hardware, so the practical choice remains constrained.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All accuracy experiments use standard zero-shot evaluation tasks drawn from the Open LLM Leaderboard V1 (Beeching et al., 2023): GSM8K (grade-school math, Cobbe et al., 2021), MMLU (world knowledge and reasoning, Hendrycks et al., 2020, with Chain-of-Thought prompting as noted in the tables), HellaSwag (language understanding, Zellers et al., 2019), and Winogrande (language understanding, Sakaguchi et al., 2021). For the PlatinumBench analysis (Appendix I), 14 carefully curated tasks are used including GSM8K, MMLU-Math, BBH subsets, SQuAD, DROP, and others (Vendrow et al., 2025). An additional sweep over PIQA, ARC-Easy, and ARC-Challenge appears in Appendix K. All evaluations are zero-shot.

- **Base model(s).** The primary model family is Llama-3, spanning Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Dubey et al., 2024). The Qwen-3 family (Yang et al., 2024) is evaluated at 8B, 14B, and 32B scales. The choice spans 1B to 70B parameters, covering regimes where quantization is expected to be challenging (small models, where parameter redundancy is lower) and relatively forgiving (large models, where redundancy provides error tolerance). For Qwen-3 models, thinking mode is turned off because "long reasoning chains-of-thought turned out to be detrimental for performance on GSM8k and MMLU-CoT" (Appendix B).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **average recovery percentage**: the ratio of the quantized model's average accuracy across the four standard benchmarks (or five for Appendix K) to the FP16 baseline model's average accuracy, expressed as a percentage (100% = no accuracy loss). For example, if the FP16 baseline achieves 78.93% average accuracy and the quantized model achieves 75.84%, the recovery is 75.84/78.93 × 100 = 96.08%. Individual task accuracies are also reported. In Appendix I, PlatinumBench recovery is reported similarly. Standard deviations are computed over 5 random seeds for the variance analysis (Appendix J, Table 12). For the MSE analysis (Section 3.2, Figure 3), the metrics are $\text{MSE}_{\text{rel}}$ (per-block MSE normalized by the block's total energy) and $\text{MSE}_{\text{rel}}^{\text{top}}$ (relative squared error on the top-magnitude element per block).

- **Baselines.** The paper implements an extensive set of baselines spanning multiple PTQ paradigms:
  1. **RTN** (Round-to-Nearest): standard absmax scaling followed by direct quantization to the target format grid. Evaluated both with and without Hadamard rotations (RTN + HT), where rotations are applied at the quantization group size unless otherwise specified.
  2. **SmoothQuant** (Xiao et al., 2023): diagonal rescaling to redistribute outlier magnitudes between weights and activations. An $\alpha$ smoothening factor is tuned; $\alpha = 0.6$ is identified as best across experiments.
  3. **QuaRot** (Ashkboos et al., 2024): strategic Hadamard rotations inserted at each linear layer, with most rotations folded into adjacent operations. RTN quantization is used post-rotation.
  4. **SpinQuant** (Liu et al., 2024): trainable rotation matrices optimized on a 1024-sample calibration subset from FineWeb, otherwise similar to QuaRot.
  5. **GPTQ** (Frantar et al., 2022): standard second-order weight quantization with Hessian dampening $\lambda = 10^{-2}$, 1024 calibration sequences from FineWeb, and standard fixed-column quantization order. For W4A4 experiments, activations use RTN.
  6. **MR-GPTQ** (this paper): the proposed variant with block-wise Hadamard rotations, MSE scale optimization, and static activation reordering.
  7. **AWQ** (Lin et al., 2024): evaluated only in the weight-only control experiments (Appendix A, Table 2).
  8. **QAT** (Quantization-Aware Training): performed using the balanced Generalized Jensen-Shannon Divergence loss (Englesson & Azizpour, 2021; Lee et al., 2025) between quantized and frozen full-precision model token distributions, on 92,995 samples (10% subset) from the Tülu 3 instruction dataset (Lambert et al., 2024).
  9. Additional baselines in Appendix K include DCT, DST, and GSR transforms (Choi et al., 2025) at block sizes {16, 32, 64, 128, 256}.
  For the **weight-only control experiments** (Appendix A), RTN, GPTQ, AWQ, and RTN+Hadamard are evaluated.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper uses a qualitative accuracy comparison rather than a FLOPs-matched compute budget, since the goal is to assess *format accuracy*, not to optimize inference-time compute allocation. All methods within a format comparison use the same weight and activation tensors, same calibration data (where applicable), and same evaluation protocol. The main controlled variable is the quantization format (INT4, INT8, FP8, NVFP4, MXFP4, NVINT4, MXINT4) and the quantization algorithm. For the kernel performance experiments (Figures 5, 6, 11, 12), throughput is measured in TFLOPs/s or tokens/second, and speedup is computed relative to a BF16 baseline on the same hardware. The "ideal" throughput curves in Figures 5 and 11 represent pure FP4 matrix multiplication without quantization-related kernel overhead.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the variance analysis in Appendix J, 5 distinct random seeds are used for GPTQ calibration set sampling and for scale selection/quantization ordering strategies. Standard deviations are reported in Table 12. The SmoothQuant $\alpha$ hyperparameter is tuned via sweep and fixed at $\alpha = 0.6$ for all main experiments. For the Appendix K rotation sweep, all transform types and block sizes are evaluated on the same 5-task subset averaged together. No formal cross-validation is used for strategy selection; the MR-GPTQ design choices (block size matching group size, static reordering, MSE scale optimization) are fixed based on the theoretical analysis in Section 3 and validated on the test benchmarks, which raises a minor concern about potential overfitting to the specific evaluation tasks, though the consistency across model families and the PlatinumBench replication partially mitigates this.

### Main Quantitative Results

The experimental results are organized around four central questions: (1) How do NVFP4 and MXFP4 compare to each other and to INT4 for LLM PTQ? (2) Do existing PTQ methods transfer effectively to FP4 formats? (3) Can MR-GPTQ close the accuracy gap between MXFP4 and NVFP4? (4) What are the practical inference speedups achievable with MR-GPTQ and the QuTLASS kernels?

#### Aggregate Format Comparison: FP4 Is Not an Automatic Upgrade Over INT4

The headline aggregate comparison appears in Table 1 for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct under W4A4 quantization. The FP16 baseline achieves 78.93% average accuracy across MMLU-CoT (72.76%), GSM8k (85.06%), HellaSwag (80.01%), and Winogrande (77.90%).

**For NVFP4**, the best PTQ methods (SmoothQuant, GPTQ, and MR-GPTQ) achieve 95.7–96.1% recovery (75.70–75.84% average accuracy), which is roughly comparable to INT4 with RTN+Hadamard (94.71% recovery, 74.75% average). The gap between NVFP4's best methods and the FP16 baseline is approximately 3–4 percentage points of average accuracy — noticeable but not catastrophic. RTN alone achieves 94.67% recovery (74.73% average), demonstrating that NVFP4's format properties (16-element groups, E4M3 scales) provide decent out-of-the-box accuracy even without sophisticated algorithms.

**For MXFP4**, the situation is dramatically worse. RTN alone achieves only 87.83% recovery (69.32% average) — a drop of nearly 10 percentage points from the FP16 baseline. This is substantially worse than INT4 with RTN+HT (94.71%) and NVFP4 with RTN (94.67%). The degradation is most severe on reasoning tasks: GSM8k drops from 85.06% to 67.85% (a 17.21-point absolute decline) and MMLU-CoT drops from 72.76% to 62.21% (a 10.55-point decline). Standard GPTQ provides only modest improvement (89.47% recovery, 70.62% average), and QuaRot is actively harmful (79.70% recovery, 62.90% average) — worse than doing nothing. This establishes the core finding that **MXFP4 is not a drop-in replacement for INT4 or NVFP4; it requires format-specialized methods.**

**For INT4** (with group size 32 and FP16 scales, matching MXFP4's bit-width), RTN achieves 92.63% recovery, and RTN+Hadamard achieves 94.71% — both outperforming MXFP4 by substantial margins. This challenges the industry claim that FP4 is inherently more accurate than INT4 at the same bit-width. NVFP4 and INT4 are roughly comparable, with NVFP4 having lower variance (approximately 0.3 points standard deviation vs. approximately 1 point for INT4, as reported in the text discussion of Table 1).

**For the hypothetical integer microscaling formats** (NVINT4 and MXINT4) introduced in Appendix D and evaluated in Table 1:

- **NVINT4** with MR-GPTQ achieves 97.12% recovery (76.66% average) — the highest of any W4A4 configuration, surpassing both NVFP4 + MR-GPTQ (96.08%) and INT4 + RTN+HT (94.71%). This is notable because NVINT4 combines INT4's uniform grid (no dead zone) with NVFP4's fine E4M3 scales.
- **MXINT4** with MR-GPTQ achieves 91.86% recovery (72.51% average), substantially better than MXFP4 (93.31% with MR-GPTQ, but note that MXFP4 benefits more from rotations as predicted by the analysis).
- The ordering across formats for the best method is: NVINT4 (97.12%) > NVFP4 (96.08%) > INT4 (94.71%) > MXINT4 (91.86%) > MXFP4 (93.31% with MR-GPTQ; but note MXFP4 is worse with simpler methods). The finding that NVINT4 outperforms NVFP4 suggests that **scale precision and group size, not floating-point vs. integer representation, are the dominant factors in microscaling accuracy.**

#### Method Effectiveness: Existing Techniques Do Not Transfer Cleanly to FP4

**Table 1 reveals a clear hierarchy of method effectiveness that differs sharply between formats:**

For **NVFP4**:
- SmoothQuant achieves the highest recovery at 95.90% (75.70% average), narrowly edging GPTQ (95.92%, 75.72%) and MR-GPTQ (96.08%, 75.84%). These three are within variance of each other (approximately 0.3 points standard deviation).
- Adding Hadamard rotations to RTN *reduces* recovery from 94.67% to 93.82% — a clear negative effect, confirming the theoretical prediction that rotations can increase dead-zone underflow at small group sizes.
- QuaRot (93.80%) and SpinQuant (93.40%) underperform RTN alone, indicating that global rotation-based methods designed for INT formats are actively harmful for NVFP4.
- The effectiveness of SmoothQuant is notable because it operates on a different principle from rotations — it redistributes outliers between weights and activations via diagonal rescaling, which interacts more favorably with NVFP4's small groups than rotation-based normalization does.

For **MXFP4**:
- MR-GPTQ achieves 93.31% recovery (73.65% average), dramatically outperforming all other PTQ methods: GPTQ (89.47%), RTN+HT (89.26%), SmoothQuant (89.06%), SpinQuant (88.00%), and QuaRot (79.70%).
- The gap between MR-GPTQ and the next-best PTQ method (GPTQ) is 3.84 percentage points — this is the largest single-method improvement for MXFP4.
- QuaRot is catastrophically bad: 79.70% recovery, 62.90% average. This represents a 9-point drop from RTN alone on MMLU-CoT (49.86% vs. 62.21%), suggesting that the combination of global rotations with MXFP4's coarse scale quantization creates severe error amplification.
- The Hadamard transform *does* help MXFP4 when applied with RTN: RTN alone achieves 87.83% recovery, RTN+Hadamard achieves 89.26%. This small positive effect is consistent with the theoretical prediction that rotations should benefit MXFP4's larger group size (32) but not NVFP4's smaller groups (16).

For **INT4**:
- RTN+Hadamard achieves 94.71% recovery, outperforming standard RTN (92.63%) and GPTQ (92.75%). This is the expected behavior from the INT quantization literature — rotations help by normalizing distributions and spreading outlier energy.
- GPTQ actually underperforms RTN+Hadamard slightly (92.75% vs. 94.71%), which is unusual and may reflect suboptimal hyperparameters for the INT4 grid.

The key takeaway from the method comparison is that **no single method dominates across all formats**: SmoothQuant is best for NVFP4, MR-GPTQ is best for MXFP4, and RTN+HT is competitive for INT4. This directly supports the paper's central claim that format-specialized algorithms are necessary.

#### MR-GPTQ Component Analysis: Which Ingredients Matter Most?

The detailed component ablation in Appendix I (Figures 13, 14, and Table 12) disentangles the contributions of MR-GPTQ's three ingredients on the PlatinumBench benchmark, which provides lower-noise evaluations:

**For NVFP4** (Figure 13a, 13c):
- The best configuration is Hadamard with block size 16 (matching the group size) + MSE scale optimization + static activation reordering, achieving 96.57% recovery (±0.75 points standard deviation).
- Removing the Hadamard transform (Ident + MSE + ActOrder) reduces recovery to approximately 93.51% (±1.30 points) — a 3-point drop, indicating that block-wise rotations provide a statistically significant benefit even for NVFP4 when coupled with MSE scale optimization and GPTQ error correction, despite the theoretical prediction that rotations hurt NVFP4 with RTN. This suggests that GPTQ's error correction can compensate for the increased per-group MSE that rotations introduce.
- The distinction between MSE and MinMax scale optimization matters more for configurations without rotations (Ident + MSE = 93.51% vs. Ident + MinMax = 93.27%) than with rotations (Had16 + MSE = 96.57% vs. Had16 + MinMax = 93.77%).
- Block size matters: Had16 (96.57%) outperforms Had128 (93.95%), confirming that matching the rotation granularity to the group size is beneficial.

**For MXFP4** (Figure 13b, 13d):
- The gap between rotated and non-rotated configurations is much larger than for NVFP4: the best rotated configuration (Had32 + MinMax + ActOrder) achieves 91.73% recovery, while the best non-rotated configuration (Ident + MinMax + ActOrder) achieves only 85.93% — a 5.8-point gap. This confirms that rotations are far more critical for MXFP4 than for NVFP4.
- For MXFP4, larger block sizes help slightly: Had128 + MinMax + ActOrder achieves 92.32% (±1.13 points), marginally better than Had32 + MinMax + ActOrder at 91.73% (±1.18 points). This is the opposite of NVFP4 (where matching the group size was best), and may reflect that MXFP4's larger native group size (32) benefits from the additional mixing provided by 128-element rotations.
- Static activation reordering provides consistent but modest gains: within the same transform and scale configuration, ActOrder variants typically outperform DefOrder (default arbitrary order) by 1–3 percentage points.

**For the standard benchmark suite** (Table 12):
- The variance for NVFP4 across 5 seeds is approximately 0.3–0.7 points standard deviation, confirming that the format differences between top methods (SmoothQuant, GPTQ, MR-GPTQ) are largely within noise. The paper's practice of bolding all top aggregate entries within 2 standard deviations is appropriate.
- For MXFP4, the variance is slightly higher (0.3–0.8 points), with MR-GPTQ configurations clearly separated from non-rotated baselines (92.79–93.78% recovery vs. 89.16–90.54% for Ident variants).
- On PlatinumBench, standard deviations are larger (0.7–2.3 points) due to the smaller number of evaluation samples, but the qualitative ordering is preserved: rotations provide large gains for MXFP4 and smaller but still meaningful gains for NVFP4.

#### Weight-Only Quantization: The Error Is Evenly Split Between Weights and Activations

Appendix A (Table 2) provides a "control" experiment with weight-only quantization (W4A16) on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. This is important for diagnosing whether the accuracy degradation in W4A4 comes primarily from weight quantization, activation quantization, or their interaction.

Under weight-only quantization:
- **INT4** with RTN achieves 97.71% recovery (77.12% average), only 1–2 points below FP16. RTN+Hadamard achieves 97.76%. GPTQ achieves 97.05%. The recovery is very high because only weights are compressed; activations remain in FP16.
- **NVFP4** with RTN achieves 98.02% recovery (77.37% average), and AWQ achieves 98.06%. GPTQ achieves 97.96%. These are essentially equivalent to INT4 — the weight-only degradation is minimal.
- **MXFP4** with RTN achieves 95.58% recovery (75.44% average) — a 2-point drop from INT4/NVFP4 even in the weight-only setting. GPTQ improves this to 96.76% (76.37% average). AWQ is slightly worse at 95.25%.

Comparing weight-only to weight-and-activation results: the FP16 baseline achieves 79.70% average (Table 2, weight-only evaluation) vs. 78.93% (Table 1, W4A4 evaluation), reflecting minor evaluation variance. Under NVFP4 + RTN, the drop from FP16 is 79.70% → 77.37% (weight-only) vs. 78.93% → 74.73% (W4A4). The additional degradation from activation quantization is approximately 2.6 percentage points (77.37% → 74.73%). Under MXFP4 + RTN, the weight-only drop is 79.70% → 75.44% (4.26 points), and the W4A4 drop is 78.93% → 69.32% (9.61 points). The activation quantization adds approximately 5.35 points of additional degradation for MXFP4 vs. 2.6 points for NVFP4, indicating that MXFP4's activation quantization is particularly damaging — likely because activation distributions have heavier tails (kurtosis 8.75 per Figure 2) that interact poorly with MXFP4's coarse scale quantization.

The paper notes that "the induced error is roughly evenly split between weight and activation quantization" (Section 5, discussion point 1). For NVFP4, this characterization is roughly accurate (the 2.6-point activation contribution vs. the total 4-point W4A4 drop). For MXFP4, the split is more like 45% weight / 55% activation, with activations being the larger contributor.

#### Model Scale and Family: Larger Models Are More Robust, but Qwen Is More Forgiving Than Llama

Tables 3–9 and Table 10 extend the evaluation across model scales and families. The key patterns:

**Llama-3 family scaling (Tables 3–6):**
- **1B model** (Table 3): Recovery is poor across all formats. The best NVFP4 method (GPTQ+Had16) achieves only 87.35% recovery (46.70% average vs. 53.47% FP16). MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ achieves 79.80% recovery with Had32. The absolute accuracy is low enough that FP4 quantization at this model scale might not be practically useful for many applications. QAT provides meaningful gains for MXFP4 (84.55% recovery with QAT+Had32), suggesting that PTQ alone may be insufficient for very small models.
- **3B model** (Table 4): Recovery improves substantially. NVFP4 reaches 95.51% with GPTQ and 96.64% with QAT. MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ (Had32) achieves 92.69% recovery. The gap between NVFP4 and MXFP4 narrows to approximately 3 points.
- **8B model** (Table 5): NVFP4 reaches 95.70% with GPTQ and 96.50% with QAT+Had. MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ (Had32) achieves 93.30% recovery. Note that the numbers in Table 5 differ slightly from Table 1 due to the evaluation being performed on real kernels via vLLM rather than simulated quantization, with the paper noting "slightly lower recoveries (within 0.2-0.3%)" for the real-kernel evaluation.
- **70B model** (Table 6): NVFP4 reaches 99.24% recovery with GPTQ+Had128 and 99.07% with standard GPTQ — essentially lossless. MXFP4 reaches 98.48% recovery with GPTQ+Had128 and 98.40% with GPTQ+Had32. The gap between formats shrinks to approximately 0.8 points. At this scale, both formats are practically usable.

**Qwen-3 family (Tables 7–9):**
- **8B** (Table 7): NVFP4 + RTN achieves 98.86% recovery — dramatically better than Llama-3.1-8B's 94.80% with the same method. MXFP4 + QAT+Had32 achieves 98.44% recovery. This suggests that Qwen-3's weight and activation distributions are inherently more quantization-friendly.
- **14B** (Table 8): NVFP4 achieves 98.94% recovery with GPTQ+Had16. MXFP4 achieves 97.28% with GPTQ+Had32.
- **32B** (Table 9): NVFP4 + RTN achieves 99.76% recovery — essentially perfect. MXFP4 + RTN+Had32 achieves 98.67% recovery, an unusual case where RTN+Had32 outperforms more sophisticated methods (GPTQ+Had32 achieves only 95.58%). This anomaly may reflect evaluation noise at this high-recovery ceiling, or an interaction between GPTQ's error correction and Qwen-3's specific weight structure.

The aggregate recovery percentages across all models are compiled in Table 10. Averaging over the three Llama-3 models (1B, 3B, 8B), the ordering for the best PTQ method is: NVINT4 + MR-GPTQ (96.3%) > NVFP4 + MR-GPTQ (95.8% at 8B, but lower for smaller models) > MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ (93.3% at 8B, but 79.8% at 1B). Qwen-3 models achieve uniformly higher recoveries, with NVFP4 + RTN reaching 99.8% at 32B and MXFP4 + RTN+Had32 reaching 98.7% at 32B.

A key practical insight from the scaling analysis: **PTQ methods are sufficient for large models (70B+), but smaller models (≤3B) may require QAT to achieve acceptable accuracy with FP4 formats**, particularly MXFP4. The paper's QAT experiments (Tables 3–5, 7) show consistent improvements over PTQ for small models, with QAT+Hadamard configurations often recovering 2–5 percentage points over the best PTQ method for MXFP4 on 1B and 3B models.

#### Real Quantization vs. Simulation: The Gap Is Small but Consistent

The experiments in Table 1 are performed with simulated quantization in PyTorch, while the per-model Tables 3–9 are performed with real kernels via the QuTLASS vLLM integration. The paper notes (Section 5, point 2) that "accuracies measured over real kernels for the Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct model track closely with the results from simulation, with slightly lower recoveries (within 0.2-0.3%)." This is an important validation — it confirms that the simulation is faithful and that the compiled FP4 kernels don't introduce unexpected numerical discrepancies. The small gap may reflect minor differences in how RTN ties are resolved or how the FP4 grid is implemented in hardware vs. simulation.

#### Kernel and Inference Performance: Near-Ideal Throughput With Negligible Overhead

The performance experiments (Figures 5, 6, 11, 12) demonstrate that MR-GPTQ's online rotation adds minimal overhead to FP4 matrix multiplication.

**Single-layer throughput (Figure 5, B200):**
- For a linear layer with shape 57344×8192 (from Llama-3.3-70B), torch BF16 achieves approximately 1400 TFLOPs/s at peak batch size (8192).
- Ideal MXFP4 (pure matmul, no quantization overhead) peaks at ~5500 TFLOPs/s.
- Actual MXFP4 with $H_{128}$ rotation peaks at ~5000 TFLOPs/s — a speedup of 3.6× over BF16, and 91% of the ideal throughput.
- Ideal NVFP4 peaks at ~4500 TFLOPs/s.
- Actual NVFP4 peaks at ~4200 TFLOPs/s.
- MXFP4 outperforms NVFP4 by ~15% in actual throughput, attributed to power-of-two scale arithmetic and fewer scale accesses due to larger group size.

**Single-layer throughput (Figure 11, RTX5090):**
- For a linear layer with shape 51200×5120 (from Qwen3-32B), BF16 peaks at approximately 175 TFLOPs/s (estimated from the figure).
- Ideal W4A4 (no quantization) peaks at ~1200 TFLOPs/s.
- Actual W4A4 + $H_{32}$ peaks at ~1050 TFLOPs/s — a speedup of 6× over BF16, and 88% of ideal.
- $H_{128}$ achieves similar throughput to $H_{32}$, confirming that the rotation overhead is largely independent of block size for this range.

**End-to-end inference (Figure 6, B200, vLLM + Llama-3.3-70B with MXFP4):**
- Prompt/decode lengths are 32/128 tokens.
- Speedup over BF16 ranges from ~1.6× at batch size 32 to ~2.2× at batch size 8192.
- The speedup increases with batch size because the matmul becomes a larger fraction of total runtime relative to attention and other operations that aren't quantized.

**End-to-end inference (Figure 12, B200, vLLM + Llama-3.3-70B, multiple formats):**
- For large batch sizes (≥256), MR-MXFP4 provides the highest throughput at ~15,000 tok/sec: 2.2× over BF16 and 1.3× over FP8.
- For small batch sizes (≤32), weight-only NVFP4 is preferable: it achieves approximately 20% lower latency than FP8.
- The paper explicitly notes that for small-batch (memory-bound) inference, "activation quantization brings no benefits to inference speed" and recommends weight-only quantization. This is an important practical caveat: the full MR-GPTQ W4A4 approach is most beneficial for high-throughput, large-batch serving scenarios, while simpler weight-only FP4 quantization is sufficient for latency-sensitive, single-user inference.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Transform type and block size (Appendix K, Tables 13–14):** For Llama-3-8B with NVFP4, all transforms (DCT, DST, Hadamard, GSR) at various block sizes {16, 32, 64, 128, 256} perform within approximately 1% average accuracy of each other. Hadamard with block size 128 achieves the highest average (71.46% with GPTQ vs. 72.74% FP16 baseline = 98.2% recovery). For MXFP4, the spread is slightly larger (~2% range), with Hadamard at block size 64 achieving the highest (70.27% with GPTQ = 96.6% recovery). The finding that "all transforms work similarly" is practically significant — it means the specific orthogonal structure isn't critical, only the distribution-normalizing property, and implementers can choose the most hardware-efficient option (Hadamard, with its fast butterfly algorithm).

**Block size matching group size (Tables 13–14 and Figure 13):** For NVFP4 (group size 16), Hadamard-16 generally outperforms Hadamard-128 by about 1–2 points on PlatinumBench (Figure 13c: Had16+MSE+ActOrder = 96.57% vs. Had128+MSE+ActOrder = 93.95%). For MXFP4 (group size 32), Hadamard-128 slightly outperforms Hadamard-32 on PlatinumBench (92.32% vs. 91.73%, Figure 13d), but the difference is within standard deviation. The paper's recommendation to match block size to group size is justified for NVFP4 but less critical for MXFP4.

**Scale quantization format (Appendix C, Figures 8–9):** For weight and activation tensors from Llama-3.1-8B, E4M3 and E8M0 scales increase MSErel by approximately 10% and 40% respectively compared to FP16 scales. Alternative 8-bit scale formats with larger mantissas (E1M6, E2M5, E3M4) and INT8 perform closer to FP16. This ablation demonstrates that the scale quantization format is a first-order determinant of MXFP4's accuracy problems, and suggests that future hardware iterations could substantially improve MXFP4 accuracy by adopting a scale format with mantissa bits.

**MXFP scale fitting (Appendix H, Table 11):** Applying the range-fitting modification (MXFP4$^\dagger$) improves RTN recovery on Llama-3.1-8B from 87.8% to 94.3% (+6.5 points), nearly matching NVFP4's RTN recovery (94.7%). For MR-GPTQ, the improvement is smaller (+1.3 points), since MR-GPTQ's other components already mitigate some scale quantization error. On Qwen3-8B, MXFP4$^\dagger$ provides gains of 2.6–3.3 points depending on the method. This ablation confirms that scale quantization error is the dominant failure mode for MXFP4, and that addressing it directly (via range-fitting or via MR-GPTQ's scale optimization) provides the largest accuracy gains.

**Static vs. dynamic activation reordering (inferred from Section 4.1 discussion):** The paper states that dynamic act-order "consistently improves accuracy" but "results in a 10-20% end-to-end inference slow-down." The static variant "provides similar improvements to standard 'dynamic' act-order, without the runtime overheads." While no direct ablation table compares static vs. dynamic act-order, the PlatinumBench results (Figures 13–14) consistently show that configurations with ActOrder outperform those with DefOrder by 1–3 points, and the paper's assertion of equivalence to dynamic act-order is based on the standard GPTQ literature where this relationship is established.

**PRM scale optimization (MSE vs. MinMax):** On PlatinumBench, MSE optimization provides consistent improvements over MinMax for both NVFP4 (e.g., Had16+MSE+ActOrder = 96.57% vs. Had16+MinMax+ActOrder = 93.77%, a 2.8-point gap) and MXFP4 (Had32+MSE+ActOrder = 90.51% vs. Had32+MinMax+ActOrder = 91.73% — here MinMax is slightly better, suggesting the benefit depends on configuration). For the standard benchmark suite (Table 12), MSE and MinMax are within variance of each other for most configurations, suggesting that the benefit is real but moderate and task-dependent.

**GPTQ vs. RTN on rotated weights:** For NVFP4, RTN+Hadamard achieves 93.82% recovery vs. MR-GPTQ's 96.08% (Table 1) — the GPTQ error correction adds approximately 2.3 points of recovery on top of the rotation. For MXFP4, RTN+Hadamard achieves 89.26% vs. MR-GPTQ's 93.31% — a 4-point gain from GPTQ. This demonstrates that rotations alone are insufficient; the second-order error correction is essential for bridging the gap to practical accuracy.

**QAT as an upper bound:** Across all model scales, QAT provides modest improvements over PTQ for NVFP4 (typically 0–1 point) and larger improvements for MXFP4 (2–5 points on small models, 0–2 points on large models). This establishes that PTQ with MR-GPTQ is close to the achievable accuracy ceiling for FP4 formats without expensive retraining.

**Hadamard block size for NVINT4 and MXINT4 (Figure 10, Tables 3–5):** The error analysis in Figure 10 predicts that Hadamard rotations should help both NVINT4 and MXINT4 (unlike NVFP4, where rotations hurt at small G). This is confirmed empirically: NVINT4 + RTN+Had16 achieves 85.21% recovery vs. NVINT4 + RTN at 81.64% on Llama-3.2-1B (Table 3) — a 3.6-point gain. MXINT4 benefits even more dramatically from rotations: MXINT4 + MR-GPTQ achieves 91.21% recovery vs. MXINT4 + RTN at 76.71% on Llama-3.2-3B (Table 4) — a 14.5-point gain. These results validate the theoretical framework's prediction that dead-zone-free integer formats benefit from rotations regardless of group size, while floating-point formats with dead zones exhibit a group-size-dependent crossover.

**Variance across random seeds (Appendix J, Table 12):** Over 5 seeds, the standard deviation for NVFP4 methods on the standard benchmark ranges from 0.16 to 0.66 points (average ~0.42), and on PlatinumBench from 0.75 to 1.68 points (average ~1.33). For MXFP4, standard deviations are 0.33–0.82 on standard benchmarks and 0.74–2.29 on PlatinumBench. The higher variance on PlatinumBench reflects the smaller number of evaluation samples in that benchmark. The paper's practice of bolding top entries within 2 standard deviations means that methods within approximately 0.8 points (standard) or 2.7 points (Platinum) of each other are considered statistically indistinguishable. This appropriately tempers the ranking of closely-matched methods like SmoothQuant vs. GPTQ vs. MR-GPTQ for NVFP4.

**Negative result — QuaRot harms MXFP4 catastrophically:** Table 1 shows QuaRot achieving 79.70% recovery on MXFP4, substantially worse than RTN alone (87.83%). The MMLU-CoT score drops from 72.76% (FP16) to 49.86% (QuaRot + MXFP4) — a 22.9-point collapse that is far beyond any other method-format combination. This negative result is important because it demonstrates that methods designed for INT formats can fail not just mildly but catastrophically when applied to FP4 formats with the wrong assumptions.

**Negative result — ReST$^{EM}$ revision training degrades performance:** While not a primary experiment in this paper, the related finding in the main text that ReST$^{EM}$-style RL optimization of the quantization model was not explored (the paper's QAT uses a different loss) is a minor gap. The QAT results that do exist (Tables 3–9) show consistent but moderate gains, suggesting that the current PTQ approaches are close to the practical ceiling.

**Robustness to evaluation benchmark:** The PlatinumBench analysis (Appendix I) largely confirms the standard benchmark findings, with the same ordering of methods and similar gaps. However, PlatinumBench reveals that the Hadamard transform provides a statistically significant advantage for NVFP4 (Figure 13c), which was less clear from the noisier standard benchmarks. This suggests that lower-noise evaluation benchmarks are valuable for making fine-grained method comparisons in the high-recovery regime where differences are small.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the evidence support the claim that "state-of-the-art methods struggle with FP4"?

**Yes, strongly supported.** Table 1 provides direct evidence: QuaRot achieves 79.70% recovery on MXFP4 (worse than RTN at 87.83%), SpinQuant achieves 88.00% (barely better than RTN), and SmoothQuant achieves 89.06% (only marginally better than RTN). Even GPTQ, the most format-agnostic method, leaves MXFP4 at 89.47% recovery — a 10-point gap from FP16. The evidence that *existing methods underperform or fail* is unambiguous and consistent across model scales.

However, the claim should be qualified: the "struggle" is primarily with MXFP4, not NVFP4. For NVFP4, SmoothQuant (95.90%), GPTQ (95.92%), and MR-GPTQ (96.08%) all achieve recovery within ~4 points of FP16, which, while not lossless, is arguably acceptable for many applications. The "struggle" narrative applies most forcefully to MXFP4, where even the best non-MR-GPTQ method (GPTQ) leaves a >10-point accuracy gap relative to FP16 on the 8B model.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that "NVFP4's small group size provably neutralizes traditional outlier mitigation techniques"?

**Supported, but the "provably" qualifier refers to the theoretical analysis, not the experimental results directly.** Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 provide the analytical proof that the dead-zone behavior differs between distributions and group sizes, and Figure 3 validates this on synthetic and real data at the MSE level. The end-to-end accuracy experiments (Table 1) show that Hadamard rotations — the primary outlier mitigation technique — *reduce* NVFP4 RTN accuracy (94.67% → 93.82%), which is consistent with the theoretical prediction. However, the paper does not provide a direct controlled experiment where a specific outlier mitigation technique (e.g., SmoothQuant's channel-wise scaling) is shown to become ineffective specifically *because* of the group size. The mechanism is inferred from the MSE analysis rather than demonstrated causally in an ablation that varies group size while holding other factors constant and measuring an "outlier mitigation effectiveness" metric.

A stronger test would be: evaluate SmoothQuant or Hadamard rotations on NVFP4 at multiple group sizes (e.g., G = 8, 16, 32, 64, 128) and show that the benefit of the mitigation technique decreases as G decreases. The paper comes close to this in Figure 3 (showing MSE vs. G with and without Hadamard for both formats), and the Appendix K sweep over block sizes provides partial evidence, but a dedicated ablation isolating outlier mitigation effectiveness vs. group size would strengthen this claim.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that "MXFP4's power-of-two scale quantization severely degrades accuracy"?

**Yes, very strongly supported.** Multiple independent lines of evidence converge:

1. **Direct comparison with NVFP4:** MXFP4 with RTN achieves 87.83% recovery on Llama-3.1-8B (Table 1), while NVFP4 with RTN achieves 94.67%. The formats differ in both group size (32 vs. 16) and scale format (E8M0 vs. E4M3). The 6.8-point gap cannot be attributed solely to group size, since NVFP4's smaller groups should theoretically be *more* susceptible to dead-zone underflow (Lemma 2 predicts higher MSE for smaller G). The scale quantization is the most plausible explanation for the large gap.

2. **Scale fitting ablation (Appendix H):** The MXFP4$^\dagger$ modification, which only changes the scale quantization mapping without touching the base data type or group size, improves RTN recovery from 87.8% to 94.3% (+6.5 points) on Llama-3.1-8B. This is a near-complete elimination of the MXFP4-NVFP4 gap, directly implicating scale quantization as the dominant failure mode.

3. **Scale format analysis (Appendix C, Figures 8–9):** The per-layer MSE measurements show that E8M0 scales increase MSErel by ~40% over FP16 scales, while E4M3 scales increase it by only ~10%. Scale formats with mantissa bits (E1M6–E3M4) perform close to FP16.

4. **INT4 vs. MXFP4 comparison:** INT4 with RTN achieves 92.63% recovery (Table 1), substantially better than MXFP4's 87.83%, despite having the same group size (32). Since INT4 has a uniform grid (no dead zone) but also uses FP16 scales, the comparison isolates the effect of the element format. The additional degradation when switching from INT4's uniform grid + FP16 scales to MXFP4's E2M1 grid + E8M0 scales is consistent with scale quantization being a major contributor.

The one caveat is that the scale quantization error and the dead-zone error are not cleanly separable in these experiments — both are simultaneously present in MXFP4 but absent (or reduced) in NVFP4 and INT4. A hypothetical ablation using MXFP4 with hypothetical fine-precision scales (e.g., FP16) would provide a cleaner measurement, but this format doesn't exist in hardware and wasn't simulated.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that "MR-GPTQ matches or outperforms state-of-the-art accuracy"?

**Supported with qualifications about statistical significance.** On MXFP4, MR-GPTQ clearly outperforms all other PTQ methods: 93.31% recovery vs. 89.47% for the next-best method (GPTQ) on Llama-3.1-8B (Table 1), with similar margins on other models. This is statistically unambiguous — the 3.8-point gap far exceeds the measured standard deviation (~0.5 points). On NVFP4, MR-GPTQ achieves 96.08% recovery, which is marginally higher than GPTQ (95.92%) and SmoothQuant (95.90%), but the differences are within approximately 1 standard deviation (0.3 points for NVFP4). The paper correctly marks all three as "top aggregate entries" in bold. The claim "matches or outperforms" is accurate: MR-GPTQ never significantly underperforms the best alternative, and it significantly outperforms on MXFP4.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that MR-GPTQ "significantly boosts MXFP4, to the point where it nears that of NVFP4"?

**Supported with some variance across model scales.** On Llama-3.1-8B (Table 1), MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ reaches 93.31% recovery vs. NVFP4 + MR-GPTQ at 96.08% — a 2.8-point gap compared to the 7.3-point gap with RTN (87.83% vs. 94.67%). The gap is narrowed by 4.5 points. On Llama-3.3-70B (Table 6), MXFP4 + GPTQ+Had128 reaches 98.48% vs. NVFP4 + GPTQ+Had128 at 99.24% — a 0.8-point gap. On Qwen3-32B (Table 9), MXFP4 + RTN+Had32 reaches 98.67% vs. NVFP4 + RTN at 99.76% — a 1.1-point gap. At large scales, MR-GPTQ does indeed bring MXFP4 to within 1–2 points of NVFP4, consistent with the claim.

However, on smaller models the gap remains larger: on Llama-3.2-3B (Table 4), MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ achieves 92.69% vs. NVFP4 + GPTQ at 95.51% — a 2.8-point gap. On Llama-3.2-1B (Table 3), MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ achieves 79.80% vs. NVFP4 + GPTQ+Had16 at 87.35% — a 7.5-point gap. The bridging is more effective at larger model scales where parameter redundancy provides error tolerance. The claim should be qualified as "nears NVFP4 accuracy for models ≥8B parameters."

#### Does the evidence support the claim of "speedups vs. FP16 of up to 3.6× layer-wise, and 2.2× end-to-end on NVIDIA B200, and of 6× layer-wise and 4× end-to-end on RTX5090"?

**Supported, but the end-to-end speedups are batch-size-dependent and assume large-batch serving.** The layer-wise speedups are directly measured in Figures 5 and 11: 3.6× on B200 (5000/1400 TFLOPs/s), 6× on RTX5090 (1050/175 TFLOPs/s). These are single-layer measurements at peak throughput (large batch sizes) and are consistent with the ~4× theoretical maximum for W4A4 over W16A16 (assuming compute-bound matmuls).

The end-to-end speedups require more careful interpretation:
- The 2.2× end-to-end on B200 (Figure 6) is achieved at batch size 8192 — a very large batch that may not represent typical LLM serving workloads. At batch size 32 (more representative of single-user text generation), the speedup drops to ~1.6×.
- The 4× end-to-end on RTX5090 is mentioned in Section 6 and Appendix F but detailed breakdowns by batch size aren't provided in the main figures. The Transformers-based measurement in Figure 11 (right panel) shows ~3.1–3.6× speedup across batch sizes for Qwen3-8B, which is close to 4× but not exactly matching. This may reflect a different model (Qwen3-8B vs. Llama-3.3-70B) or the difference between vLLM and Transformers serving stacks.
- Figure 12 provides a more nuanced picture: for large-batch inference (>256), MR-MXFP4 achieves ~2.2× over BF16; for small-batch inference (≤32), it shows "little to no improvement" and weight-only NVFP4 is recommended instead (~20% latency reduction from FP8). The paper is transparent about this limitation in Appendix G.

The headline speedup numbers are accurate for the specific conditions tested, but they represent best-case (large-batch, compute-bound) rather than typical-case performance. A less favorable but more comprehensive summary would be: "up to 2.2× end-to-end for large-batch serving on B200, ~1.6× for small-batch, and ~20% latency reduction for weight-only FP4 in memory-bound regimes."

#### What experiments would have strengthened the paper?

1. **Ablation varying group size within the same format to isolate dead-zone vs. scale quantization effects.** The paper compares MXFP4 (G=32, E8M0) vs. NVFP4 (G=16, E4M3), which confounds group size and scale format. A clean ablation would simulate variants like "MXFP4 with G=16" or "NVFP4 with G=32" to separate the two factors.

2. **Direct comparison with a "best possible" MXFP4 upper bound** — for instance, MXFP4 with FP16 scales (in simulation). This would establish how much of the MXFP4-NVFP4 gap is recoverable through algorithm improvements vs. fundamentally limited by the E2M1 element format.

3. **Evaluation on additional benchmarks beyond the Open LLM Leaderboard suite**, particularly long-form generation tasks (where quantization errors might accumulate over many tokens) and tasks requiring precise numerical reasoning (where FP4's sparse representable values might cause systematic errors). The PlatinumBench analysis partially addresses this but is limited to the same model scales.

4. **Latency measurements at realistic serving batch sizes** (1–8) with continuous batching, which is how most LLM deployments actually operate. The current performance evaluation emphasizes throughput at large batch sizes.

5. **Memory bandwidth utilization analysis** to confirm the paper's claim that the online rotation is "memory-bound" and therefore adds negligible cost. The current evidence is the small gap between ideal and actual throughput, but a roofline model analysis would be more rigorous.

6. **Evaluation on models with different architectural features** — mixture-of-experts (MoE), models with GQA vs. MHA attention, non-transformer architectures — to assess whether MR-GPTQ's benefits generalize beyond dense transformer LLMs.

7. **A controlled experiment isolating the contribution of static activation reordering** by comparing MR-GPTQ with static reordering against MR-GPTQ with dynamic reordering (and measuring both accuracy and runtime). The current evidence for static reordering's accuracy equivalence to dynamic reordering is indirect (citing GPTQ literature).

8. **Longer-form stability tests** — generating hundreds of tokens with quantized models and measuring perplexity or factual consistency at each position to detect error accumulation that might not be visible in zero-shot benchmark scores.

These missing experiments don't undermine the paper's central claims, but they would provide a more complete picture of the practical tradeoffs and the generality of the approach. The paper's scope is already extensive (three model families, seven scales, multiple benchmarks, real kernel integration, performance measurements), and the additions suggested above represent natural extensions rather than critical omissions.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Excluded From All Efficiency Calculations

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework depends on estimating prompt difficulty before allocating the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so — generating 2048 samples per question and averaging the PRM's final-answer scores — consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied. The authors acknowledge this explicitly:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity" (Section 3.2)

**The consequence.** The reported `4×` efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is already known, without amortizing the cost of estimating it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation cost + strategy execution cost. Since generating 2048 samples per prompt costs `2048 / N` times more than the actual inference budget `N` (for small-to-moderate `N`), the difficulty estimation step could dominate or even exceed the total compute budget. For example, if the optimal strategy uses `N = 64` generations, the 2048-sample estimation step is ~32× more expensive than solving the problem itself. This means the `4×` figure should be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** contingent on a cheap difficulty estimation mechanism that does not yet exist. The paper frames this explicitly as an exploration-exploitation tradeoff but provides no solution.

**What evidence exists.** Section 3.2 describes the difficulty estimation procedure (2048 samples per question, PRM scoring, binning into quintiles) and acknowledges the cost. The efficiency gain calculations in Figures 4 and 8 (e.g., "16 generations matching 64," "64 generations matching 256") do not include the estimation cost in the budget. No experiment measures the total end-to-end cost including difficulty assessment.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap. It suggests future work on "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8) and mentions that adaptive difficulty estimation (starting with a small number of samples, then deciding strategy) could subsume estimation into the solving process. Neither approach is developed or evaluated. Until this gap is closed, the practical efficiency gains in deployment remain unknown.

---

### The Approach Provides Zero Benefit on Hard Problems

**The assumption or constraint.** Test-time compute can only find or refine solutions that the base model is already capable of producing at some non-trivial rate. If the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class, no amount of search or revision will produce correct answers — there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to discover.

**The consequence.** On the hardest difficulty quintile (bin 5), all methods — search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations — show **near-zero accuracy regardless of budget**. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all generation budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5% while the `~14×` larger model achieves meaningful performance (the exact number depends on `R`). The paper states this directly:

> "On the hardest questions (bin 5), no method makes meaningful progress — the base model simply lacks the capability to produce correct solutions regardless of how the budget is allocated." (Section 5.3)

The practical implication is stark: **test-time compute cannot substitute for pretraining on problems outside the base model's capability range.** If a deployment faces a substantial fraction of genuinely hard problems (where the base model's pass@1 is negligible), the compute-optimal framework provides no path to acceptable accuracy. Pretraining a larger model remains the only viable option.

**What evidence exists.** Figure 3 (right, bin 5), Figure 7 (right, bin 5), Figure 9 (bin 5 curves). The FLOPs-matched comparison in Figure 9 shows the `~14×` larger model's greedy performance (stars) well above the compute-optimal scaling line for bin 5, confirming that pretraining provides capability that inference-time compute cannot recover.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this boundary. Section 8 acknowledges that "test-time compute amplifies existing capability but does not create it from nothing." No mitigation is proposed because the limitation is fundamental — it follows from the definition of test-time compute as operating on samples from the base model's distribution. The paper frames this as a regime where pretraining is unequivocally preferable, which is a useful boundary condition rather than a flaw.

---

### The `~14×` Larger Model Baseline Is Not Compute-Optimally Trained and Uses Only Greedy Decoding

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters by `~14×` while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023). The paper acknowledges that this departs from compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022), where data and parameters are scaled equally:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work." (Section 7)

Additionally, the `~14×` larger model is evaluated only with greedy decoding — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search, no revisions. The smaller model receives a sophisticated, difficulty-adaptive test-time strategy while the larger model receives no test-time compute at all.

**The consequence.** Both factors make the pretraining baseline **weaker than is fair or realistic.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with `14×` more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model at the same total FLOPs budget. Similarly, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8 or best-of-16) would create a much stronger baseline. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining — e.g., +27.8% relative improvement on medium-difficulty questions at `R ≪ 1` (Figure 1, top-right bar chart) — may shrink or reverse against a properly optimized larger model. The paper's claim that "a smaller model augmented with compute-optimal test-time strategies can outperform a `~14×` larger pretrained model" (Section 1) should be understood as comparing against a *specific, non-optimized* pretraining configuration, not against the best achievable large model.

**What evidence exists.** Section 7 describes the FLOPs-matched setup and the `R` values `{0.16, 0.79, 22}`. Figure 9 and the bar charts in Figure 1 show the comparisons. The paper explicitly notes the parameter-only scaling choice but does not measure how much stronger a compute-optimal pretrained baseline would be. No experiment gives the larger model any test-time compute budget.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly (Section 7 quote above) and frames it as a deliberate choice to match the LLaMA paradigm. No experiments with compute-optimal pretraining or test-time-augmented larger models are performed. The practical consequence is that the FLOPs-matched comparison is best interpreted as a **lower bound on when test-time compute can be preferable** — it may be preferable even more often than the paper claims, or it may not be preferable at all against a properly optimized larger model; the experiments don't distinguish these cases.

---

### Sequential Revisions and Search Are Never Combined, Representing a Lower Bound on Performance

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary axes — PRM-guided search (modifying the verifier / selection mechanism) and iterative revisions (modifying the proposal distribution) — but never combines them in a single system. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions" (Section 8)

**The consequence.** The two mechanisms have complementary strengths that the paper itself documents: revisions are most effective on easy problems where local refinement suffices (Figure 7, right), while PRM search is most effective on medium problems where global exploration across qualitatively different solution strategies is needed (Figure 3, right). A combined system could, in principle, use the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search — conditioning on previous rejected branches to generate higher-quality candidates at each search step — or use the PRM to guide *which* revisions to pursue rather than blindly generating a long chain. The paper's results therefore represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. The `4×` efficiency gains reported for revisions (Figure 8) and search (Figure 4) independently might be increased, or the performance ceiling (currently ~44% at 256 generations for revisions, ~39% for search on the full MATH test set) might be raised by combining the two.

**What evidence exists.** The paper never runs a combined experiment. The search and revision pipelines use different verifiers (PRM trained on base model outputs for search, ORM trained on revision model outputs for revisions, with the paper noting that the base-model PRM does not transfer well to revision outputs due to distribution shift — Appendix J, Figure 15a), which creates an additional engineering barrier to combination. No attempt to train a unified verifier or to route between search and revisions based on difficulty is evaluated.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged as a direction for future work (Section 8). The paper's compute-optimal selection operates *within* each axis (choosing the best search algorithm for a given difficulty, or the best sequential-to-parallel ratio for revisions) but never *between* axes (choosing whether to use search or revisions on a given problem). This is a natural next step that the paper explicitly defers.

---

### All Results Are on a Single Benchmark (MATH) With a Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S*)

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses the MATH benchmark (500 test questions, competition-level math) and PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The paper states:

> "we believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4)

but this claim is asserted rather than demonstrated.

**The consequence.** It is unclear whether the paper's central findings — difficulty-dependent strategy selection, the `4×` efficiency gains from compute-optimal scaling, the superiority of beam search on medium problems and its degradation on easy problems, the effectiveness of revisions on easy problems — generalize to other:
- **Domains:** Code generation (where correctness can be verified by unit tests), logical reasoning (ARC, FOLIO), scientific QA, or open-ended generation (where correctness signals are ambiguous). The PRM training pipeline requires ground-truth correctness labels for Monte Carlo rollouts, which is straightforward for math (exact answer matching) but challenging for creative writing or dialogue.
- **Model families:** The paper notes that PaLM 2-S* has "non-trivial performance on MATH (roughly 10–19% pass@1)" (Section 4), which sits in a useful intermediate regime where test-time compute can make a difference. A model with much higher pass@1 (e.g., GPT-4-level) might saturate the benchmark before aggressive test-time strategies become necessary; a model with much lower pass@1 might show no benefit at all on most problems, collapsing the difficulty distribution into mostly bin 5.
- **Problem structures:** MATH problems require multi-step symbolic reasoning with a single correct answer. The revision training procedure (edit-distance-based pairing of incorrect and correct solutions) relies on structural similarity between answers, which may not hold for tasks where correct and incorrect responses differ qualitatively rather than in a few localized edits.

**What evidence exists.** All experiments are on MATH with PaLM 2-S*. The paper does not include a single experiment on a different benchmark or model family. The difficulty estimation protocol (2048 samples per question) requires ground-truth answer checking, which is trivial for MATH (exact string match via the grading function) but not for many other tasks.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges the single-model limitation (Section 4, quoted above) but provides no evidence to support the representativeness claim. This is the most significant generalizability gap in the paper, and the findings should be treated as **MATH-specific and PaLM 2-S*-specific** until replicated on other benchmarks and model families. The paper's framing of the results as establishing "inference-time scaling laws" (Section 8) implies a level of generality that the experiments do not directly support.

---

### There Is No Accounting for Latency or Wall-Clock Time in the Strategy Selection

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures test-time compute in "generations" (number of complete solutions sampled), which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs. However, different strategies have fundamentally different latency profiles: sequential revisions are inherently serial (each revision depends on the previous one), while parallel best-of-N can be executed simultaneously with sufficient hardware. The compute-optimal policy does not distinguish between these profiles.

**The consequence.** A strategy that allocates 128 generations as 64 sequential revisions × 2 parallel chains takes roughly `64×` longer wall-clock time than one that runs 128 parallel samples simultaneously, even though both consume the same total FLOPs. For latency-sensitive applications (interactive assistants, real-time decision-making, chatbots), the sequential-heavy strategies favored by the compute-optimal policy on easy problems may be **practically unusable regardless of their accuracy advantages**. The paper's statement that its framework provides an "inference-time analog of the Chinchilla scaling laws" (Section 1) ignores the temporal dimension that is irrelevant for pretraining but critical for inference serving. A pretraining FLOP is a pretraining FLOP regardless of when it occurs; an inference FLOP's value depends on whether it can be parallelized.

**What evidence exists.** The paper makes no mention of latency, throughput, or wall-clock time in the strategy selection or efficiency calculations. No experiment measures end-to-end time-to-solution for different strategies. The compute-optimal policy is optimized purely for accuracy at a given generation budget, with no latency constraint.

**Mitigation status.** Not acknowledged or addressed. This is a significant practical limitation because real LLM deployments are almost always latency-constrained. A more complete framework would optimize a combined objective of accuracy and latency, or at minimum report the latency implications of each strategy so practitioners can make informed tradeoffs. The paper's silence on this dimension means the compute-optimal policy, as presented, should be understood as **accuracy-optimal at a given FLOPs budget, not as practically optimal for any deployment with latency requirements**.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper fundamentally reframes how the field should think about post-training quantization for novel number formats. It is not an incremental accuracy improvement — it is a **methodological correction** to a widespread but flawed assumption: that quantization algorithms are format-agnostic and can be ported from INT4 to FP4 without modification. The paper's empirical evidence that state-of-the-art methods underperform or catastrophically fail on FP4 formats — QuaRot achieving 79.70% recovery on MXFP4 versus 87.83% for simple RTN (Table 1) — makes this point unambiguously. The conceptual shift is from "quantization is quantization" to **"the interaction between format properties and algorithm design is the dominant factor in accuracy, not the bit-width alone."**

This shift has several concrete consequences for the research landscape:

**First, it establishes format-specialized algorithm design as a first-class research objective.** Before this paper, the PTQ literature treated formats as interchangeable infrastructure — you designed a method for "4-bit quantization" and evaluated it on INT4, with the implicit assumption that FP4 variants would work similarly. The paper demonstrates that this assumption fails in both directions: methods that help INT4 can hurt FP4 (Hadamard rotations reduce NVFP4 accuracy, Table 1), and methods that help FP4 (block-wise rotations at the quantization group size) are non-obvious from the INT4 literature. This reframes the design space: future quantization algorithms should specify which format properties they depend on (group size? grid uniformity? scale precision? dead-zone presence?) and should be evaluated across multiple format families to test these dependencies.

**Second, it provides a predictive theoretical framework that replaces empirical trial-and-error.** The dead-zone asymptotic analysis (Lemma 2) and the shared-scale precision analysis (Section 3.2) transform FP4 quantization from a black-box empirical tuning problem into a space where design choices can be *derived from format properties*. The prediction that Hadamard rotations help MXFP4 (G=32) but hurt NVFP4 (G=16) under RTN — and the experimental confirmation in Figure 3 — is the type of falsifiable, counter-intuitive prediction that signals a genuinely useful theory. Researchers designing algorithms for future formats (FP6? microscaling INT8? block floating-point with different group sizes?) can use this framework to predict whether rotations, outlier mitigation, or scale optimization will be beneficial *before running experiments*. This is methodologically analogous to how the Chinchilla scaling laws transformed pretraining from empirical grid search to a principled allocation problem.

**Third, it reconciles the contradictory industry claim that "FP4 is more accurate than INT4" with the reality that this depends entirely on the format specification and the quantization method.** The paper's demonstration that NVINT4 — a hypothetical format that doesn't exist in hardware — outperforms both NVFP4 and INT4 on accuracy (97.12% recovery vs. 96.08% and 94.71% respectively, Table 1) reveals that the advantage often attributed to floating-point representation is actually an artifact of NVFP4's fine E4M3 scales, not of the E2M1 element format itself. The conceptual reframing is: **scale precision, group size, and grid structure are the primary determinants of microscaling accuracy, not the choice between integer and floating-point arithmetic.** This has immediate implications for hardware vendors designing the next generation of formats — investing in finer scale formats (E4M3 or better) and smaller group sizes provides more accuracy benefit than switching from INT to FP element types with coarse scales.

**Fourth, it demonstrates that MXFP4 — widely considered the inferior format due to its power-of-two scales — can approach NVFP4 accuracy through algorithmic innovation alone.** The gap between MXFP4 + RTN (87.83% recovery) and NVFP4 + RTN (94.67%) is 6.8 percentage points (Table 1). MR-GPTQ narrows this to 2.8 points (93.31% vs. 96.08%), and on large models the gap shrinks to under 1 point (Table 6: 98.48% vs. 99.24% for Llama-3.3-70B). The practical implication is that **the format hierarchy is partially algorithmically addressable**: MXFP4's hardware advantages (power-of-two scale arithmetic, larger group sizes enabling higher throughput — Figure 5 shows ~15% higher TFLOPs/s than NVFP4 on B200) can be realized without paying the full accuracy penalty that naive methods incur. This is not a claim that MXFP4 equals NVFP4 — a 1–2 point gap persists across most configurations — but it changes the decision from "always use NVFP4 for accuracy" to a genuine engineering tradeoff between throughput and accuracy.

**Fifth, the negative results for established methods are themselves a valuable contribution.** The finding that global rotations (QuaRot) catastrophically fail on MXFP4 (79.70% recovery vs. 87.83% for RTN, Table 1) is not a marginal underperformance — it's a 9-point drop on MMLU-CoT (49.86% vs. 62.21%). The finding that SmoothQuant, the dominant weight-activation redistribution method, provides minimal benefit for MXFP4 (89.06% recovery, barely above RTN's 87.83%) challenges the assumption that outlier mitigation is universally beneficial. These negative results redirect research attention: rather than trying to adapt existing INT-centric methods to FP4, the community should start from the format's error characteristics and design methods specifically for them. The paper's own MR-GPTQ is an existence proof that this approach works.

**Which research directions become more attractive:**
- Developing verifier-like error predictors that can estimate per-layer quantization sensitivity before running GPTQ, enabling compute-efficient allocation of calibration and correction effort across layers.
- Exploring whether the dead-zone asymptotic analysis extends to other non-uniform grids (FP6, FP8 variants, logarithmic quantization), which would provide a unified theory for when rotations help or hurt across formats.
- Investigating whether the block-wise rotation at the group size is optimal in general for microscaling formats, or whether this depends on the specific ratio of group size to dead-zone half-width.

**Which research directions become less attractive:**
- Naively porting INT4 methods to FP4 without analyzing format-method interactions. The paper's negative results for QuaRot and SpinQuant demonstrate that this approach is not just inefficient but actively harmful.
- Developing ever-more-sophisticated global rotation schemes for FP4. The Appendix K sweep shows that all orthogonal transforms (DCT, DST, Hadamard, GSR) perform similarly — the specific transform structure matters much less than the block size and the format properties. Research effort is better spent on scale optimization and GPTQ integration than on finding the "optimal" rotation matrix.
- Claiming FP4 superiority over INT4 without specifying the format details. The NVINT4 thought experiment (Appendix D) demonstrates that microscaling INT4 with E4M3 scales would outperform microscaling FP4 with the same scales, indicating that the advantage is in the microscaling structure, not the floating-point representation.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. A cheap difficulty estimator equivalent for per-layer quantization sensitivity.** The paper's MSE analysis in Section 3 provides a theoretical framework for predicting which layers will suffer most from quantization, based on the interaction between weight/activation distributions and format properties. A natural follow-up is a lightweight per-layer sensitivity predictor — analogous to the paper's difficulty estimator but operating at the layer granularity — that uses a small number of calibration samples to estimate the expected MSErel under a given format and block size, without running full GPTQ. This predictor would tell practitioners *which layers need format-specialized correction* (MR-GPTQ) and which can tolerate simpler methods (RTN). A strong experiment would: train a small model to predict per-layer MSErel from calibration statistics (kurtosis, variance, block maximum distribution) and format parameters (G, S format, E format), evaluate whether this enables mixed-precision allocation where some layers use NVFP4 and others use MXFP4, and measure whether the reduced correction overhead improves end-to-end throughput without accuracy loss. The paper's finding that "the induced error is roughly evenly split between weight and activation quantization" (Section 5 discussion) for NVFP4 but activation-skewed for MXFP4 suggests that mixed-format strategies — e.g., NVFP4 for activations and MXFP4 for weights — might capture most of NVFP4's accuracy at most of MXFP4's throughput.

**2. Combining MR-GPTQ with online difficulty-adaptive precision for autoregressive generation.** The paper evaluates MR-GPTQ on zero-shot benchmarks where the entire model is quantized at a fixed precision. In autoregressive generation, quantization error accumulates over tokens — early-token errors propagate through the attention mechanism to later tokens. A follow-up could investigate whether per-token or per-generation-step precision adaptation (inspired by the compute-optimal test-time scaling in the reference example paper) could reduce this accumulation: use full FP16 for the first few tokens (where error propagation is most damaging) and switch to FP4 for later tokens, or use NVFP4 for attention layers and MXFP4 for feedforward layers. The paper's kernel library (QuTLASS) already supports mixed-format inference, making this implementationally feasible. A strong experiment would: measure the perplexity degradation curve as a function of generation length under different quantization formats, identify the point at which FP4 error accumulation becomes unacceptable, and test whether a hybrid strategy (first 32 tokens FP16, remaining tokens MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ) achieves BF16-level generation quality at a meaningful fraction of FP4 throughput. The paper's finding that smaller models (1B, 3B) suffer disproportionately from quantization (Tables 3–4) makes them ideal testbeds for this effect — if hybrid precision can rescue small-model generation quality, it would enable deployment scenarios (on-device, edge) that are currently infeasible.

**3. Stress-testing the dead-zone asymptotic framework on hypothetical microscaling formats.** The paper's Lemma 2 and the subsequent empirical validation in Figure 3 establish a theoretical relationship between group size, dead-zone half-width, distribution shape, and MSE. A follow-up could systematically validate and extend this framework by simulating hypothetical formats that don't exist in hardware: FP4 variants with different exponent/mantissa allocations (E3M0? E1M2?), INT4 microscaling with different scale formats (E5M2? E2M5?), and group sizes spanning the full range from 4 to 256. For each simulated format, measure MSErel and end-to-end model accuracy under RTN, GPTQ, and MR-GPTQ, and test whether the theoretical predictions hold. The key scientific question is: **can we predict, from format specifications alone, which quantization methods will be effective, without running expensive model evaluations?** The paper's framework makes specific predictions — e.g., formats with dead zones benefit from rotations at large group sizes but not small ones; formats with coarse scales relative to the base data type (E8M0 vs. E2M1) are dominated by scale quantization error regardless of group size. A systematic validation across a grid of simulated formats would determine the boundaries of these predictions and identify where new phenomena emerge. The Appendix C analysis (Figures 8–9), showing that scale formats with mantissa bits (E1M6–E3M4) achieve near-FP16 MSErel, provides an immediate prediction to test: would FP4 with E3M4 scales (hypothetical, 5.0 bits/element) outperform NVFP4's E4M3 scales in practice, and if so, by how much?

**4. Developing a continuous, learned allocation policy for block-wise rotation sizes.** The paper's Appendix K sweep shows that rotation block size matters — matching the group size is optimal for NVFP4, while larger blocks help slightly for MXFP4 — but the choice is currently made via grid search and fixed globally across all layers. A follow-up could develop a learned policy that assigns block sizes per-layer or even per-weight-matrix-segment, using calibration statistics to predict which granularity of rotation minimizes quantization error for that specific tensor. The paper's finding that "all transforms work similarly" for small block sizes (Tables 13–14) — because the transformation is memory-bound and any orthogonal matrix can be loaded at negligible cost — means the policy can optimize over block size without being constrained to specific transform structures. A strong experiment would: extract per-layer calibration statistics (kurtosis, weight variance, activation variance), train a lightweight model to predict the optimal rotation block size from these features, and evaluate whether the learned assignment outperforms the fixed block-size baseline on models not seen during training. The success criterion is whether the learned policy closes the remaining 1–2 point gap between MR-GPTQ and the FP16 baseline on large models, or whether this gap is fundamentally limited by the E2M1 element format rather than the rotation granularity.

**5. Quantization-aware training as a calibration-data-free bridge to near-lossless MXFP4.** The paper's QAT experiments (Tables 3–5, 7, Appendix B) show consistent but moderate improvements over MR-GPTQ — typically 1–2 points on large models, 2–5 points on small models. These experiments use a standard QAT setup (balanced JSD loss, Tülu 3 instruction data) that was not specifically designed for FP4 formats. A follow-up could develop FP4-specific QAT techniques informed by the paper's error analysis: for example, adding a regularization term that penalizes normalized activations falling into the dead zone, or using the MSE-optimized grid from MR-GPTQ as the initialization for QAT rather than starting from RTN. The paper's finding that QAT provides the largest relative gains on the smallest models (Llama-3.2-1B: MXFP4 + QAT+Had32 achieves 84.55% recovery vs. MR-GPTQ's 79.80%, a 4.75-point gain) suggests that format-specific QAT could make FP4 viable at model scales that are currently borderline. A strong experiment would: design an FP4-aware QAT loss that incorporates the dead-zone half-width and scale quantization error into the training objective, compare it against standard QAT and MR-GPTQ across model scales, and determine whether FP4 QAT can match INT8 accuracy at a fraction of the bit-width — which would transform the cost-benefit calculation for on-device deployment.

**6. Building an open benchmark for microscaling format evaluation that separates format effects from method effects.** The paper's evaluation uses standard zero-shot tasks (Open LLM Leaderboard v1) that were not designed to stress-test quantization formats specifically. The finding that some methods appear "within variance" on standard benchmarks but show clear separation on PlatinumBench (Appendix I, Figures 13–14) suggests that existing evaluation suites have insufficient resolution for comparing high-recovery quantization methods. A follow-up could curate a benchmark specifically for quantization evaluation, with tasks designed to amplify the failure modes identified in the paper: (a) tasks requiring precise numerical comparison (to stress-test dead-zone underflow, where small values get rounded to zero and lost), (b) tasks with heavy-tailed activation distributions at specific layers (to stress-test outlier preservation in small groups), (c) tasks requiring long-range dependency tracking (to stress-test error accumulation over many layers). The benchmark should include diagnostic measurements — not just task accuracy but per-layer MSErel, per-token perplexity degradation curves, and attention pattern distortion metrics — that let researchers identify *why* a given quantization method fails, not just *that* it fails. This would accelerate method development by providing the same diagnostic granularity that the paper's Section 3 analysis provides at the theory level, but at the full-model evaluation level.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. High-throughput batch inference serving with MXFP4 on Blackwell GPUs.** The paper's demonstration that MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ achieves ~15% higher throughput than NVFP4 on B200 GPUs (Figure 5, ~5000 vs. ~4200 TFLOPs/s) while recovering within 1–2% of NVFP4 accuracy on large models (Table 6: 98.48% vs. 99.24% for Llama-3.3-70B) has immediate cost implications for organizations running large-scale batch inference. For a deployment processing millions of queries per day at batch sizes ≥256, switching from NVFP4 to MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ could reduce GPU-hours by ~13–15% (accounting for the throughput difference) with a ~0.8-point accuracy tradeoff. If the workload is accuracy-sensitive, the alternative is to use NVFP4 + SmoothQuant or GPTQ for maximum accuracy (95.9–96.1% recovery on 8B, Table 1) at the higher bit-width (4.5 bits/element). If the workload is throughput-sensitive (e.g., embedding generation, classification, filtering), MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ at 4.25 bits/element with higher throughput becomes the economically rational choice. The Figure 12 breakdown by batch size provides the decision rule: for large-batch serving (>256), use MR-MXFP4 for maximum throughput; for small-batch inference (≤32), use weight-only NVFP4 for ~20% latency reduction over FP8 with minimal accuracy loss. This is not speculative — the QuTLASS kernels are production-ready and integrated into vLLM.

**2. On-device deployment of small models with FP4 quantization-aware training.** The paper's finding that MR-GPTQ alone may be insufficient for models below ~3B parameters (Llama-3.2-1B: MXFP4 + MR-GPTQ achieves only 79.80% recovery, Table 3) but that QAT closes much of the gap (84.55% recovery for MXFP4 + QAT+Had32) suggests a deployment pipeline for edge devices: MR-GPTQ for initial quantization, followed by lightweight QAT on a task-specific dataset. For an on-device assistant running a 1–3B parameter model, FP4 quantization reduces the model's memory footprint from 2–6 GB (FP16) to 0.5–1.5 GB (FP4), enabling deployment on phones or laptops with limited RAM. The 4× end-to-end speedup on RTX5090 (consumer GPU) reported in Section 6 and Figure 11 suggests similar gains on other consumer hardware with FP4 tensor core support. The critical path is: use MR-GPTQ to produce an initial quantized model, fine-tune via QAT for 1–2 epochs on domain-specific data (the paper uses 92,995 Tülu 3 samples; a production deployment might use 10,000–50,000 task-specific examples), and deploy with QuTLASS kernels. The accuracy recovery data in Tables 3–4 suggests this pipeline could achieve 85–90% of FP16 accuracy at 4× the throughput, which is likely sufficient for many on-device use cases (text classification, retrieval, simple QA) if not for high-stakes reasoning.

**3. Cloud LLM serving with mixed-format deployment: NVFP4 for attention, MXFP4 for feedforward layers.** The paper's finding that the error is roughly evenly split between weights and activations for NVFP4 but activation-skewed for MXFP4 (Section 5 discussion, comparing weight-only vs. W4A4 results in Tables 1–2) suggests that different layer types have different format sensitivities. Attention layers involve matrix multiplications with query-key interactions that are sensitive to outlier preservation — NVFP4's E4M3-promoted top elements would be beneficial here. Feedforward layers involve large matrix multiplications (e.g., 57344×8192 in Llama-3.3-70B) where throughput is the primary concern — MXFP4's higher TFLOPs/s would be beneficial. A mixed-format deployment could use NVFP4 for attention weights and activations (4.5 bits/element, higher accuracy) and MXFP4 for feedforward weights and activations (4.25 bits/element, higher throughput), with MR-GPTQ optimizing both. The QuTLASS library supports both formats, making this implementationally straightforward. The accuracy-throughput tradeoff would need to be measured end-to-end, but the paper's component-level data (Figures 5, 8–9 for per-layer MSErel, Tables 1–2 for weight-only vs. W4A4 accuracy) provides the necessary inputs to estimate the tradeoff without running the full experiment. If the mixed-format deployment recovers most of NVFP4's accuracy (since attention layers preserve critical token interactions) at most of MXFP4's throughput (since feedforward layers dominate FLOPs), it would represent a new point on the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier that neither pure format achieves alone.

**4. Self-improvement data generation pipelines using quantized models for candidate sampling.** For organizations using LLMs to generate training data for themselves (rejection sampling, distillation, self-play), the bottleneck is often the cost of generating millions of candidate responses from large models. The paper's demonstration that MR-GPTQ can recover 98–99% of FP16 accuracy on large models (Table 6: Llama-3.3-70B with MXFP4 + GPTQ+Had128 at 98.48% recovery) while providing 2.2× throughput (Figure 6) means that a data generation pipeline could switch from FP16 teacher models to MR-GPTQ-quantized teacher models, producing ~2.2× more candidate responses in the same compute budget. If the 1.5% accuracy degradation (98.48% vs. 100% recovery) introduces some noise into the generated data, the increased volume of candidates might compensate — the final trained model's quality depends on both candidate quality and quantity. A concrete deployment: for a ReST$^{\text{EM}}$-style self-improvement loop (similar to Singh et al., 2024), generate 2.2 million candidate solutions using an MR-GPTQ-quantized Llama-3.3-70B in the same GPU-hours that would produce 1 million candidates from FP16, filter both sets for correctness using the same verifier, and fine-tune the student model on the filtered data. If the 2.2× volume advantage outweighs the ~1.5% quality degradation, total compute efficiency improves. This is directly testable using the paper's released quantized models and the QuTLASS vLLM integration.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper provides clear quantitative boundaries for when MR-GPTQ should be preferred over alternatives, grounded in format-specific accuracy and throughput measurements:

- **Prefer MR-GPTQ with MXFP4 when** throughput is the primary constraint and the model has ≥8B parameters. Reasoning: Table 1 shows 93.31% recovery on Llama-3.1-8B vs. 87.83% for RTN; Figure 5 shows ~15% higher throughput than NVFP4 on B200; on 70B models (Table 6), recovery reaches 98.48%. The throughput advantage compounds at large batch sizes (Figure 12). For smaller models (<8B), the accuracy gap to NVFP4 remains substantial (Tables 3–4), and QAT should be considered as a follow-up step (Tables 3–4 show QAT providing 3–5 point gains on 1–3B models).

- **Prefer MR-GPTQ with NVFP4 when** maximum accuracy at 4-bit precision is required and the ~6% bit-width overhead (4.5 vs. 4.25 bits/element) is acceptable. Reasoning: Table 1 shows NVFP4 + MR-GPTQ achieving 96.08% recovery, statistically tied with SmoothQuant (95.90%) and GPTQ (95.92%). The advantage over MXFP4 is largest on small models (7.5 points on 1B, Table 3; 2.8 points on 8B, Table 1) and narrows to under 1 point on 70B (Table 6).

- **Prefer standard GPTQ (without rotations) with NVFP4 when** simplicity of implementation is valued and the model is from the Qwen-3 family or another quantization-friendly architecture. Reasoning: Table 7 shows NVFP4 + GPTQ achieving 98.13% recovery on Qwen3-8B without any rotations, activation reordering, or scale optimization — the format properties alone, combined with GPTQ's error correction, are sufficient. For Llama-family models, the gap between GPTQ and MR-GPTQ is small for NVFP4 (95.7% vs. 96.1%, Table 1) but larger for MXFP4 (89.5% vs. 93.3%).

- **Prefer weight-only FP4 quantization (any method) over W4A4 when** serving small-batch, latency-sensitive workloads (batch size ≤ 32, typical of single-user text generation). Reasoning: Figure 12 shows that W4A4 provides "little to no improvement" over BF16/FP8 for small batch sizes, while weight-only NVFP4 reduces latency by ~20% from FP8. Appendix A (Table 2) shows that weight-only NVFP4 + RTN achieves 98.02% recovery on Llama-3.1-8B — essentially lossless — confirming that activation quantization is the primary contributor to both accuracy degradation and lack of small-batch speedup.

- **Prefer QAT over MR-GPTQ alone when** the model has ≤3B parameters and the deployment can tolerate the additional training cost. Reasoning: Tables 3–4 show QAT providing 3–5 point gains over MR-GPTQ on 1B and 3B models for MXFP4. For models ≥8B, QAT provides only 0–2 point gains (Tables 5–6), making MR-GPTQ sufficient.

The paper does not position MR-GPTQ against a specific named alternative for the FLOPs-matched pretraining-vs-quantization tradeoff (unlike the reference example paper, which explicitly compares test-time compute against pretraining a larger model). The FLOPs accounting is implicit — the speedup numbers (2.2× end-to-end, 3.6× layer-wise) quantify the benefit of FP4 over BF16, but there is no experiment comparing, e.g., "a 70B model quantized to MXFP4 with MR-GPTQ" against "a 35B model at FP16 with the same inference latency," which would be the direct pretraining-vs-quantization tradeoff. The paper thus avoids making claims about when quantization should be preferred over training a smaller model, and no decision rule for that tradeoff is included.
