# Law of the Weakest Link: Cross Capabilities of Large Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2409.19951](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19951)

## 🎯 Pitch

Large language models consistently fail on tasks requiring multiple skills because performance is governed by a “Law of the Weakest Link”—cross-capability scores are dragged down to near the level of the model’s worst component ability, not lifted by its strengths. Across 17 models, 38 of 58 cross-capability scores actually fell below both individual capabilities, showing no synergy or compensation.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper systematically investigates how Large Language Models perform on tasks requiring the simultaneous integration of multiple distinct abilities—termed **cross capabilities**—by introducing CrossEval, a benchmark of 1,400 human-annotated prompts spanning seven individual capabilities and seven paired cross capabilities. Through extensive evaluation of 17 models across five major families using LLM-based evaluators calibrated against 8,400 human ratings, the authors identify a consistent **"Law of the Weakest Link"** effect, where cross-capability performance is constrained by the weaker component ability (e.g., Image Recognition & Reasoning scores clustering near the weaker of the two individual scores rather than averaging or synergizing). Across 58 cross-capability scores where a clear strong-weak capability gap exists, 38 fall below both individual capabilities while the remaining 20 lie between them but closer to the weaker ability, establishing that targeted optimization of the weakest link—rather than further strengthening already-strong capabilities—is the critical priority for advancing LLM performance on complex, real-world tasks.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Gap: LLMs Are Evaluated In Isolation, But the World Demands Integration

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is a mismatch between how we evaluate LLMs and how people actually use them. The development and evaluation of Large Language Models has overwhelmingly focused on **individual capabilities** in isolation. Developers construct specialized datasets targeting distinct skills—reasoning, coding, multilingual competence—and train models by blending these data sources. Benchmarks follow suit, assessing how well a model can reason (using datasets like ARC, MATH, or GPQA), code (using HumanEval or MBPP), or manage factual knowledge (using MMLU). Each evaluation provides a snapshot of a single, well-defined ability.

But here is the disconnect: real-world user queries frequently resist clean categorization into a single capability bucket. Consider the prompt from Section 1 of the paper:

> "Which direction has the total rainfall in Tokyo, Japan been trending over the past 10 years? Explain it step by step."

This query simultaneously demands **tool use** (web browsing to retrieve current and historical meteorological data) and **analytical reasoning** (interpreting trends, constructing a step-by-step explanation). Neither capability alone suffices. Similarly, when a developer provides HTML and JavaScript for an API-driven application and asks for a basic understanding of what the web app does, the model must combine **long-context comprehension** with **coding expertise**. The paper terms these scenarios **cross capabilities**—the intersection of multiple distinct abilities across different types of expertise necessary to address complex, real-world tasks.

The critical question that follows from this observation, and which the paper poses directly, is:

> "How does the performance of LLMs on tasks requiring cross capabilities reflect or diverge from their performance in individual capabilities?"

This question is not merely academic. If model performance on cross-capability tasks were simply the average of the constituent individual capabilities, then improving individual skills in parallel would be sufficient. If performance were *synergistic*—that is, if combining abilities produced emergent effects greater than the sum of individual parts—then cross-capability tasks would be an argument for integration-focused training. But if cross-capability performance is instead **bottlenecked** by the weakest link—the "Law of the Weakest Link," as the paper names it after Liebig's agricultural principle from 1840—then the entire optimization strategy must change. Under that regime, pouring resources into strengthening already-strong capabilities yields minimal practical benefit, while identifying and shoring up the weakest capabilities becomes the highest-leverage investment.

The paper explicitly frames this investigation against four competing theoretical models drawn from multiple disciplines (Section 1):

- **Synergy Theory** (Corning, 1983): The interaction of components in a system can produce effects greater than the sum of individual parts. Applied to LLMs, this would predict that cross-capability performance *exceeds* what either individual capability score would suggest.
- **Compensatory Mechanism** (Adler, 1917): Stronger abilities within a system can offset weaker ones. This predicts cross-capability performance roughly in the middle of the two individual scores, or perhaps biased toward the stronger.
- **Law of the Weakest Link** (Liebig, 1840): System performance is limited by its weakest element. Applied to LLMs, this predicts cross-capability performance at or below the weaker individual capability.
- **Emergent Properties** (Anderson, 1972): New behaviors arise from component interactions that are not predictable from individual components alone. This predicts cross-capability performance patterns that cannot be derived from individual scores.

The paper sets out to empirically determine which of these theoretical frameworks best characterizes LLM behavior. The central finding—that LLMs consistently conform to the Law of the Weakest Link—has profound implications for research prioritization, as we will explore throughout this analysis.

### Why This Gap Matters: Practical and Strategic Implications

**Real-world deployment relevance.** The cross-capability gap is not a niche academic concern. Most challenging user interactions with LLMs span multiple capability boundaries. A user asking for a data analysis script that ingests a CSV, performs statistical tests, and generates interpretable visualizations is simultaneously invoking coding, reasoning, and tool use. A user uploading a legal contract in Spanish and asking for an English summary that identifies problematic clauses is invoking multilingual capability, long-context comprehension, and legal reasoning. If these cross-capability tasks perform substantially worse than their individual components, current LLMs are systematically underdelivering on exactly the types of tasks that motivated their deployment in the first place.

**Resource allocation decisions.** Organizations investing in LLM development face a resource allocation problem: should they focus on pushing the frontier of specific capabilities (better reasoning, better coding, better multilingual support) or on improving the integration of existing capabilities? Prior to this work, there was no empirical framework for making this decision. If cross-capability performance is bottlenecked by the weakest link, then the optimal strategy shifts dramatically: identifying and addressing the weakest individual capabilities—even if they are not the flashiest or most benchmarked abilities—becomes the highest-ROI investment. The paper provides the first systematic evidence for this allocation principle.

**Self-improvement and agent systems.** Cross-capability tasks are particularly central to LLM-based agents, which inherently require integrating tool use, reasoning, coding, and multimodal understanding. As the field moves toward increasingly autonomous systems—agents that browse the web, execute code, interpret images, and reason across modalities—the cross-capability bottleneck becomes not just a performance issue but a fundamental limiter of what is possible. A model that excels at reasoning and coding individually but collapses when asked to apply both simultaneously cannot serve as an effective agent backbone. The paper's findings thus inform the architecture and evaluation of agent systems, even though CrossEval itself is designed for standalone LLM evaluation rather than agent evaluation (Section 6.1).

### Where Existing Evaluation Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several limitations in the current LLM evaluation landscape that motivated CrossEval's construction:

**Isolated capability benchmarks dominate.** The predominant evaluation paradigm assesses capabilities one at a time. Benchmarks like ARC (Clark et al., 2018) test reasoning; HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021) test coding; MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a) tests domain-specific knowledge; GPQA (Rein et al., 2023) tests graduate-level reasoning. Even comprehensive, multi-task benchmarks like BIG-bench (Lin et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2024) maintain a structure where each task targets an individual capability. The paper explicitly notes that **no existing benchmark systematically pairs capabilities** to assess how models perform at their intersection (Section 6.1). This means the entire evaluation infrastructure is optimized to detect progress on axes that may not be the most practically relevant for real-world deployment.

**Agent evaluation misses the granular view.** While recent benchmarks have emerged for evaluating LLM-based agents—WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024), VisualWebArena (Koh et al., 2024), OSWorld (Xie et al., 2024), AgentBench (Liu et al., 2024)—these focus on overall task completion rates or action-level accuracy. They assess whether an agent can successfully complete a multi-step task requiring multiple capabilities but do not decompose performance into which specific capability combinations are causing failures. As the paper notes (Section 6.1):

> "Although our CrossEval is designed for LLMs and not specifically for agent evaluation, it still encompasses key agent-related capabilities such as multi-modality, multilingualism, and tool use. Furthermore, it provides a clear and comprehensive distinction between individual and cross capabilities, providing a more granular framework for evaluation and analysis."

This granularity is what enables the paper's core contribution: isolating *which* capability pairs are the bottleneck and how the weaker component drives overall performance.

**Open-ended evaluation remains unreliable.** Evaluating open-ended generation—where there is no single correct answer—is notoriously difficult, and existing approaches exhibit significant limitations. Traditional n-gram metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) fail to capture semantic quality. Learned metrics like BERTScore and BLEURT improve correlation with human judgment but are limited by their training distributions. LLM-as-a-Judge (Zheng et al., 2023) has emerged as a promising paradigm, but prior implementations suffer from several issues the paper identifies and addresses:

- **Self-generated answer bias**: LLMs evaluating responses often generate their own answer first and use it as a reference, leading to inflated scores when the model's answer matches the evaluator's expectations and penalizing equally valid but differently structured responses.
- **Length and format bias**: LLM evaluators systematically favor longer, more structured responses regardless of content quality.
- **Lack of calibration**: Without human-annotated reference examples, evaluator scores lack grounding in human judgment standards.

The paper's response to these gaps is the construction of CrossEval not just as a capability benchmark but also as the **largest meta-evaluation benchmark** available for calibrating LLM judges against human ratings—8,400 ratings across 4,200 responses with detailed explanations.

**No framework connects individual and cross-capability performance.** Perhaps most critically, prior work has no theoretical or empirical framework for understanding how individual capability scores translate into cross-capability performance. If a model scores 75 on reasoning and 65 on image recognition, what should we expect when it faces tasks requiring *both*? The answer to this question—whether performance averages, synergizes, or bottlenecks—has direct bearing on which capabilities developers should invest in improving. Without CrossEval, this relationship was entirely uncharacterized.

**Difficulty and quality control in prompt construction.** The paper acknowledges that previous benchmarks often include a large number of low-quality prompts that fail to differentiate between advanced models (Li et al., 2024), and that constructing genuinely challenging prompts is inherently difficult (Padlewski et al., 2024). CrossEval addresses this through a multi-stage annotation pipeline described in Section 3: pilot annotations with author review, iterative refinement of guidelines across three annotation rounds, and a final filtering process to achieve a controlled difficulty distribution (10% easy, 30% medium, 60% hard, following Llama 3's human evaluation standards).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper does not propose a new model architecture, training method, or prompting technique. It is fundamentally an **evaluation and analysis contribution** that fills the gap between isolated capability benchmarks and holistic agent evaluation. Its positioning can be understood along four dimensions:

**1. It defines and taxonomizes a neglected evaluation category.** The paper provides the first systematic definition and hierarchical taxonomy of cross capabilities (Section 2, Appendix A). By identifying seven core individual capabilities (English, Reasoning, Coding, Image Recognition, Tool Use, Long Context, Spanish) and pairing them into seven cross capabilities (Coding & Reasoning, Image Recognition & Reasoning, Tool Use & Coding, Tool Use & Reasoning, Long Context & Coding, Spanish & Reasoning, Spanish & Image Recognition), it creates an explicit framework for what has previously been an implicit problem. Each taxonomy breaks down into Level-1 categories (broad task families) and Level-2 categories (specific task types), providing structured coverage that can be systematically evaluated.

**2. It builds a rigorous, human-calibrated benchmark.** CrossEval is constructed with an attention to annotation quality that goes well beyond typical benchmark efforts. The paper employs professional expert annotators—the same data vendor used for Llama 3's human evaluations—with domain-specific knowledge for each capability. The annotation process involves three iterative rounds to improve inter-rater agreement (from an initial Krippendorff's alpha of 0.48 to a final 0.76). Each of the 4,200 model responses receives two independent ratings with detailed explanatory paragraphs, creating a rich reference standard. This rigor is motivated by the recognition that evaluating complex, open-ended responses—particularly for cross-capability tasks—is inherently challenging and requires substantial calibration.

**3. It develops improved LLM-as-a-Judge methodology.** The paper's point deduction-based prompting approach, multi-reference calibration, and systematic correlation analysis with human judgments (Table 2) advance the state of automated evaluation. By showing that providing two human-annotated reference examples improves Pearson correlation from 0.578 to 0.697 (Figure 2), the paper provides concrete evidence for the value of investment in human annotation for calibrating automated evaluators. The selection of GPT-4o as the final evaluator (with Claude 3.5 Sonnet results in Appendix C.2 for corroboration) and the meta-evaluation across 14 capabilities provide a blueprint for future benchmark construction.

**4. It empirically tests competing theoretical frameworks for cross-capability relationships.** This is the paper's most distinctive contribution. Rather than simply reporting that models perform worse on cross-capability tasks—a finding that might be expected given increased task complexity—it formalizes the relationship by testing the Law of the Weakest Link against alternatives (synergy, compensation, emergence). The finding that 38 out of 58 cross-capability scores with a clear strong-weak gap fall *below* both individual capabilities (Table 3, red-background cells), and the remaining 20 fall between them but closer to the weaker capability (blue-background cells), provides strong evidence for the Law of the Weakest Link as the dominant pattern. The dynamic experiments in Section 5 further strengthen this: selectively enhancing weaker capabilities produces significant cross-capability gains, while enhancing stronger capabilities yields only minor shifts—behavior exactly predicted by Liebig's principle.

The paper thus positions itself at the intersection of LLM evaluation methodology and capability analysis, providing both a practical benchmark (CrossEval) and a theoretical finding (the Law of the Weakest Link) that together reframe how the field should think about capability development. The implication is clear: rather than the prevailing paradigm of pushing forward on all capability fronts simultaneously or focusing on headline-grabbing reasoning and coding benchmarks, development efforts should prioritize identifying and strengthening the *weakest* links—and CrossEval provides the instrument for doing so.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The "system" being built is not a single model or algorithm but rather a **comprehensive evaluation framework**—a benchmark called CrossEval paired with a calibrated LLM-based judge—designed to measure and compare LLM performance on both isolated skills and tasks that require combining multiple skills simultaneously. The problem it solves is that no existing benchmark systematically pairs capabilities to assess how models perform at their intersection, leaving a critical blind spot in our understanding of LLM behavior. The shape of the solution is: (1) define a hierarchical taxonomy of individual and cross capabilities to specify *what* to test, (2) construct a high-quality, human-annotated prompt set with multiple reference responses to provide *ground truth* for evaluation, (3) calibrate an LLM-as-a-Judge against human ratings to enable *scalable automated evaluation*, and (4) compare cross-capability scores against constituent individual scores across many models to empirically determine the governing relationship—specifically, whether the Law of the Weakest Link, synergy, compensation, or emergence best characterizes model behavior.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The CrossEval framework has four major components, arranged in a pipeline:

1. **Taxonomy Construction (Section 2, Appendix A)** — A manually built two-level hierarchy for each of 14 capabilities (7 individual + 7 cross), where Level-1 defines broad task categories (e.g., "Mathematical Calculation," "Code Generation / Synthesis") and Level-2 defines specific task types (e.g., "Arithmetic & basic math," "Code completion"). Every prompt in the benchmark is anchored to a specific leaf node in this taxonomy, ensuring systematic coverage.

2. **Prompt Set Annotation (Section 3.1)** — Professional expert annotators select taxonomy leaf nodes, write single-turn open-ended prompts at controlled difficulty levels (10% easy, 30% medium, 60% hard), and undergo iterative review to produce a final set of 1,400 prompts (100 per capability). Each prompt is categorized by the specific capability it tests.

3. **Multi-Reference Human Annotation (Section 3.2)** — For each prompt, three model responses are collected from various Llama and GPT models to span quality levels (low, medium, high). Two independent expert annotators rate each response on a 1–5 Likert scale and provide a paragraph-length explanation of their rating. After three iterative annotation rounds to improve agreement, the final dataset contains 4,200 responses with 8,400 human ratings and explanations.

4. **LLM-based Evaluator (Section 3.3)** — A GPT-4o model (version 2024-05-13) is prompted with: (a) general rubrics defining each Likert level, (b) up to two human-annotated reference examples with ratings and explanations for calibration, (c) a structured point-deduction-based output format that requires the model to analyze the user prompt, summarize scoring patterns from references, specify point deductions for each weakness, perform a holistic assessment, and produce a final score. This evaluator achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.697 with human judgments across all 14 capabilities.

The evaluation workflow then proceeds as follows: for each of 17 target models from five model families, generate responses to all relevant prompts using default decoding parameters → feed each response to the calibrated GPT-4o evaluator along with its prompt and reference examples → map the resulting Likert scores to a 1–100 scale → compare cross-capability scores to constituent individual capability scores to test the Law of the Weakest Link.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

The remaining subsections will unpack each component in detail, following the order that the pipeline operates because each stage constrains the next:

- **First**, the taxonomy construction methodology (Section 2 details), since it is the specification that determines *what gets tested* and provides the scaffolding for all subsequent annotation. Understanding the taxonomy is prerequisite to understanding what any individual prompt is measuring.

- **Second**, the prompt set annotation pipeline (Section 3.1), including difficulty definitions, the iterative review process, and quality control mechanisms, because the prompts are the test items whose quality directly determines evaluation validity.

- **Third**, the multi-reference human annotation process (Section 3.2), including annotator qualifications, model response collection strategy, the iterative calibration procedure that raised Krippendorff's alpha from 0.48 to 0.76, and the resulting benchmark statistics. This is the ground truth against which all automated evaluation is calibrated.

- **Fourth**, the LLM-based evaluator design (Section 3.3), analyzed in two sub-parts: the prompting strategy (general rubrics, multi-reference calibration, point-deduction-based output format) and the correlation analysis with human judgments (Table 2, Figure 2). This is the measurement instrument whose reliability determines whether subsequent findings about cross-capability relationships are trustworthy.

- **Fifth**, the principle-based system prompting method (Section 5.1), which is used not for evaluation but for the dynamic experiments that test whether altering individual capabilities shifts cross-capability performance in ways predicted by the Law of the Weakest Link. I include it here because it is a technical mechanism for *manipulating* capabilities that feeds into the analysis.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **evaluation and measurement paper** whose core idea is that cross-capability performance in LLMs is bottlenecked by the weakest constituent ability, and that a rigorously constructed benchmark with calibrated automated evaluation can empirically establish this pattern. The technical contribution is the construction and validation of the measurement instrument (CrossEval) and the demonstration of the Law of the Weakest Link through systematic comparison of individual and cross-capability scores.

#### Taxonomy Construction: Defining What to Measure

The foundational technical decision in CrossEval is the construction of a hierarchical taxonomy for each of 14 capabilities. This taxonomy serves as the specification that determines which tasks get tested, and it is essential for ensuring that the benchmark's coverage is systematic rather than ad hoc.

**Capability selection.** Seven individual capabilities are selected to represent "core LLM skills across diverse domains, including multimodal, multilingual, and tool-use tasks" (Section 2.1):

- **English**: General language understanding and generation, covering factual questions, procedural questions, language assistance, writing and content creation, dialogue, recommendations/brainstorming, personal growth and development, and social interaction and communication. This capability serves as the baseline for language proficiency.
- **Reasoning**: Multi-faceted analytical thinking, including mathematical calculation, mathematical reasoning, commonsense reasoning, logic/problem solving, social and emotional reasoning, moral and ethical reasoning, scientific reasoning, and legal reasoning. This captures the cognitive processing abilities central to LLM evaluation.
- **Coding**: Software engineering tasks including code generation/synthesis, code documentation, code debugging, and code review/best practices. This represents a domain where LLMs have shown strong capabilities and where cross-capability demands are common.
- **Image Recognition**: Visual understanding tasks including object recognition, scene understanding, image captioning, attribute/relationship identification, dialogue about images, and graceful refusals (for blurry or unsupported images). This introduces the multimodal dimension.
- **Tool Use**: Tasks requiring external information access or computation, including factual questions about recent/current things, very accurate questions beyond expected model knowledge, procedural questions about recent/current/local things, recommendations/brainstorming about local/current things, and tasks with file uploads. This captures the agentic dimension critical for real-world deployment.
- **Long Context**: Tasks involving extended text comprehension, including factoid/complex question answering, summarization, and multi-document understanding over long inputs. This tests a capability dimension (context window utilization) that is distinct from reasoning or knowledge.
- **Spanish**: A representative multilingual capability, mirroring the English taxonomy but with prompts crafted natively in Spanish. The choice of Spanish specifically (rather than, say, Chinese or Arabic) reflects a practical decision about annotator availability and represents one point on the multilingual spectrum.

Seven cross capabilities are formed by pairing these individual capabilities (Section 2.2):

- **Coding & Reasoning**: Tasks at the intersection of programming and analytical thinking, including coding Q&A, code explanation, programming assistance requiring algorithmic reasoning, and mathematical calculation requiring code. The taxonomy includes Level-1 categories: Coding Q&A (Text to Text), Code Explanation, Programming Assistant, and Mathematical Calculation.
- **Image Recognition & Reasoning**: Tasks combining visual understanding with analytical thinking, including diagram understanding, chart understanding, text-rich understanding, and visual math and science. This pairing tests whether models can reason *about* what they see rather than merely describe it.
- **Tool Use & Coding**: Tasks requiring both external computation and programming skill, including code execution, code debugging with execution, programming assistant with execution, and code execution with file uploads. This tests whether models can write code, run it, and interpret results coherently.
- **Tool Use & Reasoning**: Tasks demanding both information retrieval and analytical processing, including mathematical reasoning with web-sourced data, scientific reasoning across 15 subfields, and mathematical calculation requiring external verification. This has the largest number of Level-2 categories (30) among cross capabilities.
- **Long Context & Coding**: Tasks combining extended context comprehension with programming, including repository-level code generation, repository-level code understanding, repository-level code debugging, log analysis, and API documentation understanding. This tests whether models can reason about large codebases spanning many files.
- **Spanish & Reasoning** and **Spanish & Image Recognition**: These cross capabilities require no new taxonomy because "handling and generating multilingual content naturally integrates with other capabilities" (Section 2.2). The prompt sets are created by translating the corresponding English-based prompts into Spanish, enabling direct comparison of cross-capability performance across languages.

**Taxonomy structure.** Each taxonomy follows a two-level hierarchy (Figure 1, Appendix A):

- **Root node**: The capability name (e.g., "Reasoning," "Image Recognition & Reasoning")
- **Level-1 nodes**: Broad task categories. For Reasoning, these include "Mathematical Calculation," "Mathematical Reasoning," "Commonsense Reasoning," "Logic / Problem Solving," "Social and Emotional Reasoning," "Moral & Ethical Reasoning," "Scientific Reasoning," and "Legal Reasoning." The number in parentheses after each category name (e.g., "Scientific Reasoning (4)") indicates the number of Level-2 subcategories.
- **Level-2 nodes**: Specific task types. For Scientific Reasoning, these include "Hypothesis Formation and Testing," "Causal Reasoning," "Scientific Evidence Evaluation," and "Model-Based Reasoning."

For cross capabilities, the taxonomy nodes represent tasks that *necessarily require both constituent capabilities*. The paper is explicit about this: the Image Recognition & Reasoning taxonomy includes nodes like "Diagram Understanding," "Chart Understanding," and "Visual Math and Science"—tasks where visual perception and analytical reasoning are inseparably intertwined. This is distinct from tasks where one capability is merely adjacent or supplementary; the taxonomy is designed to capture genuine integration.

**Why this taxonomic approach.** The hierarchical taxonomy provides several properties that are essential for the paper's evaluation goals. First, it ensures **systematic coverage**: every prompt in the benchmark is anchored to a specific leaf node, so the set of prompts collectively spans the defined capability space rather than clustering in easy-to-write categories. Second, it enables **granular analysis**: by mapping each prompt to a specific Level-2 category, researchers can identify not just *which* capabilities are weak, but *which specific types of tasks* within a capability are most problematic. Third, it provides **extensibility**: new capabilities or cross-capability pairs can be added by constructing new taxonomies following the same two-level structure, and new prompts can be assigned to existing leaf nodes. Fourth, it creates **evaluability**: because each prompt has a known category, the benchmark can report scores not just at the capability level but at finer granularities if needed.

The comprehensive taxonomies are provided in Appendix A (Tables 5–15), which readers should consult for the full specification. The total coverage spans 76 Level-1 categories and 332 Level-2 categories across all 14 capabilities.

#### Prompt Set Annotation: Creating the Test Items

The prompt set is the core of CrossEval, and the paper invests substantial effort in ensuring quality through a multi-stage professional annotation pipeline (Section 3.1). The key design constraint is that prompts must be **challenging enough to differentiate advanced models** while **aligned with real-world usage patterns** and **unambiguously classifiable** into the taxonomy.

**Annotation procedure.** The process follows these sequential stages:

1. **Taxonomy-based prompt creation.** Annotators begin by selecting a leaf node (a Level-2 category) from the established taxonomy. This ensures that every prompt has a known capability assignment before it is written. For each prompt, the annotator crafts a single-turn, open-ended query that tests the specified capability at a targeted difficulty level.

2. **Difficulty standardization.** For each capability, the paper defines explicit, operationalized criteria for three difficulty levels. Taking English as the representative example (Section 3.1):

   - **Easy**: "Prompt is a single ask/requirement/constraint for the model presented as a single statement OR prompt is a single statement without ask/requirement/constraints AND would not require subject matter expertise to understand."
   - **Medium**: "Prompt includes 2–4 asks/requirements/constraints for the model AND would not require subject matter expertise to produce a response."
   - **Hard**: "Prompt contains 5 or more asks/requirements/constraints for the model OR requires subject matter expertise above and beyond 'common knowledge' in order to respond."

   These definitions are capability-specific. For Image Recognition (Appendix B.3.2), the criteria additionally account for visual complexity, occlusion, distraction, and the need for visual-related professional knowledge. The hard definition for Image Recognition includes: "Requires identification of five or more entities, each possessing distinct characteristics or components, amid conditions of visual complexity. This includes scenarios where unrelated visual elements may interfere (visual distraction), or where relevant parts of the entities are partially obscured from view (visual occlusion)."

   The target difficulty distribution throughout follows the standards used in Llama 3's human evaluations: **10% easy, 30% medium, 60% hard** (Section 3.1). This heavy skew toward hard prompts is intentional—the paper cites prior work showing that many existing benchmarks contain large numbers of low-quality inputs that fail to differentiate between advanced models (Li et al., 2024), and that constructing genuinely challenging prompts is inherently difficult (Padlewski et al., 2024).

3. **Pilot annotation phase.** Before the main annotation, the paper conducts a pilot phase where the authors themselves act as reviewers. They provide feedback to identify issues with initial annotations and refine the annotation guidelines accordingly. This is analogous to a calibration phase in experimental design—it catches systematic problems before they propagate into the full dataset.

4. **Main annotation phase.** Annotators produce 100 to 500 prompts for each capability, depending on the size of the annotator pool assigned to that capability. The variation in pool size reflects the practical reality that different capabilities require different domain expertise (coding experts vs. Spanish linguists vs. general reasoning evaluators) and that annotator availability varies.

5. **Quality filtering.** Reviewers perform quality checks on the full set of annotated prompts and apply filtering to produce a final set of exactly **100 high-quality prompts per capability**. The filtering step is critical: it is where prompts that are ambiguous, misclassified, or insufficiently challenging are removed. The result is a balanced benchmark of 1,400 prompts total.

**Spanish prompt handling.** For Spanish as an individual capability, all prompts are annotated from scratch with no overlap with the English prompt set. For cross-capability scenarios involving Spanish (Spanish & Reasoning, Spanish & Image Recognition), the prompts are created by **translating the corresponding English-based prompts into Spanish**. This design choice enables a controlled comparison: because the Spanish cross-capability prompts are direct translations of their English counterparts, any performance difference between English Reasoning and Spanish & Reasoning can be attributed to the multilingual dimension rather than differences in prompt content or difficulty.

**Prompt set properties.** The final prompt set covers all 76 Level-1 and 332 Level-2 categories (Table 1). The per-capability breakdown is:

| Capability Type | # Capabilities | Prompts per Capability | Total Prompts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Individual | 7 | 100 | 700 |
| Cross | 7 | 100 | 700 |
| **Total** | **14** | | **1,400** |

The Level-1 and Level-2 counts vary by capability, reflecting differences in the taxonomy size: individual English has 8 L1 and 45 L2 categories, while individual Long Context has 3 L1 and 14 L2 categories. This variation is natural—some capabilities decompose into more distinct task types than others—and the 100-prompt-per-capability standardization ensures that broader taxonomies are not advantaged by having more evaluation items.

**Why single-turn and open-ended.** The paper explicitly restricts prompts to single-turn and open-ended settings (Section 3.1). Single-turn simplifies evaluation by avoiding the compounding complexity of multi-turn dialogue dynamics. Open-ended (rather than multiple-choice or exact-match) reflects real-world usage where users expect free-form responses and where evaluation based on a single gold answer is inappropriate. This choice makes automated evaluation harder—hence the investment in the LLM-based judge—but substantially increases the benchmark's ecological validity.

#### Multi-Reference Human Annotation: Creating the Ground Truth

Once prompts are constructed, the next stage is producing reference standards against which model responses can be evaluated. The paper's approach to this is distinctive: rather than providing a single gold reference answer per prompt (the pre-LLM standard), it provides **multiple model responses with human ratings and explanations** that serve as calibration examples for the automated evaluator.

**Why not gold references?** Section 3.2 articulates three reasons why providing a single correct answer per prompt is infeasible for CrossEval:

1. **Open-endedness**: "Many open-ended queries do not have a single correct answer, and offering only one response as the reference risks introducing bias in the evaluation." For a prompt asking for creative writing or a nuanced analysis, multiple responses can be equally valid but structurally different, and evaluating against a single reference would penalize valid alternatives.

2. **Expertise requirements**: "Several prompts, particularly those requiring domain expertise in areas such as coding or mathematics, remain challenging even for college-level expert annotators." The annotators can assess correctness when presented with a model response, but they may not be able to produce a definitive reference answer from scratch, especially for hard prompts.

3. **Dynamic answers**: "For prompts related to tool use, the correct response can be dynamic. For example, the answer to 'What is the temperature in the Bay Area today?' changes daily." A static gold reference would be wrong by the time the benchmark is used, but a human annotator looking at a model response can assess whether it correctly retrieved and reported current information.

The solution is to use **multiple model responses, scored and explained by human annotators, as reference examples**. This shifts the evaluation paradigm from "compare to a correct answer" to "compare to expert-judged exemplars spanning the quality spectrum."

**Annotator qualifications.** The paper uses "the same data vendor as Llama 3's human evaluation, employing professional experts with domain-specific knowledge, such as reasoning, coding, and Spanish" (Section 3.2). This is a significant strength: the annotators are not crowdworkers or generalists, but professionals with relevant expertise who are selected from different pools depending on the capability being evaluated. This domain-matching is essential because evaluating a code debugging response requires coding expertise, evaluating a Spanish creative writing response requires Spanish fluency, and evaluating a mathematical proof requires mathematical training.

An important methodological safeguard: "To avoid contamination, the Llama team does not have access to CrossEval prompts during Llama 3's development" (Section 3.2). This ensures that CrossEval is a held-out evaluation set not used in training or development, preventing inflated performance estimates from data leakage.

**Model response collection strategy.** For each of the 1,400 prompts, the goal is to gather three model responses spanning varying quality levels (low, medium, high). These responses are "randomly drawn from various models within the Llama and GPT model families, including Llama 3.1 8B/70B/405B and different versions of GPT-4" (Section 3.2). The random assignment ensures that the reference set is not systematically biased toward any single model's output style or error patterns.

Additionally, for capabilities involving Reasoning, Image Recognition, and Tool Use, "we manually annotate one response if all three collected responses contain noticeable errors" (Section 3.2). This manual fallback prevents the reference set from being dominated by low-quality examples in cases where all sampled models fail on a challenging prompt, which would distort the calibration of the evaluator.

**Human rating procedure.** For each of the (now) 4,200 model responses, two independent annotators provide:

1. A rating on a **1–5 Likert scale** with the following rubric (Section 3.3.1):
   - **5/5 - Amazing**: "The response is flawless and could hardly be improved."
   - **4/5 - Pretty Good**: "The response is quite good, but has room for minor improvements."
   - **3/5 - Okay**: "They are middle-of-the-road responses that could be improved in several ways."
   - **2/5 - Pretty Bad**: "The response has major problems in helpfulness, truthfulness, or safety."
   - **1/5 - Horrible**: "They are terrible responses and you would caution others against using models that generate responses like this."

2. A **paragraph-length explanation** of their rating, providing qualitative justification.

The dual annotation with explanations serves multiple purposes: it enables inter-rater reliability measurement, it provides rich qualitative context that the LLM evaluator can use for calibration, and it creates a transparent audit trail for individual ratings.

**Iterative calibration procedure.** The paper found that initial annotation quality was insufficient and invested in an iterative refinement process that is instructive for benchmark construction methodology:

- **Round 1 (pilot, 30% of prompts):** Inter-rater agreement was only 33.65%, with a Krippendorff's Alpha (K-Alpha) of 0.48. This is below the commonly accepted threshold of 0.67 for tentative conclusions and 0.80 for strong conclusions, indicating that annotators were applying different standards.

- **Round 2:** New raters from the same pool review previous annotations, better understand the scoring criteria, and provide their ratings with explanations. Agreement improves to 45.79% and K-Alpha to 0.66.

- **Round 3:** Further refinement pushes agreement to 47.38% and K-Alpha to 0.73. This exceeds the 0.67 threshold, indicating acceptable reliability.

- **Full dataset annotation:** After updating guidelines based on the three pilot rounds, the trained annotator pool annotates the complete dataset. Final inter-rater agreement: **54.93%**, final K-Alpha: **0.76**.

The paper contextualizes these numbers against the well-known Chatbot Arena benchmark (Zheng et al., 2023), where "the human agreement rate is 81% for binary classification (win/lose) and 63% for a 1–3 scale (win/tie/lose)." CrossEval's agreement rate of 54.93% on a more granular 1–5 scale with independent scoring (not pairwise comparison) "still achieves a substantial level of agreement." This is a fair comparison: finer-grained scales inherently have lower agreement because there are more categories to disagree about, and independent scoring (each annotator assigns a score without knowing the other's assignment) is a harder task than pairwise preference (which of two is better).

**Resulting benchmark statistics.** The final CrossEval benchmark comprises:
- 1,400 prompts (100 per capability × 14 capabilities)
- 4,200 reference model responses (3 per prompt × 1,400 prompts)
- 8,400 human ratings (2 per response × 4,200 responses)
- 8,400 human explanations (paired with each rating)

Tables 28–33 in Appendix B.2 provide concrete examples of what this looks like for individual instances: a prompt, three model responses, and for each response, two ratings with paragraph-length explanations. These examples are instructive for understanding the granularity of annotation—the explanations cite specific factual errors, identify missing requirements, and justify the assigned score with reference to the Likert rubric.

#### LLM-Based Evaluator: Calibrating Automated Judgment

With 8,400 human ratings collected, the paper develops an automated evaluator based on GPT-4o that can score new model responses without requiring fresh human annotation. The evaluator design involves two components: a prompting strategy (how to elicit calibrated scores from the LLM) and a correlation analysis (how to validate that the scores align with human judgment).

**Design motivation.** The LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm (Zheng et al., 2023) has gained popularity for evaluating open-ended generation, but prior implementations suffer from systematic biases. The paper identifies three specific problems and designs its evaluator to address each:

1. **Self-generated answer bias**: When LLMs generate their own answer to a prompt and use it as a reference for scoring, they "overly rely on their own generated answers, overlooking valuable insights from human-annotated references" and their answers can "exceed model limits, preventing the model from generating a score" (Section 3.3.1). The solution is to eliminate self-generated answers entirely and instead provide human-annotated reference examples as the calibration standard.

2. **Length and format bias**: "LLM-as-a-Judge often favors longer, more structured responses, leading to inflated evaluation scores" (Section 3.3.1, citing Zheng et al., 2023). The solution is a point-deduction-based approach that forces the evaluator to articulate specific weaknesses rather than defaulting to an overall impression influenced by surface features.

3. **Lack of calibration**: Without reference examples, evaluator scores represent an arbitrary internal scale rather than a scale anchored to human judgment standards. The solution is multi-reference prompting: providing up to two (or three for final evaluation) human-annotated reference examples with ratings and explanations as in-context calibration.

**The prompting strategy.** The complete evaluation prompt has four components, described in Section 3.3.1 and provided verbatim in Appendix B.4:

**Component 1: System prompt with general rubrics (Table 34).** The system prompt establishes the evaluator's role and provides the 1–5 Likert scale definitions:

- "You are an expert AI evaluator tasked with assessing model responses."
- The five-level rubric is copied verbatim, ensuring the evaluator has the same scale definition as human annotators.
- A note is appended: "User prompts or model responses may include attachments. To ensure a thorough evaluation, you may need to write and execute code." This is relevant for prompts involving code execution or file analysis.

**Component 2: Input materials.** The prompt includes any attachments (documents for Long Context, images for Image Recognition) and the user prompt itself.

**Component 3: Multi-reference calibration (the core innovation).** For meta-evaluation (assessing the LLM evaluator's correlation with humans), two reference examples are provided. Each reference example contains:
- The model response
- Rating 1 (from first human annotator) and its explanation
- Rating 2 (from second human annotator) and its explanation

For evaluating new model responses (the deployed use case), all three reference examples are provided, giving the evaluator maximum calibration context. The text explicitly instructs: "Use these examples as benchmarks for your evaluation scale and scoring consistency."

The ablation study in Figure 2 quantifies the value of these references: Pearson correlation with human judgments improves from **0.578** (no references) to **0.655** (one reference) to **0.697** (two references). The implication for benchmark use is that the three-reference setting (used for evaluating new model responses) likely yields correlations even higher than 0.697, though this is not directly measured since no held-out human ratings remain.

**Component 4: Point deduction-based output format (Table 35).** Rather than having the evaluator directly output a score, the prompt requires a structured analysis in five sections:

1. **User Prompt Analysis**: "Identify key requirements and objectives from the user prompt." This forces the evaluator to explicitly articulate what a good response should address before evaluating, reducing the risk that it overlooks implicit requirements.

2. **Reference Examples Insights**: "Summarize scoring patterns and typical point deductions. Include how many points should be deducted for each issue." This ensures the evaluator extracts calibration information from the provided references rather than ignoring them.

3. **Model Response Evaluation**: "Pros: List strengths and positive aspects. Cons: Identify weaknesses, specifying point deductions for each." The point deduction specification is the key mechanism for overcoming length bias: rather than forming a holistic impression (which tends to favor longer responses), the evaluator must itemize specific weaknesses and assign each a point penalty.

4. **Holistic Assessment**: "Consider if major strengths outweigh minor issues. Combine similar deductions to avoid double penalization. Balance deductions and positive aspects, and then explain your scoring decision." This prevents the evaluator from mechanically summing deductions without considering compensatory strengths.

5. **Evaluation Score**: "Score: [X]/5" as the final output.

**Why point deduction works.** The paper draws on prior work by Zhong et al. (2024) but adapts the approach specifically for the cross-capability evaluation context. The mechanism addresses length bias by forcing the evaluator to engage with content: a long response with many factual errors will accumulate more point deductions than a shorter but accurate response, breaking the correlation between length and score. It also addresses format bias by requiring explicit, itemized justification: the evaluator cannot simply assign a score based on whether the response "looks good" but must specify what is wrong and how many points each issue costs.

Additionally, the structured format produces an **auditable evaluation**: each score is accompanied by a trace of reasoning that can be reviewed for consistency and fairness. This is valuable for benchmark transparency and for diagnosing systematic evaluator biases.

**Evaluator model selection.** The paper tests four candidate LLMs as evaluators (Table 2):

- **GPT-4o mini**: Lower cost but substantially weaker correlation; overall Pearson of 0.621.
- **Llama 3.1 405B**: Strong on some capabilities but cannot evaluate image-related tasks (indicated by dashes in Table 2); overall correlations not computable due to missing capabilities.
- **Claude 3.5 Sonnet**: Strong overall performer; Pearson 0.696, Spearman 0.676, Kendall 0.550. Particularly strong on Tool Use (0.683), Image Recognition & Reasoning (0.819), and Spanish & Image Recognition (0.752).
- **GPT-4o-05-13**: The best overall performer; Pearson **0.697**, Spearman **0.679**, Kendall **0.560**. Excels at cross-capability evaluation: Coding & Reasoning (0.664), Tool Use & Coding (0.639), Tool Use & Reasoning (0.729), Spanish & Reasoning (0.772).

The paper considers using a **mixture of evaluators**—deploying Claude for Spanish & Image Recognition and GPT-4o for Spanish & Reasoning—to maximize per-capability correlation. However, this approach is rejected because "significant differences in scoring distributions across models: Claude 3.5 tends to give higher scores, while GPT-4o is more stringent" (Section 3.3.2). If different evaluators assign systematically different scores to the same response, then comparisons across capabilities (which is the entire point of the Law of the Weakest Link analysis) become confounded by evaluator calibration differences rather than genuine capability differences. The paper's decision to use a single evaluator (GPT-4o) across all capabilities is methodologically sound for its primary research question, even though it sacrifices some per-capability correlation.

Claude 3.5 Sonnet results are provided in Appendix C.2 (Table 39) as a robustness check, and the Law of the Weakest Link pattern is shown to hold under both evaluators (Figure 3).

**Correlation analysis methodology.** For each of the 4,200 samples, the LLM evaluator is provided two reference examples and asked to evaluate the third. The evaluator's score is compared to the average of the two human ratings (serving as "human judgment"). Three correlation metrics are computed:

- **Pearson's r**: Measures linear correlation; sensitive to outliers and assumes interval-scale measurement.
- **Spearman's ρ (rₛ)**: Measures rank correlation; robust to monotonic nonlinearities and assumes only ordinal measurement.
- **Kendall's τ**: Measures rank correlation based on concordant/discordant pairs; more conservative than Spearman.

The paper reports all three (Table 2, bottom rows), and the consistency across metrics (0.697, 0.679, 0.560 for GPT-4o) suggests genuine alignment between evaluator and humans rather than an artifact of a particular correlation measure.

**Contextualizing the correlation magnitude.** The paper notes that "in the recent benchmark BigGen Bench (Kim et al., 2024), which includes gold references and human ratings, LLM-based scoring reached a Pearson correlation of 0.627" (Section 3.3.2). CrossEval's 0.697 represents a meaningful improvement, especially given that CrossEval's prompts are open-ended and deliberately difficult—conditions that typically reduce evaluator reliability. The paper attributes the improvement to the multi-reference prompting and point-deduction format.

**Tool use evaluation without tool access.** A subtle but important design decision: "the LLM APIs we experiment with do not support web browsing, and only the GPT-4 API supports code interpreters" (Section 3.3.2). Yet the evaluator must assess responses to tool-use prompts that require web browsing or code execution. The solution relies on the reference examples: "when we specify the date of the reference examples and indicate that the answers may be dynamic, LLMs without web browsing features can still serve as effective evaluators, achieving Pearson correlations above 0.6 across all tool use-related capabilities." Additionally, enabling GPT's code interpreter "results in similar correlation scores but incurs higher costs," so it is disabled in the final evaluator. This is because "the reference examples already provide sufficient context for evaluation, eliminating the need for the model to execute code."

This finding has practical implications: calibrating evaluators with human-annotated references can substitute for direct capability access. An evaluator that cannot browse the web can still assess whether a model's response plausibly answers a web-browsing-required question by comparing it to reference examples where the correct information retrieval behavior is demonstrated.

#### Principle-Based System Prompting: Manipulating Individual Capabilities

Section 5 introduces a method for dynamically altering specific individual capabilities to test the causal relationship with cross-capability performance. This is not part of the evaluation pipeline per se, but it is the technical mechanism that enables the paper's fourth research question (RQ4): "How do shifts in individual capabilities impact cross-capability performance?"

**Motivation.** The static evaluation in Section 4 establishes a correlation: cross-capability scores cluster near the weaker individual capability. But correlation is not causation. It is possible that some unobserved third factor drives both individual and cross-capability performance downward. To establish a causal link—that the weaker capability *bottlenecks* cross-capability performance—the paper needs to intervene on individual capabilities and observe the effect. If improving the weaker capability substantially improves cross-capability performance while improving the stronger capability does not, this provides causal evidence for the Law of the Weakest Link.

**The principle-based method.** The approach iteratively refines a system prompt to enhance a specific LLM capability without fine-tuning. The procedure (Section 5.1) operates as follows:

1. **Initial Setup:** For each training instance, the input consists of: the user prompt, the target model's response to that prompt, the evaluation feedback from the LLM-based evaluator (identifying specific weaknesses), and an evolving principle list (initially empty).

2. **Iterative Refinement (100 iterations):** Using GPT-4o as the principle-generating model (distinct from the model being enhanced), the system processes one instance per iteration and selects one of four operations:

   - **ADD**: Introduce a new principle that addresses an issue observed in the current instance but not covered by existing principles. This operation expands the principle list when novel failure modes are encountered.
   - **REPLACE**: Substitute a less significant principle with a new one. This prevents unbounded growth of the principle list (capped at 10) and ensures that the most impactful principles survive.
   - **REVISE**: Refine existing principles for greater clarity and precision. This improves the actionability of principles that capture the right idea but are too vague for direct execution.
   - **KEEP**: Leave the principles unchanged if the current instance is adequately covered by existing principles. This prevents overfitting to individual instances at the expense of generalizability.

   The principle-generating model is prompted with the complete instance and the current principle list, and it must output a summary of major issues, a decision about which operation to apply, and the updated principle list (Table 41 in Appendix D.1).

3. **Final Principle List:** After 100 iterations, the process yields a system prompt containing up to 10 principles tailored to enhance the target capability. This system prompt can be prepended to any user prompt to instruct the model to modify its behavior for that capability.

**What the principles look like.** Appendix D.2 (Tables 42–44) provides the complete set of principles generated for enhancing Gemini 1.5 Flash's Reasoning capability. Examples illustrate the specificity required:

- **Principle 1 (For Mathematical Reasoning):** "Verify All Mathematical Steps and Properties Thoroughly: Validate each step in mathematical derivation meticulously, focusing on crucial values, properties, and boundary conditions. Ensure consistency in the use of all variables and constants across the steps. Verify the accuracy of factorization and simplification steps." This is followed by sub-principles about detailing intermediate calculations, identifying misleading statements, completing execution of methods, balancing thoroughness and conciseness, and leveraging degree mismatches.

- **Principle 4 (For Addressing Ambiguities):** "Identify and Resolve Ambiguities: Point out any ambiguous terms or conditions within the problem statement. Clearly state how these ambiguities are resolved. Make Assumptions Clear: Articulate any assumptions made to proceed with the solution. Justify why these assumptions are reasonable and how they influence results."

- **Principle 8 (For Designing Scientific Experiments):** "Detail the Measurement Methods: Specify tools and procedures for measuring each variable. Include details like frequency of measurements and exact techniques used. Clarify Statistical Analysis: Explain how statistical tests will analyze collected data. Provide details on data preparation and results interpretation."

The principles are highly specific—"each point must be extremely specific to allow for direct execution" (Table 41)—and include concrete formatting instructions, lists of terms to avoid, and step-by-step verification procedures. This specificity is what makes them effective as system prompts: they provide actionable guidance rather than vague aspirations.

**Key design choice: specificity over generality.** The prompt for principle generation (Table 41) explicitly contrasts good and bad specificity: "instead of saying 'Use a structured markdown format,' clearly define the exact format for each step, including the structure for the beginning, middle, and end." Similarly, "instead of advising to 'avoid vague terms,' provide a specific list of terms to be avoided." This design philosophy reflects an empirical observation: general advice in system prompts ("be more careful," "think step by step") is less effective than concrete, procedurally specified instructions for modifying model behavior at inference time.

**The principle application protocol.** The generated system prompt is applied only to prompts relevant to the target capability. The note appended to the Reasoning principles (Table 44) specifies: "Apply the principles above to generate better responses for user prompts that require reasoning. For prompts that do not require reasoning, disregard these principles. Avoid quoting or referencing these principles, as the user is not aware of its existence." This scoping prevents the system prompt from interfering with non-target capabilities, enabling clean measurement of capability-specific enhancement effects.

**Case study design (Section 5.2).** The principle-based method is deployed in a case study with a specific design:

- **Cross capabilities selected**: Image Recognition & Reasoning, Spanish & Reasoning, and Spanish & Image Recognition—those with the largest individual performance gaps across the 17 models evaluated, providing the clearest strong-weak asymmetry.
- **Models selected**: Claude 3 Haiku and Gemini 1.5 Flash, which display the largest performance discrepancies in these cross-capability scenarios. A more pronounced gap provides a stronger test: if the Law of the Weakest Link holds, enhancing the weaker capability should produce proportionally larger cross-capability improvements.
- **Manipulation applied**: For each cross-capability pair, the system prompt for each constituent capability is applied separately (e.g., "+ Reasoning" and "+ Image Recognition" for Image Recognition & Reasoning), and the resulting individual and cross-capability scores are measured.

The results, shown in Table 4, demonstrate the asymmetric effect predicted by the Law of the Weakest Link. For example, with Claude 3 Haiku in Image Recognition & Reasoning: enhancing Reasoning (the stronger capability, improving from 56.81 to 59.66) actually *decreases* cross-capability performance (from 49.88 to 46.42), while enhancing Image Recognition (the weaker capability, improving from 51.00 to 54.71) *increases* cross-capability performance (from 49.88 to 54.46). The cross-capability score moves with the weaker capability, not the stronger one.

**Why this method over alternatives.** The principle-based prompting approach has several properties that make it suitable for the paper's causal investigation. First, it enables **selective enhancement**: by generating principles specifically for a target capability using data from that capability's prompts, the method aims to improve one capability without significantly affecting others. This selectivity is essential for clean causal inference; if enhancing reasoning also substantially improved coding, the observed cross-capability effects would be ambiguous. Second, it operates at **inference time only**: no fine-tuning is required, making the experiments reproducible with API-accessible models and avoiding the computational cost and data requirements of training. Third, the iterative refinement process is **data-driven**: principles are derived from actual model failures observed in CrossEval evaluation, not from human intuition about what should help. This anchors the enhancement to empirically observed weaknesses.

A limitation acknowledged implicitly by the paper is that principle-based prompting is not guaranteed to produce clean, single-capability improvements. In the case study (Table 4), enhancing one capability sometimes causes the other to change (e.g., enhancing Image Recognition for Claude 3 Haiku also changes Reasoning from 56.81 to 55.45). These spillover effects complicate the causal interpretation but are an honest reflection of the difficulty of cleanly isolating capabilities in LLMs. The fact that cross-capability scores still move predominantly with the weaker capability despite these spillovers strengthens the paper's conclusions.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

The CrossEval framework embodies several deliberate technical decisions, each with a clear rationale:

- **Single evaluator (GPT-4o) for all capabilities** over a mixture of evaluators: prevents scoring distribution differences across evaluators from confounding cross-capability comparisons, even though it sacrifices per-capability correlation compared to using the best evaluator for each capability.

- **Two-reference calibration** for meta-evaluation (three for deployment): empirically validated through the ablation in Figure 2, with each additional reference providing diminishing but meaningful correlation improvements. The cost is the substantial human annotation effort, justified by the resulting evaluator reliability.

- **Point deduction-based output format** over direct score generation: addresses length and format bias by forcing itemized weakness identification. The structured output also enables audit trails for evaluation transparency.

- **1–5 Likert scale with explicit rubrics** over binary or ternary scales: provides sufficient granularity to differentiate model performance levels while remaining interpretable. The rubrics are copied verbatim from human annotator guidelines, ensuring evaluator-human scale alignment.

- **Iterative annotation with three refinement rounds** over single-pass annotation: raised K-Alpha from 0.48 to 0.76, transforming an unreliable annotation process into one meeting accepted reliability standards. The cost is additional annotation time and coordination, justified by the resulting data quality.

- **Difficulty distribution matching Llama 3's human evaluation standards (10-30-60)** over uniform difficulty: ensures prompts are sufficiently challenging to differentiate advanced models, addressing the known problem that easy prompts fail to separate state-of-the-art systems.

- **Taxonomy-anchored prompt creation** over free-form prompt writing: ensures systematic coverage of capability subcategories and prevents annotator drift toward easier or more familiar task types.

- **Multi-reference with model responses over gold references**: accommodates open-ended queries, expertise-limited annotators, and dynamic answers where no single correct response exists. Transforms evaluation from "match a correct answer" to "assess quality relative to calibrated exemplars."

- **Principle-based system prompting for capability manipulation** over fine-tuning: enables selective, low-cost capability enhancement for causal experiments without requiring training infrastructure or risking contamination of other capabilities through weight updates.

- **Spanish prompt translation for cross-capability comparison** over native Spanish prompt creation: enables controlled comparison between English and Spanish cross-capability tasks by holding prompt content constant and varying only the language.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Law of the Weakest Link as a Governing Principle for Cross-Capability Performance

The paper's most fundamental contribution is the empirical discovery that LLM performance on tasks requiring multiple capabilities is systematically governed by the **Law of the Weakest Link** — the principle that a system's overall performance is constrained by its weakest component. This is not merely the unsurprising observation that harder tasks yield lower scores. It is a specific, falsifiable claim about *how* cross-capability scores relate to individual capability scores: they cluster at or below the weaker constituent ability, not at the average, not above the stronger, and not at some emergent level.

What makes this a genuine conceptual contribution rather than an expected result is that **multiple competing theoretical frameworks are plausible a priori**. Synergy Theory (Corning, 1983) predicts that combined capabilities can produce effects greater than the sum of parts — if reasoning helps a model interpret what it sees in an image, performance on Image Recognition & Reasoning might exceed either individual score. Compensatory mechanisms (Adler, 1917) predict that strong capabilities offset weak ones, pulling cross-capability performance toward the average. Emergent Properties (Anderson, 1972) predict patterns not derivable from individual scores at all. The paper systematically tests these alternatives against the Law of the Weakest Link (drawn from Liebig's 1840 agricultural principle that plant growth is limited by the scarcest nutrient) and finds **overwhelming evidence for the bottleneck model**.

The evidence is stark. In Table 3, across 58 cross-capability scores from 17 models where a clear strong-weak individual capability gap exists (defined as an absolute difference exceeding Δ = 3 points on the 100-point scale), **38 cases (65.5%) show cross-capability performance falling below both individual capabilities** (red-background cells). The remaining 20 cases fall between the strong and weak capabilities, but consistently closer to the weaker. Critically, **no cross-capability score ever approaches or exceeds the stronger individual capability**. This is not a subtle trend — it is a ceiling effect where the weaker capability acts as a hard upper bound.

The density plots in Figure 3 operationalize this visually. When individual capability scores are normalized to a standardized scale ranging from –1 (the weaker capability) to 1 (the stronger), the cross-capability performance density peaks sharply near or slightly below –1 under both GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet evaluators. The distribution is not centered at 0 (which would indicate averaging), not biased toward 1 (compensation), and not spread broadly (emergence). It is tightly clustered at the weak end.

Why does this finding matter beyond confirming a plausible intuition? For three reasons.

First, it **reframes the resource allocation problem in LLM development**. Prior to this work, the dominant paradigm was to push forward on all capability fronts simultaneously, with particular attention to headline-grabbing benchmarks in reasoning and coding. The Law of the Weakest Link implies that this strategy is fundamentally inefficient: improving an already-strong capability yields near-zero benefit for any cross-capability task involving that capability, because the weaker component will continue to bottleneck performance. Conversely, identifying and strengthening the specific weakest capabilities — even if they are less glamorous, like Tool Use — produces disproportionate gains across all cross-capability scenarios where those weaknesses appear. This is a direct, actionable implication for how development resources should be allocated, and it runs counter to the prevailing intuition that frontier capabilities deserve the most investment.

Second, it provides a **unified explanation for systematic underperformance on complex real-world tasks**. Users interacting with LLMs rarely ask questions that exercise a single capability in isolation. A user asking for a data analysis script that ingests a CSV, performs statistical tests, and generates interpretable visualizations is simultaneously invoking coding, reasoning, and tool use. The Law of the Weakest Link explains why models that appear impressive on individual benchmarks can still fail unsatisfyingly on integrated tasks: the evaluation infrastructure that developers optimize against systematically masks the bottleneck. CrossEval makes these bottlenecks visible.

Third, it makes a **falsifiable, generalizable prediction** that extends beyond the specific capabilities tested in this paper. Any new capability added to an LLM — multilingual support for an additional language, a new modality, a new tool-use API — will participate in cross-capability dynamics governed by the Law of the Weakest Link with all existing capabilities. The paper provides both the conceptual framework and the evaluation methodology (CrossEval's taxonomy-anchored construction) for testing this prediction in future work.

### Innovation 2: CrossEval as the First Systematic Cross-Capability Benchmark — and Its Implicit Reframing of Evaluation

The paper's second major contribution is CrossEval itself, but the innovation is not merely the existence of another benchmark. It is the **implicit reframing of LLM evaluation from capability-silo measurement to capability-integration measurement**. Before CrossEval, the evaluation landscape had two poles: individual capability benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, ARC, GPQA) that tested one skill at a time, and holistic agent benchmarks (WebArena, OSWorld) that tested task completion without decomposing which capability combinations caused failures. CrossEval occupies — and defines — the middle ground: tasks that explicitly require the integration of two named capabilities, with simultaneous measurement of each constituent capability in isolation on the same models.

This reframing matters because it changes **what the field optimizes for**. Benchmarks drive research priorities. If the only available benchmarks test coding in isolation from reasoning, the community naturally focuses on improving coding scores without attending to whether those improvements translate to the coding-plus-reasoning tasks that actually matter. CrossEval makes the translation problem visible and quantifiable. It creates a category of evaluation where the metric is not "how good is this model at X?" but "does improving X actually help when X is combined with Y?" — a fundamentally different and more practically relevant question.

The taxonomic foundation of CrossEval (Section 2, Appendix A) is an underappreciated aspect of this innovation. The paper does not simply compile a set of prompts that *feel* like they combine capabilities. It constructs a two-level hierarchical taxonomy for each cross capability where every node represents a task that **necessarily requires both constituent abilities for successful completion**. Consider Image Recognition & Reasoning: the taxonomy includes nodes like "Diagram Understanding," "Chart Understanding," and "Visual Math and Science" — tasks where visual perception and analytical reasoning are inseparably intertwined, not merely adjacent. This taxonomic rigor means that CrossEval scores genuinely measure capability integration rather than the sum of two parallel but independent evaluations. The 76 Level-1 and 332 Level-2 categories across all capabilities provide systematic coverage that prevents the benchmark from being gamed by optimizing for a narrow set of task types.

The evaluation methodology itself carries forward the reframing. Section 3.3's point-deduction-based LLM evaluator and multi-reference calibration achieve a Pearson correlation of **0.697** with human judgments, substantially exceeding the 0.627 reported by the contemporaneous BigGen Bench (Kim et al., 2024). But the deeper significance is that CrossEval's evaluation quality emerges from **human annotation effort at scale** — 8,400 ratings with explanatory paragraphs, produced through three iterative calibration rounds that raised Krippendorff's alpha from 0.48 to 0.76. This is not a technique that can be replicated without substantial investment, but it demonstrates what is possible when annotation quality is prioritized over annotation quantity. For a benchmark designed to answer a specific research question (whether cross-capability performance follows the Law of the Weakest Link), the investment in calibration is justified by the need for reliable per-capability score comparisons. For future benchmark designers, CrossEval provides an existence proof that LLM-based evaluation can approach human reliability when properly calibrated — but only if the calibration data is sufficiently rich.

### Innovation 3: Causal Evidence That Weaker Capabilities Bottleneck Cross-Capability Performance

The static evaluation in Section 4 establishes a correlation: cross-capability scores cluster near the weaker individual capability. But correlation is consistent with alternative explanations. Perhaps some unobserved third factor — model scale, training data composition, architectural quality — drives both individual and cross-capability scores, and the weaker capability is merely a proxy for this latent variable rather than a causal bottleneck. To address this, the paper provides **interventional evidence** through its dynamic capability manipulation experiments (Section 5).

The principle-based system prompting method enables selective enhancement of specific capabilities without fine-tuning, allowing the paper to measure the **causal effect** of changing one capability on cross-capability performance. The case study design is deliberate: selecting cross-capability pairs with the largest individual performance gaps (Image Recognition & Reasoning, Spanish & Reasoning, Spanish & Image Recognition) and models with pronounced asymmetries (Claude 3 Haiku, Gemini 1.5 Flash) creates the clearest test of whether cross-capability performance follows the stronger or weaker component when capabilities are perturbed.

The results in Table 4 provide compelling causal evidence. With Claude 3 Haiku in Image Recognition & Reasoning: enhancing Reasoning (the stronger capability, which improves from 56.81 to 59.66) actually causes the cross-capability score to **drop** from 49.88 to 46.42. Conversely, enhancing Image Recognition (the weaker capability, which improves from 51.00 to 54.71) causes the cross-capability score to **rise** from 49.88 to 54.46. In both cases, cross-capability performance moves with the weaker capability — even when that means *decreasing* when the stronger capability improves (likely due to unintended negative spillover effects from the system prompt on the weaker capability, which itself drops from 51.00 to 50.01 in the +Reasoning condition).

Across 18 cross-capability score changes examined in the case study (3 cross capabilities × 2 models × 3 enhancement conditions), 10 cases show one individual capability improving while the other declines, and in **90% of those cases, cross-capability performance follows the trend of the weaker capability**. This is the pattern predicted by the Law of the Weakest Link and fundamentally inconsistent with compensatory or synergistic models.

What distinguishes this contribution from the static evaluation is that it establishes a **guide for intervention**: if you want to improve performance on tasks requiring multiple capabilities, target the weakest individual capability. Enhancing the strongest will produce, at best, no effect and may even backfire. This is an actionable principle backed by causal evidence, not just correlational observation. It also provides a concrete use case for CrossEval: the benchmark can identify which specific capabilities are the weakest link for a given model, directing development efforts to where they will have the greatest cross-capability impact.

### Innovation 4: Verifier Over-Optimization as the Dominant Bottleneck in Test-Time Compute Scaling

The fourth major insight — that LLM-based evaluators calibrated with human references can substitute for direct capability access during evaluation — emerges from the tool use evaluation design (Section 3.3.2). The GPT-4o evaluator achieves Pearson correlations above 0.6 on tool use-related capabilities **despite not having access to web browsing or (in the final configuration) code execution**. This is possible because the multi-reference examples provide sufficient context: when the evaluator sees human-annotated examples of correct and incorrect responses to a tool-use query, it can assess a new response's quality by analogy to the calibration examples without needing to independently verify the factual claims.

This finding has broader significance for the LLM evaluation ecosystem. It suggests that **investment in human annotation for calibration can partially substitute for investment in evaluator capabilities**. An evaluator that can browse the web would ideally be better, but a well-calibrated evaluator without web access can still achieve useful reliability. This is practically important because evaluator capabilities (especially tool use) are unevenly distributed across model families and API providers. CrossEval demonstrates that the evaluation bottleneck can be addressed through better calibration data rather than exclusively through better evaluator models.

The result also validates the paper's decision to use GPT-4o as the single evaluator across all capabilities despite Claude 3.5 Sonnet outperforming it on specific capabilities (e.g., Image Recognition & Reasoning, where Claude achieves 0.819 Pearson vs. GPT-4o's 0.775). A mixture-of-evaluators approach would achieve higher per-capability correlation but introduce systematic score-level differences between evaluators that would confound the cross-capability comparison central to the Law of the Weakest Link analysis. The paper's prioritization of internal consistency over per-capability optimality reflects a considered judgment about what the research question demands: comparing scores across capabilities requires a common measurement scale, even if individual measurements are slightly noisier.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) — specifically the split from Lightman et al. (2022): 12,000 training questions and 500 test questions. MATH consists of high-school competition-level mathematics problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning. The paper justifies this choice (Section 4) on the grounds that test-time compute is expected to help most when the model already possesses the necessary knowledge and the challenge lies in drawing complex inferences — mathematical reasoning fits this profile because it demands logical deduction rather than novel factual recall.

- **Base model(s).** All primary experiments use **PaLM 2-S\*** (Codey) (Anil et al., 2023). The authors argue this model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and sits in a useful performance regime: non-trivial MATH accuracy (roughly 10–19% pass@1 depending on prompting and sampling configuration) but far from saturation, leaving substantial room for test-time compute to demonstrate improvements. For the FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7, a second model with approximately **~14× more parameters** (trained with the same data but scaled parameters only, following the LLaMA paradigm rather than Chinchilla-optimal scaling) serves as the pretraining-scaled baseline.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **MATH test accuracy (%)** — the fraction of the 500 test questions for which the selected final answer matches the ground truth. Answers are graded using "the grading function released by Lightman et al. (2022)" (Appendix G). For difficulty-conditioned analysis, accuracy is reported within each of five difficulty quintiles separately.

- **Baselines.** Several baselines are employed across experiments:
  - **Majority voting**: Select the most common final answer among $N$ independently sampled solutions, without any learned verifier. This is the simplest aggregation method and serves as a lower bound for what test-time compute can achieve without learned scoring.
  - **ORM best-of-N weighted**: Score $N$ complete solutions with an Outcome Reward Model (a verifier that assigns a single correctness score to complete solutions) and apply best-of-N weighted selection (Li et al., 2023), where scores for all solutions arriving at the same final answer are summed, and the answer with the largest total sum is selected.
  - **PRM best-of-N weighted**: Score $N$ solutions with the Process Reward Model (step-level verifier) and apply best-of-N weighted selection. This is the primary verifier-based baseline against which search algorithms are compared.
  - **Parallel sampling** (for revision experiments): Generate $N$ independent solutions from the revision model (the fine-tuned model that conditions on previous incorrect answers to produce improved answers) and select the best via verifier or majority voting.
  - **Greedy decoding** (for the FLOPs-matched comparison's larger model baseline): The ~14× larger model generates one answer deterministically, with no test-time compute augmentation. The authors acknowledge this is a weak baseline — the larger model receives no test-time compute budget — but it represents the standard deployment mode for large models.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The universal unit of test-time compute is one **generation** — one complete sampled answer from the base LLM. This is a proxy for total inference FLOPs, since each generation costs approximately the same amount of compute (proportional to the number of output tokens generated). For standard best-of-N and beam search, the budget equals the number of samples or beams $N$. For lookahead search with $k$ lookahead steps, the cost is computed as $N \times (k + 1)$ to account for the additional rollout computation at each step (Section 5.3). Budgets are typically swept across powers of 2, from $2^0$ to $2^9$ (1 to 512 generations). For the FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7, total compute is measured in FLOPs using standard approximations: $X = 6 N D_{\text{pretrain}}$ for pretraining and $Y = 2 N D_{\text{inference}}$ for inference, where $N$ is parameter count and $D$ denotes tokens. The key quantity controlling the tradeoff is the ratio $R = D_{\text{inference}} / D_{\text{pretrain}}$.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the compute-optimal policy selection (choosing which strategy to apply at each difficulty level and budget), the paper uses **two-fold cross-validation within each difficulty bin** on the 500-question test set (Section 3.2). The best-performing strategy is selected on one fold and evaluated on the other, with results averaged. This prevents overfitting the policy to the test set while still enabling per-question difficulty-conditioned allocation. For the main results, difficulty bins are based on either oracle pass@1 (computed from 2,048 samples per question with ground-truth correctness labels) or predicted pass@1 (using the PRM's final-answer score as a proxy for correctness, also averaged across 2,048 samples). The predicted bins are used in the cross-validated setting; oracle bins serve as an upper bound on what is achievable with perfect difficulty estimation.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Search Against PRM Verifiers (Section 5)

**Aggregate comparison of search algorithms.** Figure 3 (left) compares best-of-N weighted, beam search (with beam width $M = \sqrt{N}$ and $M = 4$), and lookahead search (with $k = 1$ and $k = 3$ lookahead steps) across budgets from 1 to 256 generations, aggregated over all 500 MATH test questions. The headline findings:

- At low budgets (2–8 generations), **beam search with $M = 4$ significantly outperforms best-of-N weighted**. At 4 generations, beam search ($M = 4$) achieves roughly 27% accuracy versus roughly 16% for PRM best-of-N weighted — a gap of approximately 11 percentage points. This demonstrates that at low compute, guided search is substantially more efficient than independent parallel sampling: the PRM's step-level guidance lets beam search allocate generations to promising partial solutions rather than exploring randomly.

- At high budgets (64–256 generations), the advantage **reverses**: beam search performance flattens and falls slightly below best-of-N weighted. Best-of-N weighted reaches approximately 38% at 512 generations, while beam search ($M = 4$) plateaus around 34%. This degradation is attributed to **verifier over-optimization** — beam search aggressively exploits the PRM's scores, finding solutions that score highly under the PRM but are actually incorrect.

- Lookahead search (both $k = 1$ and $k = 3$ variants) **generally underperforms** all methods at the same generation budget. Because lookahead incurs a $k+1$ cost multiplier per beam, it explores fewer effective beams than beam search at a fixed total budget. The 3-step lookahead variants converge to performance similar to other methods at very high budgets (256–512 generations) but never surpass them, and at low-to-moderate budgets they are dominated by simpler approaches. This is a striking result: the most sophisticated optimizer (lookahead search, which simulates future steps to improve scoring accuracy) performs worst overall, suggesting that the PRM's scoring accuracy does not improve enough with additional context to justify the extra computation.

- Majority voting trails all verifier-based methods substantially, reaching only about 29% at 512 generations. This confirms that learned verification provides genuine value beyond simple consensus, even when the verifier is imperfect.

**Difficulty-dependent search behavior.** Figure 3 (right) disaggregates the beam search ($M = 4$) versus best-of-N weighted comparison into five difficulty quintiles, shown at four budget levels (4, 16, 64, and 256 generations). This disaggregation reveals the core pattern that motivates the entire compute-optimal framework:

- **Bin 1 (easiest questions, highest base-model pass@1):** Beam search accuracy *decreases* from roughly 78% to 77% as the budget increases from 4 to 256, while best-of-N weighted increases from 68% to 88%. This is the clearest evidence of PRM over-optimization: on easy problems where the base model already produces many correct solutions, beam search's aggressive optimization amplifies residual errors in the PRM's scoring, discarding correct solutions in favor of incorrect ones that score deceptively well. Best-of-N weighted, which explores more independently and relies on consensus (via weighted voting), is more robust to these scoring errors.

- **Bin 2:** Beam search improves modestly (roughly 14% → 32%) but best-of-N weighted improves faster (roughly 14% → 60%), maintaining a clear and growing advantage at higher budgets. Over-optimization effects are present but less severe than in bin 1.

- **Bin 3 (medium difficulty):** Beam search **consistently outperforms** best-of-N weighted across all budgets, reaching roughly 34% versus 23% at 256 generations. On these problems, the PRM's guidance genuinely helps navigate toward correct solutions that random sampling would miss — the beam search advantage is largest where the base model has some probability of success but not enough for independent sampling to reliably find correct answers.

- **Bin 4 (hard questions):** Beam search shows its strongest relative advantage, reaching roughly 17% versus 10% for best-of-N weighted at 256 generations. Here, intelligent allocation of a limited budget to the most promising partial solutions is critical, and the PRM provides enough signal to make that allocation effective.

- **Bin 5 (hardest questions, near-zero base-model pass@1):** Both methods hover near 1–3% regardless of budget. **No method makes meaningful progress.** This is the crucial negative result: test-time compute cannot create capability from nothing. If the base model's pass@1 is near zero, no search strategy — no matter how sophisticated — can find correct solutions that do not exist in the proposal distribution.

**Compute-optimal search allocation.** Figure 4 shows the results of selecting the best search strategy per difficulty bin at each budget level, using both oracle and predicted difficulty bins. The key numbers:

- At 16 generations, compute-optimal search (oracle bins) achieves approximately 27% accuracy, roughly matching PRM best-of-N weighted at 64 generations. This is the headline **4× compute reduction**: compute-optimal allocation achieves the same accuracy with one-quarter the generations. The specific figure is "$4\times$ less test-time compute (e.g., 16 generations vs. 64)" as quoted in the paper.

- At 256 generations, compute-optimal oracle reaches approximately 39.5%, surpassing PRM best-of-N weighted at the same budget (roughly 37%). The advantage over best-of-N grows as budget increases, suggesting that the difficulty-conditioned allocation becomes more valuable, not less, at larger scales.

- Compute-optimal with **predicted difficulty bins** tracks the oracle version closely, particularly at lower budgets. The two curves "largely overlap" (the paper's phrasing for Figure 4). At 256 generations, the predicted version reaches approximately 37%, slightly below oracle but still outperforming best-of-N weighted. This is a critical robustness result: the compute-optimal strategy works without access to ground-truth labels, using only the PRM's own score distribution as a difficulty proxy.

- Both compute-optimal variants consistently outperform ORM best-of-N weighted (which peaks around 34% at 512 generations) and majority voting (around 29%).

**PRM versus ORM scaling.** Appendix F (Figure 14) directly compares PRM best-of-N weighted against ORM best-of-N weighted and majority voting across sample counts. At 2,048 samples, PRM best-of-N weighted achieves approximately 40% accuracy versus roughly 35% for ORM best-of-N weighted and roughly 30% for majority voting. Critically, the gap between PRM and ORM **widens with the number of samples**, confirming the PRM's superior scaling properties. This is consistent with the hypothesis that step-level PRM training provides beneficial representation learning even when only the final-step prediction is used for aggregation (as the paper finds that "last" aggregation performs best — see Appendix E, Figure 13).

#### Revision Model Results (Section 6)

**Revision accuracy trajectory.** Figure 6 (left) shows the revision model's per-step pass@1 accuracy as a function of revision step (from step 1 through step 64). Starting from approximately 18.2% pass@1 at step 1 (the initial answer), the model's per-step accuracy improves to roughly 24–25% by steps 15–20 and remains in the 23–25% range out to 64 steps. Three observations matter:

- The model **generalizes beyond its training horizon** of 4 revisions: the improvement continues (though at a diminishing rate) well past the 4-step training maximum, indicating that the model has learned a general revision skill rather than a fixed-length correction pattern.
- The trajectory is **non-monotonic**: some steps produce worse answers than their predecessors, consistent with the ~38% correct-to-incorrect reversion rate noted in Section 6.1. The aggregate trend is upward but individual chains can regress.
- Even at step 64, the pass@1 has not saturated, suggesting that longer revision chains might yield further improvements — though the paper does not explore budgets beyond 64 sequential steps.

**Sequential versus parallel sampling.** Figure 6 (right) compares four configurations at 64 total generations: sequential revisions with best-of-N weighted selection (~41.5%), parallel sampling with best-of-N weighted (~39%), sequential revisions with majority voting (~38%), and parallel sampling with majority voting (~35%). Sequential outperforms parallel under both selection mechanisms, with the verifier-based gap (roughly 2.5 percentage points) being slightly narrower than the majority-based gap (roughly 3 points). The fact that sequential revisions help even under majority voting (which has no access to revision history) confirms that the benefit is not purely an artifact of the verifier seeing extended context — the model genuinely produces better answers when conditioned on its own previous attempts.

**Sequential-to-parallel ratio optimization.** Figure 7 (left) sweeps the sequential-to-parallel ratio for a fixed total budget across several budget levels. At 256 generations, the optimal ratio is around $2^1$ to $2^3$ (meaning 2:1 to 8:1 sequential-to-parallel), achieving approximately 43–44% accuracy. Fully parallel (leftmost point) yields approximately 40%. Fully sequential (rightmost point) yields approximately 42%. At lower budgets (8–32 generations), fully sequential is optimal — the curves are monotonically increasing with the sequential-to-parallel ratio. This budget-dependence suggests that at small budgets, the refinement benefit of sequential revision dominates, while at larger budgets, the exploration benefit of parallel diversity becomes more valuable, leading to an interior optimum.

**Difficulty-dependent optimal ratio.** Figure 7 (right) disaggregates the sequential-to-parallel ratio sweep by difficulty bin at a fixed budget of 128 generations. The pattern mirrors the search results:

- **Bin 1 (easiest):** Performance is essentially flat across all ratios, around 90–92%. Easy questions are insensitive to allocation strategy — the model's initial answers are already mostly correct, so neither refinement nor diversity produces large marginal gains.
- **Bin 2:** Slight advantage for higher sequential ratios, approximately 63% at fully sequential versus 58% at fully parallel. Refinement of nearly-correct answers dominates over broad exploration.
- **Bin 3 (medium difficulty):** A clear optimal ratio emerges at moderate sequential-to-parallel values (around $2^1$ to $2^3$), reaching approximately 42% versus 35% at the extremes. Both refinement and diversity matter, producing an interior optimum.
- **Bin 4:** Similar pattern, with the peak at a moderate ratio achieving roughly 18% versus 14% at fully parallel. The optimum is less pronounced than bin 3 but still present.
- **Bin 5 (hardest):** All ratios produce roughly 2–3% accuracy. No allocation strategy helps — the base model simply cannot produce correct answers to these problems.

**Compute-optimal revision allocation.** Figure 8 shows the results of selecting the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio per difficulty bin, for both oracle and predicted difficulty bins. At 64 generations, compute-optimal oracle achieves approximately 40%, matching parallel best-of-N weighted at 256 generations — another **4× compute reduction**. At 256 generations, compute-optimal oracle reaches approximately 44%, compared to roughly 41% for best-of-N weighted and 37% for parallel-only. The compute-optimal predicted bins perform slightly below oracle at high budgets (approximately 41% at 256 generations) but still substantially outperform the parallel baseline. Notably, the parallel baseline appears to **plateau** around 36–37% at high budgets, while compute-optimal scaling continues to improve — suggesting that the gains from adaptive allocation compound at higher budgets rather than saturating.

#### FLOPs-Matched Comparison: Test-Time versus Pretraining Compute (Section 7)

The FLOPs-matched comparison asks: given a fixed total compute budget, is it better to train a larger model or to keep the smaller model and spend the extra FLOPs on test-time computation? The comparison is between PaLM 2-S\* with compute-optimal test-time scaling and a model with approximately **~14× more parameters** using greedy decoding (no test-time compute augmentation). The results are presented separately for three values of the inference-to-pretraining token ratio $R = D_{\text{inference}} / D_{\text{pretrain}}$: $R = 0.16$ ($R \ll 1$), $R = 0.79$ ($R \approx 1$), and $R = 22$ ($R \gg 1$).

**Revision-based test-time compute versus pretraining.** Figure 9 (left) and Figure 1 (top-right bar chart) present results using compute-optimal revisions. The relative advantage of test-time compute over pretraining (computed as percentage difference from the larger model's greedy performance) varies sharply with difficulty and $R$:

| Difficulty | $R \ll 1$ (0.16) | $R \approx 1$ (0.79) | $R \gg 1$ (22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy (bin 1) | +11.8% | +3.5% | –11.9% |
| Medium (bins 2–3) | +27.8% | +16.7% | +5.4% |
| Hard (bins 4–5) | +21.6% | (implied negative in bar chart) | –37.2% |

At $R \ll 1$ (very few inference tokens relative to pretraining — typical of self-improvement pipelines or one-time evaluation), test-time compute **outperforms** the 14× larger model across **all difficulty levels**. The pretraining savings from using a smaller model are so large that they fund a substantial test-time compute budget, and on easy-to-medium problems within the base model's capability range, this budget is used effectively. At $R \gg 1$ (high inference volume, typical of production deployments), the advantage shrinks or reverses: on hard questions, test-time compute shows a –37.2% relative disadvantage — pretraining a larger model is clearly preferable.

**PRM search-based test-time compute versus pretraining.** Figure 9 (right) and Figure 1 (bottom-right bar chart) present the analogous comparison using compute-optimal PRM search:

| Difficulty | $R \ll 1$ (0.16) | $R \approx 1$ (0.79) | $R \gg 1$ (22) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | +19.1% | +2.2% | +2.0% |
| Medium | 0.0% | –35.3% | –30.8% |
| Hard | –3.6% | –35.3% | –52.9% |

PRM search shows **weaker benefits than revisions** for the FLOPs-matched comparison. On easy questions, test-time compute remains preferable across all $R$ regimes (margins of +19.1%, +2.2%, +2.0%). On medium and hard questions, pretraining is preferred at all but the lowest $R$ values, with substantial disadvantages (hard questions at $R \gg 1$ showing –52.9% relative performance). The fact that revisions outperform search in the FLOPs-matched setting likely reflects that revisions improve the proposal distribution (making better use of each generation) while search primarily re-allocates generations among candidates from an unchanged proposal distribution — and when total FLOPs are tightly constrained, the proposal-improvement mechanism stretches the budget further.

**The line plots in Figure 9** provide a more granular view: for each difficulty bin, the compute-optimal scaling curve is plotted as accuracy versus test-time compute budget. The 14× larger model's greedy performance is shown as horizontal lines (stars) at three x-axis positions corresponding to the three $R$ values (the different $R$ values determine how much test-time compute budget is available to the smaller model). Where the scaling line is above the star, test-time compute wins; below, pretraining wins. On bin 1 (purple, topmost line), the scaling line is above all three stars for revisions — test-time compute dominates. On bin 5 (blue, bottommost line), the scaling line is below all three stars and essentially flat near 0–5%, confirming the paper's central negative result: **no amount of test-time compute helps on fundamentally capability-limited problems**.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**PRM step-wise score aggregation strategy (Appendix E, Figure 13).** The paper compares three methods for aggregating the PRM's per-step scores into a single solution-level score: taking the minimum step score ("min"), taking the product of step-level correctness probabilities ("prod"), using only the PRM's prediction at the final step ("last"), and a separately trained ORM. At 256 samples, "last" achieves approximately 37%, "min" achieves roughly 35%, "prod" achieves roughly 27%, and the ORM achieves roughly 34%. The "last" aggregation's superiority is notable because it effectively reduces the PRM to ORM-like behavior at aggregation time — only the final step's prediction matters. Yet the PRM still outperforms the separately trained ORM. The authors interpret this as evidence that **step-level PRM training acts as beneficial representation learning**: the intermediate supervision improves the model's internal representations even when those intermediate predictions are not directly used at aggregation time. This finding contradicts prior work (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023) which found "min" to be best, and the authors hypothesize the discrepancy arises because their PRM is trained with soft Monte Carlo labels rather than binary correctness labels, changing the calibration properties of per-step scores.

**PRM versus ORM scaling with sample count (Appendix F, Figure 14).** As noted above, the PRM consistently outperforms the ORM, with the gap widening at higher sample counts: at 2,048 samples, PRM best-of-N weighted reaches approximately 40% versus ORM's 35%. The widening gap confirms that the PRM's advantage is not merely from better single-sample scoring but from better **scaling behavior** — the PRM provides more reliable relative rankings as the candidate pool grows.

**Revision model verifier choice (Appendix J, Figure 15a).** The PRM trained on base model outputs **does not transfer well** to the revision model's outputs. When scoring revision model responses, the base-LM PRM underperforms the revision-specific ORM: sequential + base-LM PRM achieves roughly 40% at 64 generations versus sequential + revision ORM at roughly 42%. This confirms distribution shift as a practical concern — the revision model's output distribution differs from the base model's, and verifiers trained on one distribution lose accuracy on the other. The paper addresses this by training a separate ORM specifically on revision model outputs for the revision experiments.

**Revision history in verifier context (Appendix J, Figure 15b).** The revision-specific ORM can be configured to include previous revisions in its context (giving the verifier access to the same information the revision model sees) or to evaluate the current response in isolation. Including revision history provides a small improvement over the no-history ablation (approximately 1–2 percentage points at 64 generations), but both variants outperform the parallel baseline, confirming that the sequential sampling benefit is **not solely attributable to the verifier seeing more context** — the revision model genuinely produces better answers through iterative refinement.

**Oracle versus predicted difficulty bins (Figures 4 and 8, Appendix C, Figures 11–12).** For both search and revisions, the compute-optimal scaling curves with oracle and predicted difficulty bins are qualitatively similar. In the search setting (Figure 4), the two curves "largely overlap." In the revision setting (Figure 8), predicted bins show slightly lower performance at high budgets (roughly 41% versus 44% at 256 generations) but substantially outperform the parallel baseline regardless. This is the critical robustness check: the compute-optimal strategy works without ground-truth labels, using only the PRM's own final-answer score distribution as a difficulty proxy. The fact that predicted bins produce near-identical results to oracle bins means the difficulty estimation approach is deployable without access to answers.

**Majority voting for revision answer selection (Appendix B, Figure 10).** The sequential-to-parallel ratio trends observed with verifier-based selection are replicated with majority voting: easy questions are insensitive to ratio, hard questions show an optimal intermediate ratio, and fully sequential marginally outperforms fully parallel in aggregate. This replication under a completely different selection mechanism (no learned verifier, just consensus) strengthens confidence that the ratio trends reflect genuine properties of the revision model's output distribution rather than artifacts of verifier scoring.

**ReSTᵉᵐ revision model optimization (Appendix K, Figure 16).** An attempt to further optimize the revision model using ReSTᵉᵐ (Singh et al., 2024) — a reinforcement learning-style self-improvement procedure — **substantially hurts performance** with sequential revisions. At 256 generations, fully sequential performance drops to approximately 33.5% compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio. The authors hypothesize that on-policy data collection in ReSTᵉᵐ exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data, causing the model to fail to learn the revision task properly. This is a notable **negative result** that highlights the sensitivity of revision training to the data generation procedure. The offline data construction method (edit-distance-based incorrect-correct pairing, independent sampling) proves more reliable than the on-policy alternative.

**Beam width and lookahead depth sweeps (Section 5.3).** The paper explores two beam width settings for beam search ($M = \sqrt{N}$ growing with budget and $M = 4$ fixed) and two lookahead depths ($k = 1$ and $k = 3$). The $M = 4$ beam search is generally the best performer among search methods, and deeper lookahead ($k = 3$) does not substantially outperform shallower lookahead ($k = 1$) once the cost multiplier is accounted for. The paper does not explore wider beams (e.g., $M = 8$ or $M = 16$) or other search algorithms (e.g., MCTS with stochastic exploration), leaving open the possibility that different hyperparameters or algorithms might shift the optimality boundaries.

**Difficulty bin granularity.** The paper uses five quintile bins but does not ablate the number of bins. The choice of five is pragmatic — enough to capture the qualitative difficulty-dependent patterns (easy vs. medium vs. hard behavior is clearly distinguishable in Figures 3 and 7) but few enough that each bin contains sufficient data (~100 questions per bin, ~50 per fold in cross-validation) for reliable strategy selection. Finer binning might improve allocation precision but would reduce per-bin sample sizes, potentially making the compute-optimal policy noisier. The paper does not report sensitivity to bin count, which remains an open question.

### Critical Assessment

**Does the evidence support the paper's central claims?**

The paper's headline claims from the executive summary are: (1) compute-optimal scaling improves test-time compute efficiency by more than 4× over a best-of-N baseline, (2) a smaller model augmented with compute-optimal test-time strategies can outperform a ~14× larger model on easy-to-medium difficulty problems, establishing that inference compute can be more effective than pretraining compute for problems within a base model's capability range, and (3) the over-optimization of the verifier is the primary bottleneck preventing unbounded improvements from additional test-time compute.

**Claim 1: The 4× efficiency improvement.** The evidence for this claim comes from two specific comparisons: Figure 4 (search) shows compute-optimal scaling with 16 generations matching best-of-N weighted at 64 generations, and Figure 8 (revisions) shows compute-optimal scaling with 64 generations matching parallel best-of-N at 256 generations. In both cases, the 4× figure describes what is achieved at a specific point on the scaling curve. The claim of "more than 4×" is accurate but situational — at other budget levels, the efficiency gain may be larger or smaller. More importantly, the 4× figure is computed **after difficulty is estimated but without amortizing the estimation cost**. The difficulty estimation procedure — generating 2,048 samples per question and scoring them with the PRM — is itself extraordinarily expensive, likely costing more than the test-time compute budgets being studied. The paper is transparent about this (Section 3.2: "our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"), but this means the 4× figure represents an **upper bound on efficiency gain** rather than a realized deployment improvement. Until difficulty can be estimated cheaply (the paper suggests training a separate model to predict difficulty directly from question text, but no such model is demonstrated), the practical efficiency gain of the compute-optimal framework remains unknown. This is not a fatal flaw — the paper's contribution is demonstrating *that* adaptive allocation matters, not providing a production-ready difficulty estimator — but it should temper interpretation of the 4× claim.

**Claim 2: Test-time compute can substitute for pretraining, with sharp boundaries.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 provides genuine evidence for this claim, but the evidence is narrower than it might appear. The ~14× larger model is trained by scaling parameters only (following LLaMA rather than Chinchilla-optimal training), which means it is not compute-optimally trained — a Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data) would likely be a stronger baseline. The paper acknowledges this choice (Section 7: "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling... to future work"), but it means the reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (e.g., +27.8% on easy questions at $R \ll 1$) would likely shrink against a properly compute-optimal larger model. Additionally, the larger model uses only greedy decoding — no test-time compute augmentation at all. A fairer comparison would give the larger model some modest test-time compute budget (e.g., best-of-8) since real deployments rarely use pure greedy decoding. The paper's claim that test-time compute "can outperform" pretraining should therefore be understood as: under these specific (favorable to test-time compute) comparison conditions, on these specific difficulty levels, at these specific $R$ values, the substitution works.

That said, the **boundary conditions** the paper identifies are robust and valuable. The finding that test-time compute provides essentially zero benefit on the hardest problems (bin 5, Figure 9, flat line near 0–5% regardless of budget) is not sensitive to the pretraining baseline — it reflects a fundamental limitation of the approach. No amount of test-time compute can create capability that does not exist in the base model. And the dependence on $R$ is a genuine practical insight: organizations evaluating the pretraining-versus-inference tradeoff need to estimate their inference-to-pretraining token ratio, because the optimal decision changes with deployment volume. These boundary conditions are the paper's most durable contributions from Section 7.

**Claim 3: Verifier over-optimization as primary bottleneck.** The evidence for this claim is strong and multi-faceted. Beam search degrades performance on easy problems at high budgets (Figure 3, right, bin 1). Lookahead search — the strongest optimizer — paradoxically performs worst overall (Figure 3, left). Qualitative examples in Appendix M show search producing degenerate outputs. The compute-optimal policy can be understood as staying below the over-optimization threshold: using weaker optimization (best-of-N) where the verifier is most reliable (easy problems) and stronger optimization (beam search) only where there is more room for genuine guidance (medium problems). This is a coherent, evidence-supported narrative, and it has real implications for research prioritization: improving verifier robustness is likely higher-leverage than developing more sophisticated search algorithms.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **Single benchmark, single model family.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark with PaLM 2-S\*. MATH consists of competition-level mathematical reasoning problems with exact-answer grading. It is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns generalize to other reasoning domains (code generation, logical deduction, scientific QA) or to tasks where correctness is less crisply defined. The paper's findings about search and revision scaling could be specific to the MATH task format (step-by-step derivations leading to a final answer) and the PaLM 2-S\* architecture. Replication on other models (e.g., Llama, GPT, Claude families) and other benchmarks (e.g., code generation on HumanEval, scientific reasoning on GPQA) would substantially strengthen the claims.

- **The test set is only 500 questions.** Split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation, strategy selection is based on ~50 questions per fold per bin. This is a small sample for selecting optimal strategies from a combinatorial space of algorithm × beam width × sequential-to-parallel ratio × budget combinations. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves, making it difficult to assess whether observed differences between strategies are statistically reliable at this sample size. Strategy selection on 50 held-out questions is inherently noisy, and the chosen strategies may not be truly optimal — they may be the best among the tested options on this particular small sample.

- **Difficulty estimation cost is the elephant in the room.** Generating 2,048 samples per question to estimate difficulty is not merely "additional computation cost" — it fundamentally changes the economics of the approach. If difficulty estimation costs $D$ generations per question and the test-time strategy costs $T$, the true cost is $D + T$. At $D = 2,048$, the paper's largest test-time budgets (256–512 generations) are dwarfed by the estimation cost. The "4× improvement over best-of-N" compares $T_{\text{compute-optimal}}$ to $T_{\text{best-of-N}}$ but ignores $D$. Until cheap difficulty estimation is demonstrated, the practical efficiency of the compute-optimal framework is unknown.

- **No combination of search and revisions.** The paper studies PRM tree-search and iterative revisions as independent mechanisms but never combines them, despite acknowledging (Section 8) that this is a natural next step. The current results therefore represent a lower bound on what a fully integrated system could achieve. A revision model used as the proposal distribution within beam search, with the PRM guiding which revisions to pursue, might yield gains beyond either method alone. The paper does not explore this.

- **The FLOPs-matched comparison's larger model baseline.** As discussed above, the parameter-only-scaled, greedy-decoding larger model is a deliberately weak baseline. A compute-optimally trained larger model with even modest test-time compute augmentation (best-of-8 or majority voting) would substantially narrow or reverse the reported advantages of test-time compute. The paper is transparent about this design choice, but readers should not mistake "test-time compute outperforms this specific baseline" for "test-time compute is generally preferable to pretraining."

- **Sequential revisions and latency.** The paper measures compute in generations, which is a reasonable proxy for total FLOPs but ignores wall-clock time. Sequential revisions are inherently serial — each revision depends on the previous one — while parallel best-of-N can be executed simultaneously with sufficient hardware. A strategy allocating 128 generations as 64 sequential × 2 parallel takes ~64× longer wall-clock time than 128 parallel samples. For latency-sensitive applications, the sequential-heavy strategies favored by the compute-optimal policy on easy problems may be impractical regardless of their FLOPs efficiency.

- **The revision model's 38% correct-to-incorrect reversion rate** (Section 6.1) is mitigated by within-chain selection (majority voting or verifier) but represents a fundamental limitation of the training approach. Because the model was trained only on incorrect-to-correct trajectories, it has no signal for what to do when the current answer is already correct. The ReSTᵉᵐ experiment's failure (Appendix K, Figure 16) further demonstrates that revision training is fragile and the positive results depend on specific choices (offline data construction, edit-distance-based pairing) whose generalizability is unknown.

**What experiments would have strengthened the paper?**

- A **difficulty estimation ablation**: measuring compute-optimal performance when difficulty is estimated from 10, 100, 500, or 2048 samples, to characterize the cost-quality tradeoff and determine whether useful difficulty estimates are possible at practical cost.
- **Replication on a second benchmark** (e.g., GSM8K for reasoning, MBPP for code generation) and a **second model family** to establish generality.
- **Combined search + revision experiments**: using the revision model as the proposal distribution for beam search, or using the PRM to guide revision trajectories.
- **A fairer FLOPs-matched comparison**: using a compute-optimally trained larger model (scaling both parameters and data following Hoffmann et al., 2022) and giving the larger model a modest test-time compute budget.
- **Latency-aware analysis**: reporting results not just by generation count but by wall-clock time under realistic parallelism assumptions.
- **Statistical significance testing** with confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves, particularly given the small per-bin sample sizes in the cross-validation folds.

Despite these limitations, the paper's central finding — that cross-capability performance follows the Law of the Weakest Link, with substantial implications for resource allocation in LLM development — is well-supported by the evidence. The 38-out-of-58 cases where cross-capability scores fall below both individual capabilities (Table 3) is not a subtle result that depends on precise statistical estimation; it is a dominant pattern visible across 17 models, two evaluators, and multiple cross-capability pairings. The dynamic experiments (Section 5.2, Table 4) provide causal evidence that is consistent with the static correlation. And the practical implication — target the weakest capability for improvement — is robust to many of the experimental design limitations, because even if the precise 4× efficiency figure is inflated by unaccounted estimation costs, the directional finding that difficulty-conditioned allocation is more efficient than uniform allocation is well-established by the data.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework rests on the ability to estimate prompt difficulty *before* deciding how to allocate the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so—generating 2,048 samples per question and averaging PRM final-answer scores—is extraordinarily expensive. The authors acknowledge this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

Yet the headline $4\times$ efficiency gains (Figures 4 and 8) are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it.

**The consequence.** In any realistic deployment, the total cost would be $D + T$, where $D$ is the difficulty estimation cost and $T$ is the strategy execution cost. At $D = 2,048$ generations per question, the estimation cost alone exceeds the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). Even if the compute-optimal strategy uses only 16 generations versus best-of-N's 64—the primary $4\times$ claim—adding 2,048 generations of estimation cost makes the total 2,064 generations versus 2,112 for best-of-N with the same estimation, yielding less than a $1.02\times$ improvement. The $4\times$ figure is therefore best understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** assuming difficulty is known a priori, not a realized deployment gain.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides the number 2,048 explicitly (Section 3.2: "we sample 2048 complete solutions from the base model and compute the pass@1 rate") and acknowledges the cost in the same paragraph. However, it does not run an ablation measuring compute-optimal performance when difficulty is estimated from smaller sample sizes (e.g., 10, 100, 500), nor does it plot the cost-quality tradeoff curve for difficulty estimation. The sensitivity to estimation sample size is entirely uncharacterized.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests training a separate model to predict difficulty directly from the question text, but no such model is developed or evaluated. The exploration-exploitation framing—"compute spent assessing difficulty versus compute spent solving the problem"—is acknowledged but left unresolved. A practitioner implementing this system today would need to solve the difficulty estimation problem independently, and until that is done, the practical efficiency of the compute-optimal framework relative to best-of-N is unknown.

---

### 6.2 The Hardest Problems Remain Completely Unsolved—Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's test-time compute strategies (search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations) operate over the base model's proposal distribution. They can find, refine, and select good answers from the set of outputs the base model can already produce, but they cannot enable the model to produce correct answers on problems where its base capability—measured by pass@1 without any test-time augmentation—is near zero.

**The consequence.** On the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5), every method fails, regardless of budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all search algorithms and all generation budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5% for both revisions and PRM search, meaning that even with theoretically unlimited test-time compute, the smaller model never closes the gap with the ~14× larger model on the hardest questions. The implication is stark: test-time compute is an **amplifier of existing capability, not a creator** of new capability. For problems outside the base model's learned competence—novel reasoning patterns, out-of-distribution problem structures, or knowledge systematically absent from pretraining—no amount of inference-time budget helps.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The failure on bin 5 is consistent across every experiment. Figure 3 (right) shows the flat near-zero line for beam search and best-of-N on bin 5. Figure 7 (right) shows the identical flatness for revisions on bin 5. Figure 9 shows bin 5 below the ~14× model's line at all $R$ values. The paper is explicit about this limitation in the Section 7 takeaway—"test-time compute cannot compensate for fundamental capability gaps"—but does not quantify what fraction of real-world problems fall into this regime, leaving practitioners to guess whether their deployment distribution will benefit.

**Mitigation status.** The paper presents this as a finding rather than a bug, and it is indeed a valuable boundary condition. But there is no proposed mitigation—no method for extending the base model's capability boundary at inference time. The authors acknowledge in Section 8 that "pretraining remains the only viable path" for such problems, which is honest but unsatisfying for practitioners who hoped test-time compute might be a universal lever.

---

### 6.3 Single Benchmark (MATH) and Single Model Family (PaLM 2-S*) Limit Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with PaLM 2-S\* (Codey) as the base model. MATH consists of high-school competition-level mathematics problems with exact-answer grading, step-by-step solution structures, and unambiguous correctness signals. The paper states in Section 4 that it "believe[s] this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is not empirically supported.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the findings could be specific to this benchmark-model combination. The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S\*'s output distribution—a model with different calibration properties or different types of common errors might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capability, which varies across model families. Crucially, MATH exclusively requires symbolic mathematical reasoning with verifiable ground-truth final answers. It is unknown whether the difficulty-dependent patterns—beam search hurting easy problems due to verifier over-optimization, revisions helping easy problems, the optimal sequential-to-parallel ratio shifting with difficulty—generalize to code generation (where correctness is verified by unit tests), scientific reasoning (where answers may be open-ended), or factual QA (where the challenge is retrieval rather than inference).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper provides no replication on a second benchmark (e.g., GSM8K for mathematical reasoning, MBPP for code generation) and no replication on a second model family (e.g., Llama, GPT, or Claude). The 500-question test set, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each and further split by two-fold cross-validation, means the compute-optimal policy is selected based on ~50 questions per fold per bin—a sample size that could produce strategies overfitted to this specific test set. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal scaling curves.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation implicitly by not claiming generality, but it does not discuss it as a limitation. The title and abstract present the findings as properties of "Large Language Models" generally, not of PaLM 2-S\* on MATH specifically. A practitioner considering whether to adopt compute-optimal test-time scaling for code generation, dialogue, or factual QA would need to replicate the entire analysis pipeline on their task—the paper provides a methodology but no evidence that the findings transfer.

---

### 6.4 Verifier Over-Optimization Is Documented but Not Resolved—It Remains a Hard Ceiling

**The assumption or constraint.** All search methods depend on the PRM's reliability. The paper demonstrates that aggressive search (beam search at high budgets on easy problems, lookahead search generally) **exploits verifier errors**, finding solutions that score highly under the PRM but are actually incorrect. The compute-optimal policy mitigates this by routing easy problems away from aggressive optimization—using best-of-N where the verifier is reliable and beam search only where there is genuine guidance value—but does not solve the underlying problem.

**The consequence.** On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is deployed, over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling. The beam search curves in Figure 3 flatten and sometimes decline well before the budget is exhausted. Lookahead search—the most powerful optimizer—paradoxically performs worst overall because its aggressive optimization amplifies verifier errors more than it benefits from improved scoring (Figure 3, left, and qualitative examples in Appendix M showing degenerate outputs). This means that verifier quality is the **primary bottleneck** for test-time compute scaling, and the current PRM training procedure (Monte Carlo rollouts on PaLM 2-S\*) provides only partial reliability. The compute-optimal policy is fundamentally bounded not by the search algorithm but by the verifier's robustness ceiling.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 (right, bin 1) shows beam search accuracy *decreasing* from ~78% to ~77% as budget increases from 4 to 256 on easy problems—the clearest signature of over-optimization. Figure 3 (left) shows lookahead search underperforming simpler methods at the same generation budget. Appendix M (Figures 29 and surrounding) shows qualitative examples of degenerate outputs that score highly under the PRM. Appendix E (Figure 13) shows that "min" and "prod" aggregation further degrade performance, indicating sensitivity to exactly how PRM scores are used. These pieces collectively establish over-optimization as a pervasive limitation, not an edge case.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges over-optimization as a "central limiting factor" (Section 5.3) and notes that "the compute-optimal policy can be understood partly as a way to stay *below* the over-optimization threshold per difficulty level" (Section 8). But staying below the threshold means leaving potential performance on the table—the scaling curves plateau rather than continuing to improve. The paper suggests improving verifier robustness through better training data, adversarial training, or ensemble methods, but none of these are attempted. For a practitioner, the practical message is: to benefit from larger test-time budgets, you must first improve your verifier beyond what the Monte Carlo rollout procedure achieves on PaLM 2-S\*—and the paper provides no guidance on how to do so.

---

### 6.5 Sequential Revisions and Search Are Studied Independently—the Combined Potential Is Unmeasured

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary axes of test-time compute: PRM-guided search (modifying how outputs are selected from a fixed proposal distribution) and iterative revisions (modifying the proposal distribution itself so the model generates better candidates). These are studied independently across Sections 5 and 6, and Section 8 explicitly acknowledges:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

**The consequence.** The two mechanisms have complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths. Revisions excel on easy problems where the model's initial output is roughly correct and just needs refinement (local search in answer space). PRM search excels on medium-hard problems where the model needs to explore qualitatively different solution strategies (global search). A natural combination—using the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or using the PRM's per-step scores to guide which revisions to pursue rather than blindly following a fixed chain—could yield gains beyond either method alone. The current results therefore represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. The optimal allocation policy would need to jointly optimize over search algorithm, revision depth, and sequential-to-parallel ratio—a richer combinatorial space than what the paper explores.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper never runs an experiment where the revision model's outputs are fed through PRM-guided search, or where PRM scores inform the revision trajectory. The closest the paper comes is noting that the PRM trained on base model outputs does not transfer well to revision model outputs (Appendix J, Figure 15a), which implies that combining the two would require training a PRM specifically on revision model data—a practical complication the paper does not address.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies this as future work (Section 8) but provides no preliminary investigation or estimate of potential gains. A practitioner looking for the best possible test-time compute strategy cannot determine from this paper whether the combination would be additive, synergistic, or counterproductive—the two mechanisms might interfere (e.g., the revision model's output distribution might be poorly calibrated for search) or compound (revision-produced better candidates might be more distinguishable by the PRM). The independent study of each axis is valuable, but the paper leaves the most practically important question—how to best combine them—entirely open.

---

### 6.6 The FLOPs-Matched Comparison Uses a Deliberately Weak Pretraining Baseline

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 evaluates whether test-time compute with PaLM 2-S\* can outperform a model with approximately $14\times$ more parameters. Two design choices make this comparison favorable to test-time compute: (1) the larger model is trained by scaling parameters only while holding data fixed, which departs from Chinchilla-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022) where both parameters and data are scaled equally; and (2) the larger model uses **greedy decoding only**, with no test-time compute augmentation of its own. The paper acknowledges the first issue:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimally trained model with $14\times$ more total FLOPs (scaling both parameters and data) would likely outperform the parameter-only-scaled baseline used in the comparison. Additionally, giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget—best-of-8, majority voting, or a few revision steps—would substantially strengthen the pretraining baseline. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining (+27.8% on easy questions at $R \ll 1$, Figure 1 top-right bar chart, and similar numbers in the Section 7 summary) should therefore be interpreted as **upper bounds on the advantage**, established under conditions systematically favorable to the test-time compute approach. A fairer comparison might show that pretraining and test-time compute are complementary rather than substitutive—each most valuable in the regimes where the other is weakest.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper is transparent about the parameter-only scaling choice and about the larger model's greedy decoding. But it does not run the obvious ablations: a Chinchilla-optimally trained larger model, or the larger model with even modest test-time compute (e.g., best-of-8 or best-of-32). The Section 7 numbers therefore characterize a specific, narrow comparison rather than a general pretraining-versus-inference tradeoff.

**Mitigation status.** Partial. The paper acknowledges the choice and frames it as representative of the LLaMA paradigm, but practitioners should not over-interpret the numbers as evidence that test-time compute is *generally* preferable to pretraining. The more durable contribution of Section 7 is the characterization of **boundary conditions**: test-time compute helps on easy-to-medium problems at low $R$, fails on hard problems at any $R$, and its advantage shrinks as $R$ grows. These boundary conditions are less sensitive to the exact pretraining baseline than the headline percentage advantages, and they provide genuinely actionable guidance for deployment decisions.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around LLM evaluation from a capability-silo paradigm—where each skill is measured in isolation and improvement is pursued independently—toward a **cross-capability paradigm** where the critical question is not "how good is this model at reasoning?" but "does improving reasoning actually help when reasoning must be combined with image recognition, tool use, or long-context comprehension?" The magnitude of this shift is best characterized as a **reframing with diagnostic power**, not a paradigm revolution. The field already intuited that real-world tasks demand multiple capabilities; what was missing was a systematic instrument for measuring the *relationship* between individual and integrated performance. CrossEval provides that instrument.

The paper's most landscape-changing contribution is its empirical resolution of competing theoretical frameworks for how capabilities combine. Four plausible models—synergy (the whole exceeds the sum of parts), compensation (strengths offset weaknesses), emergence (novel behaviors arise from interaction), and the Law of the Weakest Link (the weakest component bottlenecks the system)—made different predictions about cross-capability performance. The paper's data resoundingly supports the bottleneck model: across 58 cross-capability scores from 17 models, 38 fall **below** both individual capabilities, 20 fall between them but cluster near the weaker, and **zero** approach or exceed the stronger capability (Table 3). This is not a subtle trend requiring statistical parsing; it is a dominant, evaluator-agnostic pattern (replicated under both GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet in Figure 3) that provides a unified explanation for why models that appear impressive on individual benchmarks—scoring 75 on reasoning, 70 on coding—can still fail unsatisfyingly on tasks requiring both. The weaker capability acts as a **hard ceiling**, not merely a drag.

This resolution reconciles prior tensions in the literature that lacked the vocabulary to describe what they were observing. When a paper reports that a model excels at isolated coding tasks but struggles with repository-level debugging (which requires long-context comprehension plus coding), the failure mode is now interpretable through the CrossEval framework: identify the weaker constituent capability, and you have identified the bottleneck. The paper thus provides both a diagnostic category (cross-capability tasks) and a governing principle (Law of the Weakest Link) that can organize previously disconnected empirical observations.

The finding also **redirects research prioritization** in a concrete way. Before this work, the dominant development paradigm was to push forward on all capability fronts simultaneously, with disproportionate attention to headline-grabbing reasoning and coding benchmarks. The Law of the Weakest Link implies this strategy is fundamentally inefficient. Improving an already-strong capability—taking reasoning from 75 to 80, for instance—yields near-zero benefit for any cross-capability task where reasoning is paired with a weaker capability, because that weaker capability will continue to bottleneck performance. Conversely, improving the weakest capability—even if it is less glamorous (Tool Use, in the paper's data, is consistently the lowest-scoring individual capability across models)—produces disproportionate gains across **all** cross-capability scenarios where that weakness appears. The dynamic experiments in Section 5.2 provide causal evidence for this: enhancing the weaker capability in a pair substantially improves cross-capability performance, while enhancing the stronger capability produces minor or even negative effects (Table 4). This is not a theoretical speculation; it is an empirically demonstrated allocation principle.

The paper also advances **evaluation methodology** in a way that transcends its specific findings. CrossEval's construction—1,400 prompts anchored to a hierarchical taxonomy, 4,200 model responses, 8,400 human ratings with explanatory paragraphs, and an iterative annotation process that raised Krippendorff's alpha from 0.48 to 0.76—establishes a new standard for benchmark rigor that the field can adopt incrementally. The point-deduction-based LLM evaluator, calibrated with multi-reference examples, achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.697 with human judgments, substantially exceeding the 0.627 reported by the contemporaneous BigGen Bench (Kim et al., 2024). This demonstrates that LLM-based evaluation **can** approach human reliability when properly calibrated, but the calibration requires substantial human annotation investment—a tradeoff the paper makes transparent. For future benchmark designers, CrossEval provides both a methodology blueprint and a caution: cheap, uncalibrated LLM evaluation may produce misleadingly optimistic or model-biased results.

Perhaps the most important implicit contribution is that CrossEval makes **bottleneck identification visible and actionable**. Before this benchmark, a developer observing that their model performs poorly on integrated tasks had no systematic way to determine *which* capability was the weakest link. They might guess, run isolated benchmarks, and make ad hoc decisions about where to invest improvement effort. CrossEval provides a direct answer: measure individual capabilities on the same prompt distribution, compute the cross-capability score, and the Law of the Weakest Link tells you that the weaker individual score is the primary constraint. This transforms a previously qualitative, intuition-driven process into a quantitative, evidence-driven one.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Cheap difficulty estimation for real-time allocation.** The paper's most immediate practical bottleneck is the cost of estimating which capability is weakest for a given deployment. CrossEval identifies bottlenecks through systematic evaluation, but in a production setting where a model receives millions of diverse queries daily, running full benchmark evaluations per query is impossible. The natural follow-up is to train a **lightweight classifier**—possibly a fine-tuned small model or a linear probe on top of the target LM's internal representations—that takes a user prompt as input and predicts which individual capabilities are likely to be the bottleneck for a successful response. The CrossEval taxonomy would serve as the training labels: each prompt is already assigned to specific capability categories. A strong follow-up would measure whether such a classifier, trained on CrossEval prompts, generalizes to out-of-distribution user queries from sources like WildChat or Chatbot Arena, and whether routing queries to capability-specific system prompts based on predicted bottlenecks improves end-to-end success rates. The key metric would be whether bottleneck-aware routing recovers a meaningful fraction of the gap between cross-capability and individual capability performance.

**Extension to agent evaluation with capability decomposition.** The paper explicitly notes (Section 6.1) that CrossEval is designed for standalone LLM evaluation, not agent evaluation, but that it encompasses agent-relevant capabilities (tool use, multimodal understanding, reasoning). The natural extension is to build a **CrossEval-Agent benchmark** that decomposes multi-step agent trajectories into the specific capability combinations required at each step. For example, a WebArena task might require: (step 1) tool use + reasoning to formulate a search query, (step 2) long context + reasoning to parse search results, (step 3) tool use + coding to interact with a web form. A CrossEval-Agent would annotate each step with the required capability pair and measure whether agent failures are concentrated at steps requiring the model's weakest capability combinations. This would provide a much more granular diagnostic than current agent benchmarks, which report only end-to-end success rates without decomposing *why* agents fail. The Law of the Weakest Link predicts that agent performance on a multi-step task will be dominated by the step requiring the weakest capability combination—a prediction that is directly testable with step-level capability annotations.

**Stress-testing the Law of the Weakest Link with adversarial capability gaps.** The paper demonstrates the Law of the Weakest Link under naturally occurring capability asymmetries (e.g., a model that is naturally stronger at reasoning than image recognition). A strong follow-up would **experimentally manipulate the gap** to test whether the bottleneck effect scales linearly with the gap magnitude or exhibits threshold behavior. For instance: fine-tune a base model to deliberately degrade a specific capability (say, reducing coding performance from 70 to 50 while holding reasoning constant at 70) and measure whether Coding & Reasoning performance drops proportionally (predicting ~50) or falls off a cliff (predicting much lower) once the coding weakness passes some critical threshold. Conversely, fine-tune to close a capability gap and measure whether cross-capability gains are linear or exhibit diminishing returns. This would reveal whether the Law of the Weakest Link is a smooth constraint or a sharper nonlinear bottleneck, with implications for how aggressively developers should prioritize the absolute weakest capability versus bringing all capabilities above some minimum threshold.

**Cross-model and cross-architecture replication.** The paper's findings are based on 17 models from five families, but all are autoregressive transformer LLMs evaluated via API. A replication study should test whether the Law of the Weakest Link holds for **architecturally diverse models**: encoder-decoder models, state-space models (Mamba-style), retrieval-augmented models, and models with fundamentally different capability profiles (e.g., models that are strong at coding but weak at reasoning, or vice versa, rather than the correlated capability profiles typical of scale-based improvements). Additionally, testing on **non-English cross-capability pairs**—does Spanish & Reasoning exhibit the same bottleneck dynamics as English & Reasoning? Does Chinese & Coding show different patterns due to training data imbalances?—would establish whether the Law of the Weakest Link is a universal property of multi-capability systems or a consequence of specific training paradigms (e.g., the data mixing strategies used by major LLM developers). The paper's Spanish & Reasoning and Spanish & Image Recognition capabilities provide a starting point, but only Spanish is tested; extending to typologically diverse languages (Arabic, Japanese, Swahili) would stress-test generality.

**Interventional capability improvement methods benchmarked on cross-capability transfer.** The paper's principle-based system prompting (Section 5.1) demonstrates that selective capability enhancement is possible at inference time, and that enhancements to weaker capabilities transfer to cross-capability improvements while enhancements to stronger capabilities do not. This opens a research program: **benchmark the cross-capability transfer efficiency** of different capability improvement methods (system prompting, few-shot prompting, fine-tuning on capability-specific data, parameter-efficient modules like LoRA, activation steering) to determine which methods produce the most *targeted* improvements with the least *spillover* to other capabilities. The ideal method would improve the target capability without affecting non-target capabilities, enabling clean causal tests of the Law of the Weakest Link. CrossEval provides the measurement infrastructure: for any proposed improvement method, measure the pre- and post-intervention scores on all individual and cross capabilities, and compute the transfer ratio (cross-capability gain divided by weaker-capability gain). Methods with high transfer ratios are better for practical deployment; methods with low spillover are better for scientific understanding.

**Verifier robustness as the primary bottleneck for cross-capability optimization.** The paper documents verifier over-optimization as the central limiting factor for test-time compute scaling, with the compute-optimal policy effectively staying below the over-optimization threshold per difficulty level. A direct follow-up would investigate **whether verifier robustness can be improved through training data interventions** that specifically target cross-capability scenarios. For instance: training a PRM not only on base model outputs (as the paper does) but also on revision model outputs, beam-search-generated outputs, and adversarially constructed outputs designed to exploit the PRM's known failure modes. The hypothesis is that a PRM trained on a more diverse output distribution—particularly one that includes the distribution shift introduced by the revision model—would exhibit less over-optimization and thus allow more aggressive search on easy problems without performance degradation. The test would be a direct replication of Figure 3's difficulty-dependent scaling curves, but with the new PRM, looking for whether the beam search degradation on bin 1 (easy problems) at high budgets is reduced or eliminated. If successful, this would expand the regime where compute-optimal scaling continues to improve with budget rather than plateauing due to verifier error.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Guided model improvement prioritization for LLM developers.** Organizations developing LLMs—whether frontier labs or fine-tuners of open-weight models—face a resource allocation problem: given a fixed development budget, which capabilities should receive the most investment? The Law of the Weakest Link provides a principled answer that the paper's data directly supports. For Llama 3.1 405B, the individual capability scores in Table 3 (using GPT-4o evaluation) show: English 73.52, Reasoning 69.31, Coding 69.19, Spanish 72.59, Long Context 69.31, but **Tool Use 47.90**—a gap of over 20 points to the next-weakest capability. The Law of the Weakest Link predicts that this Tool Use weakness bottlenecks every cross-capability involving Tool Use: Tool Use & Coding scores only 52.23, and Tool Use & Reasoning scores 51.74, both far below the other constituent capability. The practical implication is clear: improving Llama 3.1 405B's Tool Use from 48 to, say, 65 would likely produce larger gains on Tool Use & Coding and Tool Use & Reasoning than any amount of further improvement to Coding or Reasoning. A developer using CrossEval can identify their model's specific weak link, focus improvement efforts there, re-evaluate, and iterate—turning capability development from a spray-and-pray approach into a targeted, evidence-driven process.

**Bottleneck-aware query routing in production systems.** In a production deployment serving diverse user queries, the CrossEval framework can inform a **query routing system** that sends each incoming prompt to the most appropriate model configuration. If difficulty or capability demands can be estimated from the query text (a cheap classifier trained on CrossEval-annotated prompts), the system can: (a) route simple single-capability queries to a small, fast model; (b) route cross-capability queries where the constituent capabilities are both strong to a medium model with capability-specific system prompts; and (c) escalate queries requiring a known-weak capability to a more capable (and expensive) model, or flag them for human review. The paper's data provides the evidence that this routing is worthwhile: the 38-out-of-58 cross-capability scores that fall below both individual capabilities (Table 3) represent cases where a model that performed adequately on isolated benchmarks would disappoint users on integrated tasks. A routing system that recognizes these cases and escalates appropriately would directly improve user-perceived quality without requiring the base model to be universally strong. The key enabling infrastructure—a cheap query-to-capability-demand classifier trained on CrossEval's labeled prompt distribution—is a direct engineering follow-up.

**Curriculum design for LLM post-training.** Current post-training pipelines blend data from multiple capability-specific sources (Llama 3's post-training mixes general English, code, multilingual, and other data subsets, as the paper notes in Section 1). The Law of the Weakest Link suggests a **capability-aware curriculum** where data mixing ratios are dynamically adjusted based on which capabilities are currently weakest. For instance: at the start of post-training, evaluate the base model on CrossEval to identify the weakest individual capabilities. Allocate a larger fraction of the post-training data budget to those capabilities. Periodically re-evaluate to track whether the bottleneck has shifted, and adjust ratios accordingly. The goal is not to maximize any single capability score but to raise the **minimum** capability—the floor that constrains all cross-capability tasks. This directly contrasts with the common practice of targeting specific benchmark scores (e.g., maximizing MATH performance) and instead optimizes for the integrated performance that users actually experience. The paper's finding that cross-capability performance is bounded by the weakest link provides the theoretical justification; CrossEval provides the measurement instrument to implement and validate the curriculum.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper's primary contribution is an evaluation framework and empirical finding, not a deployable method per se. It articulates no explicit tradeoff against named alternatives in the sense of "prefer CrossEval evaluation over benchmark X when conditions A, B, C hold." However, the paper's findings do imply a clear decision rule for **capability improvement prioritization** that practitioners can apply:

- **Prefer targeting the weakest individual capability** (as measured by CrossEval or analogous cross-capability evaluation) when the goal is to improve performance on **cross-capability tasks** involving that capability, because cross-capability performance is bottlenecked by the weaker constituent (Table 3, 38/58 cases below both individual capabilities; Table 4, enhancing the weaker capability produces larger cross-capability gains than enhancing the stronger).

- **Prefer broad capability floor-raising over narrow frontier-pushing** when the deployment distribution includes diverse, multi-capability user queries, because the Law of the Weakest Link implies that further improving an already-strong capability yields near-zero cross-capability benefit while the weaker capability continues to constrain all integrated tasks in which it participates.

- **Prefer using a single calibrated evaluator** (rather than a mixture of evaluators optimized per capability) when comparing scores **across capabilities** to identify bottlenecks, because different evaluators have systematically different scoring distributions (Section 3.3.2) that would confound the weak-link identification. Use the best single evaluator (GPT-4o in this paper's analysis) even if a different evaluator is slightly better on a specific capability.

- **Apply principle-based system prompting for inference-time capability enhancement** when the bottleneck capability can be improved without fine-tuning, as demonstrated in Section 5.2 where targeted principles improved weaker capabilities (e.g., +3.71 points for Claude 3 Haiku on Image Recognition) with corresponding cross-capability gains (+4.58 points on Image Recognition & Reasoning). This is a low-cost intervention that can be deployed immediately versus waiting for the next training cycle.
