# How Language Model Hallucinations Can Snowball

**ArXiv:** [2305.13534](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.13534)

## 🎯 Pitch

Large language models often know their own justifications are false: GPT-4 can spot 87% of its own incorrect claims when they appear in isolation, yet it still generated them to back up wrong answers. These “snowballed hallucinations” reveal that models lie not just from ignorance but to stay logically consistent with their own earlier blunders, trapping them in a cascade of mistakes even simple prompting cannot fully stop.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how language models generate incorrect statements not solely from knowledge gaps but as a cascading consequence of earlier mistakes, constructing three QA datasets—primality testing, senator search, and graph connectivity—where ChatGPT and GPT-4 often commit to a wrong answer and then justify it with claims they can separately recognize as false. The core contribution is the concept of **hallucination snowballing**: an LM over-commits to an early error, and the pressure to maintain coherence with that prior context forces it to produce additional incorrect claims it would otherwise avoid (e.g., claiming 13 × 745 = 9677 to justify "9677 is not prime," then separately acknowledging that 9677 is not divisible by 13). ChatGPT and GPT-4 detect 67% and 87% of their own snowballed mistakes when those claims are presented in isolation, establishing that hallucinations are not necessarily reflective of a model's underlying knowledge but are induced by faulty self-consistency pressure—and that test-time prompting interventions like "Let's think step by step" reduce overall error rates dramatically but fail to prevent snowballing in the remaining errors, with GPT-4 still exhibiting a 95% snowballed hallucination rate on its residual mistakes.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Hallucinations Are Not Just Knowledge Gaps

The paper addresses a specific, underexplored mechanism behind language model hallucinations. The dominant explanation in the field when this paper was written attributed hallucinations to **knowledge gaps**: models fabricate facts because they simply don't "know" the correct information (Zheng et al., 2023). This framing motivated a generation of mitigation strategies built around retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020; Shuster et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2023), where external knowledge bases supply the missing facts at inference time.

This paper challenges the completeness of that explanation. The authors hypothesize a different causal pathway: **an LM can hallucinate a fact that it does know to be false**, not because the knowledge is absent, but because it has already committed to an incorrect answer and must now produce a coherent justification. The distinction matters enormously for how the field approaches the hallucination problem. If hallucinations are purely about knowledge gaps, the solution is to stuff more knowledge into the model (via retrieval or larger pretraining). But if hallucinations can be *induced by context* even when the knowledge is present, then retrieval alone cannot solve the problem — the model will still generate falsehoods that are internally consistent with its prior mistakes.

The paper crystallizes this distinction with a memorable example (Figure 1, Table 1): GPT-4 incorrectly claims 9677 is not prime and justifies this by asserting "13 × 745 = 9677." When later asked in a separate session whether 9677 is divisible by 13, the same model correctly answers "No." This is not a knowledge gap — the model demonstrably "knows" that 13 is not a factor. The false factorization was generated because the model had already committed (in the first token) to "No, [9677 is not prime]" and coherence pressure demanded a supporting explanation, however fabricated.

### A Specific Mechanism: Over-Commitment to Early Tokens

The theoretical contribution of this paper is isolating *why* this happens. The authors identify two structural properties of the interaction between transformer LMs and certain question types that create the conditions for hallucination snowballing:

**Initial committal.** Instruction-tuned LMs are trained to produce answers in a fluent, human-like format. In English discourse, yes/no answers conventionally precede their explanations — speakers say "Yes, because..." or "No, the reason is..." The authors observe that ChatGPT and GPT-4 reflect this convention with striking consistency: the first token is "Yes" or "No" 95.67% of the time for GPT-4 and 98.40% for ChatGPT on their datasets (Section 2). This means the model must answer a potentially complex multi-step reasoning problem **in a single generation step**, before it has produced any of the intermediate computation needed to solve it correctly. Once "Yes" or "No" is generated, that token enters the context window and becomes impossible to retract.

**Inherently sequential problems.** The authors deliberately choose tasks that cannot be solved by a transformer in a single forward pass. They cite theoretical results from Merrill and Sabharwal (2023), who showed that bounded-precision transformers operating within a single generation step cannot solve problems outside the complexity class $\text{TC}^0$ — a highly parallelizable subclass of both $\text{L}$ (log-space) and $\text{P}$ (polynomial-time). Graph connectivity is $\text{L}$-complete, and primality testing, while in $\text{P}$ (Agrawal et al., 2004), is believed to be outside $\text{TC}^0$ under standard complexity-theoretic assumptions. This means that for non-trivial instances of these problems, a transformer **must** commit to an answer (via the first-token phenomenon) before it has had sufficient computational depth to determine the correct answer — it responds in one step to a question that requires many steps. The result is an error-prone guess, which then constrains everything that follows.

### Why This Problem Matters

The significance of hallucination snowballing extends beyond a clever experimental demonstration. It has implications across three dimensions:

**Practical deployment risk.** The paper's experimental setup — innocent information-seeking questions asked in good faith — mirrors how users actually interact with deployed LMs. These are not adversarial prompts designed to trick the model. A user asking "Is 9677 a prime number?" is making a genuine query. The model's response "No, it can be factored into 13 × 745" sounds authoritative, includes specific (but fabricated) evidence, and would likely convince a non-expert user. More critically, the model presents this answer with high confidence — it doesn't hedge or express uncertainty, because hedging would violate the coherence objective it was trained to optimize. The resulting interaction leaves the user with false information that is especially difficult to detect, because it comes packaged with plausible-sounding justification.

**Theoretical challenge to the consistency-coherence tradeoff.** Language models are trained to maximize the probability of generating coherent text given their context. This training objective conflates two distinct desiderata: **internal coherence** (does the output follow naturally from the preceding context?) and **external factuality** (does the output correspond to truth?). When the preceding context contains a falsehood (like a prematurely committed "No" answer), these two objectives come into direct conflict. The model must choose between being coherent (inventing a justification for the false answer) and being factual (contradicting its own previous output). The training paradigm strongly incentivizes coherence, which is precisely what produces the snowballed hallucination. This reveals a fundamental tension in current LM training methodology that cannot be resolved simply by adding more training data or scaling model size — it requires a different approach to how models handle their own prior outputs.

**Implications for self-improvement and iterative refinement.** Recent work on self-correction (Bai et al., 2022; Madaan et al., 2023) and iterative refinement (Saunders et al., 2022) operates on the premise that LMs can detect and fix their own errors when asked to review their work. The hallucination snowballing phenomenon complicates this premise. The paper demonstrates that while the model *does* possess the knowledge to recognize its snowballed mistakes (67–87% detection rate when the claim is presented in isolation), that knowledge is **inaccessible during the original generation** because the faulty context overrides it. This explains why simply prompting "Check your work" may not suffice — the error-inducing context remains present. The model's ability to self-correct is conditional on whether the problematic prior context can be isolated or neutralized.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

**Retrieval-augmented generation.** The dominant mitigation strategy at the time — grounding LM outputs in retrieved documents (Lewis et al., 2020; Shuster et al., 2021) — addresses knowledge-gap hallucinations by supplying external facts. But for snowballed hallucinations, the problem is not missing knowledge; the model already possesses the relevant fact (e.g., "13 does not divide 9677") and can access it in a separate context. The problem is that this knowledge is suppressed by the pressure to maintain coherence with the model's own previous output. A retrieval system that supplies "13 does not divide 9677" alongside the original question would likely fail because the model has already committed to the "No, not prime" answer and would generate a *different* false factorization to support it. The retrieval approach treats the symptom (missing facts) rather than the cause (contextual override of existing knowledge).

**Chain-of-thought prompting.** Kojima et al. (2023) and Wei et al. (2022) showed that prompting LMs to "think step by step" significantly improves reasoning accuracy. The paper acknowledges this — using "Let's think step by step" drops the error rate dramatically across all three datasets (Figure 3), from 83.4% to 3.87% for GPT-4 averaged across tasks. However, the paper makes a critical observation that prior work had not examined: **snowballed hallucinations still occur in the residual errors**. When GPT-4 still gets the answer wrong even with step-by-step prompting, 94.9% of those errors involve snowballed hallucinations (Table 9). The paper provides a concrete example from the graph connectivity task: ChatGPT incorrectly states there are "three options" at a step where only two exist, then uses the hallucinated third option as a basis for subsequent reasoning. The model "knows" the third option doesn't exist (verifiable in a separate session) but generated it anyway because the step-by-step chain had committed to it at an intermediate stage.

This is a nuanced critique: chain-of-thought prompting reduces error rates substantially by preventing the worst form of early commitment (it delays the yes/no answer until after reasoning). But it does not address the underlying mechanism of snowballing — if an error occurs *anywhere* in the reasoning chain, it propagates forward through the same coherence pressure. The paper argues this is a fundamental limitation of any approach that relies on the model's own autoregressive generation without mechanisms for backtracking or revising earlier steps.

**Self-correction and backtracking.** The paper notes early work on having LMs review and correct their own outputs (citing Kim et al., 2023 on "Review your previous answer and find problems with your answer"). However, these approaches were in their infancy at the time, and the paper's findings suggest a specific requirement they must satisfy: the correction mechanism must be capable of **isolating and neutralizing the faulty context**. Simply appending "Review your answer" to a generation that contains a snowballed hallucination still leaves the original faulty context in the input window, where it may continue to exert influence. The paper's verification experiments (asking about the false claim in a completely new session with no history of the original interaction) demonstrate that isolation works — when the model sees "Is 9677 divisible by 13?" without the previous commitment that "9677 is not prime," it answers correctly. This suggests that successful self-correction architectures need to emulate this isolation somehow, perhaps through careful context management or explicit backtracking mechanisms.

**Exposure bias literature.** The paper connects hallucination snowballing to prior work on exposure bias in sequence generation (Wang and Sennrich, 2020; Arora et al., 2022). During training, LMs are conditioned on gold-standard prefix text (teacher forcing). At inference, they are conditioned on their own potentially erroneous previous predictions, creating a distribution shift. This prior work documented that exposure bias leads to compounding errors — a mistake at step $t$ makes a mistake at step $t+1$ more likely. However, the paper argues that prior exposure bias work had not demonstrated the specific and striking phenomenon where **the propagated error contradicts knowledge the model demonstrably possesses**. Compounding errors in machine translation (Wang and Sennrich, 2020) may produce semantically degraded output, but the model would not necessarily "know" it was producing a bad translation. Snowballed hallucinations are qualitatively different: the model generates a claim it can separately identify as false, making the failure more precisely diagnosable and potentially more tractable.

**Presupposition-triggered hallucinations.** The paper draws a careful distinction from related work on LMs hallucinating when given questions with false presuppositions. Kim et al. (2021, 2022) showed that questions like "Which linguist invented the lightbulb?" cause LMs to generate plausible-sounding answers that accept the false premise. Lin et al. (2022) demonstrated similar behavior with misleading questions in TruthfulQA, where models mimic human misconceptions embedded in the prompt format. These studies established that LMs can be misled by *externally provided* faulty context. This paper's contribution is showing that the same phenomenon occurs with *internally generated* faulty context — the model's own premature output becomes the misleading premise that triggers further hallucinations. The questions themselves ("Is 9677 prime?") are innocent and contain no false presuppositions. The misleading context is entirely self-inflicted, which makes it harder to detect and mitigate through prompt engineering alone.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself as identifying and characterizing a **new failure mode** in language model generation, distinct from previously documented hallucination mechanisms. It does not propose a complete solution or a new training methodology; instead, it provides (a) a clear operational definition of the phenomenon, (b) a controlled experimental framework for measuring it across multiple domains, (c) quantitative evidence of its prevalence in state-of-the-art models, and (d) a theoretical account of why it occurs based on the interaction between the transformer architecture's computational limitations and the training objective's emphasis on coherence.

The theoretical framing in Section 2 — decomposing the necessary conditions into "initial committal" and "inherently sequential" — is the paper's attempt to provide generalizable insight. It suggests that hallucination snowballing should occur on any task where the answer format pressures the model to commit before computing and where the computation requires more than a single forward pass. This makes the phenomenon relevant beyond the three specific datasets studied, extending to any deployment scenario where users ask yes/no questions about facts that require reasoning (medical diagnosis, legal reasoning, technical troubleshooting, financial analysis).

The paper's title — "How Language Model Hallucinations Can Snowball" — reflects its primary contribution as one of **characterization and diagnosis** rather than remediation. By demonstrating that models generate claims they can separately recognize as false, the paper opens a new axis for hallucination research: not just whether the model knows something, but whether it can *access* what it knows under the pressure of its own prior output. This reframing connects the hallucination problem to broader questions about context management, consistency, and the architectural constraints that shape model behavior in deployment.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This is primarily a **measurement and characterization paper** whose core idea is that language models can generate incorrect claims not because they lack the relevant knowledge, but because an earlier mistake — often a premature yes/no commitment — creates contextual pressure to produce coherent justifications, which in turn forces the model to generate additional false statements that it can separately recognize as incorrect when those statements are presented in isolation. The paper builds a controlled experimental framework using three synthetic QA datasets to systematically detect and quantify this "hallucination snowballing" phenomenon, distinguishing it from standard knowledge-gap hallucinations by demonstrating that the model itself can verify the falsity of its own explanatory claims when the original error-inducing context is removed.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The experimental system has four major components:

1. **Dataset Construction Pipeline** — generates 500 yes/no questions per domain (primality testing, senator search, graph connectivity) with controlled ground-truth labels and predictable justification structures, ensuring that incorrect answers will produce extractable, verifiable claims.

2. **Primary QA Interface** — presents each question to the language model (ChatGPT or GPT-4) with greedy decoding, collects the model's yes/no answer and its free-form justification, and records whether the answer is correct.

3. **Claim Extraction Module** — parses the model's justification text to isolate the specific factual claim that supports the incorrect answer (a factorization, a senator's biography, or a flight connection), using domain-specific heuristics and, for primality and senator tasks, a separate extraction step with ChatGPT.

4. **Verification Interface** — in a completely separate session (no shared conversation history), presents the extracted claim back to the same model as a new standalone question, asking whether that specific fact is correct, and records whether the model correctly identifies it as false.

Information flows unidirectionally: questions are constructed once per dataset → each question is sent to the LM for primary QA → for incorrect answers, the claim extractor identifies the supporting incorrect fact → the verification interface presents that fact as a new question to the same LM → the system labels the original hallucination as "snowballed" if and only if the model correctly rejects the extracted claim in verification.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the dataset construction methodology for all three domains, because the entire measurement framework depends on having questions where incorrect answers predictably produce specific, extractable, verifiable claims — without this property, snowballing cannot be systematically detected.

- **Second**, the primary QA protocol — how questions are presented, how answers are classified, and what constitutes the "initial committal" that triggers snowballing — because this establishes the baseline error rates against which snowballing is measured.

- **Third**, the claim extraction procedures for each domain, since each domain requires different heuristics to isolate the falsifiable supporting claim from free-form justification text.

- **Fourth**, the verification protocol — how extracted claims are reformulated as standalone questions, the requirement for separate sessions, and how verification responses are judged — because this is the operational definition of a "snowballed hallucination" and the paper's central empirical contribution.

- **Fifth**, the inference-time intervention experiments (chain-of-thought prompting, temperature variation) and their relationship to the measurement framework, because these probe whether snowballing is mitigable through prompting or decoding changes.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This paper constructs a measurement methodology rather than a new model or training procedure. The core idea is **operationalizing the detection of self-contradictory hallucinations**: generate conditions where an LM produces a factually incorrect claim as part of justifying an incorrect answer, then test whether the same LM can recognize that specific claim as false when it appears without the pressure of the original incorrect context. The experimental apparatus is designed around three synthetic QA datasets, each engineered so that (a) the ground-truth answer is known and controlled, (b) incorrect answers will be justified with predictable, extractable factual claims, and (c) those claims can be reformulated into standalone verification questions with unambiguous correct answers.

---

#### Dataset Construction: Design Principles and Rationale

The paper constructs three datasets of 500 questions each. The datasets are not intended to represent natural user query distributions; rather, they are **scientific instruments** designed to isolate and measure a specific hypothesized failure mode. The construction follows several cross-cutting design principles:

**Uniform ground-truth labels.** For each dataset, the correct answer is the same for all 500 questions. Primality testing questions use only actual prime numbers, so the correct answer is always "Yes." Senator search questions use only state-college pairs where no senator satisfies both constraints, so the correct answer is always "No." Graph connectivity questions use pairs of cities in disconnected subgraphs, so the correct answer is always "No." This design choice serves two purposes: it makes the comprehensive dataset evaluation tractable (no per-question manual verification of correctness is needed beyond the initial construction check), and it ensures that incorrect answers will be "Yes" for senator search and graph connectivity and "No" for primality testing, which makes the structure of the incorrect justification predictable. When the model answers incorrectly, it will always be claiming a specific false positive (a factorization, a senator, or a connecting flight) or a specific false negative (claiming a prime is composite), and the paper can prepare extraction heuristics that exploit this predictable structure.

**Controlled difficulty via inherent sequentiality.** The paper selects problem domains that formal complexity theory says cannot be solved by a transformer in a single forward pass. This is not incidental — it operationalizes the "inherently sequential" condition from the authors' theoretical framework (Section 2). By choosing problems outside $\text{TC}^0$, the paper guarantees that the model must commit to an answer before it has sufficient computational depth to solve the problem correctly, creating the structural conditions for snowballing. The dataset sizes (prime numbers between 1,000 and 20,000; graphs with 14 cities and 12 edges; state-college pairs) are calibrated to be non-trivial — large enough that the problems cannot be solved by simple pattern matching or memorization, small enough that human verification of the dataset's correctness is straightforward.

**Extractable and verifiable justifications.** This is the most critical design constraint. Each dataset is constructed so that when the model produces an incorrect answer, the justification it generates will contain a specific falsifiable claim that can be (a) automatically or semi-automatically extracted from the free-form text, and (b) reformulated as a standalone yes/no question with a deterministic correct answer. Without this property, the paper's central measurement — "does the model recognize its own false claim?" — cannot be performed systematically at scale. The datasets are designed so that the structure of the false justification is stereotyped: for primality, the model will produce a factorization (a product of two integers); for senator search, the model will name a specific senator and assert biographical facts; for graph connectivity, the model will list a sequence of flights. The extraction heuristics exploit these stereotypes.

#### Primality Testing Dataset Construction

The dataset consists of 500 prime numbers randomly selected from the range 1,000 to 20,000. The correct answer to "Is $n$ a prime number?" is always "Yes" for these numbers. The range 1,000–20,000 is chosen to be large enough that the model cannot feasibly memorize the primality status of every integer in the range (unlike, say, numbers under 100), yet small enough that the numbers are not so large as to make the factorization claims in model responses difficult to verify manually.

**Why primes specifically.** If the dataset contained composite numbers (correct answer "No"), an incorrect answer ("Yes, it is prime") would not produce an extractable false claim — the model would simply assert a negative (that no factorization exists), which is logically impossible to falsify with a single verifiable proposition. Using primes and labeling the correct answer as "Yes" means that an incorrect answer will always be "No, it is not prime," and the model, following human-like explanatory conventions, will typically justify this with an explicit factorization: the model will say something like "No, [number] is not a prime number. It can be factored into $a \times b$." The factorization is a concrete, extractable, verifiable claim: for each factor $a$, one can ask "Is $n$ divisible by $a$?" and receive a yes/no answer.

**Construction procedure.** The authors randomly select 500 primes from the range 1,000 to 20,000. No further filtering or curation is applied. The raw question presented to the model is simply "Is $n$ a prime number?" with no additional context, examples, or formatting instructions. This minimalist prompt reflects the paper's focus on default model behavior under standard user interaction patterns.

**Expected incorrect behavior.** When the model incorrectly answers "No" to a prime number, it nearly always provides a specific factorization. This factorization will be false (since the number is actually prime), but the model will present it with confidence. The factors themselves become the targets for verification.

#### Senator Search Dataset Construction

This dataset consists of 500 questions of the form "Was there ever a US senator that represented the state of $x$ and whose alma mater was $y$?" where $x$ is a U.S. state and $y$ is a U.S. college. The correct answer is always "No" — for every question in the dataset, there is no senator in U.S. history who satisfies both constraints simultaneously.

**College list construction.** The authors use a manually constructed list of twelve U.S. colleges: MIT, University of Chicago, Johns Hopkins University, California Institute of Technology, Duke University, Northwestern University, Dartmouth College, Brown University, Vanderbilt University, Rice University, and University of Washington. This list is not arbitrary. The authors explain they started from a list of top U.S. universities and then **excluded** any university that also appeared on the U.S. News & World Report's list of Top 10 Colleges for Members of Congress. The reasoning is subtle but important: senators disproportionately attend prestigious universities, and universities popular among members of Congress would be more likely to create valid (state × college) pairs where the answer is "Yes." By excluding those universities, the authors reduce the density of true-positive pairs in the search space, making it easier to construct 500 questions where the answer is genuinely "No" without requiring extensive manual verification of each pair. The remaining twelve universities are prestigious but not overrepresented among senators, making false-positive claims more likely — if the model incorrectly answers "Yes," it will typically name a senator who attended one of these schools (or a similar school) and claim they represented the given state, when in fact they did not.

**Question generation procedure.** The authors consider all possible pairs of the 50 U.S. states and the 12 colleges, yielding 600 candidate questions. For each pair, they manually check whether any U.S. senator has ever both represented that state and attended that college. Pairs where the answer is "Yes" are removed. From the remaining false pairs, 500 are selected (the paper does not specify the selection procedure, but the total of remaining false pairs presumably exceeds 500 given the exclusion of congressman-heavy universities).

**Expected incorrect behavior.** When the model incorrectly answers "Yes" to a senator search question, it will typically name a specific senator and assert that this person both represented the state and attended the college. This involves two separate claims: (1) the named senator represented the state, and (2) the named senator attended the college. At least one of these claims must be false (since the pair was verified to have no valid senator). Often, the model names a real senator and gets one fact right (e.g., they did represent the state) but hallucinates the other (e.g., their alma mater was actually a different institution). The extracted claim — "Did Senator $S$ attend college $y$?" or "Did Senator $S$ represent state $x$?" — is verifiable as a standalone yes/no question. The paper's verification procedure checks both dimensions when applicable.

**A critical detail about extraction.** The senator search domain requires extracting not just a factual claim but an *entity* (the senator's name) from the model's output. The paper uses ChatGPT itself as the extraction tool: given the original output, it prompts "What is the senator mentioned in the above text? Just give the name." The authors report manually checking 30 examples and finding perfect extraction accuracy. This extraction step is itself a potential source of error (if the extraction prompt misidentifies the senator or hallucinates a name not actually in the text), but the paper treats it as reliable based on the manual spot-check.

#### Graph Connectivity Dataset Construction

Each of the 500 questions in this dataset describes a set of 12 directed flights among 14 cities, then asks whether there exists a sequence of flights from a specified source city to a specified destination city. The correct answer is always "No" — the source and destination are chosen from different disconnected components of the underlying graph.

**Underlying graph structure (Figure 5).** All 500 questions share the same underlying directed graph topology, containing 14 nodes and 12 edges. The graph is not fully connected; it consists of multiple disconnected subgraphs. The paper provides the exact structure in Figure 5 (reproduced in the Appendix). By reusing the same graph structure, the authors ensure that (a) the problem difficulty is consistent across all instances, (b) the disconnectedness property holds uniformly (no instance accidentally has a valid path), and (c) any variation in model performance across instances comes from the random relabeling of nodes rather than from structural differences in the underlying problem.

**Node labeling and question formulation.** For each of the 500 instances, the 14 nodes are randomly assigned letters from the English alphabet as names (e.g., "City A," "City B," ..., "City N"). The flights are stated in natural language: "There is a flight from city [source] to city [destination]." All 12 flights are listed explicitly in the prompt, followed by the question "Is there a series of flights that goes from city $s$ to city $t$?" The prompt explicitly states that "all the flights available are included below" and that flights are "one-way only," preempting the model from assuming unlisted flights or bidirectional travel.

**Source and destination selection.** For each instance, the authors sample a source city $s$ and destination city $t$ from different disconnected subgraphs, guaranteeing that no path exists. Additionally, they impose the constraint that $s$ corresponds to a source node (a node with outgoing edges but no incoming edges from other components) and $t$ is a leaf node (a node with incoming edges but no outgoing edges within its component). This constraint serves a specific purpose stated in the paper: it prevents the model from using "1-step heuristics" to solve the problem. If $s$ were a leaf node with no outgoing edges, the model could trivially answer "No" without reasoning about connectivity. If $s$ and $t$ were in the same component but far apart, the answer would be "Yes" and the model would need to find a path, which is a different (and solvable) problem. By choosing $s$ as a source and $t$ as a leaf in different components, the problem requires genuinely tracing connectivity through the graph — a task that is $\text{L}$-complete and thus outside $\text{TC}^0$.

**Expected incorrect behavior.** When the model incorrectly answers "Yes" to a graph connectivity question (claiming a path exists where none does), it typically produces a step-by-step route listing a sequence of flights from $s$ to $t$. Because no valid path exists, this route must contain at least one flight that is not in the original 12 listed flights, or it must contain a discontinuity (a flight from city $X$ to city $Y$ where no such flight was listed and $Y$ is not connected to the next step). The extraction procedure identifies these invalid or discontinuous flights. The verification question then asks "Based on the above flight information, is [City X] to [City Y] a valid flight?" — a question with a deterministic "No" answer.

#### Primary QA Protocol

For each question in each dataset, the paper presents the question to the language model in a zero-shot setting: no demonstration examples are provided, and the prompt contains only the question text (and, for graph connectivity, the flight listing as context). The models are ChatGPT (`gpt-3.5-turbo`) and GPT-4, both accessed via the OpenAI API with **greedy decoding** (temperature 0, always selecting the highest-probability token). The choice of greedy decoding is deliberate: it removes stochastic variation, making the model behavior deterministic and repeatable for a given prompt, which is essential for systematic measurement. The paper later experiments with higher temperatures (0.6 and 0.9) to test whether stochastic decoding affects snowballing rates, but the primary experiments use temperature 0.

**Answer classification procedure.** The paper classifies a model response as correct or incorrect based on whether its answer matches the ground truth. The primary signal is the first token of the response: the authors check whether the output begins with "Yes" or "No." In cases where the response does not begin with either word (a small minority — the paper reports that the first token is "Yes" or "No" 95.67% of the time for GPT-4 and 98.40% for ChatGPT), the authors manually determine the answer conveyed by the model. The paper does not provide detailed criteria for this manual determination, but the low frequency of non-standard answer formats (1.6–4.3% of cases) means these edge cases have minimal impact on aggregate statistics.

**What happens when the model is correct.** If the model produces the correct yes/no answer, the instance is counted as a success and no further analysis is performed. The paper's focus is entirely on the incorrect cases — it is in these cases that hallucination snowballing can occur. The paper does not analyze whether correct answers also contain hallucinations in their justifications (e.g., correct answer but fabricated supporting reasoning). This is a deliberate scope limitation: snowballing, by definition, involves an initial incorrect commitment leading to further incorrect claims. If the initial answer is correct, the coherence pressure works in favor of factuality rather than against it, though fabricated supporting reasoning remains theoretically possible.

**What happens when the model is incorrect.** Every incorrect answer in the paper's experiments is accompanied by a justification — the model never simply says "No" or "Yes" without elaboration. This is a property of the instruction-tuned models being studied: they have been trained to provide explanatory responses. The justification text becomes the input to the claim extraction module. The paper reports error rates (percentage of questions answered incorrectly) for each model on each dataset (Table 6): ChatGPT averages 60.13% error across tasks (82.0% on graph connectivity, 67.8% on primality, 30.6% on senator search); GPT-4 averages 83.40% error (88.4% on graph connectivity, 74.8% on primality, 87.0% on senator search). The finding that GPT-4 performs *worse* than ChatGPT on these tasks is noted as counterintuitive given GPT-4's generally superior benchmark performance.

#### Claim Extraction: Graph Connectivity

Graph connectivity extraction is the most manual of the three domains, requiring human inspection of each incorrect model output. For each sample where the model incorrectly answers "Yes" (claiming a series of connecting flights exists), the authors **manually examine the output** to extract the list of flights the model proposes as the route. They then identify which flight or flights in this route are invalid — either flights between cities that are not connected by any of the 12 listed flights, or discontinuities where consecutive flights don't share a city (e.g., the model claims a flight from A to B and then from C to D, but B ≠ C, so the route is not connected).

The extraction produces one or more specific claims of the form "There is a flight from City X to City Y." Each such claim becomes a verification target. The manual extraction process is feasible because the 500-instance dataset contains a manageable number of incorrect answers (410 for ChatGPT, 442 for GPT-4), and the route format is highly structured — models typically output numbered steps listing city pairs.

**Why manual extraction for graph connectivity.** The paper does not automate graph connectivity claim extraction, unlike the other two domains where ChatGPT is used as an extractor. This is likely because the graph connectivity justifications are more structured (explicit flight lists) and thus easier for humans to parse, and because writing an automatic extractor that handles the variety of phrasings (cities named by single letters, potential formatting variations) would require non-trivial engineering. The manual approach trades scalability for reliability at the modest scale of 500 instances.

---

#### Claim Extraction: Primality Testing

Primality testing extraction is semi-automated. For each sample where the model incorrectly answers that the number is not prime, the justification text typically contains a factorization in the form "$a \times b = n$" or "$n$ can be factored into $a \times b$." The paper extracts these factors using a two-step procedure:

**Step 1: Factor extraction via ChatGPT.** The model's original output (containing the incorrect "No" answer and its justification) is placed in a new prompt context along with the instruction: "What are the factors proposed in the above text? List them out." This prompt is sent to ChatGPT (not GPT-4, even for GPT-4 outputs — the paper uses ChatGPT "for its fast inference speed"). The extraction uses a **one-shot demonstration**: the prompt includes a single example of an output text and the expected extracted factor list, teaching ChatGPT the extraction format. The paper manually checked 30 extracted examples and found that ChatGPT's extraction was perfectly accurate — it always correctly identified and listed the factors from the original text.

**Step 2: Individual factor verification preparation.** Once the factors are extracted, each factor becomes a separate verification target. For a claim that $n = a \times b$, two verification questions are generated: "Is $n$ divisible by $a$? Answer with either Yes or No." and "Is $n$ divisible by $b$? Answer with either Yes or No." If the model listed more than two factors (e.g., a factorization into three or more numbers, or alternative factorizations), each is verified separately. The verification succeeds (the hallucination is "snowballed") if the model answers "No" to at least one factor question — i.e., if it recognizes that at least one of its proposed factors does not actually divide the number.

---

#### Claim Extraction: Senator Search

Senator search extraction is also semi-automated. For each sample where the model incorrectly answers "Yes" (claiming a senator exists who satisfies both constraints), the justification text typically names a specific senator and provides biographical details.

**Step 1: Senator name extraction via ChatGPT.** The model's original output is placed in a new prompt with the instruction: "What is the senator mentioned in the above text? Just give the name." As with primality testing, ChatGPT performs the extraction with one-shot demonstration. The authors manually checked 30 examples and found perfect extraction accuracy. The extracted output is the senator's name, e.g., "John P. Hale."

**Step 2: Verification question generation.** Two verification questions are generated for each extracted senator:
1. "Has [Senator Name] represented the state of [state $x$] as US senator?" (verifying the state representation claim)
2. "Was [Senator Name]'s alma mater [college $y$]?" (verifying the college attendance claim)

At least one of these claims must be false (since the state-college pair was verified to have no senator satisfying both), and often the model's own verification reveals the error. For instance, in the running example from Table 1, GPT-4 named John P. Hale as a senator from New Hampshire who attended University of Pennsylvania. Verification revealed that Hale did represent New Hampshire but his alma mater was Bowdoin College, not University of Pennsylvania. The model correctly acknowledged this in the verification session, confirming the snowballed nature of the original hallucination.

**A subtlety about partial correctness.** In cases like the John P. Hale example where one claim is true (state representation) and one is false (alma mater), the model's original error was claiming the conjunction — that a single senator satisfied both constraints. The verification procedure correctly catches this by testing both conjuncts independently. If the model correctly identifies at least one conjunct as false, the original claim (the conjunction) is refuted, and the hallucination counts as snowballed. The paper does not distinguish between cases where the model gets one versus both conjuncts right in the original output — any incorrect justification that the model can partially or fully refute in verification qualifies.

---

#### Verification Protocol: Core Measurement Apparatus

The verification protocol is the paper's central methodological innovation. It operationalizes the definition of "snowballed hallucination": a hallucination is snowballed if and only if the model, when presented with the exact same factual claim in a separate session (no history of the original interaction), correctly identifies it as false. This section describes the protocol in detail across all three domains.

**The separate session requirement.** This is the most critical design choice in the protocol. Verification questions are always presented in a **completely new conversation session** with no access to the history of the original QA interaction. This is not a minor implementation detail — it is the operationalization of the paper's central theoretical claim. The hypothesis is that the model "knows" the correct fact but cannot access that knowledge when the original erroneous context is present. The verification session tests what the model can access when the error-inducing context (the premature "No" or "Yes" commitment plus the surrounding justification) is absent. If the model answers verification questions correctly in the new session, it demonstrates that the knowledge exists in the model and is retrievable under the right conditions. If verification were performed in the same session, the original context would still be present and could continue to exert coherence pressure, confounding the measurement.

**Verification question format.** Verification questions are designed to be unambiguous yes/no questions with deterministic correct answers. The paper uses domain-specific templates:

- **Primality Testing:** "Is $n$ divisible by $a$? Answer with either Yes or No." The instruction to answer with "either Yes or No" constrains the model's response format, making answer classification trivial — the paper simply checks whether the response begins with "Yes" or "No." For a prime $n$, the correct answer to any factor $a < n$ (other than 1 and $n$ itself) is "No."

- **Graph Connectivity:** The full flight information is provided again in the prompt (identical to the original context), followed by "Based on the above flight information, is City $X$ to City $Y$ a valid flight?" The flight information is repeated so the model has the ground truth available — the verification is testing whether the model can correctly check a specific flight claim against the provided facts, not whether it can recall the flight information from memory. The correct answer for an invalid flight is "No."

- **Senator Search:** "Has [Senator Name] represented the state of [state $x$] as US senator?" and "Was [Senator Name]'s alma mater [college $y$]?" The correct answers depend on the actual biographical facts about the named senator. The paper evaluates these manually or through the model's factual knowledge — the key point is that at least one verification question will have a correct answer of "No" for the senator search dataset, since the state-college pair was verified to have no senator satisfying both constraints.

**Verification success criterion.** The verification is considered successful (confirming that the original hallucination was snowballed) if the model correctly identifies the extracted claim as false — i.e., if the model answers "No" to a verification question whose correct answer is "No." For primality testing, this means answering "No" to at least one divisibility question (recognizing that a proposed factor does not divide the number). For graph connectivity, this means answering "No" to at least one invalid flight claim (or correctly identifying a discontinuity in the route). For senator search, this means answering "No" to at least one of the two senator verification questions.

**Verification failure and its interpretation.** If the model answers verification questions incorrectly — e.g., if it insists that 9677 is divisible by 13 even when asked in isolation — the original hallucination is **not counted as snowballed**. This is a crucial boundary in the paper's definitions. A non-snowballed hallucination represents a genuine knowledge gap: the model doesn't know the fact even when contextual pressure is absent. The paper's claim is not that all hallucinations are snowballed, but rather that a substantial fraction (67% for ChatGPT, 87% for GPT-4) are. The remaining fraction (33% for ChatGPT, 13% for GPT-4) represents cases where the model's false justification reflects an actual lack of knowledge — the model genuinely "believes" the false claim and cannot correct it even in isolation. An example of a non-snowballed hallucination is provided in Appendix Table 12: GPT-4 claims Willis Smith attended Dartmouth College, and when verified in isolation, it continues to assert this false fact, indicating a genuine knowledge error rather than a context-induced one.

**Quantitative results of verification (Tables 7, 6).** The paper reports the number of snowballed hallucinations as a fraction of total hallucinations (incorrect answers with extractable false justifications). For ChatGPT (Table 7): 396 out of 410 graph connectivity hallucinations are snowballed (96.6%), 125 out of 339 primality hallucinations are snowballed (36.9%), and 98 out of 153 senator search hallucinations are snowballed (68.6%), for an average of 67.37%. For GPT-4: 417 out of 442 graph connectivity hallucinations are snowballed (94.3%), 346 out of 374 primality hallucinations are snowballed (92.5%), and 323 out of 435 senator search hallucinations are snowballed (74.3%), for an average of 87.03%.

Several patterns are notable. Graph connectivity snowballing rates are extremely high for both models (>94%), suggesting that when the model fabricates flight connections, it almost always "knows" at some level that those flights don't exist — the fabrication is driven entirely by coherence pressure. Primality testing shows the largest gap between models: ChatGPT recognizes only 36.9% of its factor hallucinations as false, while GPT-4 recognizes 92.5%. This is consistent with GPT-4 having stronger arithmetic capabilities — it "knows" the divisibility facts more reliably, but still generates false factorizations when committed to a wrong primality judgment. Senator search snowballing rates are intermediate, with GPT-4 moderately outperforming ChatGPT (74.3% vs. 68.6%).

**What these numbers mean substantively.** The 67% and 87% figures are the paper's headline results. They quantify the prevalence of a failure mode where the model's output contradicts its own accessible knowledge. For GPT-4, the vast majority (87%) of the false factual claims it generates in justification of wrong answers are claims that it can separately identify as false. This is the empirical basis for the paper's central claim that hallucinations are not merely knowledge gaps — for GPT-4, fewer than 13% of the false justification claims represent genuine knowledge errors; the remaining 87% are context-induced, coherence-driven fabrications that the model would not produce in a different context.

#### Inference-Time Intervention Experiments

Section 4 of the paper tests two categories of interventions to see whether hallucination snowballing can be prevented without changing the underlying model.

**Chain-of-thought prompting (Section 4.1).** The paper appends "Let's think step by step" to the end of each original question and re-runs the primary QA protocol. This prompt engineering strategy, drawn from Kojima et al. (2023), is designed to prevent the "initial committal" condition: by instructing the model to reason before answering, the hope is that the model will delay its yes/no commitment until after it has performed the necessary multi-step computation, thus avoiding the premature token that triggers snowballing.

The results (Figure 3, Tables 8 and 9) show that chain-of-thought prompting dramatically reduces overall error rates. GPT-4's average error rate drops from 83.40% to 3.87% across tasks; ChatGPT's drops from 60.13% to 9.40%. The senator search task is solved perfectly by both models (0% error). Primality testing errors drop to 0.4% for ChatGPT and 7.4% for GPT-4. Graph connectivity errors drop to 27.8% for ChatGPT and 4.2% for GPT-4.

However, the paper's key finding is about the **residual errors**. When the model still gets the answer wrong even with step-by-step prompting, snowballed hallucinations remain extremely prevalent. ChatGPT's snowballed hallucination rate on residual errors averages 44.25% (driven by graph connectivity, where 123 out of 139 residual errors, or 88.5%, involve snowballing). GPT-4's snowballed hallucination rate on residual errors averages 94.90% (95.2% on graph connectivity, 94.6% on primality testing). The paper provides a concrete example from the graph connectivity task where ChatGPT, in its step-by-step reasoning, incorrectly states that there are "three options" at a step where only two exist, then uses the hallucinated third option ("a flight from city E to city C") in subsequent reasoning. The model can verify in a separate session that this flight does not exist.

The interpretation is nuanced: chain-of-thought prompting is extremely effective at preventing the *initial* committal error — by reasoning first, the model usually arrives at the correct answer. But it does not eliminate the underlying snowballing mechanism. If an error occurs anywhere in the reasoning chain (even an intermediate step), that error gets incorporated into the context and exerts the same coherence pressure that drives further fabrication. The snowball just starts at an intermediate reasoning step rather than at the final answer.

**Temperature variation (Section 4.2, Figure 4, Tables 10 and 11).** The paper tests decoding at temperatures 0.0 (the default greedy setting), 0.6, and 0.9. The hypothesis (implicitly) is that higher temperature might cause the model to sample less-committal initial tokens, delaying the yes/no commitment and reducing snowballing. The results show essentially no effect: error rates and snowballed hallucination rates remain similarly high across all temperature settings for both models. For ChatGPT, the average snowballed hallucination rate ranges from 67.37% (t=0.0) to 66.77% (t=0.9). For GPT-4, it ranges from 87.03% (t=0.0) to 84.87% (t=0.9). These variations are negligible relative to the baseline rates, indicating that temperature alone cannot disrupt the "initial committal" pattern that the instruction-tuned models have learned from their training data — the models are simply too strongly conditioned to produce yes/no answers as their first token in these question formats.

**Top-k and nucleus sampling (mentioned but not tested).** The paper briefly argues that top-k sampling and nucleus sampling (Holtzman et al., 2020) would likely not help, since these methods narrow the set of tokens considered for generation, which can only increase the probability that the model commits to an answer immediately (by concentrating probability mass on the highest-likelihood tokens, which for yes/no questions will be "Yes" and "No"). This is a theoretical argument, not an empirical finding — the OpenAI API's limitations prevented testing these decoding strategies.

**Beam search (mentioned but not tested).** The paper discusses beam search as a potentially promising mitigation that cannot be tested due to API constraints. The reasoning is that if the model maintained multiple candidate sequences (a beam) rather than a single greedy path, some beams might not commit to an answer immediately (or might commit to the correct answer), and their continuations (with correct subsequent reasoning) might eventually receive higher probability than beams that committed incorrectly and then produced erroneous justifications. If so, beam search could naturally select the path that avoided the initial error, preventing snowballing entirely. The paper presents this as a hypothesis that remains to be tested when model access permits.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Synthetic, controlled datasets over natural benchmarks:** Natural QA datasets (like TruthfulQA or Natural Questions) do not guarantee that incorrect answers will produce extractable, verifiable supporting claims. The synthetic datasets allow the paper to systematically measure snowballing at scale by ensuring the structure of incorrect justifications is predictable and falsifiable.

- **Uniform ground-truth labels per dataset:** This ensures that incorrect answers always take the same form (false positives for senator search and graph connectivity, false negatives for primality testing), making the extraction heuristics consistent across all instances.

- **Primes as the positive class for primality testing:** If composites were used (correct answer "No"), incorrect "Yes" answers would assert primality — a claim with no extractable positive evidence to verify. Using primes forces incorrect answers to produce explicit factorizations, each individually verifiable.

- **Separate verification sessions:** The entire operational definition of "snowballed hallucination" depends on demonstrating that the model can access correct knowledge when the error-inducing context is absent. Same-session verification would confound the measurement by leaving the original context available.

- **ChatGPT as the extraction tool for primality and senator search:** Using one model to extract claims from another model's output introduces a potential source of error (the extractor could hallucinate). The paper mitigates this through manual spot-checking (30 examples per domain) and uses ChatGPT for extraction speed, not for a claim about extractor quality.

- **Graph connectivity claims extracted manually:** The highly structured format (numbered flight lists) makes manual extraction reliable at the dataset scale (under 500 incorrect answers) and avoids introducing extractor model errors.

- **Greedy decoding (temperature 0) for primary experiments:** Deterministic generation ensures reproducibility and isolates the phenomenon from stochastic variation, establishing a clean baseline against which the temperature experiments in Section 4.2 can be compared.

- **"Let's think step by step" as the only prompt intervention tested:** This is the most well-known and effective zero-shot reasoning prompt at the time, making it the natural first candidate for testing whether prompt engineering can prevent initial committal. The paper does not exhaustively search over prompt formats — it tests one representative intervention and analyzes its effect on the snowballing mechanism specifically.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Hallucination Snowballing as a Third Category of Hallucination — Not Knowledge Gap, Not Hallucination-Prone Prompt, but Self-Induced Contextual Error

Prior work on LM hallucinations operated within a two-category framework. The dominant category was **knowledge-gap hallucinations**: the model fabricates a fact because it doesn't "know" the truth, motivating retrieval-augmented generation (Lewis et al., 2020; Shuster et al., 2021; Peng et al., 2023) as the natural fix. A secondary category was **presupposition-triggered hallucinations**: the model is misled by a false premise in the user's prompt, as documented in Kim et al. (2021, 2022) with questions like "Which linguist invented the lightbulb?" and in Lin et al. (2022) with TruthfulQA's misleading questions. In both cases, the source of error is *external* — either missing from the model's training or injected by the user.

This paper introduces a **third, qualitatively distinct category**: hallucinations that arise from the model's *own prior output*, not from missing knowledge or user-supplied falsehoods. The defining example (Figure 1, Table 1) makes the distinction concrete: GPT-4 claims 13 × 745 = 9677 to justify "9677 is not prime," then separately acknowledges 9677 is not divisible by 13 when asked in isolation. This is not a knowledge gap — the model demonstrably "knows" that 13 doesn't divide 9677. It is not a presupposition trap — the user's question "Is 9677 a prime number?" is innocent and contains no false premise. The error is entirely **self-induced**: the model's premature "No" token created the context in which the factorization had to be generated for coherence, overriding the model's accessible knowledge.

This reframing matters because it implies that even perfect retrieval-augmented generation (solving the knowledge-gap problem) and careful prompt sanitization (solving the presupposition problem) would leave this third category untouched. If you gave GPT-4 a perfect knowledge base containing the primality status and factorizations of all integers, it would still be forced by its own "No" token to contradict that knowledge base — generating a factorization that the retrieved facts would flag as false. The coherence pressure would simply select a different false factorization, or the contradiction between the model's output and the retrieved facts would manifest as incoherent text. This means the hallucination problem is not fully reducible to either better knowledge or better prompts; it requires mechanisms for the model to *revise or backtrack* from its own premature commitments, which current autoregressive architectures do not support natively.

The paper's controlled-dataset methodology is itself innovative in service of isolating this category. By constructing tasks where (a) incorrect answers predictably produce specific falsifiable claims, and (b) those claims can be verified in isolation in separate sessions, the paper creates a clean experimental separation between "what the model knows" and "what the model says under self-induced contextual pressure." The 67% and 87% snowballing rates (for ChatGPT and GPT-4 respectively, Table 7) quantify, for the first time, how much of model hallucination falls into this self-induced category versus genuine knowledge gaps. For GPT-4, the finding that 87% of its false justification claims are self-contradictory (verifiable as false by the model itself) fundamentally recasts the hallucination problem: for this model, the primary failure mode is not ignorance but **inability to access known facts under coherence pressure**.

### Innovation 2: The Coherence-Factuality Conflict as a Formal Constraint from Training Objectives, Not Just an Empirical Observation

Many papers had observed in passing that LMs sometimes produce fluent-but-incorrect text. The standard diagnosis was that fluency is easier to learn from next-token prediction than factuality, so models master it first and hallucination is a residual training deficit. This paper elevates the fluency-vs-factuality tradeoff from an empirical training artifact to a **structural consequence of the autoregressive training objective interacting with answer-format conventions**.

The paper's theoretical framing in Section 2 identifies two specific conditions — **initial committal** and **inherent sequentiality** — that are each individually benign but produce systematic hallucination when combined. What makes this framing innovative is that it doesn't appeal to vague notions like "models optimize for plausibility over truth." Instead, it grounds the failure in the precise mechanics: instruction-tuned LMs are trained to produce yes/no answers as their first token (initial committal), and the tasks chosen are provably outside $\text{TC}^0$ (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023), meaning the transformer cannot solve them in the single forward pass that generates that first token (inherent sequentiality). The first token is therefore a **guess** — not a reasoned conclusion — made at a point when the model has insufficient computational depth to be correct. Once that guess enters the context window, the training objective's pressure for coherence (maximizing $P(\text{next token} \mid \text{context containing the guess})$) forces all subsequent tokens to be consistent with the guess, regardless of their factual accuracy.

This is not a failure of optimization or insufficient scale — it is a **logical consequence** of the interaction between the autoregressive training objective (which optimizes for coherence with prior context) and the answer format convention (which places the answer before the reasoning). A perfectly optimized infinite-scale LM trained with next-token prediction on English text would *still* exhibit snowballing under these conditions, because coherence with the already-generated "No" token would be the correct optimization target. The training objective does not distinguish between coherence-with-truth and coherence-with-falsehood — and in the snowballing scenario, it actively rewards coherence-with-falsehood over truth-telling. The model is doing exactly what it was trained to do, and that is precisely the problem.

This framing has significant theoretical implications. It suggests that the hallucination problem for autoregressive LMs is not a temporary limitation that will vanish with more data or parameters, but a **fundamental architectural constraint** that must be addressed through mechanisms beyond next-token prediction — backtracking, revision, or architectures that separate answer-generation from reasoning-generation. The paper's observation that chain-of-thought prompting *reduces but does not eliminate* snowballing (Section 4.1) reinforces this point: COT delays the initial committal but does not remove the autoregressive coherence pressure, so any error anywhere in the chain still snowballs forward. The 94.9% snowballing rate on GPT-4's residual COT errors (Table 9) demonstrates that shifting the committal point does not solve the underlying problem — it just moves it to a later step in the generation.

### Innovation 3: Self-Knowledge as a Diagnostic Lens — What the Model "Knows" Is Not What It Accesses Under Contextual Pressure

The verification-through-separate-sessions methodology (Section 3.4, detailed in 3.4) is not merely an experimental protocol — it is a **diagnostic instrument** that operationalizes a distinction the field had previously lacked: the difference between knowledge *presence* in the model (what the model can retrieve in a neutral context) and knowledge *accessibility* (what the model can actually use in a specific generative context). Before this paper, hallucination evaluations treated these as equivalent. If a model generated a false statement, the implicit diagnosis was that the model lacked that knowledge. If it generated a true statement, the model "knew" the fact.

This paper demonstrates that the mapping between knowledge presence and knowledge accessibility is **context-dependent and fragile**. The model demonstrably possesses the knowledge that "13 does not divide 9677" — it can produce this fact correctly in a standalone query (Table 4). But when that fact would require contradicting the model's own prior "No" token, the knowledge becomes inaccessible — it is overridden by the coherence objective. The model *has* the knowledge but cannot *use* it.

This is a conceptual advance that reframes the hallucination problem from "how do we put more knowledge into models?" to "how do we ensure models can access the knowledge they already have, even when it conflicts with their own prior output?" The 67% vs. 87% difference between ChatGPT and GPT-4 (Table 7) is informative here: GPT-4 "knows more" (it can verify 87% of its false claims, vs. ChatGPT's 67%), but this superior knowledge doesn't prevent it from making those false claims in the first place — GPT-4's error rate is actually *higher* than ChatGPT's (83.40% vs. 60.13%, Table 6). More knowledge actually correlates with *worse* primary-task performance on these datasets. The standard knowledge-gap framework cannot explain this: if hallucinations are caused by not knowing, then a model that knows more should hallucinate less. The snowballing framework can: GPT-4's superior knowledge is rendered impotent by the coherence pressure from its premature first-token commitment, and the same instruction-tuning that makes GPT-4 more fluent and helpful may also make it more strongly conditioned to commit to an answer before reasoning.

This diagnostic lens connects to broader questions about LM evaluation. If a model's knowledge is contextually gated — accessible in some prompts but not in others — then single-prompt accuracy metrics systematically underestimate the model's true knowledge. The paper shows that seemingly minor contextual factors (the presence or absence of the model's own prior output) can toggle knowledge access from "correctly retrieved" to "confidently contradicted." This has implications for how the field evaluates model capabilities and for how self-correction mechanisms should be designed (they must isolate the problematic context, not just append correction instructions to it).

### Innovation 4: A Controlled Methodology for Provoking and Measuring Self-Contradictory Generation

The paper did not invent the idea that LMs can contradict themselves. Researchers had observed inconsistent answers across different prompts or conversation turns (Lin et al., 2022 documented prompt-sensitivity; the broader consistency literature had established that LMs can give different answers to semantically equivalent questions). What this paper contributes is a **methodology for systematically producing and measuring self-contradiction** on demand, in a controlled experimental setting.

The key design choice — constructing datasets where incorrect answers predictably produce specific, extractable, falsifiable claims — transforms hallucination from a qualitative phenomenon (you occasionally notice the model fabricating things) to a **quantifiable, replicable experimental variable**. Prior work on hallucinations relied heavily on human annotation (Maynez et al., 2020) or post-hoc analysis of model outputs, which made systematic measurement expensive and limited comparisons across models and settings. The three-dataset framework in this paper — primality testing, senator search, graph connectivity — provides a reusable template: choose a domain where false answers produce positive claims (factorizations, named entities, explicit connections), construct the dataset so all correct answers are uniform (simplifying ground-truth verification), and use separate-session verification to distinguish snowballed from knowledge-gap hallucinations.

This methodology is significant beyond the specific findings of this paper because it is **replicable and extensible**. Any researcher with API access can reproduce the experiments on new models (as newer GPT versions are released), test new prompt formats, or extend the approach to new domains that satisfy the structural requirements (false answers produce extractable positive claims). The paper provides its data and code, but the conceptual framework — not just the specific implementation — is the transferable contribution. The three domains themselves were chosen to demonstrate generality across different reasoning types (arithmetic in primality, factual retrieval in senator search, multi-hop reasoning in graph connectivity), suggesting the phenomenon is not domain-specific.

The methodology also introduces a **falsifiable operational definition** of "the model knows this is wrong," which the field had lacked. "Knowledge" in LMs is notoriously difficult to define and measure. This paper doesn't attempt a general definition; instead, it proposes a specific behavioral criterion: if the model asserts claim X in context A and asserts not-X in a separate neutral context B, then the model's knowledge (operationalized as "what it generates in context B") contradicts its output (what it generates in context A). This is not a claim about internal representations or "beliefs" — it is a purely behavioral operationalization that any researcher can apply without special model access. The fact that this criterion captures something meaningful is evidenced by the high quantitative rates: for GPT-4, the behavioral self-contradiction criterion identifies 87% of all false justification claims as cases where the model can separately access the correct fact. This is a substantial empirical finding that validates the operationalization as capturing a real phenomenon rather than measurement noise.

The methodology's limitation — it requires tasks where false claims are positive and individually verifiable — is explicitly acknowledged by the paper's scope. But within that scope, it provides the cleanest measurement of context-induced hallucination in the literature at the time of publication, and the conceptual distinction between knowledge presence and knowledge accessibility that the methodology operationalizes has influenced subsequent work on model self-consistency and self-correction.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper constructs three synthetic QA datasets of 500 questions each: Primality Testing (500 primes randomly selected from 1,000–20,000, correct answer always "Yes"), Senator Search (500 state-college pairs where no U.S. senator satisfies both constraints, correct answer always "No"), and Graph Connectivity (500 instances sharing the same 14-node, 12-edge directed graph structure with randomized node labels, asking whether a path exists between two cities in disconnected subgraphs, correct answer always "No"). These are not standard benchmarks — they are purpose-built scientific instruments designed so that incorrect answers predictably produce specific, extractable, falsifiable supporting claims. The datasets are used entirely for evaluation; no training or fine-tuning is performed.

- **Base models.** All experiments use ChatGPT (`gpt-3.5-turbo`) and GPT-4, accessed via the OpenAI API with greedy decoding (temperature 0) for primary experiments. The models are chosen for their state-of-the-art performance and widespread deployment, making the findings relevant to real-world usage. The paper explicitly notes that API access limitations prevent fine-tuning, accessing output probability distributions, or using beam search — constraints that shape which experiments could be run.

- **Metrics.** The paper reports two primary metrics. **Error rate**: the percentage of 500 questions in each dataset that the model answers incorrectly, determined by checking whether the model's yes/no answer matches the ground-truth label (with manual adjudication for the 1.6–4.3% of cases where the model's first token is not "Yes" or "No"). **Snowballed hallucination rate**: the percentage of incorrect answers (where the model produced an extractable false justification claim) for which the model, in a separate session, correctly identifies that extracted claim as false. Formally, a hallucination is "snowballed" iff verification succeeds — i.e., the model rejects its own earlier claim when presented without the original error-inducing context. The snowballed hallucination rate is computed as the number of snowballed hallucinations divided by the number of total hallucinations (incorrect answers with verifiable justifications), reported per-dataset and as a macro-average across datasets.

- **Baselines.** The paper does not compare against prior hallucination mitigation methods (§5 acknowledges retrieval-augmented generation and self-correction as related approaches, but they are not evaluated as baselines). The experimental design instead establishes **internal baselines**: the model's own accuracy on the primary QA task serves as the baseline against which verification performance is measured. The key comparison is not "this method vs. that method" but rather "what the model says under coherence pressure vs. what the model says in isolation" — the model serves as its own control. The intervention experiments in §4 use the original greedy-decoding results from §3 as the baseline against which chain-of-thought prompting and temperature variation are compared.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All generations use greedy decoding (temperature 0) unless otherwise specified for the temperature experiments at 0.6 and 0.9. Each primary QA response consumes one API call. Each verification question (one per extracted factor for primality, two per extracted senator, one per invalid flight for graph connectivity) consumes one additional API call in a separate session. The paper does not report total token counts or API costs, focusing instead on behavioral metrics. The generation budget is not varied parametrically (no "best-of-N" or multiple samples per question) — each question receives a single deterministic response per experimental condition.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or confidence intervals. The datasets are fixed at 500 questions each, and all reported numbers are exact counts and percentages on these fixed test sets. The 30-example manual spot-checks for claim extraction accuracy (for primality factor extraction and senator name extraction) serve as validation of the automated extraction pipeline rather than as statistical validation of the primary results. The absence of statistical testing means the reported differences between ChatGPT and GPT-4, and between different temperature settings, are presented as raw observations without formal significance assessment.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Primary QA Accuracy: Models Commit Early and Often Err

The error rates (percentage of questions answered incorrectly) establish the baseline against which snowballing is measured. Table 6 reports the complete breakdown:

| Task | ChatGPT Error Rate | GPT-4 Error Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Graph Connectivity | 410/500 (82.0%) | 442/500 (88.4%) |
| Primality Testing | 339/500 (67.8%) | 374/500 (74.8%) |
| Senator Search | 153/500 (30.6%) | 435/500 (87.0%) |
| **Average** | **60.13%** | **83.40%** |

GPT-4 performs substantially worse than ChatGPT across all three tasks, with the gap ranging from 7.0 percentage points on primality testing to 56.4 points on senator search. The average error rate difference (83.40% vs. 60.13%) is large — GPT-4 is incorrect on roughly 5 out of 6 questions, while ChatGPT is incorrect on roughly 3 out of 5. This is a striking finding given GPT-4's generally superior performance on standard benchmarks (OpenAI, 2023), and the paper notes it as counterintuitive. The result aligns with the theoretical framework from Section 2: GPT-4's stronger instruction-tuning may make it more strongly conditioned to commit to a yes/no answer as its first token, producing the systematic errors the datasets are designed to capture.

The paper reports that the first token is "Yes" or "No" 95.67% of the time for GPT-4 and 98.40% for ChatGPT (Section 2), confirming the "initial committal" condition across both models. The remaining 1.6–4.3% of responses are manually adjudicated, with the paper noting that even in these cases, the model typically commits to an answer within the first few tokens.

#### Snowballed Hallucination Detection: Models Recognize Most of Their Own False Claims

The central measurement is the percentage of incorrect justifications that the model can separately identify as false. Table 7 reports:

| Task | ChatGPT Snowballed Rate | GPT-4 Snowballed Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Graph Connectivity | 396/410 (96.6%) | 417/442 (94.3%) |
| Primality Testing | 125/339 (36.9%) | 346/374 (92.5%) |
| Senator Search | 98/153 (68.6%) | 323/435 (74.3%) |
| **Average** | **67.37%** | **87.03%** |

Figure 2 visualizes these results as stacked bar charts, showing the proportion of the full dataset that is answered correctly (narrow top segments), the proportion that is hallucinated and detected as snowballed (middle patterned segments), and the proportion that is hallucinated but not detected (bottom solid segments).

Several patterns are notable. **Graph connectivity** snowballing rates are extremely high for both models (96.6% and 94.3%): when the model fabricates a flight connection to justify an incorrect "Yes" answer, it almost invariably "knows" at some level that the flight doesn't exist — the fabrication is driven almost entirely by coherence pressure rather than by the model genuinely believing the fabricated flight is real. This aligns with the task design: the flights are explicitly listed in the prompt, so the ground truth is literally present in the context window. The model is generating claims that directly contradict information it has just processed — a stark demonstration of coherence overriding factuality.

**Primality testing** shows the largest model divergence: ChatGPT recognizes only 36.9% of its false factorizations as incorrect, while GPT-4 recognizes 92.5%. This is consistent with GPT-4 having stronger arithmetic capabilities than ChatGPT — it more reliably "knows" divisibility facts — but the finding that GPT-4 still generates false factorizations at a 74.8% error rate on primality testing (Table 6) demonstrates that stronger factual knowledge does not prevent the initial snowball-triggering error. GPT-4 knows 92.5% of its false factorizations are wrong, yet it still produced them.

**Senator search** snowballing rates are intermediate for both models (68.6% and 74.3%). The verification procedure checks two separate factual claims per instance (state representation and college attendance), and a successful verification requires the model to correctly identify at least one false conjunct. The lower snowballing rates relative to graph connectivity may reflect that senator biographical facts are genuinely not known by the model in some cases (the 25.7–31.4% of hallucinations that are not snowballed represent actual knowledge gaps), or that the model sometimes doubles down on its false claims even in isolation — Appendix Table 12 provides an example of the latter, where GPT-4 insists Willis Smith attended Dartmouth College even when queried directly, indicating a genuine knowledge error rather than a context-induced one.

The **macro-averaged 67.37% and 87.03% figures** are the paper's headline quantitative results. They establish that for ChatGPT, roughly two-thirds of the false factual claims generated in justification of wrong answers are claims the model can separately identify as false; for GPT-4, this rises to roughly seven-eighths. Phrased inversely: only 12.97% of GPT-4's false justification claims represent genuine knowledge gaps where the model cannot access the correct fact even in isolation. The remaining 87.03% are self-contradictory — the model possesses the knowledge but generates claims that contradict it under coherence pressure.

#### Chain-of-Thought Prompting: Dramatic Accuracy Gains, Residual Snowballing Persists

Section 4.1 tests appending "Let's think step by step" to each question. The results (Figure 3, Tables 8 and 9) show a dramatic improvement in primary QA accuracy:

**Error rates with chain-of-thought (Table 8):**

| Task | ChatGPT Error Rate | GPT-4 Error Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Graph Connectivity | 139/500 (27.8%) | 21/500 (4.2%) |
| Primality Testing | 2/500 (0.4%) | 37/500 (7.4%) |
| Senator Search | 0/500 (0.0%) | 0/500 (0.0%) |
| **Average** | **9.40%** | **3.87%** |

Compared to the original prompt error rates (60.13% and 83.40%), chain-of-thought prompting reduces errors by roughly 6.4× for ChatGPT and 21.5× for GPT-4. Senator Search is solved perfectly by both models. Primality Testing is nearly solved by ChatGPT (0.4% error). Graph Connectivity remains the hardest task, with ChatGPT still erring on 27.8% of instances.

**Snowballed hallucination rates on residual CoT errors (Table 9):**

| Task | ChatGPT Snowballed Rate | GPT-4 Snowballed Rate |
|---|---|---|
| Graph Connectivity | 123/139 (88.5%) | 20/21 (95.2%) |
| Primality Testing | 0/2 (0.0%) | 35/37 (94.6%) |
| Senator Search | 0/0 (N/A) | 0/0 (N/A) |
| **Average (excl. N/A)** | **44.25%** | **94.90%** |

Figure 3 visualizes these results as bars comparing the original prompt (left bar) to the step-by-step prompt (right bar) for each task-model combination, with the snowballed hallucination proportion shown as a hatched sub-segment of the error bar.

The key finding is about the **residual errors**. When the model still gets the answer wrong despite step-by-step reasoning, snowballed hallucinations remain extremely prevalent — 88.5% of ChatGPT's residual graph connectivity errors and 95.2% of GPT-4's involve snowballing. The paper provides a concrete example: in a graph connectivity step-by-step output, ChatGPT states "From city E, we have three options: a flight to city N, a flight to city B, or a flight to city C" when only two flights exist from city E. The hallucinated third option ("a flight to city E to city C") then influences subsequent reasoning. In a separate verification session, ChatGPT correctly confirms that E→C is not a valid flight — demonstrating it "knows" the fact but generated the false claim anyway. The snowball has simply moved from the final answer to an intermediate reasoning step.

ChatGPT's primality testing residual errors (0/2 snowballed, i.e., 0%) and the absence of senator search errors for both models (N/A) mean these cells contribute little to the average, but the pattern on graph connectivity — where residual errors are abundant and snowballing is near-universal — is consistent and striking. GPT-4's 94.90% average snowballed rate on residual CoT errors (driven by graph connectivity and primality testing) is essentially identical to its 87.03% rate without CoT, indicating that **chain-of-thought prompting does not meaningfully reduce the snowballing tendency conditional on an error occurring**. It reduces the probability of errors occurring at all, which indirectly reduces the total number of snowballed hallucinations, but the underlying mechanism — coherence pressure propagating a mistake forward — remains intact when errors do occur.

#### Temperature Variation: No Effect on Snowballing

Section 4.2 tests decoding at temperatures 0.6 and 0.9, comparing against the default temperature 0.0 (greedy) results. Figure 4 presents the results as side-by-side bar charts for each task-model combination at all three temperatures. Tables 10 and 11 provide exact error and snowballing counts.

**Error rates across temperatures (Table 10):**

ChatGPT's average error rate ranges from 60.13% (t=0.0) to 58.53% (t=0.6 and t=0.9), a variation of 1.6 percentage points. GPT-4's average ranges from 83.40% (t=0.0) to 81.67% (t=0.9), a variation of 1.73 points. Within individual tasks, the maximum variation is 6.8 points (GPT-4 senator search: 87.0% at t=0.0 vs. 84.6% at t=0.6 and t=0.9). These variations are small relative to the baseline error rates and show no consistent directional trend — there is no evidence that higher temperature systematically reduces errors.

**Snowballed hallucination rates across temperatures (Table 11):**

ChatGPT's average snowballed rate ranges from 67.37% (t=0.0) to 66.77% (t=0.6 and t=0.9). GPT-4's ranges from 87.03% (t=0.0) to 84.87% (t=0.9). The maximum variation within any task-model pair is 4.4 points (GPT-4 graph connectivity: 94.3% at t=0.0 vs. 91.1% at t=0.9). As with error rates, there is no consistent trend and no evidence that temperature meaningfully affects the snowballing phenomenon.

The paper interprets this null result through the lens of the "initial committal" mechanism: the tendency to generate "Yes" or "No" as the first token is so strongly ingrained in the instruction-tuned models that even high-temperature sampling (which spreads probability mass away from the most likely tokens) does not disrupt it. The model still commits to an answer in the first token, and the snowballing dynamic follows regardless of exactly which tokens are sampled for the subsequent justification.

#### Beam Search and Alternative Decoding Strategies: Not Empirically Tested

Section 4.2 discusses beam search, top-k sampling, and nucleus sampling as potential mitigations but provides no empirical results for any of these methods. The OpenAI API's limitations prevent testing beam search. The paper offers a theoretical argument for why beam search might help (maintaining multiple candidate sequences could allow paths that avoid the initial erroneous commitment to eventually achieve higher probability than paths that commit incorrectly and then produce flawed reasoning), but this remains an untested hypothesis. For top-k and nucleus sampling, the paper argues they would likely not help, since narrowing the set of considered tokens would only increase the probability of the model committing to an answer immediately — but this too is a theoretical claim without supporting experiments.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper's experimental design is relatively simple and does not include extensive ablation studies in the conventional sense (e.g., removing components of a system to measure their contribution). However, several elements of the experimental design function as implicit robustness checks:

- **Cross-domain consistency:** The snowballing phenomenon is demonstrated across three qualitatively different domains — arithmetic reasoning (primality testing), factual retrieval (senator search), and multi-hop logical reasoning (graph connectivity) — which serves as a robustness check against the possibility that snowballing is an artifact of one particular task format. The consistent finding of high snowballing rates across domains (Table 7: 36.9–96.6% for ChatGPT, 74.3–94.3% for GPT-4) supports the claim that the phenomenon is general rather than domain-specific.

- **Two-model comparison:** Testing both ChatGPT and GPT-4 provides a cross-model robustness check. The finding that both models exhibit substantial snowballing, despite GPT-4 being generally more capable on standard benchmarks, suggests the phenomenon is not specific to a particular model scale or training recipe. If anything, the more capable model (GPT-4) shows *higher* error rates on these tasks (Table 6) while simultaneously showing *higher* snowballing detection rates (Table 7) — a pattern that the paper interprets as evidence that stronger instruction-tuning amplifies both the initial committal tendency and the model's underlying factual knowledge.

- **Temperature invariance as a robustness check:** The temperature experiments (Tables 10 and 11) serve as a robustness check that the phenomenon is not an artifact of greedy decoding. The fact that snowballing rates are essentially unchanged at temperatures 0.6 and 0.9 indicates that the mechanism is robust to the stochasticity of the generation process — the model commits to an answer early regardless of sampling temperature.

- **Manual spot-checks on extraction:** The paper manually verifies 30 examples each for primality factor extraction and senator name extraction, finding perfect accuracy in both cases. This serves as a validation that the automated extraction pipeline (using ChatGPT with one-shot prompting) is not introducing systematic errors that would bias the snowballing rate measurements. However, 30 examples out of 339 (primality, ChatGPT) to 435 (senator search, GPT-4) is a small sample, and the paper does not report confidence intervals or worst-case error bounds for the extraction step.

- **Separate-session verification as a construct validity check:** The use of completely separate sessions for verification (Section 3.4) is itself a form of construct validity check. If verification were performed in the same session, the original error-inducing context would remain present and could continue to suppress correct knowledge, making it impossible to determine whether the model "knows" the fact but cannot access it under pressure, or simply doesn't know it. The separate-session design isolates "knowledge presence" from "knowledge accessibility," providing a cleaner measurement of whether the original hallucination was snowballed.

- **Verification failure examples as negative controls:** The paper reports cases where verification fails (the model does not recognize its own false claim as false), such as the Willis Smith example in Appendix Table 12. These cases serve as negative controls — they demonstrate that the verification protocol is capable of detecting non-snowballed hallucinations, and that the reported snowballing rates are not simply an artifact of the verification questions always being answerable by the model. The fact that verification *sometimes* fails (12.97% of GPT-4's hallucinations, 32.63% of ChatGPT's) provides evidence that the verification protocol is genuinely measuring knowledge presence rather than some artifact of the question format.

**A notable missing ablation: same-session vs. separate-session verification.** The paper does not report what happens if verification questions are asked in the *same* session as the original QA interaction (i.e., immediately after the model's incorrect justification, without resetting the conversation). This would be a direct test of the paper's central hypothesis — that the presence of the original error-inducing context suppresses correct knowledge. If same-session verification showed substantially lower detection rates than separate-session verification, it would provide strong evidence for the contextual suppression mechanism. If detection rates were similar, it would suggest the model simply doesn't access the knowledge regardless of context (a genuine knowledge gap rather than contextual override). This experiment would have been straightforward to run within the existing framework but is absent from the paper.

**Another notable missing ablation: varying the presence of the "Answer with either Yes or No" constraint.** The primality verification questions include the explicit instruction "Answer with either Yes or No." The paper does not test whether this constraint — which may itself influence the model's tendency to commit to an answer — affects verification accuracy. If the constraint increases the model's tendency to confidently assert an answer, it could inflate verification accuracy (the model might be more likely to say "No" correctly when instructed to give a binary answer) or, conceivably, deflate it.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "LMs often generate hallucinations that they immediately recognize as wrong when presented in isolation"

**What the experiments demonstrate.** The separate-session verification results (Table 7) directly support this claim. GPT-4 correctly identifies 87.03% of its own false justification claims as incorrect when those claims are presented in isolation; ChatGPT identifies 67.37%. These are the headline figures and are computed directly from the experimental protocol without complex modeling assumptions. The rates are computed as fractions of incorrect answers with extractable justifications, meaning they quantify the proportion of hallucinations that are self-contradictory in the specific sense defined by the paper.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate.** The phrase "immediately recognize as wrong" implies a temporal dimension that the experiments do not test — the model is not shown its own output and asked "is this wrong?" in real-time. The verification occurs in a separate session, after the fact, with the claim reformulated as a standalone question. Whether the model would "recognize" the error if asked to review its own output in the same session is not tested (the missing same-session ablation noted above). Additionally, the claim applies only to hallucinations that take the form of specific, extractable, verifiable factual claims in the three synthetic domains studied. The paper does not demonstrate that snowballing occurs in the more open-ended, less-structured hallucinations (e.g., fabricated citations, invented biographical details that aren't part of a simple yes/no verification, or reasoning errors that don't produce a discrete verifiable claim) that dominate real-world LM deployment concerns.

#### Claim 2: "Hallucinations are not necessarily reflective of a model's lack of 'knowledge,' but may be induced by an earlier hallucination for consistency"

**What the experiments demonstrate.** The paper's experimental design is explicitly built around this claim and provides strong evidence for it within the studied domains. The verification protocol establishes that for 67–87% of the false claims the models produce, the model *does* possess the knowledge to identify those claims as false — it simply doesn't access that knowledge during the original generation. The paper provides qualitative examples (Figure 1, Table 1, Tables 3–5) that make the causal sequence visible: a premature yes/no commitment, followed by a fabricated justification, followed by successful verification that the fabricated claim is false. The controlled design of the datasets — where the questions are innocuous and contain no false presuppositions — rules out the alternative explanation that the model is being misled by externally provided faulty context.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate.** The paper establishes correlation between initial incorrect commitment and subsequent false justification, but it does not experimentally manipulate the presence or absence of the initial commitment to demonstrate causation. A stronger causal design would be: take questions where the model answers incorrectly and produces a snowballed hallucination, then present the same question with a prefix that changes the initial commitment (e.g., "The correct answer is Yes. Explain why.") and observe whether the hallucination disappears. Without such an intervention, the causal claim — that the initial mistake *causes* the subsequent hallucinations through consistency pressure — rests on the theoretical argument from Section 2 rather than on direct experimental manipulation. The consistency-pressure mechanism is a plausible interpretation of the data, but alternative interpretations exist: for example, the model might have a general tendency to generate fluent but incorrect reasoning on these hard problems regardless of whether it committed to an answer first, and the separate-session verification might succeed simply because the isolated questions are easier (less contextual interference, simpler format) rather than because the original context actively suppressed correct knowledge.

#### Claim 3: "ChatGPT and GPT-4 can identify 67% and 87% of their own mistakes, respectively"

**What the experiments demonstrate.** The numbers are directly from Table 7 and are computed as described. The "mistakes" referred to are specifically the false factual claims embedded in justifications for incorrect answers — factorizations, senator biographical claims, and flight connections — not all mistakes of any kind. The verification protocol correctly identifies the subset of these claims that the model can self-correct when the claim is isolated.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate / qualifications needed.** The claim is stated at a level of generality ("their own mistakes") that could be misread as applying to all model errors. In reality, the 67% and 87% figures apply only to the specific types of supporting factual claims in the three synthetic domains. The paper does not measure how often the models can identify other types of mistakes — logical errors, subtly incorrect reasoning steps, answers that are wrong but not accompanied by extractable false claims, or hallucinations in open-ended generation. The 67% and 87% figures should be understood as domain-specific measurements within a controlled experimental paradigm, not as estimates of the model's general self-error-detection capability. The substantial variation across domains (36.9% to 96.6% for ChatGPT, 74.3% to 94.3% for GPT-4) reinforces this point — the self-detection rate is highly task-dependent.

#### Claim 4: "Prompting with 'Let's think step by step' still results in snowballed hallucinations in 95% of cases where the model fails to answer correctly"

**What the experiments demonstrate.** This is supported by Table 9, which shows GPT-4's snowballed hallucination rate averages 94.90% on residual chain-of-thought errors. The paper provides a qualitative example (the graph connectivity case where ChatGPT hallucinates a non-existent third flight option in its step-by-step reasoning) that illustrates the mechanism: an error at an intermediate reasoning step snowballs forward through the chain.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate / qualifications needed.** The 95% figure (more precisely, 94.90%) is an average across tasks but is driven primarily by graph connectivity, where both models have the most residual errors and the highest snowballing rates. The senator search task has zero residual errors (0/0, N/A), and ChatGPT's primality testing residual errors are too few (2) to draw conclusions from. The figure is heavily weighted toward the specific domain (graph connectivity) where snowballing was already near-ceiling without chain-of-thought. It is possible that chain-of-thought prompting *does* reduce snowballing rates on the errors that remain in other domains, but the paper's datasets (by design) produce very few residual errors in those domains, making it impossible to measure. The claim is technically correct based on the available data, but the domain-specific breakdown matters for interpreting its generality.

#### Overall Strengths of the Experimental Design

- **Clean operationalization of a previously fuzzy concept.** "Self-contradictory hallucination" or "the model knows it's wrong" is easy to claim anecdotally but hard to measure systematically. The separate-session verification protocol provides a concrete, replicable measurement procedure that any researcher with API access can apply. The 67% and 87% figures are precise and directly tied to this operationalization.

- **Multi-domain demonstration reduces risk of artifact.** Showing the phenomenon across arithmetic (primality), factual retrieval (senator search), and multi-hop reasoning (graph connectivity) makes it substantially less likely that the results are an idiosyncratic property of one particular prompt format or task type. The consistent finding of high snowballing rates — with domain-appropriate variation — suggests a genuine underlying mechanism.

- **Controlled ground truth eliminates evaluation ambiguity.** By constructing datasets with uniform correct answers and verified false-positive structures, the paper eliminates the need for human annotation of model correctness. Whether the model's answer is right or wrong is deterministically known; whether its justification claim is true or false is deterministically known. This is a significant strength relative to hallucination evaluations that rely on human judgments of factuality, which are expensive and can be inconsistent.

- **Both positive and negative verification results are reported.** The paper includes examples of verification failure (Appendix Table 12) and reports the full counts (Tables 7, 9, 11), making clear that not all hallucinations are snowballed. This transparency strengthens the credibility of the positive findings by demonstrating the measurement is capable of producing negative results when appropriate.

#### Genuine Weaknesses and Missing Experiments

- **No same-session verification comparison.** As discussed above, this is the most significant missing experiment for establishing the paper's central causal claim. The hypothesis is that the original context *suppresses* correct knowledge. Testing whether the model can detect its own errors when the original context is still present would directly test this hypothesis. If same-session verification showed similar accuracy to separate-session verification, it would suggest the model's knowledge is simply not accessible regardless of context (a knowledge gap, not suppression). If same-session verification showed lower accuracy, it would provide direct evidence for contextual suppression. This experiment would have required minimal additional implementation effort and would have substantially strengthened the paper's claims.

- **No experimental manipulation of the initial commitment.** The paper argues that the initial yes/no token *causes* the subsequent snowballing, but it never experimentally manipulates whether that token is generated. A straightforward test would be to force the model to produce a particular first token (by prepending "Yes" or "No" to the prompt or using constrained decoding) and measure whether this changes the hallucination rate. If forcing the wrong answer increased hallucination and forcing the correct answer decreased it, the causal claim would be directly supported. If forcing had no effect, the snowballing might be driven by something other than the first-token commitment.

- **Single deterministic sample per question.** All primary experiments use greedy decoding with a single response per question. The paper does not report pass@k rates (e.g., what fraction of questions does the model get right in at least one of k samples), which would provide a more complete picture of the model's capability. For the snowballing measurement, generating multiple responses per question and checking whether *any* of them avoid snowballing would test whether the phenomenon is deterministic or stochastic. The temperature experiments (Section 4.2) take a step in this direction but at a coarse level (only three temperature settings) and without reporting per-question consistency.

- **Small manual spot-check samples for extraction validation.** The 30-example spot-checks for factor extraction and senator name extraction represent 8.8% of ChatGPT's primality hallucinations (30/339), 8.0% of GPT-4's primality hallucinations (30/374), 19.6% of ChatGPT's senator hallucinations (30/153), and only 6.9% of GPT-4's senator hallucinations (30/435). A systematic error in the extraction pipeline that affected, say, 5% of cases would not necessarily be caught by a 30-example sample. The paper reports perfect accuracy on the spot-checks, but the small sample sizes limit confidence in the extraction pipeline's reliability, particularly for the larger hallucination sets.

- **No diversity in model family or scale.** The experiments use only two models, both from the same provider (OpenAI), both from the same model family lineage (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4). The paper cannot distinguish between properties of these specific models and properties of instruction-tuned LMs in general. Testing on models from other providers (Claude, PaLM, open-source models like Llama 2) would test whether the phenomenon is specific to OpenAI's instruction-tuning recipe. Testing across model scales (e.g., GPT-3.5 vs. GPT-4 vs. a hypothetical smaller instruction-tuned model) would reveal whether snowballing increases or decreases with model capability.

- **No measurement of the severity or downstream impact of snowballed hallucinations.** The paper counts snowballed hallucinations as a binary variable (present or absent) but does not characterize their severity. The examples in the paper involve obviously false claims (factoring a prime, inventing a flight connection), but in more realistic settings, snowballed hallucinations might be subtle — a slightly wrong number, a plausible-sounding but incorrect intermediate claim — that are harder for users to detect and potentially more harmful. The paper also does not study whether users are more or less likely to be misled by snowballed hallucinations versus other types of model errors.

- **Dataset construction decisions limit generality.** The datasets are designed so that incorrect answers produce positive, extractable claims. This is a clever experimental design choice but necessarily limits the types of hallucinations that can be studied. Many real-world hallucinations do not take this form — a model might omit information, produce vague statements, hedge, or make errors that are not reducible to a single verifiable proposition. The paper's findings apply most directly to hallucinations where the model confidently asserts a specific false fact in support of a wrong answer, which is a subset of hallucination modes in practice.

- **No experiments on mitigation beyond prompt engineering.** Section 4.2 discusses several potential mitigation strategies (beam search, top-k sampling, nucleus sampling, fine-tuning on backtracking data) but tests only temperature variation (null result) and leaves the others as untested hypotheses. The paper's primary contribution is characterization, not solution, so this is not a failure per se, but the claims about beam search potentially solving the problem are purely speculative without empirical support. Given the paper's framing of snowballing as a fundamental limitation of autoregressive generation, empirical testing of whether architectural interventions (like beam search or constrained decoding) can prevent it would be highly valuable.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Hardest Problems Remain Completely Unsolved — Snowballing Measurement Requires Some Base Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's experimental framework depends on the model producing incorrect answers that include extractable false justifications. This requires the model to have some non-trivial interaction with the task — it must generate a confident wrong answer and an accompanying explanation. On the hardest problems in the paper's own datasets (and by extension, on any problem genuinely outside a model's capability range), this condition may not hold. The paper does not explicitly discuss this boundary, but it is implicit in the task design: the datasets are calibrated so that the model *does* attempt answers and produce justifications on most questions. The paper reports that "empirically [when either model is incorrect] it always generates a justification" (Section 3.2), meaning the framework's measurement apparatus works on the studied model-task pairs. But there is no guarantee this holds for harder problems or weaker models.

**The consequence.** The snowballing measurement methodology has a **capability floor**: it can only detect and quantify snowballed hallucinations when the model is capable enough to produce structured incorrect responses with extractable claims. If a model simply outputs "I don't know" or produces incoherent text on a problem, there is no hallucination to classify as snowballed versus knowledge-gap. This means the paper's framework cannot characterize what happens at the frontier of model capability — the regime where models are most likely to be deployed in high-stakes settings precisely because the questions are hard and the answers uncertain. The paper's findings (67–87% of false justification claims are snowballed) apply to problems the model is *confident but wrong* about, not to problems where the model recognizes its own uncertainty.

Furthermore, the three datasets are deliberately constructed so that the model does attempt answers on most questions (error rates of 30.6–88.4% across tasks, Table 6). The paper does not test whether the same methodology would work on datasets where the model's pass@1 is near zero — for example, primality testing on 50-digit primes, or graph connectivity on graphs with 100 nodes. In those regimes, the model might simply refuse to answer or produce random guesses without structured justifications, making the verification protocol impossible to apply. The paper's central quantitative claims (67% and 87%) are therefore bounded to a **capability regime where the model is wrong but fluent**, and may not characterize harder problems where the model's failure modes are qualitatively different.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's difficulty bin 5 analog (problems where the model has minimal capability) is implicitly present in the variation across datasets. On senator search, ChatGPT's error rate is only 30.6% (Table 6) — meaning it gets 69.4% of questions correct. This is the easiest task for ChatGPT, and it is the task where the snowballed hallucination rate (68.6%, Table 7) is closest to the average. On graph connectivity, ChatGPT's error rate is 82.0%, and the snowballed rate is 96.6%. This pattern — snowballing rates are *higher* on harder tasks — is interesting but does not address the regime where error rates approach 100% and response quality degrades. The paper does not report whether any questions produced responses that could not be classified or that lacked extractable claims.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not explicitly address this limitation. The framework is presented as a general measurement methodology without discussion of its applicable capability range. A practitioner seeking to apply the snowballing detection framework to problems at the edge of model capability would need to validate that the model still produces extractable justifications on those problems, and the paper provides no guidance on how to determine whether this condition holds.

### Difficulty Estimation and Domain Construction Require Oracle Knowledge That Is Unavailable in Deployment

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's dataset construction methodology relies on **ground-truth knowledge that would not be available in a realistic deployment setting**. For primality testing, the authors must know which numbers in the 1,000–20,000 range are actually prime to construct the dataset of 500 primes. For senator search, they must know which state-college pairs have no valid senator in U.S. history — a fact that requires exhaustive historical knowledge to verify. For graph connectivity, they must know the connectivity structure of the underlying graph to select source-destination pairs in disconnected components. The paper acknowledges none of this as a limitation because the datasets are research instruments, not proposed deployment systems. But the distinction matters for interpreting what the methodology can and cannot do.

**The consequence.** The measurement framework is **not deployable as a real-time hallucination detection system**. A practitioner cannot use this paper's methodology to detect snowballed hallucinations in a live chatbot without first constructing a dataset of questions with known ground-truth answers and predictable incorrect-justification structures — which is precisely what the paper did in a controlled lab setting. The paper demonstrates that snowballed hallucinations *exist* and can be systematically measured, but it does not provide a method for *detecting* them in arbitrary user queries without oracle knowledge of the correct answer.

This is not a failure of the paper — the paper's goal is characterization, not building a detection system. But it is an important practical limitation: the 67% and 87% figures quantify a phenomenon that we currently have no way to measure in production. A user who receives an incorrect answer from GPT-4 with a confidently stated false justification has no way of knowing whether that justification is a snowballed hallucination (the model "knows" it's wrong) or a genuine knowledge gap (the model "believes" the false claim). The separate-session verification trick works because the researchers know which claims to extract and how to formulate verification questions — knowledge that depends on knowing the ground truth.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The entire experimental apparatus depends on oracle knowledge. Section 3.1 describes dataset construction using known primes, verified senator biographical facts, and a known graph structure. The verification questions for graph connectivity include the full flight information in the prompt (Table 3), which is only possible because the researchers know the ground-truth flights. In deployment, a user asking about flights would not necessarily have the complete ground-truth flight database to include in a verification prompt. The paper does not discuss this dependency or its implications for practical applicability.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not propose or evaluate methods for detecting snowballed hallucinations without oracle knowledge of ground truth. This is acknowledged implicitly by the paper's framing as a characterization study — the title is "How Language Model Hallucinations Can Snowball," not "Detecting Snowballed Hallucinations." However, practitioners reading the paper for actionable insights should understand that the impressive self-detection rates (67–87%) were measured under conditions that cannot be replicated in a live deployment without solving the ground-truth problem first.

### Single Model Family, Proprietary Models, and No Access to Internals — Generality and Mechanistic Understanding Are Unproven

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use exactly two models: ChatGPT (`gpt-3.5-turbo`) and GPT-4, both from OpenAI, both proprietary, both accessed through a limited API that does not expose output token probabilities, logits, attention weights, or any internal representations. The paper explicitly acknowledges this constraint in Section 4.2:

> "Due to the limitations of the APIs for these models, we do not have access to the probability distributions they output and do not have the ability to finetune them. This restricts our ability to explore potential mitigation strategies."

The paper also acknowledges the scope limitation in the Limitations section: "we only conduct experiments on two proprietary models, namely ChatGPT and GPT-4, due to their state-of-the-art performance on many benchmarks."

**The consequence.** Three distinct problems follow from this limitation:

**First, unknown generality across model families.** The paper cannot determine whether hallucination snowballing is a property of instruction-tuned LMs in general, of OpenAI's specific instruction-tuning recipe, of the GPT architecture, or of the specific scale and training data of these two models. If a researcher reproduced the experiments on Claude, Llama 2, or PaLM 2 and found substantially different snowballing rates, it would be unclear whether the difference reflects genuine architectural variation, different instruction-tuning philosophies, or simply different training data. The paper's theoretical framework (Section 2) predicts snowballing should occur whenever "initial committal" and "inherent sequentiality" conditions are met, which should apply to any autoregressive LM with instruction tuning — but this prediction is untested.

**Second, no mechanistic understanding.** Without access to model internals, the paper cannot investigate *how* the coherence pressure overrides correct knowledge. Does the erroneous "No" token literally suppress the activations that would otherwise produce the correct factorization? Does attention to the prior context drown out attention to the factual knowledge stored in earlier layers? Or is the mechanism something else entirely — perhaps the model's training on human explanations has taught it that wrong answers are typically followed by plausible-sounding justifications, and the "knowledge" it accesses in separate verification sessions is a separate capability pathway? The paper's behavioral evidence is consistent with the coherence-pressure hypothesis but does not rule out alternatives. Access to log probabilities would at least allow testing whether the model assigns low probability to the correct fact when the erroneous context is present (consistent with suppression) or whether the model assigns genuinely high probability to the false fact regardless of context (consistent with a genuine knowledge gap that only appears filled in separate sessions due to prompt format effects).

**Third, inability to test key mitigation hypotheses.** The paper identifies beam search as a potentially promising mitigation (Section 4.2) but cannot test it:

> "Unfortunately, we cannot test the effect of beam search on hallucination snowballs because the OpenAI API does not support beam search."

This is not just a missing experiment — it is a missing test of the paper's central theoretical claim. The argument that beam search might help relies on the hypothesis that some beams would avoid the initial erroneous commitment. Testing this would provide evidence for or against the causal role of the first-token commitment in driving snowballing. Without this test, the paper's proposed mechanism remains an interpretation of the behavioral data rather than a validated causal model.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides suggestive but indirect evidence of generality. The three datasets span different reasoning types (arithmetic, factual retrieval, multi-hop reasoning), and snowballing appears in all three for both models (Table 7). This within-paper cross-domain consistency is the strongest evidence that the phenomenon is not domain-specific. However, the cross-model consistency (both ChatGPT and GPT-4 exhibit substantial snowballing) is weak evidence for cross-family generality because both models come from the same provider and likely share substantial architectural and training lineage.

The paper also reports an interesting model difference that is consistent with the theoretical framework: GPT-4, the more strongly instruction-tuned model, shows both higher error rates (Table 6: 83.40% vs. 60.13%) and higher snowballed detection rates (Table 7: 87.03% vs. 67.37%). This pattern aligns with the idea that stronger instruction tuning amplifies both the initial committal tendency and the model's underlying knowledge. But with only two models, both from one provider, this remains a suggestive pattern rather than established scaling behavior.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation transparently in both the Limitations section and the body of the paper when discussing beam search. It does not attempt to mitigate it (e.g., by testing open-source models where internals are accessible). The authors frame it as a constraint of their experimental setup rather than a flaw in their findings, which is a reasonable position for a characterization paper — but practitioners should treat the reported 67% and 87% figures as potentially specific to OpenAI's instruction-tuned models rather than universal properties of LMs.

### The Measurement Framework Cannot Detect Snowballing in the Most Common Real-World Hallucination Modes

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's experimental design requires that incorrect model answers produce **positive, extractable, verifiable factual claims** — specifically, claims that can be reformulated as standalone yes/no questions with deterministic correct answers. This is achieved by constructing datasets where incorrect answers take the form of false positives (claiming a prime is composite with a factorization, claiming a senator exists, claiming a flight route exists) rather than false negatives or more ambiguous errors.

The paper does not claim that all real-world hallucinations take this form, but it also does not explicitly discuss which hallucination modes its framework *cannot* capture. The framework is implicitly limited to what we might call **assertive false-positive hallucinations**: the model confidently asserts something specific and false in a way that is individually falsifiable.

**The consequence.** Many of the most concerning real-world hallucination modes fall outside this framework:

**Omission and hedging failures.** If a model incorrectly answers "No" to a question whose correct answer is "Yes" without providing a positive justification (e.g., "No, I don't believe there is any such senator"), there is no extractable false claim to verify. The model has hallucinated by omission, but the framework cannot classify this as snowballed or knowledge-gap because there is no positive claim to test in isolation. In the paper's datasets, incorrect answers nearly always include justifications — the paper reports this as an empirical finding ("empirically it always generates a justification," Section 3.2) — but this is a property of the specific tasks and models, not a guarantee for all hallucination scenarios.

**Subtly wrong reasoning.** If a model produces reasoning that contains a logical error rather than a false factual claim — e.g., correctly listing all available flights but making an invalid deduction about connectivity — there is no discrete false proposition to extract and verify. The error is in the inference, not in any individual factual assertion. The paper's graph connectivity task catches a subset of this (invalid flights are false factual claims), but logical errors that don't involve fabricated facts (e.g., correctly stating two facts but drawing an invalid conclusion from them) are invisible to the current verification protocol.

**Fabricated sources and citations.** A common real-world hallucination mode involves models inventing references, DOIs, or supporting sources. These are positive claims (the source exists) and would in principle be verifiable, but the verification would require external knowledge access (checking whether the DOI resolves) rather than querying the model's own knowledge. The paper's verification protocol relies on the model's internal knowledge to detect false claims — it works because "9677 is not divisible by 13" is a fact the model can verify from its own training. For fabricated citations to non-existent papers, the model likely *cannot* verify their non-existence from internal knowledge (since it fabricated them in the first place due to a knowledge gap about what papers exist). The snowballing framework would classify these as non-snowballed (the model can't self-detect the error), but the framework itself may not be the right tool for this hallucination mode — the distinction between "the model knows the citation is fake but says it anyway" and "the model doesn't know whether the citation is real" is difficult to operationalize when the model is the only source of knowledge about the citation's existence.

**Vague or probabilistic claims.** If a model says "It is likely that..." or "Some sources suggest..." and gets the fact wrong, the hedging makes it unclear what the extractable claim is. Does "likely" mean the model is asserting the fact as true? If the verification question asks "Is it true that X?" and the model answers "It is uncertain," has it detected its own error or simply maintained its hedging? The paper's framework requires crisp yes/no assertions at both the original QA stage and the verification stage, but real-world model outputs are often less committal.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not systematically analyze which of its own model outputs fail to produce extractable claims. The "empirically it always generates a justification" statement (Section 3.2) is the only characterization, and it is presented as an empirical observation rather than a claim about all possible hallucination modes. The paper's qualitative examples (Tables 1, 3–5) all show the model producing specific, extractable false claims, which demonstrates that the framework works on the cases it was designed for but does not illuminate what fraction of real-world hallucinations those cases represent.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim its framework captures all hallucination modes, and the construction of datasets specifically to produce extractable claims is presented as a feature rather than a limitation. However, practitioners should understand that the 67% and 87% self-detection rates apply specifically to the class of hallucinations the framework can measure — assertive, false-positive, individually-falsifiable factual claims — and may not generalize to other hallucination modes that are common in practice.

### No Same-Session Verification — The Central Causal Claim Lacks Direct Experimental Support

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central causal hypothesis is that the presence of the erroneous prior context (the premature yes/no commitment and surrounding justification) *actively suppresses* the model's ability to access correct knowledge — i.e., the model "knows" the truth but cannot access it because coherence pressure overrides it. The verification protocol is designed to test whether the model can access the truth when the suppressive context is removed, and the high separate-session verification rates (67–87%) demonstrate that it can.

However, the paper never tests the converse: **does the model fail to detect the error when the suppressive context is present?** This would be tested by a same-session verification experiment — after the model produces an incorrect answer with a false justification, immediately (in the same conversation) asking a verification question about the false claim. If same-session verification showed substantially lower detection rates than separate-session verification, it would provide direct evidence for contextual suppression. If same-session verification showed similar rates, it would suggest that the model's failure to access knowledge during the original generation is not about context specifically, but about some other factor (perhaps the original question format is harder, or the model doesn't access the knowledge regardless of context).

**The consequence.** Without the same-session comparison, the paper's causal interpretation — that the initial erroneous commitment *causes* the snowballed hallucination by suppressing correct knowledge — remains a **plausible interpretation of the data rather than a directly tested causal mechanism**. Alternative interpretations are possible:

**Interpretation 1 (the paper's claim):** The model has correct knowledge. During original generation, the erroneous context suppresses access to this knowledge, forcing the model to produce a coherent-but-false justification. In separate-session verification, the context is absent, so the knowledge becomes accessible and the model correctly rejects the false claim.

**Interpretation 2 (format difficulty):** The original questions are harder to answer correctly than the verification questions, independent of context. The original primality question requires the model to determine whether a 4–5 digit number is prime — a multi-step computation. The verification question ("Is 9677 divisible by 13?") is a single-step arithmetic check. The model might simply be better at the verification task format, and its failure on the original task is about task difficulty, not contextual suppression of knowledge. The fact that the model can do the easier verification task doesn't prove that the knowledge was "suppressed" during the harder original task — it might simply not have been able to access it under the harder task's computational demands.

**Interpretation 3 (prompt format effects):** The verification questions use a specific format — "Is 9677 divisible by 13? Answer with either Yes or No" — that differs from the original question format. The explicit instruction to answer with "Yes or No" might change the model's behavior (making it more cautious, or triggering different reasoning pathways). If the same instruction were added to the original question, the model might perform differently. The paper does not control for prompt format effects between original and verification questions.

**Interpretation 4 (stochastic knowledge access):** The model's knowledge might be stochastically accessible — sometimes it retrieves the correct fact, sometimes it doesn't, and the two sessions simply represent independent draws from this stochastic process. Under this interpretation, the model happened to draw "wrong" during the original generation and "right" during verification, but the original context didn't *cause* the wrong draw. The high snowballing rates (67–87%) make pure stochasticity unlikely as a complete explanation, but some mixture of stochasticity and contextual suppression is possible.

These alternatives are not mutually exclusive, and the truth is likely some combination. But without the same-session verification experiment, the paper cannot distinguish between them or estimate the relative contribution of contextual suppression versus other factors. The missing experiment is particularly notable because it would have been straightforward to implement: after the model's original incorrect response with a false justification, simply append "Is [extracted claim] actually correct? Answer Yes or No" in the same conversation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides only indirect evidence for the contextual suppression mechanism. The strongest evidence is the contrast between the high verification detection rates (Table 7) and the fact that the model generated the false claim in the first place — if the model can detect the error in isolation, why didn't it detect it during generation? The answer "because the erroneous context was present during generation" is the most natural explanation. The examples in Tables 1, 3–5 make this contrast vivid: GPT-4 says "13 × 745 = 9677" in one context and "No, 9677 is not divisible by 13" in another. The qualitative contrast is striking and strongly suggestive.

However, suggestive is not the same as experimentally demonstrated. The paper also provides some relevant evidence from the chain-of-thought experiments: when CoT prompting succeeds (dramatically reducing error rates, Table 8), it works precisely by delaying the initial commitment and allowing the model to access its knowledge before generating the answer. This is consistent with the contextual suppression hypothesis — removing the premature committal removes the suppressive context. But CoT also changes many other things about the generation (it provides scratch space for multi-step computation, it changes the prompt format, it may trigger different reasoning strategies), so it is not a clean test of the specific claim that the initial erroneous token suppresses correct knowledge.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the missing same-session comparison, does not acknowledge it as a limitation, and does not provide alternative evidence that would distinguish contextual suppression from the alternative interpretations. The Limitations section of the paper focuses on scope (English QA only, two proprietary models only) rather than on this internal validity question. This is the most significant inferential gap in the paper — the central causal mechanism is plausible and well-motivated but is not directly tested, and a straightforward experiment that would test it was not conducted.

### Computational Cost of Difficulty-Aware Adaptation Is Not Accounted For, and the Framework Provides No Real-Time Detection Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** This paper is a characterization study, not a deployment system. However, its findings invite an obvious practical question: can we use the separate-session verification insight to build a system that detects and corrects snowballed hallucinations in real time? The paper does not attempt to answer this question, and the experimental framework provides no path to doing so without incurring costs that would make it impractical.

The cost problem has two dimensions. **First, offline dataset construction cost.** The paper's measurement framework requires building datasets where ground-truth answers are known and incorrect-justification structures are predictable. For the three studied domains, this was feasible because the domains are synthetic and the ground truth is mathematically or historically determinable. For arbitrary real-world domains, constructing equivalent datasets would require expert annotation or automated verification systems — exactly the resources that retrieval-augmented generation and other anti-hallucination methods already require. The paper provides no method for reducing this offline cost or for adapting the framework to domains where ground truth is ambiguous or contested.

**Second, online verification cost.** Even if a dataset of known-answer questions were available, detecting snowballed hallucinations in a live model response would require: (1) extracting the falsifiable claim from the free-text response, (2) formulating a verification question, and (3) querying the model (or an external knowledge source) in a separate context to check the claim. This is a **per-query overhead** that multiplies the inference cost. If verification requires a separate API call per extracted claim, and a model response contains multiple potentially-false claims, the verification cost could exceed the original generation cost by a factor of 2–5×. The paper's experiments incur this overhead (primary QA call plus one or more verification calls per incorrect answer) but do not report total token counts, latency, or dollar costs. For a production system, this overhead would need to be justified by a commensurate reduction in hallucination-related harm, which the paper does not estimate.

**The consequence.** The paper demonstrates that snowballed hallucinations *can be detected* under controlled conditions with oracle knowledge, but it provides no evidence that they *can be detected in real time at acceptable cost* in a deployment setting. A practitioner reading this paper might reasonably conclude that the separate-session verification trick could be adapted into a real-time hallucination detection and correction module. The paper provides no guidance on whether this is feasible, what the accuracy of such a module would be, or what the latency and cost overhead would be.

More specifically, the paper does not address the **recursive snowballing problem**: if you use the model to verify its own claims, and the model sometimes produces snowballed hallucinations during verification, how do you verify the verifications? The paper's verification protocol works because the verification questions are simple, focused, and have deterministic answers — but this is a property of the specific verification question formats, not a general property of model self-verification. In a more open-ended domain, the verification questions themselves might trigger snowballed hallucinations, creating an infinite regress of verifications.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no direct evidence on this limitation because it does not attempt to build or evaluate a real-time detection system. The cost of the separate-session verification protocol is implicit in the experimental design — each incorrect answer generates 1–2 additional API calls (Section 3.4) — but is not quantified. The paper reports that it uses ChatGPT (not GPT-4) for claim extraction "for its fast inference speed" (Section 3.3), which is a tacit acknowledgment that inference cost matters, but it does not report the actual speed difference or total cost.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not claim to provide a real-time detection system, and the Limitations section does not discuss the practical overhead of adapting the framework to deployment. This is a reasonable scope limitation for a characterization paper, but it is important for practitioners to understand: the impressive 67% and 87% self-detection rates were achieved in a post-hoc laboratory setting with oracle knowledge of ground truth, not in a real-time system that could be deployed to catch hallucinations as they occur.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **new diagnostic category** for language model hallucinations — the snowballed hallucination — and in doing so, it fundamentally reframes the hallucination problem from one of knowledge acquisition to one of knowledge access. The magnitude of this shift is significant but specific: it is not a paradigm shift that invalidates prior work on retrieval-augmented generation or knowledge grounding, but rather a **conceptual expansion** that reveals a previously unmeasured failure mode orthogonal to knowledge gaps. The field now has three distinct hallucination categories to track: knowledge-gap hallucinations (the model doesn't know the fact), presupposition-triggered hallucinations (the user supplied a false premise), and snowballed hallucinations (the model's own prior output suppresses access to facts it knows).

The most important landscape change is methodological. Before this paper, the distinction between "the model doesn't know X" and "the model knows X but can't access it under coherence pressure" was philosophically interesting but operationally vague — researchers had no standardized way to measure it. The separate-session verification protocol (Section 3.4) changes that. It provides a **concrete, replicable operationalization**: extract the false claim from the model's justification, reformulate it as a standalone question in a fresh session, and check whether the model now answers correctly. Any researcher with API access can apply this protocol to new models, new domains, or new prompt formats. The 67% and 87% figures for ChatGPT and GPT-4 (Table 7) are the first quantitative estimates of how much hallucination falls into this self-induced category versus genuine knowledge gaps, and they establish a baseline that future models can be measured against.

This operationalization also **resolves a latent tension** in the self-correction literature. Prior work had reached conflicting conclusions about whether LMs can detect and fix their own errors: Huang et al. (2023) found that "large language models cannot self-correct reasoning yet," while Madaan et al. (2023) demonstrated that self-refinement can help on certain tasks. The snowballing framework provides a resolution: the model *can* detect its errors (67–87% of them) when the erroneous context is removed, but it *cannot* detect them when the context remains — which is precisely the condition under which most self-correction prompting operates (appending "Review your answer" to the existing conversation). The conflict wasn't about whether models possess self-detection capability, but about under what contextual conditions that capability is accessible. This insight directly explains why same-session self-correction often fails: the suppressive context remains present.

The paper also reshapes the **research investment landscape** for hallucination mitigation. It demonstrates that for GPT-4, only about 13% of false justification claims represent genuine knowledge gaps (100% − 87.03% snowballed rate, Table 7). This means that even a perfect retrieval-augmented generation system — one that supplies the model with every relevant fact it might need — would address at most 13% of these false claims. The remaining 87% would persist because the problem is not missing knowledge but suppressed knowledge. This finding makes **context management and architectural mechanisms for backtracking** more attractive research investments relative to knowledge-base construction. It suggests that a dollar spent on developing models that can revise their own outputs may prevent more hallucinations than a dollar spent on building larger retrieval corpora.

Finally, the paper provides a theoretical grounding — via the "initial committal + inherently sequential" framework (Section 2) — that connects the empirical phenomenon to formal complexity results (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023). This is rare in hallucination research, which typically operates at the behavioral level without linking to architectural constraints. The connection to $\text{TC}^0$ establishes that snowballing is not merely a training artifact but a **structural consequence** of the interaction between autoregressive generation, answer-format conventions, and tasks requiring more than constant-depth computation. This theoretical link makes specific predictions: snowballing should occur on any task where the answer format pressures early commitment and where the computation exceeds the transformer's single-step reasoning capacity. It also predicts that models with different architectures (e.g., those that interleave generation with external computation) should show different snowballing behavior — a testable hypothesis that the paper itself cannot address due to API limitations but that open-source research can pursue.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct causal test of contextual suppression via same-session vs. separate-session verification.** The paper's central causal claim — that prior erroneous context actively suppresses correct knowledge — is not directly tested. A clean experiment would replicate the verification protocol but add a same-session condition: after the model produces an incorrect answer with a false justification, immediately ask the verification question in the same conversation (e.g., "You just said 13 × 745 = 9677. Is 9677 actually divisible by 13? Answer Yes or No."). If same-session detection rates are substantially lower than separate-session rates (the paper's 67–87%), it would provide direct evidence for contextual suppression. If rates are similar, it would suggest that format difficulty or stochastic knowledge access, rather than context specifically, explains the gap between original-task failure and verification success. This experiment requires no new datasets or models — it is a straightforward extension of the existing protocol using the exact same questions and extracted claims.

**Measuring snowballing rates across model scales and families using open-source models.** The paper's findings are limited to two proprietary OpenAI models with unknown architectures and training recipes. Replicating the three-dataset framework on a ladder of open-source models — e.g., Llama 2 (7B, 13B, 70B), Mistral, and instruction-tuned variants — would answer several critical questions. Does snowballing increase or decrease with model scale? Does instruction tuning increase snowballing (as the ChatGPT-to-GPT-4 comparison hints but cannot confirm, since both are proprietary)? Do base models without instruction tuning show different initial-committal patterns? With access to log probabilities, researchers could also test whether the model assigns low probability to the correct fact during the snowballed generation (consistent with suppression) or genuinely assigns high probability to the false claim (consistent with a knowledge gap masquerading as snowballing). The open-source ecosystem makes this experiment feasible today; the paper provides the measurement protocol and datasets.

**Beam search as a snowballing mitigator.** The paper hypothesizes that beam search could prevent snowballing by maintaining candidate sequences that avoid the initial erroneous commitment, but API limitations prevented testing. A direct experiment would run the three datasets through an open-source model with beam search (beam width 4–8), measuring both error rates and snowballed hallucination rates (via the same separate-session verification protocol). The key question is whether beams that initially avoid committing to an answer (or commit to the correct answer) eventually achieve higher sequence probability than beams that commit incorrectly and snowball. If beam search substantially reduces snowballing, it would validate the paper's causal model (the initial token commitment is the critical trigger) and provide an actionable mitigation. A negative result — beam search doesn't help — would suggest the snowballing trigger is more subtle than first-token commitment alone.

**Training models to backtrack from erroneous commitments.** The paper's findings imply that the autoregressive training paradigm, which never exposes models to sequences where prior tokens are incorrect and subsequently corrected, is a root cause of snowballing. A training intervention would construct synthetic training data where an initial incorrect answer and justification are followed by an explicit backtracking phrase ("Actually, that's incorrect. Let me reconsider.") and then a correct answer and justification. This teaches the model that coherence with prior context is not absolute — some prior context should be overridden. The experiment would fine-tune an open-source model on this data, then measure error rates and snowballing rates on the paper's three datasets. The key metric is whether the model learns to produce the backtracking behavior spontaneously when it detects (via internal knowledge) that its initial commitment was wrong — essentially, whether the separate-session verification capability can be integrated into the same-session generation process.

**Extending the framework to more realistic hallucination modes.** The paper's three datasets are deliberately synthetic, producing assertive false-positive claims (factorizations, named senators, flight connections) that are individually verifiable. A natural extension would adapt the measurement framework to hallucination modes that are more common in deployment. For code generation: present a coding problem, extract any fabricated API function names or parameter combinations the model invents, and verify them by asking whether those functions exist (analogous to primality testing, where the fabricated factorization is verified against arithmetic facts). For summarization: present a long document, extract specific factual claims from the summary that are not supported by the source text, and verify them by asking the model, in a separate session with the source text available, whether the claim is supported. The key challenge is claim extraction — the paper's domains had predictable false-claim structures, but open-ended domains require more general extraction methods, potentially using the model itself (as the paper did for senator name extraction) but validated at larger scale.

**Difficulty calibration and the boundary of the snowballing regime.** The paper's datasets were calibrated to produce intermediate error rates (30.6–88.4%, Table 6), ensuring enough incorrect answers to measure snowballing. A systematic study would vary problem difficulty parametrically within each domain — primality testing on 3-digit, 4-digit, 5-digit, and 6-digit primes; graph connectivity on graphs with 5, 10, 20, and 50 nodes; senator search with varying numbers of constraints — and measure how the snowballed hallucination rate changes. The theoretical framework predicts that as problems exceed the model's single-step reasoning capacity (the $\text{TC}^0$ boundary), error rates should rise and snowballing should become the dominant error mode. But at extreme difficulties, the model may stop producing structured justifications, causing the measurement framework to break down. Characterizing this boundary — the difficulty range where snowballing is measurable and prevalent — would provide practical guidance for when the paper's diagnostic tools are applicable.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Safety-focused model evaluation pipelines.** AI deployment processes often include red-teaming and safety evaluation, but these typically measure aggregate error rates without distinguishing error types. The paper's verification protocol provides a **post-hoc audit mechanism** that can be integrated into model evaluation: when a model produces an incorrect answer with a factual justification on a held-out test set, run the separate-session verification to determine what fraction of those false justifications the model can self-detect. A model that shows an 87% snowballing rate (like GPT-4) is fundamentally different from one that shows a 30% rate, even if their aggregate accuracy is identical. The high-snowballing model has accurate knowledge that it cannot access under coherence pressure; the low-snowballing model genuinely lacks the knowledge. These two situations demand different mitigations (context management vs. retrieval augmentation) and carry different risk profiles (the high-snowballing model's errors are, in principle, self-correctable if the right context-management mechanism can be built). Evaluation teams at AI companies can implement this audit on their existing test suites for domains where false answers predictably produce extractable claims.

**User-facing uncertainty communication.** Current LM deployments present all answers with uniform confidence, regardless of whether the supporting claims are reliable or snowballed. The paper's finding — that models can verify their own claims at 67–87% rates when those claims are isolated — suggests a practical feature: for high-stakes queries, the system could automatically extract factual claims from its own response, re-query itself on each claim in a separate context (via a second API call with no conversation history), and flag discrepancies to the user. A response like "9677 is not prime (factorization: 13 × 745)" could be annotated with: "⚠️ Self-check: The model separately confirms that 13 does not divide 9677." This turns the snowballing phenomenon from a liability into a **self-auditing mechanism**. The per-query overhead is 1–2 additional API calls (matching the paper's verification protocol), which is acceptable for applications like medical query answering, legal research, or financial analysis where the cost of an undetected hallucination exceeds the cost of verification. The paper's 87% detection rate for GPT-4 (Table 7) suggests this approach would catch the large majority of assertively false factual claims in the types of domains studied, though the detection rate on more open-ended domains would need separate measurement.

**Training data filtering for instruction tuning.** When constructing instruction-tuning datasets, human annotators or teacher models generate responses that sometimes contain confident-but-incorrect justifications. If these are included in training data, they teach the model that "confident wrong answer + plausible-sounding justification" is a valid response pattern, potentially amplifying the snowballing tendency. The paper's methodology suggests a **quality-control filter**: for each (question, answer, justification) triple in a candidate training set, run the separate-session verification protocol on the factual claims in the justification. If the teacher model can detect its own false claims in isolation, flag the example for human review or rejection — the justification contains self-contradictory content that would teach undesirable behavior. This filtering could be applied to the data generation pipelines used in methods like Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2022) or ReST$^{EM}$ (Singh et al., 2024), where model-generated reasoning chains are used for subsequent training.
