# Multi-Layer Transformers Gradient Can be Approximated in Almost Linear Time

**ArXiv:** [2408.13233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13233)

## 🎯 Pitch

Training transformers on long contexts no longer requires quadratic time—the gradients of full multi-layer models with arbitrary loss functions can be computed in nearly linear time n^{1+o(1)} while preserving 1/poly(n) accuracy. This result shatters the long-standing Ω(n²) backpropagation bottleneck by using low-rank attention approximations that remain stable across all layers, enabling efficient training of models like LLaMA on massive sequence lengths.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proves that a novel fast approximation method can compute the gradients of multi-layer transformer models in almost linear time \(n^{1+o(1)}\) where \(n\) is the input sequence length, while maintaining a polynomially small approximation error of \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) across the entire model. The theoretical analysis holds for general loss functions and incorporates practical sub-modules—including residual connection, causal mask, and multi-head attention—and builds on low-rank approximation of the attention matrix via polynomial kernel methods to break the quadratic \(\Omega(n^2)\) time complexity bottleneck that dominates standard backpropagation. The algorithm achieves this acceleration by decomposing the gradient into five components (\(B_2, B_4, B_6, B_7, B_8\)) and computing each in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time using structured low-rank representations of the attention matrix \(f(X)\) (such as approximating \(f(X)\) with \(U_1 V_1^\top\)), with error propagation analysis showing that the \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) per-layer error remains bounded across all \(m = n^{o(1)}\) layers. The work extends prior results—which handled only single-layer attention with \(\ell_2\) loss—to multi-layer transformers with arbitrary loss functions, establishing that gradient-based training of long-context LLMs can be performed in nearly linear time while the approximation provides strong theoretical guarantees only when the feature dimension satisfies \(d = O(\log n)\) and numerical entries use \(O(\log n)\) bits.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Quadratic Complexity of Training Transformers

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that training multi-layer transformer models requires **quadratic time complexity** in the input sequence length \(n\). While the self-attention mechanism has been crucial to the success of large language models, it introduces a computational bottleneck: for each self-attention block with input sequence length \(n\) and hidden dimension \(d\), the running time scales as \(O(n^2 d)\). As the paper notes with concrete examples, systems like LLaMA 3.1 405B support context lengths of \(n = 128\text{k}\) with \(d = 4096\), consuming 30.84 million GPU training hours. This quadratic dependency creates two critical problems: a marked decrease in training efficiency as context lengths grow, and significant energy usage that contributes to higher carbon dioxide emissions.

This is not merely a practical inconvenience—it is a **theoretical barrier** that fundamentally limits how efficiently we can train models on long contexts. If the attention matrix \(f(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is full rank in the exact computation case, no acceleration is possible without some form of approximation. The paper's central insight is that by compromising negligible accuracy through polynomial kernel approximation, a fast sub-quadratic algorithm becomes feasible.

### The Critical Gap: Inference vs. Training

The paper situates itself at a specific inflection point in the literature. Prior work by Alman and Song (2023) had already shown that the **forward pass** (inference) of self-attention can be approximated in almost linear time \(n^{1+o(1)}\). This was a significant breakthrough, but it addressed only half the problem. As the paper states:

> "This result is for the inference time (forward pass), but does not address the main challenge, which is the expensive computation in the training time (backward pass)."

The backpropagation through self-attention—required for training—involves computing gradients with respect to three sets of parameters (\(T_i(X)\), \(W_i = W_{Q_i} W_{K_i}^\top\), and \(W_{V_i}\)) and presents a fundamentally more complex computational challenge than the forward pass alone. The paper explicitly frames itself as addressing this gap: "we address this main challenge, by proving that the gradient computation in the back-propagation of self-attention can be approximated in almost linear time."

This matters enormously because training, not inference, dominates the computational cost of developing LLMs. A model may be deployed and used for inference millions of times, but it is trained once—and that training step represents the majority of resource expenditure in many production scenarios. Making training faster directly addresses the economic and environmental costs of LLM development.

### Limitations of Prior Work

The paper identifies specific, concrete limitations in the two most directly relevant prior works:

**Alman and Song (2023) — Forward pass only.** This work established that the attention forward computation can be done in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time by exploiting the fact that the attention matrix \(f(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) can be approximated by low-rank matrices \(U_1, V_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_1}\) where \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\). By reordering matrix multiplications—computing \(V_1^\top V\) first (which costs \(O(k_1 n d)\)) rather than forming the full \(n \times n\) matrix—the forward pass achieves near-linear time. However, this provides no mechanism for computing gradients through the attention mechanism, which requires handling the chain rule through the softmax nonlinearity and involves terms like \(f(X) \odot (h(X) G_i^\top)\) that cannot be trivially decomposed using the same low-rank factorizations.

**Alman and Song (2024a) — Single-layer, restricted loss, incomplete parameters.** This work extended the low-rank approximation technique to the backward pass, but with three critical limitations that the paper explicitly enumerates:

1. **Single-layer only:** The approach computes gradients only for a single self-attention layer and cannot extend to multi-layer transformers. The paper notes this is inherent in the technique—the error propagation through multiple layers was uncharacterized, meaning results for one layer provide no guarantees for stacked architectures.

2. **Restricted to \(\ell_2\) loss:** The gradient decomposition in Alman and Song (2024a) relies on the specific structure of the \(\ell_2\) loss function. As the paper states, "Previous analyses are limited to individual, specific loss functions." The closed-form expressions for gradient components derived in that work exploit properties of \(\ell_2\) that do not generalize to arbitrary loss functions like cross-entropy, which is the standard training objective for LLMs.

3. **Incomplete parameter coverage:** The prior work computes gradients only for the key-query weight matrix \(W_i = W_{Q_i} W_{K_i}^\top\), ignoring the value weight matrix \(W_{V_i}\) and—critically—the gradients with respect to the intermediate variables \(T_i(X)\). This is a serious gap because, in multi-layer networks, the gradient with respect to \(T_i(X)\) is precisely what must be back-propagated to earlier layers through the chain rule. Without computing \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\), you cannot train anything beyond the final layer.

The paper also notes that in addition to these limitations, "[AS24a] also ignore other crucial components such as the MLP layer following attention computation and the activation function." For a practical training algorithm, the \(g_i\) functions in Definition 1.3—which include layer normalization, MLPs, residual connections, and activation functions—must be integrated into the gradient computation, and the previous framework provided no mechanism for doing so.

### Theoretical Gaps at the Architecture Level

Beyond the limitations of specific prior results, the paper identifies architectural components that prior theoretical work had not addressed:

**Causal attention masks.** The causal mask \(M \in \{0,1\}^{n \times n}\) is a lower-triangular matrix that prevents each token from attending to future tokens during training. The paper notes that "The full-rank characteristic of the causal attention mask poses challenges for low-rank approximations." The attention matrix with a causal mask becomes \(\widehat{f}(X) = D^{-1}(M \odot A)\) where \(A = \exp(XWX^\top/d)\) and \(D = \text{diag}((M \odot A) \cdot \mathbf{1}_n)\). The Hadamard product with the mask \(M\) does not preserve low-rank structure in any obvious way, meaning the polynomial kernel approximation techniques that work for unmasked attention cannot be applied directly. A fast algorithm must exploit the specific structure of the causal mask—its triangular form that enables cumulative sum computations—rather than relying purely on low-rank approximation.

**Multi-head attention.** While the paper treats this as a more straightforward extension (Section B.1, K), multi-head attention increases the size of the attention matrix from \(n \times n\) to \(h \times n \times n\) for \(h\) heads, with each head processing dimension \(d_h = d/h\). The question is whether the almost-linear time guarantee survives this expansion—it does because \(h\) is treated as constant, so \(h \cdot n^{1+o(1)} = n^{1+o(1)}\).

### The Difficulty Decomposition

At a deeper level, the paper faces a technical challenge that prior work had not even formulated: the gradient of a multi-layer transformer's loss with respect to its inputs decomposes into **five distinct terms** \(C_2(X), C_4(X), C_6(X), C_7(X), C_8(X)\), each arising from different combinations of the chain rule through the softmax nonlinearity and the value computation. These terms have fundamentally different structures:

- \(C_6, C_7, C_8\): Involve the attention matrix \(f(X)\) multiplied by vectors derived from the value weights, the key-query weights, and the input representation. These correspond to the "off-diagonal" terms in the gradient (cases where \(i_0 \neq i_1\) in Lemma D.1).

- \(C_2, C_4\): Involve different structural patterns where the attention matrix terms interact with row-specific operations, corresponding to the "diagonal" terms (\(i_0 = i_1\)).

Crucially, the five terms require different low-rank factorization strategies. Some involve simple matrix-vector products like \(f(X) \cdot v\) (amenable to the same techniques as the forward pass), while others involve Hadamard products like \(f(X) \odot (G_i h(X)^\top)\) (which require the row-wise Kronecker product technique formalized in Fact C.2: \((U_1 V_1^\top) \odot (U_2 V_2^\top) = (U_1 \oslash U_2)(V_1 \oslash V_2)^\top\)). The paper must show that all five can be approximated efficiently, and that their summation—to produce \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\)—preserves the error bound.

### Why the Assumptions Are Mild

The paper makes two explicit assumptions that it argues are mild in practice:

- **\(d = O(\log n)\):** The hidden dimension is treated as logarithmic in the sequence length. The paper states this is "usually regarded as a constant" when the context length \(n\) is large. In contemporary LLMs, \(d\) is typically in the range of 1024–8192 while \(n\) can be 32k–128k, making this assumption reasonable for the asymptotic regime of interest. The logarithmic assumption ensures that \(d\) does not grow polynomially with \(n\) and thus does not dominate the complexity analysis.

- **\(m = n^{o(1)}\):** The number of layers is sub-polynomial in the sequence length. This is also standard: models like GPT-4 or LLaMA 3.1 have dozens to hundreds of layers, while \(n\) is in the tens of thousands, so treating \(m\) as \(n^{o(1)}\) is realistic.

- **\(O(\log n)\)-bit representations:** Each numerical entry in the matrices requires only logarithmic bits, meaning the numbers have bounded magnitude. This prevents exponential blowup in the error bounds and is consistent with standard floating-point representations.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution as bridging the gap between the theoretical feasibility of fast attention computation (demonstrated for forward passes) and the practical reality of training multi-layer transformers. The title phrase "Multi-Layer Transformers Gradient Can be Approximated in Almost Linear Time" is a direct claim about breaking through the barrier that prior work could not cross—**extending sub-quadratic gradient computation beyond single layers to the full architecture.**

The paper is careful to distinguish between system-level optimizations (like FlashAttention, which addresses I/O bottlenecks but not asymptotic complexity) and its theoretical contribution:

> "numerous studies... have empirically focused on accelerating attention mechanisms. However, theoretically, these methods are still constrained by quadratic time complexity."

The algorithm is designed to be "seamlessly integrated with existing approaches to further enhance their performance," positioning it as complementary to—rather than competing with—practical acceleration techniques. The paper explicitly acknowledges that GPU implementation is left as future work, making clear that this is a **theoretical result establishing feasibility and bounds**, not an engineering artifact.

The paper also positions itself as **universal** in its applicability: "Our results can be applied to any gradient-based algorithm, e.g., training, full fine-tuning, prompt-tuning, and so on." This universality claim is supported by the fact that the analysis holds for general loss functions (not just \(\ell_2\)) and for gradient computation with respect to all trainable parameters and intermediate variables. Prompt tuning is a particular case of interest because it requires computing \(\frac{dL(X)}{dX}\) (gradients with respect to the input), which the algorithm provides.

### Summary of the Motivation

The paper's motivation can be understood as filling three nested gaps:

1. **The algorithmic gap:** Forward-pass acceleration exists for attention; backward-pass acceleration does not for multi-layer models.

2. **The architectural gap:** Existing backward-pass results for single layers do not account for MLP layers, activation functions, residual connections, causal masks, or multi-head attention—all of which are standard in practice.

3. **The loss function gap:** Prior work is tied to \(\ell_2\) loss, while real LLM training uses cross-entropy or other general loss functions.

By addressing all three simultaneously, the paper aims to establish that training multi-layer transformers in almost linear time is not just possible for toy cases but is theoretically feasible under realistic architectural assumptions with practical loss functions.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation

This is a **theoretical algorithm design paper** proving that the gradient computation for multi-layer transformers can be approximated in almost linear time \(n^{1+o(1)}\) rather than the standard quadratic \(\Omega(n^2)\) time. The system being built is not a software package but a **mathematical framework**—a set of matrix factorization techniques, error propagation bounds, and chain-rule decompositions that together demonstrate it is computationally feasible to train transformers on long sequences by cleverly reordering matrix multiplications and exploiting low-rank structure in the attention matrix.

The problem it solves is that backpropagation through self-attention requires computing products with dense \(n \times n\) matrices (the attention matrix \(f(X)\) and its derivatives), which naïvely costs \(O(n^2 d)\) time per layer. The "shape" of the solution is to show that every term in the gradient decomposes into one of a few canonical forms—each involving the attention matrix \(f(X)\) in either a dot-product or Hadamard-product pattern—and that each canonical form can be approximated in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time by representing \(f(X)\) as a low-rank product \(U_1 V_1^\top\) (with rank \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\)) and then carefully ordering the remaining matrix multiplications to avoid ever forming an \(n \times n\) intermediate.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The system has six major components, connected in a pipeline that processes one transformer layer at a time during backpropagation:

1.  **The base attention mechanism** (Definition C.3): Takes an input matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\), computes the attention matrix \(f(X) = \text{Softmax}(X W X^\top / d) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\), and produces the self-attention output \(s(X) = f(X) \cdot X \cdot W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\). This is the "black box" whose gradients we need to accelerate.

2.  **The low-rank approximator** (Lemma C.13): Given \(f(X)\), computes matrices \(U_1, V_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_1}\) with \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\) such that \(\|U_1 V_1^\top - f(X)\|_\infty \leq \epsilon / \text{poly}(n)\). This is the core acceleration engine—it replaces the dense \(n \times n\) attention matrix with a compressed representation that captures its essential structure using the polynomial kernel method from Alman and Aggarwal (2022).

3.  **The gradient decomposer** (Lemma D.1 and D.9): Takes the upstream gradient \(G_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) (coming from later layers via the chain rule) and decomposes \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\) into five matrix terms \(B_2, B_4, B_6, B_7, B_8\), each with a distinct structural pattern involving \(f(X)\). This decomposition is derived from the chain rule through the softmax nonlinearity and was originally formulated by Deng et al. (2023), but only for \(\ell_2\) loss.

4.  **The canonical form accelerators** (Lemmas E.3, E.5, E.6, E.8, E.10): For each of the five terms, applies the low-rank approximation and reorders matrix multiplications to compute the term in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time. These are categorized into two patterns: "dot-product" terms (where \(f(X)\) multiplies a matrix directly) and "Hadamard-product" terms (where \(f(X)\) appears in an element-wise product with another low-rank matrix, requiring the row-wise Kronecker product technique from Fact C.2).

5.  **The weight gradient accelerators** (Lemmas F.5 and G.4): Computes gradients with respect to the key-query weight matrix \(W_i = W_{Q_i} W_{K_i}^\top\) (via the \(p(X)\) matrix defined in Definition C.12, which itself decomposes into low-rank terms \(p_1(X)\) and \(p_2(X)\)) and the value weight matrix \(W_{V_i}\) (directly via \(f(X) G_i\)), both in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time.

6.  **The error propagation framework** (Lemma H.3 and H.4): Tracks how the per-layer approximation error of \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) accumulates across \(m = n^{o(1)}\) layers, proving via mathematical induction that the total error in the final gradient remains bounded by \(1/\text{poly}(n)\). This is what makes the single-layer results extend to multi-layer architectures—without it, each layer's approximation error could compound exponentially.

Information flows backward through the network: starting from the loss function's gradient with respect to the final output \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_m(X)}\), for each layer \(i = m, m-1, \dots, 1\):

- Component 3 decomposes \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_i(X)}\) into the five terms plus the upstream gradient through the MLP layer \(g_i\).
- Component 4 computes \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\) (to send further upstream) using Component 2's low-rank factors.
- Component 5 computes \(\frac{dL(X)}{dW_i}\) and \(\frac{dL(X)}{dV_i}\) for parameter updates.
- Component 6 ensures the accumulated error stays within bounds.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the input structure: the self-attention module definition, the notation used throughout, and the loss function framework—since everything builds on the softmax attention form and the decomposition of \(L(X)\) into per-element losses.
- **Second**, the low-rank approximation engine (Lemma C.13 and the polynomial method): how an \(n \times n\) attention matrix gets compressed to \(n \times k_1\) factors while preserving accuracy, and why this enables asymptotic speedups through clever multiplication ordering.
- **Third**, the gradient decomposition into five terms (Lemma D.1, D.9): the closed-form breakdown of \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\) into \(B_2, B_4, B_6, B_7, B_8\) and how each term maps to a specific structural pattern involving \(f(X)\).
- **Fourth**, the canonical form acceleration for each term (Section E): how the dot-product terms (\(B_6, B_8, B_2\)) exploit \(U_1 V_1^\top \approx f(X)\) directly, while the Hadamard-product terms (\(B_7, B_4\)) require the row-wise Kronecker product identity \((U_1 \oslash A)(V_1 \oslash B)^\top \approx (U_1 V_1^\top) \odot (A B^\top)\).
- **Fifth**, the weight gradient accelerators (Sections F and G): how \(\frac{dL(X)}{dW_i}\) requires computing the \(p(X)\) matrix (Definition C.12) and its own low-rank decomposition, and how \(\frac{dL(X)}{dW_{V_i}}\) follows a simpler pattern.
- **Sixth**, the error propagation and multi-layer induction (Section H): why a \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) per-layer bound does not automatically guarantee a \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) total bound, and how the mathematical induction proof handles the chain rule through the \(g_i\) functions to close the argument.

#### The Self-Attention Module and Loss Function Framework

The paper works with the standard self-attention formulation, restated in Definition C.3 to fix notation and clarify which intermediate quantities are used throughout the gradient analysis.

**Self-attention module (Definition C.3).** Given input \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) with \(n\) tokens and hidden dimension \(d\), and weight matrices \(W_Q, W_K, W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\), the attention output is:

$$\text{Attn}(X) = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{X W_Q W_K^\top X^\top}{d}\right) \cdot X \cdot W_V$$

The paper simplifies notation by defining \(W := W_Q W_K^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) as the combined key-query weight matrix, since the gradients with respect to \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) can be recovered from the gradient with respect to \(W\) via the chain rule:

$$\frac{dL(X)}{dW_Q} = \frac{dL(X)}{dW} \cdot \frac{dW}{dW_Q} = \frac{dL(X)}{dW} \cdot W_K$$

This reduction means the algorithm only needs to compute gradients for \(W\) and \(W_V\) per layer, plus the gradient with respect to the input \(T_{i-1}(X)\) for backpropagation to earlier layers. The computation of \(\frac{dL(X)}{dW_K}\) follows symmetrically from \(\frac{dL(X)}{dW}\).

Three intermediate quantities are defined to structure the computation (Definitions C.6 through C.10):

- **\(u(X) := \exp(X W X^\top) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\):** The element-wise exponential of the attention logits. This is the raw, unnormalized score matrix before softmax.

- **\(\alpha(X) := u(X) \cdot \mathbf{1}_n \in \mathbb{R}^n\):** The row-wise sum of the exponential scores. Each entry \(\alpha(X)_i\) is the normalizing constant for the \(i\)-th row of the softmax.

- **\(f(X) := \text{diag}(\alpha(X))^{-1} u(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\):** The normalized attention probability matrix. Each row sums to 1: \(f(X)_{i,j}\) represents the probability that token \(i\) attends to token \(j\). This matrix is the primary computational bottleneck—it is dense \(n \times n\), and constructing it naïvely costs \(O(n^2 d)\).

- **\(h(X) := X W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\):** The "value" transformation of the input. This is a simple linear projection that can be computed in \(O(nd^2)\) time.

- **\(s(X) := f(X) \cdot h(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\):** The self-attention output. Each row \(s(X)_{i, *}\) is a convex combination (weighted by attention probabilities) of the value vectors from all tokens.

The paper notes a critical equivalence: \(s(X) = \text{Attn}(T_{i-1}(X))\) (the output of the attention module on the intermediate variable), which is used repeatedly when applying the chain rule to connect layers.

**Loss function framework (Definition 3.1).** The paper generalizes beyond the \(\ell_2\) loss used in prior work by defining the overall loss as a sum of per-element losses:

$$L(X) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{d} \ell(X)_{j,k}$$

where \(\ell(X)_{j,k} : \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} \to \mathbb{R}\) is an arbitrary differentiable function computing the loss contribution from the \((j,k)\)-th entry of the model's output. The only requirement is differentiability—no specific functional form is assumed.

**What this computes:** The total training loss as the sum of individual scalar loss values, one per position in the \(n \times d\) output matrix. For standard cross-entropy loss (described in Remark 3.2), each \(\ell(X)_{j,k}\) would be \(-(Y_{gt})_{j,k} \log((Y_{pred})_{j,k})\), but the framework also supports any other differentiable loss function.

**Why this form:** Prior work by Alman and Song (2024a) relied on the specific closed-form gradient expressions that arise from \(\ell_2\) loss, which has the form \(\|s(X) - B\|_F^2\) for a constant target matrix \(B\). The gradient of \(\ell_2\) loss with respect to \(s(X)\) is simply \(2(s(X) - B)\), which simplifies the chain rule considerably. By contrast, this paper's framework only needs to assume that the upstream gradient matrix \(G_i = \frac{dL(X)}{d\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}(X))}\) exists and can be computed—it never requires the loss to have a particular algebraic form. This makes the framework applicable to cross-entropy (the standard LLM training loss), contrastive losses, or any other differentiable objective.

**Intermediate variables and the multi-layer structure (Definition 3.3 and Lemma 3.4).** The multi-layer transformer is defined recursively:

$$T_i(X) = \begin{cases} g_0(X) & i = 0 \\ (g_i \circ \text{Attn}_i)(T_{i-1}(X)) & i \in [m] \end{cases}$$

where \(g_i\) represents all non-attention components in the \(i\)-th layer (layer norm, MLP, residual connection, dropout, positional encoding, and multi-head concatenation). The paper states: "All forward and backward computations of these practical modules can be run in linear time with respect to \(n\)."

The key Lemma 3.4 establishes the closed-form gradient components via the chain rule. Given the upstream gradient matrix \(G_i = \frac{dL(X)}{d\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}(X))} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) (which captures how the loss depends on the attention module's output), the gradients with respect to the three target quantities are:

- **Part 1 (input gradient, for backpropagation):**
  $$\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{d} G_i(j,k) \cdot \frac{d\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}(X))_{j,k}}{dT_{i-1}(X)}$$

- **Part 2 (weight gradients, for parameter updates):**
  $$\frac{dL(X)}{dW_i} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{d} G_i(j,k) \cdot \frac{d\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}(X))_{j,k}}{dW_i}$$

- **Part 3:**
  $$\frac{dL(X)}{dW_{V_i}} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sum_{k=1}^{d} G_i(j,k) \cdot \frac{d\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}(X))_{j,k}}{dW_{V_i}}$$

**What these compute:** Each equation is a double summation over all \(n \times d\) output positions of the attention module. For each position \((j,k)\), the scalar upstream gradient \(G_i(j,k)\) weights the local gradient \(\frac{d\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}(X))_{j,k}}{d\text{[target]}}\) (which captures how changing the target parameter or input would affect that specific output entry). The sum aggregates these weighted local gradients into the total gradient.

**Why this form:** This is the standard backpropagation pattern—the chain rule expressed as a sum over output dimensions. What makes it challenging is that \(\frac{d\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}(X))_{j,k}}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\) is a large object: for each \((j,k)\), it's an \(n \times d\) matrix (since \(T_{i-1}(X)\) has \(n \times d\) entries). Computing all \(nd\) of these local gradient matrices and summing them would naïvely cost \(O(n^2 d^2)\) time for a single layer, which is even worse than the forward pass. The core technical contribution of the paper is showing that this massive summation can be reorganized into \(O(n^{1+o(1)})\) time by exploiting low-rank structure.

#### The Low-Rank Approximation Engine

The foundational technique enabling all speedups is the polynomial kernel method for approximating the attention matrix \(f(X)\) with a low-rank factorization. This technique was introduced by Alman and Song (2023) for the forward pass; the current paper applies it to the more complex backward pass.

**Low-rank representation of \(f(X)\) (Lemma C.13).** For any accuracy parameter \(\epsilon\) and assuming the entries of \(X\) and \(W\) are bounded (each representable with \(O(\log n)\) bits), there exist matrices \(U_1, V_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_1}\) with rank \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\) such that:

$$\|U_1 V_1^\top - f(X)\|_\infty \leq \frac{\epsilon}{\text{poly}(n)}$$

where \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) denotes the maximum absolute entry of a matrix. The matrices \(U_1\) and \(V_1\) can be explicitly constructed in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time.

**What this computes:** A compressed representation where the \(n \times n\) attention matrix (containing \(n^2\) entries) is approximated by two tall-thin matrices containing only \(2 n k_1 = n^{1+o(1)}\) total entries. The product \(U_1 V_1^\top\) reconstructs an \(n \times n\) matrix entry-by-entry, but the key insight is that this reconstruction is never explicitly formed—instead, the factors \(U_1\) and \(V_1\) are used separately in optimized multiplication orders.

**Why this form:** The attention matrix aggregates pairwise token similarities using softmax normalization, which can be viewed as a kernel density estimate with the Gaussian (exponential) kernel. The polynomial method approximates the exponential function \(\exp(x)\) by a low-degree polynomial on a bounded interval; since the attention logits \(X W X^\top\) have bounded entries (by the \(O(\log n)\)-bit assumption), the polynomial approximation error is controlled. The rank \(k_1\) grows as \(n^{o(1)}\) (specifically, as a function of the polynomial degree needed for the target accuracy), which is much smaller than \(n\) but not constant.

**Why this enables speedups.** The standard forward pass computes \(\text{Attn}(X) = f(X) \cdot h(X)\). With the explicit \(n \times n\) matrix \(f(X)\), this costs \(O(n^2 d)\). With the low-rank factors, the computation becomes \(U_1 \cdot (V_1^\top \cdot h(X))\): first compute \(V_1^\top h(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{k_1 \times d}\) in \(O(n k_1 d)\) time, then compute \(U_1 \cdot (V_1^\top h(X))\) in \(O(n k_1 d)\) time. The key is that \(V_1^\top\) is \(k_1 \times n\), so \(V_1^\top h(X)\) costs \(O(n k_1 d)\), not \(O(n^2 d)\). The total is \(O(n k_1 d) = n^{1+o(1)}\) since \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\) and \(d = O(\log n)\).

This same reordering principle—computing \(V_1^\top \cdot \text{[matrix]}\) before multiplying by \(U_1\)—is applied repeatedly throughout the gradient computation, but the gradient terms have more complex structures than the simple forward pass, requiring additional techniques.

**The Hadamard product challenge (Fact C.2).** Several gradient terms involve element-wise products between the attention matrix and other low-rank matrices, such as \(f(X) \odot (h(X) G_i^\top)\) in term \(B_7\). The Hadamard (element-wise) product of two low-rank matrices is itself low-rank, and Fact C.2 provides the construction:

$$(U_1 V_1^\top) \odot (U_2 V_2^\top) = (U_1 \oslash U_2) \cdot (V_1 \oslash V_2)^\top$$

where \(U_1 \oslash U_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_1 k_2}\) is the row-wise Kronecker product: the \(i\)-th row of \(U_1 \oslash U_2\) is the Kronecker product of the \(i\)-th rows of \(U_1\) and \(U_2\). This produces a new low-rank representation with rank \(k_1 k_2 = n^{o(1)}\) (the product of two sub-polynomial quantities remains sub-polynomial).

**What this computes:** A way to multiply a matrix of the form \((A B^\top) \odot (C D^\top)\) by a vector \(v\) efficiently: rather than forming the \(n \times n\) Hadamard product explicitly, one forms the row-wise Kronecker products of the left factors and right factors (each costing \(O(n k_A k_B)\) time), then multiplies by \(v\) in the standard low-rank way. The rank increases multiplicatively (\(k_1\) and \(k_2\) become \(k_1 k_2\)), but since both are \(n^{o(1)}\), the product remains \(n^{o(1)}\).

**Why this is necessary:** Terms like \(f(X) \odot (h(X) G_i^\top)\) cannot be directly handled by the simple \(U_1 (V_1^\top \cdot v)\) trick because the Hadamard product must be resolved before multiplication. Without Fact C.2, one would need to form at least one \(n \times n\) intermediate matrix. The row-wise Kronecker product provides a way to fold the Hadamard product into the low-rank factors themselves, keeping all intermediate dimensions at \(n^{o(1)}\).

**Verification for the value output \(s(X)\) (Lemma C.14).** The low-rank approximation of \(f(X)\) implies a corresponding approximation for the attention output:

$$\|U_1 V_1^\top h(X) - s(X)\|_\infty \leq \frac{\epsilon}{\text{poly}(n)}$$

The proof is direct: \(\|U_1 V_1^\top h(X) - f(X) h(X)\|_\infty = \|(U_1 V_1^\top - f(X)) h(X)\|_\infty \leq n \cdot \|U_1 V_1^\top - f(X)\|_\infty \cdot \|h(X)\|_\infty\), and the bound on \(f(X)\) carries over with an extra factor of \(n \cdot \text{poly}(n)\) which remains \(\text{poly}(n)\). This establishes that all constructions using the low-rank approximation of \(f(X)\) will maintain accuracy when applied to the quantities that appear in the gradient computation.

#### The Gradient Decomposition into Five Terms

The central analytical step is decomposing \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\) into manageable pieces. This decomposition originates from Lemma D.1, which provides the partial derivatives of a single attention output entry \(s(X)_{i_0, j_0}\) with respect to a single input entry \(X_{i_1, j_1}\).

**The per-entry gradient (Lemma D.1).** For \(i_0 = i_1\) (the diagonal case where the input row matches the output row), the gradient involves five terms \(C_1\) through \(C_5\):

$$\frac{ds(X)_{i_0,j_0}}{dX_{i_1,j_1}} = C_1(X) + C_2(X) + C_3(X) + C_4(X) + C_5(X)$$

For \(i_0 \neq i_1\) (the off-diagonal case), the gradient involves three terms \(C_6, C_7, C_8\):

$$\frac{ds(X)_{i_0,j_0}}{dX_{i_1,j_1}} = C_6(X) + C_7(X) + C_8(X)$$

The specific forms of these terms are (from Lemma D.1):

- \(C_1(X) := -s(X)_{i_0,j_0} \cdot f(X)_{i_0,i_0} \cdot \langle W_{j_1,*}, X_{i_0,*}\rangle\) — a negative contribution proportional to the current output, the self-attention weight, and the query projection of the input row.
- \(C_2(X) := -s(X)_{i_0,j_0} \cdot \langle f(X)_{i_0,*}, X W_{*,j_1}\rangle\) — a negative term involving the inner product between the attention distribution of row \(i_0\) and the key projection of column \(j_1\).
- \(C_3(X) := f(X)_{i_0,i_0} \cdot h(X)_{i_0,j_0} \cdot \langle W_{j_1,*}, X_{i_0,*}\rangle\) — a positive term from the self-attention pathway.
- \(C_4(X) := \langle f(X)_{i_0,*} \odot (X W_{*,j_1}), h(X)_{*,j_0}\rangle\) — a Hadamard product term involving the attention distribution, the key projection, and the value vectors for column \(j_0\).
- \(C_5(X) := f(X)_{i_0,i_0} \cdot (W_V)_{j_1,j_0}\) — a direct contribution from the value weight matrix.
- \(C_6(X) := -s(X)_{i_0,j_0} \cdot f(X)_{i_1,i_0} \cdot \langle W_{j_1,*}, X_{i_0,*}\rangle\) — the off-diagonal analog of \(C_1\).
- \(C_7(X) := f(X)_{i_1,i_0} \cdot h(X)_{i_1,j_0} \cdot \langle W_{j_1,*}, X_{i_0,*}\rangle\) — the off-diagonal analog of \(C_3\).
- \(C_8(X) := f(X)_{i_1,i_0} \cdot (W_V)_{j_1,j_0}\) — the off-diagonal analog of \(C_5\).

**What these compute:** For a single scalar output \(s(X)_{i_0,j_0}\), how much it changes when a single scalar input \(X_{i_1,j_1}\) is perturbed. These are the fundamental building blocks of the gradient—every matrix-level gradient expression in the paper is derived from these scalar partial derivatives by aggregating over the output dimensions.

**Why this decomposition matters:** The off-diagonal and diagonal cases are structurally different because the softmax normalization creates different dependencies. For \(i_0 \neq i_1\) (off-diagonal), changing \(X_{i_1,j_1}\) only affects \(s(X)_{i_0,j_0}\) through the attention probabilities \(f(X)_{*,i_0}\) (since \(X_{i_1,*}\) influences the key representation, which changes how all positions attend to position \(i_0\)). For \(i_0 = i_1\) (diagonal), changing \(X_{i_0,j_1}\) affects the output both through the attention probabilities AND through the value vector \(h(X)_{i_0,j_0}\) directly. The five diagonal terms include the three off-diagonal terms plus two additional ones that capture these extra pathways.

**From scalar entries to matrix form (Lemmas D.3 through D.14).** The paper performs the critical step of converting the scalar gradient expressions into matrix operations. For each \(C_k\) term, a corresponding matrix \(B_k(X)\) is defined whose entries are the aggregate of \(C_k\) over all \(i_0\) and \(j_0\), weighted by the upstream gradient \(G_i\).

The key transition happens in Lemma D.8, which shows:

$$G_i(i_0, j_0) \cdot \frac{ds(X)_{i_0,j_0}}{dX} = G_i(i_0, j_0) \cdot \left(B_6(X) + B_7(X) + B_8(X) + e_{i_0} (B_2(X) + B_4(X))^\top\right)$$

where \(e_{i_0} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is the standard basis vector (1 at position \(i_0\), 0 elsewhere).

**Why this specific grouping:** The terms \(C_1, C_3, C_5\) (which appear only in the diagonal case) share the same structural patterns as \(C_6, C_7, C_8\) respectively—they differ only by whether the attention weight uses \(f(X)_{i_0,i_0}\) or \(f(X)_{i_1,i_0}\). This means \(B_6\) can represent both \(C_1\) and \(C_6\) simultaneously by using different rows of the attention matrix. The \(e_{i_0} (B_2 + B_4)^\top\) term handles the remaining diagonal-only components (\(C_2\) and \(C_4\)) which lack off-diagonal counterparts. The advantage of this grouping is that the five matrix building blocks have clear structures that map to efficient low-rank computations.

The final form (Lemma D.9) is the complete gradient:

$$\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)} = \sum_{i_0=1}^{n} \sum_{j_0=1}^{d} G_i(i_0, j_0) \cdot \left(B_6(X) + B_7(X) + B_8(X) + e_{i_0} (B_2(X) + B_4(X))^\top\right)$$

The remaining task—and the core technical contribution—is to show that each of these five matrix terms, when summed over all \((i_0, j_0)\) pairs weighted by \(G_i\), can be computed in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time.

#### The Matrix Forms of the Five Terms and How They Are Accelerated

The paper defines intermediate matrices \(z_6, z_7, z_2, z_4 \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) that capture the summation over \((i_0, j_0)\) for each term, and a scalar vector \(K \in \mathbb{R}^n\) used across multiple terms.

**The shared quantity \(K\) (Definition D.16):** For each row \(i_0 \in [n]\):

$$K_{i_0} = G_i(i_0, *) \cdot s(X)_{i_0,*}^\top$$

where \(G_i(i_0, *) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}\) is the \(i_0\)-th row of the upstream gradient and \(s(X)_{i_0,*} \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d}\) is the \(i_0\)-th row of the attention output.

**What this computes:** The inner product between the upstream gradient signal and the attention output, computed per row. Each \(K_{i_0}\) is a scalar measuring how much the loss at position \(i_0\) is "aligned" with the model's current output there.

**Why this is defined:** \(K\) appears as a weighting factor in the terms \(z_6\) and \(z_2\) (the ones involving \(s(X)_{i_0,j_0}\) multiplied by attention probabilities, as in \(C_1, C_2, C_6\)). By precomputing \(K\) (which costs only \(O(nd)\) time—an inner product per row, \(O(d)\) per row, times \(n\) rows), the subsequent matrix constructions avoid repeatedly computing these inner products for different attention terms.

**The five matrix forms (Lemma D.17):** After summing over \((i_0, j_0)\), the gradient components reduce to:

- **\(D_6 = -z_6(X) \cdot X \cdot W^\top\)** where \(z_6(X) = f(X) \cdot \text{diag}(K)\). This corresponds to terms \(C_1\) and \(C_6\) combined.

- **\(D_7 = z_7(X) \cdot X \cdot W^\top\)** where \(z_7(X) = f(X) \odot (h(X) G_i^\top)\). This corresponds to term \(C_7\) (and part of \(C_3\)).

- **\(D_8 = f(X) \cdot G_i \cdot W_V^\top\)**. This corresponds to term \(C_8\) (and part of \(C_5\)).

- **\(D_2 = -z_2(X) \cdot X \cdot W\)** where \(z_2(X) = \text{diag}(K) \cdot f(X)\). This corresponds to term \(C_2\).

- **\(D_4 = z_4(X) \cdot X \cdot W\)** where \(z_4(X) = f(X) \odot (G_i h(X)^\top)\). This corresponds to term \(C_4\).

**What these compute:** Each \(D_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) is a matrix contribution to \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\). The full gradient is the sum \(D_6 + D_7 + D_8 + D_2 + D_4\). The \(z_k\) matrices are \(n \times n\) intermediates that encapsulate the interaction between the attention weights and the upstream gradient—they are never constructed explicitly (that would cost \(O(n^2)\)), but are instead approximated through low-rank factorizations and used in optimized product orders.

**Why these forms enable acceleration:** Each \(D_k\) has the structure \(z_k(X) \cdot X \cdot [\text{weight}]\) where \([\text{weight}]\) is a \(d \times d\) matrix (either \(W^\top\), \(W\), or \(W_V^\top\)). The product \(X \cdot [\text{weight}]\) costs \(O(n d^2) = n^{1+o(1)}\) time. The challenge is computing \(z_k(X) \cdot [\text{result}]\) without forming the \(n \times n\) matrix \(z_k(X)\). This is where the low-rank approximations come in: if \(z_k(X)\) can be expressed as \(U_k V_k^\top\) with rank \(k = n^{o(1)}\), then the product can be computed as \(U_k \cdot (V_k^\top \cdot [\text{result}])\) in \(O(n k d) = n^{1+o(1)}\) time.

#### Low-Rank Construction for Each \(z_k(X)\)

The paper now shows that each \(z_k(X)\) admits a low-rank approximation constructible in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time.

**For \(z_6(X)\) (Lemma E.2):** Since \(z_6(X) = f(X) \cdot \text{diag}(K)\) and \(f(X) \approx U_1 V_1^\top\) (from Lemma C.13), set \(U_6 = U_1\) and \(V_6 = \text{diag}(K) \cdot V_1\). The error bound inherits directly:

$$\|U_6 V_6^\top - z_6(X)\|_\infty \leq n \cdot \|U_1 V_1^\top - f(X)\|_\infty \cdot \|\text{diag}(K)\|_\infty \leq \frac{\epsilon}{\text{poly}(n)}$$

**What this computes:** A low-rank factorization of \(z_6\) in \(O(n k_1)\) time, where the right factor \(V_6\) absorbs the diagonal scaling by \(K\). Note that \(\text{diag}(K)\) is diagonal, so \(\text{diag}(K) \cdot V_1\) simply scales each row of \(V_1\) by the corresponding entry of \(K\)—this is why the low-rank property is preserved: multiplying a low-rank matrix by a diagonal matrix on either side does not increase its rank.

**Why this is not trivial:** The order matters. \(f(X) \cdot \text{diag}(K)\) means the diagonal scaling is applied to the *columns* of \(f(X)\) (since \(\text{diag}(K)\) is on the right). The low-rank representation \(U_1 V_1^\top\) has \(V_1^\top\) as the rows of the right factor, so scaling the columns of \(f(X)\) corresponds to scaling the rows of \(V_1^\top\), which is equivalent to scaling the columns of \(V_1\). This is exactly \(\text{diag}(K) \cdot V_1\).

**For \(z_7(X)\) (Lemma E.4):** This is the first Hadamard product case. \(z_7(X) = f(X) \odot (h(X) G_i^\top)\). Using Fact C.2 with \(U_1, V_1\) approximating \(f(X)\) and \(U_2 = h(X), V_2 = G_i\) (both \(n \times d\) matrices, so rank \(d\)), set:

$$U_7 = U_1 \oslash h(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_1 d}, \quad V_7 = V_1 \oslash G_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_1 d}$$

The error bound (shown in Equation 5 in the paper) is:

$$\|U_7 V_7^\top - z_7(X)\|_\infty \leq d \cdot \|h(X)\|_\infty \cdot \|G_i\|_\infty \cdot \frac{\epsilon}{\text{poly}(n)} \leq \frac{\epsilon}{\text{poly}(n)}$$

**What this computes:** A rank-\((k_1 d)\) factorization where the row-wise Kronecker product combines the low-rank attention approximation with the value matrix and gradient information. The construction time is \(O(n d k_1) = n^{1+o(1)}\).

**Why the rank grows to \(k_1 d\):** The Kronecker product creates a rank equal to the product of the individual ranks: the approximation of \(f(X)\) has rank \(k_1\), and \(h(X) G_i^\top\) has rank at most \(d\) (since \(h(X)\) and \(G_i\) are both \(n \times d\) with \(d\) columns). Crucially, \(d = O(\log n) = n^{o(1)}\), so the combined rank \(k_1 d = n^{o(1)} \cdot n^{o(1)} = n^{o(1)}\) remains sub-polynomial. If \(d\) were \(\Theta(n)\), this construction would fail—the rank would become polynomial, and the asymptotic advantage would disappear. This is why the assumption \(d = O(\log n)\) is essential.

**For \(z_2(X)\) (Lemma E.7):** Similar to \(z_6\) but with the diagonal scaling on the left: \(z_2(X) = \text{diag}(K) \cdot f(X)\). Set \(U_9 = \text{diag}(K) \cdot U_1\) and \(V_9 = V_1\). This follows the same pattern as \(z_6\) but applied to the left factor instead of the right factor.

**For \(z_4(X)\) (Lemma E.9):** This is the second Hadamard product case: \(z_4(X) = f(X) \odot (G_i h(X)^\top)\). Note the order is reversed compared to \(z_7\): here it's \(G_i h(X)^\top\) rather than \(h(X) G_i^\top\). Set:

$$U_{10} = U_1 \oslash G_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_1 d}, \quad V_{10} = V_1 \oslash h(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_1 d}$$

The analysis mirrors that of \(z_7\), with the same rank \(k_1 d\) and the same \(n^{1+o(1)}\) construction time.

**Summary of the acceleration strategy:** For each of the five gradient terms, the paper:

1. Expresses the term as \(z(X) \cdot X \cdot [\text{weight}]\).
2. Constructs a low-rank factorization \(U_k V_k^\top \approx z(X)\) without forming \(z(X)\) explicitly.
3. Computes the product in three steps: (a) \(V_k^\top \cdot X\) (cost \(O(n k d)\)), (b) multiply by the weight matrix (cost \(O(k d^2)\)), (c) multiply by \(U_k\) (cost \(O(n k d)\)).
4. The total cost per term is \(n^{1+o(1)}\), and summing the five terms adds only \(O(n d)\) overhead.

#### Weight Gradient Acceleration: \(\frac{dL(X)}{dW_i}\) and \(\frac{dL(X)}{dW_{V_i}}\)

Beyond the input gradient needed for backpropagation, the algorithm must also compute gradients for parameter updates.

**Gradient with respect to \(W_{V_i}\) (Lemma G.3 and G.4):** The closed form is surprisingly simple:

$$\frac{dL(X)}{dW_{V_i}} = X^\top \cdot f(X) \cdot G_i$$

derived by combining the gradient of \(s(X)\) with respect to \(W_V\) (Lemma G.2: \(\frac{ds(X)_{i_2,j_2}}{dW_V} = X^\top f(X)_{i_2,*} e_{j_2}^\top\)) with the chain rule.

**What this computes:** A \(d \times d\) matrix (since \(X^\top\) is \(d \times n\) and \(f(X) G_i\) is \(n \times d\)). The computation is accelerated by replacing \(f(X)\) with its low-rank approximation: compute \(V_1^\top G_i \in \mathbb{R}^{k_1 \times d}\) in \(O(n k_1 d)\) time, then \(X^\top U_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k_1}\) in \(O(n k_1 d)\) time, then multiply the two \(d \times k_1\) and \(k_1 \times d\) factors in \(O(d^2 k_1)\) time.

**Why this is easier than the \(T_i\) gradient:** This term only involves a simple matrix product with \(f(X)\), not Hadamard products or diagonal scalings. The low-rank approximation applies directly without the additional constructions needed for \(z_7\) and \(z_4\).

**Gradient with respect to \(W_i\) (Lemma F.4 and F.5):** This is the most complex weight gradient. The closed form is:

$$\frac{dL(X)}{dW_i} = X^\top \cdot p(X) \cdot X$$

where \(p(X) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is defined row-wise in Definition C.12: for each \(j_0 \in [n]\),

$$p(X)_{j_0} = (\text{diag}(f(X)_{j_0}) - f(X)_{j_0} f(X)_{j_0}^\top) \cdot q(X)_{j_0}$$

and \(q(X) = G_i h(X)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) was defined in Definition C.11.

**What this computes:** The matrix \(p(X)\) captures the gradient of the softmax output with respect to the pre-softmax logits, multiplied by the upstream signal. The expression \(\text{diag}(f(X)_{j_0}) - f(X)_{j_0} f(X)_{j_0}^\top\) is the Jacobian of the softmax function (a well-known result: the derivative of \(\text{softmax}(z)_j\) with respect to \(z_k\) is \(\text{softmax}(z)_j (\delta_{jk} - \text{softmax}(z)_k)\)). This Jacobian is applied row-wise to the vector \(q(X)_{j_0}\) (which represents how the loss depends on the attention-weighted value combination), producing the effective gradient signal that gets propagated to the key-query weights.

**Why this needs further decomposition:** The matrix \(p(X)\) itself is \(n \times n\) and cannot be computed directly. However, the paper decomposes it (following Alman and Song 2024a) into two terms:

$$p(X) = p_1(X) - p_2(X)$$

where:

- \(p_1(X) = f(X) \odot q(X)\) — a Hadamard product between the attention matrix and the \(q\) matrix.
- \(p_2(X) = \text{diag}(p_1(X) \cdot \mathbf{1}_n) \cdot f(X)\) — the row sums of \(p_1\) used as diagonal scalings on \(f(X)\).

Lemmas C.15 and C.16 (adapted from Alman and Song 2024a) show that both \(p_1(X)\) and \(p_2(X)\) admit low-rank approximations. For \(p_1(X)\), the same Kronecker product technique as used for \(z_7\) and \(z_4\) applies (with \(q(X)\) approximated via low-rank factors from \(G_i\) and \(h(X)\)). For \(p_2(X)\), the computation involves first computing \(p_1(X) \cdot \mathbf{1}_n\) (which can be done using the low-rank factors of \(p_1\)), then applying diagonal scaling to \(f(X)\) (which preserves low-rank structure as in the \(z_6\) and \(z_2\) constructions). With low-rank approximations for both \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), the product \(X^\top p(X) X = X^\top p_1(X) X - X^\top p_2(X) X\) can be computed in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time by the standard reordering trick.

#### The MLP Integration: Computing \(G_i\) from \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_i(X)}\)

The final piece needed for multi-layer support is the gradient through the non-attention components \(g_i\). Lemma H.1 and H.2 address this.

**The forward form of \(g_i\) (Lemma H.1):** The paper assumes \(g_i(Z) = \varphi(Z \cdot W_g)\) where \(W_g \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is a weight matrix and \(\varphi : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) is any element-wise activation function (e.g., ReLU, GELU, SiLU). This encompasses standard MLP layers.

The gradient of \(T_i = g_i(\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}))\) with respect to \(\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1})\) is computed entry-wise. For any output position \((i_4, j_4)\):

$$\frac{dT_i(i_4, j_4)}{d\text{Attn}_i} = \varphi'(\text{Attn}_i(i_4, *) \cdot W_g(*, j_4)) \cdot e_{i_4} \cdot W_g(*, j_4)^\top$$

**What this computes:** The gradient is non-zero only on the \(i_4\)-th row (due to \(e_{i_4}\)), meaning each output row depends only on the corresponding input row (the MLP is applied position-wise, not mixing across sequence positions). The factor \(\varphi'(\text{Attn}_i(i_4, *) \cdot W_g(*, j_4))\) is a scalar (the derivative of the activation at that neuron's pre-activation value), and \(W_g(*, j_4)\) is the \(j_4\)-th column of the weight matrix.

**Computing \(G_i\) from \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_i}\) (Lemma H.2):** Given the upstream gradient \(g_{T_i} = \frac{dL(X)}{dT_i(X)}\), the gradient through \(g_i\) is:

$$G_i = \frac{dL(X)}{d\text{Attn}_i} = (g_{T_i} \odot \varphi'(\text{Attn}_i \cdot W_g)) \cdot W_g^\top$$

**What this computes:** The element-wise product \(g_{T_i} \odot \varphi'(\text{Attn}_i \cdot W_g)\) applies the activation derivative at each position (costing \(O(n d)\) time for the element-wise operations plus \(O(n d^2)\) for the matrix multiplication \(\text{Attn}_i \cdot W_g\)), followed by a right-multiplication by \(W_g^\top\) (another \(O(n d^2)\)). The total cost is \(O(n d^2) = n^{1+o(1)}\) since \(d = O(\log n)\).

**Why this is linear in the sequence length:** The MLP operates independently on each position—there is no cross-position mixing. This means the computation scales as \(O(n)\) rather than \(O(n^2)\), which is the fundamental difference between the attention and non-attention components. The paper explicitly leverages this: "All forward and backward computations of these practical modules can be run in linear time with respect to \(n\)."

#### Error Propagation and Multi-Layer Induction

The final theoretical challenge is proving that the per-layer approximation error of \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) does not compound exponentially across \(m\) layers to produce useless gradients.

**Single-layer baseline (Lemma H.3):** For a single-layer transformer \(g_1 \circ \text{Attn}_1 \circ g_0(X)\), the algorithm computes:

1. \(G_1 = \frac{dL(X)}{d\text{Attn}_1(T_0(X))}\) from \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_1(X)}\) using Lemma H.2 (no approximation error—this is exact since the MLP gradient is computed analytically).
2. \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_0(X)}, \frac{dL(X)}{dW_1}, \frac{dL(X)}{dW_{V_1}}\) using the low-rank approximations from Sections E, F, G (each with \(\leq 1/\text{poly}(n)\) error).
3. \(\frac{dL(X)}{dX}\) from \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_0(X)}\) using Lemma H.2 again.

The total error \(\|\widetilde{g_1} - \frac{dL(X)}{dX}\|_\infty \leq 1/\text{poly}(n)\) where \(\widetilde{g_1}\) is the algorithm's output. The proof is straightforward: the error from step 2 propagates through step 3 with at most \(n \cdot d \cdot \|dT_0(X)/dX\|_\infty \leq \text{poly}(n)\) amplification, so the final error remains \(1/\text{poly}(n)\).

**Multi-layer induction (Lemma H.4):** The proof proceeds by mathematical induction on the number of layers \(m\).

**Base case (\(m = 1\)):** Established by Lemma H.3.

**Inductive hypothesis:** For any \(k\)-layer transformer, the algorithm computes \(\frac{dL(X)}{dX}\) in \(O(n^{1+o(1)})\) time with error \(\leq 1/\text{poly}(n)\).

**Inductive step (\(k \to k+1\)):** Consider a \((k+1)\)-layer model \(F_{k+1}(X) = F_k(T_1(X))\) where the extra layer is added at the beginning (without loss of generality, since layers can be re-indexed). The computation proceeds as:

1. By the inductive hypothesis, \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_1(X)}\) can be computed in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time with error \(\leq 1/\text{poly}(n)\) (treating \(T_1(X)\) as the "input" to the \(k\)-layer sub-network \(F_k\)).
2. Given \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dT_1(X)}}\) (the approximation from step 1 with known error bound), compute \(\widetilde{G_1}\) via Lemma H.2 (the MLP gradient, which is exact and adds no new error—it simply transforms the input error by bounded factors).
3. Given \(\widetilde{G_1}\), compute \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dT_0(X)}}\) via Lemma E.11 (the attention input gradient, with new approximation error \(\leq 1/\text{poly}(n)\) from the low-rank constructions).
4. Given \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dT_0(X)}}\), compute \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dX}}\) via Lemma H.2 (the embedding/input transformation, again exact but transmitting the existing error).

The critical error propagation bound is established in Equations 29–30 and the surrounding argument. The key steps:

- The error in \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dT_1(X)}}\) is \(\leq 1/\text{poly}(n)\) by the inductive hypothesis.
- Lemma E.11's computation amplifies this error by at most \(\text{poly}(n)\) (through matrix multiplications with bounded matrices), so the error in \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dT_0(X)}}\) is \(\leq \text{poly}(n) \cdot (1/\text{poly}(n)) + (1/\text{poly}(n))\) (the first term from propagation, the second from the new low-rank approximation), which is \(\leq 1/\text{poly}(n)\).
- Step 4 amplifies by at most \(n \cdot d \cdot \|dT_0(X)/dX\|_\infty \leq \text{poly}(n)\), maintaining the \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) bound.

**Why this works:** The crucial property is that each layer's error amplification is bounded by a fixed polynomial in \(n\) (not an exponential function of the layer index). Since there are only \(m = n^{o(1)}\) layers, the total amplification is at most \((\text{poly}(n))^{n^{o(1)}} = n^{n^{o(1)}}\), which is still sub-exponential and can be absorbed into the "polynomially small" claim by choosing \(\epsilon\) appropriately at each layer. The paper's choice to use \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) per layer rather than a fixed constant ensures that the product over \(m = n^{o(1)}\) layers remains \(1/\text{poly}(n)\).

**The algorithm structure (Algorithm 1):** The MultiGrad procedure implements this iteratively. Starting from \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_m(X)}\) (computed from the loss function in \(O(nd)\) time), it loops backward from layer \(m\) to layer 1, calling SingleGrad at each step to compute:

- \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}}\) (to pass to the next iteration),
- \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dW_i}}\) and \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dW_{V_i}}}\) (for parameter updates at this layer).

The SingleGrad procedure itself calls the five low-rank constructors (Lemmas E.5, E.6, E.8, E.10 for the input gradient terms, Lemma 5.2 for the \(W\) weight gradient, Lemma 5.3 for the \(W_V\) weight gradient), each running in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time.

**What the algorithm does NOT do:** Algorithm 1 does not handle the actual parameter optimization—it only computes the gradients. The paper notes that "Optimize \(W_{Q_i}, W_{K_i}\) via \(\widetilde{gw}\) using optimizer" and similarly for \(W_{V_i}\) are placeholder steps indicating that any gradient-based optimizer (SGD, Adam, AdamW) can consume the approximated gradients. The algorithmic contribution is entirely in the gradient computation step, not in the optimization procedure.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: A Unified Framework for Decomposing and Accelerating ALL Gradient Components in Multi-Layer Attention

**The Idea Level: From Single-Layer Toy Cases to Full Architecture Feasibility**

The paper's most fundamental conceptual contribution is proving that the gradient computation for multi-layer transformers—something prior work had not even formulated as a tractable problem—can be decomposed into a small, fixed set of canonical matrix forms, and that every single one of those forms can be accelerated using the same low-rank approximation engine. This is not an incremental extension of prior results. It is a **qualitative leap in scope** that changes the landscape from "we can do fast forward passes and maybe fast backward passes for one layer under restrictive conditions" to "the entire training pipeline for realistic multi-layer architectures is theoretically tractable in almost linear time."

To understand why this is distinctive, consider what the field had before this work. Alman and Song (2023) established that the forward pass of self-attention could be approximated in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time, but backward passes—required for training—were not addressed. Alman and Song (2024a) took a step toward addressing this gap, but with constraints that made the result inapplicable to real training: they handled only single-layer attention, only under ℓ₂ loss, and only for one of the three gradient components needed (the key-query weight matrix \(W_i = W_{Q_i} W_{K_i}^\top\)). They explicitly did not compute gradients with respect to the value weight matrix \(W_{V_i}\), and—critically—they did not compute gradients with respect to the intermediate variables \(T_i(X)\) that must be back-propagated to earlier layers. Without \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_i(X)}\), you cannot train anything beyond the last layer. The prior work was, in essence, a proof of concept for a single isolated component, not a training algorithm.

What this paper does is fundamentally different in kind. It shows that the gradient of the loss with respect to the input of a transformer layer decomposes into exactly five matrix terms: \(B_2, B_4, B_6, B_7, B_8\) (Section D, Lemma D.9). This decomposition is derived from the chain rule through the softmax nonlinearity—a derivation originally done by Deng et al. (2023) but only for ℓ₂ loss. The paper generalizes this decomposition to arbitrary loss functions (Definition 3.1) by showing that the upstream gradient matrix \(G_i = \frac{dL(X)}{d\text{Attn}_i(T_{i-1}(X))}\) can be treated as a black-box input to the decomposition, with no assumptions about its origin beyond differentiability. This removes the dependency on any specific loss function and makes the framework universal.

But the decomposition alone is not the innovation. The innovation is that **each of the five terms maps to one of exactly two structural patterns** involving the attention matrix \(f(X)\): either a **dot-product pattern** (\(f(X)\) multiplied directly by some matrix, as in \(B_6, B_8, B_2\)) or a **Hadamard-product pattern** (\(f(X)\) in an element-wise product with another low-rank expression, as in \(B_7, B_4\)). The dot-product terms are accelerable by the same low-rank reordering trick used in the forward pass: replace \(f(X)\) with \(U_1 V_1^\top\) and compute \(U_1 \cdot (V_1^\top \cdot [\text{target}])\) rather than \((U_1 V_1^\top) \cdot [\text{target}]\). The Hadamard-product terms require a second technique—the row-wise Kronecker product identity from Fact C.2, \((U_1 V_1^\top) \odot (U_2 V_2^\top) = (U_1 \oslash U_2)(V_1 \oslash V_2)^\top\)—but are then accelerable by the same reordering principle.

This taxonomy—five terms, two patterns, one acceleration engine—is what makes the problem **tractable in a unified way**. The paper does not need five different algorithms for five terms. It needs one algorithm (low-rank approximation plus multiplication reordering) applied in two slightly different configurations (with or without the Kronecker product pre-processing). This conceptual unification is what enables the extension to multi-layer architectures: if every gradient component in every layer falls into one of these patterns, then the per-layer computation time is always \(n^{1+o(1)}\), and the total time across \(m\) layers is \(m \cdot n^{1+o(1)} = n^{1+o(1)}\) since \(m = n^{o(1)}\).

Furthermore, the paper extends the acceleration to ALL trainable parameters—not just \(W_i\) but also \(W_{V_i}\) (Lemma G.3, Lemma G.4)—and to the gradient through the non-attention components \(g_i\) (MLP layers with activation functions, Lemma H.2). The \(W_{V_i}\) gradient turns out to have the simple form \(X^\top f(X) G_i\), which is a pure dot-product pattern. The \(W_i\) gradient is more complex, requiring the \(p(X)\) matrix (the softmax Jacobian applied to the upstream signal), but the paper shows that \(p(X)\) itself decomposes into two terms—\(p_1(X) = f(X) \odot q(X)\) (a Hadamard-product pattern) and \(p_2(X) = \text{diag}(p_1(X) \cdot \mathbf{1}_n) \cdot f(X)\) (a dot-product pattern with diagonal scaling)—both of which are accelerable by the same two canonical techniques.

**The significance beyond raw performance.** This is not primarily a practical speedup claim—the paper explicitly acknowledges it lacks "enterprise-scale computational resources for training large language models to provide empirical support" (Section 7). Rather, it is a **theoretical feasibility result** that establishes the asymptotic complexity class of multi-layer transformer training. Before this work, one could reasonably believe that training transformers on long sequences requires fundamentally quadratic time—that the softmax nonlinearity and the chain rule through multiple layers create irreducible \(O(n^2)\) dependencies that no amount of clever algorithm design could circumvent. This paper proves that belief is false. The problem is not inherently quadratic; it is quadratic only if you insist on exact computation. Compromise on accuracy by a polynomially small amount, and the complexity collapses to almost linear.

This reframes the research agenda from "can we make attention training faster with hardware tricks?" (the system-level approach of FlashAttention and similar work, which improve constants but not asymptotics) to "how close can we get to \(n^{1+o(1)}\) in practice, and what accuracy-complexity tradeoff is optimal?" It establishes that the theoretical frontier is far from the current practical frontier, and that the bottleneck is not algorithmic impossibility but engineering implementation (the paper identifies specific coding challenges for GPU implementation in Section B.4).

**Evidence anchor.** The decomposition into five terms and their canonical forms is established in Lemma D.9 (matrix view of \(T_i\) gradient) and Lemma D.17 (close forms of \(D_k\)), with the acceleration guarantees for each term in Lemmas E.3, E.5, E.6, E.8, E.10. The extension to weight gradients is in Lemmas F.5 and G.4. The multi-layer induction proof is Lemma H.4, establishing \(n^{1+o(1)}\) total time with \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) total error.

### Innovation 2: Low-Rank Attention Approximation as a Universal Gradient Acceleration Primitive

**The Idea Level: The Attention Matrix Is Not Just Compressible for Forward Passes—It Is the Master Key for Backward Passes Too**

A more subtle but equally important conceptual contribution is the recognition that the low-rank approximability of the attention matrix \(f(X)\)—previously used only as a forward-pass optimization trick—is the **universal primitive that unlocks acceleration of every gradient computation** in the backward pass. This might sound like an obvious extension ("if \(f(X)\) is low-rank, just use the same factors in the backward pass"), but it is far from trivial. The backward pass involves terms like \(f(X) \odot (h(X) G_i^\top)\) (the Hadamard product of the attention matrix with a gradient-weighted value matrix) and \(\text{diag}(K) \cdot f(X)\) (the attention matrix with diagonally scaled rows), which do not preserve low-rank structure under naive multiplication orders.

The paper's insight is that these more complex operations can be reduced to operations on the low-rank factors themselves, without ever reconstructing the \(n \times n\) attention matrix. The key technical enabler is **Fact C.2**, the row-wise Kronecker product identity, which shows that the Hadamard product of two low-rank matrices is itself low-rank, with a rank equal to the product of the individual ranks. This allows terms like \(f(X) \odot (h(X) G_i^\top)\) to be expressed as \((U_1 \oslash h(X)) \cdot (V_1 \oslash G_i)^\top\)—a new low-rank factorization directly constructible from the factors of \(f(X)\) and the matrices \(h(X)\) and \(G_i\).

But the conceptual contribution goes beyond the specific algebraic trick. It is the recognition that **the same polynomial kernel approximation that compresses the attention matrix for forward passes compresses it for all gradient-related purposes simultaneously.** The low-rank factors \(U_1, V_1\) computed once per layer (in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time) are reused across all five input-gradient terms and both weight-gradient computations. There is no need to compute separate low-rank approximations for different gradient components—the attention matrix's structure is the universal bottleneck, and compressing it once compresses everything.

To appreciate why this is non-obvious, consider what the chain rule does to the attention mechanism. The gradient \(\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}\) involves derivatives of the softmax function, which produce terms like \(\text{diag}(f(X)_{j_0}) - f(X)_{j_0} f(X)_{j_0}^\top\) (the softmax Jacobian). This Jacobian involves outer products of rows of \(f(X)\), which naïvely would seem to require access to individual rows of the full \(n \times n\) matrix. The paper shows that these outer products, when aggregated over all output dimensions, produce expressions that factor through the low-rank representation. The key insight in Lemma D.17 is that the five gradient terms, after summing over the output dimensions \((i_0, j_0)\) weighted by the upstream gradient \(G_i\), all reduce to forms involving \(f(X)\) multiplied (normally or element-wise) by matrices that are themselves low-rank or diagonally scaled. The softmax Jacobian's complexity is absorbed into these forms.

**Comparison to prior work.** Alman and Song (2023) introduced polynomial kernel approximation for the attention forward pass, showing that \(f(X) \approx U_1 V_1^\top\) enables \(n^{1+o(1)}\) inference. But they did not address whether the same approximation could survive the chain rule. Alman and Song (2024a) applied the approximation to the backward pass, but only for the gradient with respect to \(W_i\) under ℓ₂ loss—they did not consider the input gradient \(\frac{dL(X)}{dX}\) or the multi-layer case. The current paper shows that **the same low-rank factors that enable fast inference also enable fast training**, and that the approximation error introduced in the forward pass propagates through the backward pass in a controlled way (polynomial amplification per layer, bounded total error across \(m = n^{o(1)}\) layers).

**Significance beyond raw performance.** This insight establishes **low-rank approximation as a general-purpose primitive for transformer computation**, not merely a forward-pass optimization. It suggests that any future work aiming to accelerate some aspect of transformer computation—whether training, fine-tuning, prompt-tuning, or even architecture search—can potentially leverage the same polynomial kernel approximation and the same canonical form patterns identified here. The paper explicitly notes this universality in Section 6: "Our results can be applied to any gradient-based algorithm, e.g., training, full fine-tuning, prompt-tuning, and so on." This is not an idle claim—it follows directly from the fact that all gradient-based methods require computing \(\frac{dL(X)}{dX}\) or \(\frac{dL(X)}{d\text{[parameters]}}\), which are exactly the quantities this paper shows are accelerable.

The work also implicitly defines a **design pattern for future theoretical acceleration results**: (1) identify all terms in the gradient computation, (2) classify them by their interaction pattern with the attention matrix (dot-product vs. Hadamard-product), (3) apply the appropriate low-rank construction (direct multiplication vs. Kronecker product), (4) bound error propagation layer by layer. This pattern is potentially reusable for other architectures (cross-attention, encoder-decoder, graph transformers) and other computational primitives (tensor attention, higher-order interactions).

**Evidence anchor.** The low-rank approximation is established in Lemma C.13 for \(f(X)\), with extensions in Lemmas C.14 (for \(s(X)\)), C.15 (for \(p_1(X)\)), and C.16 (for \(p_2(X)\)). The reuse of factors across gradient terms is visible in the proof structures: \(U_1, V_1\) appear in the constructions for \(z_6\) (Lemma E.2), \(z_7\) (Lemma E.4), \(z_2\) (Lemma E.7), \(z_4\) (Lemma E.9), the \(W_V\) gradient (Lemma G.4), and the \(W_i\) gradient (via Lemmas C.15 and C.16). All use the same underlying factors.

### Innovation 3: Error Propagation Analysis That Makes Multi-Layer Approximation Theoretically Sound

**The Idea Level: Per-Layer Approximation Bounds Survive Chain-Rule Composition Through Controlled Polynomial Amplification**

A less flashy but equally essential contribution is the rigorous analysis showing that a \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) per-layer approximation error does not compound exponentially across \(m\) layers to produce useless gradients. This is a **theoretical infrastructure contribution**—it does not introduce a new technique per se, but it makes the entire multi-layer framework trustworthy by closing the most obvious objection: "Sure, you can approximate one layer's gradient, but what happens when you chain \(m\) of them together?"

The naive concern is legitimate. If each layer's gradient computation introduces an approximation error of \(\epsilon\), and the chain rule means that the error in layer \(i\)'s gradient propagates through all earlier layers, the total error could be \(\epsilon \cdot (\text{amplification factor})^{m-1}\). If the amplification factor is greater than 1 and \(m\) is large (recall \(m = n^{o(1)}\), which can grow slowly with \(n\); for \(n = 128\text{k}\), \(n^{o(1)}\) could be dozens to hundreds), this could blow up catastrophically unless proven otherwise.

The paper addresses this through the mathematical induction proof in Lemma H.4 (formal version in Section H.3). The key technical step is establishing that the error amplification at each layer is **polynomial** in \(n\) rather than exponential. Concretely, when propagating the error in \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dT_i(X)}}\) through the attention gradient computation to obtain \(\widetilde{\frac{dL(X)}{dT_{i-1}(X)}}\), the amplification factor is bounded by \(n \cdot d \cdot \max(\|f(X)\|_\infty, \|W\|_\infty, \|X\|_\infty, \dots) \leq \text{poly}(n)\) (since all matrix entries are assumed representable with \(O(\log n)\) bits, their magnitudes are polynomially bounded). Each layer thus amplifies the incoming error by at most \(\text{poly}(n)\), and the newly introduced low-rank approximation error adds another \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) term. After \(m = n^{o(1)}\) layers, the total error is at most:

$$\text{poly}(n)^{n^{o(1)}} \cdot (1/\text{poly}(n)) + \text{lower-order terms}$$

The factor \(\text{poly}(n)^{n^{o(1)}}\) is \(n^{n^{o(1)}}\), which is sub-exponential. By choosing the per-layer approximation tolerance \(\epsilon\) to be \(1/(n^{n^{o(1)}} \cdot \text{poly}(n))\) rather than a fixed constant, the total error remains \(1/\text{poly}(n)\). Crucially, this choice is possible without breaking the \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time bound because the rank of the low-rank approximation \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\) depends on \(\log(1/\epsilon)\), and making \(\epsilon\) smaller by a sub-exponential factor only increases \(\log(1/\epsilon)\) by \(n^{o(1)}\), which is absorbed into the \(o(1)\) exponent.

**What was the prior assumption?** The default assumption in the literature—implicit in the fact that Alman and Song (2024a) only handled single layers—was that extending approximation guarantees to multi-layer architectures would require either (a) proving that errors do not amplify at all (unlikely for nonlinear chain rules) or (b) handling an exponential blowup that would require \(\epsilon\) to be exponentially small, destroying the almost-linear time guarantee. The paper's error propagation analysis demonstrates that **polynomial amplification is manageable** because the number of layers is sub-polynomial in \(n\). This is a specific instance of a more general principle: if (1) error amplification per composition step is polynomial, and (2) the number of composition steps is sub-polynomial, then the total error remains controllable without requiring exponentially small per-step tolerance.

**Significance beyond this paper.** The error propagation framework establishes a **template for proving multi-layer approximation guarantees** that could be applied to other architectures and other approximation techniques. The structure—mathematical induction on layer count, bounded polynomial amplification per layer, controlled error accumulation across \(n^{o(1)}\) layers—is general and could be reused. The paper also demonstrates that the \(g_i\) functions (MLPs, layer norms, etc.) do not introduce additional amplification beyond what the matrix norm bounds already capture, because their gradients are exact (not approximated) and have norm bounded by the same polynomial factors.

This is fundamentally a **soundness result**: it says that the fast algorithm is not just efficient but also reliable, producing gradients that are close enough to the true gradients for training to be meaningful. Without this analysis, the algorithm would be an interesting curiosity—fast, but potentially producing garbage gradients after multiple layers. With it, the paper makes a credible claim that the approximated gradients are faithful to the true optimization landscape.

**Evidence anchor.** The induction proof is Lemma H.4 (formal version of Lemma 5.5), with the base case in Lemma H.3. The error amplification bounds rely on the bounded-entry lemmas in Section C.5 (Lemmas C.17, C.18, C.19 showing \(\|f(X)\|_\infty \leq 1\), \(\|h(X)\|_\infty \leq \text{poly}(n)\), \(\|s(X)\|_\infty \leq \text{poly}(n)\)). The MLP gradient analysis establishing no additional approximation error is Lemma H.2 (the computation is exact, using analytic derivatives of \(\varphi\)).

### Innovation 4: The Assumption Architecture That Makes Almost-Linear Time Possible—and Its Honest Limitations

**The Idea Level: Explicitly Identifying the Mathematical Conditions Under Which the Quadratic Bottleneck Is Breakable**

A distinctive intellectual contribution that is easy to overlook is the paper's **explicit and honest treatment of its assumptions**. Rather than claiming a universal speedup, the paper carefully delineates the precise conditions under which the \(O(n^2) \to n^{1+o(1)}\) transition is possible: \(d = O(\log n)\) (the feature dimension is logarithmic in sequence length), each numerical entry requires only \(O(\log n)\) bits (bounded magnitudes), and \(m = n^{o(1)}\) (the number of layers is sub-polynomial). These are not incidental technicalities—they are **the boundary conditions that separate the tractable from the intractable regime** for this approach.

**Why this is an intellectual contribution.** Theoretical results that claim asymptotic improvements often hide their assumptions in fine print, leaving practitioners uncertain whether the result applies to their setting. This paper does the opposite: it states the assumptions prominently (Theorem 4.2, restated in Section 4.2) and, more importantly, explains **why each assumption matters** and **what happens if it is violated**.

- The \(d = O(\log n)\) assumption ensures that the rank of the low-rank approximation does not blow up. When constructing Hadamard-product terms via the Kronecker product, the rank becomes \(k_1 d\) where \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\) is the rank from approximating \(f(X)\). If \(d\) were \(\Theta(n^\alpha)\) for any \(\alpha > 0\), then \(k_1 d\) would be polynomial in \(n\), and the "almost linear" guarantee would collapse to polynomial. The paper thus identifies the **hidden dimension as the critical scaling parameter**—if \(d\) grows polynomially with \(n\), the technique breaks, which is a specific, falsifiable prediction about when alternative approaches would be needed.

- The \(O(\log n)\)-bit assumption ensures that all matrix entries have polynomially bounded magnitudes, which directly controls the error amplification factors in the induction proof. If entries could be exponentially large (as might happen with poor initialization or unstable training dynamics), the error bounds would become exponential and the induction would fail. This is not just a theoretical convenience—it corresponds to the practical reality that well-trained networks typically have bounded activations, but it also identifies a potential failure mode: if activations explode (e.g., during early training), the approximation quality degrades.

- The \(m = n^{o(1)}\) assumption ensures that the number of layers is small enough that polynomial error amplification across layers does not compound beyond the chosen \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) tolerance. This is realistic for current architectures (models with hundreds of layers and sequence lengths in the tens of thousands), but it also marks a boundary: if someone builds a transformer with \(n^{0.01}\) layers (growing polynomially with sequence length), the analysis would need to be revisited.

**Comparison to prior work.** Alman and Song (2023, 2024a) made similar assumptions for the forward and single-layer backward passes, but they did not explicitly discuss the role of \(m\) (since they never considered multi-layer architectures) or the interplay between \(d\) and the Hadamard-product constructions (since they did not handle all gradient components). This paper's contribution is not in inventing new assumptions but in **mapping out the complete assumption architecture** needed for a multi-layer training guarantee and identifying which components of the architecture (the Hadamard-product terms) create the most stringent dependencies.

**The honesty as a contribution.** The paper explicitly acknowledges the practical gap between its theoretical cost model and deployment reality. Section B.4 lists concrete GPU implementation challenges: defining new tensor operations in PyTorch (for the row-wise Kronecker product and the causal mask constructions), re-implementing back-propagation functions, and writing CUDA kernels for parallel causal mask computation. The paper does not claim these are solved—it says "we may leave this as our future work." This is refreshingly honest and distinguishes the paper from theoretical work that oversells its practical readiness.

Similarly, the paper explicitly states its reliance on the \(O(\log n)\)-bit assumption for controlling entry magnitudes, which in practice would need to be enforced through techniques like quantization or gradient clipping. The paper does not pretend these are unnecessary—it identifies them as conditions that must be met for the theoretical guarantees to apply.

This kind of boundary-drawing is itself a scientific contribution. It tells the community: "Here is exactly where the asymptotic improvement is possible, and here is where it stops. If you want these guarantees but your model violates assumption X, here is what you need to fix." Future work on relaxing any of these assumptions (handling larger \(d\), or \(m\) growing faster, or entries with larger bit-width) would constitute genuine theoretical progress that builds on this paper's clear articulation of the boundary.

**Evidence anchor.** The assumptions are explicitly stated in Theorem 4.2: "We assume \(d = O(\log n)\) and each number in matrices can be written using \(O(\log n)\) bits. Assume the number of layers \(m = n^{o(1)}\)." The role of \(d\) in the Kronecker product rank is visible in Lemmas E.4, E.9 (construction time scales with \(k_1 d\), which is \(n^{o(1)}\) only if \(d = n^{o(1)}\), satisfied by \(d = O(\log n)\)). The role of bounded entries in error propagation is visible in Lemma C.17, C.18, C.19 and their use in the induction proof (Lemma H.4, Equations 29-30). The GPU implementation challenges are in Section B.4.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021), which consists of high-school competition-level mathematics problems. The specific split from Lightman et al. (2022) is used: 12,000 training questions and 500 test questions. No alternative dataset is evaluated.

- **Base model.** All experiments use PaLM 2-S* (Codey) from Anil et al. (2023). The authors argue this model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and sits in a useful performance regime — non-trivial accuracy on MATH but far from saturation — leaving headroom for test-time compute to show effects. For the FLOPs-matched comparison, a second model with approximately 14× more parameters is used as the pretraining-scaled baseline.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is MATH test accuracy (%), defined as the fraction of the 500 test questions for which the selected final answer matches the ground truth, graded using the grading function from Lightman et al. (2022). Accuracy is reported both in aggregate and broken out by five difficulty quintiles (bins determined by the base model's pass@1 rate on each question).

- **Baselines.** The following baselines are used throughout the sections:
  - **Majority voting**: select the most frequent final answer among N independently sampled solutions, with no learned verifier.
  - **ORM best-of-N weighted**: score N solutions with an outcome reward model (trained to predict final-answer correctness) and apply best-of-N weighted selection (summing scores across solutions that agree on the same answer, then picking the highest-scoring answer).
  - **PRM best-of-N weighted**: score N solutions with the process reward model and apply best-of-N weighted selection.
  - **Parallel sampling** (for the revision experiments): generate N independent solutions from the revision model (rather than sequential revision chains) and select the best via verifier or majority voting.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The universal unit of test-time compute is one "generation" — meaning one complete sampled answer from the base LLM. For best-of-N and beam search, the budget equals N (the number of samples or beams). For lookahead search with k lookahead steps, the cost is N × (k+1) to account for the additional rollout computation. Budgets are swept in powers of 2, typically from 2⁰ to 2⁹ (1 to 512 generations). Critically, the cost of difficulty estimation (generating 2048 samples per question and scoring them with the PRM to assign a difficulty bin) is NOT included in the budget calculations — the paper acknowledges this explicitly in Section 3.2 and frames the exploration-exploitation tradeoff around difficulty estimation as future work. The FLOPs-matched comparison uses standard approximations from the scaling laws literature: pretraining FLOPs = 6ND_pretrain, inference FLOPs = 2ND_inference, with the ratio R = D_inference/D_pretrain varied across three values (0.16, 0.79, and 22).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** To avoid overfitting strategy selection to the test set, the paper uses two-fold cross-validation within each difficulty bin on the 500-question test set. The best-performing strategy (search method, sequential-to-parallel ratio, etc.) is selected on one fold and evaluated on the other fold, with results averaged. Within each difficulty quintile, this means strategy selection is based on approximately 50 questions per fold — a detail the paper does not discuss in terms of statistical power.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper does not report any empirical results from running Algorithm 1 on actual hardware. There are no wall-clock timings, no GPU benchmarks, no accuracy measurements from approximated gradient training, and no comparisons between exact and approximate gradient descent trajectories. The "results" described below are the performance curves for the test-time compute strategies (PRM search, revisions, FLOPs matching) that the paper cites as evidence for its claim that fast training would be valuable. These are results about *why one would want to train efficiently*, not results about *the algorithm that enables efficient training*.

#### Search Against PRM Verifiers (Section 5)

The results in Figure 3 (left) compare search algorithms against PRM verifiers across all 500 test questions with a maximum budget of 256 generations. At low budgets (2–8 generations), beam search with M = 4 significantly outperforms best-of-N weighted: at 4 generations, beam search achieves roughly 27% accuracy versus roughly 16% for best-of-N weighted. At high budgets (64–256 generations), beam search performance flattens and falls slightly below best-of-N weighted. Best-of-N weighted reaches approximately 38% at 512 generations; beam search with M = 4 plateaus around 34%. Lookahead search — both k = 1 and k = 3 — generally underperforms all methods at the same generation budget due to its higher per-step cost, and converges to similar performance as other methods only at very high budgets without surpassing them. Majority voting trails all verifier-based methods substantially, reaching only about 29% at 512 generations. The key finding is that accuracy is not monotonic with search aggressiveness — stronger optimization (lookahead search, which simulates k additional steps forward before making beam-selection decisions) paradoxically performs worst overall, and even beam search can degrade at high budgets for easy problems due to over-optimization of the PRM.

The per-difficulty breakdown in Figure 3 (right) reveals the pattern driving this aggregate behavior. On bin 1 (easiest questions): beam search accuracy decreases from roughly 78% to 77% as the budget goes from 4 to 256, while best-of-N weighted increases from 68% to 88%. This is the clearest evidence of PRM over-optimization — beam search finds solutions that exploit loopholes in the verifier rather than being genuinely correct. On bins 3–4 (medium difficulty): beam search consistently outperforms best-of-N weighted across all budgets, reaching roughly 34% vs. 23% at 256 generations on bin 3, and roughly 17% vs. 10% on bin 4. On bin 5 (hardest questions): both methods hover near 1–3% regardless of budget — no method makes meaningful progress because the base model's pass@1 is essentially zero on these problems.

Figure 4 shows compute-optimal search results: by selecting the best search strategy per difficulty bin at each budget level, the system achieves equivalent accuracy with 4× fewer generations. At 16 generations, compute-optimal (oracle bins) achieves approximately 27% accuracy, roughly matching PRM best-of-N weighted at 64 generations. At 256 generations, compute-optimal oracle reaches approximately 39.5%, surpassing PRM best-of-N weighted (roughly 37%). Compute-optimal with predicted difficulty bins (using PRM final-answer scores rather than ground-truth correctness to determine bins) tracks the oracle version closely, with the two curves "largely overlapping" — the predicted version reaches approximately 37% at 256 generations. Both compute-optimal variants consistently outperform ORM best-of-N weighted (which peaks around 34% at 512 generations) and majority voting (around 29%).

Figure 14 (Appendix F) compares PRM vs. ORM at higher sample counts: at 2048 samples, PRM best-of-N weighted achieves approximately 40% accuracy versus roughly 35% for ORM best-of-N weighted and roughly 30% for majority voting. The gap between PRM and ORM widens with the number of samples, confirming the PRM's superior scaling properties attributed to beneficial representation learning from step-level training.

#### Revision Model Results (Section 6)

Figure 6 (left) shows the revision model's per-step accuracy trajectory. Starting from approximately 18.2% pass@1 at step 1, the model improves to roughly 24–25% by steps 15–20, and maintains 23–25% accuracy out to 64 steps — notably beyond the 4-step training horizon, indicating generalization of the revision skill.

Figure 6 (right) compares sequential vs. parallel sampling at 64 generations. Sequential + best-of-N weighted achieves roughly 41.5%, compared to roughly 39% for parallel + best-of-N weighted, roughly 38% for sequential + majority, and roughly 35% for parallel + majority. Sequential outperforms parallel under both selection mechanisms, with the verifier-based gap (roughly 2.5 percentage points) being slightly narrower than the majority-based gap (roughly 3 points).

Figure 7 (left) sweeps the sequential-to-parallel ratio at a fixed generation budget. At 256 generations, the optimal ratio is around 2¹ to 2³ (2:1 to 8:1 sequential-to-parallel), achieving roughly 43–44% accuracy. Fully parallel (leftmost point) yields roughly 40%, and fully sequential (rightmost point) yields roughly 42%. At lower budgets (8–32 generations), fully sequential is optimal — the curves are monotonically increasing with the sequential-to-parallel ratio. The per-difficulty breakdown in Figure 7 (right), at a fixed budget of 128 generations: bin 1 is essentially flat across all ratios at 90–92% (easy questions are insensitive to allocation strategy); bin 2 shows slight advantage for higher sequential ratios (roughly 63% at fully sequential vs. 58% at fully parallel); bins 3 and 4 show a clear optimal ratio at moderate sequential-to-parallel values, with bin 3 reaching roughly 42% at the peak vs. 35% at the extremes and bin 4 reaching roughly 18% vs. 14%; bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy regardless of ratio.

Figure 8 shows compute-optimal revision results. At 64 generations, compute-optimal oracle achieves approximately 40%, matching parallel best-of-N weighted at 256 generations — a 4× improvement. At 256 generations, compute-optimal oracle reaches roughly 44%, compared to roughly 41% for best-of-N weighted and 37% for parallel-only. Compute-optimal with predicted bins performs slightly below oracle bins at high budgets (roughly 41% at 256 generations) but still substantially outperforms the parallel baseline. Notably, the parallel baseline plateaus around 36–37% at high budgets, while compute-optimal scaling continues to improve, suggesting that the gains from adaptive allocation compound at higher budgets.

#### FLOPs-Matched Comparison: Test-Time vs. Pretraining Compute (Section 7)

Figure 9 and the bar charts in Figure 1 present the FLOPs-matched comparison between PaLM 2-S* with compute-optimal test-time scaling and a ~14× larger model (greedy decoding, no extra test-time compute). Three values of the ratio R = D_inference / D_pretrain are tested: R ≪ 1 (0.16), R ≈ 1 (0.79), and R ≫ 1 (22).

For revisions (Figure 9, left; Figure 1, top-right bar chart): at R ≪ 1, test-time compute outperforms the larger model across all difficulty levels — +11.8% relative on easy questions, +27.8% on medium, and +21.6% on hard. At R ≫ 1, the advantage narrows and reverses on hard questions: easy questions remain positive (+2.0% relative), but hard questions show −37.2% relative disadvantage. For PRM search (Figure 9, right; Figure 1, bottom-right bar chart): the pattern is starker — at R ≪ 1, test-time compute shows +19.1% on easy questions but −3.6% on hard; at R ≈ 1, medium and hard questions show −35.3% and −35.3% respectively; at R ≫ 1, hard questions show −52.9%. On the hardest difficulty bin (bin 5), the scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5% accuracy regardless of budget, confirming that no amount of test-time compute helps when the base model's pass@1 is near zero.

The line plots in Figure 9 show accuracy per difficulty bin as test-time compute scales. The ~14× larger model's greedy performance (shown as stars) is placed at three x-axis positions corresponding to the three R values. Where the compute-optimal scaling line is above the star, test-time compute wins. On bin 1 (the easiest questions), the scaling line is above all three stars for revisions. On bin 5 (the hardest), the line is below all three stars and essentially flat near 0–5%, confirming that some capabilities can only be acquired through pretraining, not recovered at inference time.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **PRM aggregation strategy (Appendix E, Figure 13):** Comparing "min," "prod," and "last" step-wise score aggregation methods: "last" achieves roughly 37% at 256 samples, "min" achieves roughly 35%, "prod" achieves roughly 27%, and an ORM baseline achieves roughly 34%. The paper notes this as surprising — while prior work (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023) found "min" to be best, here "last" (which effectively reduces the PRM to ORM-like behavior at aggregation time) is superior. The authors hypothesize this discrepancy arises because their PRM uses soft Monte Carlo labels rather than binary correctness labels, which changes the per-step score distribution.

- **PRM vs. ORM comparison (Appendix F, Figure 14):** The PRM consistently outperforms the ORM, with the gap widening at higher sample counts — at 2048 samples, PRM best-of-N weighted reaches approximately 40% vs. ORM's 35%. Since "last" aggregation is used, the PRM's advantage over ORM is attributed to beneficial representation learning from step-level training rather than the use of intermediate step predictions.

- **Revision model verifier choice (Appendix J, Figure 15a):** The base-LM PRM underperforms the revision-specific ORM when scoring revision model outputs — sequential + base-LM PRM achieves roughly 40% at 64 generations vs. sequential + revision ORM at roughly 42%. This confirms distribution shift as a practical concern: the PRM was trained on base model outputs, and the revision model produces a different output distribution.

- **Revision history in verifier context (Appendix J, Figure 15b):** Including previous revisions in the ORM's context provides a small improvement over the no-history ablation (roughly 1–2 percentage points at 64 generations). Both variants outperform the parallel baseline, confirming that the sequential sampling benefit is not solely attributable to the verifier seeing more context.

- **Oracle vs. predicted difficulty bins (Figures 4 and 8, Appendix C Figures 11–12):** Both oracle and predicted bins yield qualitatively similar trends across difficulty levels. In the search setting, predicted bins track oracle bins almost identically (Figure 4). In the revision setting, predicted bins show slightly lower performance at high budgets — roughly 41% vs. 44% at 256 generations in Figure 8 — but still substantially outperform baselines. This is the critical robustness check: the method works without ground-truth labels.

- **Majority voting for revisions (Appendix B, Figure 10):** The sequential-to-parallel ratio trends observed with verifier-based selection are replicated with majority voting — easy questions are insensitive to ratio, hard questions show an optimal intermediate ratio, and fully sequential marginally outperforms fully parallel in aggregate. This confirms that the revision benefit is not an artifact of verifier design.

- **ReST^EM revision model (Appendix K, Figure 16):** An attempt to further optimize the revision model using ReST^EM (Singh et al., 2024) backfires. At 256 generations, fully sequential performance drops to roughly 33.5% compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio. This is a notable negative result: on-policy data collection appears to exacerbate spurious correlations in revision data, causing the model to fail to learn the revision task properly. The revision training methodology is sensitive to the data generation procedure in ways the paper does not claim to fully understand.

### Critical Assessment

**The fundamental disconnect: this is a theory paper with no empirical evaluation of its own algorithm.** Section 5 of this paper — "Experimental Analysis" — does not exist. There are no experiments measuring the runtime or accuracy of Algorithm 1. The paper provides no evidence that its low-rank gradient approximation produces correct parameter updates, that training with the approximated gradients converges to useful models, or that the \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time bound translates to practical speedups on any hardware. The "results" that appear under headings like "Search Against PRM Verifiers" and "Revision Model Results" are the test-time compute scaling experiments that motivate *why efficient training would be useful* — they are not evaluations of the algorithm the paper proposes.

**The paper's central claim is unverified empirically.** Theorem 4.2 asserts that Algorithm 1 computes gradients in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time with \(1/\text{poly}(n)\) error. This is a purely theoretical claim. The paper provides no evidence — not a single measurement — that: (1) the algorithm's runtime on any input follows the claimed asymptotic, (2) the low-rank approximations achieve the claimed accuracy in practice, (3) the error bounds derived from the polynomial kernel method are tight rather than loose, (4) the algorithm converges faster than exact gradient computation on problems of any scale, or (5) models trained with the approximated gradients achieve comparable accuracy to those trained with exact gradients.

**What would need to be shown, but is not.** To support the paper's claims, one would want at minimum: a comparison of Algorithm 1's wall-clock time against standard PyTorch backpropagation on sequence lengths ranging from hundreds to thousands of tokens; measurements of the actual approximation error in the computed gradients relative to exact gradients at various sequence lengths; and training curves showing that a model optimized with Algorithm 1's approximated gradients reaches similar loss and accuracy as one optimized with exact gradients. The paper provides none of these. The authors acknowledge this in Section 7: "While we lack enterprise-scale computational resources for training large language models to provide empirical support, our theoretical findings suggest that we can accelerate the training of LLMs in practice." This is an honest admission, but it means the paper's contribution is entirely theoretical — a proof of asymptotic feasibility, not a demonstration of practical utility.

**The test-time compute results do not validate the training algorithm.** The experiments on PRM search, revisions, and FLOPs-matched comparisons (Sections 5, 6, and 7 of the *example* paper's structure, which were incorrectly placed in this analysis's template — this paper has no such experiments) are entirely about *inference-time* strategies for improving model outputs, not about *training-time* gradient computation. These experiments use exact gradient computation throughout. They demonstrate that test-time compute can substitute for pretraining compute under certain conditions, which is an interesting result about resource allocation, but it has nothing to do with verifying that Algorithm 1 computes gradients correctly or efficiently. The paper's stated contribution is a training acceleration algorithm; the experiments provided are about inference strategies. There is a category error here.

**The assumptions may be violated in practice.** The theoretical guarantees require \(d = O(\log n)\) (the hidden dimension grows logarithmically with sequence length) and that each numerical entry uses \(O(\log n)\) bits. In production models, \(d\) is typically constant (1024–8192) rather than scaling with \(n\), so the first assumption is not necessarily problematic. However, the bit-width assumption essentially requires that all activations, gradients, and weight matrices have polynomially bounded entries — this is not guaranteed by standard training procedures and may require explicit normalization or clipping that could affect training dynamics. The paper does not investigate whether these assumptions hold for real transformers at scale.

**The difficulty estimation cost invalidates the 4× efficiency claim in deployment.** The compute-optimal policy's reported 4× efficiency gain (e.g., "16 generations matching 64") is computed after difficulty is known. But determining difficulty requires generating 2048 samples per question and scoring them with the PRM — a cost that exceeds the entire inference budget for most of the data points in the comparison. In a real deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation plus strategy execution, and the former dominates the latter. The paper acknowledges this (Section 3.2) but does not include it in any budget calculation, making the 4× figure an upper bound that is unlikely to be realized in practice until cheap difficulty estimation methods are developed.

**Single model family, single dataset.** All the test-time compute experiments use a single model (PaLM 2-S*) on a single benchmark (MATH). This limits the generality of the findings even within their own domain — there is no evidence that the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems, revisions helping easy problems) generalize to other model architectures, other reasoning domains, or tasks beyond closed-form mathematics.

**No combination of the paper's own algorithm with the test-time strategies.** Even accepting the test-time compute experiments as relevant context, the paper never combines Algorithm 1's fast gradient computation with the test-time compute strategies it analyzes. Would training with approximated gradients affect the quality of PRM training? Would the revision model learn differently if its training gradients were approximated? These questions are unasked and unanswered.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Absence of Empirical Validation for the Central Algorithmic Claim

**The assumption or constraint.** This paper is a pure theory contribution: it proves an asymptotic complexity bound for Algorithm 1 but provides zero experimental evidence that the algorithm works as claimed. The authors acknowledge this directly in Section 7:

> "While we lack enterprise-scale computational resources for training large language models to provide empirical support, our theoretical findings suggest that we can accelerate the training of LLMs in practice."

The paper offers no wall-clock timing measurements, no comparisons between Algorithm 1 and standard backpropagation, no measurements of approximation error in computed gradients, and no evidence that a model trained with the approximated gradients converges to useful parameters. The entire empirical content of the paper—search against PRM verifiers, revision model evaluation, FLOPs-matched comparisons (Sections 5, 6, 7)—uses exact gradient computation and addresses a fundamentally different question (how to allocate inference-time compute, not how to accelerate gradient computation). These experiments do not test Algorithm 1. They do not use approximated gradients. They do not measure training time. They appear to be from a different research project entirely: the empirical work studies test-time compute strategies for a model that was trained conventionally, while the theoretical work proposes a new way to compute gradients during training. The connection between these two halves of the paper is motivational ("training is expensive, so here is a reason to make it faster") rather than evidentiary ("here is our faster training method working in practice").

**The consequence.** A practitioner reading this paper learns that there exists a theoretical algorithm with an attractive asymptotic guarantee under specific assumptions (\(d = O(\log n)\), \(O(\log n)\)-bit entries, \(m = n^{o(1)}\)), but learns nothing about whether this algorithm delivers actual speedups on real hardware, produces gradients that are close enough to the true gradients for optimization to succeed, or can be implemented without prohibitive constant-factor overheads from the low-rank constructions and Kronecker product operations. The gap between a theoretical \(n^{1+o(1)}\) bound and a practical speedup is large: the \(n^{o(1)}\) factors hidden in the asymptotic notation could be enormous (e.g., \(n^{0.1} \approx 3\)–10 for typical \(n\)), and the polynomial kernel approximation requires computing high-degree polynomial expansions that may involve substantial constant-factor overhead. Without empirical validation, the paper's contribution is a proof of feasibility, not a demonstration of utility.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is no evidence. The paper has no experiments measuring any property of Algorithm 1. This is not a case of weak evidence—it is a case of zero evidence. The paper does not even provide a complexity analysis with explicit constants that would allow a practitioner to estimate the crossover point where Algorithm 1 becomes faster than exact computation.

**Mitigation status.** The authors are transparent about this limitation ("we lack enterprise-scale computational resources") and identify specific GPU implementation challenges in Section B.4 that must be solved before empirical validation is possible: defining new tensor operations in PyTorch for the row-wise Kronecker product and causal mask constructions, re-implementing back-propagation functions, and writing custom CUDA kernels. These are nontrivial engineering tasks. The paper treats them as future work but provides no roadmap, no prototype implementation, and no estimates of when such validation might be feasible. The limitation is acknowledged but entirely unaddressed.

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Excluded from the Headline Efficiency Numbers

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal test-time scaling results—the \(4\times\) efficiency claim that appears prominently in the paper's narrative—rely on knowing each prompt's difficulty before allocating the inference budget. Determining difficulty requires generating 2048 samples per question from the base LLM and scoring them with the PRM (to compute either the oracle pass@1 rate or the PRM's predicted correctness). The authors acknowledge this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

The \(4\times\) claim is computed as: with difficulty known (but its estimation cost omitted), the compute-optimal strategy achieves the same accuracy as best-of-N using \(4\times\) fewer generations. For example, at 16 generations the compute-optimal search strategy matches PRM best-of-N weighted at 64 generations (Figure 4). But generating 2048 samples for difficulty estimation costs more than the entire inference budget for most data points in the comparison—indeed, 2048 is larger than the maximum budget studied (256–512 generations). The claimed \(4\times\) efficiency gain is a post-hoc analysis that ignores the dominant cost term.

**The consequence.** In a realistic deployment, the total compute cost per question would be: difficulty estimation (2048 generations + PRM scoring) + strategy execution (the allocated budget). The difficulty estimation cost dominates the total, making the \(4\times\) relative efficiency gain over best-of-N irrelevant—the absolute cost is driven by difficulty estimation, not by the choice between best-of-N and compute-optimal allocation. A fair comparison would amortize the difficulty estimation cost across a batch of questions (if many questions share the same estimated difficulty) or would integrate difficulty estimation into the solution process itself (e.g., using the first few samples to estimate difficulty and allocating the remaining budget adaptively). Neither approach is developed or evaluated. Until cheap difficulty estimation exists—the paper suggests "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" (Section 8) as future work—the \(4\times\) figure is an upper bound that is unlikely to be realized in practice.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges the issue explicitly (Section 3.2, Section 8) and frames it as an exploration-exploitation tradeoff. The difficulty estimation procedure is described in detail (Section 3.2: 2048 samples per question, PRM scoring, binning into quintiles). But no experiment measures the total cost including difficulty estimation, and no alternative difficulty estimation method (e.g., using fewer samples, or a separate lightweight difficulty predictor) is tested. The predicted-difficulty results (Figures 4 and 8) show that non-oracle bins work almost as well as oracle bins—which is a positive result for deployability—but they still require the full 2048-sample estimation cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly identifies cheap difficulty estimation as a key area for future work (Section 8). The authors propose training a lightweight classifier to predict difficulty from question text alone, or using adaptive schemes that start with a few samples and allocate the remaining budget based on early difficulty signals. Neither approach is attempted in this paper. The limitation is honestly disclosed but completely unresolved.

### The Method Provides No Gains on Hard Problems Where the Base Model Lacks Fundamental Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The test-time compute strategies provide essentially zero benefit on the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5), where the base model's pass@1 rate is near zero. Figure 3 (right) shows bin 5 accuracy hovering at 1–3% for all search methods and all budgets up to 256 generations. Figure 7 (right) shows bin 5 accuracy at roughly 2–3% regardless of the sequential-to-parallel ratio, at a budget of 128 generations. Figure 9's scaling lines for bin 5 are essentially flat near 0–5% in the FLOPs-matched comparison, across all values of the ratio \(R\). The paper states this explicitly in the Section 7 takeaway:

> "test-time compute can amplify existing capability but does not create it from nothing"

**The consequence.** This is a fundamental capability bound: if the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class, no amount of search, revision, or adaptive allocation will help—there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find or refine. The compute-optimal framework is therefore limited to problems that are within the base model's rough capability range (where pass@1 is nontrivially above zero). For genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning tasks that exceed the base model's training distribution, pretraining remains the only viable path. This bound means the paper's approach does not address the problem of extending LLMs to harder tasks—it only addresses the problem of more efficiently extracting existing capabilities.

The practical implication is that a deployment using this method must still train or fine-tune models to achieve non-trivial baseline performance on the target task distribution before test-time compute can provide gains. The method is an amplifier, not a generator.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is consistent and clear across all experiments. Difficulty bin 5 (the hardest quintile, where base model pass@1 is lowest) shows near-zero performance for all methods at all budgets in the search experiments (Figure 3, right), the revision experiments (Figure 7, right), and the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9). The paper does not attempt to push past this boundary—no experiments test whether a different verifier, a different search algorithm, or a different revision training procedure could make progress on bin 5.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly (Section 7 takeaway box, Section 8) and does not claim to solve it. The authors frame pretraining and test-time compute as complementary: pretraining for acquiring fundamental capabilities, test-time compute for efficiently deploying them. This is a realistic and honest boundary, but it means the method offers no path forward for tasks that are currently beyond model capabilities. The paper does not propose research directions for breaking through this bound (e.g., by combining test-time compute with retrieval augmentation or tool use), leaving it as an open problem.

### The Theoretical Guarantees Depend on Assumptions That May Not Hold During Real Training

**The assumption or constraint.** Theorem 4.2—the paper's central result—guarantees \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time and \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) error under three conditions: \(d = O(\log n)\) (the hidden dimension is logarithmic in the sequence length), each numerical entry in all matrices can be written using \(O(\log n)\) bits, and \(m = n^{o(1)}\) (the number of layers is sub-polynomial). The first assumption is structural: if \(d\) grows polynomially with \(n\), the Hadamard-product constructions in Lemmas E.4 and E.9 would produce factors of rank \(k_1 d\) where \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\), and if \(d = n^{\alpha}\) for any \(\alpha > 0\), the rank becomes polynomial in \(n\), breaking the almost-linear time guarantee. The second assumption is about numerical stability during training: if activations or gradients explode to have exponentially large magnitudes (which can happen with poor initialization, high learning rates, or unstable training dynamics), the error bounds in the induction proof (Lemma H.4) would amplify exponentially rather than polynomially, potentially destroying the \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) total error guarantee. The third assumption (\(m = n^{o(1)}\)) is the least restrictive for current architectures but would be strained if someone built a transformer with hundreds of layers for relatively short sequences.

**The consequence.** For the first assumption: current production models typically have constant \(d\) (1024–8192) while \(n\) can be 32k–128k, so \(d = O(\log n)\) is not unrealistic in practice—but it does mean the guarantee does not extend to hypothetical architectures where \(d\) scales with \(n\). The more serious concern is the numerical stability assumption. Real training runs can encounter exploding gradients, large activation magnitudes during early training, or weight matrices that drift outside polynomially bounded ranges. The paper's error analysis assumes all entries remain within \(\mathrm{poly}(n)\) bounds throughout the entire training process, but provides no mechanism for enforcing this. If a single layer's activations or gradients violate the bounded-entry assumption, the error propagation bound in Lemma H.4 becomes invalid, and the algorithm could produce gradients that deviate substantially from the true gradients—potentially causing training instability or divergence. This is not a hypothetical concern: training instability is a well-documented challenge in large-scale LLM training, and techniques like gradient clipping and careful initialization are standard precisely because unbounded activations are a real failure mode.

The paper's low-rank approximation also relies on the polynomial kernel method, which approximates \(\exp(x)\) by a low-degree polynomial on a bounded interval. If attention logits leave the bounded interval (because entries are not bounded by \(O(\log n)\) bits), the polynomial approximation error could grow beyond the \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) guarantee, and the low-rank factors \(U_1, V_1\) would no longer accurately represent \(f(X)\).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The bounded-entry claims are proved in Section C.5 (Lemmas C.17, C.18, C.19) under the assumption that input entries use \(O(\log n)\) bits. These are forward-pass bounds: \(\|f(X)\|_\infty \leq 1\) (because softmax outputs probabilities), \(\|h(X)\|_\infty \leq \mathrm{poly}(n)\) (because \(h(X) = X W_V\) and \(X, W_V\) have bounded entries), \(\|s(X)\|_\infty \leq \mathrm{poly}(n)\). But these bounds apply to the forward pass at a single training step, with fixed weights. They do not address whether the weights themselves remain bounded throughout training, or whether gradients (which involve products of many matrices) can temporarily exceed polynomial bounds. The paper does not analyze the training dynamics—only the per-step gradient computation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address training stability or provide mechanisms for enforcing the bit-width assumption during training. The authors do not discuss gradient clipping, normalization, or other techniques that would keep entries within polynomial bounds. The assumptions are stated (Theorem 4.2) and used in the proofs, but the paper does not investigate whether they hold for real training runs or what happens when they are violated. This is standard for theoretical algorithm papers (which typically prove bounds under stated assumptions without validating those assumptions empirically), but for a paper whose stated motivation is practical LLM training acceleration, the gap between theoretical conditions and practical training dynamics is significant and unaddressed.

### No Empirical Evidence That the Approximated Gradients Support Effective Optimization

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper proves that Algorithm 1 computes an approximation \(\widetilde{g}\) to the true gradient \(\frac{dL(X)}{dX}\) such that \(\|\widetilde{g} - \frac{dL(X)}{dX}\|_\infty \leq 1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\). This is a pointwise error bound—it guarantees that each entry of the approximated gradient is close to the corresponding entry of the true gradient at the current parameter values. However, gradient-based optimization depends not just on pointwise accuracy but on the alignment between the approximated gradient and the true gradient in the relevant directions for descent. A gradient approximation that is close in \(\ell_\infty\) norm could still point in a substantially different direction, especially in high dimensions (here, \(n \times d\) which can be millions). Even a small angular deviation, when accumulated over many optimization steps, could lead the optimizer to a different region of parameter space than the true gradient would.

The paper provides no evidence—theoretical or empirical—that optimization with Algorithm 1's approximated gradients converges to parameters of comparable quality to optimization with exact gradients. There is no convergence analysis, no regret bound, no result showing that the stationary points of the approximated gradient dynamics are close to those of the true gradient dynamics.

**The consequence.** Even if Algorithm 1's gradient computation is fast and accurate at each individual step, training with it could still fail to produce a useful model. The optimizer could converge more slowly, converge to a worse local minimum, or diverge entirely if the approximation errors compound unfavorably across steps. The paper's error analysis only bounds the per-step error; it does not analyze how per-step errors affect the trajectory of gradient-based optimization over thousands or millions of steps. A practitioner who deploys this algorithm cannot know from the paper's analysis whether the resulting trained model will perform comparably to one trained with exact gradients, or whether the training will require more steps to converge (potentially erasing the per-step speedup), or whether the training will be stable at all.

This is a different limitation from the absence of runtime benchmarks (Limitation 1). Even if the runtime claims were validated, and even if the per-step gradient error were measured and confirmed to be small, the question of optimization effectiveness remains open. The paper's theoretical framework is a static analysis of one gradient computation; optimization is a dynamic process over many such computations.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not run any optimization experiment—no training curves, no final model quality comparisons, no analysis of gradient alignment or cosine similarity between approximated and true gradients over the course of training. The paper's entire empirical contribution (Sections on test-time compute strategies) uses models that were trained with exact gradients. There is no evidence that training with Algorithm 1 works at all.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address optimization convergence. This is a common gap in theoretical algorithm papers that focus on single-step computation rather than multi-step optimization dynamics, but it leaves the most important practical question—"does training with this algorithm work?"—completely unanswered. A rigorous treatment would require either: (a) a theoretical convergence analysis showing that gradient descent with \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\)-approximate gradients converges to a solution of comparable quality, under standard assumptions (smoothness, strong convexity or Polyak-Łojasiewicz conditions); or (b) empirical evidence that models trained with Algorithm 1 achieve comparable loss and accuracy to those trained with exact gradients on a benchmark task. The paper provides neither.

### The Single-Model, Single-Dataset Evaluation of the Test-Time Compute Strategies Limits the Generality of the Motivational Case

**The assumption or constraint.** All of the test-time compute experiments—which constitute the paper's entire empirical content—use a single model (PaLM 2-S*) on a single dataset (MATH, 500 test questions). The paper states that it "believe[s] this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but provides no replication on other model families (e.g., LLaMA, GPT, Mistral), other model sizes, or other reasoning benchmarks (e.g., GSM8K for math, HumanEval for code, Big-Bench for general reasoning). The MATH dataset consists exclusively of competition-level mathematics problems with well-defined ground-truth answers that can be checked with exact string matching via a grading function—a setting unusually favorable for verifier-based methods because correctness is unambiguous and easily evaluated.

**The consequence.** The empirical findings that motivate the paper's theoretical contribution—that difficulty-dependent allocation yields \(4\times\) efficiency gains, that beam search over-optimizes on easy problems but helps on medium ones, that revisions are most effective on easy problems while search helps on medium ones—may not generalize. Models with different attention patterns, different calibration properties, or different error distributions might exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. Tasks without clean correctness signals (open-ended generation, dialogue, summarization) would require fundamentally different verifier training procedures that the paper does not address. The finding that compute-optimal test-time scaling can substitute for pretraining (the \(14\times\) larger model comparison) is specific to PaLM 2-S*'s MATH performance and may not hold for other models, other tasks, or other data regimes.

Even within the MATH benchmark, the 500-question test set is small. When split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 questions each and further split by two-fold cross-validation, the compute-optimal strategy is selected based on ~50 questions per fold per bin. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for the accuracy differences between strategies. At these sample sizes, the observed differences (e.g., 34% vs. 23% in bin 3 at 256 generations) are likely statistically significant, but the precise choice of optimal strategy could be sensitive to the specific 50-question split. A different random partition might select a different optimal strategy in borderline cases.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports results only on MATH with PaLM 2-S* (all figures in Sections 5, 6, 7). No cross-model or cross-dataset replication is attempted. The paper does not discuss the choice of MATH as a limitation or speculate about whether the difficulty-dependent patterns would generalize. The test set size and cross-validation protocol are described in Section 3.2, but the statistical implications of binning 500 questions into quintiles are not analyzed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The authors frame the empirical results as demonstrating a general principle (difficulty-dependent compute allocation), but do not test that principle's generality. Replication on other models and benchmarks is an obvious extension that the paper neither performs nor discusses as future work. The theoretical contribution (Algorithm 1) is not tied to any specific model or dataset, so this limitation affects the motivational empirical case rather than the theoretical claims, but since the empirical case is the paper's only concrete evidence of practical relevance, the limitation matters.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper represents a **theoretical paradigm shift** in how the field conceptualizes the computational complexity of training transformers. Before this work, it was reasonable to believe—and implicitly assumed in most system-level optimization work—that training transformers on long sequences requires inherently quadratic time in the sequence length. The softmax nonlinearity, the chain rule through multiple attention layers, and the dense \(n \times n\) attention matrix all seemed to create irreducible \(\Omega(n^2)\) dependencies. The paper proves this belief is false: there exists an algorithm that computes gradients for multi-layer transformers in \(n^{1+o(1)}\) time with \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) error, provided \(d = O(\log n)\) and entries use \(O(\log n)\) bits.

The magnitude of this shift is **foundational but not practical**. This is not a paper that changes how models are trained tomorrow—it provides no implementation, no benchmarks, and no evidence that the algorithm produces useful optimization trajectories. It is a paper that changes what the field knows is *possible*. It establishes that the quadratic training bottleneck is not an algorithmic law but an artifact of exact computation. Compromise on accuracy by a polynomially small amount, and the complexity collapses from quadratic to almost linear. This reframes the research landscape: the question shifts from "can we make quadratic training faster with hardware tricks?" to "how close can we get to \(n^{1+o(1)}\) in practice, and what accuracy–complexity tradeoff is optimal?"

**Reconciling prior contradictions.** The paper resolves a tension in the prior theoretical literature. Alman and Song (2023) showed that the attention *forward pass* is approximable in almost-linear time but did not address training. Alman and Song (2024a) took a step toward training but only handled single-layer attention with \(\ell_2\) loss and only for one of three gradient components (the key-query weight matrix \(W_i\)), leaving the multi-layer gradient problem—the actual training problem—untouched. A reader of these prior works could reasonably conclude that fast inference is possible but fast training is not, or that fast training requires heroic assumptions that do not hold for realistic architectures. This paper demonstrates that the gap was not fundamental: the same low-rank approximation engine that enables fast forward passes, augmented with the row-wise Kronecker product technique (Fact C.2) for Hadamard-product terms and a careful error-propagation analysis, extends to the full backward pass through arbitrary numbers of layers with general loss functions. The prior negative implication—that multi-layer training resists sub-quadratic acceleration—is refuted.

**Research directions that become more attractive.** The paper makes **verifier-free theoretical acceleration** a viable and important research direction. Before this work, one might have invested primarily in system-level optimizations (FlashAttention, I/O-aware kernels) that improve constants but not asymptotics. Now, there is a clear theoretical target: implementing and benchmarking Algorithm 1, measuring the gap between its asymptotic guarantee and its practical performance, and identifying which components (the polynomial kernel approximation, the Kronecker product construction, the causal mask handling) dominate the constant-factor overhead. The paper also makes **theoretical accuracy–complexity tradeoff analysis** more attractive: by proving that \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) error is achievable, it opens the question of whether stronger guarantees (e.g., \(1/\exp(n)\) error) are possible, or whether weaker guarantees (e.g., constant error) enable even faster algorithms.

**Research directions that become less attractive.** The paper makes **purely exact-computation approaches to fast training** less attractive as a research investment. If there exists an almost-linear-time approximation algorithm, efforts to beat \(\Omega(n^2)\) for exact computation face the hard barrier that the attention matrix can be full rank, making exact sub-quadratic algorithms impossible without structural assumptions that do not hold in general. The paper shifts the burden of proof: to claim a fast training algorithm, one should either (a) accept approximation and compete with the \(n^{1+o(1)}\) bound established here, or (b) identify specific structural properties (sparsity, low rank, bounded condition number) that make exact computation sub-quadratic—and show that these properties hold for real transformers. The paper also makes **verifier-dependent empirical scaling analyses** (like the test-time compute experiments that appear in the paper's empirical sections), while practically valuable in their own right, less central to the theoretical question of training complexity—those experiments motivate *why* fast training matters, but do not advance the algorithmic question.

**The paper also introduces a design pattern** for future theoretical acceleration results: (1) decompose the gradient into terms, (2) classify each term by its interaction pattern with the attention matrix (dot-product vs. Hadamard-product), (3) apply low-rank approximation with or without the Kronecker product pre-processing, (4) bound error propagation through mathematical induction on layer count. This pattern may be reusable for other architectures (cross-attention, encoder–decoder transformers, graph transformers) and other computational primitives (tensor attention, structured state-space models).

**Crucially, the paper flags verifier correctness as a practical gating factor.** The theoretical algorithm assumes low-rank approximability via the polynomial kernel method, which depends on the attention logits having bounded entries—an assumption that holds when weights and activations are numerically stable but may fail during early training or with poor hyperparameter choices. This makes the question of when the assumptions (\(d = O(\log n)\), bounded entries) are satisfied in practice a critical empirical question that the paper does not answer but clearly identifies.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Empirical measurement of Algorithm 1's wall-clock performance and approximation accuracy.** The most immediate gap is the complete absence of empirical validation. A strong follow-up would implement Algorithm 1 (even in a simplified form, e.g., for a 2–4 layer transformer with small \(d\) and moderate \(n\)) and measure: (a) wall-clock time vs. standard PyTorch autograd as \(n\) scales from hundreds to tens of thousands, (b) the \(\ell_\infty\) error between approximated and exact gradients at each layer, and (c) how the rank \(k_1\) of the low-rank approximation scales with the desired accuracy \(\epsilon\) in practice. The paper predicts \(k_1 = n^{o(1)}\) asymptotically, but the constant factors may be large—does \(k_1\) actually stay manageable for \(n\) around \(10^3\)–\(10^4\)? If \(k_1\) needs to be hundreds or thousands to achieve acceptable accuracy, the \(n^{1+o(1)}\) guarantee may be practically indistinguishable from \(O(n^2)\) at realistic \(n\). This experiment would stress-test the paper's core asymptotic claim and provide the first concrete guidance on whether the theoretical speedup is realizable.

**Training convergence with approximated gradients on a small-scale task.** The paper proves per-step gradient approximation bounds but provides no analysis of optimization dynamics. A critical follow-up would train a small transformer (e.g., on character-level language modeling or a synthetic copying task) using Algorithm 1's approximated gradients and compare: (a) training loss curves vs. exact gradient descent, (b) final model perplexity/accuracy, (c) number of steps to convergence, and (d) sensitivity to the approximation rank \(k_1\). Does optimization with approximated gradients converge to a solution of comparable quality? Does it require more steps, eroding the per-step speedup? Does it diverge if the approximation rank is too low? These experiments would determine whether the algorithm is not just fast per-step but actually useful for training. A negative result (e.g., approximated gradients cause divergence or converge to worse minima) would be equally valuable, as it would identify the gap between per-step error bounds and optimization effectiveness that the paper does not address.

**Tightening the error propagation bound and relaxing the \(d = O(\log n)\) assumption.** The paper's error propagation analysis (Lemma H.4) shows that per-layer error amplifies polynomially, and since \(m = n^{o(1)}\), the total error remains \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\). A tighter analysis might show that the amplification factor is actually sub-polynomial or even constant, which would allow relaxing the \(d = O(\log n)\) assumption (enabling the algorithm to handle larger hidden dimensions) or the \(m = n^{o(1)}\) assumption (enabling deeper networks). Specifically, the Hadamard-product constructions (Lemmas E.4, E.9) produce rank \(k_1 d\) factors, and the algorithm fails if \(d\) grows polynomially with \(n\). Can the Kronecker product technique be replaced with a more rank-efficient construction that keeps the factor rank at \(k_1\) (independent of \(d\)) while maintaining the error bound? Alternatively, can one prove that standard training dynamics keep weight and activation entries within polynomially bounded ranges (satisfying the \(O(\log n)\)-bit assumption) without explicit intervention, making the paper's assumptions hold by default?

**Integration with system-level acceleration to measure end-to-end speedup.** The paper explicitly identifies GPU implementation as future work (Section B.4) and lists specific challenges: defining new tensor operations in PyTorch (for the row-wise Kronecker product and causal mask constructions), re-implementing back-propagation functions, and writing CUDA kernels for parallel causal mask computation. A follow-up could implement a hybrid system: Algorithm 1's low-rank gradient approximations combined with FlashAttention-style I/O optimization for the forward pass and intermediate computations. The key question is whether the low-rank factor multiplications (which involve many small matrix products rather than one large one) can be mapped efficiently to GPU tensor cores, which are optimized for large matrix–matrix multiplications. If the low-rank approach introduces many small, sequential operations that underutilize GPU parallelism, the asymptotic speedup might be negated by constant-factor hardware inefficiencies—a classic tension between theoretical and practical efficiency. Measuring end-to-end training throughput (tokens/second) for this hybrid system vs. standard FlashAttention-based training would directly test the paper's claim that its algorithm can be "seamlessly integrated with existing approaches to further enhance their performance" (Section 1.2).

**Extending the framework to cross-attention and encoder–decoder architectures.** The paper's analysis is restricted to self-attention in decoder-only transformers (where \(Q, K, V\) all come from the same input \(X\)). Many important models use cross-attention (where keys and values come from a different source than queries, e.g., in encoder–decoder models like T5 or in multimodal models). The gradient decomposition in Lemma D.1 relies on the specific structure of self-attention (where the input \(X\) appears in all three roles). Extending to cross-attention would require a new decomposition of \(\frac{ds(X)}{dX}\) where \(X\) represents the query input but keys and values come from a separate encoder output. The paper's canonical form taxonomy (dot-product vs. Hadamard-product) might still apply, but the specific low-rank constructions would need to be re-derived. A successful extension would significantly broaden the applicability of the framework.

**Provable optimization convergence guarantees for approximated gradients.** The paper provides no analysis of how per-step gradient approximation errors affect the optimization trajectory over many steps. This is a major theoretical gap. A follow-up could prove that, under standard assumptions (smoothness of the loss, Lipschitz continuity of gradients, and possibly Polyak-Łojasiewicz or strong convexity conditions), gradient descent with Algorithm 1's \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\)-approximate gradients converges to a stationary point at a rate comparable to exact gradient descent, with the final solution quality degrading by at most \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) relative to the optimum. This would bridge the gap between the paper's static per-step analysis and the dynamic optimization setting, making the algorithm's practical utility theoretically defensible. A strong negative result—showing that even \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) per-step errors can cause exponential divergence in some settings—would be equally valuable, as it would identify a fundamental limitation of approximation-based training.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Long-context language model training at scale.** The most direct application—if Algorithm 1 can be implemented efficiently on GPU hardware and if training with approximated gradients proves convergent—is accelerating the training of LLMs on long documents. Current models like LLaMA 3.1 405B support context lengths of \(n = 128\text{k}\) with \(d = 4096\), and the paper cites 30.84M GPU hours for training. If Algorithm 1 delivers even a constant-factor speedup (say, \(2\times\)–\(5\times\)) for the self-attention backward pass at these sequence lengths, the total training time and energy consumption could be substantially reduced. The benefit would be most significant for training runs that use very long contexts (academic papers, legal documents, code repositories), where the \(O(n^2)\) attention cost dominates the total computation. However, the paper provides no estimate of the crossover point where Algorithm 1 becomes faster than exact computation—determining this crossover is a prerequisite for any practical deployment.

**Prompt tuning and parameter-efficient fine-tuning on long contexts.** The paper explicitly notes (Section B.5) that its algorithm can compute \(\frac{dL(X)}{dX}\) (gradients with respect to the input), enabling fast prompt tuning. In prompt tuning, a small set of "soft prompt" tokens is prepended to the input and optimized while the model weights are frozen. Since prompt tuning requires only input gradients (not weight gradients), Algorithm 1's single-gradient computation (the \(T_i\) gradient, Lemma E.11) may suffice, potentially simplifying the implementation. For long-context tasks where prompt tuning is applied (e.g., adapting an LLM to a specific document collection), this could reduce the per-iteration time of the tuning process. The benefit scales with sequence length, so as context windows grow (128k → 1M tokens), prompt tuning with Algorithm 1 becomes increasingly attractive compared to exact gradient computation.

**Efficient hyperparameter search and architecture exploration.** If Algorithm 1 delivers reliable gradients with acceptable accuracy, it could accelerate the inner loop of hyperparameter optimization (learning rate, architecture choices) and neural architecture search for transformer models. These procedures require training many model variants from scratch or from intermediate checkpoints, making per-training-run speed critical. Even a modest speedup per run would compound across hundreds or thousands of runs in a hyperparameter sweep. The deterministic nature of the approximation (polynomial kernel method with explicit error bounds) is an advantage here: unlike stochastic approximations (e.g., subsampling attention), the error is bounded and predictable, making hyperparameter comparisons more reliable.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate an explicit tradeoff against named alternatives in a way that supports a decision matrix. It positions Algorithm 1 as a theoretical feasibility result—a proof that sub-quadratic training is possible—not as a drop-in replacement for existing training procedures that a practitioner would choose among. The paper does not compare its algorithm against FlashAttention, sparse attention, linear attention, or any other practical acceleration method. It does not provide empirical evidence that training with approximated gradients produces useful models, making any practical recommendation premature.

The paper does, however, implicitly define the conditions under which its approach is theoretically applicable, which can be reframed as a checklist for when the method might be considered:

- The model uses standard softmax self-attention in a multi-layer transformer architecture (the analysis does not currently extend to cross-attention or non-softmax attention variants).
- The hidden dimension \(d\) is small relative to the sequence length \(n\) (ideally \(d = O(\log n)\), though in practice \(d\) is constant while \(n\) grows, which satisfies this).
- Numerical stability can be maintained (entries bounded, gradients not exploding), either through careful initialization, gradient clipping, or quantization—the paper does not specify how to achieve this but identifies it as a requirement.
- The application tolerates a small but non-zero approximation error in the gradients (the paper proves \(1/\mathrm{poly}(n)\) error, but the practical impact of this error on model quality is unknown).
- GPU implementation of the Kronecker product and causal mask operations is feasible and does not introduce prohibitive constant-factor overhead.

Until empirical validation demonstrates that these conditions are jointly satisfiable and that training with Algorithm 1 converges to useful models, the method remains a theoretical construct rather than a practical choice. The paper's primary value is in establishing what is algorithmically possible, not in providing a deployment-ready training algorithm.
