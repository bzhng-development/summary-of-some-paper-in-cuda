Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman and Ricky joint talk at UCLA (a Q&A-style event for their old club UPE, focused on industry secrets they wish they’d known before graduating):

**Balancing technical and soft skills**

One of the first hard truths they wanted the students to understand is that writing perfect code is not enough to be a great engineer. Ryan and Ricky both observed that the best engineers they’ve worked with in industry were rarely the students at the very top of their college programming classes. Instead, the people who excel are those who pair technical competence with the ability to influence others, work effectively in teams, and communicate concisely, especially in writing. The industry filters for a much broader set of skills than a CS curriculum does.

**Advocating for yourself and building visibility**

Because promotions are ultimately decided by people, doing great work in silence is a common trap. Ryan and Ricky stressed that you have to actively advocate for yourself. Visibility matters as much as the quality of your output—you need to make sure the right people know what you’ve accomplished and what you’re capable of. This doesn’t mean bragging, but it does mean deliberately making your impact legible to decision-makers rather than assuming it will speak for itself.

**Maximizing luck for promotions**

Luck plays a real role in promotions, but Ryan and Ricky don’t treat it as something completely random. They described a posture of increasing your **luck surface area**: having the initiative to constantly look for new opportunities and staying ready to act when one appears. Doing more projects, meeting more people, and volunteering for stretch work all widen the pool of chances that something career-accelerating will come your way. The core insight is that you can manufacture more "luck" by being more active.

**Big Tech vs. startups after college**

On the classic post-graduation dilemma, both Ryan and Ricky agreed they would choose Big Tech over a startup right out of school. Their reasoning is pragmatic: a well-known company gives you instant credibility on your resume and a solid foundation of engineering skills, processes, and mentorship that are harder to guarantee in a young startup. After a few years, you can pivot to a startup with both brand equity and a stronger skill set, and you’ll be better equipped to evaluate which startup is worth joining.

**Impact is not lines of code**

One of the biggest mindset shifts from college to industry is understanding that impact means concrete, measurable outcomes, not effort or code volume. Ryan and Ricky were explicit: the tech industry rewards results. If anything, writing *less* code to achieve a larger outcome is the more impressive and desirable path. Someone who deletes 10,000 lines of legacy code and simplifies a system can have more impact than someone who ships 20,000 new ones. Impact is a proxy for the business or user value your work generates, not how hard you worked.

**Big Tech’s financial reality**

Working your way up in a large tech company is a high-confidence path to financial stability and freedom, but Ryan and Ricky were careful to set expectations: Big Tech won’t make you "crazy rich." If that level of extreme wealth is someone’s goal, taking a bet on an early-stage breakout startup is a more realistic route, albeit one with far higher risk. Big Tech offers a smoother, more predictable wealth-building curve; startups offer a lottery ticket.

**Job hopping: early vs. late career**

They drew a sharp distinction between job hopping early in your career versus later. Early on, moving companies every couple of years can accelerate your learning, salary growth, and title progression. But after the Senior or Staff level, influence and credibility become the dominant force behind further promotion—and those take time to build within an organization. Hopping too frequently at that stage resets your political and social capital, becoming counterproductive just when you need it most to reach higher levels.

**AI as a tool, not a replacement**

A question from the audience surfaced the anxiety about AI replacing software engineering jobs. Ryan and Ricky’s stance was pragmatic: engineers already use a vast array of non-AI tools to write code (IDEs, linters, frameworks, libraries), and LLMs are simply another productivity tool in that lineage. For the next five years, they wouldn’t worry about AI eliminating the need for software engineers. Beyond that horizon, they said, it’s genuinely impossible to say, but in the medium term they see enhancement, not replacement.

**College recruiting and the internship-to-return-offer path**

For students still in the job hunt, their advice was hands-on. The bare minimum to get noticed is having some projects on your resume that demonstrate real, applied work beyond coursework. After that, persistence matters—and being willing to take a role at a scrappier, less prestigious company early on can build experience faster than holding out for a name-brand job. For internships specifically, they noted that most come with a well-defined project. The straightforward formula for a return offer is to finish that project as quickly as possible with high-quality code. If you can propose meaningful improvements on top of the original scope, that puts you in an even stronger position.
