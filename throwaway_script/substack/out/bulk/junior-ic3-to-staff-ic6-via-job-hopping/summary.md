Here are the takeaways from this Zach Wilson interview (went from Junior IC3 to Staff IC6 in 5 years through three strategic job hops — Meta → Netflix → Airbnb):

**Meta: rapid IC3→IC4, then stalled at the Senior frontier**

Zach joined Meta as a junior engineer (IC3) and was promoted to mid-level (IC4) in a single half — an unusually fast clip that he attributes partly to excellent execution and partly to the company realizing "they hired him at the wrong level." He earned two “Greatly Exceeds Expectations” ratings (the second-highest rating achievable), yet the promotion to Senior (IC5) never materialized. The crux of the failure was a **manager-switching trust gap**: his manager kept changing, and the accumulated trust and advocacy he’d built with each manager evaporated with every transition. When the promo case reached calibration, the room concluded his impact "wasn’t sustainable" — a common trap for engineers who deliver heroically but haven‘t yet demonstrated the *repeatable, scalable* impact Senior requires. Zach had the execution but not the long-term trust narrative that a stable manager partnership provides, and the numbers alone couldn't overcome it.

**Netflix: the job-hop promotion to Senior — and the burnout that followed**

His previous Meta manager recruited him to Netflix, where the engineering bar meant the company "only hired Senior engineers at the time." Zach interviewed well — a word he returns to repeatedly — and secured what he calls the **job-hop promotion**: using an external offer to level up when internal advancement is blocked. It’s a direct pipeline from having strong internal performance *and* the ability to sell that story externally. At Netflix he performed well enough that his manager gave him Staff-level scope — a vote of confidence that inadvertently set up his undoing. "He didn’t scale himself well," Ryan notes. Zach took on the broader surface area without building the delegation habits, influence patterns, and communication rhythms that Staff scope demands, and he burned out. He quit and took nine months entirely away from tech — a rare, deliberate reset.

**Airbnb: negotiating a Staff offer through competitive leverage**

After the sabbatical, Zach re-entered the market in simultaneous discussions with Google, Meta, and Airbnb. This wasn’t accidental; having "interest from many top companies at the same time" was, in his telling, the enabling condition for Airbnb to offer him a Staff interview loop and ultimately a Staff (IC6) offer. Ryan observes that Zach was "thoughtful to land impact wherever he was so he had something to sell during interviews." The throughline is **selling your story**: Zach repeatedly emphasizes that job-hop promotions depend less on raw achievements than on how compellingly you package those achievements into a narrative that matches the target level. And that narrative can only be built if you’ve actually *done* the work at each stop — there’s no faking the underlying substance.

**Internal brand as an invisible leveling engine**

Running beneath the specific job hops is a deeper idea Zach surfaces: your **internal brand** determines what opportunities come to you inside an organization — the meaty projects, the sponsorship, the trust that boards a promo case. At Meta, his internal brand was strong early (hence the fast IC3→IC4), but the manager churn eroded it when it mattered most for IC5. At Netflix, a strong internal brand from his Meta days was literally the reason he was recruited. Ryan connects this to his own recent writing on the subject, noting how the pattern holds across Zach’s arc: every inflection point traces back not just to ability, but to whether the *right people* knew about that ability and trusted it. Job hopping was the accelerant, but internal brand was the fuel that made each hop possible.

---

Zach’s arc is a case study in making the **job-hop promotion** work as a deliberate career lever, but the machinery beneath it is more fundamental. His **selling your story** discipline — packaging real, earned impact into a level-appropriate narrative — is what converted each external interview into a level-up offer, while the **manager-switching trust gap** at Meta is the negative space that proves how fragile internal advocacy can be without continuity. Together they form a single insight: promos live and die on who trusts your trajectory, whether that trust is built inside one company or rebuilt fresh across several. (Ryan notes this echoes his own recent piece on internal brand; the throughline is unmistakable.)
