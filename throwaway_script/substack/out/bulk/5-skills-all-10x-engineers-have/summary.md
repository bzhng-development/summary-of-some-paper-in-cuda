Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (distilled patterns from the most impactful engineers he's worked with):

**Code ROI**

Every exceptional engineer Ryan has encountered possesses an unusually sharp sense of the return on investment of their code. This is not a vague preference but a guiding compass that dictates where they spend their limited time. Concretely, these engineers are often difficult to pin down unless the topic at hand is something they deem high-ROI. Ryan clarifies this isn't antisocial behavior—"they aren't trying to be mean; it is out of necessity to have more impact." The skill manifests as a constant, almost automatic filtering mechanism. To cultivate it, he suggests actively interrogating the "why" behind your work by mapping the cost and benefit of potential projects. A practical entry point is discussing the ROI of upcoming work with your tech lead or manager—they should already have that context—and getting involved in your team's project roadmapping and prioritization sessions, which are live-fire exercises in this exact trade-off analysis.

**Technical Breadth and Depth**

These engineers refuse to choose between being a specialist or a generalist. Instead, they develop both incredible technical depth and technical breadth simultaneously, and Ryan sees each dimension serving a distinct, critical function. Their depth allows them to solve problems that few others can. The most visible proof, in his experience, surfaces during severe outages: when dozens of engineers are scrambling to diagnose a baffling issue, "it is often the top engineers that find the fix"—an ability rooted in genuinely deep knowledge of the system. Their breadth, meanwhile, helps them rapidly absorb new systems they've never seen before. In a big-tech codebase too vast for anyone to know deeply, this rapid comprehension is "worth its weight in gold." To develop this dual capacity, Ryan prescribes following your curiosity. In an industry with no structured curriculum, curiosity becomes the teacher: when he encounters something he doesn't know, he asks questions purely out of personal interest, a habit he says has helped him grow his understanding faster over time. His second prescription is simpler: work with strong engineers. So much unwritten context gets transmitted by asking them technical questions and observing how they solve problems.

**Influence Without Authority**

The ability to guide others without formal power is a through-line across all the top engineers Ryan knows. Their strong communication skills, combined with their judgment on code ROI and their deep technical foundation, allow them to steer decisions and teams. A signature trait is how their succinct, clear reasoning tends to be "debate-winning"—they can take a complex topic and explain it simply, which makes influencing others feel almost natural rather than forced. To develop this, Ryan advises prioritizing both writing and speaking, treating every explanation as a chance to practice and then reflecting honestly on what fell flat. For instance, "if I explain something and see it fall flat, I'll think about how I can explain it more clearly next time." He also brings in a more counterintuitive learning vector: learn to sell. Citing Dale Carnegie's "How To Win Friends And Influence People," he underscores the principle that "the only way on earth to influence other people is to talk about what they want and show them how to get it."

**Uplifting Others**

These engineers function as force multipliers, making everyone around them better in ways that extend far beyond formal one-on-one mentorship. Ryan outlines a three-pronged pattern they use to grow others: knowledge sharing—writing wikis, giving presentations, and contributing to Q&A groups; collaboration—growing people during the work itself through code reviews, design reviews, and technical discussions; and building tools—automating common processes or making it drastically easier to debug regressions so that every engineer on the team becomes more effective. To build this skill yourself, his advice is to look for a pattern of everyone solving the same problem manually, then build tooling to simplify that workflow and spread it widely. And for a lower-friction starting point, share your expertise directly: "code review is one of the best ways to get started."

**Ownership Mentality**

A strong sense of ownership sets these engineers apart, and Ryan defines it in a specific way. They take responsibility for the large initiatives they lead, and they don't care who does the work—only that it gets done right. This mindset enables them to scale themselves through delegation. Crucially, they are willing to do whatever is needed, even if that task isn't writing code. The underlying principle Ryan extracts is simple: think like an owner. Own your projects end-to-end, put your name on them, and act as though your reputation is on the line with the outcome. That posture internalizes the "do whatever it takes" drive that characterizes the engineers he admires most.

The essay closes by tying the skills together into a strategy for growth. Being intentional about learning these five dimensions will accelerate your trajectory, and one of the highest-leverage moves is simply positioning yourself near an incredible engineer. You don't need formal mentorship to absorb a great deal; strong engineers "become even stronger just by asking them technical questions and seeing how they work."

---

Ryan's portrait of engineering excellence hangs on a single, load-bearing frame: **Code ROI**. It is the root skill that feeds the others—their **Technical Breadth and Depth** equips them to judge ROI accurately, their **Influence Without Authority** is wielded selectively for high-ROI bets, their impulse toward **Uplifting Others** multiplies ROI across the org, and their **Ownership Mentality** is the refusal to let low-ROI details slip. His closing advice—simply work alongside these people—is itself an ROI play: the highest-leverage growth hack is proximity.
