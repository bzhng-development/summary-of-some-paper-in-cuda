Here are the takeaways from this Leslie Lamport interview (Turing Award winner, inventor of Paxos, LaTeX, and the Bakery algorithm):

**The Bakery Algorithm and why concurrency needs proof**

Dijkstra’s 1965 paper defined the mutual-exclusion problem: how to synchronize processes so at most one is in its critical section at a time (originally so two users wouldn’t print to the same printer simultaneously under early time-sharing). In 1972 Lamport read a CACM solution, thought “that shouldn’t be so hard,” and whipped off a simple two-process version. The editor replied two weeks later pointing out the bug. That had two effects: it taught him concurrent programs are genuinely hard to get right and **require a proof of correctness**, and it made him determined to “solve that damn problem.”

The result was the Bakery algorithm, inspired by a deli counter where customers take numbered tickets and are served in order — but with no central server. Each process chooses its own ticket. The proof revealed a property that seemed impossible: the algorithm worked even without assuming atomic reads and writes. A process reading a value mid-write could “get absolutely any value and the algorithm still worked.” When he showed the proof on a whiteboard to colleague Anatol Holt, Holt couldn’t find an error but went home convinced “there must be something wrong with it.” There wasn’t. Lamport believes it was also the first mutual-exclusion algorithm to be truly first-come, first-served — if one process chose its number before another attempted to enter, the first entered first. Dijkstra’s original solution had a starvation problem (solved later by Knuth), but the fairness property was new.

**Working with Dijkstra and the gift of abstraction**

Dijkstra wrote short papers called EWDs and circulated them. One described an early concurrent garbage-collection algorithm; Lamport realized the free-list handling, which Dijkstra treated as a special process requiring its own coordination, could be folded into the regular data structure with no special treatment. He mentioned it to Dijkstra, and the next draft listed Lamport as an author — which struck him as “very generous” since it seemed like “a very simple idea, a very obvious idea.” Much later he understood it wasn’t obvious to most people, and that Dijkstra had recognized in him a “remarkable ability at abstraction.” Only in the last decade or so, well after the Turing Award, did Lamport himself come to see that his success came not from being “particularly smart” but from this specific gift. He spent a month in the Netherlands with Dijkstra’s colleague Carl Scholten, meeting Dijkstra once a week at his house — “one afternoon a week working, talking and drinking beer” — and the only published output was a variant of the Bakery algorithm whose provenance he can’t precisely reconstruct. (The Dutch beer, he notes, “was not very alcoholic.”)

**“Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events” — his most cited paper and its overlooked idea**

Someone sent Lamport a paper on distributed databases with replicated copies needing synchronization. He realized their solution ordered events into a sequence that could differ from the order in which they actually happened. Drawing on the four-dimensional space-time view from special relativity (one event “happens before” another if a signal traveling at most the speed of light could have been emitted from the first and received before the second), he defined **happens-before in a distributed system** by analogy: whether the first event could have affected the second via messages actually sent in the system. “The thing that blew people away was this definition.”

The paper contained two ideas — he was warned against putting two in one paper. The second, which he considered “really the important idea,” was that you could use the happens-before ordering to implement any distributed system by describing it as a **state machine** — a device with a state that receives commands executed in order, producing a new state and a value. This was the foundation for building fault-tolerant systems by thinking in terms of state machines. “But that part was completely ignored.” Twice people told him there was nothing about state machines in the paper. He had to reread it to verify he wasn’t going crazy. He also used the paper to advocate for **invariance proofs** over behavioral proofs that reason about sequences of events. The number of possible sequences grows exponentially with length, making it easy to miss cases. An invariance proof — establishing a Boolean-valued invariant of the state — scales quadratically with the number of processes, not exponentially. “If you want to do it in practice, that’s the way to do it.” He concedes there are exceptions, like the Bakery algorithm, where thinking in partial orderings is the right approach.

**The Byzantine Generals problem and why a good name matters**

After Time-Clocks described building a distributed system with no failures, Lamport started working on what failures could do. He assumed the worst: a failed process might do absolutely anything. Using digital signatures — which he knew because Whit Diffie was a friend; he once sketched an early signature algorithm “literally on a napkin” at a coffee house — he had a solution. At SRI he found colleagues working the same problem for a contract to build a multi-computer system to fly airplanes. They lacked digital signatures but had a cleaner abstraction: agreeing on a single command, then executing that algorithm repeatedly. Their version required four processes to tolerate one fault; with digital signatures you needed only three. The general algorithm for *N* processes without signatures, by Marshall Pease, was “a work of genius” — “almost incomprehensible” and “brilliant.”

Lamport realized the result was important and needed to be well known. He had learned from Dijkstra’s Dining Philosophers problem that “it was because of that cute story that that problem was popular.” So he invented the **Byzantine Generals** story — multiple generals must agree on attack/retreat, but one might be a traitor. He’d originally considered “Albanian generals” (Albania being a black hole under its communist regime), but his boss noted there were Albanians in the world. “Byzantine, there aren’t any Byzantines around. And that was the perfect name.” The problem was worth solving because it was obvious computers would fly airplanes (the 1970s oil crisis was pushing designs toward aerodynamically unstable planes a pilot couldn’t control but a computer could). Engineers assumed three computers sufficed to tolerate one fault; they hadn’t realized they needed four. Later an engineer at Boeing read the paper and his reaction was “oh, shit, we need four computers.”

**The Paxos algorithm and the algorithm-code distinction**

Paxos addressed the same problem as Byzantine Generals — building a fault-tolerant state machine — but for the class of faults industry cared about: computers that stop, rather than doing arbitrary things. At DEC SRC in the mid-1980s, Lamport encountered a distributed operating system (built by ex-Xerox PARC people who had invented personal computing and the Ethernet) that maintained consistency of shared storage across a building. “I didn’t believe that what they did was possible.” He set out to write an impossibility proof, and in the middle of it realized “oh, this isn’t a proof, this is an algorithm that does it.”

This crystallized a distinction central to his career: an **algorithm vs. a program**. “A program is written in some particular code, but an algorithm can be implemented in programs written in any kinds of code. It’s at a higher level of abstraction.” Most people building concurrent systems start with code, but the synchronization kernel is too subtle for that — “you don’t want to think in terms of code because the encoding conflates a lot of issues that are irrelevant to the concurrency aspect. You should be thinking, first get an algorithm that does that synchronization and then implement that algorithm.”

The Paxos paper sat unpublished for eight years. Referees called it “okay, not terribly important.” Butler Lampson recognized its significance and started proselytizing — building systems with Paxos and state-machine thinking — so the idea spread regardless. Eventually a new editor published it. Lamport famously presented it dressed as an Indiana Jones-style archaeologist describing an ancient manuscript; the lecture went well, but “I think nobody understood the significance of the algorithm” except Lampson, who “really deserved his Turing Award” as one of the senior figures behind distributed personal computing at PARC.

**Paxos vs. Raft and what “understanding” means**

The Raft authors sent Lamport a draft; he told them “send it back to me when you have an algorithm” or “when you have a proof.” Someone whose judgment he trusts read the final version and said “it’s basically the Paxos paper but with some of the details filled in,” described in a different order. Paxos has two phases: a leader-election phase you do once, and a second phase you repeat until the leader fails. Engineers like to think about it in the opposite order — do the second phase until failure, then do the leader-election thing. Raft describes it that way. The Raft team claimed Raft was simpler, backed by classroom experiments. But a bug was later found in Raft — and the version students found more understandable was the one *with* that bug. For Lamport, **understanding means you can write a proof of it**. For most people, “understanding means a warm fuzzy feeling.” The Raft framing gave them that feeling; Lamport’s framing “helps you get a better understanding of why it actually works.”

**Building LaTeX**

When Lamport wanted to write a book, TeX was the typesetting system to use, but he needed macros. He realized “with a little extra effort I could make macros usable by other people.” He’d previously used Scribe, which he liked because it separated ideas from formatting — “the writing that matters, not the typesetting.” Peter Gordon at Addison-Wesley convinced him to write the book, and connected him with a typographic designer responsible for the standard LaTeX styles. Lamport built LaTeX in “six or nine months or so” of “spare time” — and he’s now willing to say he spent some of that time “when I was allegedly billing the time to some project that had nothing to do with it.”

**Why writing improves thinking**

His maxim: **“If you’re thinking without writing, you only think you’re thinking.”** When writing the LaTeX book, he discovered things that were hard to explain needed to be changed — and he made changes as a result. But the deeper insight came from proving concurrent algorithms. Standard mathematical proof style couldn’t handle the explosion of details. He devised a **hierarchical proof structure** — a proof is a sequence of steps, each with its own proof, which is either a paragraph or a further sequence of steps with proofs. “You break the whole problem up into these smaller pieces so there’s never any question of where is this coming from.” He tried the technique on ordinary math and “it worked beautifully.” When he showed it to a seminar of mathematicians, they became angry — “I really thought that they might physically attack me.” His diagnosis: fear they’d have to write proofs to satisfy a computer. But the method doesn’t require more formality; it simply reveals what you haven’t said. “If you believe something is correct but don’t really write it down, you only think you know it. And that’s where errors come in. It really makes you honest.”

**Why he wasn’t an academic**

Lamport started programming and drifted into what we now call computer science. “It never seemed to me that computer science was an academic subject.” Even after he realized computer science existed as a field, he didn’t think people needed a university to learn it. When he faced a choice between doing computer science or teaching math, he chose computer science “for fairly random reasons.” Later he simply “didn’t think it would be fun teaching computer science.”

**The grand theory of concurrency and the primacy of math over languages**

For years, researchers sought the “Turing machine of concurrency” — an abstraction that captures concurrent computing the way Turing machines capture sequential computing. Lamport now believes the answer is **state machines** — not the version with commands and values, but a simpler form with just a state and a next-state relation. “To me that’s the Turing machine of concurrency.” It can describe things that aren’t computable, like a variable taking any integer. That’s a feature, not a bug: “infinity was introduced to simplify things.” Restricting to finite integers makes arithmetic more complicated, not less. Computer scientists are “hung up on languages” — inventing new ones, believing the language improves thinking. “You can’t beat math.” Language-based approaches to concurrency are “just the wrong way.” If you want to give a language semantics, you’d do it in terms of a state machine anyway. Use computer languages for efficiency, but for understanding, math reigns. “The abstractions of mathematics which people find difficult are really what’s simplifying things.”

**Why he doesn’t think he’s smart**

When Lamport says he never considered himself smart, he’s describing the Dunning-Kruger effect in reverse: “people who are good at something don’t realize how good they are at it because it’s simple to them.” (Concisely: “stupid people think they’re smart because they’re too stupid to realize they’re not.”) His gift, abstraction, felt ordinary to him for decades. “It’s only recently, last ten or so years, that I realized how much better I am at that than other people.”

**Advice to his younger self**

“One thing I’ve learned fairly early in my life is that I shouldn’t waste time trying to answer questions that I don’t have to answer.” He declines to play the what-I-should-have-done game — that’s a question he simply doesn’t need to answer.

---

His career is a sustained argument for thinking before coding: the **algorithm-code distinction**, the **state machine** as the universal abstraction, the insistence that **understanding means you can write a proof** — not getting a “warm fuzzy feeling.” The **hierarchical proof structure** that infuriated mathematicians and the invariance proofs he preferred over behavioral sequences both stem from the same conviction that writing forces intellectual honesty. And beneath all of it is the **gift of abstraction** Dijkstra spotted early and Lamport himself recognized late — an ability so native to him that his biggest discoveries felt “obvious,” even when they became the foundation for how the world builds reliable distributed systems.
