## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **EM-LLM**, a training-free architecture that integrates key aspects of human episodic memory and event cognition—surprise-based event segmentation, graph-theoretic boundary refinement, and a two-stage memory retrieval process combining similarity-based and temporally contiguous access—into pre-trained LLMs to enable them to handle practically infinite context lengths while maintaining computational efficiency. Evaluated on the LongBench and ∞-Bench benchmarks across multiple base LLMs including Mistral-7B, LLaMA-3, and Phi-3, EM-LLM consistently outperforms the state-of-the-art KV-retrieval method InfLLM, surpasses both RAG pipelines and full-context models on most tasks, and successfully performs passkey retrieval across 10 million tokens—a scale computationally infeasible for full-context models. The paper establishes that event-based memory organization driven by Bayesian surprise and graph-theoretic refinement produces token groupings that correlate strongly with human-perceived event boundaries, but that the benefits of different retrieval mechanisms—similarity-based retrieval versus temporal contiguity—are task-dependent, with contiguity buffers improving performance primarily on tasks requiring temporal reasoning while similarity-based retrieval dominates otherwise.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Cannot Process Unbounded Contexts Without Degrading

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **contemporary LLMs cannot maintain coherence, accuracy, and retrieval fidelity when processing contexts significantly longer than their training window size**. This is not merely a matter of inconvenience—it is a structural limitation that constrains what LLMs can be used for. The context window serves as the primary mechanism for incorporating domain-specific, private, or temporally up-to-date information into a model's generation process (Section 1). If that window is capped at a few thousand tokens, an LLM cannot reason over an entire book, cannot maintain a coherent conversation spanning days, and cannot synthesize information across a large corpus of documents without external machinery.

The paper identifies two distinct root causes for this limitation, both architectural in nature (Section 1, Section 2.1):

1. **Computational complexity**: Applying standard softmax attention over a sequence of length $n$ requires $O(n^2)$ operations. For a typical 7-billion-parameter model generating tokens autoregressively, this quadratic scaling makes contexts beyond roughly 32K–128K tokens prohibitively expensive in both memory and wall-clock time. The weighted sum of value vectors that constitutes the attention output must be recomputed for every new token, and the key-value (KV) cache storing all past states grows linearly with sequence length. At 10 million tokens—one of the scales EM-LLM is evaluated on—a full softmax attention matrix would be computationally infeasible on current hardware even with optimized kernels like FlashAttention (Dao, 2024).

2. **Representational degradation ("diluted attention")**: Even if one could afford the computation, the resulting aggregated embeddings become excessively noisy. Softmax attention computes a weighted sum of all past value vectors, where weights are determined by the softmax-normalized similarity between the current query and all past keys. When the number of attended tokens grows very large, the softmax distribution flattens out—the model's attention is spread thinly across thousands of tokens, and the resulting aggregate loses distinctiveness (Tworkowski et al., 2023). The paper cites this as a direct cause of performance degradation: "the resulting aggregated embeddings (the weighted sums of value vectors) risk becoming excessively noisy and losing their distinctiveness" (Section 1).

3. **Positional encoding extrapolation failure**: Beyond the quadratic computation and dilution problems, Transformers struggle with contexts longer than their training window because positional encodings—whether absolute position embeddings (Vaswani et al., 2017) or relative encodings like Rotary Position Embeddings, RoPE (Su et al., 2024)—do not generalize well to out-of-distribution positions encountered at test time (Kazemnejad et al., 2024). A model trained on 4K-token sequences has never seen position index 32,768 during training, and its positional encoding mechanism has no principled way to represent it. This is a separate failure mode from the attention computation itself: even if you could afford to compute attention over 100K tokens, the model wouldn't know how to interpret the positional relationships among the extended sequence.

These three problems compound each other. The computational cost prevents scaling native attention to the needed lengths; the dilution problem degrades the quality of what attention can still be computed; and the positional encoding problem corrupts the spatial relationships that attention relies on. Together, they create a ceiling on the practical context length of deployed LLMs.

### Why This Problem Matters

The paper's motivation is grounded in three practical and scientific stakes:

**Practical deployment in knowledge-intensive applications.** The context window has become the standard vehicle for injecting domain-specific, proprietary, or time-sensitive information into LLM prompts. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) partially addresses this by fetching relevant documents before generation, but the paper demonstrates (Section 4.1, Appendix A.2) that RAG underperforms compared to methods that allow the LLM itself to attend directly to the full context—because RAG's single retrieval step, performed before generation, cannot adapt to the model's evolving information needs layer-by-layer and token-by-token. If LLMs could natively handle contexts of millions of tokens, they could ingest entire codebases, full legal case histories, or multi-year customer interaction logs without external retrieval pipelines—simplifying system architectures while improving output quality.

**Enabling continuous, personalized interaction.** The paper explicitly envisions "continuous, personalised exchanges over extended periods" (Section 6). A model that can maintain and selectively recall episodic memories from an arbitrarily long interaction history could serve as a persistent assistant that remembers past conversations, user preferences, and contextual details without explicit re-prompting. Current context-window limits force either periodic summarization (lossy) or RAG-style retrieval (coarse-grained), both of which degrade the continuity of interaction.

**Scientific understanding of memory in artificial and biological systems.** The paper positions itself at the intersection of machine learning and cognitive science (Section 2.2). By building an LLM architecture explicitly inspired by human episodic memory—event segmentation via prediction error, boundary refinement via coherence optimization, and retrieval combining similarity with temporal contiguity—the work serves as a testbed for computational theories of human memory. The paper's finding that LLM-based surprise signals correlate with human-perceived event boundaries (Section 4.2, Figure 4) suggests that the engineering problem of long-context LLMs and the scientific problem of human memory organization may have shared solutions, making progress on one potentially informative for the other.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper critically evaluates four families of prior work, identifying specific limitations that EM-LLM is designed to address.

#### 1. Computational Efficiency Solutions Without Memory Structure

Several approaches have tackled the $O(n^2)$ attention cost directly:
- **Optimized attention kernels** like FlashAttention-2 (Dao, 2024) restructure the attention computation to reduce memory I/O, achieving significant speedups but not changing the asymptotic quadratic complexity.
- **RingAttention** (Liu et al., 2024c) and **StripedAttention** (Brandon et al., 2023) distribute the attention computation across multiple devices, increasing the feasible context length but still requiring computation proportional to $n^2$ and sufficient hardware to parallelize it.
- **Linear attention** variants (Katharopoulos et al., 2020) replace the softmax attention with kernel-based approximations that achieve $O(n)$ complexity, but at a cost to representational capacity—the softmax attention's ability to sharply focus on relevant tokens is lost.
- **KV cache compression** techniques like H2O (Zhang et al., 2023) and Dynamic Memory Compression (Nawrot et al., 2024) selectively evict "unimportant" KV pairs from the cache to bound memory usage, but the eviction decisions are made heuristically based on accumulated attention scores, not on the semantic or episodic structure of the content.

The paper's critique of these approaches is implicit but clear: they treat the KV cache as an undifferentiated bag of key-value vectors to be compressed or selectively retained, without any notion of **event structure**. Tokens that belong to the same semantic episode are treated identically to tokens spanning an event boundary. This misses an opportunity: if the cache were organized into meaningful, coherent segments, retrieval could operate at the granularity of episodes rather than individual tokens, potentially improving both efficiency and recall quality.

#### 2. Retrieval-Based Methods and the InfLLM Baseline

The paper pays particular attention to **InfLLM** (Xiao et al., 2024a), which it identifies as the state-of-the-art KV-retrieval method and the direct baseline for EM-LLM's experiments (Section 4.1). Understanding InfLLM's design is essential to understanding what EM-LLM changes and why.

**How InfLLM works:** InfLLM segments the entire context into **fixed-size memory units** (blocks of a predetermined number of tokens, e.g., 512). For each memory unit, it identifies a small set of "representative tokens"—those with the highest accumulated attention scores within that block—and stores these as compact proxies for the block's content. When generating a new token, InfLLM performs k-nearest-neighbor (k-NN) lookup using dot-product similarity between the current query and these representative tokens. The top-$k$ most similar memory units are retrieved, and their full KV caches are loaded into the LLM's context window for softmax attention. Tokens not in the retrieved units are effectively ignored (their attention scores are forced to zero). This can be understood as a form of hierarchical attention (Figure 2): the first level selects which blocks to attend to (via k-NN on representative tokens), and the second level applies standard softmax attention within the selected blocks.

**What InfLLM gets right:** The paper credits InfLLM with two important insights. First, retrieving entire blocks of tokens (rather than individual KV pairs) preserves **local context within each block**—tokens that appeared together in the original sequence can attend to each other, maintaining coherence that would be lost if tokens were retrieved individually. Second, the use of k-NN retrieval on representative tokens makes the method **training-free**: it can be applied to any pre-trained LLM without fine-tuning because it only modifies which KV pairs are presented to the attention mechanism, not the attention computation itself.

**Where InfLLM falls short—the fixed-segmentation limitation:** This is the paper's central critique. InfLLM segments the context into **fixed-size blocks** without any regard for the semantic or episodic structure of the content (Section 2.1):

> "While group-based retrieval represents a promising direction, our approach significantly advances this concept by dynamically determining token groupings in a manner akin to human memory formation. This effectively addresses a fundamental limitation of InfLLM's fixed-size segmentation and enables more adaptive and context-sensitive processing of extended information."

The consequences of fixed segmentation are significant:
- **Event boundaries fall at arbitrary positions.** A block boundary might split a coherent paragraph mid-sentence, separating tokens that belong together into different retrieval units. When one of those units is retrieved but the other is not, the model sees a fragment rather than a complete episode.
- **Unrelated tokens are grouped together.** A fixed block might span a natural event boundary, mixing tokens from two different topics into a single retrieval unit. When that unit is retrieved based on similarity to one topic, the model receives irrelevant tokens from the other topic as noise.
- **All tokens within a block are treated as equally important for retrieval.** The representative-token selection (highest accumulated attention scores) partially mitigates this by selecting the most "influential" tokens per block, but it's still operating within an arbitrary partition. If a block spans two events, the representative tokens might belong to one event, and retrieval based on them would ignore the tokens from the other—or vice versa.

**Other k-NN retrieval methods** (Memorizing Transformers by Wu et al. (2022), Unlimiformer by Bertsch et al. (2023)) retrieve individual tokens rather than blocks, avoiding the arbitrary-boundary problem but losing the benefit of local context preservation. The paper frames block-based retrieval as the right direction but dynamic, semantically meaningful segmentation as the missing ingredient.

#### 3. RAG and Single-Step Retrieval Limitations

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG; Lewis et al., 2020) splits the context into chunks (typically fixed-size, like InfLLM's blocks), embeds them into a vector database using a separate retriever model, and retrieves the top-$k$ most similar chunks given the query. The retrieved chunks are prepended to the LLM's prompt as additional context. The paper's experimental comparison (Section 4.1, Appendix A.2) reveals two key limitations of this approach:

- **Single retrieval step at the beginning.** RAG retrieves context once, before generation begins, and the same retrieved chunks are used for every subsequent token. In contrast, EM-LLM's KV retrieval operates at **every layer of the Transformer** and for **every generated token**—each attention head can retrieve different episodic events as the generation context evolves. The paper provides a striking quantitative illustration: "the ratio of blocks retrieved by a layer which were not retrieved by any other layer" is large, meaning different layers attend to different parts of the context (Figure 5 in Appendix A). In RAG, this ratio is always zero—all layers see the same retrieved chunks.

- **Retriever bottleneck.** RAG's accuracy is bounded by the quality of the embedding model used for retrieval. The paper demonstrates this concretely: using the SOTA NV-Embed-v2 retriever (a fine-tuned Mistral-7B model) yields 39.27% average score on LongBench with LLaMA-3-8B as the downstream LLM, while using the smaller all-mpnet-base-v2 retriever yields only 30.75% (Table 8). EM-LLM achieves 47.24% using the same downstream LLM but no separate retriever—its retrieval operates directly on the LLM's own key representations, avoiding the quality gap between retriever and generator.

The paper also notes that RAG's chunk-based retrieval suffers from the same fixed-segmentation problem as InfLLM (and experiments with a surprise-based RAG variant, RAG-S, find that dynamic segmentation does not help RAG because retrieved chunks become "highly disjointed" without the contiguity mechanisms EM-LLM provides—Table 9, Appendix A.2).

#### 4. Positional Encoding Extrapolation Methods Without Memory Organization

A separate line of work extends LLM context windows by modifying positional encodings to extrapolate beyond training lengths:
- **Position Interpolation** (Chen et al., 2023) and **YaRN** (Peng et al., 2024) scale down RoPE's rotation frequencies so that test-time positions map into the range seen during training.
- **LongRoPE** (Ding et al., 2024) extends this scaling to beyond 2 million tokens.
- **SelfExtend** (Jin et al., 2024) modifies the attention mechanism itself to use different RoPE scales for different attention heads.

These methods can extend the feasible context window (the paper highlights LongRoPE achieving 2048K tokens in Figure 1, bottom), but they do not address the quadratic computation or attention dilution problems. A model with a 2M-token positional encoding still incurs $O(n^2)$ attention cost over 2M tokens and risks having its attention diluted across the entire sequence. The paper positions these methods as complementary to EM-LLM—they could extend the "local context" window within which full softmax attention is applied, while EM-LLM handles the "evicted tokens" beyond that window through episodic retrieval.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

EM-LLM's core intellectual move is to reframe the long-context problem from an **engineering challenge** (how to compress, distribute, or approximate attention) to a **cognitive organization challenge** (how to structure, segment, and retrieve information in a way that mirrors human episodic memory). The paper does not claim to solve the fundamental attention scaling problem—it still uses approximate retrieval (k-NN) to avoid full quadratic attention. Rather, it claims that **how you organize the KV cache before you retrieve from it** is the key missing piece that separates the SOTA (InfLLM) from what is achievable.

The three cognitive mechanisms EM-LLM introduces—surprise-based event segmentation, graph-theoretic boundary refinement, and two-stage retrieval with contiguity—are all motivated by empirical findings in human memory research (Section 2.2):

- **Surprise-triggered event boundaries**: Human studies show that moments of high prediction error (violation of expectations by incoming sensory input) correspond to event boundaries perceived by subjects (Zacks et al., 2007; 2011; Roseboom et al., 2019; Sinclair et al., 2021). Kumar et al. (2023) found that Bayesian surprise computed from an LLM's next-token prediction errors correlates strongly with human-annotated event boundaries in podcast transcripts—a direct empirical bridge that EM-LLM exploits by using the LLM's own prediction error as a segmentation signal.

- **Coherence-based boundary refinement**: The paper's graph-theoretic refinement step (optimizing modularity or conductance of the key-similarity adjacency matrix) is motivated by the observation that human event memories exhibit "high intra-event similarity and low inter-event similarity" (Section 3.3). Tokens within a coherent episode should have similar key representations (since the LLM attends to them in similar ways), while tokens spanning an event boundary should be more dissimilar. This is a testable hypothesis about information geometry in the KV cache, not just a heuristic.

- **Contiguity and asymmetry in retrieval**: Human free recall studies (Howard and Kahana, 2002) show that when a person recalls an item from a list, they are disproportionately likely to next recall items from nearby positions in the original sequence (temporal contiguity) and to recall in the forward direction (temporal asymmetry). Ji-An et al. (2024) demonstrated that attention heads in Transformer LLMs exhibit the same contiguity and asymmetry patterns when performing in-context learning. EM-LLM's contiguity buffer is a direct architectural implementation of this finding: when an episodic event is retrieved by similarity, its temporal neighbors are also loaded to enable the model's induction heads to exhibit contiguity effects.

The paper is careful not to overclaim about biological fidelity. It describes EM-LLM as "integrating key aspects of human episodic memory and event cognition" (Abstract), not as a neural model of the hippocampus. The mechanisms are cognitive-level abstractions—surprise as a boundary signal, coherence as a refinement objective, contiguity as a retrieval bias—implemented through engineering mechanisms (thresholding, graph clustering, queue-based buffers) that are computationally tractable within a Transformer architecture.

The paper positions its contribution relative to prior work as **complementary rather than competing**. It does not claim to replace positional encoding methods, optimized attention kernels, or RAG pipelines. Rather, it argues that whatever one's approach to the attention computation itself, the **organization of the memory store** from which attention retrieves is a separate, under-explored dimension of the problem—and one where cognitive science offers actionable insights that prior work has overlooked.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

EM-LLM is a **training-free architecture** that can be plugged into any existing pre-trained Transformer-based LLM to let it handle context lengths far beyond its original training window—up to 10 million tokens and beyond—by mimicking how human episodic memory organizes and retrieves experiences. The system solves the problem of "how do we store and selectively access an indefinitely long conversation or document history without being able to attend to all of it at once?" by structuring the LLM's key-value (KV) cache into meaningful, event-like segments and then retrieving those segments using a combination of content-similarity matching and temporal neighbor recall.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The EM-LLM system has five major components that operate in a repeating cycle as the model processes a long sequence token by token:

1. **Surprise-Based Event Boundary Detector** — monitors the LLM's own next-token prediction surprise during autoregressive generation. When surprise exceeds an adaptive threshold, it marks an event boundary, splitting the continuous token stream into discrete episodic memory units.

2. **Graph-Theoretic Boundary Refinement Module** — takes the initial surprise-based boundaries and adjusts them by optimizing a graph-clustering metric (modularity or conductance) computed over the attention-key similarity matrix within a local window. The goal: maximize within-event key similarity and minimize between-event key similarity.

3. **Episodic Memory Store** — a structured key-value cache where each episodic event (a contiguous block of tokens with refined boundaries) is stored as a separate, retrievable unit, along with "representative tokens" (the tokens within each event that accumulated the highest attention scores) used as compact proxies for k-NN lookup.

4. **Two-Stage Memory Retrieval System** — when generating a new token, retrieves relevant events from the store using (a) a similarity buffer populated by k-NN search on representative tokens, and (b) a contiguity buffer populated by temporal neighbors of the similarity-retrieved events, implemented as a queue with natural decay.

5. **Context Window Assembly** — constructs the LLM's extended context window from three groups: initial tokens (128 attention sinks), the retrieved episodic events (from both similarity and contiguity buffers), and the local context (the most recent tokens within the LLM's native training length). The assembled window stays within the LLM's computational budget while providing access to information from arbitrarily far in the past.

Information flows as follows: a long input sequence arrives → the LLM processes it in chunks, with surprise computed per token → surprise thresholding marks candidate event boundaries → boundary refinement adjusts boundaries to optimize key-similarity coherence → refined events are stored in the episodic memory as KV cache blocks with representative token indices → when generating output, the current query keys are compared against all stored representative tokens via approximate k-NN → the top-$k_s$ similar events are loaded into the similarity buffer → temporal neighbors of those events are enqueued in the contiguity buffer → both buffers plus initial tokens and local context form the context window for the next generation step → the process repeats layer by layer and token by token.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the event boundary detection mechanism via Bayesian surprise: how the LLM's own prediction error is converted into a segmentation signal, the adaptive threshold formula, and why surprise is a psychologically and computationally grounded choice for this role.
- **Second**, the graph-theoretic refinement algorithm: how the attention-key similarity matrix is treated as an adjacency matrix, the two community-detection metrics (modularity and conductance), and how boundary positions are iteratively adjusted to optimize these metrics.
- **Third**, the episodic memory store structure: how events are represented, what representative tokens are and how they are selected, how the store is managed in memory (GPU/CPU/disk offloading), and the chunking strategy that makes the system computationally tractable.
- **Fourth**, the two-stage retrieval process: the k-NN similarity-based retrieval mechanism, the contiguity buffer as a queue data structure, how the sizes of the two buffers are balanced, and why this combination mirrors human free recall patterns.
- **Fifth**, the context window assembly and attention mechanism: how initial tokens, local context, and retrieved events are combined, how positional embeddings are assigned to discontinuous retrieved tokens, and how hierarchical attention emerges from this architecture.
- **Sixth**, the computational complexity analysis showing why EM-LLM scales to millions of tokens while full-context attention becomes infeasible.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **architectural design paper** whose core idea is that organizing an LLM's key-value cache into semantically coherent, event-like segments—using signals already present in the model's inference-time computation—enables efficient retrieval over arbitrarily long contexts without any fine-tuning. The three cognitive mechanisms (surprise segmentation, graph-theoretic refinement, two-stage retrieval with contiguity) are each independently motivated and independently evaluated, but their combination yields the full EM-LLM system.

---

#### Surprise-Based Event Boundary Detection

Event boundaries in EM-LLM are identified using the LLM's own next-token prediction error—what the paper calls "Bayesian surprise." This concept requires unpacking because it is the linchpin that makes the entire segmentation process possible without external supervision, additional models, or manual annotation.

**What is "surprise" in the context of an LLM?** An autoregressive language model, at each position $t$ in a sequence, computes a probability distribution over its vocabulary $P(x_t | x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}; \theta)$—the likelihood it assigns to each possible token given all preceding tokens. When the actual token $x_t$ (the ground-truth token from the input sequence) is revealed, the model's surprise at that token is defined as the negative log-likelihood:

$$S(x_t) = -\log P(x_t | x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}; \theta)$$

where $\theta$ represents the model parameters (frozen at inference time), $x_t$ is the token that actually appears at position $t$, and $P(x_t | x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}; \theta)$ is the probability the model assigned to that specific token before seeing it.

**What it computes:** the degree to which the model "expected" to see token $x_t$ versus being surprised by it. If the model assigns high probability to the token that actually appears (e.g., "the cat sat on the __" → "mat" with probability 0.9), surprise is low ($-\log(0.9) \approx 0.105$). If the token is unexpected (e.g., "the cat sat on the __" → "ceiling" with probability 0.001), surprise is high ($-\log(0.001) \approx 6.91$). The output is a non-negative scalar per token, where larger values indicate greater unpredictability.

**Why this form:** Negative log-likelihood is the standard information-theoretic measure of surprise (also called "surprisal" in the cognitive science literature). It has the property that it diverges to infinity as the model's probability approaches zero—tokens the model considers impossible produce extremely high surprise values—creating a natural contrast between routine, predictable tokens and novel or unexpected ones. Using the raw probability directly would be too compressed (differences near zero probability would be invisible), while using log-probability without negation would produce negative numbers with the wrong monotonicity (less probable tokens would have more negative values, which is unintuitive for thresholding).

**But a single token's surprise is noisy.** A model might be mildly surprised by many tokens in a row without any of them representing a genuine shift in topic or event structure. To separate meaningful "event boundaries" from background fluctuations, EM-LLM applies an adaptive threshold that adjusts to the local statistics of surprise:

$$T = \mu_{t-\tau:t} + \gamma \cdot \sigma_{t-\tau:t}$$

where $\mu_{t-\tau:t}$ is the mean surprise value over a sliding window of the most recent $\tau$ tokens (looking back from position $t$), $\sigma_{t-\tau:t}$ is the standard deviation of surprise over the same window, and $\gamma$ is a scaling factor that controls threshold sensitivity.

**What it computes:** a dynamic threshold that rises and falls with the ambient level of surprise in the recent context. The term $\mu_{t-\tau:t}$ centers the threshold at the local mean; the term $\gamma \cdot \sigma_{t-\tau:t}$ sets the bar at a fixed number of standard deviations above that mean. A token $x_t$ is flagged as a candidate event boundary if its surprise $S(x_t)$ exceeds $T$.

**Why this form:** Fixed thresholds fail because different contexts have different baseline levels of surprise. A passage of dense technical text might have consistently moderate surprise (many unpredictable domain-specific terms), while a simple narrative might have consistently low surprise. A fixed threshold would either over-segment the technical text (flagging every moderately surprising token) or under-segment the narrative (missing genuine transitions). The sliding-window adaptive threshold solves this by comparing each token's surprise to what is "normal for this neighborhood." The parameter $\gamma$ provides a single, interpretable knob: $\gamma=1$ means a token must be more surprising than roughly 84% of the local distribution (one standard deviation above the mean, assuming approximate normality); $\gamma=2$ means roughly 98% (two standard deviations); $\gamma=3$ means roughly 99.9%. The paper finds that $\gamma=1$ works best across most models and tasks (Appendix D.1), meaning relatively sensitive segmentation is preferred—many small events rather than few large ones.

**Why surprise specifically—the cognitive grounding.** The paper grounds this choice in a specific empirical and theoretical claim about human cognition (Section 3.2, Section 2.2):

> "the boundaries between such events are the access points for memory retrieval and are widely believed to correspond to points in time with high prediction errors between the brain's generative model and its raw sensory input (a.k.a., surprise)"

The logic is: if the brain segments continuous experience into discrete events at moments when its predictions are violated, and if an LLM's next-token prediction is analogous to the brain's sensory predictions, then the LLM's own prediction error should provide a segmentation signal without any external supervision. This is not merely an analogy—Kumar et al. (2023) empirically demonstrated that Bayesian surprise computed from LLM next-token probabilities correlates strongly with human-annotated event boundaries in the same narrative transcripts. EM-LLM operationalizes this finding: the segmentation signal is free (computed from the LLM's existing output logits), requires no additional model or training data, and has empirical grounding in both cognitive theory and prior computational work.

The paper also notes an important practical property: "surprise-based segmentation requires no additional computation" beyond what the LLM already computes during autoregressive generation (Section 1, Contributions). The log-probability of each token is available from the model's output distribution—the only added cost is computing the running mean and standard deviation and comparing against the threshold.

**The output of this step** is a set of candidate boundary indices $B = \{b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_k\}$ where each $b_i$ is the position of a token whose surprise exceeded the local threshold. These boundaries partition the token sequence into initial, coarse-grained events. But because the thresholding is purely local (each token's surprise is compared independently to the local window), the boundaries may not optimally group semantically related tokens—a boundary might be placed slightly too early or too late relative to the natural coherence structure in the attention keys. This motivates the refinement step.

#### Graph-Theoretic Boundary Refinement

The surprise-based boundaries provide an initial guess, but the paper makes a key observation that motivates refinement:

> "we theorise that memory recall will be most efficient with high intra-event similarity between keys while maintaining low inter-event similarity" (Section 3.3)

In other words, tokens within the same episodic event should have similar key representations (because they are part of the same semantic unit and the model attends to them in related ways), while tokens in different events should be more dissimilar. If a surprise-based boundary falls slightly too early, it might split two tokens that "belong together" from the attention mechanism's perspective. If it falls too late, it might group tokens from different semantic contexts. The refinement step adjusts the boundaries to optimize this coherence objective.

**The adjacency matrix construction.** To formalize the notion of "similarity between keys," EM-LLM constructs a weighted adjacency matrix for each attention head $h$ over the tokens within a processing window (a "chunk" of the input sequence). For tokens $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ in the chunk, the adjacency matrix $A^h$ is defined as:

$$A^h_{ij} = \text{sim}(K^h_i, K^h_j)$$

where $K^h_i$ and $K^h_j$ are the key vectors (the linear projections of the input embeddings used in the attention mechanism) for tokens $x_i$ and $x_j$ in attention head $h$, and $\text{sim}(\cdot, \cdot)$ is a similarity function. The paper uses **dot product similarity** $K^{hT}_i \cdot K^h_j$ (Section 3.3).

**What it computes:** an $n \times n$ matrix where entry $A^h_{ij}$ represents how "closely related" tokens $i$ and $j$ are from the perspective of attention head $h$. High values indicate that the two tokens have similar key representations—when the model's query vectors interact with these keys, they will produce similar attention patterns, meaning the tokens are treated as semantically related by the model.

**Why dot product similarity:** the paper explicitly states this is chosen "due to its effectiveness in capturing semantic relationships in high-dimensional spaces and to align with the mechanism of self-attention in Transformers" (Section 3.3). This is a critical design choice: the similarity metric used for refinement is exactly the same computation that the attention mechanism uses to compare queries and keys. This means the refinement is optimizing the same representational geometry that the attention mechanism operates on—tokens grouped together by the refinement will tend to have similar attention patterns, which is exactly what makes block-level retrieval effective (all tokens in a retrieved block contribute coherently to the attention output).

**The refinement as graph clustering.** With the adjacency matrix constructed, refining event boundaries becomes a graph clustering problem: partition the nodes (tokens) into communities (events) such that edges (key similarities) are dense within communities and sparse between them. The initial boundaries $B = \{b_1, \ldots, b_k\}$ define an initial community assignment: all tokens between $b_i$ and $b_{i+1}$ belong to community $i$.

The paper refines these boundaries using one of two standard graph-clustering quality metrics:

**Metric 1 — Modularity** (Newman and Girvan, 2004):

$$f_M(A^h, B) = \frac{1}{4m} \sum_{i,j} \left[ A^h_{ij} - \frac{1}{2m} \left(\sum_i A^h_{ij}\right) \left(\sum_j A^h_{ij}\right) \right] \delta(c_i, c_j)$$

where $m = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} A^h_{ij}$ is the total edge weight in the graph (summing all entries and dividing by 2 since the matrix is symmetric), $c_i$ is the community (episodic event) containing token $i$, and $\delta(c_i, c_j)$ is the Kronecker delta function, which equals 1 if tokens $i$ and $j$ are in the same community and 0 otherwise.

**What it computes:** modularity measures whether the actual edge weights within communities exceed what would be expected in a random graph with the same degree distribution. The term $A^h_{ij}$ is the observed similarity between tokens $i$ and $j$. The subtracted term $\frac{1}{2m}(\sum_i A^h_{ij})(\sum_j A^h_{ij})$ is the expected similarity under a null model where edges are placed randomly while preserving each node's total edge weight (its "degree"). The Kronecker delta $\delta(c_i, c_j)$ restricts the sum to pairs in the same community. If within-community similarities are higher than the random baseline, modularity is positive; if they are lower, modularity is negative.

**Why modularity:** it penalizes the trivial solution of putting all tokens in one community (expected and observed similarities would match, so $f_M \approx 0$), and it rewards partitions where communities are internally coherent relative to the background structure of the graph. The paper's goal—high intra-event key similarity and low inter-event similarity—is exactly what modularity quantifies. Maximizing modularity pushes boundaries to positions where the cut separates dissimilar tokens and groups similar ones.

**Metric 2 — Conductance:**

$$f_C(A^h, B) = \min_{S \in \mathcal{V}} \frac{\sum_{i \in S, j \notin S} A^h_{ij}}{\min(v_o(S), v_o(V \setminus S))}$$

where $S = \{b_i, b_i+1, \ldots, b_{i+1}\}$ is the set of tokens in one community (between two consecutive boundaries), $\mathcal{V} = \{b_1, b_1+1, \ldots, b_k\}$ is the set of all nodes in the induced subgraph (all tokens between the first and last boundaries), $v_o(S) = \sum_{i,j \in S} A_{ij}$ is the total internal edge weight of community $S$ (volume), and $v_o(V \setminus S)$ is the volume of all nodes outside $S$.

**What it computes:** conductance measures the fraction of total weighted edges incident to a community that cross its boundary (are cut). The numerator $\sum_{i \in S, j \notin S} A^h_{ij}$ is the total weight of edges from inside $S$ to outside $S$ (the "cut size"). The denominator $\min(v_o(S), v_o(V \setminus S))$ normalizes by the smaller community's volume, preventing the metric from favoring extremely small or extremely large partitions. Lower conductance means a better community structure—most of the high-weight edges are internal to the community, and few cross the boundary.

**Why conductance:** unlike modularity, which compares to a null model, conductance directly measures the ratio of cut edges to community size. It is more interpretable in absolute terms—a conductance of 0.1 means that 10% of the edge weight incident to the community crosses its boundary. However, conductance can be sensitive to community size imbalances, which the $\min$ normalization in the denominator partially addresses.

**The refinement algorithm.** Algorithm 1 in the paper (Section 3.3) describes the refinement procedure:

**Step 1 — Initial boundary identification:** All token positions $i$ where $-\log P(\text{tok}[i]) > T$ (the surprise exceeds the adaptive threshold) are collected as initial boundaries $B$.

**Step 2 — Sequential boundary adjustment:** For each pair of consecutive initial boundaries $(\alpha, \beta) = (B[i], B[i+1])$, the algorithm searches for the optimal position $\hat{\beta}$ in the range $(\alpha, \beta]$ that optimizes the chosen metric function $f$:

$$B[i+1] \leftarrow \arg\max_{\hat{\beta} \in (\alpha, \beta]} f(A, \{\alpha, \hat{\beta}\})$$

For each candidate $\hat{\beta}$, the algorithm re-evaluates the metric function with the boundary moved to that position. Since only one node changes community at a time (the token at position $\hat{\beta}$ moves from the right community to the left community, and tokens between the old $\beta$ and new $\hat{\beta}$ shift communities accordingly), the metric can be updated incrementally rather than recomputed from scratch. The average number of tokens whose community changes per boundary adjustment is approximately $n/k$, where $n$ is the total tokens and $k$ is the number of events, yielding a per-boundary adjustment cost of $O((n/k)^2)$.

**Key algorithmic properties:**

- **Monotonic improvement guarantee:** The argmax in the refinement step ensures that each boundary adjustment either improves the metric or leaves it unchanged. The algorithm never degrades the initial surprise-based segmentation—it starts from a "good" initialization (surprise boundaries, which already achieve higher modularity than fixed or random segmentation, as shown in Table 2) and only moves boundaries when doing so improves the metric.
- **No iterations required:** Unlike full community detection algorithms like Louvain (Blondel et al., 2008) that iterate to convergence, EM-LLM performs a single pass of sequential adjustments. The paper frames this as a cost-effectiveness tradeoff: "our algorithm simply implements a cost-effective way to look for any potential increase to this metric" (Appendix E.3) rather than seeking global optimality.
- **Computational complexity:** To avoid constructing the full $n \times n$ adjacency matrix over the entire sequence (which would be $O(n^2)$), the algorithm processes the sequence in **chunks** of size $m$. Each chunk's adjacency matrix costs $O(m^2)$ to construct. With $n/m$ chunks, the total matrix construction cost is $O(nm)$. The refinement cost within each chunk is $O(k \cdot (n/k)^2) = O(n^2/k)$, where $k$ is the number of events. Since the paper enforces that $n/k \leq m$ (event sizes are bounded by chunk size), the total complexity is $O(nm)$ (Appendix C.1). In practice, $m$ is chosen to be relatively small (typically 512 tokens, matching the chunk size used for attention computation), so the refinement adds modest overhead.

**Why refinement helps—the empirical evidence.** Table 2 demonstrates that surprise-only segmentation (S) already achieves 8.7–18.6% higher modularity than random segmentation across different LLMs (Mistral-7B, LLaMA2-7B, LLaMA3-8B). Adding modularity-based refinement (SM) increases this gain to 18.7–39.9%—roughly doubling the improvement in most cases. The conductance metric similarly improves. Crucially, fixed-size segmentation with refinement (FM) does not match the combination of surprise plus refinement (SM), showing that the initial surprise-based boundaries are essential: refinement alone, starting from arbitrary boundaries, cannot recover the structure that surprise detection provides.

#### Episodic Memory Store: Structure and Management

Once event boundaries are refined, the token sequence is partitioned into discrete episodic memory units. Each unit is a contiguous block of tokens with known start and end positions. The memory store is the organized collection of these units' key-value (KV) caches plus indexing structures for efficient retrieval.

**What is stored per event:**

1. **Full KV cache:** The key and value vectors for every token in the event, as computed by each attention head during the forward pass. These are necessary because, when the event is retrieved during later generation, the model must attend to the actual key and value representations to compute the attention output. The KV cache for a single event of length $L$ tokens, for a model with $H$ attention heads and key/value dimension $d$, requires storing $2 \times H \times L \times d$ floating-point numbers (keys plus values). For a 7B-parameter model with 32 heads and $d=128$, a 512-token event requires approximately $2 \times 32 \times 512 \times 128 = 4.19$ million float16 values, or about 8.4 MB.

2. **Representative tokens:** A small subset of tokens within each event that serve as compact proxies for k-NN retrieval. The paper follows InfLLM's approach (Xiao et al., 2024a): for each event, the tokens with the highest **accumulated attention scores** (summed across all attention heads and positions within the event) are selected as representatives. The representative token count is a small constant per event (typically 1–4 tokens), and their key vectors from one or more layers are stored as the indexing keys for retrieval.

**Why representative tokens:** Computing k-NN similarity between the current query and every token in every stored event would defeat the purpose of retrieval—the retrieval step itself would become $O(n)$ in the stored context length. By storing only a few representative key vectors per event, retrieval complexity depends on the number of events (much smaller than the number of tokens) rather than the total context length. The representative tokens are chosen based on accumulated attention scores because tokens that received high attention during encoding are likely to be the most informative or central tokens in that event—the ones best suited to represent the event's content during retrieval.

**Memory hierarchy (GPU, CPU, disk).** Since the full KV cache for millions of tokens would exceed even high-end GPU memory, EM-LLM uses a tiered storage system:

- **Local context (most recent tokens)** is kept in GPU memory for immediate access, with full softmax attention applied. The local context size is set to the LLM's native training window (typically 4K tokens, matching the base model's training length).
- **Retrieved events** are loaded into GPU memory when they are needed for a generation step, then evicted when no longer in the active context window.
- **The bulk of the episodic memory store** resides in CPU memory. The paper reports that with a sequence length of 1M tokens, the KV cache requires approximately 488 GB (half-precision, 32 layers, 32 KV heads; Table 11), which far exceeds GPU capacity but fits in CPU RAM. The representative tokens, by contrast, require only about 30.5 GB (one representative per block, times number of blocks).
- **For even longer sequences**, EM-LLM supports offloading to disk as a fallback (Appendix C.3.2). A least-recently-used (LRU) policy, adapted from InfLLM's GPU-to-CPU management, governs which events move from CPU to disk. The paper notes that disk offloading means "our framework only requires approximately 2GB of CPU memory per instance in order to run with little overhead, as long as there is enough available disk space."

**Why a multi-tier hierarchy:** This design directly confronts the quadratic attention problem by ensuring that the model never computes attention over more tokens than fit in GPU memory at once. The retrieval step selects which subset of the full context (the episodic memory) is relevant to the current generation step, and only that subset is materialized in GPU memory. The rest remains in cheaper, larger storage tiers, accessed only when retrieved.

#### Two-Stage Memory Retrieval: Similarity and Contiguity

When the model needs to generate a new token, it must decide which past episodic events to include in its context window. EM-LLM's retrieval process has two stages, each populating a separate buffer with a fixed total budget of $k$ events.

**Stage 1: Similarity-based retrieval ($k_s$ events).** The first stage retrieves events based on content similarity between the current query and the representative tokens of each stored event. The mechanism is k-nearest-neighbor (k-NN) search:

1. For each attention head $h$, the current **query vector** $Q^h$ (computed from the most recent token or tokens) is compared against the stored representative key vectors of all events in the episodic memory.
2. Similarity is computed as the **dot product** between the query and the representative key vectors: $\text{sim}(Q^h, K^h_{\text{rep}}) = Q^{hT} \cdot K^h_{\text{rep}}$. This is the same similarity function used in the attention mechanism itself.
3. Events are ranked by similarity, and the top $k_s$ events are selected. These form the **similarity buffer**.

For large memory stores (millions of tokens, thousands of events), exact k-NN would be prohibitively expensive. EM-LLM uses **approximate k-NN** via the FAISS library (Douze et al., 2024; Johnson et al., 2019), which builds efficient indexing structures (typically inverted file indices or product quantization) to find near-nearest neighbors in sublinear time. The paper uses a maximum memory unit count of $(n - n_l) / b$, where $n$ is total sequence length, $n_l$ is local context length, and $b$ is the minimum event size (enforced to prevent degenerate, single-token events that would inflate the event count and retrieval cost).

**Why k-NN retrieval works as an attention approximation.** Appendix F.1 provides a proof sketch showing that k-NN retrieval on key-value pairs is an approximate form of softmax attention over the full sequence. The argument rests on the **exponential dominance** property of the softmax: because softmax exponentiates the dot-product scores, keys with even moderately higher similarity to the query receive exponentially larger weights. If the top-$k$ most similar keys capture the vast majority of the attention mass (typically $\alpha \geq 0.95$ or higher for $k$ in the hundreds), then restricting attention to only those keys introduces only a small error $\epsilon$ in the attention output:

$$\| \sum_{i=1}^n a_i v_i - \sum_{i \in K'} a'_i v_i \| \leq \epsilon$$

where $a_i$ are the full-softmax attention weights, $a'_i$ are the renormalized weights over the retrieved subset, and $K'$ is the set of $k$ nearest neighbors. The sharper the softmax distribution (the more peaked it is around a few highly similar keys), the tighter this approximation.

**Stage 2: Contiguity-based retrieval ($k_c$ events).** The second stage populates a **contiguity buffer** that promotes the retrieval of events temporally adjacent to those already retrieved by similarity. The mechanism is implemented as a **queue**:

1. When an event is retrieved by the similarity buffer, its **neighboring events** from the original sequence—those within $\pm n$ positions in the temporal order—are identified. The paper uses $n=1$ for all experiments (Section 3.4, Appendix D.1), meaning the immediately preceding and immediately following events are considered.
2. These neighbor events are **enqueued** into the contiguity buffer, which has a fixed capacity of $k_c$ events.
3. The contiguity buffer operates as a FIFO queue: as new events are enqueued, older events are **dequeued** (removed). If an event is already in the buffer, it is not duplicated. This queue structure creates a "natural decay of temporal context as new events are processed" (Section 3.4).
4. The total number of retrieved events is $k = k_s + k_c$, with the ratio $k_r = k_c / k$ controlling the balance between similarity-driven and contiguity-driven retrieval. The paper sets $k_r = 0.3$—meaning 30% of the retrieved events come from contiguity, 70% from similarity—after ablating over $\{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ on LongBench with Mistral and finding a slight preference for $k_r = 0.3$ (Appendix D.1, Figure 13).

**Why contiguity?** The paper grounds this in two lines of evidence:

1. **Human free recall studies** (Howard and Kahana, 2002; Figure 3A): When people recall items from a studied list, they show a strong **temporal contiguity effect**—the probability of recalling item $i+j$ immediately after item $i$ decreases as $|j|$ increases. They also show **temporal asymmetry**—forward transitions ($i \to i+1$) are more likely than backward transitions ($i \to i-1$). These effects are robust across numerous experimental paradigms and are considered signatures of human episodic memory retrieval.

2. **LLM attention heads exhibit the same patterns** (Ji-An et al., 2024; Figure 3B): Transformers' "induction heads"—attention heads associated with in-context learning—show contiguity and asymmetry effects in their attention patterns that closely mirror human free recall curves. The paper's insight is that these heads can only express this behavior if temporally contiguous tokens are available in the context window. Without a contiguity buffer, the similarity-based retrieval might bring in events from distant parts of the input that are topically similar but temporally isolated, preventing the model from leveraging its built-in contiguity preference.

> "Transformers have the inherent ability to act as episodic memory retrieval models, if provided with the right information within their context window." (Section 2.2)

**The complementarity of similarity and contiguity.** The two buffers serve different functions that are task-dependent. Similarity retrieval finds events that are **semantically or topically related** to the current generation context—this is essential for tasks like question answering, where the model needs to find specific information from anywhere in the long context. Contiguity retrieval provides **temporal context** around similarity-retrieved events—this is essential for tasks requiring understanding of narrative flow, temporal order, or causal sequences, where events that happened nearby in time provide crucial context even if they are not topically similar to the query.

The paper shows empirically (Section 4.4) that the value of contiguity varies across tasks: "certain tasks still appear to benefit more from either surprise-only, refinement, or contiguity." Where contiguity is not crucial, adding a contiguity buffer reduces the similarity buffer size (since $k$ is fixed), potentially decreasing performance by crowding out more directly relevant events. The finding that "a contiguity buffer that is as big or smaller than the similarity buffer yields the best results" (Figure 13) confirms that similarity is the primary retrieval signal, with contiguity as a complementary enhancement rather than a replacement.

#### Layer-Wise Retrieval and Hierarchical Attention

A critical design feature of EM-LLM—and one of the key differentiators from RAG—is that retrieval is performed **independently at each attention layer** of the Transformer.

**How it works:** For each attention head $h$ in each layer $l$, the query vectors $Q^{l,h}$ are computed from the layer's input (the output of the previous layer, or the embedding layer for layer 1). These query vectors are compared against the representative key vectors stored for that same head and layer. Different layers (and different heads within a layer) can retrieve **different subsets of events** from the episodic memory.

**Why layer-wise retrieval matters:** The paper provides evidence (Figure 5, Appendix A.2) that different layers indeed retrieve different events. In one analyzed example from $\infty$-Bench's Longbook.Choice.Eng task, with over 500 chunks, a substantial fraction of the blocks retrieved by any given layer were **not retrieved by any other layer**. This means the layers specialize in attending to different parts of the context—lower layers might focus on local syntactic structure, middle layers on semantic content, and higher layers on task-relevant information. Layer-wise retrieval allows each layer to access the information most relevant to its computational role, rather than being constrained to a single set of retrieved chunks chosen before generation begins (as in RAG).

This architecture can be understood as a form of **hierarchical episodic attention** (Figure 2). At the first level, a coarse selection is made: which events are retrieved at all (k-NN on representative tokens). At the second level, standard softmax attention is applied within and across the retrieved events. This two-level structure preserves the benefits of block-based retrieval (tokens within an event can attend to each other, maintaining local coherence) while avoiding the quadratic cost of full-context attention.

#### Context Window Assembly and Positional Encoding

The final step in EM-LLM's processing cycle is assembling the context window that the LLM will actually attend to. The window consists of three groups (Section 3.1):

**Group 1: Initial tokens (128 tokens).** The first 128 tokens of the sequence are always retained in the context window. This follows the observation by Xiao et al. (2024b) and Han et al. (2024b) that the first few tokens of a sequence act as **attention sinks**—they accumulate disproportionately high attention scores across many heads and help stabilize the attention distribution. Removing these initial tokens degrades performance even when they contain no task-relevant information. By keeping them permanently in the context window, EM-LLM preserves this beneficial property without incurring significant cost (128 tokens is negligible compared to typical window sizes of 4K–8K tokens).

**Group 2: Retrieved episodic events ($k$ events from similarity and contiguity buffers).** These are the events retrieved by the two-stage process, loaded from the episodic memory store into GPU memory. The number of tokens in this group depends on the event sizes (which vary dynamically due to surprise-based segmentation) and the total budget $k$ (expressed as a maximum number of tokens to ensure the window stays within computational limits).

**Group 3: Local context (most recent tokens).** The most recent tokens, up to the LLM's native training window size, are kept with full softmax attention. This group "represents the most recent tokens, maximising information about the current task" and plays a role "similar to the focus of attention in cognitive models of working memory, holding the most immediately relevant information for the current task" (Section 3.1, citing Cowan, 2001).

**Positional encoding for discontinuous retrieved tokens.** A subtle technical problem arises: the retrieved events come from arbitrary positions in the original sequence, so they are not contiguous with the local context. If standard positional encodings were applied naively, the model would misinterpret their positions as being immediately adjacent to the local context tokens. Following the approach of Raffel et al. (2020) and InfLLM (Xiao et al., 2024a), EM-LLM assigns a **fixed, constant position embedding** to all retrieved tokens, treating them as a single "block" with a designated position that does not vary. This signals to the model that these tokens are retrieved from elsewhere and should not be interpreted as temporally contiguous with the local context. The initial 128 attention-sink tokens retain their original positions.

#### Computational Complexity and Scalability

The paper carefully analyzes the computational scaling of EM-LLM relative to full-context attention, demonstrating why it can handle 10M-token sequences that are infeasible for standard approaches.

**Full-context attention complexity:** Computing attention over a sequence of length $n$ requires constructing the $n \times n$ attention matrix (pairwise query-key dot products), which costs $O(n^2)$ operations per attention head. The $n^2$ term dominates, and for $n = 10^7$ (10M tokens), this requires approximately $10^{14}$ dot-product computations per head—prohibitively expensive.

**EM-LLM attention complexity:** The sequence is processed in chunks of size $m$ (e.g., $m=512$ tokens). For each chunk, attention is computed over:
- The chunk's own tokens ($m$ tokens, functioning as queries)
- The local context ($n_l$ tokens, typically 4K)
- The retrieved events ($n_r$ tokens, the sum of tokens in the $k$ retrieved events)

This gives per-chunk attention complexity of $O(m \cdot (n_l + n_r))$. With $n/m$ chunks, the total attention complexity across the full sequence is $O(n \cdot (n_l + n_r))$—**linear in $n$**, since $n_l$ and $n_r$ are constants (bounded by the context window size) that do not grow with total sequence length.

The k-NN retrieval step adds complexity of approximately $O(n(n - n_l) / (mb))$, where $b$ is the minimum event size. This is also linear in $n$ because each chunk performs one retrieval operation against a growing memory store (the number of stored events increases linearly with $n$), and each retrieval operation costs approximately $\log(N_{\text{events}})$ with approximate k-NN indexing.

The combined complexity is $O(n(n_l + n_r) + n(n-n_l)/(mb))$, which grows much more slowly than $O(n^2)$ as $n$ becomes large. Figures 9 and 10 (Appendix C.2) visualize this: at 100K tokens, EM-LLM's complexity is already an order of magnitude lower than full-context attention; at 10M tokens, full-context attention requires roughly $10^{14}$ operations while EM-LLM requires roughly $10^{10}$—a 10,000× reduction.

**Practical memory and hardware considerations.** The paper reports all experiments run on single nodes with 4 GPUs of 32GB each and 100GB+ CPU memory (Appendix C.3)—hardware that is modest by modern research standards (H100 GPUs typically have 80GB). To fit within these constraints for million-token sequences, EM-LLM employs:
- GPU-to-CPU offloading of the KV cache for inactive events
- CPU-to-disk offloading of least-recently-used events when CPU memory is exhausted
- Representative token offloading to CPU for very long sequences (1M+ tokens), though this increases wall-clock time due to slower k-NN lookup
- Optional model parallelism across GPUs using Hugging Face Accelerate for sequences above 2.5M tokens, to keep representative tokens on the same GPU as the layers that need them

The wall-clock overhead relative to InfLLM is modest: for Mistral-7B with 4K local + 2K retrieved tokens, EM-LLM with surprise-only segmentation (S) takes 1.57 seconds per chunk versus InfLLM's 1.40 seconds (a 12% increase). Adding modularity refinement (SM) raises this to 2.27 seconds (62% increase), primarily due to the $O(m^2)$ cost of computing the key-similarity adjacency matrix for each chunk (Table 10, Appendix C.3.1). The paper notes that this overhead "increases steadily as sequence length increases, due to the increasing number of representative tokens involved in the k-NN calculation," but remains manageable—the key tradeoff is accuracy of segmentation versus computational cost of refinement.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Event Segmentation as a First-Class Memory Organization Problem for LLMs

The dominant paradigm in long-context LLM research treats the key-value cache as an undifferentiated, flat store of vectors to be either compressed, selectively retained, or retrieved at the granularity of individual tokens or fixed-size blocks. InfLLM (Xiao et al., 2024a) represents the state-of-the-art in this paradigm: it segments the context into uniform blocks and retrieves the most similar ones via k-NN lookup on representative tokens. This approach makes a critical, implicit assumption—that **the boundaries between retrieval units don't matter**, or that fixed-size partitioning is "good enough." EM-LLM's central intellectual move is to reject this assumption entirely and reframe the problem as one of **memory organization**: before you can efficiently retrieve from a long context, you must first segment it into coherent, semantically meaningful units. This shifts the research question from "how do we compress/retrieve from attention more efficiently?" to "how do we structure the KV cache so that retrieval is maximally effective?"

What makes this framing genuinely novel—rather than an obvious extension—is that the paper identifies a **specific, measurable failure mode** of fixed segmentation and provides evidence that it matters for downstream performance. Fixed-size blocks inevitably split coherent episodes at arbitrary positions (an event boundary might fall mid-sentence) while simultaneously grouping unrelated tokens (a single block might span two different topics). The consequence, visible in Table 2, is that fixed segmentation (with or without refinement) achieves lower modularity, higher conductance, and worse intra/inter-similarity ratios than surprise-based segmentation across three different LLM families. This is not merely a theoretical concern: the performance gap between InfLLM and EM-LLM on retrieval-intensive tasks—up to 40% relative improvement on PassageRetrieval (Table 3) and 29.7% on multi-document QA tasks—provides direct evidence that poor segmentation degrades the model's ability to locate and attend to relevant information buried in a long context.

The paper further elevates this framing by connecting it to cognitive science. Human episodic memory does not store raw sensory streams—it segments continuous experience into discrete events at prediction-error boundaries (Zacks et al., 2007; Clewett et al., 2019). EM-LLM operationalizes this principle by using the LLM's own next-token prediction error (Bayesian surprise) as the segmentation signal. The finding that LLM-derived surprise boundaries correlate strongly with human-perceived event boundaries (Figure 4B, Wasserstein distance to human annotations) validates this connection empirically and suggests that the engineering problem of KV cache organization and the scientific problem of human memory encoding may share underlying principles. This is a bridge between two communities—NLP systems and cognitive neuroscience—that rarely interact at the architectural level.

This contribution is **fundamental rather than incremental**: it proposes a new axis of the long-context problem (memory organization) that prior work had not recognized as distinct from retrieval or compression, and it provides both theoretical motivation (cognitive grounding) and empirical validation (segmentation quality metrics + downstream performance) for why this axis matters.

---

### Innovation 2: Surprise as a Zero-Cost, Intrinsically Available Segmentation Signal for LLMs

Prior work on segmenting or chunking text for LLM context processing has relied almost exclusively on external criteria: fixed token counts (InfLLM, SLED; Xiao et al., 2024a; Ivgi et al., 2023), sentence or paragraph boundaries from pre-existing parsers, or embedding-based similarity thresholds computed by a separate model (as in some RAG chunking strategies). The implicit assumption is that meaningful segmentation requires either manual specification or an auxiliary model trained for the purpose. EM-LLM challenges this by demonstrating that the **LLM's own autoregressive prediction error**—a signal already computed during inference at zero additional cost—can serve as a high-quality, adaptive segmentation boundary detector that outperforms fixed baselines and correlates with human event perception.

What distinguishes this from a simple engineering trick is its **double grounding**. Computationally, the negative log-likelihood of the ground-truth token under the model's predicted distribution is a natural information-theoretic measure of "unexpectedness" that requires no additional forward passes, no auxiliary models, and no training data—the logits are already produced during autoregressive generation, and computing the threshold (sliding-window mean and standard deviation) is $O(1)$ per token. Cognitively, Bayesian surprise is the dominant theoretical account of how the human brain segments continuous experience into discrete events (Zacks et al., 2007; 2011; Fountas et al., 2022), and Kumar et al. (2023) provided direct evidence that LLM surprise predicts human event boundaries in narrative transcripts. EM-LLM is the first to operationalize this finding as an architectural component of an LLM inference system, closing the loop from cognitive theory to engineering practice.

The adaptive threshold mechanism (Equation 1) is notable not for its complexity but for its **robustness to context shift**: by using a sliding window's local mean and standard deviation rather than a global fixed threshold, the detector automatically recalibrates to the ambient level of surprise in the current context. This avoids the brittleness that would plague a fixed-threshold approach—dense technical text and simple narrative would require radically different thresholds. The paper's ablation over $\gamma \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ (Appendix D.1, Figure 12) reveals a consistent preference for $\gamma = 1$ across five different base LLMs, suggesting the method is remarkably insensitive to model-specific tuning. This is practically important: a segmentation method that required per-model or per-task threshold optimization would be far less deployable.

The empirical validation that this signal actually produces better-structured memory than fixed partitioning is centralized in Table 2. Surprise-only segmentation (S) achieves 8.7–18.6% higher modularity than random segmentation across Mistral-7B, LLaMA2-7B, and LLaMA3-8B, with corresponding improvements in conductance and intra/inter-similarity ratios. Adding graph-theoretic refinement (SM) roughly doubles these gains (to 18.7–39.9% for modularity). Crucially, the improvement over fixed-size segmentation is not marginal—it is large enough to drive the downstream performance gains seen in Tables 1 and 3–7.

This contribution is **fundamental as a concept but incremental in its specific implementation**: the idea that LLM prediction error can serve as a segmentation signal is novel and opens a research direction; the specific thresholding formula (sliding window with $\gamma \sigma$) is a straightforward application of standard statistical process control. The paper acknowledges this by focusing its claims on the principle rather than the formula.

---

### Innovation 3: Graph-Theoretic Refinement Optimizes the KV Cache Geometry for Retrieval

A natural criticism of surprise-based segmentation is that it is purely **local**—each token's boundary decision is made based on its surprise relative to a neighborhood window, with no global consideration of whether the resulting segments are coherent in the representation space that matters most for retrieval: the attention key geometry. EM-LLM's boundary refinement step addresses this by posing a precise optimization problem over the attention-key similarity matrix: given initial boundaries from surprise, adjust them to maximize within-event key similarity while minimizing between-event key similarity. This is a genuinely novel contribution because it connects two domains—graph community detection and LLM key-value cache organization—that have not previously been linked.

The intellectual move here is recognizing that the **utility of a memory segment during retrieval depends on its internal coherence**. When an event is retrieved, all its constituent tokens are loaded into the context window and participate in softmax attention. If those tokens have highly similar key representations (the dot products among their key vectors are large), they will form a coherent, mutually reinforcing attention pattern—the attention mechanism can "make sense of" them as a group. If they are dissimilar (the event spans a natural boundary, mixing tokens from different topics), the attention pattern will be diluted and confused. The refinement step optimizes for this directly by treating the key-similarity matrix as a weighted graph and maximizing modularity or minimizing conductance over the partition induced by the boundaries.

What makes this more than an optimization trick is the paper's empirical demonstration that **the refinement objective matters in a model-specific way**. Table 2 shows that modularity-based refinement (SM) consistently outperforms conductance-based refinement (SC) in terms of similarity metrics. Figure 4A demonstrates that both surprise-based and refined segmentations produce key-similarity structures closer to human event segmentation than fixed methods. And the ablation in Section 4.4 shows that refinement helps most on tasks where precision of retrieval matters—the addition of modularity refinement achieves the best performance in 60% of tasks across LongBench and ∞-Bench (Appendix A.1, Tables 3–7). This task-dependence of refinement's benefit confirms that the optimization is not just a generic "make things better" step—it specifically improves the quality of retrieval, which matters more for some tasks than others.

The algorithmic design—a single pass of sequential boundary adjustments rather than iterative convergence to a global optimum (as in Louvain; Blondel et al., 2008)—reflects a deliberate **cost-quality tradeoff**. The paper acknowledges that full community detection would likely achieve higher modularity, but the $O(nm)$ complexity of the single-pass variant keeps it practical for long-context inference. This is a pragmatic engineering choice that does not diminish the conceptual contribution: the key insight is that the key-similarity matrix has community structure that can be exploited, not that any particular algorithm is optimal for finding it.

This contribution is **incremental as an algorithm (graph clustering is well-established) but fundamental as a reframing** of what makes a "good" memory unit in an LLM's KV cache. Prior work defined goodness implicitly (fixed-size, equal treatment of all tokens); EM-LLM provides an explicit, measurable, and optimizable objective.

### Innovation 4: Contiguity as an Architectural Primitive—Not Just an Empirical Bias—in LLM Retrieval

The human memory literature has documented for decades that free recall exhibits **temporal contiguity** (items experienced close together in time tend to be recalled together) and **temporal asymmetry** (forward transitions are more likely than backward; Howard and Kahana, 2002). Ji-An et al. (2024) recently demonstrated that Transformer attention heads, particularly those involved in in-context learning, exhibit the same contiguity and asymmetry patterns in their attention scores. EM-LLM is the first architecture to **explicitly incorporate contiguity as a retrieval primitive**—not as an empirical observation about model behavior, but as a designed mechanism (the contiguity buffer) that gives the model's contiguity-sensitive heads the structured input they need to express this behavior over arbitrarily long sequences.

The distinction from prior work is significant. InfLLM and similar methods retrieve blocks purely by similarity—the top-$k$ most similar blocks, regardless of their temporal positions. This means that for a given query, the retrieved context might consist of semantically relevant but temporally scattered fragments. Ji-An et al. showed that Transformers *can* exhibit contiguity effects when contiguous tokens are present, but without a mechanism to ensure contiguous tokens are loaded together, the effect cannot manifest. EM-LLM's contiguity buffer is an architectural commitment to providing this structured input: when an event is retrieved by similarity, its temporal neighbors are automatically included, creating the contiguous sequences that contiguity-sensitive heads need.

The paper's ablation results (Section 4.4, Appendix A.1) reveal a nuanced picture that strengthens rather than weakens this contribution. The contiguity buffer does not help uniformly—some tasks benefit, others do not, and on some tasks, the addition of contiguity actually reduces performance by shrinking the similarity buffer (since the total $k$ is fixed). This task-dependence is exactly what one would expect if contiguity is a genuine cognitive primitive rather than a universal performance booster: tasks requiring temporal reasoning (narrative comprehension, tracking event sequences) benefit from contiguity because understanding "what happened before and after" is essential; tasks requiring pure fact retrieval (finding a specific passage that answers a question) benefit more from maximizing similarity-based retrieval, and the contiguity buffer's reduction of the similarity budget hurts. This is a **diagnostic finding**: the paper has identified that contiguity is *sometimes* useful, which means future systems should treat it as a tunable parameter (or even a dynamic, per-task adjustment) rather than a fixed architectural feature.

The queue-based implementation—FIFO with natural decay, capacity $k_c$, ratio $k_r = k_c/k$ tunable—is notable for its simplicity and its conceptual alignment with memory models where temporal context fades as new events are processed. The finding that $k_r = 0.3$ (30% contiguity, 70% similarity) is optimal across the tested values (Appendix D.1, Figure 13) suggests that contiguity is a complementary signal that should augment but not dominate similarity-based retrieval—again mirroring human memory, where semantic similarity and temporal proximity interact but do not substitute for each other.

This contribution is **fundamental as a concept** (establishing contiguity as an architectural primitive for LLM memory) but **modest in its performance impact** compared to surprise segmentation and refinement. The paper is transparent about this: contiguity achieves the best performance in 44% of tasks across benchmarks, while refinement achieves it in 60%. The value of the contiguity buffer is not primarily in the magnitude of performance gains but in the **architectural vocabulary** it introduces—future systems can build on the idea that retrieval should blend similarity and contiguity, adjusting the ratio per task, per layer, or even dynamically during generation.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All primary experiments use two benchmarks: **LongBench** (Bai et al., 2023), a bilingual multi-task benchmark covering single-document QA, multi-document QA, summarization, few-shot learning, retrieval, and code tasks across 15 subtasks; and **∞-Bench** (Zhang et al., 2024), designed for evaluating long-context understanding beyond 100K tokens, with subtasks including retrieval (Retrieve.PassKey, Retrieve.KV, Retrieve.Number), multiple-choice (En.MC), code debugging (Code.Debug), and math finding (Math.Find). The paper uses the standard test splits for both benchmarks.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments span five instruction-tuned LLM families: **Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2**, **LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct**, **LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct**, **Phi-3-Mini-4K-Instruct**, and **Phi-3.5-mini-Instruct**. The paper argues this selection "represents a diverse range of model architectures and training paradigms" (though no formal justification is provided for why these specific sizes—all 7–8B parameters—are chosen). For the human event segmentation correlation experiments (Section 4.2), Mistral-7B, LLaMA2-7B, and LLaMA3-8B are used without instruction tuning. For the PG-19 segmentation quality experiments (Table 2), the same three base models are used.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric for LongBench and ∞-Bench is **task accuracy** as defined by each benchmark's standard evaluation protocol (exact match for QA and retrieval tasks, ROUGE-L for summarization, pass@1 for code tasks). For segmentation quality analysis (Section 4.2–4.3), three graph-theoretic metrics are employed: **modularity** (higher is better, measuring the density of within-community edges relative to a random null model), **conductance** (lower is better, measuring the fraction of edge weight crossing community boundaries), and **intra-/inter-community similarity ratio (I/IS)**, defined as $\text{intra} = \sum_{i \in S, j \in S} A_{ij}$ divided by $\text{inter} = \sum_{i \in S, j \notin S} A_{ij}$ (higher is better). For human correlation experiments, **Wasserstein distance** is used to compare the distribution of boundary positions between model segmentations and human annotations.

- **Baselines.** The primary baseline is **InfLLM** (Xiao et al., 2024a), identified as the state-of-the-art KV-retrieval method at the time of writing. InfLLM uses fixed-size memory units, representative token selection per unit, and k-NN retrieval. The paper matches InfLLM's local-plus-retrieved token budget for fair comparison (e.g., 4K local + 2K retrieved for Mistral, 4K+4K for LLaMA-3 and LLaMA-3.1, 1K+3K for Phi-3 and Phi-3.5). Additional baselines include **RAG** with two retriever models (NV-Embed-v2 by Lee et al., 2024—a fine-tuned Mistral-7B retriever ranking first on MTEB at time of writing—and all-mpnet-base-v2 by Reimers, 2022—a 110M-parameter BERT-based retriever), both with LLaMA-3-8B and LLaMA-3.1-8B as downstream generators, using $l=300$ word chunks and $k=5$ retrieved chunks following Li et al. (2024c). **Full-context** models (processing all tokens with standard softmax attention) serve as an upper bound, evaluated via API access. A custom **RAG-S** variant that segments context using EM-LLM's surprise-based boundaries before retrieval is also evaluated.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The primary unit of comparison is the **context window composition**: the number of local tokens plus the number of retrieved (evicted) tokens that the model attends to. For InfLLM and EM-LLM, this is expressed as, e.g., "4K+2K" meaning 4,096 local tokens and 2,048 retrieved tokens. The paper does not measure wall-clock time or FLOPs directly in its primary comparisons, but reports wall-clock overhead in Appendix C.3.1 (Table 10) as seconds per 512-token chunk processed. Memory requirements are separately reported in Table 11.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is used for the LongBench or ∞-Bench evaluations—the test sets are standard and fixed. Statistical significance at the benchmark level is assessed using a **two-tailed z-test**: the paper reports $p < 0.05$ for all base LLMs on LongBench except Phi-3.5 ($p = 0.23$). The paper acknowledges that "the lower, task-level significance [is] largely due to the sample size of individual tasks rather than chance" (Appendix A.1), since individual subtasks have relatively few examples. For the PG-19 segmentation quality experiments (Table 2), results are reported as the **difference from random segmentation**, averaged across the dataset, with standard deviations included. For human correlation experiments, the Wasserstein distance comparison is performed on three short podcast transcripts (7–30 minutes each) from Kumar et al. (2023)'s released dataset.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Comparison with KV-Retrieval-Based LLMs (InfLLM Baseline)

**Headline result:** EM-LLM outperforms InfLLM across all five base LLMs tested, with benchmark-level average improvements ranging from +1.62% (LLaMA-3-8B, Table 4) to +8.98% (Phi-3-Mini-4K, Table 6) on LongBench, and +1.47% (Mistral-7B, Table 3) to +4.70% (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Table 5) on ∞-Bench. The best single method variant (varying by base LLM) achieves improvements on 80% of individual LongBench task groups (Table 1).

**Per-model breakdown (Table 1):**

- **Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 (4K+2K):** EM-LLM with surprise + modularity refinement + contiguity (SM+C) achieves 43.71% LongBench average vs. InfLLM's 41.90% (+1.81 pp). On ∞-Bench, the same variant achieves 66.16% vs. 65.78% (+0.38 pp). The largest single-task improvement is on PassageRetrieval: 84.92% (S+C variant) vs. InfLLM's 64.00%—a 32.7% relative increase. Retrieval tasks as a group show the most dramatic gains: up to +32.69% relative on PassageRetrieval (Table 3).

- **LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct (4K+4K):** EM-LLM with surprise + modularity (SM) achieves 47.24% LongBench average vs. InfLLM's 46.95% (+0.29 pp). However, on ∞-Bench, EM-LLM performs *worse* than InfLLM: 48.90% vs. 50.31% (−1.41 pp), driven primarily by a dramatic drop on Retrieve.KV (4.80% vs. InfLLM's 5.00%, but note both are near floor) and on Math.Find (16.57% vs. 23.70%, a −30.1% relative decrease; Table 4). This is a notable failure case that the paper does not discuss in the main text.

- **LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct (4K+4K):** EM-LLM with surprise only (S) achieves 51.58% LongBench average vs. InfLLM's 51.05% (+0.53 pp). On ∞-Bench, surprise only (S) achieves 66.66% vs. InfLLM's 64.00% (+2.66 pp), with the largest gain on Retrieve.KV: 96.80% vs. 81.00% (+19.5% relative; Table 5). This is the strongest overall ∞-Bench result across all model variants.

- **Phi-3-Mini-4K-Instruct (1K+3K):** EM-LLM with contiguity only (S+C) achieves 35.46% LongBench average vs. InfLLM's 34.46% (+1.00 pp; Table 6). Note the smaller context window (1K+3K) reflects Phi-3's shorter native training length.

- **Phi-3.5-mini-Instruct (1K+3K):** EM-LLM with surprise only (S) achieves 34.86% LongBench average vs. InfLLM's 34.22% (+0.64 pp; Table 7). This is the smallest improvement and the only model where the benchmark-level improvement is not statistically significant ($p = 0.23$).

**Task-level patterns (across all models, from Tables 3–7):**

- **Retrieval tasks show the largest and most consistent gains.** PassageRetrieval improvements range from +4.17% (LLaMA-3, Table 4) to +32.69% (Mistral, Table 3) relative to InfLLM. Retrieve.KV improvements are dramatic for LLaMA-3.1 (+19.51% relative, Table 5) but inconsistent—recall LLaMA-3's degrade on this task.
- **Multi-document QA tasks (HotpotQA, 2WikiMQA, Musique) show substantial improvements.** The maximum relative improvement across these three tasks ranges from +6.17% to +29.70% depending on the model and task (Tables 3–7).
- **Summarization tasks (GovReport, QMSum, MultiNews) show small and mixed effects.** Maximum relative improvements are typically < 3%, and some variants show slight degradations (e.g., MultiNews for Phi-3, Table 6: −0.49%).
- **Few-shot learning tasks (TREC, TriviaQA, SAMSum) show inconsistent effects.** SAMSum benefits significantly on LLaMA-3 (+1.98 pp, Table 4) but degrades on Phi-3 (−0.54 pp, Table 7). TREC improves on Mistral (+2.90%, Table 3) but shows no change on LLaMA-3 (Table 4).
- **Code tasks (LCC, RepoBench-P) are mixed.** EM-LLM improves RepoBench-P on LLaMA-3.1 (+2.83% relative, Table 5) but degrades it on LLaMA-3 (−3.44% relative, Table 4) and Phi-3.5 (−3.20% relative for LCC, Table 7).

**Best-performing EM-LLM variant by task count (Appendix A.1, Tables 3–7):** Across all five base LLMs and both benchmarks, the surprise + modularity refinement (SM) variant achieves the best performance in the largest number of individual task configurations (60% of tasks, as stated in Section 4.4). The contiguity-only variant (S+C) is best in 44% of tasks. The fully combined variant (SM+C) is best in a smaller fraction, suggesting that contiguity and refinement sometimes compete for the fixed retrieval budget rather than synergizing.

#### Comparison with RAG and Full-Context Models

**Headline result:** EM-LLM substantially outperforms both RAG pipelines and full-context models on LongBench, while achieving comparable or better performance on ∞-Bench, using LLaMA-3.1-8B as the base LLM (Table 9; Figure 1, top panel).

**LongBench results (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Table 9):**

- **EM-LLM (S variant, 4K+4K):** 51.58% average
- **Full-context (all tokens in softmax attention):** 39.30% average
- **RAG with NV-Embed-v2 retriever:** 36.44% average
- **RAG with all-mpnet-base-v2 retriever:** 30.75% average (Table 8, with LLaMA-3-8B)
- **RAG-S (surprise-segmented chunks with NV-Embed-v2):** 25.89% average

The gap between EM-LLM and full-context is particularly striking: +12.28 percentage points. The paper attributes this to full-context models suffering from diluted attention over long sequences—when all tokens compete for attention weight, the softmax distribution flattens and relevant information is lost in noise. EM-LLM's retrieval mechanism, by contrast, forces the model to attend to a smaller, curated subset of the context, maintaining sharper attention distributions.

The RAG comparison reveals the value of layer-wise retrieval. EM-LLM outperforms NV-Embed-v2 RAG by +15.14 percentage points on LongBench. The paper argues (Appendix A.2, Figure 5) that this is because RAG retrieves chunks once before generation, using a separate embedding model, and the same chunks are used by all layers. EM-LLM retrieves different events for different layers, allowing each attention head to access the most relevant parts of the context for its specific computational role. The RAG-S variant performs even worse than standard RAG (25.89% vs. 36.44%), which the paper attributes to surprise-based chunks being "highly disjointed" when retrieved without contiguity mechanisms—small, semantically coherent chunks that lack surrounding context confuse the model.

**∞-Bench results (LLaMA-3.1-8B, Table 9):**

- **EM-LLM (S variant, 4K+4K):** 66.66% average
- **Full-context:** 66.33% average
- **RAG (NV-Embed-v2):** 58.97% average

The gap between EM-LLM and full-context narrows to +0.33 percentage points on ∞-Bench—essentially tied. This is notable because ∞-Bench has much longer sequences on average (>100K tokens) than LongBench (~12K tokens), meaning full-context models are operating at the edge of their feasible context length. The paper interprets this as evidence that EM-LLM maintains performance as context length grows while full-context models degrade.

**Extreme-length retrieval (Figure 1, bottom panel):** On the Retrieve.PassKey task from an extended version of ∞-Bench, EM-LLM achieves **100% accuracy at 10 million tokens** (10.2M, per Section 4.1). This is compared against a series of context-extension methods (YaRN, LongLoRA, LongRoPE, etc.) whose context windows top out at 128K–2048K tokens. The 10M-token result represents a 5× increase over the longest previously reported context window (LongRoPE's 2048K). The paper notes this is "a scale computationally infeasible for such [full-context] models."

#### Human Event Segmentation Correlation (Section 4.2)

**Headline result:** EM-LLM's surprise-based event boundaries correlate significantly with human-perceived event boundaries, and this correlation is stronger than for fixed-size or random segmentation.

**Figure 4A analysis:** The figure compares six segmentation methods (Fixed, Fixed+Modularity, Fixed+Conductance, Surprise, Surprise+Modularity, Surprise+Conductance) plus Human annotations, across layers of LLaMA2-7B, on three metrics: modularity, conductance, and intra-/inter-similarity ratio. Key observations:

- **Human-perceived events achieve the highest similarity metrics** across most layers, confirming that event segmentation in human perception produces coherent, well-separated memory units in the LLM's key-similarity space.
- **Surprise-based methods (S, SM, SC) achieve metrics very close to human annotations**, while fixed-based methods (F, FM, FC) perform substantially worse—and in some cases, worse than random (the paper notes that "InfLLM's method [F] perform[s] worse than random" in Figure 4A legend, though the exact numbers are in Figure 4B).
- **Refinement improves surprise-based segmentation further:** SM and SC consistently show higher modularity and I/IS and lower conductance than S alone across most layers.

**Figure 4B analysis:** The Wasserstein distance between the distribution of boundary positions for each method and the human-annotated distribution (converted to a Mixture of Gaussians for fair comparison; Appendix B.1) reveals:

- **Surprise-based methods are consistently closest to human annotations** (lowest Wasserstein distance). The exact values are not provided in the main text but are visible in the bar chart, with SC achieving the closest match.
- **Fixed methods are further from human annotations than random segmentation** in several cases. The paper notes this explicitly: "InfLLM's method (F) performing worse than random" (Figure 4 caption).
- **Refinement improves alignment with human data** for both surprise-based and fixed-based segmentation, but the combination of surprise initialization + refinement produces the best results.

**Generalization across models and podcasts (Appendix B.2, Figures 7–8):** The same patterns replicate across Mistral-7B (Figure 7), LLaMA-3-8B (Figure 8), and across two different podcast stimuli ("Monkey" and "Tunnel" podcasts). This reproducibility across models and stimuli strengthens the claim that the correlation between LLM surprise and human event segmentation is not model-specific or stimulus-specific.

#### Segmentation Quality on PG-19 (Section 4.3, Table 2)

**Headline result:** Surprise-based segmentation with modularity refinement (SM) achieves the best graph-theoretic coherence metrics across all three LLMs tested, substantially outperforming both fixed and surprise-only baselines.

**Quantitative results (Table 2, reported as difference from random segmentation):**

For **Mistral-7B:**
- Modularity gain over random: S = +18.6, SM = **+39.9** (best), SC = +29.5, FM = +29.2, FC = +6.7, F = −2.3
- Conductance reduction vs. random: S = −23.6, SM = −24.6, SC = **−27.6** (best), FM = −16.9, FC = −12.5, F = +9.1 (worse than random)
- I/IS gain over random: S = +17.9, SM = **+35.3** (best), SC = +21.6, FM = +31.2, FC = +3.7, F = −4.3

The pattern is consistent: **SM (surprise + modularity) achieves the best modularity and I/IS**, while **SC (surprise + conductance) achieves the best conductance**—expected, since each refinement method optimizes its respective metric. More importantly, **surprise-based methods consistently outperform their fixed-based counterparts** using the same refinement metric (SM > FM, SC > FC), demonstrating that the initial surprise segmentation provides a superior starting point for refinement.

For **LLaMA2-7B and LLaMA3-8B**, the same ordinal pattern holds, with slightly lower absolute values. SM achieves modularity gains of +18.7 and +27.0 respectively.

**Key insight from Table 2:** Fixed segmentation (F) performs **worse than random** on modularity and I/IS across all models (negative values in the "Mod ↑" and "I/IS ↑" rows for F). Fixed + modularity refinement (FM) recovers to positive values but still underperforms surprise-only (S). This demonstrates that the quality of initial boundaries matters—refinement can improve a poor initialization but cannot fully compensate for it.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Surprise threshold sensitivity (γ parameter, Appendix D.1, Figure 12):** The paper sweeps $\gamma \in \{1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5\}$ using Mistral-7B on LongBench. Higher γ values produce fewer events (larger mean event size) and generally lower performance. γ=1 is best for Mistral, LLaMA-3.1, Phi-3, and Phi-3.5; γ=2 is best for LLaMA-3. The paper notes that "the addition of our refinement algorithm (SM) seemed to show particularly high improvements over surprise-only at larger values of γ"—refinement partially compensates for coarser initial segmentation, but the best absolute performance remains at γ=1.

**Contiguity buffer ratio ($k_r = k_c / k$, Appendix D.1, Figure 13):** Sweeping $k_r \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ on LongBench with Mistral-7B and γ ∈ {1, 2} shows a slight overall preference for $k_r = 0.3$. The paper notes task-dependent variation: some tasks perform best at $k_r = 0.7$ (more contiguity), but most prefer the lower ratios. This task-dependence is cited as evidence that contiguity's importance varies by task type. All subsequent experiments use $k_r = 0.3$.

**Retrieved buffer size (Appendix D.3, Table 12, Figure 14):** An ablation varying the number of retrieved tokens (1K, 2K, 4K, 6K) for EM-LLM (S variant) with Mistral-7B on LongBench's summarization tasks shows mostly flat performance: GovReport 31.26–31.44%, MultiNews 26.59–26.67%. QMSum shows some sensitivity (23.24% at 1K to 24.47% at 4K). Figure 14 shows that longer examples benefit more from larger retrieved buffers, consistent with the intuition that longer documents require more retrieved context for adequate coverage.

**Human segmentation correlation across models (Appendix B.2, Figures 6–8):** Reproducing Figure 4's analysis with Mistral-7B (Figure 7) and LLaMA-3-8B (Figure 8) shows the same qualitative patterns: surprise-based methods are closest to human annotations, fixed methods are farthest. LLaMA-2 (Figure 6) and LLaMA-3 (Figure 8) show slightly different layer-wise profiles for the metrics but maintain the same ordinal ranking of methods.

**RAG with surprise-based chunking (RAG-S, Appendix A.2, Table 9):** Using EM-LLM's surprise-based segmentation as the chunking strategy for a standard RAG pipeline (with NV-Embed-v2 retriever) performs significantly *worse* than fixed-size chunking (25.89% vs. 36.44% LongBench average). The paper hypothesizes this is due to surprise-based chunks being smaller and more semantically focused, leading to "highly disjointed" retrieved context when temporal neighbors are not included. This is a negative result that highlights the importance of contiguity for RAG-style retrieval when using adaptive segmentation.

**Memory management and scalability (Appendix C.3):** Table 11 provides concrete memory requirements: 1M-token sequences require ~488 GB for the full KV cache in half-precision (for a 32-layer, 32-head model) plus ~30.5 GB for representative tokens. The paper demonstrates scalability to 10M tokens (Section 4.1) using CPU offloading and disk fallback, though wall-clock time and memory numbers for 10M tokens are not explicitly reported in Table 11.

**Layer-wise retrieval diversity (Appendix A.2, Figure 5):** Analyzing a single example from ∞-Bench's Longbook.Choice.Eng task, the paper measures the ratio of blocks retrieved by a layer that were *not* retrieved by any other layer, versus the total retrieved blocks for that layer. The substantial fraction of layer-unique retrievals is used as evidence that layer-wise retrieval provides diversity that single-step RAG cannot match. However, this is a single-example anecdotal analysis, not a systematic study across tasks or examples.

### Critical Assessment

#### Where the Experiments Genuinely Support the Claims

**Claim: EM-LLM outperforms the SOTA retrieval model InfLLM.** This claim is well-supported across five base LLMs and two benchmarks (Tables 1, 3–7). The statistical significance at the benchmark level holds for 4 of 5 models. However, the claim's strength is moderated by the observation that improvements are highly **task-dependent and sometimes negative**—on LLaMA-3-8B's ∞-Bench results (Table 4), EM-LLM *underperforms* InfLLM by 1.41 percentage points due to catastrophic drops on Math.Find and Retrieve.KV. A reader could reasonably interpret the evidence as "EM-LLM improves InfLLM on most but not all configurations, with particularly strong gains on retrieval and multi-document QA and weak or negative results on some reasoning and code tasks." The paper's framing of "consistently outperforming" in the abstract is somewhat stronger than the data supports if "consistently" is interpreted as "across all task-model pairs."

**Claim: EM-LLM outperforms RAG and full-context models.** This claim is supported for the specific comparison with LLaMA-3.1-8B on LongBench (Table 9, Figure 1 top). However, the full-context comparison uses an **API-based evaluation** whose implementation details (which API, what context-window configuration, whether FlashAttention was used) are not reported. More importantly, the full-context model is evaluated with the *same* base LLM (LLaMA-3.1-8B) but processing all tokens—yet LLaMA-3.1-8B has a maximum native context window, and the paper does not specify how it was extended (if at all) for sequences exceeding that window. If the full-context model was run with standard softmax attention over sequences much longer than its training length, positional encoding extrapolation failure (documented in Section 2.1) would be an additional source of error beyond attention dilution. The comparison is thus partially confounded: EM-LLM's advantage may partly reflect the base LLM's inability to handle out-of-distribution sequence lengths rather than EM-LLM's retrieval mechanism per se.

**Claim: EM-LLM achieves passkey retrieval across 10M tokens.** This is a single-result claim supported by the Figure 1 bottom panel, but insufficient methodological detail is provided: what was the retrieval setup (how many events were stored, what was the retrieval budget, what was the chunk size)? What was the passkey placement (beginning, middle, end)? The passkey retrieval test is a well-known benchmark for long-context evaluation, and performance is highly sensitive to where the key is placed (the "lost in the middle" problem; Liu et al., 2024a). Without reporting results at multiple positions, the 100% accuracy claim is incomplete.

**Claim: EM-LLM's event segmentation correlates with human-perceived events.** This claim is supported by the Wasserstein distance comparisons in Figure 4B and Appendix B.2 (Figures 6–8). The finding that surprise-based methods are closer to human annotations than fixed or random segmentation is robust across three LLMs and two podcast stimuli. However, the sample size is tiny: **three podcast transcripts** (7–30 minutes each). The human data from Kumar et al. (2023) represents event boundaries from a small number of participants listening to short audio clips. Whether the correlation holds for longer, more diverse narrative types (books, technical documents, multi-speaker conversations) is completely untested.

#### Significant Weaknesses

**1. The difficulty estimation / chunking strategy is not systematically ablated.** The paper processes sequences in "chunks" for both the boundary refinement and the attention computation, but the chunk size $m$ is never varied or its impact measured. All the complexity analysis (Appendix C.1) depends on $m$ being small relative to $n$, yet $m$ also determines the maximum event size and the window over which refinement operates (since the adjacency matrix is constructed per-chunk). How does performance change with $m = 256$ vs. $m = 1024$? This is a significant gap because $m$ controls the tradeoff between refinement quality (larger $m$ allows more global optimization) and computational cost ($O(nm)$ complexity).

**2. The representative token selection is inherited from InfLLM without ablation.** The paper uses "tokens with the highest accumulated attention scores" as event representatives (Section 3.4), following InfLLM's protocol. But EM-LLM's events are semantically coherent—unlike InfLLM's fixed blocks—so the optimal representative selection strategy might differ. Could a single centroid key vector (averaged over the event) work better than the highest-attention tokens? Does the number of representative tokens per event matter? These questions are unexplored.

**3. Layer-wise retrieval is claimed as a key advantage but never directly ablated.** The paper argues that different layers retrieving different events is a major advantage over RAG (Section 4.1, Appendix A.2), and provides Figure 5 as evidence. But Figure 5 is a **single example**—not a systematic study. The paper never reports what happens if retrieval is restricted to a single layer or if all layers share the same retrieved events. Without this ablation, the claimed advantage of layer-wise retrieval remains an intriguing hypothesis rather than an empirically validated mechanism.

**4. The comparison to InfLLM does not control for the representative token count or retrieval budget.** EM-LLM and InfLLM use the same context window structure (initial + retrieved + local), but EM-LLM's events are variable-sized while InfLLM's are fixed. If EM-LLM's events are smaller (more granular segmentation), then for the same token budget $k$, EM-LLM retrieves more events than InfLLM—potentially gaining an advantage from accessing more distinct parts of the context rather than from better segmentation per se. The paper does not report average event sizes or control for this.

**5. The ∞-Bench results are incomplete and inconsistent.** ∞-Bench results are reported for only three of five base LLMs (Mistral, LLaMA-3, LLaMA-3.1) in Tables 3–5, while Phi-3 and Phi-3.5 are evaluated only on LongBench (Tables 6–7). No explanation is given. Furthermore, the ∞-Bench average for LLaMA-3 (Table 4) shows EM-LLM *underperforming* InfLLM (48.90% vs. 50.31%), driven by catastrophic drops on Retrieve.KV and Math.Find. The paper does not discuss these failures or hypothesize why LLaMA-3 behaves differently from LLaMA-3.1 on these tasks.

**6. The human correlation study uses three short podcasts—the finding may not scale.** The paper's bridge to cognitive science rests heavily on the correlation between LLM surprise and human event perception (Figure 4). This is a compelling result but is based on a dataset of three 7–30 minute narratives. The paper does not test whether the correlation holds for written text (all benchmarks are text; the human data is from audio transcripts), for longer documents, or for non-narrative content (code, QA, technical reports). Extrapolating from three short podcasts to claims about "parallels between this artificial system and its biological counterpart" (Abstract) overstates the evidence.

**7. No comparison with simple sliding-window attention as a baseline.** A natural baseline for long-context processing is to use only the most recent $W$ tokens (sliding window), discarding all earlier context. This would match the local context component of EM-LLM's architecture but without any retrieval. How much does episodic retrieval add over simply having a large local window? The paper never reports this ablation.

#### Experiments That Would Have Strengthened the Paper

- **Varying chunk size $m$** and measuring the impact on segmentation quality metrics (Table 2) and downstream performance, to characterize the refinement-quality-vs-cost tradeoff.
- **Ablating representative token selection**: comparing top-attention tokens vs. mean-pooled key vectors vs. random token selection, to determine whether InfLLM's strategy is optimal for event-based memory.
- **Single-layer vs. all-layer retrieval**: forcing all layers to share the same retrieved events (as RAG does) vs. allowing per-layer retrieval, to measure the contribution of layer-wise diversity.
- **Retrieval budget scaling**: reporting performance as a function of $k$ (number of retrieved events) to characterize the retrieval-efficiency curve, rather than fixing $k$ to match InfLLM.
- **Position-controlled passkey retrieval**: placing the passkey at multiple positions (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% of the sequence) for the 10M-token experiment, to test whether EM-LLM suffers from the "lost in the middle" problem that affects full-context models.
- **Ablating the initial 128 attention-sink tokens**: measuring whether their presence matters for EM-LLM specifically, or whether the episodic retrieval mechanism makes them redundant.
- **Cross-domain human correlation**: testing surprise-human correlation on written text (not just audio transcripts), on non-narrative domains, and on longer sequences, to assess whether the cognitive alignment generalizes beyond the three-podcast setup.
- **Training a parametric boundary detector**: comparing the surprise-based segmentation to a lightweight classifier trained to predict event boundaries from hidden states, to determine whether learnable segmentation could improve upon the zero-cost surprise signal.

#### Conditional Nature of the Claims

The paper's central claim—that EM-LLM "consistently outperform[s] the state-of-the-art retrieval model InfLLM"—holds unambiguously for **retrieval tasks and multi-document QA tasks** across most model configurations, with improvements of 10–40% relative. It holds more weakly for **summarization and few-shot learning**, where improvements are small (<3 pp) and sometimes negative. It does **not** hold for **LLaMA-3-8B on ∞-Bench**, where EM-LLM underperforms InfLLM (−1.41 pp average), nor for certain code tasks where EM-LLM degrades performance (RepoBench-P on LLaMA-3, −3.44%; LCC on Phi-3.5, −3.20%).

The claim that EM-LLM "surpasses full-context models in most tasks" (Abstract) is supported for LongBench (+12.28 pp) but essentially tied for ∞-Bench (+0.33 pp). The LongBench advantage may be partly attributable to positional encoding extrapolation failure in the full-context model (sequences average ~12K tokens but the base LLM may not handle this natively), rather than purely to EM-LLM's retrieval mechanism.

The claim of "parallels between this artificial system and its biological counterpart" (Abstract) is supported by the three-podcast correlation study but should be interpreted as **suggestive rather than conclusive**. The paper demonstrates that LLM surprise correlates with human event segmentation in a specific, small-domain setting, not that EM-LLM's full pipeline (segmentation + refinement + retrieval) operates analogously to human episodic memory in general.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cost of Difficulty Estimation Is Unaccounted For in Performance Comparisons

**The assumption or constraint.** EM-LLM's surprise-based event segmentation and graph-theoretic refinement operate on the fly during inference, requiring no external supervision or pre-processing. However, the downstream performance comparisons in Tables 1 and 3–7 are reported **per chunk processed** without accounting for the additional wall-clock time and computation that refinement introduces relative to the InfLLM baseline. The paper is transparent about this cost in Appendix C.3.1 (Table 10): processing a single 512-token chunk with Mistral-7B takes 1.40 seconds for InfLLM, 1.57 seconds for EM-LLM with surprise-only segmentation (S or S+C variants, a 12% increase), and 2.27 seconds for EM-LLM with modularity refinement (SM or SM+C variants, a 62% increase). In Appendix C.1, the paper formalizes this cost as $O(nm)$ for computing the key-similarity adjacency matrix across chunks of size $m$, plus $O(n^2/k)$ for the per-chunk boundary refinement over $k$ events. The paper explicitly acknowledges that this refinement cost "increases steadily as sequence length increases, due to the increasing number of representative tokens involved in the k-NN calculation" (Appendix C.3.1).

**The consequence.** The headline efficiency claims—particularly that EM-LLM "consistently outperform[s] the state-of-the-art retrieval model InfLLM"—are reported in terms of **accuracy at matched retrieval budgets**, not accuracy at matched wall-clock time or total FLOPs. A practitioner selecting between InfLLM and EM-LLM for a latency-sensitive deployment would face a concrete tradeoff: the SM variant provides better accuracy (best in 60% of tasks, per Section 4.4) but takes 62% longer per chunk. If the deployment budget is fixed in wall-clock time rather than in token count, InfLLM could process 1.62× more chunks in the same time—or the practitioner could run InfLLM with a larger retrieval budget ($k$) to potentially close the accuracy gap. The paper never reports an accuracy-vs-wall-clock comparison, making it impossible to assess whether the accuracy gains justify the time cost for time-constrained applications.

A second consequence concerns the scalability narrative. Appendix C.2 (Figures 9–10) and the complexity analysis in Appendix C.1 demonstrate that EM-LLM's attention computation scales linearly with sequence length $n$, while full-context attention scales quadratically. But the $O(nm)$ refinement cost—where $m$ is the chunk size—means the *total* computational cost grows with $n$ even though the attention cost per chunk is bounded. For very long sequences (the 10M-token regime the paper highlights), the refinement cost over thousands of chunks could become substantial, potentially eroding the linear-scaling advantage. The paper does not report wall-clock time measurements for sequences beyond the initial 51.2K tokens used in the Table 10 measurement.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 10 (Appendix C.3.1) provides the only wall-clock time comparison, measured over the first 100 chunks (51.2K tokens) of a long sequence. The complexity analysis (Appendix C.1) provides asymptotic bounds but no empirical timing curves as a function of $n$. The paper does not report total end-to-end inference time for any LongBench or ∞-Bench example, nor does it provide accuracy-vs-time curves that would allow a practitioner to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of refinement.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges the cost by reporting it (Table 10) and by noting that the S variant (surprise only, no refinement) adds only 12% overhead while achieving the best performance on 44% of tasks (Section 4.4). This suggests a practical compromise: use surprise-only segmentation (S) when latency matters and refinement (SM) when accuracy matters. However, the paper does not provide guidance on when refinement's 62% cost is worth its accuracy benefit—the task-level results in Tables 3–7 show that SM is best in 60% of task configurations but S or S+C is best in the remaining 40%, yet no systematic analysis of which task types benefit from refinement is provided. The paper also does not explore algorithmic optimizations that could reduce refinement cost (e.g., computing the adjacency matrix at reduced precision, using only a subset of attention heads, or applying refinement only periodically rather than every chunk).

### The 10M-Token Passkey Retrieval Result Lacks Critical Experimental Detail

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports that EM-LLM "successfully perform[s] retrieval across 10 million tokens" (Abstract) and "achiev[es] 100% accuracy on the Passkey.Retrieval task with sequences up to 10.2M tokens" (Section 4.1). This result is presented as the primary evidence for EM-LLM's scalability to "practically infinite context lengths" (Abstract). However, the paper provides almost no methodological detail about how this experiment was conducted: the passkey placement within the 10M-token sequence, the retrieval budget (how many events were retrieved), the chunk size, the base LLM used, or whether the experiment used surprise-only or surprise+refinement segmentation.

**The consequence.** The passkey retrieval test is well-known to be sensitive to where the key is placed within the sequence—the "lost in the middle" problem (Liu et al., 2024a) shows that LLMs struggle to retrieve information placed in the middle of long contexts, even when they succeed at retrieving from the beginning or end. A 100% accuracy claim without reporting performance at multiple positions (e.g., beginning, 25%, 50%, 75%, end) is incomplete: if the passkey was placed near the beginning (where it falls within the initial attention-sink tokens or early episodic events that are frequently retrieved) or near the end (where it falls within the local context window), the result would not demonstrate the retrieval mechanism's effectiveness—it would demonstrate that the passkey was never truly "retrieved" at all. The passkey retrieval test is also sensitive to the retrieval budget: if a large fraction of the 10M-token sequence is retrieved (e.g., $k$ is set high enough that the passkey-containing event is almost always included), 100% accuracy may reflect generous retrieval rather than precise retrieval. Without reporting $k$ and the sequence length, the result cannot be interpreted.

Furthermore, the paper does not specify which base LLM was used for the 10M-token experiment. The Figure 1 bottom panel compares EM-LLM against context-extension methods (YaRN, LongLoRA, LongRoPE) that were evaluated on different model families, making it unclear whether EM-LLM's advantage reflects the retrieval architecture or differences in the underlying base model's capabilities.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 1 (bottom panel) plots EM-LLM as a point at 10M tokens with 100% retrieval performance, alongside baseline methods whose context windows range from 6K to 2048K tokens. The source of the baseline data is cited as Ding et al. (2024), but the specific EM-LLM configuration used is not reported. Section 4.1 states the result in a single sentence without methodological detail. The paper reports Retrieve.PassKey performance for shorter sequences in the ∞-Bench results (Tables 3–5), where all methods (InfLLM and all EM-LLM variants) achieve 100%—but these sequences are far shorter than 10M tokens.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the missing experimental detail, does not report passkey placement, does not vary the retrieval budget, and does not specify the model configuration used. The 10M-token result is effectively anecdotal—it demonstrates feasibility but provides no evidence about the reliability, positional robustness, or retrieval-efficiency characteristics of EM-LLM at extreme scales. A practitioner considering EM-LLM for million-token retrieval tasks cannot infer from the paper whether performance degrades for mid-sequence keys, how retrieval budget affects accuracy, or whether the result generalizes across base models.

### The Comparison with Full-Context Models Is Confounded by Positional Encoding Failures and Base Model Limitations

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 4.1 and Table 9 compare EM-LLM against a "full-context" baseline where the same base LLM (LLaMA-3.1-8B) processes all tokens in the input sequence with standard softmax attention. On LongBench, EM-LLM achieves 51.58% versus full-context's 39.30%—a +12.28 percentage point advantage that the paper attributes to EM-LLM's retrieval mechanism avoiding diluted attention. On ∞-Bench, the gap narrows to 66.66% versus 66.33%—essentially tied. The paper interprets this as evidence that "EM-LLM's hierarchical attention avoids the issue of diluted attention in large context windows that affects full-context models" (Section 4.1).

**The consequence.** The full-context baseline is confounded by a separate failure mode unrelated to EM-LLM's retrieval mechanism: **positional encoding extrapolation failure**. LLaMA-3.1-8B, like most LLMs, was trained on sequences of a specific maximum length. LongBench examples average ~12K tokens (with Mistral's tokenizer; Appendix D.3), and ∞-Bench examples average >100K tokens. If LLaMA-3.1-8B's native training length is, say, 8K tokens (the paper does not specify this), then sequences longer than 8K require positional encodings for positions the model has never seen during training. As the paper itself documents in Section 2.1, "Transformers struggle with extrapolating to contexts longer than their training window size" (Kazemnejad et al., 2024), and standard RoPE encodings do not generalize well to out-of-distribution positions. The full-context model's poor LongBench performance (39.30%) may therefore reflect positional encoding failure—the model cannot properly represent token positions in the extended sequence—rather than attention dilution. EM-LLM, by contrast, assigns a fixed position embedding to all retrieved tokens (Section 3.1), effectively sidestepping the extrapolation problem for retrieved context. The +12.28 pp advantage thus conflates two separate effects: (1) EM-LLM's retrieval mechanism avoiding diluted attention, and (2) EM-LLM's positional encoding strategy avoiding extrapolation failure. The paper cannot attribute the gain to retrieval quality alone.

On ∞-Bench, where the full-context baseline and EM-LLM are essentially tied (66.33% vs. 66.66%), a different confound may operate: if ∞-Bench's longer sequences ($>100$K tokens) cause even more severe positional encoding failure for the full-context model, then EM-LLM's tie with the full-context baseline—despite the baseline's positional encoding disadvantage—could indicate that EM-LLM's retrieval mechanism is not fully compensating for the information lost by not attending to the full context.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report LLaMA-3.1-8B's native training length, does not state how the full-context baseline was configured (e.g., whether any positional encoding extension method was applied), and does not ablate the positional encoding strategy independently of the retrieval mechanism. The full-context results in Table 9 are from an unspecified API, with no details about the underlying implementation (Appendix C.3 notes that "for the full-context results... we used an API"). The paper's own related work section (Section 2.1) provides extensive evidence that positional encoding extrapolation is a significant problem, yet it does not control for this when interpreting the full-context comparison.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this confound. A proper comparison would either (a) apply the same positional encoding strategy (e.g., RoPE scaling or position interpolation) to the full-context baseline to eliminate extrapolation failure as a factor, or (b) report the full-context baseline's performance at sequence lengths within the native training window (where positional encoding is not a problem) versus beyond it, to isolate the attention dilution effect. Without either control, the full-context comparison cannot be interpreted as evidence about retrieval versus dilution—it measures an unknown mixture of retrieval benefit and positional encoding robustness.

### Retrieval Budget and Event Size Are Not Controlled in the InfLLM Comparison, Creating a Potential Confound

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper compares EM-LLM against InfLLM by matching the **total token budget** in the context window (e.g., 4K local + 2K retrieved for Mistral, 4K+4K for LLaMA-3, 1K+3K for Phi-3). Both methods fill the retrieved portion of the window with memory units: InfLLM uses fixed-size blocks, while EM-LLM uses variable-size episodes determined by surprise-based segmentation. The paper does not report the average event size produced by EM-LLM's segmentation, nor does it control for the number of distinct memory units retrieved.

**The consequence.** If EM-LLM's surprise-based segmentation produces events that are, on average, **smaller** than InfLLM's fixed-size blocks, then for the same total retrieved token budget, EM-LLM would retrieve **more distinct events** than InfLLM—sampling from more locations in the original sequence. The performance advantage could then reflect broader coverage of the context (accessing information from more positions) rather than the quality of segmentation or the retrieval mechanism per se. Conversely, if EM-LLM's events are larger (less granular segmentation), it would retrieve fewer events, potentially missing relevant information that InfLLM's finer-grained fixed blocks would capture. The direction and magnitude of this confound cannot be assessed from the reported data.

This confound interacts with the task-dependent results in Tables 3–7. For retrieval tasks (PassageRetrieval, Retrieve.KV), where finding a specific piece of information anywhere in the context is the goal, retrieving more distinct events (if EM-LLM produces smaller ones) would be advantageous regardless of segmentation quality. For summarization tasks (GovReport, QMSum), where broad coverage across the document is needed, retrieving fewer, larger events might be sufficient, and the number of distinct events matters less. The paper's finding that EM-LLM's gains are largest on retrieval tasks (+16.6% average over InfLLM, Appendix E.2) and smallest on summarization tasks (<3% maximum relative improvement, Tables 3–7) is consistent with the hypothesis that event count (not just event quality) drives the gains, but the paper does not control for this.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not report average event sizes for any model or task configuration, does not report the number of distinct events retrieved per generation step, and does not ablate the retrieval budget in terms of event count versus token count. Figure 12 (Appendix D.1) plots mean event size as the x-axis for the γ-sensitivity ablation, showing that mean event size varies with γ (from ~10 to ~85 tokens per event for Mistral-7B on LongBench, depending on γ and task), but the relationship between event size and performance is not analyzed. The paper does not report the fixed-block size used for InfLLM in its experiments (the number 512 is mentioned for EM-LLM's chunk size in Appendix C.1, but InfLLM's block size is not specified).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper could have controlled for event count by either (a) using the same number of retrieved units for both methods (letting the token budgets differ) or (b) reporting the relationship between event size and performance to characterize the confound. Neither approach is taken. A practitioner cannot determine whether EM-LLM's advantage derives from segmentation quality, retrieval granularity, or an interaction between the two.

### The Method Has Only Been Demonstrated on Text with Clean Correctness Signals; Generalization to Open-Ended or Subjective Tasks Is Unvalidated

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use benchmarks with ground-truth answers that can be evaluated with exact-match accuracy (LongBench, ∞-Bench, PG-19 segmentation quality metrics). The surprise-based segmentation mechanism relies on next-token prediction error from an autoregressive language model, which is well-defined for any text generation task regardless of whether the output is "correct." However, the downstream evaluation of whether EM-LLM's retrieval and memory organization *improves generation quality* has only been tested on tasks where correctness is objectively measurable: question answering, key-value retrieval, summarization (evaluated with ROUGE-L), and code generation (evaluated with pass@1). The paper does not evaluate EM-LLM on open-ended generation tasks (dialogue, creative writing, long-form reasoning with subjective quality criteria) or on tasks where the information need is ambiguous or evolving.

**The consequence.** The paper's core claim—that organizing the KV cache into semantically coherent, surprise-delimited episodes improves an LLM's ability to use long contexts—may not hold for tasks where "relevant information" is more diffuse or subjective. In open-ended dialogue, for instance, effective long-context use might require the model to maintain a consistent persona, recall user preferences stated across many turns, or make subtle conversational references to much earlier exchanges. The "correct" information to retrieve is not a single passage that answers a query but a distributed set of facts, stylistic cues, and interactional norms. EM-LLM's similarity-based retrieval (k-NN on representative tokens) is optimized for finding passages that are topically similar to the current query—a strategy that works well for factoid QA and retrieval tasks but may miss the subtler, less topically aligned information needed for coherent open-ended generation.

A related concern is that the surprise-based segmentation mechanism may behave differently on non-narrative, non-expository text. The human correlation studies (Section 4.2) use podcast transcripts—spoken narratives with clear event structure. The PG-19 experiments (Table 2) use English books—also narrative text. Whether surprise-detected boundaries are meaningful for code files, legal contracts, mathematical proofs, or multi-turn chat logs (where topics shift abruptly and unpredictably) is untested. If segmentation quality degrades on these text types, the downstream retrieval and generation quality would also degrade, but the paper provides no evidence either way.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The LongBench and ∞-Bench evaluations cover a range of task types: single-document QA (narrative, technical), multi-document QA, summarization, few-shot learning, retrieval, and code. This is broader than many long-context papers, but all tasks have objective correctness criteria. The human correlation studies (Section 4.2) and PG-19 experiments (Section 4.3) use exclusively narrative/expository text. The paper does not evaluate EM-LLM on dialogue benchmarks (e.g., Multi-Session Chat, PersonaChat), long-form reasoning tasks (e.g., chain-of-thought over long contexts), or multi-modal contexts. The paper acknowledges in the Discussion (Section 5) that "integrating modality-specific buffers into EM-LLM might enhance performance on multi-modal tasks," but does not address the open-ended generation gap.

**Mitigation status.** Partially acknowledged through omission rather than explicit discussion. The paper's claims are appropriately scoped to the evaluated benchmarks in the results sections, but the Abstract and Conclusion make broader claims about enabling "virtually infinite context windows" and "continuous, personalised exchanges over extended periods" (Section 6) that imply applicability beyond the tested task types. A practitioner considering EM-LLM for dialogue systems or creative applications has no experimental evidence about whether the method helps, hinders, or is neutral for those use cases.

### The Contiguity Buffer's Benefits Are Task-Dependent and Sometimes Negative; the Paper Provides No Guidance for Per-Task Tuning

**The assumption or constraint.** EM-LLM's two-stage retrieval process includes a contiguity buffer of size $k_c$ that retrieves temporal neighbors of similarity-retrieved events. The ratio $k_r = k_c / k$ (where $k = k_s + k_c$ is the total retrieved event budget) controls the balance between similarity-driven and contiguity-driven retrieval. The paper sweeps $k_r \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7\}$ on LongBench with Mistral-7B (Appendix D.1, Figure 13) and finds a slight overall preference for $k_r = 0.3$, which is used in all subsequent experiments. The paper observes that "certain tasks appear to benefit more from either surprise-only, refinement, or contiguity" (Section 4.4) and that "where contiguity is not crucial, adding such a buffer to our context window also reduces the size of the similarity buffer, and therefore provides potentially less directly relevant events."

**The consequence.** The fixed $k_r = 0.3$ setting means that on tasks where temporal contiguity is important, EM-LLM may be under-utilizing the contiguity buffer and missing relevant temporal context; on tasks where contiguity is unimportant, EM-LLM is wasting 30% of its retrieval budget on temporally adjacent but semantically irrelevant events, reducing the effective similarity budget from $k$ to $0.7k$. The paper's own data support this: in Table 5 (LLaMA-3.1-8B on LongBench), the best variant for retrieval tasks (PassageRetrieval) is S (surprise only, no contiguity), achieving 99.50% versus SM+C's 97.50%. In Table 3 (Mistral-7B), the S+C variant (surprise + contiguity, no refinement) achieves the best PassageRetrieval at 84.92%. In Table 4 (LLaMA-3-8B), the S variant achieves 87.50% PassageRetrieval versus S+C's 86.00%. The pattern is clear: on the task where pure similarity-based retrieval should dominate (finding a specific passage), adding contiguity often *reduces* performance because the contiguity buffer crowds out relevant similarity-retrieved events.

Yet the paper also shows that contiguity helps on some tasks: the S+C variant is best in 44% of configurations (Section 4.4, Appendix A.1). A practitioner deploying EM-LLM faces a tuning burden: they must determine the optimal $k_r$ for their specific task and domain, but the paper provides no systematic guidance for doing so—no characterization of which task features predict contiguity's usefulness, no analysis of how performance varies with $k_r$ on individual tasks (only aggregate results are shown in Figure 13), and no recommendation for dynamic or per-example adjustment of $k_r$.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 13 (Appendix D.1) shows aggregate LongBench performance for three $k_r$ values across two $\gamma$ settings, with per-task curves in the subplots. The per-task curves reveal substantial variation: some tasks show clear preferences for specific $k_r$ values, while others are flat. Table 1 reports the "best single method in terms of overall performance for each ablation," meaning the reported numbers are cherry-picked per model—they do not reflect what a practitioner would achieve using a fixed $k_r$ without per-task tuning. The paper does not report performance when a single $k_r$ is used consistently across all tasks.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the task-dependence qualitatively (Section 4.4) but does not treat it as a limitation requiring mitigation. The suggestion that "similarity buffer is still the most crucial part of our approach" and that contiguity's value is reduced "when combined with refinement... due to the improved similarity of refined events, hence further reducing the need for contiguous events" (Section 4.4) is a hypothesis, not a validated finding—it is based on the observation that SM outperforms S+C in more configurations, but this could reflect many factors beyond the contiguity-similarity interaction. The paper does not propose a method for automatically tuning $k_r$ per task or per example, nor does it analyze whether a dynamic $k_r$ (e.g., starting with high similarity ratio and shifting toward contiguity if the model's generation suggests temporal reasoning is needed) could recover the benefits of both buffers without requiring manual tuning.
