# Mixture-of-Depths: Dynamically allocating compute in transformer-based language models

**ArXiv:** [2404.02258](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02258)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Mixture-of-Depths (MoD)**, a conditional computation method for transformer-based language models that learns to dynamically allocate FLOPs across sequence positions and model depth by capping the number of tokens that participate in self-attention and MLP computations at a given layer — routing the remaining tokens through a cheap residual connection instead.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Mixture-of-Depths (MoD)**, a conditional computation method for transformer-based language models that learns to dynamically allocate FLOPs across sequence positions and model depth by capping the number of tokens that participate in self-attention and MLP computations at a given layer — routing the remaining tokens through a cheap residual connection instead. Using expert-choice routing with a static top-k selection mechanism on isoFLOP comparisons of decoder-only models trained on language modeling data, MoD transformers achieve baseline performance with "upwards of 50% faster" step times during post-training sampling, while the optimal MoD variant actually improves upon the isoFLOP-optimal baseline by as much as 1.5% on the training objective for equivalent FLOPs and wall-clock time — establishing that transformers can reallocate saved compute into larger models that outperform standard architectures, only when learned routing identifies which tokens can safely skip computation.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Uniform Compute Expenditure Across All Tokens Is Inefficient

The central gap this paper addresses is a structural inefficiency in the standard transformer architecture: **every token in every sequence receives the identical amount of computation, regardless of how difficult that token is to predict or how important it is for future tokens**. In a vanilla transformer, the self-attention mechanism computes query-key interactions across all token pairs, and the subsequent MLP processes every token representation. This uniform allocation means that predicting the comma after "Hello" in a greeting costs the model the same number of FLOPs as predicting a rare entity name in a complex scientific passage — even though the former is almost trivially easy and the latter may require deep reasoning.

This is not merely an aesthetic concern. The paper's motivating insight is that transformers likely **over-spend compute on many tokens**, and that recovering even a fraction of this wasted computation could be redirected toward making models larger, training them longer, or serving them faster — all without degrading performance. The intuition is captured in the opening line:

> "Not all problems require the same amount of time or effort to solve. Analogously, in language modeling not all tokens and sequences require the same time or effort to accurately make a prediction. And yet, transformer models expend the same amount of compute per token in a forward pass."

This framing transforms what might appear to be a narrow architectural tweak into a question about fundamental resource allocation: **can a neural network learn, end-to-end from the language modeling objective, which tokens deserve computation and which do not?** If the answer is yes, then the uniform-compute assumption baked into every standard transformer is an unnecessary constraint, and relaxing it should yield models that are both faster and more capable for a given training budget.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Economics of Training and Serving

The importance of this problem is directly tied to the enormous costs of modern language model development and deployment. Two specific economic pressures make dynamic compute allocation urgent:

**First, training FLOPs are the primary constraint on model quality.** Scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022; Kaplan et al., 2020) have established that model performance improves predictably with total training compute. However, these laws assume a fixed relationship between parameters and FLOPs-per-token — in a standard transformer, each parameter contributes to the FLOP count of every forward pass. If some tokens can skip computation entirely, the FLOPs-per-parameter ratio decreases, meaning that for a given training FLOP budget, one can afford **more parameters** or **more training tokens** than would otherwise be possible. This directly shifts the isoFLOP curve, potentially enabling better models at the same cost.

**Second, inference latency and cost scale with per-token FLOPs.** In production deployments, the time and hardware cost to generate each token in autoregressive sampling is directly proportional to the FLOPs required per forward pass. A model that can skip computation on a substantial fraction of tokens — say, 50% or more — would generate responses with correspondingly lower latency and cost. This is particularly important for real-time applications (chatbots, coding assistants, translation) where users experience latency directly, and for large-scale serving where compute costs multiply across millions of queries.

The paper frames the potential upside concretely. They train MoD transformers that are "upwards of 50% faster to step during post-training sampling" while matching or exceeding baseline performance. In practical terms, this means a deployed model that serves twice as many queries per unit of hardware, or a training run that produces a better model in the same wall-clock time.

### Prior Approaches to Conditional Computation

The idea of conditional computation — expending compute only when needed — is not new. The paper acknowledges a substantial lineage of work dating back over a decade. Understanding where prior methods succeeded and where they struggled is essential to appreciating what MoD contributes.

#### Early Conceptual Work and the Hardware Mismatch Problem

The term "conditional computation" was introduced by Bengio (2013) and developed in several subsequent papers (Bengio et al., 2013, 2016; Cho and Bengio, 2014). The core idea was compelling: neural networks should learn to make discrete decisions about when and where to apply computation, potentially yielding massive efficiency gains by decoupling model capacity from per-example FLOPs. However, these early formulations encountered a fundamental obstacle: they tended to produce **dynamic computation graphs**. In a dynamic graph, the sequence of operations depends on the input data and the network's runtime decisions, meaning that tensor shapes and operation schedules cannot be determined ahead of time. Modern hardware accelerators (GPUs, TPUs) are designed around **static computation graphs** where all tensor dimensions and memory allocations are known before execution begins. This allows compilers to optimize memory access patterns, schedule operations for maximum throughput, and minimize kernel launch overhead. Dynamic graphs break these optimizations, leading to poor hardware utilization that can erase the theoretical FLOP savings.

The paper is explicit about this tension:

> "General formulations of this challenging problem may not work well with existing hardware constraints since they tend to introduce dynamic computation graphs. The most promising conditional computation methods may instead be those that are harmonious with our current hardware stack, which prioritizes static computation graphs, and known tensor sizes that are selected to maximize hardware utilization."

This is not merely an implementation detail — it is the central constraint that determines which conditional computation approaches are viable in practice. MoD is designed from the ground up to satisfy this constraint: by fixing $k$ (the number of tokens routed to self-attention and MLP) ahead of training, the computation graph and all tensor sizes remain static. Only the **identities** of the $k$ tokens change based on router decisions.

#### Early-Exiting Methods

A prominent family of conditional computation approaches applies **early exiting**: each token or sequence is processed by transformer layers sequentially, and at each layer a learned halting mechanism decides whether the token has been processed "enough" and can skip all remaining layers (Elbayad et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Schuster et al., 2022).

The paper identifies a specific limitation of early-exiting that MoD is designed to overcome. In early-exit methods, once a token exits, it **cannot re-enter** later layers. This means it cannot participate in self-attention computations in deeper blocks, which has two consequences: (1) the token cannot be updated based on representations formed in later layers, and (2) later tokens cannot attend to it. MoD takes a fundamentally different approach:

> "In MoD, unlike in early-exit methods, a token can skip middle layers, then be updated via self-attention with tokens that have gone through all the middle layers. We speculate that this might be a useful property."

This is a crucial architectural distinction. MoD allows a token to bypass a block's computation entirely (passing through a residual connection unchanged), but then participate in self-attention at a **later** block where it can be updated by attending to tokens that did undergo the intermediate computations. This "skip-then-rejoin" pattern has no analog in early-exiting methods, and it creates a richer space of possible compute allocation strategies: tokens can temporarily save compute while still benefiting from the deeper representations that other tokens build.

#### Universal Transformers and Adaptive Computation Time

Another line of work explored iterating transformer layers with shared weights for an adaptive number of steps per token, analogous to adaptive computation time in RNNs (Dehghani et al., 2018; Graves, 2016; Simoulin and Crabbé, 2021). In the Universal Transformer (Dehghani et al., 2018), each token position applies the same transformer block repeatedly until a halting mechanism triggers. This allows per-token computation depth to vary, but it fundamentally ties computation to **sequential iteration**: a token that needs more processing goes through the same block multiple times, and all tokens must go through at least one pass of each block.

The limitation relative to MoD is twofold. First, the sequential iteration makes parallelization difficult — you cannot process all tokens in a batch simultaneously if they require different numbers of iterations. Second, the computation graph is inherently dynamic since the number of iterations varies per token and per input. MoD avoids both problems by using a fixed-depth architecture where the **number of blocks** each token encounters is predetermined (by the router's top-$k$ decisions across layers), but the computation is still executed as a single forward pass through the static graph.

#### Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) as a Reference Point

The paper positions MoD as building directly on the routing machinery developed for Mixture-of-Experts transformers (Shazeer et al., 2017; Fedus et al., 2022; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Zoph et al., 2022), but with a crucial conceptual shift. In MoE, tokens are routed to one of several **expert MLPs**, and the total FLOPs remain approximately constant because the experts collectively have the same capacity as a single large MLP. The routing decision determines **which parameters** process each token, not **whether** the token gets processed at all.

MoD takes the same routing logic but applies it to a different end:

> "Our mixture-of-depths method can be thought of as using the routing logic from MoE transformers, but rather than having multiple experts, MoD deploys a single expert which can be dynamically skipped."

This reframing is deceptively simple but has profound implications. By routing tokens to either a standard block computation or a null operation (the residual connection), MoD **reduces total FLOPs** in a way that MoE does not. The router's job becomes identifying which tokens can safely skip computation, rather than which expert is best suited to process them. The total compute budget is set by the capacity $k$ — if $k$ tokens per sequence are allowed into the block and $S > k$ is the total sequence length, then $S - k$ tokens save FLOPs by bypassing both self-attention and the MLP.

This distinction is important because MoE and MoD address complementary inefficiencies: MoE addresses the **parameter inefficiency** of large MLPs (not all parameters are needed for every token), while MoD addresses the **compute inefficiency** of processing every token through every block (not all tokens need every block's computation). The paper later shows that these can be combined (as MoDE models, Section 4.3), suggesting that the two forms of routing capture independent sources of waste in standard transformers.

#### CoLT5: The Closest Prior Work

The paper identifies CoLT5 (Ainslie et al., 2023) as the method most similar to MoD. CoLT5 uses conditional routing to select whether a given token passes through a heavy (full-capacity) or light (reduced-capacity) pathway for each feedforward layer, and similarly routes tokens to attend to either all other tokens or a select subset.

Several aspects of CoLT5 prefigure MoD. Both use soft top-$k$ for routing decisions. Both allow tokens to skip expensive computations in favor of cheaper alternatives. Both operate on a static computation graph with predetermined capacities.

However, the paper positions MoD as addressing a specific gap in CoLT5's scope:

> "CoLT5 focuses on a encoder-decoder setting, and thus does need to contend with the problem of efficient sequential decoding given the non-causal nature of the top-k operation. In contrast, our current work with MoD focuses on the decoder-only setting, and so we propose a predictive router to enable efficient inference for conditional computation in transformers."

This is a critical practical distinction. In encoder-decoder architectures, the encoder processes the full input sequence bidirectionally, so top-$k$ routing across the entire sequence is natural — future tokens in the input are available. In decoder-only autoregressive models, however, each token can only attend to previous tokens, and top-$k$ selection requires knowing the router weights of all tokens in the sequence, including future ones. This creates a fundamental tension for autoregressive sampling: during training, top-$k$ can operate across the full sequence, but during inference, future router weights are unavailable. CoLT5 sidesteps this by operating in the encoder-decoder paradigm; MoD must solve it for the decoder-only setting that dominates modern LLMs (GPT, PaLM, LLaMA, etc.).

The paper's solution — training an auxiliary predictor to mimic the top-$k$ decisions causally — is therefore not just an implementation trick but a necessary innovation for making conditional computation practical in autoregressive language models.

#### Stochastic Depth and Token Dropping as Baselines

The paper also considers simpler non-learned approaches to conditional computation, positioning them as important baselines that demonstrate the necessity of learned routing. One could randomly drop tokens from computations (analogous to stochastic depth for layers, Huang et al., 2016) or reduce expert capacity in MoE models and rely on token dropping for load balancing.

The experimental results bear out why these are insufficient. MoD transformers that use stochastic routing (top-$k$ on router weights sampled from a Gaussian distribution, rather than learned) "perform drastically worse than both the baseline and normal MoD transformer" (Figure 3). Similarly, simply reducing expert capacity in MoE models (forcing some tokens to be dropped) underperforms the integrated MoDE approach where tokens explicitly learn to choose the residual path. This confirms that **the network must learn which tokens to skip** — random or incidental dropping destroys information the model needs, while learned routing preserves it by selectively skipping only computationally redundant tokens.

### Contradictory Pressures in Conditional Computation Design

One of the paper's key insights — though not explicitly stated as a single claim — emerges from the accumulated discussion of prior work: **conditional computation methods face a trilemma between (1) hardware efficiency (static graphs), (2) causal compatibility (autoregressive deployment), and (3) learned, context-sensitive allocation decisions.** Prior approaches typically satisfied two of these three at most:

- **Dynamic graph methods** (Universal Transformers, adaptive computation time) achieved learned allocation but sacrificed hardware efficiency and sometimes causal compatibility.
- **Early-exiting methods** achieved hardware efficiency (fixed graph, just with branching) but produced less flexible allocation patterns (tokens can exit but not re-enter).
- **CoLT5** achieved learned allocation and hardware efficiency, but only in the non-causal encoder-decoder setting.
- **Stochastic methods** achieved hardware efficiency and causal compatibility but lacked learned, context-sensitive allocation.

MoD's design can be understood as an attempt to satisfy all three simultaneously: the fixed capacity $k$ ensures a static computation graph, the auxiliary predictor enables causal autoregressive deployment, and the learned router (trained by the language modeling gradient flowing through the router weights multiplied onto the block output) provides context-sensitive allocation. Whether it succeeds is an empirical question, but the design space is clearly motivated by the failures of prior work along these three axes.

### How MoD Positions Itself: A Tool for Tuning the FLOPs-Per-Parameter Ratio

The paper's explicit framing is that MoD is "another tool one can use to tune a model's compute per forward pass (and hence inference time)" (Section 5). This positions MoD not as a replacement for existing efficiency techniques (MoE, distillation, quantization, etc.) but as an orthogonal axis of optimization that can be combined with them. The MoDE experiments (Section 4.3) demonstrate this directly — MoD routing can be integrated with MoE routing to yield models that are both more parameter-efficient (via expert sparsity) and more compute-efficient (via depth sparsity).

Crucially, the paper emphasizes that the FLOP savings from MoD are fungible: they can be reinvested into the model by adding parameters (making the model deeper or wider) while maintaining the same total training FLOP budget. This is why the isoFLOP-optimal MoD variant is actually **larger** than the isoFLOP-optimal baseline — the per-token FLOP savings from routing tokens around blocks are redirected into additional transformer layers that process the tokens that are routed through. The paper frames this reinvestment as the primary mechanism by which MoD achieves performance improvements: it is not that skipping computation is inherently beneficial, but rather that it frees up a compute budget that can be spent more productively elsewhere.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation

This paper presents a method for modifying the transformer architecture so that **the model itself learns which tokens in a sequence need expensive computation (self-attention and MLP) and which can skip it**, routing tokens through either a standard transformer block or a cheap residual connection at each layer. The system solves the problem of uniform compute expenditure across tokens by using a learned router that selects a fixed number of tokens per layer to receive full computation, while the rest bypass the block unchanged — thereby reducing total FLOPs per forward pass without introducing dynamic computation graphs or sacrificing the ability to train on existing hardware.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The MoD transformer has four interconnected components:

1. **Base transformer blocks** — standard decoder-only transformer layers comprising multi-head self-attention followed by an MLP, identical to those in a vanilla transformer. These are the "expensive" computation path.

2. **Residual connections** — the "cheap" path: tokens that are not selected for a block simply pass through unchanged, with their output equal to their input. This path costs negligible FLOPs.

3. **Per-block routers** — small learned functions (linear projections) that take each token embedding as input and produce a scalar weight expressing the router's preference for that token to participate in the block's computations. These are trained end-to-end by the language modeling objective.

4. **Top-$$k$$ selection mechanism** — a static operation that, given the router weights for all tokens in a sequence, selects the $$k$$ tokens with the highest weights to route through the block. The parameter $$k$$ is fixed before training, which keeps the computation graph and all tensor sizes static throughout.

**Information flow.** A sequence of token embeddings enters a layer. The router computes a scalar weight for each token via a linear projection. The top-$$k$$ weights are identified, and those $$k$$ tokens are routed through self-attention and the subsequent MLP (where self-attention operates only on these $$k$$ tokens as both queries and keys/values), with their router weights multiplied onto the block output. The remaining $$S - k$$ tokens bypass the block entirely, passing through a residual connection. The outputs of both paths are then interleaved back into the original sequence order and passed to the next layer. At layers designated as "full-attention" layers (every other layer in the optimal configuration), all $$S$$ tokens participate normally with no routing. The system alternates between routing blocks and full-attention blocks throughout the model depth.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the compute budget definition (capacity $$C$$) — the core mechanism that enforces FLOP savings and enables static computation graphs, and how it relates to total sequence length $$S$$.

- **Second**, the routing schemes considered (token-choice vs. expert-choice) — why expert-choice was selected, how top-$$k$$ produces mutually exclusive routing with perfect load balancing, and the specific formulation for a two-path setting.

- **Third**, the router implementation — the exact equation governing a token's output at a layer, the linear projection that produces router weights, and the critical design choice of multiplying router weights onto the block output (placing them on the gradient path).

- **Fourth**, the autoregressive sampling problem — why non-causal top-$$k$$ fails at inference, the two solutions (auxiliary BCE loss and auxiliary predictor MLP), and their empirical trade-offs.

- **Fifth**, training methodology — hyperparameters, the alternating routing pattern (every other block), and how the isoFLOP analysis was conducted across model scales.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily an **architectural design and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that learned, top-$$k$$-based routing can dynamically allocate a fixed compute budget across tokens and layers, reducing FLOPs per forward pass while maintaining or improving performance relative to isoFLOP baselines.

---

#### Defining a static compute budget via capacity

The fundamental mechanism that enforces compute savings in MoD is the concept of **capacity**: the total number of tokens that comprise the input to a given computation. In a vanilla transformer block, the self-attention and MLP each have a capacity of $$T$$ — the total number of tokens across the sequence and batch dimensions. Every token participates in every computation.

MoD reduces the capacity below $$T$$ for certain blocks:

> "We can achieve our goal of using a smaller compute budget per forward pass compared to a vanilla transformer by lowering the capacity of the computations."

Let $$S$$ be the sequence length and $$C$$ be the user-defined capacity per batch element, where $$C < S$$ is an integer. At a routing block, only $$C$$ tokens will be processed by self-attention and the subsequent MLP. The remaining $$S - C$$ tokens route around the block entirely.

**Why capacity rather than dynamic decisions determines FLOPs.** The paper emphasizes a critical implementation detail: in static-graph implementations (required for hardware efficiency), computations are padded to their capacity regardless of how many tokens actually route to them. This means the FLOP count is determined by the **declared capacity**, not by the outcome of routing decisions. Specifically, if self-attention operates on $$C$$ tokens, the query-times-key matrix multiplication has dimension $$C \times C$$, requiring $$C^2$$ FLOPs (scaled by the head dimension). In contrast, a vanilla transformer with sequence length $$S$$ requires $$S^2$$ FLOPs for the same operation. If $$C = S/2$$, then the attention FLOPs drop to $$(S/2)^2 / S^2 = 1/4$$ of the vanilla cost — a 75% reduction. Similar proportional savings apply to the MLP.

The paper states:

> "For example, if we were to set a block's capacity to $$T/2$$ (i.e., half the number of tokens as would be the case in a vanilla transformer) then query-times-key matrix multiplication during self-attention becomes 25% as FLOP-intensive as in a vanilla transformer ($$(T/2)^2$$ vs. $$T^2$$)."

This static capacity design is what makes MoD "harmonious with our current hardware stack" (Section 1). Because $$C$$ is fixed before training, the compiler knows all tensor dimensions ahead of time and can optimize memory access patterns and operation scheduling. The router's job is to determine **which** $$C$$ tokens fill the capacity, not **whether** the capacity should be larger or smaller.

The paper hypothesizes that if the network learns to choose the right tokens to fill the capacity — specifically, those tokens that genuinely benefit from the block's computation — then performance can be preserved despite the FLOP reduction:

> "We hypothesize that certain tokens might not require as much processing as others, and these tokens can be identified through learning. Therefore, if the network learns to choose the right tokens to fill up its capacities, then it may preserve its performance."

The total compute budget per forward pass is thus determined by two hyperparameters: the capacity $$C$$ (or equivalently, the fraction $$C/S$$) and the **routing frequency** (how many layers use reduced capacity vs. full capacity). The paper explores capacities ranging from 12.5% to 95% of the total sequence, finding that 12.5% is optimal, and routing frequencies of every block vs. every other block, finding that every other block is optimal.

#### Routing schemes: token-choice vs. expert-choice

The paper considers two learned routing paradigms, both inherited from the Mixture-of-Experts literature, and selects expert-choice routing after analyzing their relative strengths for the specific use case of routing between computation and a null operation.

**Token-choice routing.** In token-choice routing, each token independently selects its preferred computational path. A router produces per-token probability distributions across the available paths (in MoD's case, two paths: the block computation and the residual connection). Each token is then shuttled to the path it assigns the highest probability.

The paper identifies a specific problem with this approach: load balancing. There is no guarantee that tokens will distribute themselves evenly across paths. All tokens might converge to the same path (e.g., all tokens preferring the block computation), which would defeat the purpose of compute reduction. To prevent this, token-choice routing typically requires an **auxiliary balancing loss** that penalizes uneven distribution.

More subtly, token-choice routing can **under-process** or **over-process** tokens when the capacity of a path is exceeded. If more tokens select a path than its capacity allows, some tokens must be dropped from that path — but the identity of dropped tokens depends on implementation details (e.g., priority based on sequence position or batch order) rather than on a learned assessment of which tokens are least important.

**Expert-choice routing.** In expert-choice routing, the direction of the choice is reversed: each **computational path** selects the top-$$k$$ tokens based on the tokens' router weights, rather than tokens choosing paths. Concretely, each token produces a scalar router weight (not a probability distribution). For a given path, the tokens with the top-$$k$$ highest router weights are selected to route through that path.

The paper identifies three key advantages of expert-choice routing for MoD's specific use case:

1. **Perfect load balancing without auxiliary losses.** Since exactly $$k$$ tokens are guaranteed to be selected per path, there is no need for a balancing loss to prevent all tokens from routing to one path. The $$k$$ is enforced directly by the top-$$k$$ operation.

2. **Relative routing weights enable prioritization.** Because the top-$$k$$ selection depends on the magnitude of the router weights, the router can ensure that critical tokens are among the top-$$k$$ by assigning them higher weights. The paper states:

   > "Since the top-$$k$$ operation depends on the magnitude of the router weights, this routing scheme allows for relative routing weights to help determine which tokens most need the block's computations; routers can try to ensure that the most critical tokens are among the top-$$k$$ by setting their weight appropriately, which is not possible with token-choice routing schemes."

   In token-choice routing, a token's probability matters but the **relative** magnitude across tokens does not — a token with probability 0.99 for a path is treated the same as one with probability 0.51 if both are above the threshold.

3. **Mutually exclusive sets from a single top-$$k$$ operation.** Because MoD routes tokens to only two paths — block computation or residual — a single top-$$k$$ operation efficiently splits the tokens into two disjoint sets: the $$k$$ tokens with the highest weights (routed to the block) and the remaining $$S - k$$ tokens (routed to the residual). This prevents both the over-processing problem (a token being selected by both paths) and the under-processing problem (a token being selected by neither), since the paths are complementary. The paper notes:

   > "Because we only route through two paths, a single top-$$k$$ operation can efficiently split the tokens into two mutually exclusive sets, one for each computational path, preventing the over- or under-processing problem mentioned above."

**Stochastic routing as a control.** To validate that learned routing specifically is necessary (not just any non-uniform allocation), the paper implements a stochastic routing baseline. Here, router weights are sampled from a Gaussian distribution rather than learned, and the same top-$$k$$ mechanism is applied. The results in Figure 3 show this performs "drastically worse than both the baseline and normal MoD transformer," confirming that the network must learn to identify which tokens can safely skip computation.

#### Router implementation and the output equation

The router is implemented as a simple linear projection that maps each token embedding to a scalar. Let $$X^l = \{x^l_i \mid i \text{ is an integer}, 1 \leq i \leq S\}$$ be the set of token embeddings in a sequence of length $$S$$ at layer $$l$$.

The router weight for token $$i$$ is:

$$r^l_i = w^T_\theta x^l_i$$

where $$w_\theta$$ is the learned weight vector of the linear projection and $$x^l_i$$ is the token embedding. This produces a single scalar per token per routing layer.

**The output equation.** The core computation of an MoD layer is defined by Equation 1 in the paper. Let $$R^l$$ be the set of all router weights $$\{r^l_1, r^l_2, ..., r^l_S\}$$. Let $$P_\beta(R^l)$$ be the $$\beta$$-th percentile of the set $$R^l$$, where $$\beta = 1 - C/S$$ and $$C$$ is the user-defined capacity per batch element (the number of tokens that will be processed by the block). Since $$C < S$$, we have $$\beta < 1$$, and the threshold $$P_\beta(R^l)$$ identifies the weight value that separates the top $$C$$ tokens from the rest.

The output for token $$i$$ at layer $$l+1$$ is:

$$x^{l+1}_i = \begin{cases} r^l_i \cdot f_i(\tilde{X}^l) + x^l_i, & \text{if } r^l_i > P_\beta(R^l) \\ x^l_i, & \text{if } r^l_i < P_\beta(R^l) \end{cases}$$

where $$\tilde{X}^l$$ is the set of tokens whose router values $$r^l_j > P_\beta(R^l)$$ — that is, the top-$$C$$ tokens — and $$f$$ comprises self-attention and the subsequent MLP.

**What this equation computes in operational terms.** For each token in the sequence, the router produces a scalar weight. The top $$C$$ tokens (those with weights above the $$\beta$$-th percentile) are selected to participate in the block. For these selected tokens:
- Self-attention is computed over the set $$\tilde{X}^l$$ (only the selected tokens serve as queries, keys, and values).
- The MLP processes the attention output.
- The result $$f_i(\tilde{X}^l)$$ is multiplied by the router weight $$r^l_i$$ — this is a scalar multiplication that gates the contribution of the block's output.
- The gated block output is added to the residual input $$x^l_i$$.

For tokens not selected (those with $$r^l_i < P_\beta(R^l)$$):
- Their output is simply their input: $$x^{l+1}_i = x^l_i$$.
- They undergo no self-attention computation and no MLP computation.

Note that the condition $$r^l_i > P_\beta(R^l)$$ (strict greater-than) and $$r^l_i < P_\beta(R^l)$$ (strict less-than) cover all tokens since ties at exactly the percentile boundary have measure zero in practice with floating-point weights. However, the implementation must handle the edge case where multiple tokens have identical router weights near the threshold — the paper doesn't detail the tie-breaking mechanism, but in practice any deterministic tie-breaking (e.g., by position) would work since the capacity is fixed.

**Why multiply the router weight onto the block output?** This design choice is critical and non-obvious. By multiplying $$r^l_i$$ (a learned scalar) by $$f_i(\tilde{X}^l)$$ (the block output), the router weight is placed **on the gradient path** — the language modeling loss gradient will flow through this multiplication and update $$w_\theta$$ (the router's parameters) during backpropagation. This enables the router to be trained end-to-end by the language modeling objective without any separate router-specific loss (during training, before the auxiliary losses for autoregressive sampling are added).

The paper explicitly notes:

> "Notably, we multiply the output of the function $$f$$ by the router weights. This puts the router weights along the 'gradient path', thus subjecting them to the forces of gradient descent through the course of the language modeling task."

This decision means the router learns to assign high weights to tokens whose processing by the block contributes most to reducing the language modeling loss, and low weights to tokens that can be safely skipped. The router is not trained by any explicit "importance" label — it discovers importance through the downstream effect of its selections on the training objective.

**What about tokens that bypass the block?** The paper experimented with also including router weights along the computational path for bypassed tokens (e.g., multiplying the residual by the router weight), but found it "seems to be sufficient — and implementationally simpler — to only include the router weights along the computational path for those tokens that do not bypass the block's computations." So bypassed tokens pass through unchanged, with no gradient flowing through them from the router mechanism.

**Self-attention scope.** A crucial consequence of this design is that self-attention at a routing layer operates only on the selected tokens $$\tilde{X}^l$$. This means:
- A token that bypasses the block is not used as a key or value for other tokens at this layer.
- However, that same bypassed token may be selected at a later layer and attend to tokens that were processed in the intermediate layers.
- Tokens that undergo the block computation at this layer can attend to each other but not to bypassed tokens (since bypassed tokens are not in $$\tilde{X}^l$$).

This creates the "skip-then-rejoin" pattern that distinguishes MoD from early-exiting methods. A token might bypass several consecutive routing layers, remaining entirely unchanged, then be selected at a deeper layer where it can attend to representations that other tokens built up during those intermediate computations.

#### The autoregressive sampling problem and its solutions

The top-$$k$$ operation is inherently **non-causal**: determining whether a given token's router weight is among the top-$$k$$ for the sequence requires knowledge of the router weights of **all** tokens in the sequence, including future tokens that come after the current position. During training, this is not a problem — the full sequence is available, and top-$$k$$ can be computed across all positions simultaneously. During autoregressive sampling, however, the model generates tokens one at a time and cannot know the router weights of future tokens that haven't been generated yet.

The paper is explicit about this difficulty:

> "While expert-choice routing has a number of advantages, it has one distinct problem: the top-$$k$$ operation is non-causal. This means that whether a given token's routing weight is among the top-$$k$$ for the sequence depends on the values of the routing weights for tokens that come after it, which we don't have access to when autoregressively sampling."

This is the same problem that prevents MoE models with top-$$k$$ routing from being directly deployed autoregressively, and it is why CoLT5 operates in an encoder-decoder setting where the full input sequence is available bidirectionally. For decoder-only models — the dominant paradigm for modern LLMs — this must be solved.

**Solution 1: Auxiliary binary cross-entropy loss.** The first method introduces a simple auxiliary loss during training that teaches the router to produce values that can be thresholded causally. The loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = -\left(y \log(\sigma(r)) + (1 - y) \log(1 - \sigma(r))\right)$$

where $$r$$ is the router's output scalar, $$\sigma(r)$$ is the sigmoid of that scalar, and $$y \in \{0, 1\}$$ is the target: 1 if the token was among the top-$$k$$ in the non-causal top-$$k$$ selection, and 0 otherwise.

**What this loss computes operationally.** During training, the full non-causal top-$$k$$ operation is performed to determine which tokens are selected ($$y=1$$) and which are not ($$y=0$$). The router's raw output $$r$$ is passed through a sigmoid to produce a probability between 0 and 1. The binary cross-entropy loss then penalizes the router when its sigmoid output diverges from the binary top-$$k$$ target. 

**Why this enables causal sampling.** The loss pressures the sigmoid of the router's output to center around 0.5, with selected tokens pushed above 0.5 and non-selected tokens pushed below 0.5. At inference time, a simple threshold at 0.5 can be used: tokens with $$\sigma(r) > 0.5$$ are routed through the block, and tokens with $$\sigma(r) < 0.5$$ are routed around it. This decision depends only on the current token's router weight, not on any future token — it is fully causal.

The paper notes that this auxiliary loss "empirically affects the primary language modeling objective by approximately 0.2 - 0.3%," meaning it introduces a small but measurable degradation in language modeling performance as a trade-off for enabling causal routing.

**Solution 2: Auxiliary predictor MLP.** The second method introduces a small auxiliary MLP (a secondary "router" or predictor) that receives the same inputs as the router but with a **stop gradient** applied to its input. This predictor is trained to output a binary prediction: will this token be among the top-$$k$$ or not? The target is the same binary label $$y$$ derived from the non-causal top-$$k$$ operation.

At inference time, the predictor's output determines routing decisions causally, while the original router's top-$$k$$ mechanism is disabled. The paper reports:

> "This method does not affect the language modeling objective, and empirically does not significantly impact the step speed."

The difference from Solution 1 is that the auxiliary task is offloaded to a separate network component (the predictor MLP) rather than imposed on the router itself. This preserves the router's ability to produce weights optimized purely for the language modeling objective, while the predictor handles the causal approximation task. The trade-off is additional parameters and a small computational overhead for the predictor.

**Why this task is easy.** The paper provides evidence that predicting top-$$k$$ membership is an easy auxiliary task. The predictor "quickly achieves 99% accuracy" during training, and Figure 6 shows the prediction "is upwards of 97% accurate soon into training." This high accuracy means the causal approximation closely mimics the non-causal top-$$k$$ decisions, and the performance gap between causal and non-causal routing is small — Figure 6 confirms that "switching from the non-causal top-$$k$$ routing scheme in training to a causal predictor-based approach during auto-regressive sampling leads to minimal performance degradation."

The ease of this prediction task makes intuitive sense: whether a token's router weight will be among the top-$$k$$ depends largely on the token's own representation (its difficulty, information content, or role in the sequence), and the model appears to learn a representation from which this can be predicted accurately using only the token's own embedding.

**Which method is used in the reported results?** The paper primarily uses the predictor-based method (Solution 2) for the autoregressive evaluation results in Figure 6, since it does not degrade the language modeling objective. The auxiliary loss method (Solution 1) is presented as an alternative that trades a small LM performance degradation for implementation simplicity (no separate predictor network needed).

#### Training configuration and isoFLOP methodology

The paper uses a consistent set of hyperparameters across all experiments (Section 3.6 and Figure 3):

- **Optimization:** Not explicitly specified in detail for the optimizer, but the paper states all models use "the same basic hyperparameter configurations" including "cosine schedules equal to 1× the training steps" (a cosine learning rate schedule that decays to zero over the full training duration), "128 batch size," and "2048 sequence length."
- **Model scaling:** Models vary in "number of layers, heads, and embedding size to produce differently sized models during isoFLOP analyses."
- **Routing configuration:** The optimal configuration (determined by hyperparameter sweep at 6e18 FLOPs) is **12.5% capacity** (so $$C = 0.125 \times S = 256$$ tokens out of 2048 sequence length) applied to **every other block** (alternating routing blocks with full-attention blocks).

**The alternating routing pattern.** The finding that routing every other block is crucial deserves careful explanation. The paper reports:

> "While routing every other block was crucial for strong performance, we found that aggressive capacity reduction was best (gradual improvements were observed when reducing the capacity down to 12.5% of the total sequence, corresponding to 87.5% of tokens routing around blocks, with performance degrading beyond this point)."

This means the model architecture interleaves two types of layers:

1. **Full-attention layers:** All $$S = 2048$$ tokens participate in self-attention and the MLP normally — identical to a vanilla transformer block. Every token is used as query, key, and value.

2. **Routing layers:** Only $$C = 256$$ tokens (12.5% of the sequence) participate in self-attention and the MLP, selected by the learned router. The remaining 1792 tokens bypass the block.

The pattern is: full-attention → routing → full-attention → routing → ... (or vice versa, starting with a routing layer depending on the specific architecture). This alternating design means that even tokens that route around a block can immediately participate in full-attention at the next layer, allowing them to be updated by attending to the representations of tokens that did undergo the intermediate computation.

**Why alternating is crucial.** The paper offers a clear rationale: "it seems the network is robust to significant capacity reductions as long as there is frequent opportunity for full capacity self-attention and MLP computations." If every layer were a routing layer with 12.5% capacity, a token that was never selected would effectively disappear from the network — it could never serve as a key or value for other tokens, and it could never attend to other tokens' updated representations. The full-attention layers provide a periodic "reset" where all tokens synchronize their representations through full self-attention.

**IsoFLOP analysis methodology.** The paper conducts an isoFLOP analysis at three total training FLOP budgets: 6e18, 2e19, and 1e20 FLOPs. For each budget, multiple model sizes are trained (ranging from 60M to 3B parameters), and the loss is measured on held-out data. The optimal model is the one that achieves the lowest loss at that FLOP budget.

Critically, MoD changes the relationship between model size (parameters) and FLOPs per forward pass. A 220M parameter MoD model with 12.5% capacity routing every other block uses fewer FLOPs per forward pass than a 220M parameter vanilla transformer because approximately half the layers process only 12.5% of the tokens. This means that for a given training FLOP budget, an MoD model can afford to be **larger** (more parameters) than the baseline because its per-token FLOPs are lower.

The paper formalizes this:

> "A fortunate consequence of this effect is that there exist smaller MoD models that, while they are not themselves isoFLOP optimal for their hyperparameter setting, are nevertheless as- or better-performing than the optimal baseline model while being faster to step."

**Normalized FLOPs per forward pass.** Figure 4 uses "relative FLOPs per forward pass" normalized to the isoFLOP-optimal baseline. This metric captures the total FLOPs a model uses in one forward pass relative to the baseline model. Values less than 1.0 mean the MoD model uses fewer FLOPs per forward pass. The paper notes that this metric is "tightly correlated" with wall-clock step time, so faster step times correspond to lower relative FLOPs per forward pass.

**Key empirical finding for model sizing.** The paper reports a predictive rule for finding the optimal MoD configuration:

> "The optimal MoD transformer is that which uses as many FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP optimal baseline. This finding allows one to directly predict which sized MoD transformer will perform optimally for a given isoFLOP training budget: one just needs to tune the model size for a given MoD configuration (i.e., capacity and routing frequency) to produce a model that uses as many FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP-optimal baseline, and they will have the optimally performing MoD variant for that configuration."

In other words, the FLOP savings from routing are best **reinvested** into the model by adding parameters until the per-forward-pass FLOP count matches that of the baseline. This produces a model that is larger (more parameters) but uses the same FLOPs per forward pass — and therefore trains in approximately the same wall-clock time — while achieving lower loss.

**Adding depth vs. width.** The paper states: "Empirically, we find that it is better to add depth than to add width when adding FLOPs to the model." This means the reinvested FLOPs are used to add more transformer layers rather than to increase the hidden dimension or number of attention heads. Deeper models benefit more from the MoD routing pattern because tokens that skip intermediate layers can still participate in deeper layers via the alternating full-attention blocks.

**Learned routing vs. stochastic routing.** The paper explicitly compares learned routing against a stochastic baseline where router weights are sampled from a Gaussian distribution (mean and variance not specified) and the same top-$$k$$ mechanism is applied. This baseline is analogous to stochastic depth or block dropout, but applied at the token level rather than the layer level. The result (Figure 3): "MoD transformers that use stochastic routing ... perform drastically worse than both the baseline and normal MoD transformer." This confirms that the performance benefit comes from **learned, context-sensitive** routing decisions, not merely from any form of non-uniform compute allocation.

**MoDE: Combining MoD with MoE.** Section 4.3 describes two ways to integrate MoD routing with Mixture-of-Experts:

1. **Staged MoDE:** MoD routing is applied first (before self-attention), deciding which tokens will participate in the block at all. Then, within the block, MoE routing is applied to the MLP, routing the selected tokens to different experts. This allows tokens to skip self-attention entirely.

2. **Integrated MoDE:** A single routing operation funnels tokens to either one of the expert MLPs or to a "no-op" expert (the residual path). This simplifies the routing machinery by having one router handle both the "which tokens skip computation" and "which expert processes each non-skipping token" decisions simultaneously.

The paper reports that integrated MoDE "was distinctly better than simply reducing the capacity of experts in conventional MoE models, and relying on token dropping to implement residual routing." The explanation offered:

> "We believe this is because with the integrated MoDE machinery, tokens explicitly learn to choose the residual path around the experts, as opposed to preferring an expert but being dropped when implemented as a capacity reduction."

This is an important finding: when capacity is simply reduced in an MoE model, tokens that are dropped are those that happen to be lower priority in the implementation's drop logic (e.g., later in the sequence), not those that the model has learned can safely skip computation. The integrated MoDE router gives the model agency over which tokens bypass experts, leading to better allocation.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Conditional Computation as a Token-Level Routing Problem on a Static Compute Budget

The dominant paradigm for conditional computation in transformers has been to make the **amount** of computation adaptive — let the network decide, per token or per sequence, how many layers to execute (early-exiting, Universal Transformers) or how many iterations to perform (adaptive computation time). These approaches inherit a fundamental tension: variable computation depth produces dynamic computation graphs, which break the compiler optimizations that make modern hardware efficient. The field has largely accepted this as an unavoidable trade-off: you can have adaptive compute allocation, or you can have hardware-efficient static graphs, but not both.

MoD's conceptual move is to flip the problem from "how much computation?" to "**which tokens?**" The total computation — the FLOP budget per forward pass — is fixed before training by capping the number of tokens that can enter each block at a predetermined capacity k. What is adaptive, learned, and context-sensitive is the **identity** of the k tokens selected. This is not a minor reframing; it dissolves the static-vs-dynamic graph tension entirely. Since k never changes, every tensor shape, memory allocation, and operation schedule is known at compile time. The only thing that varies is which tokens populate those static tensors.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond the immediate empirical gains. It identifies a previously overlooked degree of freedom in transformer design: the FLOPs-per-parameter ratio. In a standard transformer, every parameter contributes FLOPs on every token. MoD shows that this coupling can be loosened — by routing tokens around blocks, the same parameter count can consume fewer FLOPs (saving compute) or, equivalently, more parameters can be packed into the same FLOP budget (reinvesting saved compute into a larger model). The paper's finding that isoFLOP-optimal MoD transformers are systematically larger than isoFLOP-optimal baselines (Figure 4) is a direct consequence of this decoupling, and it suggests that the field's standard practice of parameter-FLOP coupling may be leaving performance on the table.

This is a fundamental shift in how to think about conditional computation, not an incremental refinement of existing approaches. Prior work asked: "Can we spend less compute on easy tokens?" MoD asks: "Given a fixed compute budget, can we learn to spend it only on tokens where it matters?" The difference is subtle but consequential — it converts conditional computation from a mechanism that produces unpredictable, hardware-unfriendly compute patterns into one that produces fully predictable, optimizable ones.

The evidence for why this reframing matters comes from the stochastic routing baseline (Figure 3), which uses exactly the same static compute budget and top-k mechanism as learned MoD, but with random token selection. Its drastically worse performance demonstrates that the static budget is necessary but insufficient — what makes the system work is not the budget itself but the **learned allocation** within it. The budget provides the constraint that enables hardware efficiency; the learned router provides the intelligence that makes the constraint non-damaging.

### Innovation 2: The Skip-Then-Rejoin Pattern as a New Capability in Token-Level Routing

Prior conditional computation methods that allow tokens to skip computation — specifically early-exiting approaches — treat the decision to skip as **terminal**. Once a token exits the transformer's depth, it cannot re-enter later layers, and future tokens cannot attend to it. This creates an irreversible information loss: the token's representation is frozen at the exit point, and all deeper contextual information is inaccessible to it and about it.

MoD's alternating routing pattern — full-attention layers interleaved with routing layers — enables a qualitatively different behavior that the paper calls out explicitly: a token can skip middle layers, then be updated via self-attention at a deeper full-attention layer by attending to tokens that did undergo those intermediate computations. This "skip-then-rejoin" pattern has no analog in prior conditional computation work.

The significance of this pattern is not that it improves metrics (though it does, since alternating routing is "crucial for strong performance") but that it reveals a more flexible model of what conditional computation can mean. Early-exiting methods imply a monotonic relationship between depth and computation: deeper processing = more computation, and the only question is when to stop. MoD implies a **non-monotonic** relationship: a token may skip expensive computation entirely at one depth, then benefit from the representations that other tokens built at those depths by attending to them later. Computation depth and representation quality are partially decoupled — a token that skips processing isn't condemned to a frozen, shallow representation; it can "catch up" through attention to tokens that went deeper.

This also has implications for how we understand self-attention's role in the transformer. In a vanilla transformer, every token serves as both a query (receiving information) and a key (providing information) at every layer. MoD's routing shows that these roles can be separated: a token can skip being a query at layer l (saving its own FLOPs) but still be available as a key at layer l+1 (contributing to other tokens' updates). The paper notes this explicitly in Section 5: "If a token does not participate in self-attention at a certain block, then later tokens will also not be able to attend to it." But the alternating full-attention blocks partially undo this constraint — a token unavailable as a key at a routing layer is available again at the next full-attention layer. The network thus faces a joint optimization problem: deciding which tokens to route through blocks affects both those tokens' own representations and other tokens' access to them.

This is a fundamental conceptual contribution, not just an architectural trick. It identifies a new axis — temporal availability as keys/queries — along which routing decisions propagate consequences through the computation graph, and it shows empirically (through the alternating pattern's necessity) that this axis matters for performance.

### Innovation 3: Routing as Compute Reinvestment Rather Than Compute Reduction

The natural intuition for conditional computation is that it saves compute — and indeed, MoD models can achieve "upwards of 50% faster" step times. But the paper's deeper finding, and the one that makes MoD more than just an efficiency technique, is that the primary performance benefit comes not from saving FLOPs but from **reinvesting them into a larger model**.

Figure 4 reveals a striking pattern: the isoFLOP-optimal MoD transformer at every budget level uses approximately the same FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP-optimal vanilla baseline. The MoD model is better (lower loss) not because it's cheaper to run, but because it's **bigger** — the per-token FLOP savings from routing are redirected into additional parameters (preferentially depth, per the paper's finding) until the per-forward-pass FLOP count matches the baseline. The model that wins is the one that spends exactly as much total compute as the baseline, but allocates it across more parameters by being selectively sparse about which tokens use each parameter.

This finding reframes the value proposition of conditional computation. Before MoD, the dominant story was: "conditional computation lets you run the same model cheaper." MoD adds: "conditional computation lets you run a **better** model at the **same** cost." The difference matters because it suggests that the ceiling on conditional computation's benefits is not determined by how many tokens can be skipped, but by how productively the saved FLOPs can be reinvested. If adding depth or width eventually saturates, MoD's advantage diminishes; if representational capacity continues to benefit from scale, MoD's advantage compounds.

This is an empirical finding, not a theoretical guarantee, but it has the flavor of a scaling insight: it suggests a new relationship between three quantities — parameters, FLOPs-per-token, and loss — where the optimal operating point is not at minimum FLOPs-per-token but at the point where saved FLOPs can be most productively reinvested. The paper's explicit statement that "one just needs to tune the model size for a given MoD configuration ... to produce a model that uses as many FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP-optimal baseline" operationalizes this as a practical recipe.

The concept is incremental in form (it builds on standard isoFLOP analysis methodology) but fundamental in implication: it establishes that compute efficiency and model quality are not in tension — under the right routing scheme, they can be jointly optimized by reallocating compute spatially (across parameters) rather than reducing it temporally (per forward pass).

### Innovation 4: The Non-Causal to Causal Routing Gap as a Solvable Auxiliary Problem

A persistent obstacle for conditional computation in autoregressive models has been the tension between training-time mechanisms that can leverage full-sequence information and inference-time constraints that require causal decisions. This problem appears in any method that uses top-k or similar global operations across tokens — including MoE with expert-choice routing — and has often been cited as a reason why such methods are impractical for decoder-only architectures.

The paper treats this not as a fundamental obstacle but as an **auxiliary prediction problem** of modest difficulty. The key empirical observation is that predicting whether a token will be among the top-k, given only the token's own representation, is easy: the auxiliary predictor achieves 97-99% accuracy soon into training (Figure 6), and switching from non-causal top-k to the causal predictor at inference causes "minimal performance degradation."

The significance of this finding is that it **lowers the perceived barrier** to using global routing operations in autoregressive models. If the gap between non-causal (training) and causal (inference) routing can be closed with a lightweight predictor trained on a simple auxiliary task, then the design space for conditional computation in decoder-only transformers expands considerably. Methods previously dismissed as "non-causal and therefore impractical for generation" become viable if their non-causal decisions can be predicted causally with high accuracy.

The paper provides two solutions with different trade-offs — the auxiliary BCE loss (simpler, but degrades LM objective by 0.2-0.3%) and the predictor MLP (no LM degradation, but additional parameters) — demonstrating that the problem has multiple viable resolutions. This is not a theoretical contribution but a practical one: it removes a specific, well-known obstacle that previously narrowed the scope of conditional computation research.

The innovation is incremental in form (auxiliary predictors are common in ML) but significant in effect because it addresses a bottleneck that had shaped the direction of an entire subfield. CoLT5 avoided this problem by operating in the encoder-decoder setting; MoD shows that the decoder-only setting — dominant in modern LLMs — is not fundamentally incompatible with top-k routing, provided one is willing to train a small predictive model to bridge the causal gap.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use a language modeling dataset of unspecified provenance — the paper does not name a specific corpus (e.g., The Pile, C4, or a proprietary dataset). The auto-regressive evaluation in Section 4.2 uses "exactly the same held-out data comprising 256000 sequences (500M tokens)," but the training data source is not disclosed. This omission matters for reproducibility but is common in industrial labs using internal data. The paper's focus is on the architectural mechanism rather than dataset-specific behavior.

- **Base model(s).** The baseline architecture is a standard decoder-only transformer — the paper does not identify a specific named model family (e.g., PaLM, LLaMA), but the hyperparameters (128 batch size, 2048 sequence length, cosine schedule) are consistent with contemporary autoregressive language model training. Models are scaled from 60M to 3B parameters for isoFLOP analyses. The choice of scale is deliberate: small enough to train many variants for hyperparameter sweeps and isoFLOP curves, but large enough (up to 3B) to demonstrate trends that plausibly extend to larger models.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **training loss** measured as "final log probability" on the language modeling objective — effectively negative log-likelihood or cross-entropy loss on held-out data. The paper also reports **steps per second** (wall-clock step time during training) and **relative FLOPs per forward pass** normalized to the isoFLOP-optimal baseline. For autoregressive evaluation (Section 4.2, Figure 6), the same language modeling loss is measured on 500M held-out tokens. The paper does not report downstream task benchmarks (e.g., perplexity on standard evaluation suites, zero-shot/few-shot performance on reasoning or knowledge tasks), which is a notable absence.

- **Baselines.** The paper uses three explicit baselines, each serving a distinct purpose in the experimental logic:
  - **IsoFLOP-optimal vanilla transformer**: The standard architecture against which all MoD variants are compared. This is the "baseline" referred to throughout — a decoder-only transformer trained with the same FLOP budget, optimized for model size (parameters) at each FLOP level. The isoFLOP curve for this baseline defines the reference performance that MoD must match or exceed.
  - **Stochastic routing MoD**: An MoD transformer with identical architecture and capacity constraints, but with router weights sampled from a Gaussian distribution rather than learned. This isolates the effect of learned routing: if stochastic routing performs comparably, then any benefit comes from the capacity reduction itself (e.g., regularization), not from intelligent token selection.
  - **Capacity-reduced MoE with token dropping**: In the MoDE experiments (Section 4.3, Figure 7), the paper compares integrated MoDE against simply reducing expert capacity in a conventional MoE model and relying on the implementation's token dropping mechanism (which typically drops later tokens in the sequence when capacity is exceeded). This baseline tests whether explicit learned routing to a "no-op" path is better than incidental routing via capacity overflow.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in **total training FLOPs**, the standard currency in the scaling laws literature. IsoFLOP comparisons are conducted at three budgets: 6e18, 2e19, and 1e20 FLOPs. For forward-pass efficiency, the paper uses **relative FLOPs per forward pass** normalized to the isoFLOP-optimal baseline model. The paper explicitly notes that this metric is "tightly correlated" with wall-clock step time, so lower relative FLOPs-per-forward-pass implies faster steps. The capacity parameter $C$ (or equivalently, the fraction $C/S$) determines the per-forward-pass FLOP reduction: with $C/S = 0.125$ (12.5% capacity) at every other layer, the self-attention FLOPs at routing layers drop to $(C/S)^2 = 0.0156$ (roughly 1.6% of vanilla attention cost), and MLP FLOPs drop proportionally to $C/S = 0.125$. The alternating pattern means approximately half the layers operate at full capacity and half at reduced capacity.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation, statistical significance tests, confidence intervals, or multiple random seeds. This is consistent with the isoFLOP analysis paradigm common in scaling laws research (where training multiple replicates at each configuration is prohibitively expensive), but it means the reported differences — particularly the 1.5% improvement claim and the specific capacity optimum at 12.5% — should be understood as point estimates without quantified uncertainty. The 500M-token held-out set for auto-regressive evaluation provides a large enough evaluation corpus that sampling variance in the loss metric is likely small, but the absence of error bars or seed analysis limits the ability to assess whether small performance differences are reliable.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Hyperparameter Sweep at 6e18 FLOPs (Figure 3)

The paper first conducts a hyperparameter sweep at 6e18 training FLOPs to determine the optimal MoD configuration before scaling to larger budgets. This sweep varies two key dimensions: **routing frequency** (every block vs. every other block) and **capacity** (from 12.5% to 95% of the sequence length).

**Headline findings from Figure 3:**

- **Alternating routing is crucial.** Routing every other block (rather than every block) is necessary for strong performance. The paper states this explicitly: "While routing every other block was crucial for strong performance, we found that aggressive capacity reduction was best." This means the architecture alternates between full-attention layers (all 2048 tokens participate) and routing layers (only 256 tokens participate).

- **12.5% capacity is optimal.** Among the tested capacities, 12.5% (256 tokens out of 2048) yields the best performance. The paper reports "gradual improvements were observed when reducing the capacity down to 12.5% of the total sequence, corresponding to 87.5% of tokens routing around blocks, with performance degrading beyond this point." This means the network can tolerate extremely aggressive routing — nearly 9 out of 10 tokens skip computation at each routing layer — without performance collapse, as long as full-attention layers are interleaved frequently.

- **The isoFLOP curve is shifted "down and to the right."** This is the paper's characterization of how MoD changes the relationship between model size and loss. Compared to the vanilla transformer isoFLOP curve (which has a U-shape — loss decreases with model size up to an optimum, then increases due to undertraining), the MoD curve achieves lower loss at its optimal point, and that optimum occurs at a **larger** model size. The paper states: "the optimal MoD transformer achieves a lower loss than the optimal baseline, and also has more parameters."

- **Specific model comparisons (Figure 3, left panel, numbered models):**
  - **Model #1 (isoFLOP-optimal baseline):** 220M parameters, achieves the best loss among vanilla transformers at 6e18 FLOPs.
  - **Model #3 (MoD variant):** Also 220M parameters, uses 12.5% capacity every other block. This model "slightly outperforms the isoFLOP optimal baseline" while being "upwards of 60% faster to step during training." The paper notes that "when run on equivalent hardware these two model variants take approximately the same amount of wall-clock time to train" — the speed advantage per step is offset by needing more training steps to consume the same total FLOP budget, but the end result is equal wall-clock time with better performance.
  - **Model #2 (stochastic routing):** The variant with Gaussian-sampled router weights. It performs "drastically worse than both the baseline and normal MoD transformer," confirming that learned routing is essential — the benefit is not from capacity reduction alone.

**Interpretation of the speed-vs-loss tradeoff.** Model #3 achieves a key practical win: it matches the baseline's loss while stepping 60% faster. This means that during inference (where wall-clock time per step is the bottleneck, not total FLOPs), this model would generate tokens substantially faster than the baseline at equal quality. The paper frames this as "smaller MoD models that, while they are not themselves isoFLOP optimal for their hyperparameter setting, are nevertheless as- or better-performing than the optimal baseline model while being faster to step."

#### IsoFLOP Analysis at Three Budgets (Figure 4)

The paper scales the optimal configuration (12.5% capacity, every other block) to three total training FLOP budgets: 6e18, 2e19, and 1e20 FLOPs. Model sizes range from 60M to 3B parameters.

**Headline findings from Figure 4:**

- **The "down and to the right" shift persists across scales.** At all three FLOP budgets, the MoD isoFLOP curve sits below the baseline curve (lower loss) and its minimum occurs at a larger model size. This suggests the benefit is not an artifact of a particular scale and may extend to larger models.

- **The optimal MoD model uses approximately the same FLOPs per forward pass as the optimal baseline.** This is the paper's key predictive rule, quoted in Section 3.4: "The optimal MoD transformer is that which uses as many FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP optimal baseline." Figure 4 (right panel) shows relative FLOPs per forward pass (normalized to the isoFLOP-optimal baseline). The MoD variants that achieve the best loss are those with relative FLOPs near 1.0 — meaning they have been scaled up (by adding parameters, preferentially depth) until their per-forward-pass FLOP count matches the baseline, fully reinvesting the routing savings.

- **Faster models exist at the cost of some performance.** There exist MoD variants with relative FLOPs per forward pass substantially below 1.0 (meaning they are faster than the baseline) that still match or slightly exceed baseline loss. These are the "smaller MoD models" referred to in the 6e18 analysis — they are not isoFLOP-optimal for their configuration but are practically useful. The paper states: "there exist MoD variants that are appreciably faster to step than the isoFLOP-optimal baseline ... while also achieving a lower loss."

- **Quantitative loss improvements.** The paper reports that MoD transformers "improve upon vanilla transformers by as much as 1.5% on the final log probability training objective for equivalent training FLOPs" (Section 1 and Discussion). This 1.5% figure refers to relative improvement in the loss value, not absolute percentage points. At 1e20 FLOPs, the isoFLOP-optimal MoD variant achieves lower loss than the isoFLOP-optimal baseline, but exact loss values are read from Figure 4 (left panel), which does not provide a numerical table — the figure must be read visually.

- **Depth is preferred over width for reinvesting FLOPs.** The paper states: "Empirically, we find that it is better to add depth than to add width when adding FLOPs to the model" (Section 4.1). This means the additional parameters in the larger MoD models are allocated to more transformer layers rather than to wider hidden dimensions or more attention heads. This makes intuitive sense given the architecture: deeper models provide more opportunities for the alternating full-attention/routing pattern, and more layers mean more decision points where the router can choose to process or skip tokens.

**Step-wise speed gains have two sources.** The paper breaks down why MoD models are faster:
  1. **Lower FLOPs-per-parameter ratio:** For a given model size, MoD uses fewer FLOPs per forward pass because a fraction of tokens skip computation at routing layers.
  2. **Smaller models can match larger baselines:** Since isoFLOP-optimal MoD models are larger than isoFLOP-optimal baselines, there exist *smaller* MoD variants that match the baseline's loss while using fewer parameters (and therefore fewer FLOPs). The paper states: "there exist MoD transformers that perform as well as isoFLOP-optimal baselines and are faster to step, both because they use fewer FLOPs per parameter and because they use fewer parameters."

#### Auto-Regressive Evaluation (Figure 6)

The paper evaluates MoD variants during autoregressive sampling on 500M held-out tokens, comparing the non-causal top-k routing used during training against the causal predictor-based routing needed for inference.

**Headline findings from Figure 6:**

- **Switching to causal routing causes minimal degradation.** The paper reports: "When switching from the top-k routing method to the predictor-based routing method we observed little performance degradation." Figure 6 shows that the auxiliary predictor "is upwards of 97% accurate soon into training," explaining why the causal approximation works well — the predictor accurately mimics the non-causal top-k decisions.

- **The speed-vs-performance tradeoff persists at inference.** The paper states: "As in the training setting, there exist MoD variants that are better performing than the isoFLOP-optimal baseline, while requiring fewer FLOPs per forward pass." This means the inference-time speed advantages (upwards of 50% faster step times) translate to deployment, not just training.

- **Both auxiliary methods work.** The paper tested both the auxiliary BCE loss method (Solution 1) and the auxiliary predictor MLP method (Solution 2). The main results in Figure 6 use the predictor-based method, since it "does not affect the language modeling objective." The BCE loss method is reported to degrade the LM objective by "approximately 0.2-0.3%" but is presented as a simpler alternative.

**Memory savings noted but not systematically studied.** The paper reports observing "memory savings relative to equivalently sized baseline models at larger sizes, with some variants requiring fewer total devices (i.e., a smaller TPU topology)." This is attributed to the reduced KV cache size during autoregressive sampling — tokens that skip layers do not need their keys and values stored for those layers. The paper does not quantify these savings precisely, stating: "We did not study this extensively, but we anticipate that as one scales to larger models, these savings could be an important consideration."

#### Mixture-of-Depths-and-Experts (MoDE) Results (Figure 7)

The paper tests whether MoD routing can be combined with MoE routing, yielding models that are sparse in both depth and expert utilization.

**Headline findings from Figure 7 and Section 4.3:**

- **MoD improvements compound with MoE.** The paper states: "the performance improvements offered by MoD compound with those of MoE." Both the staged MoDE (MoD routing before self-attention, then MoE routing in the MLP) and integrated MoDE (single router choosing between experts and a no-op) show this compounding.

- **Integrated MoDE outperforms capacity-reduced MoE.** The paper explicitly compares integrated MoDE against simply reducing expert capacity in a standard MoE model (which causes tokens to be dropped from their preferred expert when capacity is exceeded). The finding: integrated MoDE is "distinctly better." The explanation offered is that tokens in integrated MoDE "explicitly learn to choose the residual path around the experts, as opposed to preferring an expert but being dropped when implemented as a capacity reduction." In other words, learned routing to a no-op is better than incidental dropping — the network benefits from being able to decide which tokens genuinely don't need expert processing, rather than having tokens forcibly dropped based on capacity limits and implementation-specific priority rules.

#### Routing Decision Analysis (Figure 5)

The paper provides a qualitative analysis of the routing decisions made by a trained MoD model to understand what the router learns.

**Headline findings from Figure 5:**

- **Sparse but non-uniform routing.** As expected, routing layers select only 12.5% of tokens for processing. However, the router does not select tokens uniformly. Figure 5 (left) shows a visualization of routing decisions across layers, with a "vertical band of dark blue towards the end of the sequence" — suggesting that tokens near the end of the sequence are more frequently selected for processing than earlier tokens.

- **Some tokens are consistently selected; others consistently bypass.** The paper observes: "some tokens appear to engage each block along the transformer's depth, while others decide to route around blocks whenever possible." This suggests the router learns to identify persistent token-level properties (difficulty, information content, grammatical role) that predict whether computation is beneficial.

- **Router weight distribution matches design expectations.** The histogram in Figure 5 (right) shows that "approximately 12.5% of weights are above 0.5 and 87.5% are below" — consistent with the auxiliary loss (when used) pressuring the sigmoid of router weights to center around 0.5 and the top-12.5% to exceed the threshold.

- **Correlation with prediction entropy.** The paper reports a preliminary finding: "tokens that engage with blocks more frequently are correlated with output predictions that have higher entropy, which possibly corresponds to predictions that are more difficult to make." This is stated cautiously ("Preliminary analyses suggest") and is not quantified with a correlation coefficient or statistical test, but it provides an intuitive interpretation: the router learns to spend compute on tokens where the model is most uncertain.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Stochastic routing vs. learned routing**: Stochastic routing (top-k on Gaussian-sampled weights) performs "drastically worse than both the baseline and normal MoD transformer" (Figure 3, model #2 vs. model #3 vs. model #1). This is a critical ablation because it isolates the contribution of learned token selection: capacity reduction alone is harmful; only when the network learns which tokens to skip does performance recover or improve.

**Routing frequency (every block vs. every other block)**: The paper sweeps this dimension in the hyperparameter tuning (Figure 3). Routing every other block is "crucial for strong performance." Routing every block with 12.5% capacity would mean tokens that are never selected effectively disappear from the network for the entire depth. The alternating pattern ensures every token periodically participates in full self-attention and can be updated. This ablation is not shown as a separate controlled comparison in a dedicated figure, but is reported as the outcome of the hyperparameter sweep.

**Capacity fraction (12.5% to 95%)**: The paper sweeps capacity values and finds "gradual improvements were observed when reducing the capacity down to 12.5%." Performance degrades beyond 12.5%, establishing this as the optimal point. Again, this is reported as the sweep outcome rather than a controlled ablation with other variables held constant.

**Token-choice vs. expert-choice routing**: The paper does not present experimental results comparing these routing schemes directly. It provides a conceptual argument for why expert-choice is preferable (perfect load balancing, relative weights matter, single top-k yields mutually exclusive sets for two paths) but does not empirically validate that expert-choice outperforms token-choice on this task. This is a notable missing ablation — the claim that expert-choice is superior for this use case is theoretical, not empirical.

**Auxiliary BCE loss vs. auxiliary predictor MLP for causal routing**: The paper describes both methods but does not present a direct comparison of their performance. The BCE loss method is reported to degrade the LM objective by 0.2-0.3%, while the predictor method "does not affect the language modeling objective." The predictor is used for the main autoregressive results (Figure 6), but the paper does not show a head-to-head loss comparison between models using the two methods at equivalent capacity. This is a minor gap — the predictor method is clearly preferred, so the comparison is not essential, but it would quantify the cost of the simpler BCE loss approach.

**Adding depth vs. adding width for FLOP reinvestment**: The paper states empirically that "it is better to add depth than to add width when adding FLOPs to the model" but does not present a controlled comparison (e.g., isoFLOP-optimal MoD model with extra depth vs. one with extra width at the same total parameters and FLOPs). This claim appears to be based on the overall isoFLOP analysis rather than a dedicated ablation.

**Staged MoDE vs. integrated MoDE**: Both are described (Section 4.3, Figure 7) but the paper does not present a quantitative comparison between them. The paper notes that staged MoDE "is advantageous because it allows for tokens to skip the self-attention step" while integrated MoDE "is advantageous because it simplifies the routing machinery," but relative performance is not reported. The key comparison is both MoDE variants against the capacity-reduced MoE baseline, which both outperform.

**Non-MoD token dropping vs. learned no-op routing**: The comparison of integrated MoDE against "simply reducing the capacity of experts in conventional MoE models, and relying on token dropping to implement residual routing" (Section 4.3) serves as an ablation showing that learned routing to a no-op path is better than incidental dropping. The paper explains the mechanism: with capacity reduction, tokens are dropped based on implementation priority (typically position in sequence/batch), not learned importance; with integrated MoDE, the router explicitly learns to route tokens to the no-op when they don't need expert processing.

**There is no ablation of the alternating pattern's specific layout.** The paper does not test whether starting with a routing layer vs. a full-attention layer matters, whether the specific interleaving ratio (1:1 routing to full) is optimal, or whether more complex patterns (e.g., two full-attention layers between routing layers) perform differently. The optimal pattern is empirically determined to be "every other block" from the hyperparameter sweep, but the space of possible patterns is not explored.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments support the paper's central architecture claim — that MoD can match or exceed isoFLOP-optimal baselines while reducing per-forward-pass FLOPs — with clean, internally consistent evidence across three training scales and multiple model sizes. The isoFLOP methodology is standard and well-executed: fixing total training FLOPs and varying model size to find the optimum for each architecture enables fair comparison between vanilla and MoD transformers without conflating training compute with architectural differences.

However, the experiments also reveal important boundaries on what has been demonstrated:

**The performance improvement claim (1.5% on training loss) is narrow in scope.** The paper reports improvements on the "final log probability training objective" — that is, next-token prediction loss on the training distribution. There are no downstream evaluations: no perplexity on standard benchmarks (WikiText, Penn Treebank), no zero-shot or few-shot task performance (LAMBADA, HellaSwag, MMLU, etc.), no generation quality metrics. This is a significant gap because improved training loss does not always translate to improved downstream performance, particularly when the architecture differs from the baseline in ways that could affect generalization (the routing mechanism itself could induce subtle distributional shifts in the model's representations). The paper's claim is explicitly about training efficiency, not about downstream capability, but practitioners care primarily about the latter.

**The dataset is unspecified.** The paper never names the training corpus. This matters because the effectiveness of learned routing may depend on data characteristics: a corpus with high variability in token difficulty (e.g., code mixed with natural language, technical documents with simple connectives) might show larger MoD benefits than a corpus where token difficulty is relatively uniform. Without knowing the dataset, it is impossible to assess how general the 12.5% optimal capacity finding is, or whether the routing patterns observed (e.g., end-of-sequence tokens being selected more) are dataset artifacts.

**The scale ceiling is 3B parameters and 1e20 FLOPs.** While respectable for an academic study, this is orders of magnitude below the scale of production LLMs (70B, 175B, 540B+ parameters). The paper's claim that trends "persist across scales" is based on three data points (6e18, 2e19, 1e20 FLOPs) spanning less than two orders of magnitude. It is possible that at much larger scales, the optimal capacity fraction changes, the benefit of added depth saturates, or the routing mechanism interacts with other phenomena (e.g., in-context learning, emergent capabilities) in unanticipated ways. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by not claiming universality, but the framing ("one just needs to tune the model size...") suggests more confidence in extrapolation than the experimental range justifies.

**The speed claims conflate two distinct advantages.** The paper reports that MoD models are "upwards of 50% faster to step during post-training sampling." This figure comes from two separate sources that are sometimes conflated in the narrative: (1) FLOPs-per-parameter savings (routing reduces per-forward-pass FLOPs for a given model size), and (2) the fact that smaller MoD models can match larger baselines (fewer parameters means even fewer FLOPs). The 60% faster figure for model #3 in Figure 3 likely combines both effects: it's a 220M MoD model that matches a 220M baseline, but the baseline is not the isoFLOP-optimal baseline (which would be larger and slower). The isoFLOP-optimal MoD variant uses approximately the same FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP-optimal baseline (relative FLOPs near 1.0 in Figure 4), meaning it is **not** faster at inference — it is equally fast but better performing. The truly faster models are those that sacrifice some performance. This nuance is sometimes obscured in the paper's rhetoric: the "upwards of 50% faster" claim applies to specific (non-isoFLOP-optimal) configurations, not to the best-performing MoD variant.

**The 12.5% capacity optimum may be architecture- or data-specific.** The paper sweeps capacity from 12.5% to 95% and finds 12.5% is best. But this sweep is conducted at a single routing frequency (every other block), a single sequence length (2048), and a single dataset. It is plausible that the optimal capacity depends on these factors. For example, longer sequences might tolerate more aggressive routing (lower capacity) because the absolute number of tokens processed remains sufficient; shorter sequences might require higher capacity. The paper does not explore this interaction.

**Missing ablations limit mechanistic understanding.** Several questions about why MoD works remain unanswered by the experiments:
  - Does the benefit come primarily from skipping MLP computation, self-attention computation, or both? The paper routes tokens around the entire block (self-attention + MLP), so the relative importance of each cannot be disentangled from the reported results. An ablation routing only the MLP or only the self-attention would clarify which computation is most over-spent in standard transformers.
  - How does the routing pattern interact with attention heads? Are certain heads specialized for processing "important" tokens while others handle the full sequence? The paper does not analyze attention patterns in MoD vs. baseline models.
  - What would happen if capacity were allocated per-layer rather than uniformly? Could earlier layers use higher capacity (more tokens processed) and later layers use lower capacity? The paper's alternating pattern is uniform; non-uniform allocation is not explored.
  - The "skip-then-rejoin" pattern is claimed as an advantage over early-exiting, but there is no direct comparison against an early-exiting baseline to quantify this advantage. The claim remains speculative, supported only by the alternating pattern's empirical necessity.

**The autoregressive evaluation is limited to the training objective.** Figure 6 shows that the causal predictor maintains language modeling loss during autoregressive sampling, but there is no evaluation of generation quality — no human evaluation, no automated metrics for coherence/diversity/repetition, no qualitative examples of generated text. Since MoD changes which tokens participate in self-attention at each layer, it could affect generation in ways not captured by loss: for instance, it might produce more repetitive outputs (if important context tokens are skipped) or less coherent long-range dependencies. The absence of generation-quality evaluation is a meaningful gap for a method whose primary deployment advantage is faster autoregressive sampling.

**The MoDE results are preliminary.** Only two MoDE configurations are tested (staged and integrated), and the comparison is primarily against a capacity-reduced MoE baseline, not against a full isoFLOP analysis of MoDE variants. The paper does not determine the optimal MoDE configuration (what combination of MoD capacity and MoE sparsity is best? Does the optimal MoD capacity change when combined with MoE?), nor does it scale MoDE to the larger FLOP budgets used for the main MoD analysis. The MoDE results demonstrate compatibility but do not establish that the combination is more than the sum of its parts.

**No comparison to alternative efficiency methods at equal speed.** The paper compares MoD against a vanilla transformer baseline and a stochastic routing baseline, but not against other methods that could achieve similar speedups — for example, simple model compression (distillation, pruning), or training a smaller model for longer (overtraining). The "over-training" approach (training a smaller model on more tokens than would be compute-optimal, to get a faster model at equal quality) is mentioned in the Discussion as something MoD can be combined with, but is not included as a baseline. This makes it difficult to assess whether MoD's routing mechanism specifically provides value, or whether equivalent speed-performance tradeoffs could be achieved by simpler means.

Despite these limitations, the core empirical demonstration is solid: learned, capacity-constrained routing can recover performance that random token dropping destroys, and the savings can be productively reinvested into larger models. The isoFLOP curves at multiple scales, the stochastic routing ablation, and the auto-regressive evaluation collectively establish that MoD is a viable technique worthy of further investigation — particularly at larger scales and on downstream tasks. The specific optimal hyperparameters (12.5% capacity, every other block) should be treated as starting points rather than universal constants until replicated across diverse datasets and model scales.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: The Proof of Concept Is Limited to Training Loss on an Unspecified Dataset — No Downstream Task Evaluation Exists

**The assumption.** The paper validates MoD exclusively through language modeling loss (next-token prediction cross-entropy) on held-out training-distribution data. There are no results on standard perplexity benchmarks (WikiText, Penn Treebank), no zero-shot or few-shot evaluations on reasoning or knowledge tasks (MMLU, HellaSwag, LAMBADA, etc.), and no generation quality metrics (coherence, diversity, repetition). The dataset itself is never named — the paper refers only to "256000 sequences (500M tokens)" of held-out data for auto-regressive evaluation (Section 4.2), but the training corpus identity and characteristics are undisclosed.

**The consequence.** Improved training loss does not guarantee improved downstream performance, particularly when the architecture differs from the baseline in ways that could affect generalization. MoD changes which tokens participate in self-attention at each layer, which could have subtle effects on the model's ability to form long-range dependencies, maintain coherent multi-sentence context, or leverage in-context learning — none of which are captured by next-token prediction loss on held-out data from the same distribution. A practitioner considering MoD for a chatbot, coding assistant, or document summarization system would need to know whether the 1.5% training loss improvement translates to better task performance, or whether the routing mechanism introduces degradation in specific capabilities (e.g., factual recall, instruction following, multi-step reasoning) that training loss averages over. The paper provides no evidence either way.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper measures only "final log probability" (Section 1 and throughout Section 4). The auto-regressive evaluation (Figure 6) uses the same language modeling loss metric on 500M held-out tokens. No downstream task results are reported, and the paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation or suggest that such evaluation would be valuable for future work. The dataset is described only by its size (500M tokens) with no information about domain composition, preprocessing, or relationship to standard public corpora.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap. The claims throughout — "improve upon vanilla transformers by as much as 1.5%," "achieve baseline performance for equivalent FLOPS," "can be upwards of 50% faster" — are all scoped to the training objective. The paper does not warn readers that downstream task performance is unevaluated or that training loss improvements may not transfer. A practitioner reading only the abstract and conclusions might reasonably but incorrectly infer that MoD improves model quality in task-relevant ways, when in fact the evidence supports only improved next-token prediction efficiency on an unspecified training distribution.

### Limitation 2: The Optimal Configuration (12.5% Capacity, Alternating Blocks) May Be Specific to the Tested Scale, Sequence Length, and Dataset

**The assumption.** The hyperparameter sweep at 6e18 FLOPs (Figure 3) establishes 12.5% capacity (256 tokens out of 2048 sequence length) applied to every other block as the optimal configuration. This configuration is then used without modification at 2e19 and 1e20 FLOPs (Figure 4) and for all autoregressive evaluations (Figure 6). The paper implicitly assumes that the optimal capacity fraction and routing frequency are invariant to model scale, sequence length, and data distribution — or at least, that the 6e18 sweep results generalize to the tested range.

**The consequence.** The 12.5% capacity optimum may not hold at larger scales (e.g., 70B+ parameters), longer sequence lengths (e.g., 8192, 32768, or 100K+ tokens), or on different data distributions. At longer sequence lengths, the absolute number of tokens processed at 12.5% capacity grows (e.g., 1024 tokens for an 8192-length sequence), which could change the optimal fraction — perhaps shorter sequences need proportionally higher capacity because the absolute number of processed tokens becomes too small for self-attention to be informative. Conversely, very long sequences with many repetitive or low-information tokens might tolerate even more aggressive routing (e.g., 5% capacity). The optimal routing frequency (every other block) might also change with model depth: a 100-layer model may need full-attention layers at a different cadence than a 24-layer model. Finally, the dataset's characteristics — the distribution of token predictability, the prevalence of long-range dependencies, the mixture of easy and hard tokens — almost certainly affect which tokens benefit from computation and therefore what capacity is optimal. Without knowing the dataset, a practitioner cannot assess whether their data resembles the paper's training corpus in ways that would preserve the 12.5% optimum.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The hyperparameter sweep (Figure 3) tests capacities from 12.5% to 95% and routing frequencies of every block vs. every other block, but only at 6e18 FLOPs, sequence length 2048, and on the undisclosed dataset. The paper extrapolates the 12.5% / every-other-block configuration to 2e19 and 1e20 FLOPs without re-sweeping hyperparameters at those scales. The isoFLOP curves at larger budgets (Figure 4) show that this configuration continues to outperform baselines, which provides some evidence of robustness across the tested scale range (up to 3B parameters), but does not verify that 12.5% remains **optimal** at those scales — only that it remains **better than the baseline**. The optimal capacity might drift with scale, and a practitioner scaling MoD to 70B or 175B parameters would need to re-sweep rather than trust the 12.5% figure.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss the sensitivity of the optimal configuration to scale, sequence length, or dataset. It does not recommend re-sweeping hyperparameters at larger scales, nor does it characterize the shape of the capacity-performance curve sharply enough to assess how much performance would degrade if the "wrong" capacity were chosen (e.g., would 25% capacity perform nearly as well? Is the optimum sharp or broad?). The paper says only that it is "best to use 12.5% capacity blocks, every other block" (Section 4.1), presenting this as a finding rather than a contingent optimum. A partial mitigation is that the paper does provide the full hyperparameter sweep results in Figure 3, so a practitioner can see how performance varies with capacity and routing frequency at the tested scale — but the generalizability of those curves is unknown.

### Limitation 3: The Autoregressive Sampling Solution Addresses Causal Routing but Not the KV Cache Memory Savings Claim

**The assumption.** The paper reports observing "memory savings relative to equivalently sized baseline models at larger sizes, with some variants requiring fewer total devices (i.e., a smaller TPU topology)" and speculates that "as one scales to larger models, these savings could be an important consideration when choosing model variants to train, and could have significant positive effects in regards to the KV cache size during autoregressive sampling" (Section 4.1). The mechanism is intuitive: tokens that skip layers do not need their keys and values stored for those layers during autoregressive generation, so the KV cache should be smaller than in a vanilla transformer where every token's keys and values are stored at every layer.

**The consequence.** The KV cache memory claim is qualitative and unquantified. The paper explicitly states: "We did not study this extensively" (Section 4.1). This matters because KV cache memory is often the **binding constraint** on batch size and sequence length during autoregressive inference — more so than FLOPs. If MoD reduces KV cache size proportionally to the capacity reduction (i.e., by ~87.5% at routing layers), this would be a more significant practical advantage than the FLOP reduction for many deployment scenarios. Conversely, if the alternating full-attention layers require full KV caches, the overall memory savings might be modest (roughly half the layers store full caches, half store reduced caches, yielding ~44% total KV cache reduction). Without quantification, practitioners cannot assess whether MoD's memory savings are sufficient to, for example, double the maximum generation length on fixed hardware, or increase batch size without OOM errors. Additionally, the prediction method for causal routing (the auxiliary predictor MLP) has its own memory and compute cost that is not factored into any reported efficiency metric.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no KV cache size measurements, no memory footprint comparisons at any model scale, and no analysis of how the routing pattern interacts with the standard KV cache implementation (which typically pre-allocates memory for the maximum possible keys and values at each layer). The memory savings observation is presented as an anecdotal aside ("We noticed that MoD transformers had memory savings...") rather than a systematic measurement. The auto-regressive evaluation (Figure 6) measures only loss, not memory usage, latency, or throughput under realistic inference serving conditions.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to quantify memory savings, recommending it only as a direction for future investigation: "We did not study this extensively, but we anticipate that as one scales to larger models, these savings could be an important consideration." For a method whose headline advantage is deployment efficiency, the absence of memory footprint analysis — particularly for the KV cache, which dominates autoregressive inference memory — is a significant gap. A practitioner choosing between MoD and alternative efficiency techniques (quantization, distillation, FlashAttention, multi-query attention, etc.) cannot compare their effects on the memory bottleneck without this data.

### Limitation 4: The "Skip-Then-Rejoin" Advantage Over Early-Exiting Is Claimed but Never Empirically Validated

**The assumption.** The paper draws an explicit architectural distinction between MoD and early-exiting methods (Elbayad et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2021; Schuster et al., 2022), arguing that MoD enables a qualitatively different behavior: "In MoD, unlike in early-exit methods, a token can skip middle layers, then be updated via self-attention with tokens that have gone through all the middle layers. We speculate that this might be a useful property" (Section 2). The alternating routing pattern — full-attention layers interleaved with routing layers — is said to be "crucial for strong performance" (Section 4.1), and this is interpreted as evidence that the skip-then-rejoin capability matters.

**The consequence.** The paper never compares MoD against an early-exiting baseline under isoFLOP conditions, so there is no evidence that the skip-then-rejoin mechanism specifically provides value beyond what early-exiting could achieve with the same compute budget. The alternating pattern's necessity proves that some form of periodic full-attention is required — but early-exiting methods could also incorporate periodic re-entry, or could space their exit decisions to achieve a similar effect. Without a direct comparison, the claimed advantage of MoD's routing pattern over early-exiting remains a speculation ("We speculate that this might be a useful property"), not an empirical finding. A practitioner choosing between conditional computation approaches cannot assess from this paper whether MoD's added architectural complexity (routers at every other layer, auxiliary predictors for causal deployment, gradient flow through router weights) produces better speed-performance tradeoffs than the relative simplicity of early-exiting.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not implement, train, or evaluate any early-exiting baseline. The comparison to early-exiting methods is purely conceptual, appearing in the Background section (Section 2). The statement that the alternating pattern is "crucial" (Section 4.1) comes from the hyperparameter sweep comparing routing every block vs. every other block — but routing every block with 12.5% capacity is an extreme condition where tokens that are never selected effectively disappear from the network, which is a much harsher constraint than typical early-exiting methods (where tokens that exit early still participated in all previous layers). The "crucial" finding may reflect the specific severity of MoD's capacity constraint rather than a fundamental advantage over early-exiting.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge the absence of an early-exiting baseline as a limitation, nor does it suggest such a comparison as future work. The skip-then-rejoin advantage is presented as a distinguishing feature (Section 2) and the alternating pattern's necessity is presented as confirming evidence (Section 4.1), but the logical chain connecting these observations to a demonstrated advantage over early-exiting is incomplete. An empirical comparison — training an early-exiting model with equivalent capacity constraints and isoFLOP budget — would be needed to substantiate the claim. Without it, the architectural distinction is conceptual, not proven consequential.

### Limitation 5: The Speed Claims Are for Specific (Non-Optimal) Configurations — the Best-Performing MoD Model Is Not Faster

**The assumption.** The paper's abstract and introduction prominently claim that MoD transformers can be "upwards of 50% faster to step during post-training sampling" and that they "require a fraction of the FLOPs per forward pass." The 50%+ figure comes from specific model configurations — notably Model #3 in Figure 3 (220M parameters, 12.5% capacity, every other block), which matches the isoFLOP-optimal baseline's loss while stepping "upwards of 60% faster" during training. These speed claims are genuine for these specific (non-isoFLOP-optimal) configurations.

**The consequence.** The speed and the performance improvements come from **different models** — they are not simultaneously achievable. The isoFLOP-optimal MoD variant (the one that achieves the 1.5% training loss improvement) uses approximately the same FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP-optimal baseline (Figure 4, right panel: relative FLOPs per forward pass near 1.0). This model is **not faster** — it is equally fast but better performing. Conversely, the MoD variants that are substantially faster (relative FLOPs below ~0.7 in Figure 4) sacrifice performance relative to the isoFLOP-optimal MoD model. A practitioner faces a tradeoff: choose the optimal MoD model for best quality at the same speed as the baseline, or choose a smaller/faster MoD model that matches or slightly exceeds the baseline's quality but does not achieve the full 1.5% improvement.

The paper acknowledges this implicitly in Section 4.1 ("there exist smaller MoD models that... are nevertheless as- or better-performing than the optimal baseline model while being faster to step") but the abstract and conclusions present speed and quality improvements as if they characterize a single model: "These models match baseline performance for equivalent FLOPS and wall-clock times to train, but require a fraction of the FLOPs per forward pass, and can be upwards of 50% faster to step." The "match baseline performance" model and the "upwards of 50% faster" model are different points on the isoFLOP curve. The abstract's phrasing conflates them, potentially misleading readers into believing the best MoD model is both better and faster, when in fact it reinvests all FLOP savings into additional parameters and runs at the same speed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 shows Model #3 (220M MoD, 60% faster stepping) slightly outperforming Model #1 (220M baseline). Figure 4 (right panel) shows the isoFLOP-optimal MoD variants (lowest loss) cluster around relative FLOPs per forward pass of 1.0, while faster MoD variants (lower relative FLOPs) have higher loss. The paper explicitly states: "The optimal MoD transformer is that which uses as many FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP optimal baseline" (Section 4.1). This directly implies that the optimal model is not faster, and the faster models are not optimal.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this tradeoff in the body text (Section 4.1, Figure 4 discussion) but the abstract and Section 1 present a unified picture that blurs the distinction. The phrase "upwards of 50% faster" becomes a headline number even though it applies only to configurations that are **not** the best-performing MoD variants. A practitioner seeking to deploy the best possible model would get no speed improvement; a practitioner seeking maximum speed would leave performance on the table. The paper does not provide a clear tradeoff curve (e.g., a Pareto frontier of speed vs. loss) that would help practitioners choose their operating point, nor does it quantify how much performance is sacrificed for a given speed gain relative to alternative efficiency methods like training a smaller model for longer.

### Limitation 6: The Routing Mechanism Introduces Extra Hyperparameters and Training Complexity Without a Demonstrated Path to Practical Deployment

**The assumption.** MoD adds several new hyperparameters and architectural components beyond a standard transformer: the capacity fraction C/S, the routing frequency (which blocks are routing vs. full-attention), the router projection dimension, the choice between auxiliary BCE loss and auxiliary predictor MLP for causal deployment, the predictor MLP architecture (if used), and the strategy for reinvesting saved FLOPs (depth vs. width). The paper demonstrates that performance is sensitive to some of these choices — routing every block vs. every other block is the difference between success and failure (Figure 3), and capacity below 12.5% causes degradation. The paper implicitly assumes that the hyperparameter sweep at 6e18 FLOPs (Figure 3) is sufficient to identify a configuration that generalizes, and that the auxiliary predictor method (Section 3.5) solves the causal routing problem cleanly.

**The consequence.** A practitioner adopting MoD must either trust the paper's specific hyperparameter settings (12.5% capacity, every other block, predictor-based causal routing, add depth for FLOP reinvestment) or conduct their own expensive hyperparameter sweep. The paper provides limited guidance on how these hyperparameters interact or how they should be adjusted for different model scales, sequence lengths, or data distributions. More importantly, the auxiliary predictor for causal routing — while effective in the paper's evaluation — introduces an additional network component that must be trained, maintained, and whose predictions must be monitored for accuracy in production. If the predictor's accuracy degrades on out-of-distribution inputs (unusual text domains, adversarial inputs), the model may silently route tokens incorrectly, potentially damaging output quality in ways that are hard to diagnose. The paper reports 97-99% predictor accuracy (Figure 6), but this is measured on held-out data from the same distribution as training — there is no evaluation of predictor robustness under distribution shift, adversarial inputs, or long-context scenarios where the predictor must generalize to sequence lengths not seen during training.

Additionally, the paper does not provide ablation on the router's architectural choices. The router is a simple linear projection (one learned weight vector per routing layer), but alternative designs (deeper MLP routers, routers that condition on position, routers with per-head granularity) are not explored. A practitioner cannot assess whether the simple linear router is sufficient or whether more expressive routers would yield better allocation — and more expressive routers would add further parameters and hyperparameters to tune.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 3 provides a hyperparameter sweep at one scale (6e18 FLOPs, ~220M parameters), varying capacity and routing frequency. The paper does not sweep router architecture, predictor architecture, or the auxiliary loss weight. The causal predictor accuracy is reported as a single number (97-99% in Figure 6) without breakdown by token position, sequence length, or input characteristics. There is no discussion of what happens to model behavior if the predictor makes errors during autoregressive generation — e.g., a token that should be processed is skipped, or vice versa.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates this by providing clear, prescriptive recommendations: "one just needs to tune the model size for a given MoD configuration (i.e., capacity and routing frequency) to produce a model that uses as many FLOPs per forward pass as the isoFLOP-optimal baseline" (Section 4.1). This is a practical recipe for the capacity/frequency parameters. However, the paper does not address the broader deployment complexity — the need to train, validate, and monitor the auxiliary predictor; the interaction between routing decisions and production concerns like batching, padding, and variable sequence lengths; or the lack of robustness evaluation for the routing mechanism. The Discussion section (Section 5) focuses on future extensions (decoupling queries from keys, long-term memory, routing among different computation types) rather than on the practical engineering challenges of deploying the current MoD design in production autoregressive systems. For a method whose primary value proposition is deployment efficiency, this gap between research demonstration and production readiness is substantial.
