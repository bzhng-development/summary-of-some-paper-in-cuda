Here are the takeaways from this Ethan Evans interview (former Amazon VP who shares decades of observed corporate politics, from empire building and reorgs to firing managers and navigating high-stakes career moves):

**Empire building as a rational response to bad incentives**

Ethan defines empire building as focusing more on raw headcount than on impact, and he says it exists because it is rewarded. Counting people is the easiest thing to do when leaders are deciding who is ready to promote; impact is subjective, but "no one can debate, well, Ethan has 42 people and Ryan has 17." Some companies even have written thresholds—at Amazon after his time, he heard it was 80 or 90 people to become a director in some orgs, despite a leadership principle that "there's no bonus for additional headcount." Once that number is written down, "there damn sure is a bonus"—literally several hundred thousand dollars a year for accumulating enough people. He has seen leaders park people under a high-potential manager for six months, check the promo box, and then return them—"that's insanity where we're moving people around and changing their lives just to hit a number."

The mechanics get weirder. If someone drops below the threshold after promotion, companies rarely down-level them; instead they say "you're going to have to find a director-level role." Ethan has seen people get reorged under a peer not for business reasons, but because the peer didn't have scope for promotion—"I'm being told, go be their scope." This drives his core point: the incentives encourage managers to rationalize accumulating headcount, and ambitious high performers will find a way to hit the goal. If you give high performers a goal, "they're going to figure out a way to hit that goal. And if that requires claiming they need people or seeking to take over other groups, they're ambitious, they're going to find a way to rationalize that to themselves."

He acknowledges impact is harder to assess, which is why the easy metric wins: "I have to look at you and say, Ryan, we love the work you've done and you're on the right path, but Ethan was more impactful. And that just makes you mad." Even when he became a director at Amazon, it was with only 22 people in the early days before the number crept up. The whisper numbers became formal thresholds later, and once written, they drove exactly the behavior you'd expect.

**The reorg as a multi-purpose tool**

Reorgs are almost never purely about business structure. Because reorgs cost productivity, leaders wait as long as possible, then attempt to "get everything we want." The triggering condition is usually a genuine business change—a new market, an acquisition, a new project—or a cascade from someone leaving. But once the ship is leaving the dock, leaders ask "how much else can we throw on the deck?" Multiple people are involved, so you either align them on your ulterior motives or construct a narrative that sells only the business rationale.

Ethan's reorgs, he says, were very much about retention. "People don't like change. Who is it I absolutely need to keep, and who is it, if they leave, I'll either be okay or even maybe happy?" He would use reorgs to give a juicier scope to someone he was at risk of losing, while making sure the "suffering" fell on lower performers who could leave without damage. The leader is a **storyteller** constructing the best possible narrative—one that rewards deserving people, accomplishes business goals, and ensures any departures are survivable. Ryan notes this feels like "a hidden chess" game, and Ethan agrees.

The dark side of this is **managing out via reorg**: putting someone on something they don't want and calling it "the only seat available." Ethan tells the story of a leader at Amazon whose group was the "other" or "garbage can"—database maintenance, QA, small things. Everyone assumed the guy would quit, but he was "one of those very calm, nice guys who is willing to do what was needed" and stayed 20 years. But the technique, if you want someone to leave, is to give them "other" and let them self-select out. He connects this to the Japanese concept of the **window seat**, where being placed by the window signals you've been exiled to irrelevance, far from the center of action. Ryan reflects that it feels unfair that "the nice guy gets the garbage can," and Ethan agrees it is, even calling out that "being that nice isn't helping you." He often tells clients: "You are a very nice person, and that is not a compliment in this context."

**The squeaky wheel gets the grease, and how to be pushy without being a jerk**

Ethan's view is blunt: "If you never ask for anything, you'll never get anything." In a reorg, he has some people who've been vocal about their career goals and some who haven't. The quiet, dutiful performers get deprioritized—"I know you'll put up with it, and so you can end up behind just because you're such a nice guy." He's not saying you need to be a jerk, but pushy people get more, up to a point; if they become too demanding, they can get less because they've antagonized.

He tells the story of how he managed this tension early in his career. He wanted to signal to his manager Neil that he expected a director promotion, but without issuing a direct threat. He crafted the line: "I need to understand how important my career is to Amazon, because my career is very important to me. If it's not as important to Amazon as it is to me, I need to think about that." No one can argue that your career shouldn't be important to you, and "I need to think about that" isn't a direct threat—but everyone understands he's serving notice. It worked; Neil pushed the promo through. The lesson is to find wording that "clearly conveys the message, but in a way you can't argue." It has to be tuned to the workplace: Amazon was "a relatively sharp-elbowed, pushy place," and the same message would be offensive in some cultures.

When people on his own team were clearly empire-building, Ethan's approach was to get in a private room and ask, "Let's both be honest that you're empire building. Let's talk about how I'm going to get you there by some defendable path." He made **deals**: "You want this thing, I will help you get it, but you're going to show incredible performance on the things I need, and then I will deliver." His example: a VP told him his team was too expensive and he needed to open a low-cost offshore dev center. Ethan found a senior manager who wanted to make director, pitched him, "Would you go back to India and start this dev center? If you build it, I think we can use that as a platform to get you to director." The man moved home to India for three years, Ethan crafted a narrative about "a director-level accomplishment," and he got promoted. That deal-making approach became a signature pattern. He says with pride that he almost always followed through, except for one exception early in his career he still feels bad about 20 years later.

**"Stealing scope" and the polite fiction of the selfless servant**

When company growth slows, scope becomes zero-sum and Ethan sees more "cannibalism." As a senior leader, you can't allow people to "go rogue and be pirates in your own organization—they're raiding and pillaging, and you seem clueless." But the savvier approach for anyone seeking scope is to tell your manager: "If we have an org change and there's an opening, I just want you to know I'm ready to step up and I will stand by you." This sounds selfless because you're offering to be the stable leader rather than someone who might quit during a shake-up. "Ryan just became most qualified, not because he's best, but because he's most reliable." The underlying message—"I just want more scope, I'll take whatever you have"—is the same, but the framing transforms you from pirate to servant.

Ethan calls this the **polite fiction**: both parties know the real motive, but the surface story is "I want what's best for the org." He lived this himself. At one point, he was repeatedly put under a VP who had no engineering leaders, and Ethan just said yes to everything his boss handed him. He ended up managing eight wildly diverse functions—reverse logistics, selling video-game downloads, Amazon's app store, a B2B business shipping pallets of paper. It totaled 200 people. He didn't want to work on grading video games, but by being the person who solved whatever problem the boss had, he became indispensable. Someone once told him the simple way to get promoted is **"solve problems for your boss"**—five words. Even if the boss is selfish, they'll take care of the one person making their life easier.

He did balance this with creating his own scope. When his manager questioned why Ethan's team was spending 10 out of 800 people on custom T-shirt printing (a bet that eventually became a billion-dollar business for Amazon), Ethan said: "If you're going to tell me I can't spend 1% of my resources on this thing I believe in, we have another discussion to have, because you're micromanaging me." The combination—be the helpful person and the inventor—is powerful.

**Bad managers and the mutiny calculus**

When a report goes to their skip about a bad manager, the skip faces a subconscious cost-benefit that Ethan unpacks starkly. If I believe the report, I now have three problems: I have to decide what to do with my manager, I might have to manage them out, I have to hire and train a replacement, and while they're gone I have to do their work myself. If I don't believe the report, I have an "overly sensitive, high-maintenance" employee who is two levels down—and if they quit, the manager does the backfill. The system is loaded against the lone whistleblower.

That's why Ethan's advice is: **never mutiny alone**. Take two or three people, or send them in sequence. He tells the story of a leader who was treating the women on his team terribly but not in front of Ethan. It took multiple reports bubbling up from different people before he realized he'd been blind. "It took a long time for the different rumors to bubble up. Had any one of them come to me early, I probably wouldn't have listened to because it was a lone report." The mutiny succeeds when the skip-level leader hears the same problem from multiple, independent sources and realizes "I've been missing something."

For a high-performing IC who finds their manager lacking, Ethan advocates sanity-checking with peers first: "I'm really struggling with our boss in this area. Are you having that problem?" If they say no, you learn something about your own style; if they say yes, you need to find who is willing to speak up. If you can tell your skip, "These three people are all willing to share similar stories, would you consider at least talking to them?"—most managers will. The alternative path is to avoid the mutiny entirely by framing a move as "I'm not compatible with our leader—maybe it's him, maybe it's me—but I'm not here to throw your manager under the bus, I'm just telling you I need a change." Ethan himself used a more advanced version of this: when his VP kept saying no to a role Ethan wanted, he told the VP, "I have an offer from an SVP in a different org. You know if I give my word to that guy, I have to go. What do you want me to do?" He was refused for weeks; the next morning he was in the new role. He framed it not as a threat but as "what would you do," tapping into the VP's understanding that, once Ethan gave his word to an SVP, he was gone.

**The craft of political messaging and the "interpersonally warm, professionally firm" framework**

Ethan's superpower across all these stories is wordcraft. He says the mistake people make is not thinking through their wording before they're in the room. He calls his approach **interpersonally warm, professionally firm**: "Being friendly, smiling, not getting agitated, while saying what I need to about what I really want, but in a way where I don't bring anger or you-versus-me into it." Body language matters enormously—he points out that the same line "I'm going to have to think about that" can be delivered with a warm smile like an anime kitten or with a confrontational edge, and the outcomes are completely different.

A key phrase is "How can you help me?" because "everyone loves to think of themselves as helpful." He credits the book **Leadership and Self-Deception** (Arbinger Institute) with reshaping his mindset: don't see the other person as an obstacle or an API to wrench the right results from, but as a human with their own pressures and problems. "Once people feel like you're caring about them as a person, they usually respond that way."

For specific conversations, he thinks in terms of **chess openings**—pre-planned opening moves where you've rehearsed the key phrase and chosen the right moment (not five minutes after a production outage on a Friday). Then, like chess, you follow general principles like "move toward the common ground." He admits he had a temper early in his career and got fired twice, put on layoff lists, precisely because he was volatile and critical. Learning to put the temper away made an enormous difference. "People don't react well to anger or criticism, even if you're right."

**Handling politically skilled operators: good, evil, and the deal-making middle**

When Ethan encounters someone else with high political skill, his first question is: what's their motive? The skill sets of influence and politics are the same—"Darth Vader had executive presence in spades"—so you're trying to figure out, "Are they US special forces or Russian special forces?" There are three categories: people who want to do good (easy, highly effective collaboration); people who are completely out for themselves (lightsaber fight); and a middle ground of people whose motives you can work with even if they're not altruistic. "In this limited scope, we can both make money together."

For the truly self-serving, Ethan's advice is to make yourself a less attractive target. Bring allies—most "slippery people" love to operate in the dark, and if the room is full of witnesses, it's easier for them to go along than to fight you. "What you want to do is put them in a situation where it's easier to go along with you than to fight you." And he stresses that the slippery person isn't telling themselves they're evil; their internal narrative is "I'm practical. Other people are wishy-washy." If you can speak to that narrative—"You're right, you're very practical, and in this situation the practical thing is to help me out"—you can often deflect them. "It's important to remember that the other person's narrative, we think of them as like, that person is so unethical. They must wake up in the morning and think about how they can rob their grandmother. That's not what they say about themselves."

**Orgs stealing scope, and the skunk trick**

When other orgs are angling to absorb your scope, Ethan sees a few defensive plays. One is the **skunk trick**: list all the unsexy things you maintain that they don't know about, and have the customers for those things show up and say how important they are—"We actually run the Yugoslavian tax engine. Are you ready to take on that?" Another is to be thorny: "overreact and attack back and make yourself less attractive than the next soft target." But you have to be careful not to discredit yourself. A strong platform of high performance is the best defense—"we know reorgs cause a six-month hit in productivity, we're killing it on growth, we shouldn't talk about this for six months." Delay tactics often work because in six months the attacker will have moved on.

The attacker side, which Ethan shares with some discomfort, ranges from genuinely good arguments ("I can get this group from $100M to $200M; here's how") to the more political. He notes that "respect between two engineering teams is inversely proportional to their distance"—the further apart, the more they belittle each other because they can't see the work. (His example: describing Amazon S3 as "just a big disk drive.") The less savory attack is to go highlight flaws in the other org. But his preferred framing even for that is: "I can't help but notice you're having a lot of trouble here. Is there anything I can do to help?" Then, once you've demonstrated results under your leadership, casually mention consolidation.

**The meat shield and dealing with difficult people in other orgs**

One of Ethan's managers once put a person into a role explicitly called a **meat shield**: "This person is a huge pain for our org. Your job is to keep them off of us. It's going to be a terrible job, but I'll reward you later." The manager was honest about it—"go take one for the team and I'll pay you back."

For a difficult person in another org who is creating pain, Ethan's approach is to first understand why they're valuable to their org. Their leadership isn't saying "we have this evil person, but as long as they're pointed at other orgs, that's fine." They're telling themselves the person has sharp elbows but gets so much done. Ethan's external narrative to that leader: "This person is really making things difficult. I know they're valuable to you, but Option 1 is you find a new interface person for us. Option 2 is we're going to have to adjust to distance ourselves for the sake of my team—and unfortunately that option will create a lot of friction between our orgs that will take both of our time." He is presenting it as a tragedy that will make life harder for both of them, without blustering or threatening. "Most leaders at a certain level are sensitive to unspoken messages. They understand I'm giving them a chance to collaborate, but that there's a stick up my sleeve."

**Weak managers, the umbrella vs. funnel, and changing the balance of safety**

Ethan draws a graphic image: all managers are either an **umbrella or a funnel**. When the bad stuff comes down, they either shield their team or funnel it directly onto them. The manager who says yes to everything is choosing a safer path for themselves—pushing back upward is scary and dangerous, while pushing down on a direct report is easy because "I have rank, I control your performance review." The way to address this is to **change the balance of safety**: either help them feel safe pushing back on their boss, or help them feel not safe asking for your fourth weekend.

The safe-pushback approach is subtle: "Ethan, there seems to be a pattern where we keep getting asked to do weekend work. I would love to help you push back on that. I can give you data. But I also have to be clear that my family is very important to me and I can't keep working weekends. I don't want you in a position where you accept work I'm going to have to decline, and then you'll have committed and it won't happen. How can we plan to push back next time?" This is done in advance, on a Tuesday, not when the crisis is in hand.

He emphasizes that you have to have leverage. "If you're not willing for the manager to say, 'Well, if you don't like it, quit,' you can't say anything." When Ethan told his manager "I need to know how important my career is to Amazon," he was also looking for an outside job. "I decided, I'm going to do everything I can diplomatically to win this over, but I'm also okay leaving." If you have created the leverage—a valuable skill, a good reputation—"if your stick is long enough, it doesn't take very much force, which is why I'm able to be polite about it." He recommends the book **Extreme Ownership** by Jocko Willink: "Don't be a victim. You have agency."

**The mechanics of firing managers**

Firing managers is harder than firing engineers for three reasons: their weaknesses are more subtle (they got made managers for some reason), their work is hard to measure (how do you put metrics on hiring, performance reviews, and team output?), and "managers are part of the system—they know what it looks like, they have a quicker Spidey sense for how to position themselves to be hard to get rid of."

Ethan's ideal approach is to surface the idea socially without ever saying "fired" or "performance," which would create legal problems of "constructive termination." He might say: "I don't think we've gotten off to the best start here. I'm wondering how you feel about it. Would you like to work on that, or would you like my help finding some other option?" A smart manager will counter with something like: "I'm so sorry that you're not satisfied. Of course I'm doing my utmost. But if you're not happy, I'd like to discuss what changes we could make." That's the negotiation—the manager is saying "I can make this hard for you; pay well for my exit." The alternative path is classic performance management with documented goals, but "almost all managers will quit before that—once you start the process, they'll be like, okay, this is going to take 90 days, I have 90 days to find a new job."

**The leverage you have when you're being fired**

Ethan unpacks exactly what leverage an employee has during a termination: (1) **time and hassle**—the process is time-consuming, HR audits paperwork, and you can save your manager all that if they provide severance; (2) **the story you'll tell the team**—"I can either leave talking about what a terrible experience this was and what a monster you are, or I can leave saying this just wasn't a fit and I'm excited to move on and Ryan treated me amazingly through the whole process"; (3) **the manager's self-image**—"most managers hate firing, they want to look in the mirror and say, well, it didn't work out but I took care of him." The company's money isn't really their money, so spending a little to feel good about themselves is an easy trade.

He also shares a tactic for buying time during a difficult feedback session: "Ryan, I appreciate you're starting to give me some performance feedback. Honestly, this moment, I can't have it productively. Can we have it tomorrow morning?" If the manager says no, you now have a beef with HR: "He was so mean to me. I asked for space and he said no, I have to ridicule you right now." That puts the manager in the hot seat. But he's also clear that once you're on a formal performance improvement plan, you are almost certainly going to be fired: "By the time someone gets around to giving you a PIP, they've tried everything else they could think of, and at this point they just want you gone as quietly as possible." He hears this story constantly—people write him convinced they'll turn it around, then three months later: "You were right, I got fired."

**Growing past senior engineer: the new skill, letting go, and the "trust in bad times" formula**

The core reason people get stuck is they keep doing the current skill harder instead of learning the new skill of the next level. "What gets you promoted early in life is hard, effective work, delivery, shipping. As you get further, you need to delegate, communicate, let go of your hard skills." He cites Marshall Goldsmith's book **What Got You Here Won't Get You There**.

For the most senior promos, when two people are equally qualified, the tiebreaker is **trust** and **relationship**. Trust is earned more in bad times than good times—his analogy is "when two people have been in a war together and I put my life on the line for you." At work, this looks like jumping on a crisis, canceling a vacation, showing up when the chips are down. "When we lost the Acme account, Ryan came through. He flew back from Hawaii early." Relationship is simply: "Do I like you? I'm going to spend eight hours a day with this person. Why would I not choose the person I enjoy?" He quotes a Bezos line: "When you come into the room, do you suck all the oxygen out of it? Or is it, 'Oh, Ryan's here, this just got better'?" He knows this will infuriate some engineers, but "I'm not here to apologize for the way the world works—the way the world works sucks, but it does work that way."

For the promo process itself, the key is getting champions ahead of time. His favorite method: have your manager email peers and skips six months in advance and say, "Ryan's going to be up for promotion, and because your group is adjacent, I'm going to need to ask you for feedback. So I'm letting you know you've got six months to watch Ryan." When the VP later says they only saw Ryan twice, you can say, "I told you. I put you on notice." If your manager won't do it, the IC can go directly: "I'm up for promotion. I'm pretty sure my manager is going to ask you for feedback. What can we do over the next six months so you're in a natural position to give input?" The unspoken leverage is that the other VP knows they'll need your VP's help for their own people's promos. The system runs on reciprocity.

His biggest plug: he made a profession of getting his people promoted. It became his recruiting tool—"Come work for me and I'll make it happen." He tells the story of his star employee who went from mid-level engineer (L5) to director across eight years, three promotions, and they powered each other's careers. "He powered my career and I powered his."

**Influence without authority: the engineer who got to Jeff Bezos**

Ethan gives the example of an entry-level new-college-graduate engineer who had the idea for Amazon Cloud Drive (a Box/Dropbox competitor). He fought his way into an invite-only engineering conference by making a proposal, then emailed Jeff Bezos and asked, "I have this idea, I'm going to be at the poster session, could you stop by my poster?" He got the audience, pitched the vision, and Jeff liked it and emailed the guy's triple-skip boss. The VP didn't like the idea at all, but Jeff had said "look into it," so the project got funded and built. Similarly, the Fire TV originated from a not-super-senior person doing legwork and telling a compelling story. "Influence can come from anywhere. It usually begins with a good story, based in some real fact, told with enthusiasm and told repeatedly to the people who have the power."

**Backchanneling: the good kind and the manipulative kind**

Positive backchanneling is about giving people a chance to ask questions and voice concerns in private, where they don't have to bring them out in public. "I'm giving you a chance to feel understood, consulted, listened to." It builds trust through emotional buy-in: "Who can I count on when the chips are down? Ryan came to me and asked my input and showed understanding for my needs. Totally, I'm funding that." Negative backchanneling—undermining someone behind their back—Ethan says isn't usually necessary, but it exists, and when it does, it's often dressed as a mutually beneficial backroom deal: "we're snakes together."

**Avoiding politics: the leverage of being essential**

For the engineer who despises everything they've just heard, the advice is simple. Choose a leader who shares your disdain. Work on the hardest area where expertise really matters and you can stand out as irreplaceable. Ethan recalls Andy Jassy talking about a few principal engineers on AWS working on such hard problems that he "absolved them of all that other stuff." He also describes an employee Jassy knew wasn't working that hard, but "he's in a role where we need him, and I think he knows we need him, so we're just going to have to live with it." Andy's reaction was almost admiring: "Good on him. He's kind of got us." That's the path—if you don't want to play politics, you have to have leverage so strong you can opt out. Finding high-growth companies also helps, because a growing pie means fewer zero-sum battles. During his 15 years at Amazon, the company went from 14,000 to 1.4 million people, his team from 6 to 800—"that kind of growth opens up doors."

**The advice to his younger self, and the across-the-board thread**

If Ethan could go back to his early self, he would say: "It's not just being right. It's not just being technically astute. There's all this other stuff going on—wake up to it." What ties the entire three-hour conversation together is his single-sentence distillation: **"Take it seriously that your relationships are what actually control your progress."**

---

His **polite fiction** framing runs through the entire conversation—from reorg narratives to termination negotiations, the surface story and the real story coexist, and the skill is delivering both without forcing the other party to acknowledge the gap. That skill feeds directly into his **interpersonally warm, professionally firm** stance, which lets him say hard things without burning relationships. His career-long pattern of **deal-making**—with demanding high performers, with bosses who needed dev centers, with VPs who needed a stable leader during a shake-up—all rest on the same foundation: create leverage by being essential, then cash it in with a story that makes the other person feel like they're winning. And the capstone advice, "take it seriously that your relationships are what actually control your progress," is the thread that holds every tactic together—because even the best story lands only if someone wants to hear it from you.
