Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay on how AI will affect software engineering careers (drawing on the recent Devin announcement and Andrej Karpathy’s self-driving analogy):

**The self-driving analogy for coding automation**

Andrej Karpathy (ex-director of AI at Tesla, founding member of OpenAI) tweeted a framework: automating software engineering will follow the same pattern as automating driving. Both progress through stages—first humans perform the task while data is collected; then AI assists with well-defined subtasks (lane-keeping, or autocompleting a few lines of code); then AI handles more complex maneuvers (lane changes, turns, stop lights, or larger code chunks and coordinating agents); and finally the system becomes feature-complete while we work through the long tail of quality improvements toward full autonomy. Ryan notes that Google started on self-driving cars in 2009, and only now are we seeing limited market testing—it’s not a perfect comparison, but “the high-level analogy stands.” Devin, the viral autonomous AI software engineer, resolves only 13.86% of real GitHub issues unassisted. It’s “an impressive step towards a feature-complete software engineering AI,” but “more aspirational than functional today.”

**Ambiguity levels and which work gets automated first**

The key career-planning insight is that **less ambiguous work gets automated first**. Ryan maps engineering levels to the ambiguity each can handle: Junior (IC3) handles zero ambiguity (“we know exactly what we need to do and how to implement it”); Mid-level (IC4) handles feature-level ambiguity (solution known, implementation details not); Senior (IC5) handles roadmap-level ambiguity (“we know what problem to solve and why, but we don't know what to do to solve it yet”); Staff (IC6) handles strategy-level ambiguity (“we don't even know what problems we should tackle—IC6s create scope”). Well-scoped tasks with code pointers sit at the bottom of that stack and will be the first things AI eats. It will take “a lot longer for us to trust AI to handle more ambiguous asks like ‘make our infrastructure more efficient.’”

**What to invest in now**

If you’re worried about building replaceable skills, “focus on learning higher-level behaviors”—directional thinking, the ability to influence, high-level tech planning. Ryan also gives a blunt imperative: “start using AI tools if you aren’t already. Tools have always made our lives easier. AI is no different. If you aren’t using these tools you’ll get left behind.” He closes on an optimistic note: the small, tedious details of software engineering (compiler errors, missing semicolons) have always been a pain, and the higher-leverage his own work has become, the more fun it’s been. He’s “excited about what these tools unlock,” and doesn’t see engineers disappearing anytime soon.
