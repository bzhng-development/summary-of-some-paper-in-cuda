Here are the takeaways from this edition of The Peterman Post (Ryan Peterman on the mechanics of setting up cross-team workstreams at the L4/L5 level, framed around his own L6 Staff promotion):

**Influencing across teams as a baseline for Staff**

Ryan opens with a clear calibration: influencing across teams is a "baseline expectation for Staff Engineers (L6)." The core challenge at the L4/L5 level is therefore to demonstrate this capability *before* the promo. Setting up a cross-team workstream requires two distinct phases—first finding a problem large enough to warrant multi-team effort, then convincing those other teams the problem is worth solving and that your direction is correct. The rest of the piece is a concrete playbook for the second phase, drawn from the workstream that anchored his own promotion.

**The concentric-circles model for getting buy-in**

The alignment strategy is methodical, moving outward from a small nucleus: **team alignment**, then **org alignment**, then **partner alignment**. Ryan first discussed the opportunity with a specialist and a tech lead on his own team—getting them on board and using their feedback to refine the direction. With that small coalition formed, he wrote publicly about the problem and why it needed solving, which brought managers in his org into alignment and "made staffing discussions easier." Finally, he booked dedicated discussions with relevant tech leads in the partner org to secure their buy-in for collaboration. He notes your specific sequence might differ, but this concentric pattern works for most cases, and consulting your manager on who to align with is a useful shortcut since "your manager should know the org structure well."

**The diagnosis when buy-in fails**

For engineers who struggle to get others to prioritize work they've identified as impactful, Ryan offers a blunt diagnosis: "either the work isn't that impactful or you're not explaining its impact well." If the root cause is communication, two correctives apply. First, **speak in terms of impact your audience already knows**—tying your workstream to a metric people already care about makes persuasion dramatically easier. Second, **be concise**: people are busy, and the goal is to "maximize how much of your message you can get across with limited time." The practical implication is that the better you are at communicating, the more natural alignment becomes, which Ryan flags as why so many strong engineers emphasize communication as a force multiplier.

**Roadmapping as the second act**

Once people agree the problem is worth solving, you need a concrete plan. Ryan's sequence: **leverage domain expertise** (he already had high-level ideas and began hashing out designs to map what needed to get done), **align on project plans** (leading a few meetings to agree on solutions, divide ownership, and set rough timelines), and **communicate alignment** broadly—sharing an update for each cross-team project covering what was planned, why, who was doing what, and rough timelines. The pattern repeats: communication quality correlates directly with how smoothly alignment lands.

**Two concrete investments in communication skill**

Ryan offers a pair of specific, actionable suggestions. For speaking, he points to **Toastmasters**, an international public-speaking club he's participated in since college, crediting it with helping him better articulate himself in meetings within a supportive practice environment. For writing, his prescription is straightforward: "write more and solicit feedback to become a better writer"—one of the top reasons he writes his Substack, and he can already see measurable improvement compared to earlier articles.

**On ownership and the funnel for senior engineers**

The final piece of the original question—how to maintain ownership and leadership through the workstream—he intentionally defers. It's a topic that "comes up a lot" and is "worth its own post since there are a lot of misconceptions." He closes with a teaser aimed squarely at engineers who feel more senior colleagues often take opportunities from them, promising the next piece will address that dynamic directly.
