Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the author himself, reflecting on how mentorship accelerated his growth to Staff Engineer in 3 years at Instagram):

**The two forms of mentorship**

Ryan opens by distinguishing between two common forms of mentorship that each played a role in his career. **Explicit mentorship** is the traditional model: you set up regular check-ins with someone specifically to get advice. **Implicit mentorship**, by contrast, happens when you work alongside someone who is strong in the skills you want to develop—you grow just by collaborating with them, absorbing their approaches without any formal arrangement. They each have distinct strengths, and both were essential to his path to Staff (IC6).

**Explicit mentorship: closing the scope gap**

When Ryan’s manager told him that finding staff-level scope was his single biggest gap for reaching IC6, the manager didn’t just give feedback—he actively found a mentor in the org who excelled at that exact skill and made the introduction. Ryan and this new mentor set up weekly one-on-ones. Crucially, Ryan always drove the agenda as the mentee, coming to every session with more questions than they had time for and taking copious notes. “This is important because you can’t expect your mentor to keep track of what you need. You should own your career growth and pull what you need.” His questions were tactical and direct: “How can I find staff-level scope?” “How do I influence other teams to take on shared roadmaps?” “What did your journey to staff look like?”

As a mentee, Ryan felt he didn’t have much to offer in return, so the bare minimum he gave back was honest appreciation and demonstrable results from the advice. Now, as a mentor himself, he sees that watching a mentee grow quickly is one of the most satisfying parts of the relationship—it’s exactly what mentors value in return. To find this kind of mentorship, he advises looking around your org for someone strong in what you need, then simply asking for some one-on-one time: “You’d be surprised at how giving people are if you ask politely.” Some companies offer formal mentorship programs, and your manager should help you navigate the search regardless. But Ryan notes that most people only think of this explicit model—when, for him, the implicit kind turned out to be even more powerful for technical growth.

**Implicit mentorship via a “10x engineer”**

The second story centers on Haixia Shi, Instagram’s de facto video domain specialist. Ryan describes him as an IC who writes and reviews more code than anyone in the org, receives the most questions because of his domain expertise yet remains the most responsive, consistently solves problems that many Staff engineers can’t, and produces project work with industry-wide influence—all sustained “half over half” since they started working together. For an app where video is central to the user experience, being the go-to specialist is a big deal.

Ryan never received formal mentorship from Haixia, but he didn’t need to. “Engineering excellence radiates from him.” Strong engineers become stronger simply by asking him technical questions and observing how he approaches problems. The value is in the collaboration itself—picking up countless small tips and tricks while building features and debugging issues shoulder to shoulder with someone operating at that level. “Working with peers like him makes coming to work delightful and is one of the key contributors to my engineering career growth.” Ryan’s technical skills have come almost entirely through this implicit model, and his advice for finding it is blunt: work on teams with a high density of talent. On those teams, you’ll grow no matter what specific project you’re on.

His closing synthesis is that mentorship is one of the most effective career accelerators available, and the practical starting point is talking with your manager, who can position you near the right people and projects. For those still struggling to find a mentor, he offers his own writing as a low-commitment form of mentorship—a direct invitation to the audience the post is speaking to.

---

Ryan’s career is a case study in deliberately pursuing both forms of mentorship: his **explicit mentorship** closed the strategic “scope gap” that blocked his Staff promotion, while his **implicit mentorship** under a “10x engineer” gave him the hard technical skills that came “almost entirely” from collaboration. The through-line is agency—his manager pointed the way, but Ryan drove every agenda, absorbed every tip, and chose teams dense with talent so that growth became ambient. His framing that the mentee must “own your career growth and pull what you need” echoes across both models, turning mentorship from a passive hope into an accelerant he could ignite.
