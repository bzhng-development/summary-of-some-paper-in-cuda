Here are the takeaways from this Ricky interview (went from Junior to Staff Engineer at Google by 28, stayed on one team for 6 years):

**Junior to Mid-Level (L3 to L4): building fundamentals through discomfort**

Ricky’s first promotion came after about a year and a half, taking his total compensation from $180K to $250K. The work itself was standard for an early-career engineer—he focused on finishing projects independently, without hand-holding, and building the fundamental hard skills he’d need later. But his internal experience was anything but easy. He was overwhelmed by imposter syndrome and constantly worried about looking “dumb.” The thing that got him through was forcing himself to ask questions anyway, even when it felt uncomfortable. He describes himself as having strong soft skills from the start, deliberately managing expectations with his manager and making sure everything he worked on was aligned with what the L4 promotion required. At this stage, the formula was simple: ship work, communicate clearly, and don’t let self-doubt stop you from getting the information you need.

**Mid-Level to Senior (L4 to L5): ownership and saying no**

The next promotion, also about a year and a half, bumped his comp from $250K to $350K and introduced a sharper strategic edge to his approach. Several principles anchored this jump. First, he identified an area on his team that lacked a clear owner and became the “go-to person” for it—a move that naturally generated leadership opportunities and higher impact without requiring anyone to anoint him. Second, he got deliberate about **planning his promotion out**: he studied the expected behaviors for L5 and actively sought projects that would demonstrate those behaviors. That meant learning to say no to work that didn’t grow him or didn’t map to the next level’s bar—even when it came from his manager. His tactic wasn’t to refuse outright but to present a well-thought-out alternative that was more impactful. Third, he **managed up**: he put together a roadmap of projects he believed would be both impactful and promotion-worthy, then got buy-in from his manager before executing. “After that, it was just a matter of execution.”

Ryan notes this is a clean playbook: identify the evaluation criteria, design projects to hit them, align your manager in advance, and protect your time. Ricky credits the speed less to raw technical ability and more to a deliberate blend of technical and soft skills—knowing what to build and knowing how to position it.

**Senior to Staff (L5 to L6): leveraging trust and expanding influence**

The Staff promotion took three years and moved compensation from $350K to $520K. The core mechanism this time was influence beyond his immediate team. Having stayed on the same team for years, he’d become an established leader in his org, and he used that momentum to push the boundaries of what nearby teams were doing and challenge the status quo. He describes his role as “pushing on what adjacent teams were doing”—asking questions that made people reconsider their assumptions, driving cross-team alignment, and having enough leverage to make those challenges stick. The critical insight is how much this depended on trust. By staying at Google the whole time, he’d accumulated a reputation and relationships that made it much easier to exert that influence. He contrasts this with the common advice to job-hop frequently, suggesting that the compounding effect of organizational trust can sometimes outweigh the faster title bumps from switching companies.

**Management and the cost of growing too fast**

During his time as a Senior engineer, he discovered he genuinely enjoyed mentoring others and watching them grow. When his team absorbed a sudden influx of 20 new hires, he was already informally helping manage them, and transitioning into a formal manager role felt like a natural step. But the transition brought growing pains he hadn’t anticipated: awkward interactions with much older colleagues, discomfort running into reports outside of work, a heavier meeting load, and the emotional weight of delivering critical feedback—all skills he found harder than the IC work he’d been doing. His most striking reflection is that he wishes the opportunity had come later. The increased responsibility made his schedule significantly less flexible, and the stress of that rigidity in his early twenties is something he’d undo if he could. “I wish career growth opportunities came later” is a counterintuitive take that Ryan flags as rare among fast-promoted engineers.

**Imposter syndrome, luck, and deliberate stability**

Early on he doubted his skills so deeply that he felt like a “personality hire”—someone kept around for being enjoyable to work with rather than for competence. That feeling only receded after he’d stacked up a series of successful projects. He came to understand that most Googlers experience some version of imposter syndrome, and that learning that fact was itself part of the cure. He frames his success as roughly **50% luck and 50% preparation**: he landed on a stable team with a supportive management chain, and he paired that good fortune with working both “diligently and strategically.” His advice for capitalizing on luck is to “be ready when opportunities come up”—luck multiplies when you’ve already put yourself in a position to act.

On longevity at Google, he adopted a lightweight self-check: “I checked every three to six months whether I’d rather be working somewhere else.” The answer kept coming back no—he enjoyed his team, found genuine career growth, and valued the stability. The consistency gave him the accumulated trust that powered his Staff promotion and stands as a deliberate counter-example to rote advice about jumping companies for faster advancement.

**What he’d tell his younger self**

The advice is simple and self-directed: trust yourself more and act on ideas sooner. He feels he spent too much time second-guessing his instincts early on, and that the cost of overthinking was slower movement on things he already sensed were right.
