Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay on efficient communication for software engineers:

**The case for writing over meetings**

Ryan opens with a premise he calls "not controversial": meetings hurt software engineer productivity because they carry an especially high cost for people who need long, uninterrupted stretches of focus. Async communication via writing is one of the best tools to reduce meeting load while still moving information efficiently, and it can help "defragment our calendars."

**Higher baud rate**

The first structural advantage of writing is raw speed—what Ryan frames as a **higher baud rate**. A quick search shows that on average people read roughly 2x faster than they can listen, and the efficiency gap widens when you account for skimming. Readers don't need every word to understand the full message; they can skip unnecessary words and absorb only the important ones without losing the message. Speaking simply cannot be skimmed.

**Easier to reference**

Technical content rarely lands in a single pass. Listening to a presentation makes it easy to miss critical details; written material can be reread for anything you might have missed. Writing is also searchable—"a huge bonus for when you want to look back on the content down the road." A recorded talk, even if archived, does not offer the same low-friction retrieval as a searchable document.

**Preparation drives higher quality**

Ryan's experience is that the time invested in editing prose yields a higher-quality end result than the same time spent prepping a speech. His theory: a good speech requires nailing far more variables—"it's not just what you say but how you say it" (vocal variety, pacing, filler-word discipline)—not just the content. With writing, "you only need to consider the words," so time spent editing goes further than speech rehearsal does. Because there are fewer delivery mechanics to manage, the return on preparation effort is higher.

**Practical recommendations**

Given that writing is often more efficient than speaking, he offers three concrete moves. First, **opt for written information exchange**—postmortems, design discussions, and planning exercises can all happen via write-ups, with shortened real-time discussions scheduled only if genuinely needed. Second, **take meetings offline**—some gatherings, especially status meetings, need no synchronous discussion at all; propose canceling them in favor of async writing. Third, **use writing templates**—never start from a blank slate; structured templates save time and scaffold thinking.

He closes with a concession: speaking still has its place for real-time engagement, sensitive conversations, and building connectedness. But outside those zones, writing is the more effective lever for an engineer's impact.

---

Ryan's framework rests on a single, measurable axis—**baud rate**—and extends it into a practical discipline: treat writing not as a fallback but as the default channel, with meetings reserved for the narrow cases where synchronous voice adds something irreplaceable. His **preparation drives higher quality** insight adds a second, subtler dimension: writing's efficiency isn't just about transmission speed, but about how much quality improvement you get per minute of prep. Together, these form a tight playbook for defragmenting an engineer's calendar without sacrificing information flow.
