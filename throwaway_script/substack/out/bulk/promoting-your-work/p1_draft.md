Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (the author himself sharing his playbook for promoting engineering work to get recognized):

**How much time to spend: the launch-post habit**

Ryan's core mechanism for promotion is simple and low-overhead: always write a summary of results from notable projects. He estimates spending "an hour or two" to put together a launch post, and this is the main way people learned about his work. The deliberate minimalism is the point—you don't need a constant drip of self-promotional content, just a disciplined habit of documenting wins after they ship. An important secondary benefit: these posts double as performance-review artifacts, so you can just link to them later instead of reconstructing impact from memory, making review season "less work."

**Who to share with: relevance over strategy**

He used two distribution channels. First, he **tagged relevant people**—the engineers and managers directly involved in the project. Not a targeted list of influencers, just the people who naturally cared about the work. Second, his org had a **biweekly highlights process**—a post aggregating top wins that many managers read—and his launch posts were often pulled into it organically. The through-line: he never strategized about who to get in front of; he shared with whoever the work was relevant to, and if it was genuinely impactful it spread further on its own ("sometimes I'd get asked to speak at team meetings"). The one non-negotiable: always include your manager, because they need the ammunition to represent your work during performance reviews.

**The launch post template: lead with wins**

The writing advice echoes his broader philosophy on communication—"make the first line interesting." For a launch post, that means the first line should be "a summary of the biggest wins." If the impact is compelling enough, people will be curious to read the details. The structure is wins-first, then the mechanics of what changed. This gets the critical message across to skimmers and increases the chance someone reads the whole thing. Ryan emphasizes that self-promotion is "never a replacement for quality work"—focus almost all your energy on doing great work, then do the incremental work to document and share the results.
