# rStar-Math: Small LLMs Can Master Math Reasoning with Self-Evolved Deep Thinking

**ArXiv:** [2501.04519](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.04519)

## 🎯 Pitch

A 7B language model achieves 90% on the MATH benchmark without any distillation from larger models by using a self-evolving Monte Carlo Tree Search framework, outperforming OpenAI’s o1-preview. This is enabled by training a step-level process reward model on preference pairs rather than direct scores, avoiding the noise that typically collapses such training. Through four iterative rounds where models generate and verify their own training data, the system progressively bootstraps to solve 53.3% of AIME Olympiad problems, matching the top 20% of human competitors.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **rStar-Math**, a self-evolvable System 2 reasoning approach that enables small language models (SLMs) to achieve frontier-level math reasoning by exercising "deep thinking" through Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), where a math policy SLM performs test-time search guided by an SLM-based process reward model. The approach features three named innovations: a **code-augmented CoT data synthesis** method (performing extensive MCTS rollouts to generate step-by-step verified reasoning trajectories, where only steps with successfully executed Python code are retained), a **process preference model (PPM)** training method (constructing step-level preference pairs from Q-values and using a pairwise ranking loss rather than naïvely using Q-values as direct reward labels), and a **four-round self-evolution recipe** (iteratively rebuilding both the policy SLM and PPM from scratch, with each round generating higher-quality training data that progressively tackles more challenging math problems). On the MATH benchmark, rStar-Math boosts Qwen2.5-Math-7B from 58.8% to 90.0% and Phi3-mini-3.8B from 41.4% to 86.4%, surpassing o1-preview by +4.5 and +0.9 percentage points respectively, while on AIME 2024 it solves 53.3% (8/15) of problems — ranking among the top 20% of the brightest high school math students — establishing that SLMs can rival or surpass frontier reasoning models through self-evolved deep thinking only when the policy model attains a reasonably strong capability and the reward model provides reliable step-level guidance through the PPM's preference-based training.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Central Problem: Small Language Models Cannot Do Deep Math Reasoning

The paper confronts a fundamental tension in contemporary AI research: the most visible advances in mathematical reasoning come from massive, often proprietary models — OpenAI's o1 series, GPT-4o, Claude, DeepSeek-V3 — while smaller, more accessible models consistently lag behind, especially on competition-level and Olympiad-level math. This gap is not just a matter of academic curiosity. It carries significant practical implications:

- **Deployment accessibility**: SLMs (1.5B–7B parameters) can run on consumer hardware or modest institutional GPUs, while frontier models require datacenter-scale infrastructure. If SLMs could match frontier reasoning capabilities, advanced math problem-solving would become widely deployable in educational tools, tutoring systems, and research assistance without relying on cloud APIs.
- **Cost and independence**: Distillation from superior models (a dominant paradigm in the field) creates economic and practical dependencies. Every improvement requires access to a more capable teacher model — typically a closed, paid API like GPT-4 or o1. A self-evolution pipeline that generates its own training data breaks this dependency entirely.
- **Scientific understanding**: The gap between large and small model reasoning capabilities is poorly understood. Is it fundamentally a capacity problem (SLMs lack the parameters to encode the necessary knowledge and reasoning patterns), or is it a training data and inference strategy problem (the knowledge exists but isn't effectively accessed)? Answering this question has implications for model architecture design, training resource allocation, and the fundamental limits of small models.

The paper's opening statistics make the gap concrete: Qwen2.5-Math-7B scores only 58.8% on MATH (a high-school competition benchmark) and **0.0%** on AIME 2024 (the American Invitational Mathematics Examination, designed to challenge the top ~2% of high school math students). Phi3-mini-3.8B, a general-purpose SLM, scores 41.4% on MATH. These models are effectively useless for Olympiad-level mathematics despite their substantial pretraining on mathematical content. Yet the same models, augmented with rStar-Math's self-evolved System 2 reasoning, reach 90.0% and 86.4% on MATH respectively — performance levels comparable to o1 — and solve 53.3% of AIME problems. This suggests the capability was latent, not absent.

### Why This Problem Is Important: The System 1 / System 2 Divide

The paper frames its contribution using the cognitive science distinction between **System 1** (fast, intuitive, single-pass generation) and **System 2** (slow, deliberate, multi-step reasoning) thinking, popularized by Daniel Kahneman. In today's LLM landscape:

**System 1 thinking** — having the model generate a complete solution in a single autoregressive pass — is the default deployment mode for essentially all open-source models. It is fast but error-prone, particularly on multi-step reasoning tasks where a single mistake in an intermediate step cascades into a completely wrong final answer. The paper notes that "a correct final answer does not ensure the correctness of the entire reasoning trace" (Section 1), citing Lanham et al. (2023). This is a crucial observation: in math, you can get the right answer through a flawed reasoning process, which means SFT data that only checks final answers includes systematically corrupted intermediate steps.

**System 2 thinking**, as embodied by OpenAI's o1, breaks the reasoning process into explicit steps, evaluates partial progress, branches when uncertain, and self-corrects when errors are detected. This approach has demonstrated dramatic improvements on reasoning benchmarks, but its implementation has remained largely opaque and proprietary. The open-source community has struggled to replicate these gains at scale with smaller models.

The importance of bridging this System 1/System 2 divide with SLMs extends beyond mathematics. The reasoning patterns required — decomposing complex problems, evaluating intermediate progress, self-correcting errors, applying theorems at appropriate moments — are fundamental to scientific reasoning, legal analysis, medical diagnosis, and any domain requiring structured multi-step inference. Mathematics serves as the cleanest testbed because problems have unambiguous correctness criteria (verified answers can be checked automatically), but the reasoning architecture should transfer.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three broad failure modes in existing approaches, each of which motivates a specific component of rStar-Math.

#### Failure Mode 1: Data Synthesis via Distillation Plateaus and Cannot Exceed the Teacher

The dominant strategy for improving LLM math reasoning has been **GPT-distilled chain-of-thought data synthesis**. Models like MetaMath (Yu et al., 2023b) and NuminaMath (Jia LI and Polu, 2024a) use GPT-4 or similar frontier models to generate solution trajectories for training data. This approach has two fundamental limitations:

**The teacher ceiling.** A student model fine-tuned on GPT-4-generated solutions cannot, by construction, exceed GPT-4's reasoning capability. Any problem that GPT-4 cannot solve is excluded from the training set entirely. As the paper puts it: "Hard problems that the teacher LLM cannot solve are excluded in the training set" (Section 2). This creates a self-reinforcing limitation — the model never sees examples of the reasoning patterns that would be required to solve problems beyond the teacher's ability.

**Intermediate step contamination.** Even for solvable problems, GPT-4's solutions contain flawed intermediate steps. The paper notes that in math reasoning, the final answer being correct does not guarantee that every step along the way is logically sound. A student model trained on these trajectories learns from corrupted demonstrations, internalizing reasoning patterns that include errors. Rejection sampling methods (Yuan et al., 2023) can filter based on final answer correctness but cannot detect errors in intermediate steps that happen to lead to the correct answer by chance.

The consequences are quantifiable: the paper cites OpenMathInstruct-2 (Toshniwal et al., 2024), which saw only a 3.9% improvement on MATH despite an 8× increase in dataset size. The distillation-based synthesis approach shows **diminishing returns** — you cannot simply scale your way to frontier reasoning by generating more GPT-4 solutions, because the bottleneck is data quality, not quantity.

#### Failure Mode 2: Process Reward Models Remain an Unsolved Challenge

The paper identifies process reward models (PRMs) as a particularly attractive but stubbornly difficult technology. Unlike outcome reward models (ORMs) that provide a single score for a complete solution, PRMs evaluate each intermediate step, offering dense feedback signals that could guide both training and test-time search. The appeal is clear: with step-level feedback, a search algorithm can prune unpromising reasoning paths early rather than waiting until the final answer is reached, dramatically improving search efficiency.

However, the paper argues that training effective PRMs has proven extremely difficult for two reasons:

**Human annotation doesn't scale.** The gold standard for PRM training data — used in Lightman et al. (2023)'s seminal work "Let's Verify Step by Step" — is human-labeled step-level correctness annotations. The PRM800k dataset provides such labels, but the labeling cost is astronomical (the dataset was created by hiring dozens of human labelers to evaluate individual reasoning steps). For a self-evolution pipeline that needs to continuously generate new training data over multiple rounds, relying on human annotation is completely impractical.

**Automatic annotation produces noisy labels.** More recent approaches attempt to bypass human labeling by using Monte Carlo sampling or MCTS to automatically assign scores to intermediate steps (Luo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024c; Chen et al., 2024). The idea is intuitive: if a partial solution frequently leads to correct final answers in rollouts, assign it a high score; if it rarely does, assign a low score. The paper acknowledges this approach in principle but identifies a critical subtlety: **the resulting Q-values, while informative about relative step quality, are too imprecise for direct use as regression targets.** The paper explains this precisely (Section 3.3):

> "For instance, among a set of correct steps, it is difficult to rank them as best, second-best, or average and then assign precise scores. Similarly, among incorrect steps, differentiating the worst from moderately poor steps poses analogous challenges. Even expert human annotation struggles with consistency, particularly at scale, leading to inherent noise in training labels."

Training a PRM to predict these noisy Q-values via MSE loss (as in Chen et al., 2024) or pointwise loss (as in Luo et al., 2024) produces models whose predictions inherit this noise, limiting their effectiveness at guiding search. The quantities are meaningful as **relative preferences** (step A is better than step B) but unreliable as **absolute scores** (step A should be scored 0.73). This distinction — between preference and regression — is the key insight that motivates the paper's PPM approach.

#### Failure Mode 3: Test-Time Compute Scaling Lacks the Infrastructure to Be Effective

The concept of **test-time compute scaling** — allocating additional computation at inference rather than during training — has gained significant attention recently, particularly following Snell et al. (2024). The basic idea is that if you can generate multiple candidate solutions and select the best one using a verifier, you can trade inference compute for accuracy in a way that may be more efficient than training a larger model.

However, the paper observes that "open-source methods for scaling test-time computation have shown limited gains in math reasoning, often due to policy LLM or reward model limitations" (Section 2). This is not a failure of the concept but a failure of the components: a weak policy model cannot generate promising candidates regardless of how many rollouts are performed, and a weak reward model cannot reliably distinguish good solutions from bad ones. The prior work in this area — tree-of-thoughts (Yao et al., 2024), self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023), best-of-N (Brown et al., 2024) — all assume either a capable policy model, a capable verifier, or both. For SLMs, neither assumption holds out of the box.

The paper's diagnosis is that prior test-time compute approaches treat the policy model and reward model as fixed, given components. They do not address the **co-evolution problem**: how do you bootstrap from weak initial models to strong ones when each component's quality depends on the other? A weak policy model generates poor training data for the reward model, and a weak reward model provides poor guidance for improving the policy model. Breaking this chicken-and-egg problem requires a structured self-evolution process, which is exactly what rStar-Math's four-round recipe provides.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

rStar-Math positions itself at the intersection of two research threads — **self-generated training data** and **test-time compute scaling** — while addressing the specific failure modes described above. Its positioning can be understood through several key claims:

**Against distillation: self-evolution replaces teacher dependence.** Rather than relying on GPT-4 or other frontier models for training data, rStar-Math demonstrates that SLMs can generate their own high-quality training data through MCTS-guided search. The paper frames this as a fundamental philosophical shift: the goal is not to compress a larger model's knowledge into a smaller one (distillation), but to enable the smaller model to discover reasoning strategies through systematic exploration and self-verification. The paper explicitly states this in the abstract: "rStar-Math... demonstrate[s] that small language models (SLMs) can rival or even surpass the math reasoning capability of OpenAI o1, without distillation from superior models."

**Against existing PRM training: preference beats regression.** The PPM is presented as a novel departure from prior process reward modeling approaches. Instead of trying to predict noisy Q-values directly, the PPM learns to rank steps — which positive steps are better than which negative steps — using a pairwise Bradley-Terry ranking loss. This reframes the problem from a regression task (predict the exact score) to a preference learning task (predict which of two steps is better), which the paper argues is more robust to annotation noise because relative judgments are more reliable than absolute scores.

**Against static test-time compute: co-evolution enables progressive improvement.** The four-round self-evolution recipe is not just an engineering optimization; it is the paper's answer to the chicken-and-egg problem described above. Each round uses the models from the previous round to generate better training data, which trains better models for the next round. The technical report quantifies this progression in Table 2: the percentage of the 747k training problems that can be solved (i.e., for which at least one correct trajectory is generated) increases from 60.17% in Round 1 (using a 236B bootstrap model) to 90.25% in Round 4 (using the 7B self-evolved models). Crucially, the transition to PPM-augmented MCTS in Round 3 dramatically increases capability on Olympiad-level problems (from 56.04% to 62.16% solved, then to 80.58% in Round 4), demonstrating that the reward model quality directly determines data quality for challenging problems.

**The specific gap this paper fills.** Prior to rStar-Math, there was no demonstrated path for a small (≤7B) language model to achieve o1-level math reasoning through self-generated data alone. Systems achieving strong performance on MATH and AIME either used much larger models (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct at 85.6% on MATH) or relied on distillation from frontier models. The paper's contribution is demonstrating that the capability gap is **not primarily a capacity limitation** of small models but rather a **training and inference strategy limitation** — and providing a concrete, reproducible recipe for closing it.

This positioning is reinforced by the paper's choice to evaluate across four SLMs of different sizes (1.5B to 7B) and architectures (general-purpose Phi3-mini and math-specialized Qwen variants), showing that the methodology transfers across model families and that the gains are not specific to any one base model. The inclusion of Phi3-mini-3.8B — a general-purpose model without math specialization — is particularly telling: it demonstrates that the self-evolution pipeline can instill mathematical reasoning capability in a model not specifically pretrained for it, implying the method teaches reasoning strategies rather than merely amplifying existing math-specific knowledge.

### The Intellectual Lineage: Prior Work the Paper Builds Upon

While rStar-Math is presented as a self-contained system, it draws on several lines of prior work that the reader should understand to appreciate what is novel versus what is adapted:

**From rStar (Qi et al., 2024):** The paper builds directly on the MCTS-based mutual reasoning framework introduced in rStar, which demonstrated that smaller LLMs could improve their problem-solving through MCTS-guided search. rStar-Math extends this in two critical ways: (1) introducing code execution as a verification mechanism for intermediate steps, replacing pure natural language generation which is harder to verify automatically, and (2) introducing the self-evolution loop that progressively improves both the policy model and reward model, rather than using fixed models.

**From AlphaGo-style MCTS (Silver et al., 2017):** The terminal-guided Q-value annotation scheme — where leaf nodes receive +1 for correct answers and −1 otherwise, with these values back-propagated up the tree — is adapted directly from AlphaGo's approach to evaluating board positions based on win/loss outcomes. The key adaptation is that in AlphaGo, terminal states are unambiguous (game outcomes are determined by the rules), while in math reasoning, terminal answer correctness must be verified against ground-truth labels.

**From process reward modeling (Lightman et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024c):** The paper inherits the problem formulation — that step-level feedback is more valuable than outcome-level feedback for complex reasoning — from the PRM literature. The PPM innovation is in the training methodology (preference pairs instead of regression) rather than in the problem formulation itself.

**From Bradley-Terry preference modeling (Ouyang et al., 2022):** The pairwise ranking loss used for PPM training is the same loss function used in RLHF for training reward models from human preference data. The novelty is applying it to automatically constructed step-level preference pairs derived from MCTS Q-values rather than human pairwise judgments.

**From code-augmented reasoning (Wang et al., 2024b; Gou et al., 2023):** The idea of interleaving natural language reasoning with executable code is not new — tools like ToRA and MathCoder demonstrated that code execution improves mathematical reasoning accuracy by catching calculation errors. rStar-Math's contribution is integrating this into the MCTS framework as a **filtering mechanism**: only nodes with successfully executed code are retained as valid candidates, which both eliminates obviously flawed steps and provides a dense verification signal during the search process.

### The Resource Constraint Narrative

An understated but practically important aspect of the paper's motivation is the **resource constraint** under which this work was conducted. The acknowledgments section reveals: "In the early stages of this work, we faced significant challenges due to limited GPU resources and restricted access to the GPT-4 API." The self-evolution pipeline — using 4×40GB A100 GPUs for rounds 2–4 data generation — is designed to be accessible to academic research groups without industrial-scale compute. This constraint shaped the methodology: the choice of SLMs (≤7B) was not just a research question but a practical necessity, and the development of self-generation techniques was forced by limited API access.

This context matters because it demonstrates that the results are not contingent on massive compute — the entire 4-round self-evolution pipeline, including training multiple 7B models and running MCTS on 747k problems, runs on hardware that a well-funded university lab could assemble. The paper implicitly argues that the bottleneck for SLM math reasoning is not GPU budget but algorithmic design: with the right data synthesis and verification methods, accessible hardware can produce frontier-level results.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper describes a system — **rStar-Math** — that enables small language models (1.5B–7B parameters) to solve challenging math problems at a level comparable to OpenAI's o1, without ever using a superior model to generate training data. The problem it solves is a chicken-and-egg bootstrapping challenge: a weak SLM cannot generate high-quality training data for itself, yet improving the SLM requires high-quality training data. The solution is a **self-evolution pipeline** where Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), guided by a progressively improving reward model, systematically explores solution spaces, verifies intermediate steps through code execution, and generates increasingly higher-quality step-by-step reasoning trajectories that train the next generation of models — with this cycle repeating four times, each round producing stronger policy and reward models that in turn generate better data for the next round.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

rStar-Math consists of four interconnected components that operate in a cyclical self-improvement loop:

1. **Policy SLM (the "generator")** — a small language model (Qwen2.5-Math-7B, or similar) fine-tuned to generate one-step math reasoning in a code-augmented format. At inference time, it proposes candidate next steps during MCTS; at training time, it is supervised fine-tuned on high-quality trajectories harvested from MCTS rollouts.

2. **Process Preference Model — PPM (the "evaluator")** — a second SLM (also ≤7B, initialized from the policy model's weights) that scores partial reasoning trajectories on a scale from −1 to +1, providing step-level feedback during MCTS to guide the search toward promising solution paths. The PPM is trained on preference pairs constructed from MCTS Q-values, learning to rank good steps above bad steps rather than predict exact scores.

3. **Monte Carlo Tree Search Engine (the "search procedure")** — the inference-time algorithm that interleaves policy-guided generation with PPM-guided evaluation. Starting from a math problem, MCTS iteratively builds a search tree by selecting promising branches, generating candidate next steps, executing Python code to filter invalid steps, scoring retained steps with the PPM, and back-propagating terminal outcomes to update Q-values. The search runs for 4–16 rollouts per problem during training and 4 rollouts during evaluation.

4. **Self-Evolution Pipeline (the "meta-trainer")** — the overarching four-round procedure that orchestrates the co-improvement of the policy SLM and PPM. Each round: (a) uses the current best policy model and PPM to run MCTS on 747k math problems, generating step-by-step verified reasoning trajectories; (b) filters these trajectories using Q-value-based quality heuristics; (c) fine-tunes a new policy SLM from the base model on top-ranked trajectories; (d) trains a new PPM from the policy model using step-level preference pairs; (e) deploys these stronger models in the next round's MCTS data generation, progressively solving harder problems and expanding the training set coverage from 60.17% to 90.25% of the problem pool.

Information flow during training: a math problem enters the MCTS engine → the policy SLM generates candidate steps with embedded Python code → code execution filters invalid steps → the PPM scores surviving steps → the UCT formula balances exploration and exploitation to select the next node → rollouts proceed until terminal answers are reached → terminal answers are verified against ground-truth labels → Q-values are back-propagated up the tree → high-Q trajectories are extracted and filtered → the policy SLM is fine-tuned on selected trajectories → preference pairs are constructed from Q-value comparisons → the PPM is trained on these pairs → the improved models are deployed in the next round's MCTS.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and training methodology paper** whose core idea is that small language models can bootstrap themselves to frontier math reasoning capability through a self-evolution process where MCTS with code-augmented generation produces increasingly high-quality training data, and a process preference model trained on relative step comparisons provides reliable step-level guidance during search. The three named innovations — code-augmented CoT, PPM training via preference pairs, and the four-round self-evolution recipe — are each necessary components of a unified pipeline that progressively overcomes the bootstrap problem.

---

#### Code-Augmented CoT Generation with MCTS Node Expansion

**The generation format.** At each step of MCTS, the policy SLM does not generate free-form natural language reasoning. Instead, it produces a structured output containing two elements: (1) **one step of natural language chain-of-thought reasoning**, embedded as Python comments (preceded by `#`), and (2) the corresponding **executable Python code** that implements the reasoning. The paper's Figure 2 provides an example: for the step "Calculate the total distance walked south," the model generates:

```python
# Step 1: Calculate the total distance walked south
total_south = 1/2 + 1/2
```

This interleaving of natural language explanation and executable computation is the "code-augmented" aspect. The natural language provides the human-legible reasoning trace, while the Python code provides a machine-executable verification signal — the step is not just stated, it is computed.

**Why this format over pure natural language?** The paper identifies a fundamental vulnerability in natural-language-only reasoning: "LLMs often suffer from hallucination, producing incorrect or irrelevant steps yet still arrive at the correct answer by chance" (Section 3.2). A model might write "the sum of 1/2 and 1/2 is 1" and proceed correctly, or it might write "the sum of 1/2 and 1/2 is 2" and then miraculously recover the correct answer through a subsequent error. In a pure NL setting, detecting the intermediate error is extremely difficult — it requires semantic understanding of whether a reasoning step is logically coherent, which is precisely the capability that is absent in a weak SLM. Code execution provides an objective, mechanical filter: if the Python code runs without errors, the computational aspect of the step is at least internally consistent. Critically, code execution catches the most common failure modes — undefined variables, type errors, incorrect library imports, arithmetic mistakes — without requiring any model to "understand" the reasoning.

**Node expansion mechanics.** MCTS operates on a tree where each node represents a partial solution. The root node is the math problem text `$x$`. At step `$i$`, the current state is the concatenation of the problem and all previous steps: `$x \oplus s_1 \oplus s_2 \oplus ... \oplus s_{i-1}$`. The policy model receives this state as input and generates `$n$` candidate next steps `$s_{i,0}, s_{i,1}, ..., s_{i,n-1}$`, where `$n = 8$` by default (increased to 16 in Round 4 for challenging problems, and reduced to 5 in the bootstrap round for efficiency with the large 236B model). Each candidate is a complete code block with NL comments.

**The code execution filter.** Before any candidate is evaluated by the PPM or added to the tree, it must pass a code execution test. The candidate step `$s_{i,j}$` is concatenated with the code from all previous steps, forming a complete runnable script: `$s_1 \oplus s_2 \oplus ... \oplus s_{i-1} \oplus s_{i,j}$`. This full concatenation is executed in a Python interpreter. If execution succeeds (no exceptions, no `SyntaxError`, no `NameError`, no `TypeError`), the node is retained as a **valid candidate**. If execution fails, the node is discarded — it represents a step that introduces an execution error and therefore cannot be part of a valid solution. This is the paper's key mechanism for "mitigating errors in intermediate steps" (Section 1).

The paper does not explicitly report what fraction of generated candidates pass the execution filter, but the effectiveness of this mechanism can be inferred from the progressive improvement in training data quality across rounds — the filter eliminates obviously broken generations, while the PPM (once trained) provides the finer-grained quality discrimination among executable candidates.

**The UCT selection formula.** Among the valid candidates at a given step, the algorithm must decide which node to explore further. The selection uses the Upper Confidence bounds for Trees (UCT) formula, which balances exploitation (choosing nodes that have shown high reward) with exploration (choosing nodes that have been visited less frequently). The formula is:

$$UCT(s) = Q(s) + c \sqrt{\frac{\ln N_{\text{parent}}(s)}{N(s)}}$$

where:
- `$Q(s)$` is the average Q-value of node `$s$`, defined as `$Q(s) = q(s) / N(s)$` where `$q(s)$` is the accumulated total reward at the node and `$N(s)$` is the number of times the node has been visited,
- `$N_{\text{parent}}(s)$` is the visit count of `$s$`'s parent node,
- `$c$` is the exploration constant, set to `$c = 2$` throughout the paper to "promote greater exploration" (Appendix A.1).

**What UCT computes.** The first term `$Q(s)$` is the exploitation component — it favors nodes that, based on past experience, have led to correct answers. The second term `$c \sqrt{\frac{\ln N_{\text{parent}}(s)}{N(s)}}$` is the exploration bonus — it grows when a node's parent has been visited many times but the node itself has been visited relatively few times, indicating uncertainty about its true value. The square root of the log-ratio creates an exploration bonus that grows logarithmically with parent visits but shrinks as the node itself is visited more, ensuring that eventually exploitation dominates for well-explored branches.

**Why this form.** UCT is the standard bandit-based selection formula for MCTS, introduced in Kocsis and Szepesvári (2006) and widely used in game-playing systems like AlphaGo. The theoretical property that matters is that UCT provides asymptotic convergence guarantees — as the number of rollouts goes to infinity, the probability of selecting a suboptimal action goes to zero at a polynomial rate. In the math reasoning context, this property means that with sufficient rollouts, the search will not miss promising solution paths even if they initially appear unpromising due to sampling noise. The exploration constant `$c = 2$` is higher than the typical default of `$\sqrt{2}$` used in many MCTS implementations, reflecting the paper's deliberate choice to favor exploration over exploitation — which makes sense given that the initial Q-value estimates are unreliable (especially in rounds 1–2 before the PPM is trained).

**The complete node selection procedure at step `$i$`.** The process is: (1) the policy model generates `$n$` candidate steps from the current state; (2) each candidate's code is appended to the code from all previous steps and executed; (3) candidates that fail execution are discarded; (4) surviving candidates are scored by the PPM (if available) to obtain an initial `$q(s_{i,j})_0$` value, or assigned `$q(s_{i,j})_0 = 0$` if no PPM is available; (5) the UCT formula is computed for each candidate; (6) the candidate with the highest UCT value is selected for expansion. This procedure is repeated at each step of the tree until a terminal node is reached.

**Terminal nodes.** A terminal node is reached when the policy model generates a final answer step. The paper does not specify exactly how the model signals "this is the final answer," but based on the example in Figure 2 and the prompt template in Appendix A.3, the final step typically contains a `print()` statement that outputs a numerical value and is followed by an `<answer>` tag with a boxed answer. During training data generation, terminal nodes are evaluated against ground-truth labels: `$q(s_d) = 1$` if the answer is correct, `$q(s_d) = -1$` otherwise. During evaluation (inference), ground truth is not available, so the PPM's score on the terminal node is used as the final quality signal.

#### Q-Value Annotation: Terminal-Guided and PPM-Augmented

**The Q-value annotation problem.** During MCTS, each intermediate step needs a quality signal — the Q-value — that represents the expected future reward from that step. This Q-value serves two purposes: (1) it guides node selection during the current MCTS through the UCT formula, and (2) after the search is complete, it provides the per-step quality annotations used to select high-quality trajectories for SFT training data and to construct preference pairs for PPM training. The accuracy of these Q-values directly determines the quality of the training data and therefore the effectiveness of the entire self-evolution pipeline.

**Why extensive rollouts matter.** A single rollout through the tree provides very noisy Q-value estimates — a step that happens to lead to a correct answer in one trajectory might be mediocre, while a step that leads to an incorrect answer might be excellent but unlucky in the downstream sampling. The paper's solution, inspired by Go players who "retrospectively evaluate the reward of each move based on game outcomes" through "repeated gameplay" (Section 3.2), is to run **many rollouts** — 16 rollouts per problem by default (8 in the bootstrap round, up to 128 in Round 4 for unsolved Olympiad problems) — and aggregate the terminal outcomes across all trajectories that pass through each step. Steps that consistently lead to correct answers accumulate high Q-values; steps that occasionally succeed receive moderate Q-values; steps that never succeed receive low or negative Q-values.

**Terminal-guided annotation (used in Rounds 1–2).** When a PPM is either unavailable (Round 1) or insufficiently accurate (Round 1's PPM-r1, trained on limited 8-rollout data), the Q-value annotation relies entirely on terminal node outcomes. The formal update rule after the `$k$`-th rollout is:

$$q(s_i)_k = q(s_i)_{k-1} + q(s_d)_k$$

where:
- `$q(s_i)_k$` is the accumulated Q-value at step `$i$` after the `$k$`-th rollout,
- `$q(s_i)_{k-1}$` is the accumulated Q-value from the previous `$k-1$` rollouts,
- `$q(s_d)_k$` is the terminal node's reward in the `$k$`-th rollout: `$+1$` if the trajectory reaches the correct answer, `$-1$` if it reaches an incorrect answer.

**What this computes.** This is a simple additive accumulation: every time a rollout passes through step `$i$` and eventually reaches a correct answer, `$q(s_i)$` increases by 1; every time it reaches an incorrect answer, `$q(s_i)$` decreases by 1. The average Q-value `$Q(s_i) = q(s_i) / N(s_i)$` is then the fraction of correct-outcome rollouts minus the fraction of incorrect-outcome rollouts, mapped to the range `$[-1, 1]$` (if all rollouts are correct, `$Q = 1$`; if all are incorrect, `$Q = -1$`; if half and half, `$Q = 0$`).

**Why this form.** This terminal-guided annotation scheme is directly adapted from AlphaGo (Silver et al., 2017), where board positions are assigned value based on whether the game is eventually won or lost. The critical property is that **no learned evaluation function is needed** — the Q-values are entirely derived from terminal outcomes, which are verified against ground-truth labels. This makes the scheme usable even in the bootstrap round when no reward model exists. However, it has a significant weakness: in early rollouts, before a node has been visited many times, the Q-value estimate is extremely noisy. A step that would be excellent might be visited only once in the first few rollouts, and if that particular rollout happens to fail due to downstream errors (not the step's fault), the step gets a spuriously low Q-value that may cause it to be underexplored in subsequent rollouts.

**PPM-augmented annotation (used in Rounds 3–4).** Once a reliable PPM is available (PPM-r2 and later), each step receives an initial score `$q(s_i)_0$` from the PPM before any rollouts are performed:

$$q(s_i)_0 = \text{PPM}(x \oplus s_1 \oplus s_2 \oplus ... \oplus s_{i-1} \oplus s_i)$$

The PPM takes as input the complete partial trajectory from the root to the current step and outputs a scalar value in `$[-1, 1]$`. This initial score is then updated through MCTS back-propagation using the same terminal-guided update rule:

$$q(s_i)_k = q(s_i)_{k-1} + q(s_d)_k$$

**What this changes.** The PPM provides a **non-zero initial Q-value** before any rollouts begin. In terminal-guided annotation, `$q(s_i)_0 = 0$` for all steps, meaning the UCT formula initially provides no exploitation signal — all nodes appear equally promising, and selection is driven entirely by the exploration term until sufficient rollouts have accumulated. This is inefficient because it treats obviously bad steps (e.g., steps that introduce algebraic errors) the same as promising steps until proven otherwise. With PPM-augmented annotation, the PPM's initial score immediately biases search toward steps that are semantically likely to be correct, dramatically improving the efficiency of the search. The paper reports that the transition to PPM-augmented MCTS in Round 3 leads to "significantly higher-quality trajectories that cover more math and Olympiad-level problems in the training set" (Section 3.4.2), with Olympiad-level problem success rate jumping from 56.04% in Round 2 to 62.16% in Round 3 (Table 2).

**Why the PPM is not used for terminal scoring during training.** An important design choice: during training data generation, terminal nodes are still scored using ground-truth labels (`$+1$` or `$-1$`), not the PPM. The paper states: "For terminal node `$s_d$`, we do not use PRM for scoring during training data generation. Instead, we assign a more accurate score based on ground truth labels as terminal-guided rewarding" (Section 3.2). This ensures that the terminal reward signal is noise-free — there is no risk of the PPM incorrectly classifying a correct answer as wrong or vice versa. The PPM's role is limited to guiding intermediate node selection and providing initial `$q(s_i)_0$` values, while the ultimate correctness signal comes from objective ground-truth verification.

**The extensive rollout commitment.** The paper performs 16 MCTS rollouts per problem by default, with additional rollouts (up to 128) in Round 4 for problems that remain unsolved after 16 rollouts. The cost of this commitment is significant: Appendix A.1 reports that data generation in rounds 2–4 used 15 nodes of 4×40GB A100 GPUs, with each round taking about 3 days, and Round 4 extending to 1 week due to increased rollouts. The return on this investment is the progressively improving Q-value accuracy, which directly determines the quality of the training data extracted from the search trees.

#### Process Preference Model (PPM) Training

**The core insight: preference beats regression.** The paper's key methodological contribution for reward modeling is reframing the training objective from **score regression** (predict the exact Q-value of each step) to **preference ranking** (predict which of two steps is better). This reframing is motivated by a subtle but critical observation about Q-value quality: "although Q-values are still not precise enough to score each reasoning step despite using extensive MCTS rollouts, the Q-values can reliably distinguish positive (correct) steps from negative (irrelevant/incorrect) ones" (Section 1). In other words, the Q-values contain a strong **ordinal signal** (step A is better than step B) but a weak **cardinal signal** (step A should be scored 0.73). Training a model to regress onto precise numerical values forces it to fit noise in the cardinal signal; training it to produce relative rankings only requires fitting the ordinal signal, which is much more robust.

**Preference pair construction.** For each math problem that has a mix of correct and incorrect trajectories in its MCTS tree, the PPM training data is constructed as follows. At each step position `$i$` (for all steps except the final answer step), two positive candidates and two negative candidates are selected from the MCTS tree:

- **Positive candidates:** the two candidates `$s^{\text{pos}}$` with the highest Q-values among all candidates at that step position that appear in trajectories leading to a correct final answer. The requirement that the trajectory must be correct is crucial — a step might have a high Q-value by chance but belong to a trajectory that ultimately fails; such steps are excluded from the positive set.

- **Negative candidates:** the two candidates `$s^{\text{neg}}$` with the lowest Q-values among all candidates at that step position that appear in trajectories leading to an incorrect final answer. Similarly, only genuinely bad steps (in trajectories that fail) are selected.

- **Shared prefix constraint.** For intermediate steps (all steps except the final answer step), the positive and negative candidates must share the same preceding steps: `$y^{\text{pos}}_i = s_1 \oplus ... \oplus s_{i-1} \oplus s^{\text{pos}}_i$` and `$y^{\text{neg}}_i = s_1 \oplus ... \oplus s_{i-1} \oplus s^{\text{neg}}_i$`. This ensures that the comparison isolates the quality of step `$i$` specifically — both candidates are evaluated in the same context, differing only in the choice at step `$i$`. Without this constraint, the model might learn to distinguish problems rather than steps.

**Final answer step exception.** For the final answer step, the shared-prefix constraint is typically impossible because "identical reasoning trajectories rarely yield different final answers" (Section 3.3). Instead, the paper selects two complete correct trajectories with the highest average Q-values as positive examples and two complete incorrect trajectories with the lowest average Q-values as negative examples. The comparison is at the full-trajectory level rather than the step level.

**The Bradley-Terry pairwise ranking loss.** The PPM is trained using the standard Bradley-Terry preference model with a pairwise ranking loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ppm}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{2 \times 2} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y^{\text{pos}}_i, y^{\text{neg}}_i) \in \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \left( \sigma \left( r_\theta(x, y^{\text{pos}}_i) - r_\theta(x, y^{\text{neg}}_i) \right) \right) \right]$$

where:
- `$\mathcal{D}$` is the set of preference pairs constructed as described above, with `$2$` positive and `$2$` negative examples per step position, yielding `$2 \times 2 = 4$` comparisons per step,
- `$r_\theta(x, y_i)$` is the PPM's scalar output for the partial trajectory `$y_i$` from the problem `$x$` up to step `$i$`,
- `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the logistic sigmoid function `$\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$`.

**What this loss computes.** For each preference pair, the loss compares the PPM's scores for the positive and negative examples. If `$r_\theta(x, y^{\text{pos}}_i) > r_\theta(x, y^{\text{neg}}_i)$`, the difference is positive, `$\sigma$` is close to 1, `$\log(\sigma)$` is close to 0, and the loss contribution is small — the model is correctly ranking the positive step above the negative step. If `$r_\theta(x, y^{\text{pos}}_i) \ll r_\theta(x, y^{\text{neg}}_i)$`, the difference is largely negative, `$\sigma$` is close to 0, `$\log(\sigma)$` is largely negative, and the loss contribution is large — the model is making a ranking error. The expectation over `$(x, y^{\text{pos}}_i, y^{\text{neg}}_i)$` averages over all constructed preference pairs.

**Why this form.** The Bradley-Terry model with pairwise ranking loss is the same approach used in RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) for training reward models from human preference comparisons. The key property that makes it suitable here is that it only requires **relative judgments** — "A is better than B" — rather than **absolute scores** — "A should be scored 0.73." The model learns to output scores that are monotonically related to step quality without being calibrated to any specific numerical scale. This is precisely what is needed: during MCTS node selection, the PPM's scores are used to compare candidates (via UCT), so what matters is the ordering, not the absolute values.

**Contrast with the Q-value regression alternative (PQM).** The paper directly ablates against a Process Q-value Model (PQM) trained to regress onto Q-values using mean squared error:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{prm}}(x) = \| \mathbf{Q} - \mathbf{Q}' \|^2$$

where `$\mathbf{Q} = (Q(s_1), Q(s_2), ..., Q(s_d))$` are the annotated Q-values and `$\mathbf{Q}' = (Q'(s_1), Q'(s_2), ..., Q'(s_d))$` are the model's predictions. The paper finds that PQM "struggles on more challenging benchmarks, such as MATH and Olympiad Bench, due to the inherent imprecision of Q-values" (Section 4.3, Table 8). On MATH, PPM achieves 89.4% while PQM achieves 88.2%; on AIME 2024, PPM achieves 50.0% while PQM achieves 46.7%. The gap grows on more difficult benchmarks, consistent with the hypothesis that Q-value noise is more severe for harder problems where rollouts are sparser.

**PPM architecture.** The PPM is initialized from the fine-tuned policy model's weights, with the next-token prediction head (the language modeling head) replaced by a **scalar-value head**. This head consists of a single linear layer followed by a `tanh` activation function that constrains outputs to the range `$[-1, 1]$`. The paper does not specify the exact dimension of this linear layer, but it is standard to pool the final hidden state (e.g., by taking the embedding of the last token) and project it through the linear layer. Initializing from the policy model rather than from scratch provides the PPM with the policy model's mathematical knowledge, which is essential for evaluating reasoning quality — the PPM needs to understand the math to judge whether a step is logically sound, not just syntactically correct.

**Training hyperparameters.** The PPM is trained for 1 epoch with a batch size of 512. The learning rate is `$7 \times 10^{-6}$` using the AdamW optimizer. The training data is filtered to only include math problems where the MCTS generated both correct and incorrect solutions — problems where all solutions are correct or all are incorrect provide no preference signal. The paper reports that this filtering reduces the training set but the remaining problems provide cleaner training signal.

**Why PPM quality progressively improves across rounds (Table 4).** The paper provides a carefully controlled comparison: fixing the policy model at policy SLM-r1 and evaluating different PPM versions (PPM-r1 through PPM-r4) on MATH. PPM-r1 scores 75.2%, PPM-r2 scores 84.1% (+8.9 percentage points), PPM-r3 scores 85.2% (+1.1 points), and PPM-r4 scores 87.0% (+1.8 points). The large jump from r1 to r2 confirms that the limited 8-rollout data in Round 1 produced unreliable Q-values that contaminated PPM-r1's training. The 16-rollout data in Round 2 produced substantially better PPM training data, and subsequent rounds provided incremental improvements as the policy model generating the training data improved. This progressive improvement is essential for the self-evolution loop to work — if the PPM quality plateaued after Round 2, the self-evolution would stall.

#### Step-by-Step Verified Reasoning Trajectory Selection for SFT

**The trajectory quality problem.** After MCTS completes on a training problem, the search tree contains many trajectories — some correct (terminal node scored +1), some incorrect (terminal node scored −1). Not all correct trajectories are equally valuable for SFT training. A trajectory might reach the correct answer but contain error-prone or suboptimal intermediate steps that happen to not derail the solution. Training the policy model on such trajectories would teach the model to generate those suboptimal patterns, degrading rather than improving its reasoning capability.

**Q-value-based trajectory ranking.** The paper uses per-step Q-values to assess overall trajectory quality. For each trajectory `$t = x \oplus s_1 \oplus s_2 \oplus ... \oplus s_d$`, the average Q-value across all steps is computed:

$$\bar{Q}(t) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^{d} Q(s_i)$$

where `$Q(s_i) = q(s_i) / N(s_i)$` is the average Q-value at step `$i$` after all rollouts. This average Q-value serves as a holistic quality metric: a trajectory where every step had high Q-value (each step consistently led to correct answers across many rollouts) gets a high average, while a trajectory where some steps had borderline Q-values gets a lower average.

**Selection criteria.** For each math problem, the paper selects the **top-2 trajectories with the highest average Q-values among those leading to correct answers**. The "leading to correct answers" constraint is essential — it ensures that the selected trajectories are demonstrably correct (the terminal answer matches the ground truth), while the Q-value ranking ensures that among correct trajectories, only those with uniformly high-quality intermediate steps are retained.

**Why top-2?** The paper does not explicitly justify the choice of 2 trajectories, but the reasoning can be inferred: selecting only 1 trajectory per problem would maximize quality but severely limit data diversity — the policy model would see only one solution path per problem and might overfit to that specific approach. Selecting more trajectories (e.g., 5 or 10) would increase diversity but risk including trajectories with noisier intermediate steps, which could degrade the training signal. Top-2 represents a practical tradeoff that balances quality and diversity.

**Problem difficulty categorization.** After MCTS completes, each problem is assigned a difficulty category based on the correctness rate of its `$N$` generated trajectories:
- **Easy:** all `$N$` trajectories are correct (correctness rate = 100%)
- **Medium:** a mix of correct and incorrect trajectories (`$0 < \text{correctness rate} < 1$`)
- **Hard:** all `$N$` trajectories are incorrect (correctness rate = 0%)

This categorization serves a practical purpose in the PPM training pipeline: only medium-difficulty problems provide useful preference pairs (positive and negative examples exist within the same problem). Easy and hard problems are excluded from PPM training data. For the policy SLM SFT data, all difficulty levels are used, but hard problems that have no correct trajectories after the initial 16 rollouts receive additional MCTS with up to 128 rollouts in Round 4 to attempt to generate at least one correct trajectory for training.

**The importance of this selection for final model quality.** The paper's ablation study (Table 7) demonstrates that fine-tuning with step-by-step verified trajectories significantly outperforms alternatives. Qwen2.5-Math-7B fine-tuned on these trajectories achieves 78.4% on MATH (System 1/greedy), compared to 73.4% with rejection sampling (where an ORM ranks complete solutions, providing only sparse verification) and 72.4% with random sampling from the same policy model. The 5.0 percentage point gap between step-by-step verified and rejection sampling is attributed to the "denser verification during math solution generation" — the step-level Q-values eliminate trajectories where some steps are good and others are bad but the final answer is coincidentally correct, which rejection sampling would include.

**Code-augmented CoT's role in this pipeline.** The paper emphasizes that even randomly sampled code-augmented CoT solutions from policy SLM-r3 already outperform GPT-4 synthesized datasets like NuminaMath and MetaMath (Table 7). This is an important validation: it shows that the self-evolved policy model, after rounds of MCTS-guided improvement, generates higher-quality solutions than GPT-4 — not because the 7B model is "smarter" than GPT-4, but because the code execution filter and MCTS verification produce cleaner training data with fewer hidden errors in intermediate steps. The GPT-4 synthesized datasets, by contrast, contain unchecked intermediate errors that contaminate the SFT signal.

#### The Four-Round Self-Evolution Recipe

**The bootstrap problem.** The four-round recipe is designed to solve the fundamental chicken-and-egg problem: a weak initial SLM cannot generate high-quality training data (it produces mostly incorrect solutions with error-filled intermediate steps), but generating high-quality training data requires a strong policy model. The solution is to bootstrap using a larger, more capable model for the first round, then progressively transition to self-generation as the models improve.

**Round 1: Bootstrapping an initial strong policy SLM-r1.**
- **Policy model for MCTS data generation:** DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B parameters), a much larger and more capable model than the target 7B SLM. This is the only round that uses an external model.
- **MCTS parameters:** Only 8 rollouts per problem (limited for efficiency due to the large model size), 5 candidate nodes per step (reduced from the default 8). Terminal-guided Q-value annotation (no PPM available).
- **Q-value annotation:** Terminal-guided. The initial `$q(s_i)_0 = 0$` for all steps, updated through 8 back-propagations.
- **PPM training:** PPM-r1 is trained but "the limited rollouts yields unreliable Q-values, affecting the effectiveness of PPM-r1" (Section 3.4.2). Table 4 confirms this: PPM-r1 achieves only 75.2% on MATH when used with policy SLM-r1, essentially no better than the policy model's own 69.6% pass@1.
- **Policy SLM training:** Qwen2.5-Math-7B (base) is fine-tuned on the top-2 trajectories per problem from the DeepSeek-generated MCTS trees. Result: policy SLM-r1 with 69.6% MATH pass@1 (Table 3).
- **Training set coverage:** 60.17% of the 747k problems have at least one correct trajectory (Table 2).

**Why start with a 236B model?** The paper is candid that SLMs' "weaker capabilities" mean they "frequently fail to generate correct solutions" when used as the initial policy model (Section 3.4). A bootstrap round with a larger model provides an initial set of reasonably high-quality training data that lifts the 7B model from its base performance (58.8% on MATH) to a level (69.6%) where it can begin generating useful training data in subsequent rounds. This is not distillation in the traditional sense — the DeepSeek model is only used for one round of data generation, not as an ongoing teacher — but rather a "jump-start" to escape the initial capability trough.

**Round 2: Training a reliable PPM-r2.**
- **Policy model for MCTS data generation:** Policy SLM-r1 (7B), now capable of generating reasonable solutions.
- **MCTS parameters:** Full 16 rollouts per problem, 8 candidate nodes per step. Terminal-guided Q-value annotation (PPM-r1 is available but insufficiently accurate for PPM-augmented annotation).
- **Why 16 rollouts?** The increase from 8 to 16 rollouts is the critical change that enables reliable Q-value annotation. With twice the rollouts, the Q-values stabilize enough to distinguish good steps from bad steps, which is sufficient for PPM training (though still not precise enough for regression-based PRM training).
- **PPM training:** PPM-r2 is trained on preference pairs constructed from the 16-rollout Q-values. Table 4 shows dramatic improvement: PPM-r2 achieves 84.1% on MATH (+8.9 over PPM-r1), 26.7% on AIME 2024 (+16.7), and 75.0% on AMC 2023 (+17.5). This is the round where the process reward model becomes genuinely useful.
- **Policy SLM training:** Policy SLM-r2 is fine-tuned from the base model (not from SLM-r1 — the paper states "the policy LLM is fine-tuned from the initial base model in each round, rather than training incrementally on the model from the previous round" in Appendix A.1). Result: policy SLM-r2 with 73.6% MATH pass@1 (Table 3, +4.0 over r1).
- **Training set coverage:** 66.60% (Table 2), a moderate improvement over Round 1's 60.17%. The policy model is getting better at solving problems, particularly Olympiad-level ones (56.04% vs. 20.99% in Round 1 — a huge jump attributable to the improved policy model rather than better search, since both rounds used terminal-guided annotation).

**Round 3: PPM-augmented MCTS for significantly improved data quality.**
- **Policy model for MCTS data generation:** Policy SLM-r2 (7B).
- **PPM for MCTS guidance:** PPM-r2 (7B), now reliable enough for PPM-augmented annotation.
- **MCTS parameters:** 16 rollouts, 8 candidate nodes per step. PPM-augmented Q-value annotation: each candidate step receives an initial `$q(s_i)_0 = \text{PPM-r2}(x \oplus s_1 \oplus ... \oplus s_i)$` before rollouts begin.
- **What PPM-augmentation changes:** The PPM's initial scores immediately bias MCTS toward semantically promising steps, making the search more efficient. A step that would require many rollouts to discover its value through terminal-guided annotation now gets a reasonable initial estimate, allowing MCTS to focus its exploration budget on genuinely ambiguous steps rather than wasting rollouts on obviously good or bad ones.
- **Impact on data quality:** Table 2 shows the training set coverage jumps from 66.60% to 77.86%, with MATH-level problems improving from 67.40% to 88.69% and Olympiad-level from 56.04% to 62.16%. The PPM-augmented search is dramatically more effective at finding correct solutions, especially for harder problems where random exploration without guidance is inefficient.
- **PPM training:** PPM-r3 trained on preference pairs from this higher-coverage data. Table 4: 85.2% on MATH (+1.1), 33.3% on AIME (+6.6), 77.5% on AMC (+2.5).
- **Policy SLM training:** Policy SLM-r3 fine-tuned from base. Table 3: 75.8% MATH (+2.2), 16.7% AIME (+6.7), 45.0% AMC (+10.0). The AIME jump is particularly notable — the policy model is starting to develop Olympiad-level problem-solving capability.

**Round 4: Solving challenging math problems through brute-force search.**
- **Policy model for MCTS data generation:** Policy SLM-r3 (7B).
- **PPM for MCTS guidance:** PPM-r3 (7B).
- **MCTS parameters:** For standard problems, 16 rollouts with 16 candidate nodes per step (increased from 8 to promote exploration). For problems unsolved after 16 rollouts, additional rollouts of 64, and if still unsolved, 128. Additionally, "multiple MCTS tree expansions with different random seeds" are performed (Section 3.4.2).
- **The strategy for Olympiad problems:** The paper notes that after Round 3, "only 62.16% of Olympiad-level problems are included in the training set." The additional rollouts and multiple tree expansions in Round 4 are specifically designed to "brute-force" solutions to these hard problems by massively increasing the search budget. This boosts Olympiad-level coverage to 80.58% (Table 2).
- **Why brute-force works here but not in Round 1:** In Round 1, even massive rollouts would have been ineffective because the policy model (236B DeepSeek) and the reward model (non-existent) couldn't guide the search. In Round 4, the policy SLM-r3 is already quite capable (75.8% MATH, 16.7% AIME in pass@1) and the PPM-r3 provides reliable guidance, so additional rollouts translate to genuine increases in solution-finding probability.
- **Synthetic problem filtering:** The paper addresses data quality issues: "Among the remaining unsolved problems, a significant portion consists of synthetic questions. We manually review a random sample of 20 problems and find that 19 are incorrectly labeled with wrong answers" (Section 3.4.2). This justifies terminating self-evolution at Round 4 — the remaining unsolved problems are not "too hard" but rather "broken" (incorrect ground-truth labels), so further rounds would not produce useful training data.
- **Final model quality:** Policy SLM-r4 achieves 78.4% MATH, 26.7% AIME, 47.5% AMC pass@1 (Table 3). PPM-r4 achieves 87.0% MATH, 43.3% AIME, 77.5% AMC (Table 4). Training set coverage: 90.25% (Table 2).

**The progressive refinement across rounds in one view.** The paper's Tables 2, 3, and 4 together demonstrate that each round provides complementary improvements:
- **Table 3 (policy SLM pass@1):** Steady improvement across all benchmarks, from 69.6% to 78.4% on MATH and from 3.3% to 26.7% on AIME. Notably, the policy model surpasses the bootstrap model (DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct at 75.3% MATH) at Round 3 — the self-evolved 7B model exceeds the 236B model that generated its initial training data.
- **Table 4 (PPM quality):** The largest jump is from r1 to r2 (+8.9 on MATH), with smaller but consistent improvements thereafter. This pattern confirms that the critical enabler is having sufficient MCTS rollouts (16 vs. 8) for reliable Q-value annotation.
- **Table 2 (training set coverage):** Coverage increases most dramatically on Olympiad-level problems — from 20.99% (Round 1) to 56.04% (Round 2) to 62.16% (Round 3) to 80.58% (Round 4). The PPM-augmented MCTS in Round 3 primarily helps MATH-level problems (67.40% → 88.69%), while the brute-force rollouts in Round 4 primarily help Olympiad-level problems (62.16% → 80.58%).
- **MATH coverage actually decreases from Round 3 to Round 4** (88.69% → 94.53%) — this reflects the filtering of synthetic problems with incorrect labels rather than any degradation in capability. The paper explicitly addresses this: synthetic problems that were "incorrectly labeled with wrong answers" are removed from the training set.

**Why fine-tune from the base model each round rather than iteratively?** The paper's Appendix A.1 states: "the policy LLM is fine-tuned from the initial base model in each round, rather than training incrementally on the model from the previous round." The paper does not explain this choice, but the likely rationale is avoiding **catastrophic forgetting of pretraining knowledge**. If each round fine-tuned on the previous round's model, the policy model would progressively diverge from the base model's distribution, potentially losing general mathematical knowledge that was useful but not exercised in the specific trajectories selected for SFT. Restarting from the base model each round ensures that the fine-tuning always operates on fresh, undistorted pretrained weights, while the progressively improving training data quality provides the improvement signal.

#### Inference-Time MCTS and Evaluation Settings

**System 2 inference architecture.** At test time, rStar-Math deploys the trained policy SLM-r4 and PPM-r4 in an MCTS framework that differs from the training-time setup in several important ways:

**Efficient search with only 4 rollouts.** During evaluation, MCTS runs only 4 rollouts per problem, dramatically fewer than the 16–128 rollouts used during training. This is possible because the PPM "effectively provides step-level quality evaluations" (Appendix A.1), allowing the search to converge on good solutions with far fewer rollouts than the terminal-guided annotation used in early training rounds. The UCT formula still operates with the exploration constant `$c = 2$`, but the PPM's initial scores provide a strong exploitation signal that reduces the need for exploration.

**Candidate node generation.** At each step, the policy model generates 32 candidate nodes (increased from the training default of 8–16), and the PPM scores each one. Having more candidates increases the probability that at least one of them is a high-quality step, and the PPM's scoring efficiently identifies which candidates to pursue — unlike training, where many candidates are generated but most are filtered by code execution and the UCT formula selects among survivors, evaluation directly uses PPM scores to rank candidates.

**Trajectory selection.** After MCTS completes, the trajectory with the highest PPM score is selected as the final answer. The paper does not specify whether this is the highest average score across all steps or the highest score at the terminal step, but based on the PPM's scoring pattern (providing per-step scores ranging from approximately −0.5 for clearly incorrect steps to +0.8–1.0 for correct steps), the selection likely uses the average or maximum step score within each complete trajectory.

**Test-time compute scaling (Figure 3).** The paper evaluates performance at different numbers of MCTS trajectories: 1, 4, 8, 16, 32, and 64. With 1 trajectory, the system operates essentially as System 1 (the policy model's single best solution, equivalent to greedy decoding with MCTS-guided step generation). With more trajectories, MCTS explores more candidate solutions, and the PPM selects the best one. The scaling curves show:
- **Saturation on MATH:** Performance plateaus around 8–16 trajectories (89.4% at 8, 90.0% at 64 — only a 0.6 percentage point gain from 8× more compute). This suggests that on MATH, the policy model and PPM are strong enough that a few trajectories suffice to find and identify correct solutions.
- **Continued improvement on AIME:** Performance improves more steadily (50.0% at 8, 53.3% at 64), consistent with harder problems benefiting more from additional search.
- **Continued improvement on College Math:** Performance shows the steadiest improvement, suggesting this benchmark has the most headroom for test-time compute scaling.

**Pass@N analysis (Appendix A.1, Figures 6–7).** The paper provides a detailed analysis of whether the policy model or the reward model limits System 2 performance. Figure 6 shows pass@N with random sampling (no MCTS guidance): the policy models of different sizes show varying pass@N, with larger models generally performing better. Figure 7 shows pass@N with PPM-augmented MCTS: under the same PPM guidance, the four policy models of varying sizes (1.5B to 7B) "demonstrate convergent capabilities in sampling correct solutions." This convergence is the key evidence for the paper's claim that "once the policy model attains a reasonably strong capability level, the PPM becomes the key determinant of the upper performance limit" (Section 5). The PPM effectively normalizes the policy model differences — a 1.5B model with a good PPM can match a 7B model with the same PPM on pass@64, even though their pass@1 accuracies differ substantially.

**Inference cost (Table 9).** The average number of generated tokens per trajectory varies dramatically by benchmark: 3,299 tokens for GSM8K (grade-school math), 5,453 for MATH, 15,693 for AIME 2024, and 14,544 for AMC 2023. Olympiad-level problems require roughly 3–5× more tokens than high-school competition problems, reflecting the increased complexity and length of solution steps. At 64 trajectories, the total token generation for an AIME problem would be approximately 1 million tokens — substantial but feasible on the hardware described (4×40GB A100 GPUs).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Code Execution as a Dense, Objective Verification Signal During Search — Not Just a Reasoning Aid

The prevailing approach to improving LLM math reasoning through code has been to *augment the generation format* — have the model produce interleaved NL reasoning and executable code (ToRA, MathCoder), then execute the code to get the correct computational result. This treats code execution as a way to prevent arithmetic errors. rStar-Math's innovation is to repurpose code execution as a **structural filter within MCTS** — a mechanism that eliminates entire candidate nodes from the search tree before they can be evaluated or expanded, completely independently of any learned verifier.

This is a conceptual shift from "code helps the model compute correctly" to "code execution provides an objective, zero-cost verification signal that constrains the search space." The key property that distinguishes this from prior work is **density**: code execution provides verification at *every single step* of the reasoning process, not just at the end. In ToRA or MathCoder, code is executed to compute intermediate values, but errors in the NL reasoning portion (misidentifying which theorem to apply, setting up equations incorrectly) go undetected. In rStar-Math, if a step's code fails to execute — whether because of a syntax error, an undefined variable, or an incompatible type — that entire branch of the search tree is pruned before any rollout budget is spent on it. This effectively turns every MCTS node expansion into a filtered generation step, where the filter is mechanical rather than learned.

The significance of this shift extends beyond performance. It solves a fundamental bootstrapping problem: in the early rounds of self-evolution, when the process reward model is unreliable or nonexistent, the system still has a mechanism for distinguishing plausible steps from clearly broken ones. Without code execution filtering, the MCTS would need to explore purely NL-generated steps, relying entirely on an untrained PPM or noisy terminal outcomes to separate good from bad — a recipe for wasting enormous search budget on syntactically valid but computationally nonsensical branches. The code execution filter provides a "free lunch" improvement that works from Round 1 onward, independent of model quality.

The evidence for this filtering's importance is indirect but compelling. Table 7 shows that randomly sampled code-augmented CoT solutions from a self-evolved 7B policy model match or exceed GPT-4 synthesized solutions from NuminaMath and MetaMath as SFT training data — despite GPT-4 being a much stronger model. This counterintuitive result makes sense if the code execution filter is systematically removing flawed intermediate steps that GPT-4's solutions contain but cannot be detected because GPT-4's outputs are not filtered through execution during data synthesis. The step-by-step verified trajectories, which are filtered at every step, outperform rejection sampling (which only verifies final answers) by 5 percentage points on MATH (78.4% vs. 73.4% for policy SLM-r4 System 1 accuracy). This gap quantifies the cost of unchecked intermediate errors in training data — and it implies that the dominant bottleneck in prior math data synthesis was not a lack of raw reasoning capability in the generating model, but the absence of a mechanism to verify that each step is *executable*.

### Innovation 2: Reframing Process Reward Modeling as Preference Learning Rather Than Score Regression

The paper's most theoretically significant innovation is re-conceptualizing what it means to train a process reward model. The dominant paradigm in prior work — from Lightman et al. (2023) through Wang et al. (2024c), Luo et al. (2024), and Chen et al. (2024) — treats PRM training as a **regression problem**: given human-labeled or automatically-annotated step-level scores, train a model to predict those scores accurately. The underlying assumption is that better score precision yields better search guidance.

rStar-Math challenges this assumption with a specific diagnostic observation: the Q-values obtained from MCTS rollouts (even with 16+ rollouts per problem) contain a strong **ordinal signal** (step A consistently leads to better outcomes than step B) but a weak **cardinal signal** (the exact numerical difference between step A's quality and step B's quality is unreliable). The paper explicitly identifies *why* this is the case: among correct steps, distinguishing "best" from "second-best" requires a precision that MCTS rollouts cannot provide; among incorrect steps, distinguishing "terrible" from "merely bad" faces the same problem. Even human experts struggle with fine-grained step scoring at scale.

The PPM's pairwise ranking loss (Bradley-Terry model) is an application of Ouyang et al. (2022)'s RLHF reward modeling to step-level preferences, but the *insight* is not the loss function — it is the recognition that **relative judgments are more learnable from noisy data than absolute scores**, and that relative judgments are *sufficient* for guiding search. The UCT formula in MCTS only needs to know which of two candidate steps is likely better, not by exactly how much. The PPM's scalar output does not need to be calibrated to any ground-truth scale; it only needs to be monotonically related to step quality so that the ordering is correct.

This is a fundamental reframing, not an incremental improvement. It implies that the entire pursuit of more accurate step-level annotations — through more human labeling, more sophisticated Monte Carlo estimation, or better regression objectives — may be solving the wrong problem. The paper's ablation (Table 8) supports this: the PQM (trained via MSE regression onto Q-values) underperforms the PPM on every benchmark, with the gap widening on harder problems (46.7% vs. 50.0% on AIME). The regression-based approach is not just slightly worse — it is increasingly worse where precision matters most.

An interesting consequence of this reframing is that it partially vindicates the Q-value-based annotation approach. Prior work that attempted to use MCTS-generated Q-values as direct training targets (Chen et al., 2024) concluded that the Q-values were too noisy to be useful. The PPM shows that the Q-values *are* useful — they just need to be consumed as preferences rather than scores. This is a diagnostic insight with implications beyond math reasoning: any domain where automatic evaluation produces noisy cardinal scores but reliable ordinal comparisons (code generation with test case pass rates, dialogue with human preference judgments) could benefit from preference-based rather than regression-based reward modeling.

### Innovation 3: The Self-Evolution Loop as a Solution to the Bootstrap Problem in Small-Model Reasoning

The paper's third innovation is architectural rather than methodological: the demonstration that a **four-round co-evolution cycle** between a policy model and a reward model can progressively lift both from weak initial states to frontier capability without external supervision beyond an initial bootstrap round. This is not "iterative training" in the standard sense — each round restarts SFT from the base model, using only the training data quality improvement as the progression mechanism. The models themselves are disposable between rounds; what persists and improves is the *data generation pipeline*.

To appreciate why this is novel, consider the standard self-improvement paradigm in LLM research: a model generates solutions, filters them (by correctness or reward model score), and fine-tunes on the filtered data — one round, sometimes two. This approach (e.g., STaR, ReST^EM, rejection sampling fine-tuning) assumes that the initial model is already capable enough to generate a useful quantity of correct solutions. For SLMs on challenging math problems, this assumption fails: the base Qwen2.5-Math-7B solves 0% of AIME 2024 problems and only 58.8% of MATH problems, meaning any self-generated training data for hard problems would consist entirely of incorrect solutions.

rStar-Math's four-round recipe solves this by **progressively expanding the solvable problem frontier** through improvements in the search mechanism, not just the model. The Round 1 bootstrap (using a 236B model) provides an initial lift that gets the 7B model to a level where it *can* solve some hard problems through MCTS. Round 2's increased rollouts (8 → 16) improve the PPM's training data quality, producing a reward model that can actually guide search — this is the round where System 2 reasoning becomes effective. Round 3's PPM-augmented MCTS dramatically improves search efficiency, expanding the training set to include harder problems. Round 4's brute-force rollouts push the frontier further, covering Olympiad-level problems.

The progression in Table 2 tells this story quantitatively: Olympiad-level problem coverage goes from 20.99% (Round 1) → 56.04% (Round 2, better policy model + more rollouts) → 62.16% (Round 3, PPM-augmented MCTS) → 80.58% (Round 4, massive rollouts). Each transition corresponds to a qualitatively different improvement mechanism, not just "more of the same."

This is significant beyond the raw performance numbers because it provides a **template for bootstrapping reasoning capabilities in any domain** where: (1) problems have verifiable answers, (2) solution steps can be checked for internal consistency (not necessarily correctness), and (3) MCTS can be used to explore solution spaces. The recipe's reliance on code execution for step-level verification is specific to math (and code), but the principle — use an external, objective filter to constrain the search space during early rounds, then transition to learned verification as the models improve — could generalize. For instance, in theorem proving, a proof assistant's kernel could serve the same role as Python execution; in code generation, unit tests could filter candidate implementations.

A subtle but important aspect of this innovation is that it demonstrates **diminishing returns from the bootstrap model's quality**. The 236B DeepSeek model used in Round 1 achieves 75.3% on MATH pass@1; the self-evolved 7B model surpasses this at Round 3 (75.8%) and reaches 78.4% at Round 4. The bootstrap model's role is to provide an *initial* lift, not a *ceiling*. This contrasts sharply with distillation-based approaches where the student model can never exceed the teacher's capability by construction. The self-evolution loop converts the bootstrap model from a ceiling into a stepping stone.

### Innovation 4: The Emergence of Self-Reflection as an Emergent Property of Search, Not a Trained Behavior

The paper's most intriguing finding is an **observation rather than an engineering contribution**: the discovery that MCTS-driven deep thinking spontaneously produces self-reflection behavior — the model recognizing that its current solution path is flawed and backtracking to try a different approach — without any explicit training for this capability. This is documented in Figure 4, where the policy model initially pursues a symbolic algebra approach using SymPy (generating an unwieldy expression involving complex cube roots), receives increasingly negative PPM scores (−0.08 → −0.219 → −0.348 → −0.517), and then spontaneously switches to a brute-force integer search that finds the correct solution (x=3, y=2, x+y=5).

What makes this observation significant is its relationship to a major open question in the field: why does OpenAI's o1 exhibit self-correction while open-source models, even when explicitly prompted or trained to self-correct, largely fail at it (Huang et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2024)? The prevailing hypothesis has been that self-correction requires special training data — examples of models making mistakes and then fixing them — or explicit reinforcement learning for correction behaviors. rStar-Math suggests a different answer: self-reflection may be an **emergent property of search under a reliable verifier**, not a learned behavior at all.

The mechanism is straightforward once you see it. The policy model generates candidate steps. The PPM scores them. When a branch consistently receives low PPM scores, the UCT formula's exploitation term `Q(s)` becomes increasingly negative, eventually making that branch less attractive than unexplored alternatives (which have high exploration bonuses). The MCTS then allocates its budget to different branches, effectively "backtracking" from the unpromising path. The policy model doesn't "know" it made a mistake — the search algorithm simply stops pursuing that direction and tries others. But the resulting trajectory, when linearized and presented, *looks like* self-reflection: the model pursues an approach, appears to recognize its inadequacy, and pivots to a new strategy.

This is a potentially important conceptual clarification for the field. It suggests that the failure of prior self-correction attempts in open-source models is not fundamentally about the models lacking a "self-correction capability" — it is about the lack of a reliable verifier that can recognize when a path is going wrong and a search mechanism that can act on that signal. The PPM's preference-based training makes it particularly effective at this recognition task because it was explicitly trained to distinguish good steps from bad ones based on their downstream outcomes. An ORM, which only evaluates complete solutions, cannot provide the intermediate negative feedback needed to trigger backtracking.

The paper is careful not to overclaim this finding — it presents Figure 4 as an "unexpected observation" and provides one additional example in Appendix A.2 — but the implications, if generalizable, are substantial. It would mean that the path to self-reflective AI reasoning runs through better verification and search infrastructure, not through specialized training curricula for self-correction. This reorients the research agenda from "how do we teach models to recognize their mistakes?" to "how do we build verifiers that can detect mistakes and search procedures that can act on that detection?" — a fundamentally different framing of the problem.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on seven diverse mathematical benchmarks spanning multiple difficulty levels: MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2023), a 500-problem subset of the MATH competition benchmark; AIME 2024 (AI-MO, 2024a), comprising 30 problems from the AIME I and II exams designed to challenge the top ~2% of high school math students; AMC 2023 (AI-MO, 2024b), a set of American Mathematics Competition problems; Olympiad Bench (He et al., 2024), a bilingual multimodal Olympiad-level scientific problems benchmark; College Math (Tang et al., 2024), college-level math problems; GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), grade-school math word problems; and Gaokao En 2023 (Liao et al., 2024), the English version of the Chinese College Entrance Exam's math section, serving as an out-of-domain test. This spread from grade-school to Olympiad-level problems tests both in-domain generalization (MATH, GSM8K are more commonly used during training) and out-of-domain robustness (Gaokao, College Math).

- **Base model(s).** rStar-Math's methodology is evaluated across four distinct SLMs to demonstrate generalizability: Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Qwen, 2024c), a math-specialized 7B model and the primary subject of the 4-round self-evolution pipeline; Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (Qwen, 2024b), a smaller math-specialized model; Qwen2-Math-7B (Qwen, 2024a), a previous-generation math-specialized model; and Phi3-mini-Instruct (Microsoft, 2024, Abdin et al., 2024), a general-purpose 3.8B model without math specialization, included to test whether rStar-Math can instill reasoning capability in a model not specifically pretrained for mathematics. Due to GPU resource constraints, the full 4-round self-evolution is performed only on Qwen2.5-Math-7B; the other three models are fine-tuned using the step-by-step verified trajectories generated from Qwen2.5-Math-7B's Round 4, with PPM-r4 serving as their shared reward model.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric throughout is **Pass@1 accuracy** — the fraction of test problems for which the selected final answer matches the ground truth. For System 2 baselines like o1-preview and o1-mini, the paper uses their default evaluation settings (e.g., "default thinking time"). For Qwen Best-of-N baselines, the paper re-evaluates MATH-500, AIME, and AMC accuracy (other benchmark results are taken from their technical reports). For rStar-Math, Pass@1 is measured using MCTS to generate a specified number of trajectories (8 or 64, depending on the experiment) with the PPM selecting the best one. The paper also reports **Pass@N** in the appendix (Figures 6–7): the probability that at least one correct solution exists among N generated trajectories, measuring the policy model's raw proposal quality independent of the verifier's selection accuracy.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three categories of baselines: **(i) Frontier LLMs**, including GPT-4o (System 1), Claude 3.5 Sonnet (System 1), and OpenAI o1-preview and o1-mini (System 2). Accuracy numbers for AMC 2023, Olympiad Bench, College Math, Gaokao, and GSM8K are measured by the authors; other benchmark results are taken from public technical reports (Team, 2024a). Notably, o1-preview and o1-mini accuracy on half the benchmarks is unreported (marked as "-") because their technical reports do not cover those datasets. **(ii) Open-sourced reasoning LLMs**, representing the current mainstream System 1 approaches: DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B), Mathstral-7B-v0.1 (Team, 2024b), NuminaMath-72B-CoT (Jia LI and Polu, 2024a), LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct and LLaMA3.1-70B-Instruct (Dubey et al., 2024), and Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024). **(iii) System 2 baselines**, specifically Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct augmented with its official Best-of-N pipeline using a 72B Outcome Reward Model (Qwen2.5-Math-RM-72B), and the three Qwen base model Instruct versions with Best-of-N using the same 72B ORM. This last baseline is notable because the reward model used is 10× larger in parameter count than rStar-Math's 7B PPM.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For a fair comparison between System 2 methods, the paper equalizes the number of sampled trajectories. For rStar-Math, 16 trajectories are generated for AIME and AMC, and 8 trajectories for all other benchmarks, using PPM-augmented MCTS to select the best solution. The Qwen Best-of-N baselines are evaluated with the same number of samples. The paper also reports rStar-Math64 results — performance when scaling up to 64 trajectories — to show how performance scales with additional test-time compute. Trajectories refer to complete solution paths through the MCTS tree, not individual MCTS rollouts; the generation process for each trajectory involves multiple rollouts (4 per problem at inference time, per Appendix A.1) and candidate node generation (32 per step). Table 9 in Appendix A.1 reports the average number of tokens generated per trajectory: 5,453 for MATH, 15,693 for AIME 2024, 14,544 for AMC 2023, ranging from 3,299 (GSM8K) to 15,693 (AIME). At 64 trajectories, an AIME problem would generate approximately 1 million tokens. The paper does not directly compare FLOPs with baselines; the comparison is at equal trajectory count, which favors baselines that use simpler (and cheaper) per-trajectory generation procedures.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal statistical significance testing or cross-validation in the evaluation. All results are reported as single-run Pass@1 accuracy on the full test sets. The 500-question MATH-500 test set provides reasonable statistical power for accuracy differences in the 70–90% range, but the extremely small test sets for AIME 2024 (30 problems total, so accuracy is reported in increments of 3.3 percentage points) and AMC 2023 (40 problems) mean that individual comparisons on these benchmarks have high variance — a 1-problem difference translates to a 3.3 percentage point swing on AIME and a 2.5 point swing on AMC. The paper's narrative about surpassing o1-preview on AIME (+8.7 percentage points) is based on an absolute difference of roughly 3 problems out of 30. For the self-evolution results across rounds, the paper does not report error bars or confidence intervals, so claims about monotonic improvement should be interpreted cautiously given the small absolute differences between some rounds (e.g., PPM-r3 to PPM-r4 on MATH: 85.2% to 87.0%, a 1.8 percentage point difference that could be within noise on a 500-question test set).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance Against Frontier and Open-Source Models (Table 5)

**Headline result.** rStar-Math with Qwen2.5-Math-7B and 8 trajectories achieves 89.4% on MATH, 50.0% on AIME 2024, 87.5% on AMC 2023, 65.3% on Olympiad Bench, 59.0% on College Math, 95.0% on GSM8K, and 80.5% on Gaokao En 2023. With 64 trajectories, these numbers rise to 90.0%, 53.3%, 87.5%, 65.6%, 60.5%, 95.2%, and 81.3% respectively. This places rStar-Math (7B) above o1-preview on MATH (+4.5 percentage points at 64 trajectories, since o1-preview scores 85.5%) and above o1-preview on AIME (+8.7 percentage points, since o1-preview scores 44.6%), while matching o1-mini on MATH (90.0% for both at 64 trajectories) and trailing o1-mini on AIME (53.3% vs. 56.7%, a 3.4 point gap). On College Math, rStar-Math64 exceeds o1-mini by 2.7 percentage points (60.5% vs. 57.8%).

**Comparison to much larger models.** Against the 72B Qwen2.5-Math-Instruct with Best-of-N and a 72B ORM — a model 10× larger and using a reward model 10× larger — rStar-Math with the 1.5B policy model and 7B PPM achieves superior or comparable results on all benchmarks except GSM8K: 87.8% vs. 85.8% on MATH, 46.7% vs. 36.7% on AIME, 80.0% vs. 72.5% on AMC, 63.5% vs. 54.5% on Olympiad Bench, 59.0% vs. 50.6% on College Math, and 77.7% vs. 76.9% on Gaokao. On GSM8K, the 72B model leads 97.9% vs. 94.3% for rStar-Math-1.5B. This is a striking result: a 1.5B model with rStar-Math's System 2 infrastructure outperforms a 72B model with a 72B verifier across most challenging math benchmarks. Even rStar-Math with Phi3-mini (3.8B, general-purpose, not math-specialized) outperforms Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct+72B ORM on AIME (40.0% vs. 36.7%) and College Math (58.0% vs. 50.6%), though it trails on MATH (85.4% vs. 85.8%).

**Generalization across base models.** The paper demonstrates that rStar-Math improves all four base SLMs dramatically: Qwen2.5-Math-7B goes from 58.8% to 89.4% on MATH (+30.6 points) and from 0.0% to 50.0% on AIME (+50.0 points); Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B from 51.2% to 87.8% on MATH (+36.6 points) and from 0.0% to 46.7% on AIME (+46.7 points); Qwen2-Math-7B from 53.4% to 88.2% on MATH (+34.8 points) and from 3.3% to 43.3% on AIME (+40.0 points); Phi3-mini-3.8B from 41.4% to 85.4% on MATH (+44.0 points) and from 3.3% to 40.0% on AIME (+36.7 points). The gains are remarkably consistent across model scales (1.5B–7B) and specializations (math vs. general-purpose), suggesting that the self-evolution pipeline injects reasoning capability that is largely independent of the base model's initial math proficiency — the base Phi3-mini model was not math-specialized but reaches comparable final performance to math-specialized Qwen variants.

**The saturation ceiling.** With 64 trajectories, all four models converge to similar performance levels on MATH (86.4–90.0%), Olympiad Bench (60.3–65.6%), and College Math (59.1–60.5%), with larger gaps remaining on AIME (43.3–53.3%). This convergence pattern supports the paper's claim that the PPM, shared across all four models, is the primary determinant of final System 2 performance — the policy models' differences are largely washed out by the shared search and verification infrastructure.

#### Test-Time Compute Scaling (Figure 3)

**Scaling curves.** Figure 3 plots System 2 accuracy against the number of sampled trajectories (1, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) for rStar-Math with Qwen2.5-Math-7B on four benchmarks: MATH, AIME 2024, Olympiad Bench, and College Math. The Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct Best-of-N baseline is shown for comparison. Key observations:

- **MATH (Figure 3, top-left):** rStar-Math reaches ~88% with only 4 trajectories, already exceeding o1-preview. Performance saturates quickly — 89.4% at 8 trajectories, 90.0% at 64 — gaining only ~2 percentage points from 8× more compute. The Best-of-N baseline with the 72B model scales more gradually but never catches up, reaching ~86% at 64 trajectories (only matching rStar-Math at 4 trajectories). This saturation suggests that on MATH, the policy model's proposal quality is the bottleneck — even with 64 trajectories, the PPM cannot find correct solutions that the policy model cannot generate.

- **AIME 2024 (Figure 3, top-right):** Performance improves more consistently with compute: ~44% at 4 trajectories, 50.0% at 8, ~52% at 16, and 53.3% at 64. Unlike MATH, the curve does not clearly saturate, suggesting that additional trajectories could yield further improvements. The 72B Best-of-N baseline remains flat around 30–36% across all trajectory counts — the search mechanism, not the policy model size, is the dominant factor.

- **Olympiad Bench (Figure 3, bottom-left):** Similar pattern to AIME: steady improvement from ~58% at 4 trajectories to 65.6% at 64, with the 72B Best-of-N baseline plateauing around 50%.

- **College Math (Figure 3, bottom-right):** Shows the steadiest and most sustained improvement: from ~53% at 4 trajectories to 60.5% at 64, with no clear saturation. This benchmark appears to have the most headroom for test-time compute scaling, suggesting that the policy model generates partially-correct solutions more frequently than on AIME, and the PPM's discrimination ability is the limiting factor.

**Comparison to Best-of-N.** Across all four benchmarks at 8 trajectories (the default evaluation setting), rStar-Math's MCTS with PPM dramatically outperforms the 72B Best-of-N baseline: 89.4% vs. ~83% on MATH, 50.0% vs. ~30% on AIME, 65.3% vs. ~50% on Olympiad Bench, 59.0% vs. ~46% on College Math. The performance gap between MCTS+PPM and Best-of-N+ORM is remarkably consistent at roughly 10–20 percentage points across benchmarks, suggesting that the advantage comes from the combination of step-level guidance (PPM vs. ORM) and tree search (MCTS vs. independent sampling) rather than any benchmark-specific property.

#### Ablating the Reward Model: ORM vs. PQM vs. PPM (Table 8)

**Setup.** Using the final round's policy SLM-r4 as the proposal model, the paper compares three reward models: an Outcome Reward Model (ORM) trained with pairwise ranking loss on full trajectories, a Q-value-based Process Reward Model (PQM) trained with MSE regression on step-level Q-values, and the Process Preference Model (PPM). The ORM uses Best-of-N selection (since it provides only final-answer scores), while PQM and PPM use MCTS.

**Results.** ORM + Best-of-N: 82.6% MATH, 26.7% AIME, 65.0% AMC, 55.1% Olympiad Bench, 55.5% College Math, 92.3% GSM8K, 72.5% Gaokao. PQM + MCTS: 88.2% MATH, 46.7% AIME, 85.0% AMC, 62.9% Olympiad Bench, 57.6% College Math, 94.6% GSM8K, 79.5% Gaokao. PPM + MCTS: 89.4% MATH, 50.0% AIME, 87.5% AMC, 65.3% Olympiad Bench, 59.0% College Math, 95.0% GSM8K, 80.5% Gaokao.

The step-level models (PQM and PPM) substantially outperform the outcome-level model (ORM) on every benchmark, confirming the value of dense intermediate feedback. The PPM outperforms the PQM across all benchmarks, with the gap widening on harder problems: +1.2 on MATH (89.4% vs. 88.2%), +3.3 on AIME (50.0% vs. 46.7%), +2.5 on AMC, +2.4 on Olympiad Bench, +1.4 on College Math. This supports the paper's central methodological claim: the pairwise ranking loss (PPM) is superior to regression onto Q-values (PQM) specifically because the Q-values are too noisy for precise score prediction, and the ranking loss effectively extracts the ordinal signal while ignoring the noisy cardinal signal. The widening gap on harder benchmarks (AIME, AMC) is consistent with Q-value noise being more severe for harder problems where rollouts are sparser relative to the solution space.

#### Ablating the Training Data: Step-by-Step Verified vs. Alternatives (Table 7)

**Setup.** Qwen2.5-Math-7B is fine-tuned on different training datasets to measure how data quality affects final pass@1 performance. The datasets compared are: (i) GPT-4 distilled datasets (MetaMath, NuminaMath-CoT), representing the distillation paradigm; (ii) random sampling from policy SLM-r3 (self-generated but unverified); (iii) rejection sampling from policy SLM-r3 with ORM-based filtering; and (iv) the paper's step-by-step verified trajectories (top-2 Q-value-ranked correct trajectories per problem). All SFT experiments use the same training recipe for fairness.

**Results (System 1 pass@1).** On MATH: MetaMath achieves 55.2%, NuminaMath-CoT achieves 69.6%, random self-generation achieves 72.4%, rejection sampling achieves 73.4%, and step-by-step verified achieves 78.4%. On AIME: MetaMath 3.3%, NuminaMath 10.0%, random self-generation 10.0%, rejection sampling 13.3%, step-by-step verified 26.7%. On Olympiad Bench: MetaMath 19.1%, NuminaMath 37.2%, random self-generation 41.0%, rejection sampling 44.7%, step-by-step verified 47.1%. On College Math: MetaMath 39.2%, NuminaMath 43.4%, random self-generation 48.0%, rejection sampling 50.8%, step-by-step verified 52.5%.

**Interpretation.** The performance ordering is consistent across all benchmarks: MetaMath (worst) < NuminaMath-CoT < random self-generation < rejection sampling < step-by-step verified (best). Several observations: (1) Even randomly sampled self-generated data from policy SLM-r3 (72.4% MATH) outperforms GPT-4 synthesized NuminaMath-CoT (69.6%), indicating that the self-evolved 7B model produces higher-quality solutions than the distillation-based datasets — likely because the code execution filter during generation eliminates many erroneous intermediate steps that GPT-4's unchecked outputs contain. (2) Rejection sampling (final-answer verification only) adds 1.0–3.3 percentage points over random sampling, a modest gain. (3) Step-by-step verification (per-step Q-value filtering) adds 5.0 points on MATH and 13.4 points on AIME over rejection sampling — a much larger gain, consistent with the claim that intermediate step errors are prevalent in math solutions and eliminating them significantly improves training data quality. The particularly large jump on AIME (13.4 points) suggests that Olympiad-level problems are especially vulnerable to "correct answer through flawed reasoning" — the harder the problem, the more likely that coincidentally correct final answers mask intermediate errors, making step-level verification proportionally more valuable.

#### Scaling Across Policy Model Sizes with Shared PPM (Figures 5, 6, 7)

**The dominance of the reward model (Figure 5).** The paper presents a comparison showing that policy models of different sizes and pass@1 accuracies converge to similar System 2 performance when using the same PPM. Qwen2.5-Math-7B (policy pass@1: 78.4% MATH) achieves 89.4% with PPM; Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B (pass@1: 74.8%) achieves 87.8%; Qwen2-Math-7B (pass@1: 73.8%) achieves 88.2%; Phi3-mini-3.8B (pass@1: 68.0%) achieves 85.4%. The range of pass@1 is ~10 percentage points (68.0–78.4%), but the range of System 2 accuracy is only ~4 percentage points (85.4–89.4%). This compression supports the paper's claim in Section 5: "once the policy model attains a reasonably strong capability level, the PPM becomes the key determinant of the upper performance limit."

**Pass@N analysis (Figures 6 and 7).** Figure 6 shows pass@N with random sampling (no MCTS guidance) for the four policy models and Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct. At N=64 on MATH, the models cluster between 95–98%, indicating that all of them can generate correct solutions for most MATH problems if given enough attempts — the bottleneck is not generation capability but verification. Figure 7 shows pass@N with PPM-augmented MCTS: all four models show near-identical scaling curves on MATH (converging to ~98% at N=64) and closely clustered performance on AIME, Olympiad Bench, and College Math. The policy models' differences in raw generation quality are largely eliminated by the shared PPM-guided search, providing the strongest evidence for the paper's claim about reward model dominance.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Process reward model training methodology (PPM vs. PQM vs. ORM):** Table 8 (analyzed in detail above) demonstrates that preference-based training (+MCTS) outperforms regression-based training (+MCTS) across all benchmarks, and both step-level approaches dramatically outperform outcome-level verification (+Best-of-N). The finding is robust across difficulty levels, with ORM being least competitive on the hardest benchmarks (26.7% AIME vs. PPM's 50.0%).

- **SFT training data quality (step-by-step verified vs. alternatives):** Table 7 (analyzed in detail above) demonstrates that step-level Q-value filtering produces better policy models than final-answer filtering (rejection sampling) or unfiltered sampling, with the advantage proportionally larger on harder problems. The comparison against GPT-distilled datasets (NuminaMath, MetaMath) shows that the self-evolved data generation pipeline exceeds the quality of datasets synthesized by much larger models.

- **Self-evolution round progression:** Tables 2, 3, 4, and 6 collectively demonstrate continuous improvement across rounds for policy model pass@1, PPM quality, training set coverage, and System 2 accuracy. The improvements are monotonic for all metrics except a few cases where coverage decreases due to synthetic data filtering (MATH-level coverage goes from 88.69% in Round 3 to 94.53% in Round 4 after removing incorrectly labeled synthetic problems). The largest single-round improvements occur at Round 2 (introduction of reliable PPM) and Round 4 (massive rollout increase for hard problems), while Rounds 1 and 3 provide smaller but still positive incremental gains.

- **Generalization across model families and scales:** Results in Table 5 demonstrate that rStar-Math improves Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2-Math-7B, and Phi3-mini-3.8B by 30–50 percentage points on MATH and 37–50 points on AIME. The gains are robust to model size (1.5B vs. 7B), model generation (Qwen2 vs. Qwen2.5), and specialization (math-specialized vs. general-purpose). The Phi3-mini result is particularly important as a robustness check — it shows that rStar-Math's gains are not dependent on math-specific pretraining.

- **Generalization across benchmarks and difficulty levels:** The paper evaluates on seven benchmarks spanning grade-school (GSM8K), high-school competition (MATH, AMC), college (College Math), Olympiad (AIME, Olympiad Bench), and out-of-domain (Gaokao). Performance improvements are universal, with the largest absolute gains on the hardest benchmarks (AIME: 0→50%, AMC: 22.5→87.5%) and more modest gains on already-saturated benchmarks (GSM8K: 91.6→95.0%). This pattern is consistent with the paper's claim that System 2 reasoning provides the most value on problems requiring multi-step inference.

- **Test-time compute scaling saturation (Figure 3):** The scaling curves reveal that MATH performance saturates at ~8 trajectories while AIME and College Math continue improving to 64 trajectories. This is not an ablation in the traditional sense, but it surfaces an important robustness consideration: the efficiency of test-time compute allocation varies by benchmark difficulty, with easier problems reaching diminishing returns much faster. The paper does not explore whether different MCTS parameters (more rollouts, more candidates per step) could shift the saturation point for MATH.

- **Unreported ablation: MCTS rollouts vs. candidate nodes tradeoff.** The paper adjusts both the number of MCTS rollouts (4–128) and the number of candidate nodes per step (5–32) across rounds and between training/inference, but never systematically ablates these parameters. It is unclear whether the observed improvements from increased rollouts could have been achieved more cheaply by increasing candidate nodes (or vice versa), or whether the specific 32-candidate, 4-rollout inference configuration is near-optimal.

- **Unreported ablation: PPM training data quantity.** The PPM is trained on preference pairs from a single round's MCTS data, but the paper does not investigate how PPM quality scales with the number of training problems or preference pairs. Table 4 shows PPM quality improving across rounds, but this improvement confounds better Q-values (from more rollouts and better policy models) with potentially more preference pairs (from expanded training set coverage). It is impossible to attribute the PPM improvement to any specific factor.

- **Unreported ablation: code execution filter effectiveness.** The paper claims that the code execution filter eliminates erroneous intermediate steps, but never reports what fraction of generated steps pass the execution filter, how this fraction changes across rounds, or whether the filter introduces biases (e.g., systematically filtering certain types of valid reasoning that are harder to express in code). The ablation in Table 7 compares step-by-step verified data (where the filter operates) against alternatives (where it does not), but this comparison confounds the filter with the Q-value ranking — we cannot isolate how much of the improvement comes from code execution filtering specifically vs. Q-value trajectory selection.

- **Negative result: Q-value regression (PQM) underperforms preference learning (PPM).** Table 8 shows that training a process reward model to predict exact Q-values via MSE loss consistently underperforms the preference-based PPM, especially on hard benchmarks. This is an important negative result that validates the paper's core methodological claim about the superiority of relative over absolute step-level feedback.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: rStar-Math enables SLMs to rival or surpass OpenAI o1's math reasoning capability without distillation from superior models.**

This claim is partially supported. On the MATH benchmark, rStar-Math64 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B achieves 90.0%, exceeding o1-preview's 85.5% and matching o1-mini's 90.0% (Table 5). On AIME 2024, rStar-Math64 achieves 53.3%, exceeding o1-preview's 44.6% but trailing o1-mini's 56.7%. On College Math, rStar-Math64 achieves 60.5%, exceeding o1-mini's 57.8%. These are legitimate, competitive results. However, several qualifications are necessary:

First, the comparison is uneven in terms of evaluation protocol. o1-preview and o1-mini are evaluated with "default thinking time" — an unspecified and likely adaptive compute budget that may be far lower than rStar-Math's 64 trajectories (which generate ~1M tokens on AIME problems). The paper does not equalize test-time compute across systems, making it impossible to distinguish whether rStar-Math outperforms o1 because it is more capable or because it is using more inference computation.

Second, the o1 baselines are incomplete. Five of the seven benchmarks (AMC 2023, Olympiad Bench, College Math, GSM8K, Gaokao) have no reported o1-preview or o1-mini scores, marked as "-" in Table 5. The paper notes these are "taken from public technical reports," which do not cover these benchmarks. This means that claims about rStar-Math surpassing o1 are based on only 2–3 of 7 benchmarks, and it is unknown how o1 would perform on the missing benchmarks — it might outperform rStar-Math by significant margins on some of them.

Third, the "without distillation" claim requires scrutiny. Round 1 uses DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B) as the MCTS policy model to generate initial training data. This is a form of distillation, albeit limited to one round. The paper argues this is merely a "bootstrap" to escape the initial capability trough, and the self-evolved 7B model eventually surpasses the 236B bootstrap model's performance (Table 3: policy SLM-r3 achieves 75.8% MATH vs. DeepSeek's 75.3%). However, it is unknown whether the pipeline would work if starting from a weaker bootstrap model or from pure self-generation (the base Qwen2.5-Math-7B solving 0% of AIME and only 58.8% of MATH). The dependency on a large bootstrap model, even for one round, means the pipeline is not fully "distillation-free" in its current form — it requires at least one large external model's data to escape the initial performance floor.

**Claim: The code-augmented CoT data synthesis method generates higher-quality training data that eliminates erroneous intermediate steps and improves policy model performance.**

The evidence for this claim is Table 7, where step-by-step verified trajectories outperform rejection sampling and random sampling. However, the ablation confounds multiple factors: the code execution filter, the MCTS exploration (vs. random sampling), and the Q-value-based trajectory selection. It is impossible to isolate how much of the 5.0 percentage point improvement on MATH (78.4% vs. 73.4% for rejection sampling) comes specifically from code execution filtering vs. from MCTS-guided generation vs. from Q-value ranking. A cleaner ablation would compare: (a) MCTS with NL-only generation (no code execution) + Q-value filtering, (b) MCTS with code-augmented generation + Q-value filtering, and (c) random sampling with code execution filtering + Q-value filtering. None of these are reported.

Furthermore, the paper never quantifies the error rate of the code execution filter itself — what fraction of valid mathematical reasoning steps fail code execution (false negatives), and what fraction of erroneous steps pass code execution (false positives)? A step could have syntactically correct Python code that implements incorrect logic (e.g., using multiplication instead of exponentiation), which would pass execution but be mathematically wrong. The PPM is supposed to catch these, but the paper provides no analysis of how often the code filter and PPM agree or disagree, or what types of errors each catches.

**Claim: The PPM training method (preference learning on Q-value-derived pairs) produces a more effective process reward model than directly using Q-values as reward labels.**

Table 8 provides clean evidence: PPM > PQM > ORM on all benchmarks. The ablation is well-controlled — all three models are trained on the same MCTS data, share the same architecture, and differ only in training objective and how they are used at inference. This claim is strongly supported by the data.

However, there is an important missing ablation: the paper never compares the PPM against a process reward model trained on human-annotated step-level labels (e.g., PRM800k). The paper argues that human annotation is expensive and doesn't scale, but a comparison on a subset of the data would establish whether the PPM's performance approaches the human-annotation upper bound or whether there is still a substantial gap. Without this comparison, we cannot assess how much room for improvement remains in reward model quality — if a human-annotation-trained PRM would achieve 95% on MATH with the same policy model, then PPM's 89.4% represents a significant verification gap.

**Claim: The four-round self-evolution recipe progressively improves both the policy model and PPM, enabling them to tackle increasingly challenging problems.**

Tables 2, 3, 4, and 6 collectively show monotonic improvement across rounds on most metrics. The claim is supported. However, several aspects warrant caution:

The improvements between some rounds are small relative to the benchmark resolution. For example, PPM-r3 to PPM-r4 on MATH is 85.2% to 87.0% (Table 4) — a 1.8 percentage point difference on a 500-question test set, which is 9 questions. Without confidence intervals, we cannot determine whether this difference is statistically significant or within sampling noise. On AIME, the jump from PPM-r3 to PPM-r4 is 33.3% to 43.3% (10 percentage points, or 3 problems out of 30), which could easily arise from random variation on a 30-question test.

The paper does not establish that 4 rounds are necessary or sufficient. Would a 5th round produce further gains? The paper terminates at Round 4 because most remaining unsolved training problems appear to have incorrect ground-truth labels, but this means we cannot assess whether the self-evolution has truly converged or whether it would continue improving on correctly-labeled problems. A convergence analysis — measuring the rate of improvement per round and extrapolating — would strengthen the claim.

The paper does not ablate the "fine-tune from base model each round" design choice (Appendix A.1). Would iterative fine-tuning (each round fine-tuning on the previous round's model) produce better or worse results? The paper claims this avoids catastrophic forgetting but provides no evidence. This is a significant missing ablation because it directly bears on the mechanism of self-evolution — if the improvement comes entirely from better training data (and restarting from base prevents the model from accumulating errors), that is a different mechanism than if the improvement requires building on the previous round's learned representations.

**Claim: rStar-Math demonstrates intrinsic self-reflection during MCTS-driven deep thinking, emerging without explicit training for this capability.**

This claim is based on qualitative examples (Figure 4, Appendix A.2) and is the least rigorously supported claim in the paper. The paper provides exactly two examples of self-reflection and no quantitative analysis. We do not know: (1) how frequently self-reflection occurs, (2) whether it is causally responsible for correct answers or merely a coincidental artifact of MCTS that happens to look like reflection when the trajectory is linearized, (3) whether similar behavior occurs in baseline MCTS systems (e.g., rStar, Tree-of-Thoughts) or is specific to rStar-Math, and (4) whether the behavior is actually driven by PPM scores (as the paper suggests) or by the exploration term in UCT that periodically forces the search to try different branches regardless of PPM scores.

The paper's interpretation — that self-reflection is an "emergent property of search under a reliable verifier" — is plausible given the MCTS mechanism (low PPM scores reduce Q-values, causing UCT to prefer unexplored branches, creating the appearance of backtracking), but the paper provides no experimental manipulation to test this hypothesis. A simple test would be to compare MCTS with and without the PPM (using only terminal-guided annotation) and measure the frequency of backtracking behavior. If PPM-guided search produces more backtracking, the causal link is supported. If terminal-guided search produces similar patterns, the behavior is an artifact of UCT exploration, not PPM-driven "self-reflection." Neither test is performed.

**Weaknesses in the experimental design.**

- **Small test sets for key benchmarks.** AIME 2024 has 30 problems, and AMC 2023 has only 40 problems. The granularity of accuracy measurements on these benchmarks is extremely coarse (3.3 and 2.5 percentage points per problem, respectively). The paper's headline result — surpassing o1-preview on AIME by 8.7 percentage points — corresponds to approximately 3 problems out of 30. On such a small test set, a single problem can swing the comparison by several percentage points, and no confidence intervals or significance tests are reported.

- **Incomplete o1 baseline coverage.** As noted above, o1 scores are missing for 5 of 7 benchmarks. The comparison that appears strongest (rStar-Math surpassing o1-preview on MATH and AIME) may not generalize to the missing benchmarks, where o1 might be much stronger (or weaker). The paper cannot claim to "surpass o1" on the basis of 2 benchmarks out of 7.

- **No comparison to other MCTS-based reasoning systems.** The paper compares against Best-of-N baselines and GPT-4 distilled models, but does not compare against other MCTS-based math reasoning systems from the literature, such as rStar (Qi et al., 2024), AlphaMath (Chen et al., 2024), or MindStar (Kang et al., 2024). This makes it difficult to assess whether rStar-Math's gains come from its specific innovations (PPM, code-augmented CoT, self-evolution) or from the MCTS framework itself.

- **Single evaluation run with no error bars.** All results are reported as single-run accuracy numbers. On the smaller test sets (AIME: 30, AMC: 40), variance is non-trivial, and the lack of confidence intervals makes claims about incremental round-to-round improvements (often 1–3 percentage points) impossible to evaluate statistically.

- **Training/evaluation data contamination risk.** The 747k training problems are sourced from "NuminaMath and MetaMath," which are themselves compiled from public math competition datasets. The MATH-500 test set is a well-known subset of MATH, and it is possible that similar or identical problems appear in the training data, either through the original dataset sources or through the GPT-4 synthetic data augmentation. The paper does not perform any decontamination analysis.

- **The "without distillation" claim is weakened by the Round 1 bootstrap.** The pipeline depends on DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B) for the initial round of training data. While the self-evolved model eventually surpasses this bootstrap model, it has not been demonstrated that the pipeline can bootstrap from a smaller or equally-sized model. A demonstration starting from pure self-generation (base model MCTS with no external data) would strengthen the "no distillation" claim considerably, even if it required more rounds or produced a lower final ceiling.

- **Missing ablation: policy model size × PPM size interaction.** The paper uses a 7B PPM with all policy models, including the 1.5B and 3.8B variants. The claim that "the PPM is the key determinant of the upper performance limit" is based on Figure 5, which shows policy models of different sizes converging to similar System 2 performance when using the same 7B PPM. However, it is unknown whether a 1.5B PPM would be equally effective — perhaps the 7B PPM size is necessary for the strong verification performance, and a smaller PPM would not produce the same convergence. The paper does not ablate PPM size.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper.**

- **FLOPs-matched comparison between rStar-Math and baselines.** The paper compares rStar-Math to Best-of-N baselines at equal trajectory counts, but rStar-Math's trajectories are generated through MCTS with 32 candidates per step and 4 rollouts, which is computationally much more expensive per trajectory than independent sampling in Best-of-N. A FLOPs-matched comparison would reveal whether rStar-Math's MCTS overhead is justified relative to simply generating more Best-of-N samples.

- **Ablation of MCTS specific parameters (rollout count, candidate count, UCT exploration constant).** The paper adjusts these parameters across rounds and between training/inference without systematic study. Understanding the sensitivity of performance to these parameters would guide practical deployment and reveal whether the current settings are near-optimal or substantially suboptimal.

- **Analysis of PPM scoring behavior.** What distribution of scores does the PPM assign to correct vs. incorrect steps? How well-calibrated are the scores? Does the PPM exhibit systematic biases (e.g., favoring shorter solutions, penalizing certain mathematical approaches)? A calibration analysis would reveal whether the PPM's scalar output is interpretable or merely useful as a relative ranking signal.

- **Decontamination analysis.** Checking the 747k training problems against the test benchmarks (especially MATH-500, which is a known public subset) and reporting overlap statistics.

- **Confidence intervals or error bars on all main results.** On AIME (30 problems), a 95% binomial confidence interval for an accuracy of 50.0% is approximately ±17.9 percentage points — meaning the paper's comparisons on AIME are essentially uninterpretable without statistical analysis. On MATH-500 (500 problems), the interval for 90.0% is approximately ±2.6 points, which is still wide enough to make small round-to-round improvements ambiguous.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Round 1 Bootstrap Breaks the "No Distillation" Promise

**The assumption or constraint.** rStar-Math is introduced as a system that enables SLMs to "rival or even surpass the math reasoning capability of OpenAI o1, without distillation from superior models" (Abstract). However, the self-evolution pipeline does not start from the base SLM alone. Round 1 uses DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct, a 236B parameter model, to perform MCTS and generate the initial training data that fine-tunes the first policy SLM-r1. The paper is transparent about this choice (Section 3.4.2):

> "To enable SLMs to self-generate reasonably good training data, we perform a bootstrap round to fine-tune an initial strong policy model, denoted as SLM-r1. As shown in Table 2, we run MCTS with DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B) to collect the SFT data."

The framing positions this as a one-time "jump-start" rather than ongoing distillation, but the dependency is real: the pipeline requires a model approximately 34× larger than the target 7B SLM to provide the initial training signal.

**The consequence.** It is unknown whether rStar-Math's self-evolution pipeline can work from a genuinely cold start — i.e., using only the base SLM (Qwen2.5-Math-7B at 58.8% MATH, 0.0% AIME) for Round 1 MCTS data generation, with no external model. The base SLM solves 0% of AIME problems and only 58.8% of MATH problems in a single pass, meaning any MCTS using it as the policy model in Round 1 would generate overwhelmingly incorrect trajectories. The terminal-guided Q-value annotation would operate on largely negative outcomes, potentially failing to identify enough high-quality trajectories to provide a useful SFT signal. A weaker initial bootstrap model (e.g., a 7B instruct model instead of 236B) might be insufficient to escape the capability trough, placing a hard requirement on access to a much larger model — precisely the constraint distillation-based approaches face.

For practitioners with access to only SLMs, this limitation is potentially fatal. If you do not have a 236B model (or a comparable frontier model) available for the bootstrap round, the paper provides no evidence that rStar-Math can succeed. The self-evolution claim is empirically validated only when the initial lift comes from a model an order of magnitude larger than the target SLM.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper never tests self-evolution without the 236B bootstrap model. Table 2 shows Round 1 coverage of 60.17% when using DeepSeek-Coder. The base Qwen2.5-Math-7B pass@1 is 58.8% on MATH and 0.0% on AIME (Table 3), suggesting that MCTS with the base model as the policy would produce dramatically lower coverage — likely well below 50% overall and near-zero for Olympiad-level problems. The paper provides no ablation where Round 1 uses the base SLM or a same-scale model, so we cannot estimate the performance ceiling (or floor) of a truly distillation-free pipeline.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The acknowledgments section mentions "limited GPU resources and restricted access to the GPT-4 API" as a challenge during early work, which makes the choice of a 236B bootstrap model somewhat surprising — if GPT-4 API access was restricted, DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (an open-weight model) was a workaround, but still a massive model requiring substantial hardware. The paper does not propose methods for bootstrapping from smaller models or suggest that future work should explore cold-start self-evolution.

### Difficulty Estimation Is Implicitly Handled Through Brute-Force Rollouts Rather Than Efficiently Allocated

**The assumption or constraint.** The four-round self-evolution recipe treats all problems uniformly during initial MCTS: 16 rollouts per problem in Rounds 2–3, with additional rollouts (64, then 128) only after a problem is determined to be unsolved. The paper never develops a mechanism for estimating problem difficulty *before* committing search budget, nor does it adaptively allocate rollouts based on problem characteristics. The difficulty categorization into easy/medium/hard (Section 3.4.1) is performed *after* MCTS completes, for the purpose of filtering training data, not for guiding the search itself.

**The consequence.** The self-evolution pipeline wastes enormous computational resources on easy problems that do not need 16 rollouts, while simultaneously under-allocating resources to hard problems initially (only 16 rollouts, when they may need 64+). This inefficiency is most severe in Round 4, where unsolved Olympiad problems receive up to 128 rollouts plus multiple tree expansions with different random seeds — an approach the paper itself describes as a "straightforward strategy" (Section 3.4.2) rather than a principled allocation. The total compute cost of data generation (15 nodes of 4×40GB A100 GPUs for ~3 days per round, extending to 1 week for Round 4) is driven in part by this uniform allocation.

More importantly, this limitation means rStar-Math does not learn *how much to think* per problem — a capability that human mathematicians and (reportedly) o1 possess. A skilled problem-solver can often estimate problem difficulty within the first few steps and adjust their time investment accordingly. rStar-Math's MCTS explores uniformly until terminal outcomes reveal whether the problem was easy or hard, by which point the compute has already been spent.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's test-time compute scaling analysis (Figure 3) reveals strikingly different saturation points across benchmarks. MATH performance saturates at ~8 trajectories, while College Math continues to improve through 64 trajectories. This is *post-hoc* evidence that compute requirements vary by problem difficulty, but the paper never develops a mechanism to exploit this variation during deployment — it reports results at fixed trajectory counts (8 or 64) regardless of problem characteristics. Table 2 shows that Round 2 achieves 97.88% coverage of GSM-level problems with 16 rollouts, suggesting that the vast majority of those rollouts on easy problems were unnecessary (the correct answer was found early and additional rollouts only confirmed it). The paper does not measure what fraction of MCTS rollouts produce redundant information (i.e., confirm already-discovered correct solutions) vs. novel exploration.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss difficulty estimation, adaptive compute allocation, or early stopping during MCTS. This is a missed opportunity given that the PPM's step-level scores could, in principle, serve as an online difficulty signal — if early steps consistently receive high PPM scores, the problem might be easy and warrant fewer rollouts; if early steps receive low or ambiguous scores, the problem might be hard and warrant more. This connects to a broader literature on compute-optimal test-time scaling (Snell et al., 2024) that the paper does not engage with.

### The Performance Gains Are Not FLOPs-Normalized, Making Efficiency Claims Unsubstantiated

**The assumption or constraint.** All comparisons between rStar-Math and baselines in Table 5 and Figure 3 are at **equal trajectory counts**, not equal computational cost. A single rStar-Math MCTS trajectory involves: generating 32 candidate nodes per step (vs. 1 for Best-of-N), scoring each with a 7B PPM (vs. a single ORM evaluation per complete solution), executing Python code for each candidate, and performing 4 MCTS rollouts with back-propagation (Appendix A.1). Table 9 reports average token counts per trajectory — 5,453 for MATH, 15,693 for AIME — but these are only the output tokens; they do not account for PPM forward passes, code execution overhead, or the fact that generating 32 candidates per step produces ~32× more tokens than a single step in Best-of-N sampling.

Meanwhile, the Qwen Best-of-N baseline uses independent parallel sampling: generate N complete solutions independently, score each with an ORM, select the best. There is no tree search, no per-step candidate expansion, no code execution. The per-trajectory computational cost of rStar-Math is vastly higher than Best-of-N, yet the paper compares them 1-to-1 in trajectory count.

**The consequence.** The efficiency comparison is fundamentally uninterpretable. If rStar-Math achieves 89.4% on MATH with 8 trajectories while Qwen Best-of-N achieves ~85.8% with 8 trajectories, we cannot conclude that rStar-Math is "more efficient" — it may be using 10× more FLOPs per trajectory, meaning Best-of-N could generate 80 trajectories in the same compute budget and potentially outperform rStar-Math. The paper's claim of "significantly outperforming state-of-the-art System 2 baselines" (Section 4.2) is ambiguous between "more accurate at equal trajectory count" (supported by the data) and "more accurate at equal compute" (completely unsupported).

This is particularly important for the smallest models. The paper emphasizes that rStar-Math with a 1.5B policy model and 7B PPM outperforms Qwen2.5-Math-72B-Instruct with a 72B ORM (Table 5). But the 1.5B policy model's MCTS is guided by a 7B PPM, which is 4.7× larger than the policy model itself. The total inference FLOPs per trajectory include 32 PPM forward passes per step (one for each candidate node), each running a 7B model. The effective model size during inference is not 1.5B — it is a 1.5B model + 32 calls to a 7B model per step, which is an unusual and potentially expensive architecture that the "1.5B model" headline understates.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no FLOP analysis, no latency measurements, no token-per-trajectory breakdown that separates policy model tokens from PPM tokens, and no cost-normalized comparison against baselines. Table 9 reports total tokens per trajectory without distinguishing generation tokens (policy model) from verification tokens (PPM). Appendix A.1 mentions inference hardware (4×40GB A100 GPUs) and data generation times (3 days per round, 1 week for Round 4), but these are training-time costs, not inference-time costs per problem. There is no measurement of wall-clock time per trajectory or per benchmark.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed at all. The paper does not acknowledge the need for FLOPs-normalized comparisons or discuss the computational overhead of MCTS relative to Best-of-N. This is the most significant omission in the experimental methodology because it directly affects the paper's central narrative — that small models with rStar-Math can match much larger models. Without cost normalization, we do not know whether rStar-Math is genuinely more efficient or simply trading model size for inference compute in a way that happens to look good at equal trajectory count.

### AIME and AMC Test Sets Are Too Small for Reliable Comparisons, and o1 Baselines Are Mostly Missing

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's most impressive and headline-grabbing results are on the competition-level benchmarks: AIME 2024 (30 problems) and AMC 2023 (40 problems). On AIME, rStar-Math claims to surpass o1-preview by +8.7 percentage points (53.3% vs. 44.6%) and to rank "among the top 20% the brightest high school math students" (Abstract). These are the benchmarks where rStar-Math's advantage over existing approaches is largest and most newsworthy. However, the test set sizes make these comparisons statistically fragile: on AIME, each problem represents 3.3 percentage points, meaning the margin over o1-preview corresponds to approximately 2.6 problems out of 30. On AMC (40 problems), each problem is 2.5 percentage points.

Furthermore, o1-preview and o1-mini scores are reported for only 2 of 7 benchmarks (MATH and AIME, per Table 5). The remaining benchmarks — AMC 2023, Olympiad Bench, College Math, GSM8K, and Gaokao En 2023 — have o1 entries marked as "-" because their technical reports do not include those datasets. This means the paper cannot actually characterize how rStar-Math compares to o1 across its full evaluation suite; it can only claim superiority on the two benchmarks where o1 scores happen to be available.

**The consequence.** The statistical uncertainty on AIME and AMC comparisons is large enough that the paper's strongest claims — surpassing o1-preview, achieving top-20% high school student performance — could be artifacts of sampling noise. A 95% binomial confidence interval for 53.3% accuracy on a 30-problem test is approximately ±17.9 percentage points, meaning the true accuracy could plausibly be anywhere from ~35% to ~71%. The difference between rStar-Math (53.3%) and o1-preview (44.6%) on AIME is well within this noise envelope, making the claim of superiority statistically unjustified without a larger test set or repeated sampling.

The missing o1 baselines create an asymmetric comparison: the paper can claim to surpass o1 on the benchmarks where o1 happens to be weaker or where comparison is possible, while remaining silent on benchmarks where o1 might dominate. If o1-preview were evaluated on AMC 2023 or Olympiad Bench and achieved, say, 95% and 80% respectively, the narrative would shift from "rStar-Math surpasses o1" to "rStar-Math surpasses o1 on MATH and AIME but trails substantially on other competition benchmarks." The paper cannot rule this out.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 5 shows o1 scores as "-" for 5 of 7 benchmarks. The paper acknowledges this briefly: "accuracy numbers for other benchmarks are taken from public technical reports" (Section 4.1). No confidence intervals are reported anywhere. No discussion of statistical significance appears. The AIME and AMC test set sizes (30 and 40 problems) are noted in Section 4.1 but their implications for statistical power are not discussed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper could partially address the small-test-set problem by reporting Pass@N curves (which use the full test set and show scaling trends rather than single-point estimates) or by aggregating AIME problems across multiple years to create a larger test set. It does not do either. The missing o1 baselines could be addressed by independently evaluating o1 on the missing benchmarks through the OpenAI API — something well within the capabilities of a research group at Microsoft Research Asia, but not attempted. The paper does not flag these as limitations or areas for future work.

### Code Execution Filtering Handles Syntactic but Not Semantic Errors, and Its False Negative Rate Is Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** The code-augmented CoT generation method relies on Python code execution as a filter: "only nodes with successful Python code execution are retained" (Section 3.2). The paper argues this "mitigates errors in intermediate steps" and distinguishes its approach from pure natural language generation where "incorrect or irrelevant steps yet still arrive at the correct answer by chance" (Section 3.2). The implicit assumption is that mathematical reasoning errors manifest as code execution failures — that is, if a step is wrong, its Python code will raise an exception.

**The consequence.** This assumption is demonstrably incomplete. A reasoning step can be semantically wrong (implementing incorrect logic) while being syntactically valid and executing without errors. For example, a step that computes `total_south = 1/2 + 1/2` when the correct computation should be `total_south = 1/2 * 2` would execute perfectly but produce the wrong answer. Similarly, a step could apply the wrong theorem, set up an incorrect equation, or solve for the wrong variable — all while producing valid, executable Python code. The code execution filter only catches the subset of errors that manifest as runtime exceptions (SyntaxError, NameError, TypeError, etc.), which are primarily errors in programming, not errors in mathematical reasoning.

Conversely, the code execution filter may introduce **false negatives**: mathematically valid reasoning steps that are difficult or awkward to express in executable code, causing them to fail execution despite being logically sound. For instance, reasoning by contradiction, proof by induction, or geometric arguments may not have natural Python translations, and the requirement to produce executable code for every step could systematically exclude these reasoning patterns from the training data, narrowing the policy model's learned reasoning strategies to only those expressible as imperative computations.

The paper provides no measurement of either failure mode. We do not know what fraction of mathematically correct steps fail the code execution filter (false negatives), what fraction of mathematically incorrect steps pass it (false positives that the PPM must catch), or whether the filter introduces systematic biases in which types of mathematical reasoning appear in the training data.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None that isolates the code execution filter's accuracy. Table 7 compares step-by-step verified trajectories against alternatives, but this comparison confounds the code execution filter with Q-value-based trajectory selection and MCTS-guided generation. The paper never reports: (a) the pass rate of the code execution filter (what fraction of generated candidates survive), (b) the correlation between code execution success and mathematical correctness, or (c) examples of mathematically valid reasoning that the filter rejects. The qualitative examples in Figure 4 and Appendix A.2 show only surviving trajectories; we never see what was filtered out.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this limitation. The code execution filter is presented as a straightforward quality improvement mechanism without discussion of its failure modes or the types of reasoning it cannot verify. A simple diagnostic — manually annotating a sample of filtered and surviving steps for mathematical correctness — would quantify the filter's precision and recall, but this is not performed. The paper does not discuss alternative verification mechanisms (e.g., symbolic computation, unit testing against known properties, human evaluation) that might complement code execution for reasoning patterns it cannot capture.

### Self-Reflection Is an Anecdotal Observation, Not a Validated Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper presents intrinsic self-reflection as a key finding — "a key breakthrough in OpenAI o1 is its intrinsic self-reflection capability" and rStar-Math "unexpectedly observe that our MCTS-driven deep thinking exhibits self-reflection during problem-solving" (Section 5). This claim is supported by exactly two qualitative examples: Figure 4 (where the model switches from a SymPy-based approach to a brute-force search after receiving negative PPM scores) and an additional example in Appendix A.2 (where the model corrects a parallelogram coordinate computation). The paper claims this capability "emerged" without explicit training and suggests that "advanced System 2 reasoning can foster intrinsic self-reflection."

**The consequence.** The claim rests entirely on anecdotal evidence. We have no measurement of: (1) how frequently self-reflection behavior occurs across the test set, (2) whether it is causally responsible for correct answers or merely an artifact of MCTS exploration that *looks like* reflection when linearized, (3) whether similar patterns appear in baseline MCTS systems without PPM guidance (e.g., using only terminal-guided annotation), and (4) whether the behavior is driven by PPM scores specifically or by the UCT exploration term that periodically forces the search to try alternative branches regardless of PPM feedback.

This matters because the self-reflection claim ties rStar-Math to o1 in the paper's narrative — both systems are presented as exhibiting emergent self-correction, implying a deeper connection between their reasoning mechanisms. If the observed behavior is actually an inevitable consequence of MCTS (the UCT formula naturally shifts exploration away from branches that yield negative terminal outcomes, creating backtracking patterns in the linearized trajectory), then it is not a discovery about rStar-Math specifically but a known property of tree search that has been documented since AlphaGo. The paper would be claiming a capability that is simply how MCTS works, not an emergent property of the trained models.

Conversely, if the behavior genuinely reflects the PPM recognizing low-quality steps and the policy model responding with alternative strategies, that is a more interesting claim — but it requires experimental manipulation (e.g., comparing MCTS with and without PPM, or comparing uct with pure exploitation) to establish.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Exactly two qualitative examples: Figure 4 and Appendix A.2. No quantitative measurements. No comparison to baseline MCTS configurations. No ablation examining whether self-reflection frequency correlates with PPM quality across rounds (which would support the causal link). No analysis of whether trajectories exhibiting self-reflection are more likely to be correct than those that do not.

**Mitigation status.** The paper treats self-reflection as an interesting observation deserving of a "Findings and Discussions" section (Section 5) but does not attempt to validate it systematically or establish its causal mechanism. The section title — "The emergence of intrinsic self-reflection capability" — implies a level of discovery that the evidence does not support. A more measured framing (e.g., "We observe backtracking behavior in MCTS trajectories, consistent with UCT exploration under PPM guidance") would be more accurate given the evidence provided. The paper does not suggest future work to systematically characterize or validate this behavior.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

rStar-Math represents a **reframing of what is possible with small language models in mathematical reasoning**, not through a single breakthrough technique but through the integration of several ideas — code execution as a structural filter, preference-based process reward modeling, and self-evolution through MCTS — into a coherent pipeline that progressively lifts models from weak initial states to frontier capability. The magnitude of this shift is substantial but specific: it changes the conversation around SLM reasoning from "can small models do hard math?" (the answer was largely "no" before this work) to "what is the minimal bootstrap requirement, and how do we make it even smaller?" (the answer is now "a single round with a 236B model, and ideally less").

The paper's most important conceptual contribution is **decoupling reasoning capability from model scale for mathematical problem-solving**. Prior to this work, the dominant narrative — reinforced by GPT-4's strong math performance, o1's reasoning breakthroughs, and the broad trend of scaling laws — was that complex, multi-step mathematical reasoning required either very large models (70B+) or proprietary systems with undisclosed training recipes. The distillation paradigm accepted this premise: the best you could do with a small model was compress a large model's knowledge. rStar-Math breaks this premise by demonstrating that a 7B model, given the right self-evolution infrastructure, can generate training data that *exceeds the quality of GPT-4-synthesized datasets* (Table 7: 78.4% vs. 69.6% on MATH for comparable SFT training), and can ultimately match or surpass the larger models it was initially bootstrapped from (Table 3: policy SLM-r3 surpasses DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct's 75.3% MATH with 75.8%).

This has immediate consequences for **how the field should think about the bottleneck in small-model reasoning**. The paper provides strong evidence that the bottleneck is not primarily a capacity problem — small models *can* encode the necessary mathematical knowledge — but rather a *training data quality and inference strategy* problem. The base Qwen2.5-Math-7B at 58.8% MATH and 0.0% AIME appears dramatically underpowered; the same model after rStar-Math's self-evolution reaches 89.4% and 50.0% respectively with System 2 search, and even its System 1 pass@1 reaches 78.4% and 26.7% (Table 3). The latent capability was present but inaccessible without the right training data. This reframes the challenge from "how do we build bigger models?" to "how do we extract and verify the capability already present in modestly-sized models?" — a fundamentally different research agenda.

The paper also provides a **unifying explanation for contradictory findings in the self-correction literature**. Prior work reached opposite conclusions about whether LLMs can self-correct reasoning errors: Huang et al. (2023) found "large language models cannot self-correct reasoning yet," while o1 demonstrated clear self-reflection capability. The paper's observation that self-reflection emerges in rStar-Math's MCTS trajectories *without explicit training* (Figure 4, Appendix A.2) suggests a resolution: self-correction is not a capability that must be trained into the model through specialized datasets, but rather an **emergent property of search under a reliable step-level verifier**. Models prompted or fine-tuned to self-correct fail because they lack the verifier that can recognize when a path is going wrong; o1 succeeds because it has one (whether learned or algorithmic). This reframing redirects the research question from "can we train models to self-correct?" to "can we build verifiers that reliably detect errors during search?" — a different and potentially more tractable problem.

The PPM training methodology introduces a **diagnostic insight with broad applicability**: when automatic evaluation produces noisy cardinal scores but reliable ordinal comparisons, preference-based training outperforms regression. This insight is not specific to math reasoning — any domain where Monte Carlo or heuristic methods can rank outputs more reliably than score them (code generation with partial test pass rates, dialogue with comparative human judgments, theorem proving with proof-step utility estimates) could benefit from the same reframing. The paper's explicit comparison between PQM (MSE regression onto Q-values) and PPM (pairwise ranking loss) in Table 8 provides a template for investigating this tradeoff in other domains: the PPM outperforms the PQM across all benchmarks, with the gap widening on harder problems (+3.3 on AIME, +2.5 on AMC), precisely where score noise is most severe. This is a transferable methodological finding, not just a math-specific trick.

Finally, the paper shifts the **economics of math reasoning research**. The entire 4-round self-evolution pipeline — including MCTS on 747k problems, training multiple 7B policy models and PPMs, and evaluation — runs on 15 nodes of 4×40GB A100 GPUs for approximately 3 weeks total (Appendix A.1). This is within reach of many academic research groups, dramatically lowering the barrier to entry for frontier-level math reasoning research compared to training 70B+ models from scratch or relying on proprietary API access. The paper's acknowledgments mention that "limited GPU resources and restricted access to the GPT-4 API" were constraints during early work, implying that the methodology was developed *because of* rather than *despite* these constraints. The result is a demonstration that resource-constrained research can produce frontier-level results through algorithmic innovation rather than compute scale — a potentially important signal for the broader research community.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Cold-start self-evolution without a large bootstrap model.** The paper's self-evolution pipeline depends on a single round of data generation using DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Instruct (236B). An obvious and important follow-up is to determine whether the pipeline can succeed when Round 1 uses only the base SLM (or a same-scale model) with more rounds of self-evolution to compensate for the lower initial data quality. A concrete experiment: run 6–8 rounds of self-evolution starting from Qwen2.5-Math-7B base (58.8% MATH, 0.0% AIME) with terminal-guided MCTS only, progressively increasing rollouts (16 → 32 → 64 → 128) as the policy model improves, and measure whether performance asymptotically approaches the bootstrap-assisted pipeline's results or plateaus at a lower ceiling. If cold-start self-evolution succeeds, the "no distillation" claim becomes fully substantiated and the methodology becomes truly independent of external large models. If it fails (plateauing at, say, 75% MATH rather than 90%), we learn something important about the minimum capability threshold required for self-improvement to take off — a finding with implications for self-improving AI systems more broadly. The paper's Table 2 shows that Round 1 coverage is only 60.17% overall and 20.99% for Olympiad problems when using a 236B model; starting from a 7B model these numbers would be drastically lower, making this a genuine stress test of the self-evolution hypothesis.

**FLOPs-normalized comparison of MCTS search against Best-of-N at equal cost.** The paper's comparisons are at equal trajectory counts (Table 5, Figure 3), but MCTS trajectories are substantially more expensive than Best-of-N samples due to per-step candidate generation (32 candidates), PPM scoring (32 forward passes of a 7B model per step), and code execution. A rigorous follow-up would measure the total FLOPs per trajectory for both rStar-Math MCTS and Qwen Best-of-N, then compare accuracy at equal total FLOPs rather than equal trajectory count. Specifically: for each benchmark, measure the wall-clock time and estimated FLOPs (policy model tokens × 2 × parameters + PPM tokens × 2 × parameters + code execution overhead) per trajectory for both systems, then plot accuracy vs. total FLOPs. This would reveal whether rStar-Math's MCTS overhead is justified — it is possible that Best-of-N with 10× more trajectories at the same total cost matches or exceeds rStar-Math, which would change the interpretation from "MCTS is more effective" to "MCTS is more sample-efficient but not more compute-efficient." The paper's Figure 3 shows rStar-Math at 8 trajectories outperforming Best-of-N at 64 trajectories on AIME and Olympiad Bench, providing suggestive evidence that the efficiency gain is real, but without FLOP normalization this remains conjecture.

**Systematic characterization of the code execution filter's precision, recall, and bias.** The paper's code-augmented CoT method is central to its data quality claims, but the code execution filter is never evaluated. A targeted follow-up would sample ~500 intermediate steps from MCTS trajectories (stratified by policy model round, problem difficulty, and step position), manually annotate each for mathematical correctness (is the step logically sound? does it advance the solution correctly?), and compute: (a) the filter's precision (what fraction of steps passing code execution are mathematically correct?), (b) recall (what fraction of mathematically correct steps pass code execution?), and (c) whether the filter systematically excludes certain reasoning patterns (geometric proofs, induction, contradiction arguments). This study would reveal whether the filter is a blunt instrument that catches only programming errors or a genuinely useful mathematical verification mechanism. If recall is low for certain reasoning types, follow-up work could explore hybrid verification: augmenting code execution with symbolic computation checks (SymPy equality verification), property-based testing (generating random inputs and checking invariants), or lightweight theorem proving for steps that are difficult to express as imperative code. The paper's Table 7 shows that step-by-step verified trajectories outperform rejection sampling by 5.0 percentage points on MATH for SFT training, but we cannot attribute this gain to the code filter specifically vs. the Q-value ranking without the proposed annotation study.

**Cross-domain generalization of the PPM preference-learning framework.** The paper's PPM methodology — using MCTS-derived Q-values to construct step-level preference pairs and training with pairwise ranking loss — is evaluated only on mathematical reasoning. A natural extension would test this framework in code generation (where intermediate steps are partial implementations and terminal verification comes from unit test pass/fail), theorem proving (where intermediate steps are proof tactics and terminal verification comes from the proof assistant's kernel), or multi-step planning (where intermediate steps are subgoals and terminal verification comes from plan executability). For code generation, the analog would be: (1) run MCTS where each step generates a code block with embedded test execution as the structural filter, (2) back-propagate unit test outcomes as Q-values, (3) train a code-step PPM using Bradley-Terry ranking loss on preference pairs, (4) measure whether preference-based step-level verification outperforms outcome-level verification (Best-of-N with test pass/fail) and regression-based step-level verification (MSE onto Q-values). The paper's finding that the PPM advantage widens on harder benchmarks (Table 8: +1.2 on MATH vs. +3.3 on AIME) predicts that the preference-learning advantage would be most pronounced on difficult code generation tasks where unit test coverage is sparse and Q-value noise is high.

**Difficulty-adaptive MCTS compute allocation.** The paper's test-time compute scaling analysis (Figure 3) reveals that MATH performance saturates at ~8 trajectories while AIME and College Math continue improving through 64 trajectories, implying dramatically different compute requirements per problem. The PPM's step-level scores provide a natural online difficulty signal: if early steps receive consistently high PPM scores, the problem is likely easy and MCTS can terminate early; if early steps receive low or highly variable scores, the problem warrants additional rollouts. A follow-up could implement a simple adaptive policy: run 2 MCTS rollouts, compute the average and variance of PPM scores on the first few steps, and use these statistics to predict the number of additional rollouts needed to reach a target confidence threshold (modeled as the probability that the best trajectory is correct). Train this adaptive policy on the training set's MCTS data (where we can compute the actual relationship between early-step PPM statistics and the number of rollouts needed to find a correct solution) and evaluate its efficiency against the paper's fixed 4- or 8-trajectory evaluation. The goal would be to match the fixed-budget accuracy while reducing average compute by 30–50%, particularly on easy problems. This connects rStar-Math to the broader compute-optimal test-time scaling literature (Snell et al., 2024) and addresses the paper's current inefficiency of uniform rollout allocation.

**Evaluation of rStar-Math on theorem proving and formal mathematics.** The paper briefly mentions that "rStar-Math can generalize to more challenging math tasks, such as theorem proving" and provides one qualitative example (Appendix A.2) of the system proving an Olympiad-level statement involving Fermat's Little Theorem. A rigorous follow-up would evaluate rStar-Math on a formal theorem proving benchmark (e.g., miniF2F, ProofNet, or the Lean version of IMO problems) by adapting the pipeline: replace Python code execution with Lean or Isabelle proof checking as the structural filter (each MCTS step generates a proof tactic; tactics that fail kernel verification are pruned), use ground-truth proof validity as terminal reward, train a PPM on proof-step preference pairs, and measure whether self-evolution improves the policy model's theorem proving capability. This would test whether the paper's methodology generalizes beyond computation-heavy math word problems to formal reasoning where code execution cannot serve as a verification proxy. The key challenge is that proof steps are much sparser than computation steps (a typical proof might have 5–10 tactics vs. 10–20 computation steps), so MCTS rollouts would need to be deeper per trajectory and Q-values might be noisier — the PPM's preference-based training might be even more valuable in this regime.

**Ablation of the "restart from base model each round" design choice.** The paper fine-tunes each round's policy model from the original base model rather than iteratively fine-tuning from the previous round's model (Appendix A.1), with the stated rationale of avoiding catastrophic forgetting. This is a significant design choice that has never been ablated. A direct comparison would train two policy model lineages through 4 rounds of rStar-Math self-evolution: (a) the paper's approach (restart from base each round, using progressively better training data), and (b) iterative fine-tuning (each round fine-tunes the previous round's model on the new round's training data). Measure both pass@1 and System 2 accuracy after each round. The hypothesis: iterative fine-tuning might achieve higher performance per round (since it builds on previously learned improvements) but might also plateau earlier (due to accumulation of subtle errors from imperfect training data, or due to reduced model plasticity after multiple fine-tuning epochs). The paper's current approach treats models as disposable between rounds — only the data pipeline persists. If iterative fine-tuning outperforms restart-from-base, it would suggest that model weights *can* accumulate improvements across rounds and the self-evolution is more than just a data improvement process. If restart-from-base outperforms iterative fine-tuning, it would confirm that data quality improvement is the sole mechanism and model weights are merely vessels that degrade with repeated fine-tuning — an important finding for self-improving AI systems.

**Training a difficulty estimator from MCTS statistics to enable compute-optimal deployment.** The paper's evaluation uses fixed trajectory counts (8 or 64) regardless of problem difficulty, but the PPM-augmented MCTS generates rich per-step statistics that could predict problem difficulty online. A follow-up could train a lightweight classifier on the MCTS tree statistics from the training data (features: average PPM score after 2 rollouts, variance of PPM scores, fraction of candidate nodes passing code execution, average trajectory length, Q-value convergence rate) to predict the problem difficulty bin (easy: correct solution found within 4 rollouts; medium: correct solution found within 16 rollouts; hard: correct solution not found within 16 rollouts, based on the paper's categorization in Section 3.4.1). At test time, run 2 cheap MCTS rollouts, extract features, predict difficulty, and allocate the remaining compute budget accordingly (4 rollouts for easy, 16 for medium, 64 for hard). Evaluate whether this adaptive allocation matches the fixed 8-trajectory performance with lower average compute. The paper's Table 2 shows that GSM-level problems have 97.88% coverage after 16 rollouts in Round 2 — the majority of those rollouts were wasted on problems that were solved in the first few trajectories. An adaptive scheme could dramatically reduce inference cost on easy problems while preserving (or improving) performance on hard ones, directly addressing the practical deployment limitation of MCTS's high per-problem cost.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device and edge mathematical reasoning for educational tools.** The paper demonstrates that a 1.5B parameter model with a 7B PPM achieves 87.8% on MATH and 46.7% on AIME using 8 MCTS trajectories (Table 5), performance levels that exceed most commercial tutoring systems and rival expert human performance on high-school competition math. While the 7B PPM is too large for on-device deployment on current smartphones, the core finding — that a small policy model can achieve frontier-level reasoning when paired with a reliable verifier — suggests an architecture where a 1.5B policy model runs locally, generates candidate steps, and queries a cloud-hosted 7B PPM for scoring. The PPM queries are lightweight (single forward passes on partial trajectories) and could be batched efficiently. The practical benefit is that a mathematics tutoring app could provide step-by-step, self-reflective reasoning for complex problems (up to AIME-level difficulty) without streaming the entire reasoning process through a cloud API — only verification scores need to leave the device, dramatically reducing bandwidth and latency compared to cloud-hosted generation. The paper's finding that the PPM "spots theorem-application steps" (Section 5, Appendix A.2) is particularly relevant for educational applications, as it means the system could potentially highlight *why* a particular reasoning step was scored highly, providing explanatory feedback to students.

**Automated training data generation for self-improving math models.** The paper's self-evolution pipeline is, at its core, a method for generating high-quality, step-by-step verified reasoning trajectories from unlabeled math problems. This capability has direct practical value for any organization building math-capable language models. The paper demonstrates that fine-tuning on rStar-Math-generated trajectories produces policy models that outperform those trained on GPT-4-distilled datasets (Table 7: 78.4% vs. 69.6% for NuminaMath-CoT on MATH), and the entire pipeline runs on 4×40GB A100 GPUs — hardware accessible to many companies and research groups. This means that the "data moat" that proprietary labs have built through expensive GPT-4 API distillation can be circumvented: an organization with a collection of math problems (even without solutions) can run the rStar-Math pipeline to generate verified reasoning trajectories, train a competitive math model, and avoid dependency on external API providers. The finding that randomly sampled code-augmented CoT from the self-evolved policy model already matches GPT-4-distilled data quality (Table 7: 72.4% vs. 69.6%) suggests that even a single round of self-evolution can produce training data competitive with distillation, making this practical with modest compute.

**Cost-efficient batch inference for mathematical benchmark evaluation and competition participation.** The paper reports that rStar-Math with 64 trajectories achieves 90.0% on MATH and 53.3% on AIME (Table 5). For organizations that regularly evaluate models on mathematical benchmarks or participate in math reasoning competitions (e.g., AIME, AMC, Olympiad-level challenges), rStar-Math provides a substantially more cost-effective approach than deploying 70B+ models or paying for API access to o1-level systems. Using the paper's 7B policy model + 7B PPM architecture, the cost per AIME problem at 64 trajectories is approximately 1M tokens of generation (Table 9: 15,693 tokens per trajectory × 64 trajectories ≈ 1M tokens, plus PPM scoring overhead). Running this on consumer GPUs (4×40GB A100s, as the paper uses for inference) could process all 30 AIME problems in minutes to hours, at an electricity cost of dollars rather than the hundreds or thousands of dollars that API access to frontier models might cost for equivalent performance. For organizations that need to solve thousands of math problems (e.g., generating training data, evaluating student solutions, or curating competition problem banks), this cost advantage compounds.

**Verification-as-a-service for mathematical reasoning.** The paper's central finding — that the PPM is the key determinant of System 2 performance, with different policy models converging to similar accuracy when using the same PPM (Figures 5, 7) — suggests a novel deployment model: a shared, highly-optimized 7B PPM could serve as a verification backend for many different, potentially much smaller policy models deployed in different contexts. A school district could deploy a 1.5B policy model on classroom tablets for interactive problem-solving, while a university math department could deploy a 7B policy model on a local server for contest preparation, and a math competition organization could deploy a specialized fine-tuned policy model — all querying the same cloud-hosted PPM for step-level verification. The paper shows that PPM-r4 achieves 87.0% MATH when paired with a fixed policy model (Table 4), and that this performance is relatively insensitive to which policy model generates the candidates (Figure 5). This decoupling of generation and verification into separately deployable components, enabled by the PPM's demonstrated transferability across policy models, could create an ecosystem where verification capability improves centrally while generation models diversify for different use cases and hardware constraints.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not explicitly frame rStar-Math as a choice against named alternatives with a clear decision rule. It demonstrates superiority over GPT-distillation for training data synthesis, over Best-of-N with outcome reward models for test-time verification, and over process reward models trained with regression (PQM) for step-level guidance. However, these comparisons are empirical findings, not a prescriptive framework. The paper does not provide guidance on when a practitioner should choose self-evolution over distillation, PPM over ORM, or MCTS over Best-of-N as a function of their resource constraints, problem difficulty distribution, or available bootstrap models. The conditional guidance below is therefore inferred from the paper's results and limitations, not directly stated by the authors.

**Prefer rStar-Math-style self-evolution over GPT-distillation when:**
- You cannot or prefer not to depend on proprietary API access for training data generation (the paper's self-generated data matches or exceeds GPT-4-distilled data by Round 2; Table 7).
- Your target domain has a reliable, automatic structural filter for intermediate steps (like Python code execution for math) — the filter is load-bearing during early rounds and its absence would require an alternative dense verification mechanism.
- You have access to a large bootstrap model for at least one round of initial data generation (the paper cannot confirm that cold-start self-evolution works from an SLM alone).
- Your problem distribution includes a substantial fraction of hard problems where the teacher model (e.g., GPT-4) would fail to generate correct solutions, leaving gaps in the distillation-based training set (the paper's coverage of Olympiad-level problems reaches 80.58% vs. far lower coverage from GPT-4-based datasets).

**Prefer PPM (preference-based step-level reward model) over ORM (outcome reward model) or PQM (regression-based PRM) when:**
- You need dense, step-level feedback to guide search (ORM only provides terminal signals, limiting search efficiency — Table 8 shows ORM + Best-of-N achieves 82.6% MATH vs. PPM + MCTS at 89.4%).
- The automatic Q-values or scores for intermediate steps are noisy in their absolute magnitudes but reliable in their relative ordering (the paper's central diagnostic: Q-values have strong ordinal signal but weak cardinal signal, Section 3.3).
- The difficulty distribution skews toward hard problems — the PPM advantage over alternatives grows with problem difficulty (Table 8: PPM outperforms PQM by +1.2 on MATH, +3.3 on AIME, +2.5 on AMC).

**Prefer MCTS with PPM over Best-of-N with ORM when:**
- Per-trajectory computational cost is not the binding constraint (the paper does not provide FLOPs-normalized comparisons, so the efficiency advantage of MCTS is unproven in compute-matched settings).
- Problems require multi-step reasoning where early errors cascade — MCTS with step-level PPM feedback can prune bad branches early, while Best-of-N with ORM only evaluates complete solutions.
- You can afford the engineering complexity of implementing MCTS with code execution, UCT-based selection, and PPM scoring (vs. the simplicity of Best-of-N which requires only parallel sampling and a single ORM evaluation per sample).

**The bootstrap limitation means you should NOT prefer rStar-Math if:**
- You have no access to any model larger than your target SLM for the initial bootstrap round (the paper provides no evidence that cold-start self-evolution works).
- Your domain lacks a structural filter analogous to code execution that can provide dense, objective verification during early rounds when the learned reward model is unreliable. The paper does not evaluate rStar-Math without the code execution filter, and it is unknown whether the pipeline succeeds with only terminal-guided Q-value annotation for step-level filtering.
