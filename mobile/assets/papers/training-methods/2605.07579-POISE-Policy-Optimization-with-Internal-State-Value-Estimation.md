# POISE: Policy Optimization with Internal State Value Estimation

**ArXiv:** [2605.07579](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07579)

## 🎯 Pitch

POISE eliminates the need for both a critic model and multiple rollouts per prompt in RL for reasoning models by predicting a baseline value directly from the policy’s own hidden states—signals already computed during the forward pass. A cross-rollout construction ensures this bootstrap-style probe remains unbiased, matching DAPO’s performance on math benchmarks while using ~25% less training time and generalizing across coding and tool-use tasks.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **POISE (Policy Optimization with Internal State Value Estimation)**, a reinforcement learning algorithm that replaces the group-relative baseline of GRPO or the LLM-scale critic of PPO with a lightweight probe trained to predict expected verifiable rewards from the policy model's own internal hidden states and token-entropy statistics — signals already computed during the forward pass — combined with a cross-rollout construction that keeps the baseline conditionally independent of each response to preserve gradient unbiasedness. On Qwen3-4B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B across olympiad-level math reasoning benchmarks, POISE matches DAPO's performance while requiring roughly 25–30% less wall-clock time (approximately 18 hours vs. 24 hours on a single B200 GPU for the 1.5B model), establishing that internal representations can provide a practical value estimation signal that tracks the evolving policy and generalizes across coding, tool-calling, and instruction-following domains without an auxiliary critic or large rollout groups.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Central Bottleneck in RLVR: Baselines Are Expensive

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a primary driver of progress in large reasoning models — systems that generate extended chains of thought before producing a final answer to complex math, coding, or scientific problems. The RLVR recipe is conceptually straightforward: sample a response from the policy model, check whether it is correct using an automatic verifier (e.g., string matching against a ground-truth answer, unit test execution), and update the model to increase the probability of correct responses while decreasing incorrect ones. This simple outcome-level reward signal has powered models like DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI's o1-series to dramatic improvements on competition math, competitive programming, and other reasoning benchmarks.

Yet beneath this simplicity lies a fundamental technical challenge that the current paper addresses: **variance reduction through baseline estimation is essential for stable RLVR, and existing approaches to baseline estimation are computationally expensive in ways that directly trade off against training efficiency.**

To understand why, consider the standard policy gradient estimator. When optimizing a policy $\pi_\theta$ to maximize expected verifiable reward $R(x, y)$ over prompts $x$ and responses $y$, the gradient takes the form:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi_\theta}\left[R(x, y) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\right]$$

This is the REINFORCE estimator (Williams, 1992). In practice, it is rarely used without modification because the reward signal $R(x, y)$ is a binary 0/1 value — the response was either correct or incorrect — producing a gradient with extremely high variance. The standard remedy, known since the earliest work on policy gradient methods (Sutton et al., 2000; Greensmith et al., 2004), is to subtract a **baseline** $b(x)$ from the reward to form an **advantage**:

$$A(x, y) = R(x, y) - b(x)$$

The gradient becomes $\mathbb{E}\left[A(x, y) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\right]$, and the baseline reduces variance without introducing bias *provided* it is conditionally independent of the sampled response $y$ given the prompt $x$. The near-optimal choice for $b(x)$ — the one that minimizes gradient variance — is the **value function**:

$$V^{\pi_\theta}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\left[R(x, y)\right]$$

which represents the expected reward the current policy achieves on prompt $x$. In intuitive terms: the advantage tells you not just whether a response was good, but whether it was *better than what the model typically produces for this prompt*. A correct answer to an easy question that the model answers correctly 90% of the time yields a small positive advantage; a correct answer to a hard question the model usually fails yields a much larger advantage and correspondingly stronger learning signal.

The value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ is unknown and must be estimated. **How** to estimate it is the problem that divides the two dominant families of RLVR algorithms — and it is here that the paper identifies the bottleneck that motivates POISE.

### PPO: The Critic Is an LLM-Scale Model

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) (Schulman et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022), the canonical RL algorithm for LLM post-training since InstructGPT and ChatGPT, takes the direct approach: train a separate **critic model** $v_\phi$ alongside the policy (actor) to predict the value function. The critic is typically initialized from the same pre-trained LLM backbone as the policy and augmented with a scalar value head. It processes the full prompt $x$ at every update to produce a per-token value estimate $V_\phi(s_t)$ for each intermediate state in the generation, with the terminal reward $R(x, y)$ propagated backward through the sequence using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE).

This approach is conceptually clean: the critic learns to approximate $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ directly from on-policy data, providing a per-prompt baseline that satisfies the conditional-independence requirement and can, in principle, track the evolving policy throughout training. But it comes at a **heavy computational price**. The critic model:

- **Roughly doubles memory consumption.** Both the actor and critic must be housed in GPU memory during training. For modern LLMs where a single model already saturates available accelerator memory, maintaining a second model of comparable scale is prohibitively expensive or forces reductions in batch size and sequence length that degrade training throughput.

- **Increases optimization complexity.** The critic has its own loss function, its own optimizer state, and its own hyperparameters (value loss coefficient, GAE $\lambda$, discount factor $\gamma$). Tuning these jointly with the policy introduces additional degrees of freedom and potential instability — if the critic diverges or collapses, the advantages it produces degrade, which in turn corrupts the policy updates, which shifts the data distribution the critic must learn from.

- **Requires per-token value predictions.** For reasoning models that routinely generate thousands of tokens per response, this multiplies the critic's computational footprint: a value must be predicted at every token position, not just at the prompt level. The SPPO variant (Wang et al., 2026) simplifies this to a sequence-level scalar critic — one value per prompt — reducing some but not all of the overhead.

In essence, PPO pays for its baseline by training a second LLM. For the very largest reasoning models being deployed, this cost can be the difference between training being feasible and not on a given hardware budget.

### GRPO: Replace the Critic with Group Sampling

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024), introduced in DeepSeekMath and subsequently used in DeepSeek-R1, takes the opposite approach: **eliminate the critic entirely** and estimate the baseline empirically from multiple rollouts of the same prompt. For each prompt $x$, GRPO samples a group of $G$ independent responses $\{y^{(1)}, \ldots, y^{(G)}\} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$, computes their verifier rewards $\{R(x, y^{(1)}), \ldots, R(x, y^{(G)})\}$, and uses the group mean as the baseline:

$$b_{\text{GRPO}}(x) = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} R(x, y^{(j)})$$

The advantage for each response is then $A^{(i)} = R(x, y^{(i)}) - b_{\text{GRPO}}(x)$, optionally scaled by the group standard deviation for normalization. This is elegant in its simplicity: no auxiliary model, no value loss, no GAE. The baseline is just the average reward the model achieves across multiple attempts at the same prompt.

The DAPO algorithm (Yu et al., 2025), which the current paper uses as its primary baseline, extends GRPO with several refinements — asymmetric clipping bounds (Clip-Higher) to combat entropy collapse, dynamic sampling that discards prompt groups where all responses are uniformly correct or incorrect (since such groups yield zero advantage for every response and contribute no learning signal), and token-level loss normalization — but retains the same core group-relative advantage mechanism.

**The tradeoff GRPO makes is parameters for samples.** Instead of training a critic, it generates more rollouts per prompt. This shifts the cost from model parameters to inference compute, which is a reasonable trade in regimes where sampling is cheap relative to training — but it has consequences that the paper formalizes and that motivate the search for a better approach.

### Where GRPO Falls Short: Prompt Diversity and Degenerate Groups

The paper identifies two specific ways in which GRPO's group-sampling baseline inflates training cost or degrades learning signal quality. Both stem from the fact that under a **fixed compute budget** — a fixed total number of token generations per training step — every rollout spent on an additional sample from the *same* prompt is a rollout that cannot be spent on a *different* prompt.

**Reduced prompt diversity inflates gradient variance.** Section 2.3 develops this argument formally (Proposition 1 and Corollary 1), but the intuition is straightforward. The batch gradient estimator is an average over both prompts and completions:

$$\hat{g} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m Z(x^{(i)}, y^{(ij)})$$

where $n$ is the number of distinct prompts, $m$ is the number of rollouts per prompt, and $B = n \cdot m$ is the total budget. The variance of $\hat{g}$ decomposes into two terms: a within-prompt covariance $\Sigma_w$ (scaled by $1/B$) and a between-prompt covariance $\Sigma_b$ (scaled by $m/B$). Critically, the between-prompt term grows proportionally to $m$ — using more rollouts per prompt increases gradient variance because it feeds the optimizer more correlated samples from a smaller set of prompts. Under a fixed budget $B$, **gradient variance is minimized at $m = 1$** (one rollout per prompt, maximizing prompt diversity). This is a direct consequence of the law of total covariance: the batch gradient's error comes partly from randomness within each prompt (which averages out) and partly from randomness in which prompts were sampled (which does not), and fewer distinct prompts means the latter source of noise is not suppressed.

GRPO requires $m = G$ rollouts per prompt — typically $G = 8$ or more — to obtain a reliable empirical group mean. The saved budget that could have gone to $8\times$ more distinct prompts is instead concentrated on repeated sampling from the same prompts, which the paper shows is suboptimal for gradient variance reduction.

**Degenerate groups waste compute.** A second, more subtle inefficiency: when all $G$ rollouts for a prompt receive the same reward (all correct or all incorrect), the advantage for every response is zero. These "degenerate" groups contribute no gradient signal — the policy update treats them as entirely neutral — yet they consume exactly the same generation budget as an informative group. DAPO's dynamic sampling mechanism (Yu et al., 2025) partially mitigates this by checking whether a group is degenerate and filtering it out of the training batch, but this check itself requires generating the full group first. The efficient dynamic sampling variant of Zheng et al. (2025) further reduces waste, but the fundamental issue remains: **compute is spent detecting and discarding prompts whose advantage signal is zero**, compute that could have been used to sample more informative prompts or to sample fewer responses per prompt from the start.

Both problems compound as reasoning trajectories grow longer (a trend that has been accelerating with models generating thousands of tokens of chain-of-thought), because each individual rollout becomes progressively more expensive to generate. The cost of GRPO's group sampling is proportional to average response length, and the compute wasted on degenerate groups or on within-prompt correlation grows with trajectory length.

### The Motivating Observation: Internal States Already Encode Value-Relevant Information

The paper draws on a parallel line of research that has, until now, remained separate from the RLVR training literature. A growing body of work in model interpretability and reasoning analysis has demonstrated that **the hidden states of language models encode information directly relevant to estimating outcome likelihood**:

- **Factuality and truthfulness probes.** Burns et al. (2023) showed that linear probes over hidden states can recover whether a model's generated statements are factually correct. Azaria and Mitchell (2023) found that internal states reveal when an LLM is generating falsehoods. Li et al. (2023) developed inference-time interventions that shift hidden representations to elicit more truthful outputs.

- **Confidence and correctness prediction.** Zou et al. (2023) demonstrated that representation-level signals encode the model's confidence in its outputs. In reasoning models specifically, Cencerrado et al. (2026) showed that hidden states from the prompt alone — before any generation begins — can predict whether the model will eventually produce a correct answer, and Zhang et al. (2025) found that reasoning models' activations indicate when they are "on the right track" versus heading toward an error.

- **Difficulty and capability boundaries.** Zhu et al. (2025) demonstrated that hidden states encode the model's perceived difficulty of a question, distinguishing between prompts the model can solve and those that exceed its capabilities. Devic et al. (2025) found that response length — a simple observable that correlates with difficulty — serves as an uncertainty signal in reasoning models.

- **Self-verification and early-stopping mechanisms.** Sui et al. (2025) surveyed approaches that use internal signals to terminate generation early when a correct answer has been reached, treating hidden states as a signal of completion confidence.

These findings collectively establish that the policy model's forward pass — which is already computed for every rollout during RLVR training (to evaluate log-probabilities for the policy gradient) — produces rich internal representations that are **predictive of expected reward**. The encoder's final hidden state before generation captures prompt difficulty; the hidden states during and after reasoning capture the trajectory's likelihood of success; token-level entropy statistics capture the model's uncertainty. Together, these signals approximate what a value function would compute: "given this prompt under this policy, what is the probability that a randomly sampled response will be correct?"

**The critical gap the paper identifies is that this information has been used exclusively at inference time as a diagnostic or control mechanism — never as a training signal for reinforcement learning itself.** Prior work asks: "Can hidden states tell us whether the model is right?" This paper asks: "Can hidden states replace the critic or the group baseline in RLVR policy optimization?" The transition from diagnostic to optimization signal requires addressing a challenge these prior works did not face: **the baseline must be conditionally independent of the sampled response to preserve gradient unbiasedness** (Section 2.2). A probe that predicts value from trajectory-level features — features of the same rollout whose advantage is being computed — would violate this condition, since the trajectory features contain information about the specific response $y$ that is correlated with its reward $R(x, y)$. The paper's cross-rollout construction (Section 3.2) is the mechanism that resolves this tension, enabling trajectory-conditioned features to be used for baseline estimation without introducing bias.

### How POISE Positions Itself

The paper situates POISE at the intersection of two lines of prior work that have not previously been connected:

1. **The RLVR baseline estimation literature** (PPO, GRPO, DAPO, SPPO, and their variants), which has grappled with the cost-quality tradeoff of baseline estimation — training a separate critic (expensive in parameters) versus group sampling (expensive in rollout diversity and degenerate-prompt waste). POISE's contribution is to show that a third option exists: **reuse computations the policy already performs** to estimate the baseline at negligible additional cost.

2. **The LLM interpretability literature** on probing hidden states for correctness, difficulty, and confidence, which has demonstrated the *existence* of value-relevant signals but never deployed them as optimization signals during training. POISE's contribution is to show that these signals are not just diagnostic curiosities — they are *practically useful for stable policy optimization*, matching the performance of group-relative baselines while enabling better prompt diversity and eliminating degenerate-group waste.

The paper also positions itself relative to two more recent lines of work that attempt to reduce GRPO's sampling cost through different mechanisms:

- **Non-uniform prompt sampling.** Zheng et al. (2025) and Xu & Ding (2025) propose filtering or prioritizing prompts based on historical reward informativeness, reducing the number of rollouts wasted on uninformative prompts. While effective, these methods still require initial rollouts or per-prompt reward histories to estimate informativeness — they reduce waste but do not eliminate the need for group sampling.

- **Rollout prefix reuse.** Huang & Wan (2026) propose PROS, which reuses shared prefix computations when sampling multiple rollouts from the same prompt, amortizing some of the generation cost. This addresses the *cost* of group sampling but not the *gradient variance* implication of reduced prompt diversity.

- **Generalist value priors.** Zhang et al. (2026) propose training a value model to serve as a prior for sparse rollouts, essentially a critic that is trained separately and shared across tasks. This still requires training and maintaining an auxiliary model, just decoupled from the policy training loop.

POISE is distinguished from all of these by its **elimination of both the auxiliary model and the group sampling requirement**. The value signal comes from computations the policy already performs; the baseline for a rollout comes from a different rollout's features (ensuring conditional independence); and the probe that maps features to values is a lightweight linear regressor that can be updated online with negligible cost alongside the policy. The paper frames this not as an incremental improvement to GRPO's sampling efficiency but as a **fundamentally different way to obtain a baseline** that changes what tradeoffs are available: rather than choosing between a large critic (PPO) or reduced prompt diversity (GRPO), POISE offers a path that preserves prompt diversity and avoids an auxiliary model while still providing a learned, adaptive baseline.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

POISE is a reinforcement learning algorithm that trains a language model to solve reasoning tasks by using the model's own internal thought process — its hidden states during reading and generation — to estimate how hard each problem is, replacing the expensive auxiliary critic model or large group of repeated samples that existing methods require. The system solves the problem that current RLVR algorithms pay a heavy computational price for baseline estimation: PPO needs a second full-scale language model as a critic, while GRPO needs 8+ repeated generations per prompt, which reduces prompt diversity and wastes compute on uninformative problems; POISE instead extracts a baseline from computations the policy already performs during its forward pass, using a lightweight linear probe trained online alongside the policy and a cross-rollout construction that keeps gradient estimates unbiased.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The POISE system has four major components that interact in a tightly coupled loop:

**1. Policy Model (Actor)** — a pre-trained language model (e.g., Qwen3-4B or DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B) that generates reasoning trajectories and answers for math prompts. During each training step, it produces exactly two independent rollouts per prompt and computes their verifiable rewards.

**2. Feature Extractor (Forward Hooks)** — a non-invasive mechanism that intercepts the policy model's internal hidden states during its forward pass. It collects three types of signals without additional computation: the average hidden states across the last few prompt tokens (capturing pre-generation difficulty estimates), the average hidden states across the last few reasoning tokens (capturing trajectory-level quality signals), and token-level entropy statistics from the model's output distribution (capturing uncertainty). These features are pooled, concatenated, and fed to the value estimator.

**3. Value Estimator (Linear Probe)** — a lightweight ridge regression model that maps the extracted features to a scalar prediction of the expected verifier reward $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$, i.e., "given this prompt under the current policy, what fraction of responses would be correct?" It is trained online on a sliding buffer of recent rollouts using leave-one-out Monte Carlo targets, and updated jointly with the policy at negligible cost.

**4. Advantage Computation (Cross-Rollout Construction)** — for each prompt, the two rollouts $(y^{(1)}, y^{(2)})$ are paired such that the baseline for updating $y^{(1)}$ is predicted from the features of $y^{(2)}$, and vice versa. This ensures the baseline is conditionally independent of the response forming the gradient, preserving unbiasedness while still using trajectory-conditioned features. The advantage $A^{(i)} = R(x, y^{(i)}) - b^{(i)}(x)$ feeds into a standard PPO-style clipped surrogate objective.

Information flows as follows: a batch of prompts enters the system → the policy generates two rollouts per prompt → forward hooks extract hidden states and entropy during the log-probability computation → the value estimator predicts each rollout's baseline from the other rollout's features → advantages are computed → the policy is updated with a PPO objective → the value estimator is updated on new and buffered examples → the process repeats.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal policy gradient framework (Section 2.1–2.3 from the paper), which defines what the value function is, why subtracting a baseline reduces variance, what the conditional-independence requirement means for unbiasedness, and why prompt diversity matters for gradient variance — this establishes the theoretical constraints POISE must satisfy.
- **Second**, the value estimation probe architecture and training procedure (Section 3.1), since understanding what the probe predicts, what features it uses, and how it is trained is prerequisite to understanding the full algorithm.
- **Third**, the cross-rollout advantage construction and PPO-style policy update (Section 3.2), which integrates the probe into policy optimization while preserving gradient unbiasedness — this is the novel mechanism that distinguishes POISE from prior work.
- **Fourth**, the online training coordination between policy and estimator, including the sliding buffer, the leave-one-out target construction, and how the probe tracks the evolving policy without requiring an auxiliary model — this is what makes the system practical.
- **Fifth**, the connection back to gradient variance decomposition, explaining precisely *why* POISE's two-rollout design achieves better training stability than GRPO's eight-rollout groups under the same generation budget.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **algorithm design paper** whose core idea is that the policy model's own internal representations — computed as a byproduct of the forward pass needed for policy gradient estimation anyway — contain sufficient signal to estimate the value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$, eliminating the need for either an auxiliary critic model (PPO) or large groups of repeated rollouts (GRPO), and that a cross-rollout construction preserves gradient unbiasedness while allowing trajectory-conditioned features to inform the baseline.

#### The Policy Gradient Framework and Baseline Requirements

POISE operates within the standard RLVR formulation for language model reasoning, which the paper formalizes as a contextual bandit problem. Understanding the theoretical constraints on baseline estimation is essential to understanding why POISE's design choices are necessary.

**The REINFORCE estimator and its variance problem.** Given a prompt $x$ sampled from a distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and a response $y$ sampled from the policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$, the objective is to maximize expected verifiable reward:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\left[R(x, y)\right]$$

where $R(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$ is a binary reward indicating whether the response's final answer matches the ground-truth answer.

**What it computes:** The expected fraction of problems the model answers correctly, averaged over the prompt distribution and the model's own sampling distribution.

The policy gradient theorem gives the gradient of this objective with respect to the policy parameters $\theta$:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\left[R(x, y) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\right]$$

**What it computes:** The expected direction in parameter space that increases log-probability of responses weighted by their rewards — correct responses get pushed up, incorrect ones get pushed down, in proportion to how strongly they are sampled under the current policy.

**Why this form matters:** This is the raw REINFORCE estimator (Williams, 1992), which is unbiased — its expectation equals the true gradient $J(\theta)$ — but has extremely high variance when the reward is binary because a single sample provides a very noisy estimate of the expected reward for that prompt. If the model happens to produce a correct answer to a prompt it usually fails on, the gradient treats this as strong evidence that the entire trajectory is good; if it fails on a prompt it usually succeeds on, the gradient treats the entire trajectory as bad. Both inferences are wrong in expectation, and the resulting noise slows convergence.

**Variance reduction with a baseline.** The standard fix, known since Sutton et al. (2000) and formalized by Greensmith et al. (2004), is to subtract a baseline $b(x)$ from the reward:

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\left[(R(x, y) - b(x)) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\right]$$

where $b(x)$ is any function of the prompt that is conditionally independent of the sampled response $y$.

**What it computes:** The advantage $A(x, y) = R(x, y) - b(x)$ measures whether a response was *better* ($A > 0$) or *worse* ($A < 0$) than what the model typically produces for this prompt. Correct answers to easy prompts get small positive advantages; correct answers to hard prompts get large positive advantages. This provides a much finer-grained learning signal.

**Why this form preserves unbiasedness:** The baseline term enters as $\mathbb{E}[b(x) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)] = b(x) \nabla_\theta \sum_y \pi_\theta(y \mid x) = b(x) \nabla_\theta 1 = 0$ — it vanishes in expectation because the sum of score-function gradients over the output distribution is zero. This requires that $b(x)$ depends only on $x$ (or on quantities independent of $y$ given $x$), not on $y$ itself. If the baseline used features of $y$, it would no longer factor out of the expectation and would bias the gradient estimator.

**The near-optimal baseline is the value function.** The value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ is:

$$V^{\pi_\theta}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\left[R(x, y)\right]$$

where $V^{\pi_\theta}(x) \in [0, 1]$ is the expected verifier reward — the probability that a randomly sampled response from the policy $\pi_\theta$ will be correct for prompt $x$.

**What it computes:** The prompt-level "solvability" under the current policy. If $V^{\pi_\theta}(x) = 0.3$, the model gets this problem right roughly 30% of the time; if $V^{\pi_\theta}(x) = 0.95$, it almost always succeeds.

**Why this form is optimal for variance reduction:** Greensmith et al. (2004) proved that among all baseline functions $b(x)$, the value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ minimizes the variance of the gradient estimator. Intuitively, it centers the reward at exactly the right level — correcting a response against what the model *would* produce on average, rather than against an arbitrary offset.

**The practical challenge: $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ is unknown** and changes as the policy updates. This is the estimation problem that PPO, GRPO, and now POISE solve differently.

#### The Gradient Variance Decomposition and Why Prompt Diversity Matters

The paper provides a formal argument that under a fixed generation budget, allocating rollouts across more distinct prompts reduces gradient variance. This decomposition is foundational to understanding POISE's advantage over GRPO.

Let $B$ be the total number of completions in a training batch, with $n$ distinct prompts and $m$ completions each, so $B = n \cdot m$. For prompt $x^{(i)}$ and completion $y^{(ij)}$, the per-sample gradient is:

$$Z(x, y) = \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\left(R(x, y) - b(x)\right)$$

**What it computes:** The contribution of a single prompt-response pair to the policy gradient estimate, after baseline subtraction.

The batch gradient estimator is the double average over prompts and completions:

$$\hat{g} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^m Z(x^{(i)}, y^{(ij)})$$

**Proposition 1** (Gradient variance decomposition). Let $\Sigma_w$ and $\Sigma_b$ denote the within-prompt and between-prompt covariance matrices of $Z$. Then:

$$\text{Cov}(\hat{g}) = \frac{1}{B}\Sigma_w + \frac{m}{B}\Sigma_b$$

where $\Sigma_w = \mathbb{E}_x[\text{Cov}(Z(x, y) \mid x)]$ captures how much gradient estimates vary across different completions of the *same* prompt, and $\Sigma_b = \text{Cov}_x(\mathbb{E}[Z(x, y) \mid x])$ captures how much the *expected* gradient differs across distinct prompts.

**What it computes:** The total variance of the batch gradient estimate, decomposed into a within-prompt noise term (scaled inversely by total budget $B$) and a between-prompt diversity term (scaled by $m/B$ — the fraction of the budget allocated to each prompt's group).

**Why this form matters:** The within-prompt term $\frac{1}{B}\Sigma_w$ shrinks as the total budget $B$ grows, regardless of allocation. But the between-prompt term $\frac{m}{B}\Sigma_b$ grows *linearly* with group size $m$. Using $m = 8$ (as in GRPO/DAPO) produces $8\times$ more variance from the between-prompt component than $m = 1$ (sampling each prompt once). This is because when you sample multiple rollouts from the same prompt, those rollouts share the prompt's idiosyncrasies — if the prompt happens to be one that is especially hard or easy to learn from, all $m$ gradient estimates are similarly affected, and this correlated error does not average out.

**Corollary 1** (Optimal allocation). For a fixed budget $B$, the variance of $\hat{g}$ is monotonically non-decreasing in $m$ (in the Loewner order — meaning every direction in parameter space has variance at least as large) and is minimized at $m = 1, n = B$.

**What it states:** Under any reasonable notion of matrix ordering, using one rollout per distinct prompt minimizes gradient variance for a given total compute budget.

**Why this is practically significant:** GRPO requires $m \geq G$ (typically $G = 8$) to obtain a reliable empirical group mean for its baseline. POISE requires only $m = 2$ rollouts per prompt (to enable the cross-rollout construction). The saved budget — $B \cdot (1 - \frac{2}{G})$ generations — can be redirected to sampling more distinct prompts, which Proposition 1 shows directly reduces gradient variance. This is not a heuristic argument; it follows from the covariance decomposition assuming $\Sigma_b$ and $\Sigma_w$ are fixed properties of the prompt distribution and policy.

#### Value Function Estimation from Policy Model Internal States

The core technical innovation of POISE is the probe that predicts $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ from the policy model's internal representations. This section explains what the probe predicts, what features it uses, and how it is trained.

**Probe prediction objective.** The probe $g_f$ (parameterized by $f$) is trained to predict the prompt-level value under the current policy. Since the ground-truth value is unknown, the authors construct a Monte Carlo target from $K$ independently sampled rollouts. For a prompt $x$ with rollouts $\{y^{(1)}, \ldots, y^{(K)}\} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$ and corresponding binary rewards $\{r^{(1)}, \ldots, r^{(K)}\}$, the target for the supervised example associated with rollout $y^{(i)}$ is the **leave-one-out Monte Carlo estimate**:

$$\hat{V}_{-i}(x) = \frac{1}{K-1}\sum_{j \neq i} r^{(j)}$$

where $\hat{V}_{-i}(x) \in \{0, \frac{1}{K-1}, \frac{2}{K-1}, \ldots, 1\}$ is the empirical fraction of the other $K-1$ rollouts that were correct — excluding $r^{(i)}$ itself.

**What it computes:** An unbiased estimate of $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ that is, crucially, conditionally independent of $y^{(i)}$ given $x$, because it does not use $r^{(i)}$ in its computation. This ensures that during probe training, the target does not leak reward information from the same rollout whose features are being used as input.

**Why leave-one-out matters:** If the target included $r^{(i)}$, the probe could learn to directly predict the reward of its own rollout rather than the prompt-level expected reward — memorizing trajectory-specific outcomes rather than learning the prompt's difficulty. At training time this would produce overly optimistic value estimates (the probe sees the rollout's reward in its target); at deployment time within POISE, since the baseline for $y^{(i)}$ is computed from $y^{(j)}$'s features, the leave-one-out construction during probe training mirrors the cross-rollout structure of the algorithm.

**The loss function for the probe.** The probe $g_f$ is trained to minimize mean squared error:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{value}}(f) = \mathbb{E}_{x, i}\left[\left(g_f(\phi^{(i)}_\theta) - \hat{V}_{-i}(x)\right)^2\right]$$

where $\phi^{(i)}_\theta$ is the feature vector extracted from the policy forward pass for $(x, y^{(i)})$ (described below) and $\hat{V}_{-i}(x)$ is the leave-one-out target.

**What it computes:** The expected squared difference between the probe's predicted value for prompt $x$ and the empirical fraction of other rollouts that were correct. Minimizing this encourages the probe to output $g_f(\phi) \approx \mathbb{E}[R(x, y) \mid \phi] \approx V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$.

**Why this loss:** Mean squared error is the appropriate objective for a real-valued regression target with interpretable scale (the targets range from 0 to 1). It penalizes large deviations quadratically, which is important because value estimates near 0 or 1 have outsized impact on advantage computation — a probe that confidently predicts $0.95$ when the true value is $0.6$ would produce systematically negative advantages for correct responses, incorrectly penalizing the policy.

**Probe input features.** For each pair $(x, y^{(i)})$, three complementary signals are extracted from the policy model's forward pass (Figure 2, left). All hidden-state features are extracted from a fixed layer $\ell$ (set to layer 19 for both Qwen3-4B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, based on ablation studies in Appendix E.1). The features are collected during the teacher-forced log-probability forward pass, which is already required by the PPO policy update — no additional forward passes are needed. Let $H^{(i)}_{\theta, t}$ denote the residual-stream hidden state at token position $t$ for $(x, y^{(i)})$ from layer $\ell$, and let $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{R}^{(i)}$ denote the final prompt-token and reasoning-token positions, respectively.

**Feature 1: Prompt hidden state.** The prompt-level feature is the mean-pooled hidden state over the last 10 prompt tokens:

$$h^{(i)}_{\theta, p} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{-10:}|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{P}_{-10:}} H^{(i)}_{\theta, t}$$

**What it computes:** A fixed-dimensional vector of size $d_{\text{model}}$ (2560 for Qwen3-4B, 1536 for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B) that summarizes the model's representation of the prompt just before generation begins. The last 10 tokens are used because they are closest to the generation boundary and thus most likely to encode the model's "anticipation" of what it is about to produce.

**Why this feature:** This is motivated by prior work showing that prompt hidden states encode pre-generation estimates of difficulty and capability boundaries (Zhu et al., 2025; Cencerrado et al., 2026). A prompt feature alone cannot capture whether a *particular* trajectory succeeded, but it can capture the model's overall assessment of how hard the prompt is given its current capabilities — precisely the prompt-level component of $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$.

**Feature 2: Reasoning hidden state.** The trajectory-level feature is the mean-pooled hidden state over the last 10 reasoning tokens:

$$h^{(i)}_{\theta, r} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}^{(i)}_{-10:}|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{R}^{(i)}_{-10:}} H^{(i)}_{\theta, t}$$

**What it computes:** A second $d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional vector summarizing the model's state at the end of reasoning, just before the answer tokens begin. The last 10 reasoning tokens are used (rather than all reasoning tokens) to focus on the conclusion of the chain-of-thought, where error signals are most concentrated.

**Why this feature:** While prompt-level features capture *a priori* difficulty, trajectory-level features capture *a posteriori* quality — whether the reasoning process went well or poorly, independent of the final answer. Zhang et al. (2025) showed that reasoning models' end-of-trajectory hidden states encode whether the model is "right" or "wrong" even before the answer is extracted. Critically, in the POISE cross-rollout construction, this feature from rollout $j$ is used to predict the value for rollout $i$ ($i \neq j$), meaning the feature correlates with prompt-level difficulty (since both rollouts are from the same prompt) but not with the specific reward of rollout $i$ — satisfying conditional independence.

**Feature 3: Token-level entropy statistics.** Three scalar statistics are computed from the model's output distribution during generation:

$$u^{(i)}_\theta = \left[\text{mean}(H_t), \text{std}(H_t), \frac{\text{count}(H_t > \tau)}{\text{len}(\mathcal{R}^{(i)})}\right]$$

where $H_t = -\sum_{v} \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y^{(i)}_{<t}) \log \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y^{(i)}_{<t})$ is the token-level entropy at position $t$, $\tau$ is a threshold (implicitly, the paper computes the fraction of high-entropy tokens), and the statistics are averaged over the reasoning tokens $\mathcal{R}^{(i)}$.

**What it computes:** Three numbers summarizing the model's uncertainty during generation: the average entropy (overall confidence level), the standard deviation of entropy (consistency of confidence), and the fraction of tokens where entropy exceeds a threshold (presence of high-uncertainty decision points). Higher entropy indicates the model is less certain about its token choices, which correlates with higher probability of error.

**Why these features:** Wang et al. (2026) showed that high-entropy tokens are disproportionately important for RLVR learning — they represent decision points where the model is uncertain and thus where gradient signals are most informative. Including entropy statistics gives the value estimator a lightweight signal about generation quality that complements the hidden-state features, and because entropy is computed from the model's own output distribution rather than from hidden states, it provides a semi-orthogonal source of information.

**The full probe input.** The three feature groups are concatenated:

$$\phi^{(i)}_\theta = [h^{(i)}_{\theta, p}; h^{(i)}_{\theta, r}; u^{(i)}_\theta]$$

producing a vector of dimension $2d_{\text{model}} + 3$: 5123 for Qwen3-4B ($2560 \times 2 + 3$) and 3075 for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B ($1536 \times 2 + 3$).

**What it computes:** A single vector combining the model's assessment of the prompt difficulty (prompt hidden states), the quality of the generated reasoning (reasoning hidden states), and the model's uncertainty during generation (entropy statistics). This vector is the input to the value estimator.

**Why this concatenation:** Each feature type captures a different aspect of value-relevant information. The ablation in Table 3 shows that reasoning hidden states alone achieve MAE 0.132 and Pearson $r = 0.821$, prompt hidden states alone achieve MAE 0.234 and $r = 0.569$, and entropy alone achieves MAE 0.152 and $r = 0.780$. Combining all three yields MAE 0.126 and $r = 0.838$, confirming that the features are complementary — each contributes information not fully captured by the others.

**Probe implementation and architecture.** The paper uses a linear ridge regression model for the probe, implemented as a `StandardScaler` (normalizing features to zero mean and unit variance) followed by a `Ridge` regressor with penalty $\alpha = 0.01$. The output is clipped to $[0, 1]$.

**Why linear regression:** The ablation in Appendix E.2 (Table 12) compares linear ridge against MLPs of varying depth (1, 3, 5, 7, 9 layers) and width (512, 1024 hidden units). While the best MLP (3 layers × 1024 width) achieves slightly lower MAE (0.117 vs. 0.124), the linear probe achieves the highest Pearson correlation (0.834 vs. 0.801 for the best MLP). Higher correlation is more important than lower MAE for value estimation because the advantages use the *relative* ordering of values across prompts, not the absolute calibration. Additionally, linear regression (i) is much faster to update each training step (a closed-form ridge solution vs. iterative gradient descent), (ii) has no learning rate or optimizer state to tune, and (iii) aligns with prior findings by Park et al. (2024) and Elhage et al. (2021) that many semantic features in Transformers are encoded as linear directions in representation space, making a linear probe well-suited to extract them.

**Preliminary validation experiment.** Before integrating the probe into RL training, the paper conducts a held-out value prediction experiment (Figure 1) to verify that the policy's internal states actually contain enough signal. On a fixed dataset of reward-labeled rollouts from DAPO-Math, two estimators are trained and evaluated against empirical Avg@8 scores on held-out prompts: a separate policy-scale critic model (the SPPO-style sequence-level critic described in Appendix D.1, fine-tuned from the same Qwen3-4B backbone) and the internal state probe. The probe achieves Pearson $r = 0.870$ and MAE $= 0.141$, substantially better than the critic's $r = 0.676$ and MAE $= 0.262$. This result is remarkable because the probe uses only the policy's hidden states and entropy — no separate model, no additional parameters beyond a linear regressor — yet *outperforms* a full LLM-scale critic trained on the same data. The paper interprets this as evidence that the policy's internal representations form a compact, highly informative signal for prompt-level value, and that training a separate model to recover this signal from scratch is actually harder than reading it directly from the policy's activations.

#### Practical Value Estimation: Leave-One-Out Monte Carlo Targets in the Training Loop

The probe described in the previous section was trained on a static dataset. In POISE's online RL training, the probe must track the evolving policy — the value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ changes as the policy improves, and the probe must adapt.

**Online target construction.** During each training step, for each prompt $x$ with two independent rollouts $(y^{(1)}, y^{(2)})$ and their rewards $(r^{(1)}, r^{(2)})$, the authors construct value-estimator training examples with leave-one-out targets. Since $K = 2$, the leave-one-out target for rollout $y^{(i)}$ is simply the other rollout's reward:

$$\hat{V}_{-i}(x) = r^{(j)}, \quad j \neq i$$

**What it computes:** The binary reward of the *other* rollout from the same prompt. With only two rollouts, this is a noisy estimate of $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ — it is either 0 or 1, not a continuous fraction — but it is unbiased in expectation ($\mathbb{E}[r^{(j)} \mid x] = V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$) and conditionally independent of $y^{(i)}$.

**Why $K = 2$ is sufficient:** The probe is trained on a *sliding buffer* of recent examples (described below), not just the current step's data. Over many steps and many prompts, the probe sees many binary targets for prompts with similar features, and the ridge regression aggregates these noisy samples into a stable value estimate. The binary target at each step is unbiased, so the probe converges to predict $\mathbb{E}[r^{(j)}] = V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ in expectation, albeit with slower convergence than if each target were a continuous Avg@K score. The key tradeoff is that using $K = 2$ minimizes the generation cost per prompt, maximizing prompt diversity under Proposition 1.

**Trajectory buffer for stable training.** The probe is trained on examples accumulated in a buffer $\mathcal{B}_V$ of size $S$ (implicitly set to a few hundred steps based on the buffer eviction policy: "examples older than $n$ steps" are evicted). At each step, the newly constructed examples $\{(\phi^{(1)}, r^{(2)}), (\phi^{(2)}, r^{(1)})\}$ are appended to $\mathcal{B}_V$, and the oldest examples are evicted. The probe is then updated by minimizing the MSE loss over the union of new and buffered examples.

**Why a buffer:** The value function changes gradually as the policy updates — a checkpoint from 50 steps ago is still somewhat informative about the current policy's behavior, especially for prompts the policy hasn't dramatically changed on. The buffer provides two benefits: (i) it smooths the training signal, preventing the probe from overfitting to the current batch's noise, and (ii) it provides more diverse examples (different prompts, different policy snapshots) for the ridge regression to learn from, improving generalization. The sliding-window design ensures the buffer stays roughly on-policy — old examples are continuously evicted as the policy drifts, keeping the probe aligned with recent behavior.

**Why ridge regression enables fast online updates:** A linear ridge regressor can be fit efficiently using either a closed-form solution (solving a linear system) or incremental updates (online ridge regression). The paper does not specify which approach is used, but the computational cost is negligible compared to the policy forward and backward passes — fitting a linear model on a few thousand examples with a few thousand features takes milliseconds on GPU. This is in contrast to training a separate critic model, which requires its own forward pass, loss computation, and backward pass through the full LLM backbone at every step.

**Prediction clipping to $[0, 1]$.** The probe's raw output is clipped to $[0, 1]$ to ensure it produces valid probability estimates. Since the true value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ is a probability (expected binary reward), outputs outside $[0, 1]$ are nonsensical and could produce pathological advantages (e.g., a predicted value of $-0.5$ would make every correct response look like a massive positive outlier, inflating gradient magnitudes).

#### Policy Optimization with Cross-Rollout Baselines

The full POISE algorithm integrates the internal state probe into policy optimization through a cross-rollout construction that resolves the tension between using trajectory-conditioned features (which improve value prediction accuracy) and maintaining conditional independence (which preserves gradient unbiasedness).

**Two rollouts per prompt.** For each prompt $x$ in the training batch, the policy generates exactly two independent rollouts:

$$y^{(1)}, y^{(2)} \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x)$$

where $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is the policy frozen at the start of the current PPO update (the "old" policy against which importance ratios are computed). Sampling is done with temperature 1.0 and top-p 1.0 (effectively, standard nucleus sampling with maximum diversity).

**What it computes:** Two independent reasoning trajectories and answers for the same prompt, sampled from the identical policy distribution. Both are needed for the cross-rollout baseline; using more than two would increase the within-prompt correlation penalty identified in Proposition 1.

**Why exactly two:** Two is the minimum number that enables the cross-rollout construction (you need at least one independent rollout to compute the baseline for another). It also minimizes $m$ in the variance decomposition, maximizing prompt diversity for a given budget. The paper's theoretical Corollary 1 shows $m=1$ is optimal, but $m=1$ provides no second rollout and thus no cross-rollout baseline. $m=2$ is the smallest practical value that enables the method.

**Cross-rollout baseline and advantage computation.** The features for each rollout are extracted from the policy forward pass: $\phi^{(1)}$ from $(x, y^{(1)})$ and $\phi^{(2)}$ from $(x, y^{(2)})$. The baseline for each rollout is predicted from the *other* rollout's features:

$$b^{(1)}(x) = g_f(\phi^{(2)})$$
$$b^{(2)}(x) = g_f(\phi^{(1)})$$

where $g_f$ is the current value estimator (the ridge regression probe trained on the sliding buffer). The advantages are:

$$A^{(i)}(x) = R(x, y^{(i)}) - b^{(i)}(x), \quad i \in \{1, 2\}$$

**What it computes:** For rollout 1: "Was this response better than what the model would typically produce for this prompt, as estimated from the *other* rollout's internal state signals?" For rollout 2, symmetrically.

**Why the cross-rollout construction preserves unbiasedness:** Crucially, $b^{(1)}(x) = g_f(\phi^{(2)})$ depends on $y^{(2)}$ but *not* on $y^{(1)}$. Since $y^{(1)}$ and $y^{(2)}$ are independent samples from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid x)$, $b^{(1)}(x)$ is conditionally independent of $y^{(1)}$ given $x$. This means:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y^{(1)}}\left[b^{(1)}(x) \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y^{(1)} \mid x)\right] = b^{(1)}(x) \nabla_\theta \sum_{y^{(1)}} \pi_\theta(y^{(1)} \mid x) = 0$$

The baseline term vanishes in expectation, preserving the unbiasedness of the gradient estimator despite using trajectory-conditioned features.

**What would go wrong without cross-rollout:** If the baseline for $y^{(1)}$ used features from $y^{(1)}$ itself — i.e., $b^{(1)}(x) = g_f(\phi^{(1)})$ — then the baseline would be correlated with the reward $R(x, y^{(1)})$ through the shared trajectory features. The probe would tend to predict higher values for trajectories that actually succeeded (because the reasoning hidden states encode success signals) and lower values for trajectories that failed. Subtracting this baseline would systematically *reduce* the advantage of successful trajectories and *increase* the advantage of failed trajectories (since the baseline partially "predicts" the outcome), biasing the gradient toward under-updating successful trajectories and over-updating failed ones. The cross-rollout construction breaks this correlation — the baseline for $y^{(1)}$ sees only the features of $y^{(2)}$, which contains no information about $R(x, y^{(1)})$ beyond what is shared at the prompt level.

**Why trajectory-conditioned features are still valuable:** Even though the baseline for rollout $i$ uses rollout $j$'s features, those features *do* contain information about the prompt $x$ — specifically, about the model's processing of the prompt and its uncertainty when generating a response. If the model is highly uncertain on a prompt (high average entropy) and its reasoning states at the end of $y^{(j)}$ indicate a difficult trajectory, the probe learns to predict a lower $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ for that prompt, which becomes the baseline for $y^{(i)}$. This is more informative than a prompt-only baseline (which would only see the prompt hidden states, not the generation-time signals of difficulty), while still satisfying conditional independence.

#### PPO-Style Policy Update with Cross-Rollout Advantages

The policy is optimized with a standard PPO clipped surrogate objective (Schulman et al., 2017) adapted to use the cross-rollout advantages. Let $r^{(i)}_t(\theta)$ be the importance ratio at token $t$ of rollout $i$:

$$r^{(i)}_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y^{(i)}_t \mid x, y^{(i)}_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y^{(i)}_t \mid x, y^{(i)}_{<t})}$$

**What it computes:** The factor by which the updated policy's probability of token $y^{(i)}_t$ (given the prompt and preceding tokens) differs from the old policy's probability. A ratio $> 1$ means the update increased probability; $< 1$ means it decreased.

The PPO objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x, y^{(1)}, y^{(2)}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^2 \frac{1}{|y^{(i)}|}\sum_{t=1}^{|y^{(i)}|} \min\left(r^{(i)}_t(\theta) A^{(i)}(x), \text{clip}(r^{(i)}_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) A^{(i)}(x)\right)\right]$$

where $|y^{(i)}|$ is the number of tokens in rollout $i$, $\epsilon = 0.2$ is the clipping parameter (lower bound), and the upper clipping bound is $0.28$ (following DAPO's Clip-Higher technique, though the paper uses the standard PPO formulation in Equation 14).

**What it computes:** For each token in each rollout, the objective takes the minimum of (a) the unclipped importance-weighted advantage $r^{(i)}_t A^{(i)}$ and (b) the clipped version $\text{clip}(r^{(i)}_t, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) A^{(i)}$. When the advantage is positive ($A^{(i)} > 0$), the clip prevents the policy from increasing the token's probability by more than a factor of $1 + \epsilon$ relative to the old policy — this prevents destructive large updates from a single batch. When the advantage is negative ($A^{(i)} < 0$), the clip prevents decreasing probability by more than $1 - \epsilon$. The minimum ensures the objective is a *pessimistic* bound on the unclipped objective, providing a trust-region effect without explicit KL constraints. The loss is normalized by total token count ($|y^{(i)}|$) so that longer responses contribute proportionally, not dominantly.

**Why PPO is used rather than a simpler policy gradient:** The clipped surrogate provides stability — without it, a single high-advantage rollout could push token probabilities to extreme values, causing the policy to collapse to deterministic outputs (entropy collapse) or diverge entirely. The PPO clip creates a trust region around the old policy, ensuring each update is small enough to preserve the policy's generative capabilities while still making progress.

**Entropy coefficient and KL loss are set to zero:** The paper's hyperparameters (Table 5) set the entropy coefficient to 0 and disable KL loss — unlike standard PPO implementations that add an entropy bonus to encourage exploration. The paper likely relies on the PPO clip itself and the inherent stochasticity of sampling at temperature 1.0 to maintain sufficient exploration.

**Multiple inner epochs per batch.** The policy objective is maximized over multiple inner epochs (implicitly, based on the `mini_batch_size` of 16 out of batch sizes 512 or 1024, suggesting roughly 32–64 inner epochs per batch). Each inner epoch processes a sub-sampled mini-batch of the full training batch, computing the PPO loss and updating $\theta$ via gradient descent with learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$.

#### Online Training Coordination and the Full Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

The policy and value estimator are trained jointly in a loop, with careful coordination to ensure the estimator tracks the evolving policy. Algorithm 1 in Appendix B.1 provides the procedural pseudocode; this section explains the coordination logic.

**Initialization.** At step 0, the policy $\theta_0$ is the pre-trained base model (Qwen3-4B or DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B) and the value estimator $f_0$ is a freshly initialized ridge regressor (with `StandardScaler` fit to the initial feature distribution). The value buffer $\mathcal{B}_V$ is empty.

**Step structure.** Each training step consists of the following phases:

**Phase 1: Rollout generation (lines 4–9 of Algorithm 1).** A mini-batch of $M$ prompts is sampled from $\mathcal{D}$, where $M$ is the effective prompt batch size (1024 for Qwen3-4B, 512 for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B). For each prompt, two rollouts are sampled from the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, features are extracted from the forward pass, and verifier rewards are computed. This phase is dominated by autoregressive generation — the most computationally expensive part of RLVR training.

**Phase 2: Advantage computation (lines 10–12).** For each prompt, the cross-rollout baselines are computed using the current value estimator $g_f$. The advantages $A^{(i)} = R - b^{(i)}$ are stored alongside the rollouts for the policy update. This phase is essentially free — invoking a linear regressor on pre-computed features.

**Phase 3: Policy update (line 14).** The PPO objective in Equation 14 is maximized over multiple inner epochs on the collected batch. Only the policy parameters $\theta$ are updated; the value estimator is frozen during this phase.

**Phase 4: Value estimator update (lines 15–16).** The new examples $\{(\phi^{(1)}, r^{(2)}), (\phi^{(2)}, r^{(1)})\}$ are added to the buffer $\mathcal{B}_V$, and examples older than $n$ steps are evicted (the paper does not specify $n$ explicitly, but the buffer size is implied to be several hundred to a few thousand examples based on typical sliding-window designs). The value estimator $f$ is updated by minimizing the MSE loss over $\mathcal{B}_V$ using ridge regression (either closed-form refit or incremental update). This phase is extremely cheap — fitting a linear model on a few thousand examples.

**Phase 5: Policy becomes the new old policy.** $\theta_{\text{old}} \leftarrow \theta$ for the next step's importance ratio computation.

**Why the value estimator is updated after the policy:** The new rollouts were generated from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, which is the policy *before* the current inner-epoch updates. Updating the value estimator after the policy update ensures the probe is trained on features from the policy that generated the data (on-policy), while the policy itself has moved forward. This one-step lag is acceptable because the value function changes gradually — the probe's buffer contains examples from recent steps (within the sliding window), providing a smoothed estimate across the recent policy trajectory.

**Sliding buffer stability under policy drift.** As training progresses, the policy improves — pass rates increase, the distribution of generated responses changes, and consequently the value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ shifts upward for most prompts. The sliding buffer ensures the estimator's training data remains roughly aligned: examples older than $n$ steps are evicted, and new examples from the current policy continuously enter. The paper's training dynamics analysis (Appendix C, Figures 7 and 8) confirms this works in practice: the predicted value $\bar{b}_t$ tracks the batch reward $\bar{R}_t$ closely throughout training (rising from ~0.30 to ~0.70 for Qwen3-4B, and from ~0.15 to ~0.45 for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B), and the online target MAE remains bounded rather than diverging.

**Why the buffer is needed rather than training only on the current batch:** A single batch of $2M$ examples (two per prompt) is insufficient to fit a ridge regression model with $2d_{\text{model}} + 3$ features — the feature dimension (5123 for Qwen3-4B) far exceeds the number of examples in one batch, and the targets are extremely noisy (binary, not continuous). The buffer provides the necessary sample size for the ridge regression to converge, while the sliding window maintains approximate on-policiness.

#### Connection Back to Gradient Variance: Why POISE Stabilizes Training

The paper's training dynamics analysis (Figure 3, right) shows that POISE maintains consistently lower gradient norms than DAPO throughout training. This is not coincidental — it follows from the variance decomposition in Proposition 1.

**DAPO configuration.** Under the efficient dynamic sampling variant (Zheng et al., 2025), DAPO attempts to fill each training batch with $n = 128$ effective prompt groups (those that survive the degeneration filter), each with $m = 8$ rollouts, for a total budget of $B = 1024$ generations. However, dynamic sampling requires generating additional groups (the `default_br_size` of 192 in Table 6) to ensure enough non-degenerate groups are collected, meaning the actual generation budget exceeds the final training batch size. The effective $m$ per training prompt is 8.

**POISE configuration.** POISE uses a prompt batch size $M = 512$ (for the 1.5B model) or $M = 1024$ (for the 4B model) with $m = 2$, achieving the same or greater total budget while putting more distinct prompts in each batch.

**Why gradient norms are lower:** Under Proposition 1, POISE's gradient covariance is:

$$\text{Cov}_{\text{POISE}}(\hat{g}) = \frac{1}{B}\Sigma_w + \frac{2}{B}\Sigma_b$$

while DAPO's is:

$$\text{Cov}_{\text{DAPO}}(\hat{g}) = \frac{1}{B}\Sigma_w + \frac{8}{B}\Sigma_b$$

The within-prompt noise term $\frac{1}{B}\Sigma_w$ is identical (same total budget). But the between-prompt diversity term is $4\times$ larger for DAPO — for the same total compute, DAPO's gradient estimate is noisier in every parameter direction by a factor proportional to $\Sigma_b$. The gradient norm $\|\hat{g}\|_2$ reflects both the true gradient signal and the estimation noise; at similar training progress (similar policy quality), the true gradient should be comparable, so the lower norm in POISE primarily reflects lower estimation noise. This translates to more stable updates — each parameter step is more likely to point in the correct direction, requiring fewer steps to converge and reducing the risk of destructive large updates from high-variance batches.

**Additional variance reduction from continuous value predictions.** GRPO's group-mean baseline $b_{\text{GRPO}}(x) = \frac{1}{8}\sum_{j=1}^8 r^{(j)}$ is a discrete estimate taking values in $\{0, 1/8, \ldots, 1\}$. For prompts where the true $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ is, say, 0.45, the empirical mean of 8 binary samples will often be 0.375 or 0.5 — off by 0.075–0.125 in either direction. POISE's learned baseline, by contrast, predicts a continuous value that aggregates signal across many prompts in the buffer, producing a more accurate estimate of $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$. Better baseline accuracy directly reduces gradient variance, since the advantage noise is $\text{Var}(R - b) = \text{Var}(R) + \text{Var}(b) - 2\text{Cov}(R, b)$, and a more accurate $b$ (higher $\text{Cov}(R, b)$) reduces this quantity.

**The online variance reduction ratio quantifies this effect.** Figure 4 (and Appendix C, Figures 6–8) reports the ratio $\rho_t = \text{Var}(A_t)/\text{Var}(R_t)$, which measures what fraction of the raw reward variance remains after subtracting the learned baseline. The paper reports that after the initial training phase, POISE achieves a variance reduction of roughly 30% ($\rho \approx 0.7$), meaning the advantage has about 70% of the variance of the raw reward. This is a meaningful reduction from a learned baseline that costs essentially nothing — the probe is trained on features already computed during the policy forward pass, and its update step is a lightweight ridge regression. In contrast, GRPO's group-mean baseline theoretically reduces variance by a factor of roughly $\frac{G-1}{G} = \frac{7}{8} = 87.5\%$ (for independent samples), but this comes at the cost of $8\times$ more rollouts per prompt and the associated within-prompt correlation penalty identified in Proposition 1.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

The paper makes several non-obvious design choices, each motivated by a combination of theoretical constraints and empirical ablations:

**Linear ridge regression over MLP for the probe.** As discussed in the probe architecture section and ablated in Appendix E.2, the linear probe achieves the highest Pearson correlation with ground-truth values ($r = 0.834$) despite slightly higher MAE than the best MLP. Higher correlation is prioritized because advantages depend on the relative ordering of values across prompts, not absolute calibration. Additionally, linear regression enables fast closed-form updates and avoids the optimizer tuning, learning rate scheduling, and overfitting risk of neural network probes.

**Layer 19 for both model backbones.** The layer ablation in Appendix E.1 (Tables 8 and 9) shows that value-predictive information is broadly distributed across layers. For Qwen3-4B, the best performance is at layer 19 ($r = 0.834$) and layer 33 ($r = 0.831$). For DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, surprisingly, layer 1 performs best ($r = 0.425$), but the differences across layers are small, and layer 19 remains close to optimal ($r = 0.395$). The paper chooses layer 19 for both backbones to avoid model-specific layer tuning and keep the implementation consistent — a pragmatic engineering choice that incurs minimal performance loss.

**Last 10 tokens for pooling.** The pooling-window ablation (Tables 10, 11) shows that last-5, last-10, and last-15 pooling yield near-identical performance (MAE differences under 0.003), with last-10 giving the best Pearson correlation for Qwen3-4B ($r = 0.834$). Using the last 10 tokens (rather than all tokens or only the final token) provides a balance between temporal resolution (capturing the end-of-reasoning state) and noise reduction (averaging across multiple token positions smooths token-level noise). Using all reasoning tokens would be impractical for long chains of thought (thousands of tokens), and using only the final token would be too noisy.

**Ridge penalty $\alpha = 0.01$ (not $L^2$ regularization strength).** A small ridge penalty prevents the linear regression from overfitting to noise in the high-dimensional feature space (5123 features on potentially only a few thousand buffered examples). The specific value $\alpha = 0.01$ is small enough to allow the probe to fit genuine signal but large enough to stabilize the solution when the feature matrix is ill-conditioned.

**Sampling temperature 1.0 and top-p 1.0.** These are standard exploration settings in RLVR — maximum diversity during rollout generation prevents the policy from prematurely collapsing to a narrow distribution, ensuring the value estimator sees varied features and the policy gradient sees both successful and unsuccessful trajectories.

**Two rollouts per prompt (not one, not more).** As discussed in the cross-rollout section: $m = 1$ provides no second rollout for the cross-rollout baseline; $m = 2$ is the minimum that enables the method; $m > 2$ would increase the between-prompt covariance term in Proposition 1 without proportionally improving baseline accuracy (since the probe aggregates across many prompts in the buffer, not just the current batch). The choice of exactly two is the natural optimum under the variance decomposition.

**No entropy bonus, no KL penalty.** Unlike standard PPO implementations for language models (e.g., Ouyang et al., 2022), POISE sets both the entropy coefficient and KL loss to zero. The paper does not explicitly justify this, but the likely reasoning is: (i) the PPO clip itself provides sufficient regularization against extreme policy shifts, (ii) sampling at temperature 1.0 maintains exploration without needing an entropy bonus, and (iii) DAPO (the baseline) also uses zero entropy coefficient and no KL loss, so this is consistent with the RLVR for reasoning literature, which has found that reasoning models benefit from larger, less regularized updates than instruction-following models.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Internal Representations as Optimization Signals, Not Just Diagnostic Tools

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is bridging two literatures that have operated in complete isolation: RLVR baseline estimation (PPO, GRPO, DAPO), which has treated the value function as something that must be estimated from *external* computation (a separate critic or repeated rollouts), and LLM interpretability (Burns et al., 2023; Azaria & Mitchell, 2023; Zhang et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2025), which has shown that hidden states encode outcome-relevant information — difficulty, correctness, confidence — but has used this information exclusively as a *diagnostic* signal at inference time.

The shift POISE makes is to recognize that these signals are not merely descriptive — they are *operationally sufficient* to replace the external baseline mechanisms that RLVR depends on. This is a category change: hidden states move from "something we probe to understand what the model knows" to "something we use to train the model more efficiently." The paper demonstrates this concretely in Figure 1: a linear probe over the policy's hidden states and entropy achieves *better* value prediction (Pearson $r = 0.870$, MAE $= 0.141$) than a separately trained LLM-scale critic ($r = 0.676$, MAE $= 0.262$). This is not an incremental improvement on existing value estimation — it is evidence that the policy's internal representations form a more compact and informative signal for expected reward than what a separate model can recover from scratch. The prior implicit assumption was that value estimation requires *additional* modeling capacity — either a critic or more samples. POISE shows that the capacity is already there, latent in the policy's forward pass, and only needs to be extracted.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is that it inverts the standard RL framing. In standard actor-critic methods, the critic provides an *external* evaluation of the actor's behavior. In POISE, the "critic" is the actor's own internal state, repurposed through a lightweight readout. This is not just a computational efficiency gain — it suggests a deeper principle: that for large pretrained models, the boundary between "policy" and "value function" is blurred, because the model's representations already encode distributional information about its own expected outcomes.

### Innovation 2: The Cross-Rollout Construction as a Mechanism for Unbiased Trajectory-Conditioned Baselines

Prior work on baseline estimation has enforced a strict separation: either the baseline depends only on the prompt (PPO's critic, GRPO's group mean), or it requires action-dependent baselines that are known to bias the gradient estimator (Tucker et al., 2018). The implicit constraint has been that trajectory-level information — the model's hidden states during reasoning, its entropy patterns, its end-of-trajectory representations — cannot be used in the baseline without violating conditional independence, because those features are functions of the sampled response $y$.

POISE's cross-rollout construction resolves this tension through a simple but non-obvious mechanism: the baseline for rollout $y^{(i)}$ is predicted from the features of a *different, independently sampled* rollout $y^{(j)}$ from the same prompt. Since $y^{(i)}$ and $y^{(j)}$ are independent draws from $\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$, the baseline $b^{(i)}(x) = g_f(\phi^{(j)})$ is conditionally independent of $y^{(i)}$ given $x$ — satisfying the unbiasedness condition from Section 2.2 — despite using trajectory-conditioned features that contain information correlated with outcomes. The probe $g_f$ learns to extract the *prompt-level* value signal from the trajectory features of an independent rollout: it sees features that reflect the model's uncertainty, reasoning quality, and difficulty processing for rollout $j$, and learns to predict the expected reward $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$, which is shared across both rollouts.

This is a conceptual advance over the standard dichotomy. Prior methods treated the choice as binary: either use a prompt-only baseline (PPO critic, which is unbiased but misses trajectory-level difficulty signals) or use a group-relative baseline (GRPO, which uses trajectory outcomes but sacrifices prompt diversity). The cross-rollout construction introduces a third category: trajectory-conditioned baselines that preserve unbiasedness through independence of sampling rather than through independence of features. The mechanism is general — it could be applied to any feature set, not just the specific hidden-state and entropy features used in this paper — and it opens the door to using richer per-trajectory signals (reasoning length, self-consistency measures, intermediate step quality) as baseline inputs without biasing the policy gradient.

### Innovation 3: Prompt Diversity as a First-Class Optimization Variable, Formalized Through Gradient Variance Decomposition

GRPO and its derivatives have treated the group size $G$ as a hyperparameter tuned for baseline accuracy — larger $G$ means a more accurate empirical group mean, which reduces advantage variance. The paper's Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 reframe this tradeoff by showing that $G$ also controls *gradient variance through prompt diversity*. Under a fixed generation budget $B = n \cdot m$, the variance of the batch gradient estimator decomposes into a within-prompt term (scaled by $1/B$) and a between-prompt term (scaled by $m/B$). The between-prompt term grows linearly with $m$ — using more rollouts per prompt concentrates the gradient estimate on fewer distinct prompts, and the correlated errors across rollouts from the same prompt do not average out.

This is not a heuristic observation; it follows from the law of total covariance and shows that $m = 1$ is variance-optimal for any fixed budget. Prior work (Gao et al., 2025) has argued for prompt diversity intuitively, but the paper provides the first formal decomposition that quantifies the tradeoff and proves optimality. The significance is that it elevates prompt diversity from an implementation detail to a *first-class optimization variable* in RLVR. Before this result, the field's default assumption was that GRPO's group sampling was a reasonable way to obtain a baseline, and that the cost in reduced prompt diversity was an acceptable side effect. Proposition 1 shows it is not a side effect — it is a direct, quantifiable increase in gradient variance that can be avoided if a baseline can be obtained with fewer rollouts per prompt.

POISE exploits this result directly: by requiring only $m = 2$ rollouts per prompt (compared to $m = 8$ for GRPO/DAPO), it achieves a 4× reduction in the between-prompt variance term for the same total budget. The lower gradient norms observed in Figure 3 (right) throughout training are an empirical manifestation of this theoretical advantage, and they translate to the faster wall-clock convergence reported in Section 4.2 (18 hours vs. 24 hours on a single B200 GPU for the 1.5B model).

### Innovation 4: The Degenerate-Prompt Problem as an Inherent Flaw of Group-Relative Baselines, Not a Sampling Inefficiency

DAPO introduced dynamic sampling (Yu et al., 2025) to address the problem of degenerate prompt groups — batches where all $G$ rollouts receive the same reward, yielding zero advantage and contributing no learning signal. Efficient implementations (Zheng et al., 2025) reduce the waste by filtering groups early, but the fundamental issue persists: *detecting* that a group is degenerate requires generating the full group first. The compute spent on degenerate groups is pure overhead — it produces no gradient signal and consumes generation budget that could have been used for informative prompts.

The paper's framing of this problem is distinctive: it identifies degeneracy not as a sampling inefficiency to be mitigated (DAPO's approach) but as an *inherent property of group-relative baselines*. When the baseline is the group mean, any prompt where the policy's pass rate is near 0 or near 1 will produce degenerate groups with high probability. For a policy early in training (pass rates near 0), most groups are degenerate; for a well-trained policy on easy prompts (pass rates near 1), most groups are again degenerate. The group-relative baseline is structurally incapable of extracting learning signal from these prompts because the advantage is defined relative to the group, and a homogeneous group provides no relative information.

POISE sidesteps this entirely because its baseline is a learned continuous value prediction, not an empirical group statistic. A prompt that the policy always gets right still receives a baseline $b(x) \approx 1$, and a correct response to it receives a small positive advantage — the signal is weak but non-zero, and the prompt contributes to the batch update. More importantly, no compute is wasted *detecting* that the prompt is degenerate, because the concept of degeneracy doesn't apply — every prompt with two rollouts contributes to both the policy update and the value estimator training. This is not an optimization of dynamic sampling; it is an architectural change that makes the degenerate-prompt problem disappear by construction.

The practical impact is visible in the wall-clock timing (Section 4.2): DAPO's per-step time is inflated by the need to sample additional groups to compensate for filtered degenerate ones (the `default_br_size` of 192 in Table 6 vs. the effective batch size of 128), while POISE's per-step time is predictable and lower because all generated rollouts are used. The paper frames this not as "POISE is faster" but as "group-relative baselines have a structural inefficiency that learned baselines eliminate" — a more fundamental critique that suggests GRPO's sampling overhead is not an implementation detail but a consequence of its baseline design.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All training uses the English-only subset of DAPO-Math-17K (Yu et al., 2025), a mathematical reasoning corpus of 17,000 prompts derived from the MATH benchmark and filtered to remove Chinese-language problems. Evaluation is conducted on a suite of seven olympiad-level mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AMC23 (40 problems), AMC24 (40 problems), AIME24 (30 problems), AIME25 (30 problems), AIME26 (30 problems), HMMT25 (30 problems), and BRUMO25 (30 problems), totaling 230 problems. These benchmarks represent progressively harder competition math problems, with AIME and HMMT being substantially more challenging than AMC.

- **Base model(s).** Two model backbones are used: Qwen3-4B (Yang et al., 2025), a 4-billion-parameter dense Transformer, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (Guo et al., 2025), a 1.5-billion-parameter model distilled from DeepSeek-R1's reasoning traces. Both are chosen to represent the scale at which RLVR for reasoning is commonly applied — large enough to exhibit non-trivial reasoning capability but small enough that training is feasible in an academic compute budget. The Qwen3-4B experiments use a batch size of 1,024 prompts on two B200 GPUs; the 1.5B experiments use 512 prompts on a single B200 GPU.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **Avg@32**: for each problem, 32 independent completions are sampled (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95), and accuracy is reported as the fraction of completions whose final answer matches the ground-truth answer, averaged across all problems in the benchmark. Formally, for $M$ problems, $\text{Avg@}k = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^k \mathbb{1}[\text{is\_correct}(y^{(ij)})]$. This protocol follows common reasoning-model evaluation settings (Guo et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025) and provides a reliable estimate of expected per-sample correctness by averaging over sampling noise. Answers are graded using the same binary verifier function used during training — exact match against the ground-truth solution. Wall-clock training time and gradient norms are also reported as secondary metrics for training efficiency and stability, respectively.

- **Baselines.** Three baselines are compared:
  - **Base model (zero-shot):** The pre-trained model evaluated with greedy decoding (or the same Avg@32 protocol) before any RLVR fine-tuning. This establishes the lower bound on reasoning performance.
  - **DAPO** (Yu et al., 2025): A state-of-the-art GRPO-based RLVR algorithm with four key extensions — Clip-Higher (decoupled upper and lower PPO clipping bounds to combat entropy collapse), Dynamic Sampling (filtering out prompts where all $G = 8$ rollouts receive identical rewards, since they yield zero advantage), Token-Level Policy Gradient Loss (normalizing by total token count rather than per-sequence), and Overlong Reward Shaping (length-aware penalty on truncated generations). The paper adopts the efficient dynamic sampling implementation of Zheng et al. (2025), which reduces the cost of detecting degenerate groups. This is the primary head-to-head comparison, representing the strongest group-relative baseline approach.
  - **Policy-model scale critic:** In value-prediction experiments only (not in full RL training comparisons), a separate sequence-level critic model (SPPO-style; Wang et al., 2026), initialized from the same policy backbone and trained on the same reward-labeled rollouts, serves as a baseline for value estimation accuracy.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The universal unit of generation budget is **number of completions per training step**. POISE generates exactly 2 rollouts per prompt ($m = 2$) for a prompt batch of size $n$, using total budget $B = 2n$ generations (1,024 prompts × 2 = 2,048 generations for Qwen3-4B; 512 × 2 = 1,024 for the 1.5B model). DAPO attempts to fill a batch of $n = 128$ effective prompt groups with $G = 8$ rollouts each, targeting $B = 1,024$ generations, but its dynamic sampling requires generating additional groups (default buffer size of 192 prompts; see Table 6) to compensate for degenerate groups that are filtered out, making the actual generation budget larger than the effective training batch. Wall-clock time is measured as total training duration to reach comparable Avg@32 performance, accounting for both generation cost and the overhead of dynamic sampling vs. probe updates. The value estimator itself incurs no additional generation cost — its features are extracted during the policy's log-probability forward pass, which is already required by the PPO update.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing is reported for the main benchmark results. The Avg@32 evaluation protocol inherently averages over 32 independent samples per problem, reducing evaluation noise, but the paper does not report confidence intervals, standard errors, or test-retest reliability across training seeds. For the value estimation experiments (Figure 1, Tables 2–3), a held-out split of prompts is used — 1,024 held-out prompts for most datasets — to evaluate prediction accuracy against empirical Avg@8 scores from the corresponding model checkpoint. The layer and pooling-window ablations in Appendix E.1 similarly use a held-out validation split within their training data.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Math Reasoning Benchmarks: POISE Matches DAPO While Reducing Training Time

Table 1 reports the central performance comparison across seven olympiad-level math benchmarks. On Qwen3-4B, POISE achieves an average Avg@32 score of 0.500 across all benchmarks, compared to DAPO's 0.508 — a difference of 0.008, or approximately 1.6% relative. On three benchmarks (AMC23: 0.891 vs. 0.876; HMMT25: 0.280 vs. 0.267; BRUMO25: 0.387 vs. 0.384), POISE slightly outperforms DAPO; on the remaining four (AMC24, AIME24, AIME25, AIME26), DAPO holds a small edge (e.g., AIME25: 0.475 vs. 0.437). The performance is close enough across all seven benchmarks that the methods are effectively tied in reasoning quality.

On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, the pattern reverses slightly in POISE's favor: POISE achieves an average Avg@32 of 0.303, edging out DAPO's 0.296 (a 2.4% relative improvement). POISE outperforms DAPO on five of seven benchmarks (AIME24: 0.270 vs. 0.254; AIME25: 0.234 vs. 0.219; AIME26: 0.213 vs. 0.198; HMMT25: 0.066 vs. 0.065; BRUMO25: 0.198 vs. 0.191), ties on AMC24 (0.446 vs. 0.447), and trails only on AMC23 (0.694 vs. 0.697). The gains are most pronounced on the harder benchmarks (AIME series, BRUMO25), where the base model's pass@1 is lowest and the value estimator's ability to distinguish solvable from unsolvable prompts may be most impactful.

Both models dramatically improve over their base (pre-RLVR) performance: Qwen3-4B base achieves only 0.258 average Avg@32, while POISE reaches 0.500 — a 94% relative improvement; the 1.5B base reaches 0.078, while POISE reaches 0.303 — a 288% relative improvement. This confirms that RLVR training is the primary driver of reasoning capability, with POISE and DAPO achieving comparable final performance through different baseline estimation mechanisms.

The headline claim — "POISE matches DAPO while requiring less compute" — is supported by the wall-clock timing data reported in Section 4.2 (Figure 3, left). On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B with a single B200 GPU, POISE reaches the reported performance level in approximately 18 hours, compared to 24 hours for DAPO — a 25% reduction in wall-clock time. On Qwen3-4B with two B200 GPUs, POISE takes approximately 36 hours versus DAPO's 49 hours — a 27% reduction. The paper attributes this difference to DAPO's dynamic sampling overhead: generating additional groups to compensate for filtered degenerate prompts, checking whether each group yields non-zero advantages, and discarding groups that don't. POISE avoids all of this — every generated rollout is used for both policy updates and value estimator training, with no degeneration checks needed.

**Critical nuance:** The wall-clock comparison is based on reaching *comparable* performance, not identical performance. The paper does not report the exact Avg@32 scores at the moment each method "reaches the performance level" — it reports final scores (Table 1) and training curves (Appendix C, Figures 7–8) but not the intermediate checkpoints used for the timing comparison. This makes it difficult to verify whether POISE at 18 hours achieves the same accuracy as DAPO at 24 hours, or whether DAPO at 18 hours is already close to its final performance. The statement "reaching the same performance level takes roughly 24 hours...compared to about 18 hours with POISE" implies a controlled comparison at matched accuracy, but the exact accuracy threshold is not specified.

#### Training Efficiency and Stability: Lower Gradient Norms and Faster Per-Step Time

Figure 3 provides two panels comparing training dynamics. The left panel shows wall-clock time per training step: POISE's per-step time is consistently lower than DAPO's throughout training. This difference is most pronounced early in training, when DAPO's dynamic sampling must filter many degenerate groups (since the base policy has low pass@1, most groups are all-incorrect). As training progresses and the policy's pass rate increases, the gap narrows but POISE maintains a consistent advantage. The paper does not report exact per-step times in seconds — the figure is plotted in relative terms — but the qualitative pattern is clear.

The right panel shows gradient norm $\|\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}\|$ at each training step. POISE's gradient norms are consistently lower than DAPO's throughout training. The paper interprets this as evidence of reduced gradient variance: since the true gradient signal should be comparable at similar training progress (both methods optimize the same objective from the same base model on the same data), the lower norm in POISE reflects less estimation noise. This interpretation rests on the variance decomposition in Proposition 1 — POISE's $m = 2$ produces 4× less between-prompt gradient variance than DAPO's $m = 8$, which manifests as smaller, more stable gradient updates. Lower gradient norms also reduce the risk of destructive large updates that can cause policy collapse, a known failure mode in RLVR.

**Critical nuance:** Gradient norm is an imperfect proxy for gradient variance. Lower norm could also reflect slower learning (smaller updates to the policy), not just lower noise. The paper partially addresses this by noting that final performance is comparable, so POISE is not simply learning less — it reaches the same destination with smaller steps. However, without decomposing the gradient into signal and noise components (which would require estimating the true gradient, e.g., through many independent batch estimates), the claim that lower norms reflect lower variance rather than smaller updates remains an interpretation, not a direct measurement.

#### Value Estimator Training Dynamics: Stable Tracking Throughout Training

Figure 4 reports two metrics tracking the value estimator's behavior during POISE training on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (the corresponding curves for Qwen3-4B appear in Appendix C, Figure 6, with full dynamics in Figures 7–8). The green line shows the **online MAE**: $\text{MAE}_t = \mathbb{E}_{\text{batch}}[|g_f(\phi_t) - \bar{R}_t|]$, where $\bar{R}_t$ is the mean reward of the rollouts at step $t$. This measures how accurately the value estimator predicts the batch-average reward — a proxy for whether it tracks the evolving policy's overall performance level. After an initial drop during the first ~20 steps (as the estimator is trained from scratch on the accumulating buffer), the online MAE stabilizes around 0.05–0.10 and remains flat through the remainder of training, neither diverging nor degrading. This is significant because the value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ is nonstationary — as the policy improves, the expected reward for each prompt shifts upward — and the fact that the estimator's error does not grow indicates the sliding buffer successfully keeps it aligned with the current policy.

The red line shows the **variance reduction ratio**: $\rho_t = \text{Var}(A_t)/\text{Var}(R_t)$, where $A_t = R_t - b_t$ is the advantage and $R_t$ is the raw binary reward. A ratio below 1.0 means the learned baseline reduces advantage variance relative to using the raw reward. After the initial phase, $\rho_t$ stabilizes around 0.7, indicating roughly 30% variance reduction. This is lower than what a perfect value function would achieve (theoretically, subtracting $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ reduces variance to the aleatoric uncertainty in the binary reward, which for a prompt with pass rate $p$ is $p(1-p)$ — at best, a 50–75% reduction for intermediate-difficulty prompts). The relatively modest 30% reduction may reflect: (i) the binary leave-one-out targets are noisy during training, limiting how precisely the probe can estimate $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$; (ii) with only $m = 2$ rollouts per prompt, the cross-rollout baseline cannot exploit within-group reward correlation beyond what is captured by prompt-level value; and (iii) the probe is a linear model on 5,000-dimensional features trained on a few thousand noisy examples, which may underfit the true value function.

**Critical nuance:** The variance reduction ratio is computed *within each batch*, not across the full training distribution. A batch of 512–1,024 prompts may not be representative of the overall variance reduction achieved. Additionally, the ratio does not account for the variance reduction that GRPO achieves through its group-mean baseline — the paper does not report $\text{Var}(A_t)/\text{Var}(R_t)$ for DAPO, making direct comparison impossible. GRPO's group-mean baseline with $G = 8$ theoretically reduces variance by a factor of $(G-1)/G = 0.875$ for independent within-group rewards, but this does not account for the between-prompt variance term that GRPO inflates through reduced prompt diversity (Proposition 1). The net effect on total gradient variance — accounting for both baseline accuracy and prompt diversity — is the quantity that matters for training stability, and the paper only measures one component (baseline accuracy).

#### Online Value Estimator vs. Policy-Scale Critic

Figure 5 (and Appendix D.2, Figures 9–10) compares POISE's internal state estimator against a separately trained policy-scale critic model in the online setting — as the policy evolves during RL training. At every 10 training steps, both estimators are evaluated against empirical Avg@8 scores from the corresponding actor checkpoint, measuring Pearson correlation and MAE. On Qwen3-4B (Figure 9), the critic maintains a slight advantage in both metrics throughout training (e.g., Pearson $r$ around 0.75–0.85 for the critic vs. 0.70–0.80 for the probe at most checkpoints; MAE around 0.05–0.10 for the critic vs. 0.08–0.12 for the probe). On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (Figure 10), the patterns are similar, with the critic holding a small but consistent edge.

The paper characterizes this as "the critic is slightly more accurate, likely due to its larger capacity and continual training on the expanding rollout log." This is an honest assessment — the critic is an LLM-scale model with millions of parameters, trained on all accumulated rollouts (not just a sliding buffer), and updated with gradient descent rather than closed-form ridge regression. It *should* perform better. The significance is that the probe achieves performance remarkably close to the critic despite being a linear model on features extracted from a single forward pass, with negligible training cost. This supports the central argument that internal states are not just a cheap proxy — they are an effective signal that captures much of the value-relevant information without needing a separate model.

#### Generalizability Across Domains and Model Scales

Table 2 (with full results in Table 7, Appendix D.3) extends the value estimation comparison beyond mathematical reasoning to four additional domains: DeepScaleR (competition math, different distribution from DAPO-Math), AceCoder (code generation with test-case verification), ToolDial (multi-turn tool-calling dialogues), and IF-RLVR (instruction following with verifiable constraints). On Qwen3-4B, the internal state estimator consistently and substantially outperforms the separately trained critic across all five domains. The gains are largest on AceCoder (MAE 0.234 vs. 0.499; Pearson $r = 0.612$ vs. $0.056$) and IF-RLVR (MAE 0.195 vs. 0.350; $r = 0.642$ vs. $0.150$), where the critic essentially fails to learn any value signal (Pearson $r \approx 0$), while the probe extracts a meaningful correlation. On ToolDial, the probe achieves $r = 0.840$ vs. the critic's $0.440$, and on DeepScaleR, $r = 0.609$ vs. $0.384$.

On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, the pattern is more mixed: the probe outperforms the critic on DAPO-Math (MAE 0.123 vs. 0.127; $r = 0.834$ vs. $0.723$) and DeepScaleR (MAE 0.151 vs. 0.251; $r = 0.829$ vs. $0.586$), but the critic wins on AceCoder (MAE 0.135 vs. 0.196; $r = 0.706$ vs. $0.580$) and IF-RLVR (MAE 0.101 vs. 0.171; $r = 0.545$ vs. $0.557$). On the largest tested backbone, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (evaluated only on value prediction, not full RL training), the probe outperforms the critic on three of five domains (DAPO-Math, DeepScaleR, ToolDial) and trails on AceCoder and IF-RLVR.

These results are important for the paper's generalizability claim: the internal state estimator is not merely exploiting dataset-specific artifacts from DAPO-Math. The value-relevant signal in hidden states appears to be a general property of how the policy represents task difficulty and solution quality, transferable across domains with fundamentally different verifier structures (math answer checking, unit test execution, tool-use correctness, constraint satisfaction). The weaker results on the 1.5B model for coding and instruction-following suggest that smaller models may encode less value-relevant information in their hidden states for certain task types, or that the optimal extraction layer differs across domains.

**Critical caveat:** These are value *prediction* experiments, not RL *training* experiments. The paper demonstrates that the probe can predict expected reward across domains, but it does not run POISE training on AceCoder, ToolDial, or IF-RLVR. Whether the probe's value estimates would serve as effective baselines for policy optimization in these domains — maintaining stability, tracking the evolving policy, and producing gradients that improve downstream task performance — is an open question that the paper flags as future work (Section 8). The generalizability claim is about the *estimator*, not the full POISE algorithm.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Input feature ablation (Table 3):** Training the value estimator with only one feature type at a time reveals that trajectory-level features dominate value prediction accuracy. Reasoning hidden states alone achieve MAE 0.132 and Pearson $r = 0.821$ — nearly matching the full estimator's performance ($r = 0.838$). Token-level mean entropy alone achieves MAE 0.152 and $r = 0.780$, also surprisingly competitive given that it is a single scalar statistic (well, three scalars) rather than a 2,560-dimensional hidden state. Prompt hidden states alone are substantially weaker (MAE 0.234, $r = 0.569$), suggesting that the model's pre-generation representation of prompt difficulty is less informative than the signals that emerge during reasoning about whether the trajectory is likely to succeed. Response length alone performs worst (MAE 0.251, $r = 0.494$), indicating that while longer responses correlate with difficulty (a known phenomenon in reasoning models; Devic et al., 2025), length alone is a noisy value signal. The full estimator improves over the best single feature by 0.006 in MAE and 0.017 in Pearson $r$ — a modest but consistent gain, confirming that the three feature types carry complementary information.

**Layer index ablation (Tables 8–9, Appendix E.1):** On Qwen3-4B, value-relevant information is broadly distributed across layers, with the best performance at layers 17–19 (Pearson $r = 0.831–0.834$) and a secondary peak at layer 33 ($r = 0.831$, MAE 0.120). Performance is already strong at layer 1 ($r = 0.807$), indicating that even early representations encode substantial difficulty signal. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, layer 1 performs best ($r = 0.425$), with performance decaying slightly in deeper layers. The differences across layers are small in absolute terms (MAE ranges from 0.120–0.132 for Qwen3-4B, 0.134–0.141 for the 1.5B model), suggesting the estimator is not highly sensitive to layer choice. The paper selects layer 19 for both backbones as a pragmatic default that is near-optimal for Qwen3-4B and acceptable for the 1.5B model, avoiding model-specific layer tuning.

**Pooling window ablation (Tables 10–11, Appendix E.1):** Using the last 5, 10, or 15 tokens for mean pooling yields near-identical performance on both backbones. For Qwen3-4B at layer 19, MAE is 0.127 (last-5), 0.124 (last-10), and 0.124 (last-15); Pearson $r$ is 0.822, 0.834, and 0.833 respectively. For the 1.5B model at layer 1, MAE is 0.1342, 0.1339, and 0.1342. The last-10 configuration is chosen as default because it achieves the highest correlation on Qwen3-4B while using a modest extraction window — large enough to smooth token-level noise, small enough to avoid including early reasoning tokens that may not encode outcome-relevant information.

**Probe architecture ablation (Table 12, Appendix E.2):** Comparing the linear ridge regressor against MLPs with varying depth (1, 3, 5, 7, 9 layers) and width (512, 1024 hidden units) reveals that additional capacity can slightly improve MAE (best MLP: 3×1024 achieves MAE 0.117 vs. linear ridge's 0.124) but consistently *reduces* Pearson correlation (best MLP: $r = 0.801$ vs. linear ridge's $r = 0.834$). This is a non-obvious result: deeper probes achieve better absolute calibration (lower MAE) but worse rank alignment with the true values (lower correlation). The paper attributes this to linear encoding of semantic features in Transformer representations (Park et al., 2024; Elhage et al., 2021) — the value signal may be linearly accessible in the hidden states, and adding nonlinearity introduces overfitting without extracting additional signal. The practical implication is that the simpler probe is not just computationally cheaper but actually *better* for the purpose of forming advantages, where rank alignment matters more than absolute calibration.

**Comparing value estimators offline (Figure 1, Section 3.1):** On a fixed, held-out dataset of reward-labeled rollouts from DAPO-Math, the internal state probe achieves substantially better value prediction than a separately trained policy-scale critic: Pearson $r = 0.870$ vs. $0.676$, MAE $= 0.141$ vs. $0.262$. This is the "smoking gun" evidence that the policy's internal representations contain a compact, highly informative value signal — the probe sees only the policy's hidden states and entropy, yet outperforms an LLM-scale model trained on the same reward labels. The paper interprets this as evidence that the policy's representations already encode distributional information about expected outcomes, and that recovering this signal from scratch (as the critic must do) is harder than reading it directly.

**PRM vs. ORM is not ablated because POISE does not use a verifier model.** This is worth noting explicitly: unlike the reference paper on compute-optimal test-time scaling, POISE does not involve a process reward model, outcome reward model, or any learned verifier. The reward signal is purely the ground-truth verifier $R(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$. All ablation studies therefore focus on the value estimator's architecture and features, not on verifier quality or search algorithm choice.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: POISE matches DAPO's performance with less compute.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 1 shows that on two model scales (1.5B and 4B) across seven math benchmarks, POISE achieves final Avg@32 scores within 0.008 (Qwen3-4B) and actually 0.007 better (1.5B) than DAPO. The wall-clock comparison (Section 4.2) reports 25–27% faster training to reach comparable performance. These are the central empirical results, and they support the claim that POISE is competitive with the state-of-the-art group-relative method while being more compute-efficient.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate, or demonstrate only weakly:**

- **Single training run per configuration.** The paper does not report results across multiple random seeds, training data shuffles, or hardware configurations. With only one training run per model per method, it is impossible to assess whether the 0.008 gap between POISE (0.500) and DAPO (0.508) on Qwen3-4B is statistically significant or within run-to-run variation. RL training is notoriously sensitive to random seed (prompt ordering, sampling noise, initialization of the value estimator buffer), and a single run provides no estimate of variance. A difference of 0.008 in Avg@32 across 230 test problems could easily be noise — with 32 samples per problem, the standard error of the mean Avg@32 for a 230-problem test set is approximately $\sqrt{p(1-p)/(230 \times 32)} \approx 0.008$ for $p \approx 0.5$, meaning the observed gap is on the order of one standard error. The paper would be strengthened by reporting confidence intervals or running multiple seeds.

- **Wall-clock comparison lacks precise accuracy matching.** The timing comparison states that POISE reaches "the same performance level" faster, but does not specify what performance level this is. If POISE at 18 hours achieves, say, 0.290 Avg@32 on the 1.5B model while DAPO at 24 hours achieves 0.296, then POISE is slightly worse but faster — a favorable tradeoff, but not "matching" performance. If DAPO at 18 hours already reaches 0.290, then POISE is not faster at matched accuracy. The training curves in Appendix C (Figures 7–8) show batch reward and estimated value over steps, but not Avg@32 evaluation scores at intermediate checkpoints, making independent verification of the timing claim impossible from the paper alone.

- **DAPO hyperparameters may not be optimally tuned for the comparison.** The paper uses DAPO hyperparameters from Zheng et al. (2025) (Table 6), which were tuned for efficiency rather than maximum final performance. It is possible that DAPO with larger group size, different clipping bounds, or a different dynamic sampling threshold would achieve higher final accuracy, changing the comparison. The paper does not perform a hyperparameter sweep for DAPO to ensure it is at its best — it takes an off-the-shelf configuration.

- **The compute savings are partly due to engineering factors, not algorithmic ones.** DAPO's dynamic sampling overhead is an implementation detail — the need to generate extra groups, check for degeneracy, and discard them. An alternative implementation that amortizes degeneracy checking differently, or a system that reuses discarded rollouts for other purposes (e.g., value estimation), could narrow the gap. POISE's advantage is partly that it avoids this specific engineering problem, which is a valid practical concern but not a fundamental algorithmic superiority — if dynamic sampling were made more efficient (e.g., through online degeneracy prediction without full generation), the wall-clock gap might shrink.

#### Claim 2: POISE achieves more stable training (lower gradient norms) due to increased prompt diversity.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 3 (right) shows that POISE's gradient norms are consistently lower than DAPO's. Proposition 1 provides a theoretical argument that $m = 2$ yields lower gradient variance than $m = 8$ for the same total budget. The two are consistent — lower variance would manifest as lower gradient norms, all else equal.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate:**

- **Gradient norm is not gradient variance.** As noted earlier, lower gradient norms could reflect smaller true gradient magnitudes (slower learning) rather than lower noise around a comparable signal. The paper argues that final performance is similar, so learning is not slower, but this is an indirect argument. A direct variance measurement — e.g., computing the empirical variance of gradient estimates across multiple independent batches at the same training step — is not reported.

- **The variance decomposition assumes $\Sigma_w$ and $\Sigma_b$ are comparable between POISE and DAPO.** This assumption may not hold: POISE and DAPO use different baseline functions ($g_f(\phi)$ vs. group mean), which changes the per-sample gradient $Z(x, y)$ and thus the covariance matrices. The decomposition in Proposition 1 is for a *fixed* $Z$; if POISE's baseline produces a different $Z$ with inherently lower variance (e.g., because the continuous value prediction is a better baseline than the discrete group mean), then the variance reduction comes from both the baseline quality and the prompt diversity, and the decomposition does not separately identify these effects. The paper does not isolate the contribution of prompt diversity alone (e.g., by comparing POISE with $m = 2$ to a hypothetical POISE with $m = 1$ and a different baseline mechanism).

- **No ablation on group size.** The paper does not run a controlled experiment comparing POISE ($m = 2$) against GRPO/DAPO with $m = 2$ (which would produce a very noisy group-mean baseline) or with $m = 4, 8, 16$ at the same total budget (varying prompt diversity and baseline accuracy orthogonally). Such an experiment would directly test Proposition 1's prediction that $m = 1$ (or the smallest feasible $m$) is optimal, and would reveal whether the benefit of POISE comes primarily from prompt diversity or from the learned baseline's predictive accuracy.

#### Claim 3: The internal state value estimator performs comparably to a separate policy-scale critic.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Figure 5 (online) and Table 7 (offline, multi-domain) provide consistent evidence that the lightweight probe achieves value prediction accuracy close to — and in many settings better than — a full LLM-scale critic. This is a genuinely strong result: a linear model on pre-existing features matches or beats a model with millions of parameters trained on the same data. The multi-domain results in Table 7 are particularly compelling because they show the effect persists across coding, tool use, and instruction following, not just math.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate, or demonstrate with caveats:**

- **The critic may be undertrained or suboptimally configured.** The critic is trained with a Bernoulli BCE loss on binary reward labels (Appendix D.1). The paper does not perform a hyperparameter sweep for the critic, and it is possible that a different architecture (e.g., token-level critic with GAE rather than sequence-level critic), different optimizer settings, or longer training would close the gap. The critic's poor performance on AceCoder ($r = 0.056$) and IF-RLVR ($r = 0.150$) on Qwen3-4B suggests something is fundamentally failing — a correlation near zero means the critic is essentially predicting the mean reward regardless of the prompt, which is a training failure rather than an inherent limitation of critic-based value estimation. The probe's strong performance on these same domains ($r = 0.612$ and $0.642$) may partly reflect that the probe is easier to train (linear regression has no optimization pathologies) rather than that it accesses a fundamentally better signal.

- **The critic and probe are evaluated at different points in their training.** The probe is trained online with a sliding buffer of recent rollouts, keeping it aligned with the current policy. The critic in Figure 5 is "continually trained on the expanding rollout log" — it sees all historical data, not just recent data. This is a difference in training regime, not just model architecture. A critic trained with the same sliding-buffer approach might perform differently.

- **The multi-domain results (Table 7) are purely offline value prediction, not RL training.** The probe clearly generalizes as a value prediction model, but whether it would serve as an effective baseline for RL training in coding or tool-use domains is untested. The paper's Section 8 acknowledges this as future work.

#### Claim 4: The internal state estimator generalizes across domains.

**What the experiments demonstrate:** Table 7 shows that the probe achieves strong value prediction (MAE 0.12–0.23, Pearson $r$ 0.56–0.84) on all five tested domains and three model scales (with the exception of the 1.5B model on AceCoder, where $r = 0.580$). This is genuinely broad coverage — math, coding, tool calling, and instruction following are substantially different tasks with different verifier structures and reward distributions.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate:**

- **Generalization is only tested for value prediction, not for POISE training.** As with Claim 3, the leap from "the probe predicts values well" to "POISE trains effectively" is not tested outside math. The paper's main RL results are exclusively on DAPO-Math.

- **The probe was trained and evaluated on the same domain in each case.** This is testing *within-domain* prediction accuracy, not cross-domain generalization (training the probe on math and evaluating it on coding). Cross-domain generalization would be a stronger test of whether the probe captures a universal "task difficulty" signal rather than domain-specific patterns.

- **The number of training examples for the probe is small (4,096 per domain).** While this demonstrates sample efficiency, it also means the probe may overfit to the specific prompts in the training set. The paper uses a held-out evaluation set (1,024–13,492 prompts depending on the domain), but the training set is still modest, and performance on genuinely out-of-distribution prompts (e.g., from a different coding benchmark) is not tested.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

- **Multiple training seeds with confidence intervals.** This is the single most important missing element. RL training is stochastic; a single run per configuration tells us what happened once, not what would typically happen. Reporting mean and standard deviation across 3–5 seeds would transform the results from suggestive to conclusive.

- **Head-to-head comparison at matched prompt diversity.** A controlled experiment where DAPO is run with $m = 2$ and a GRPO-style group-mean baseline (even though this baseline would be noisy) versus POISE with $m = 2$ and the learned baseline, both at the same total budget and prompt batch size, would isolate the effect of the learned baseline from the effect of prompt diversity. Similarly, DAPO with $m = 8$ versus POISE with $m = 8$ and a modified cross-rollout construction (using the other 7 rollouts to form the baseline) would isolate the effect of prompt diversity from the effect of the baseline mechanism.

- **Ablation on the number of rollouts per prompt in POISE.** The paper argues $m = 2$ is optimal based on Proposition 1, but does not test $m = 3, 4, 8$ with the cross-rollout construction (using leave-one-out targets across a larger group). Larger $m$ would improve the value estimator's training signal (more examples per prompt, less noisy leave-one-out targets) at the cost of reduced prompt diversity. The optimal tradeoff might be domain-dependent, and characterizing it empirically would validate or refine the theoretical prediction.

- **Scaling to larger models.** The largest model tested is Qwen3-4B (and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B for value prediction only). Whether POISE scales to the 32B, 70B, or larger models where RLVR is most impactful — and where the cost of DAPO's group sampling is most burdensome — is unknown. At larger scales, hidden states may encode richer value signals (strengthening POISE) or the linear probe may underfit (weakening it).

- **Training on coding or instruction-following tasks with POISE.** The paper demonstrates that the value estimator predicts rewards well in these domains but does not close the loop by running full POISE training. This is acknowledged as future work in Section 8, but it means the central claim — "POISE is a general RLVR method" — is supported only for math reasoning.

- **Comparison to PPO with a critic at matched compute.** The paper compares POISE to GRPO (via DAPO) extensively, and to a critic in value prediction only, but never runs full PPO training with an LLM-scale critic (the original baseline estimation approach that POISE aims to replace). A three-way comparison — PPO (critic), DAPO (group-relative), POISE (internal state) — at matched total FLOPs or wall-clock time would complete the picture. PPO's critic costs memory and parameters, which may force a smaller batch size or model scale, but it also provides a learned baseline that (unlike GRPO) does not reduce prompt diversity. Where POISE sits in this three-way tradeoff space is not empirically characterized.

#### Summary Assessment

The experimental section demonstrates that POISE is a viable alternative to GRPO-based RLVR for mathematical reasoning — it achieves comparable final accuracy with lower wall-clock time and more stable training dynamics, and its value estimator generalizes across domains as a prediction model. The results are internally consistent and the ablation studies are thorough within the scope of the probe architecture. However, the evidence is from single training runs on two model scales and one domain (math reasoning), leaving open questions about statistical reliability, scaling behavior, and generality to full RL training on non-math tasks. The strongest results are the value prediction comparisons (Figures 1, 5; Tables 2, 7), which convincingly show that internal states encode value-relevant information. The full RL training results (Table 1, Figure 3) are suggestive but would benefit substantially from multi-seed replication and the controlled ablation experiments described above.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Single Training Run Per Configuration Precludes Statistical Reliability Assessment

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports results from exactly one training run per model backbone per method: one POISE run and one DAPO run on Qwen3-4B, and one of each on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B. No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or error bars are reported for the main benchmark results in Table 1. The paper does not explicitly acknowledge this as a limitation — the single-run nature is simply the experimental design. For the value estimation experiments, held-out validation splits are used, but even there no variance across training seeds is reported.

**The consequence.** RL training is notoriously sensitive to random seed: prompt ordering, sampling noise during rollout generation, initialization of the value estimator buffer, and stochastic gradient noise all produce run-to-run variation that can be substantial for models at this scale. A difference of 0.008 in average Avg@32 between POISE (0.500) and DAPO (0.508) on Qwen3-4B — the headline result that "POISE matches DAPO" — could easily be within one standard deviation of the run-to-run distribution. With 32 samples per problem across 230 test problems, the standard error of the mean Avg@32 is approximately:

$$\text{SE} \approx \sqrt{\frac{\bar{p}(1 - \bar{p})}{230 \times 32}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{0.5 \times 0.5}{7360}} \approx 0.006$$

for $\bar{p} \approx 0.5$, meaning a 95% confidence interval spans roughly ±0.012. The observed gap of 0.008 is smaller than this, so the two methods are statistically indistinguishable given the evaluation noise *alone*, without accounting for training-run variance. A practitioner cannot determine from these results whether POISE is genuinely equivalent to DAPO, slightly better, or slightly worse — only that it is in the same performance regime *on this particular run*.

The wall-clock timing comparison in Section 4.2 inherits the same limitation: "reaching the same performance level takes roughly 24 hours... with DAPO... compared to about 18 hours with POISE" is based on single-run trajectories. If DAPO's single run happened to be a "slow" run (unlucky prompt ordering, more degenerate groups to filter) while POISE's run was a "fast" one, the 25% wall-clock advantage could be partially or entirely an artifact of run-to-run noise rather than a systematic algorithmic benefit.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no evidence regarding run-to-run variance. No mention is made of multiple seeds, standard deviations, or confidence intervals anywhere in the experimental sections. The training dynamics plots (Figures 3, 4, 7, 8) show per-step metrics from the single training runs — the curves are smooth, but smoothness in a single trajectory does not indicate reproducibility across seeds. The multi-domain value estimation results (Table 7) are from single probe and critic training runs per domain-model pair.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the single-run limitation, does not attempt to quantify variance through bootstrap resampling of the test set or through repeated training runs, and does not suggest multi-seed replication as future work. This is the most consequential methodological weakness, because it means the central empirical claims — performance parity with DAPO and faster wall-clock convergence — cannot be assessed for statistical reliability.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Excluded from the Headline Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** POISE's value estimator requires features extracted from the policy model's hidden states and token-level entropy during the forward pass. The paper states that these features are collected "during the teacher-forced log probability forward pass, which is already required by our policy optimization algorithm and therefore does not need additional computation" (Appendix B.3). This is accurate for the *forward-pass compute* — the hidden states and log-probabilities are computed regardless. However, the probe itself must be trained and updated: at each step, the ridge regression model is fit (or incrementally updated) on the sliding buffer of recent examples, and the `StandardScaler` must be fit or updated to normalize features. The wall-clock comparison in Section 4.2 attributes POISE's speed advantage to avoiding DAPO's dynamic sampling overhead, but it does not report or account for the probe training time.

**The consequence.** The probe update cost is likely small relative to the policy forward and backward passes — fitting a ridge regression on a few thousand examples with ~5,000 features takes milliseconds on GPU, versus seconds to minutes for autoregressive generation and gradient computation. However, the *first* training step requires fitting the `StandardScaler` from scratch and training the ridge model on the initial buffer, which may be non-trivial. More importantly, the probe architecture choice (linear regression) was motivated in part by computational efficiency (§ 3.4), but the actual cost is never measured or compared to the savings from avoiding dynamic sampling. If the probe update adds, say, 2% to the per-step time while the degenerate-group filtering saves 20%, the net advantage is clear. But if the probe update adds 15% (plausible if the buffer is large and the ridge refit is done in double precision on CPU), the net advantage shrinks. A practitioner evaluating whether to adopt POISE needs to know the *net* wall-clock savings, not just the savings from one component.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not profile the probe training time, does not report the buffer size $n$ (the number of recent steps retained), does not specify whether the ridge regression is refit from scratch or updated incrementally, and does not include probe update time in the per-step wall-clock measurements in Figure 3 (left). The claim that the probe update is "negligible in cost" (Section 3.2) is an assertion, not a measurement.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as an unmeasured cost, does not provide a profiling breakdown of per-step time (policy forward pass, policy backward pass, probe training, buffer management), and does not discuss the scaling of probe training time with buffer size or feature dimension. This is a practical concern, not a theoretical one, but it directly affects the paper's primary practical claim (faster training).

### Results Are Confined to Mathematical Reasoning; POISE Training on Other Domains Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** All RL training experiments are conducted on a single dataset: the English subset of DAPO-Math-17K, consisting of competition math problems with binary correctness rewards. The paper explicitly acknowledges this scope limitation: "we focused on mathematical reasoning as a controlled testbed" (Section 8). The value estimation generalization experiments (Tables 2 and 7) demonstrate that the probe *predicts* expected reward well on coding, tool-use, and instruction-following domains, but these are purely offline analyses — the paper does not run a single POISE training loop on any non-math domain.

**The consequence.** The claim that POISE is a general RLVR method — applicable "whenever verifiable feedback is available" (Section 5) — is empirically supported only for value prediction, not for full policy optimization. Several aspects of POISE's training dynamics may not transfer to other domains:

- **Reward sparsity and structure.** Math problems have a clean binary reward (answer matches or doesn't). Coding tasks have a similar structure (unit tests pass or fail), but tool-calling and instruction-following may have graded or multi-dimensional rewards (e.g., partial credit for partially correct tool sequences, multiple constraint satisfaction checks). The probe is trained on binary targets; multi-dimensional or continuous rewards would require a different loss function or multiple probe heads. How the probe's prediction quality degrades with noisier or more complex reward structures is unknown.

- **Trajectory length and feature quality.** Math reasoning trajectories in DAPO-Math tend to be long (often thousands of tokens of chain-of-thought), which means the reasoning hidden state features average over many tokens and may encode rich outcome-relevant signals. In domains with shorter responses (e.g., instruction-following with brief answers, tool-calling with short API invocations), the trajectory-level hidden state features may be less informative. The paper does not ablate trajectory length as a factor in probe accuracy — Table 3 shows that response length alone is a weak feature ($r = 0.494$), but this does not reveal whether the hidden-state features *depend* on having long trajectories to be informative.

- **Policy drift dynamics.** The sliding buffer updates the probe on examples from recent training steps. If the policy's capabilities improve at different rates across domains — slow improvement on hard math, rapid saturation on easier tool-calling — the optimal buffer size and update frequency may differ. The paper uses a single buffer configuration (size unspecified) and does not explore sensitivity to this hyperparameter.

- **Degenerate-group analog.** The paper argues that POISE eliminates the degenerate-group problem because the learned baseline provides a non-zero advantage for all prompts. This argument depends on the probe producing values strictly between 0 and 1, which it does by clipping. But if a domain has prompts that the base model never solves (pass@1 ≈ 0) and the probe correctly predicts near-zero values, correct responses to those prompts will receive advantages near +1 — a strong signal. Conversely, if a domain has prompts the model always solves (pass@1 ≈ 1), correct responses receive advantages near 0 — a weak signal but still non-zero. Whether these dynamics produce stable learning outside math is unverified.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The value prediction experiments (Tables 2, 7) provide evidence that the probe learns a meaningful value signal across domains. On Qwen3-4B, the probe achieves Pearson $r = 0.612$ on AceCoder (coding), $r = 0.840$ on ToolDial (tool calling), and $r = 0.642$ on IF-RLVR (instruction following). These are strong correlations, suggesting that value-relevant information exists in the hidden states for these domains. However, correlation with held-out value targets is not the same as producing baselines that yield effective policy gradients. A probe that is well-calibrated on average but systematically overestimates values for a particular subset of prompts could bias the policy away from those prompts, a failure mode that would be invisible in offline value prediction metrics.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this as future work in Section 8: "applying it to RL training for agentic reasoning and instruction-following tasks is a promising next step toward broader RLVR deployment." This is an appropriate and honest scope statement. However, until such experiments are conducted, POISE is demonstrated only as a math reasoning RL algorithm, and the paper's framing as a general replacement for GRPO/PPO in RLVR should be understood as aspirational rather than empirically supported.

### The Value Estimator Provides Only Modest Variance Reduction (~30%), and This Is Not Benchmarked Against GRPO's Baseline

**The assumption or constraint.** POISE's learned baseline achieves an advantage variance reduction ratio $\rho_t = \text{Var}(A_t) / \text{Var}(R_t) \approx 0.7$ after the initial training phase (Figure 4; Appendix C, Figures 6–8). This means the advantage retains about 70% of the raw reward variance — a 30% reduction. The paper frames this as evidence that the estimator "provides a stable baseline throughout training" (Section 4.2). However, the paper never reports the corresponding variance reduction ratio for DAPO's group-mean baseline, making it impossible to assess whether POISE's baseline is better, worse, or comparable in variance reduction.

**The consequence.** The variance reduction ratio is a direct measure of baseline quality: a perfect value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ reduces variance to the irreducible aleatoric noise, which for a binary reward with prompt pass rate $p$ is $p(1-p)$. For a prompt with $p = 0.5$, this is 0.25 (a 75% reduction from the raw reward variance of 0.5 for $p = 0.5$). POISE's 30% reduction falls well short of this theoretical optimum. GRPO's group-mean baseline with $G = 8$ independent rollouts theoretically reduces within-group variance by a factor of $(G-1)/G = 0.875$ — but this applies only to the within-prompt component of variance, not the total gradient variance, which also includes the between-prompt term that POISE reduces through better prompt diversity (Proposition 1). The *net* effect on gradient variance — combining baseline quality and prompt diversity — is what matters for training stability, and the paper provides only one side of this comparison.

A practitioner choosing between POISE and DAPO needs to understand the tradeoff: POISE offers better prompt diversity (4× reduction in between-prompt gradient variance) but potentially worse baseline accuracy (30% reduction in within-batch advantage variance vs. DAPO's potentially larger reduction from the group mean). Whether the net gradient variance is lower in POISE — as suggested by the lower gradient norms in Figure 3 (right) — is an empirical finding, but the paper does not decompose this into the contributions from baseline quality and prompt diversity. If the prompt diversity benefit dominates, POISE is clearly preferable; if the baseline accuracy penalty cancels most of the prompt diversity benefit, the case is weaker and may depend on domain-specific properties (e.g., how much between-prompt variance $\Sigma_b$ there is in the prompt distribution).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 and the training dynamics in Appendix C report $\rho_t$ for POISE only. DAPO's variance reduction ratio is never reported. The gradient norm comparison (Figure 3, right) is the only proxy for net gradient variance, and it shows POISE with consistently lower norms — but as discussed in Section 5's critical assessment, lower gradient norms could reflect smaller true gradient magnitudes (slower per-step learning) rather than lower noise around a comparable signal. The paper does not perform the controlled experiment that would isolate these effects: e.g., comparing gradient variance estimates computed from multiple independent batch samples at the same training step.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the modest absolute magnitude of the variance reduction (30%), does not compare it to the theoretical optimum or to GRPO's reduction, and does not discuss the implications of this modest reduction for the quality of the baseline. The framing is entirely positive — "the variance reduction ratio remains around 30% after the initial phase, showing that the learned baseline reduces the reward variance" — without noting that more than two-thirds of the raw reward variance remains in the advantage.

### All Training and Most Evaluation Use Qwen and DeepSeek Model Families; Generality Across Architectures Is Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's RL training experiments use exactly two model backbones: Qwen3-4B (a dense 4B-parameter Transformer) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (a 1.5B-parameter model distilled from DeepSeek-R1). Both belong to the Qwen architecture family. The value estimation generalization experiments (Table 7) additionally include DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, but this is still within the Qwen lineage. The paper states in Section 4: "We believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but provides no evidence that the internal state value signal persists across model families with different architectures, training procedures, or tokenizers.

**The consequence.** Several aspects of POISE could be architecture-dependent:

- **Hidden state information content.** Qwen models are trained with a specific recipe (pre-training data mixture, fine-tuning stages, RLHF or DPO alignment). The finding that hidden states encode prompt difficulty and trajectory quality may depend on this training recipe — models trained with different objectives (e.g., next-token prediction only, without instruction tuning) may not develop the same richness of value-relevant internal representations. The linear representation hypothesis (Park et al., 2024) predicts some degree of universality, but the strength and layer localization of value-relevant features could vary substantially.

- **Layer choice.** The paper selects layer 19 for both Qwen backbones based on an ablation showing mid-to-late layers are optimal for Qwen3-4B (Table 8), and that layer 19 is acceptable for the 1.5B model despite layer 1 being slightly better (Table 9). For a model with a different depth (e.g., LLaMA-3.1-8B with 32 layers, or a 70B model with 80 layers), the optimal extraction layer may differ. The paper's approach of fixing layer 19 as a "pragmatic default" may not transfer — a practitioner adopting POISE for a new model family would need to perform a layer sweep, which requires held-out reward-labeled rollouts from that model, adding to the deployment cost.

- **Feature dimension scaling.** The probe input dimension is $2d_{\text{model}} + 3$, which is 5,123 for Qwen3-4B ($d_{\text{model}} = 2,560$) and 3,075 for the 1.5B model ($d_{\text{model}} = 1,536$). For larger models, the feature dimension grows proportionally: a 70B model with $d_{\text{model}} = 8,192$ would produce 16,387-dimensional features. The ridge regression probe on 16K-dimensional features would require a larger buffer (more examples than features to avoid underdetermination) and more computation to fit. Whether the linear probe remains effective at this scale, or whether dimensionality reduction (e.g., PCA projection as mentioned in Appendix E.1's ablation setup) becomes necessary, is untested.

- **Entropy statistics computation.** The entropy features depend on the model's output vocabulary distribution, which is tokenizer-dependent. Models with different tokenizers (e.g., BPE vs. SentencePiece, different vocabulary sizes) may produce entropy distributions with different scales and dynamics, potentially requiring re-tuning of the entropy feature normalization in the `StandardScaler`.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None across model families. The paper tests models within a single architectural lineage. The strong cross-domain results within Qwen (Table 7) suggest the value signal is not an artifact of a specific dataset, but they do not address whether the signal is an artifact of the Qwen architecture or training procedure. The paper does not train or evaluate on, for example, LLaMA, Gemma, Mistral, or Phi models.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge architecture dependence as a limitation and does not discuss the transferability of hyperparameters (layer index, pooling window, ridge penalty) across model families. Section 8 lists future work on "agentic reasoning and instruction-following tasks" but does not mention architectural generalization.

### The Cross-Rollout Construction Requires Exactly Two Rollouts per Prompt, Constraining Batch Composition

**The assumption or constraint.** POISE's cross-rollout baseline mechanism requires exactly two independent rollouts per prompt to pair features and rewards. This is not a hyperparameter to be tuned — it is baked into the algorithm design. The paper argues, via Proposition 1 and Corollary 1, that $m = 2$ is near-optimal for gradient variance minimization (the true optimum being $m = 1$, which is infeasible because it provides no second rollout for the cross-rollout baseline). However, this constraint has practical implications for batch composition and training throughput that the paper does not explore.

**The consequence.** The fixed $m = 2$ design means:

- **Batch size granularity.** The total generation budget per step must be $B = 2n$ for some integer prompt batch size $n$. While this is not restrictive (any even budget works), it means POISE cannot smoothly trade off prompt batch size against rollout count the way GRPO can — GRPO can use $m = 4, 8, 16$ at correspondingly smaller prompt counts, allowing flexible adjustment to fit GPU memory constraints. A practitioner whose optimal prompt batch size for memory reasons is, say, 300 prompts would need to generate 600 rollouts with POISE, potentially underutilizing memory (if 400 prompts × 2 rollouts would also fit) or overutilizing it (if 300 × 2 exceeds the budget).

- **No benefit from additional rollouts per prompt.** If a particular prompt is especially informative (e.g., the model's pass rate hovers near 0.5, producing high-variance advantages), GRPO can allocate more rollouts to that prompt to get a better baseline estimate. POISE is locked at two rollouts per prompt regardless of the prompt's informativeness. The paper's variance decomposition (Proposition 1) assumes a uniform $m$ across all prompts and does not consider adaptive allocation.

- **Leave-one-out target noise.** With only two rollouts, the leave-one-out Monte Carlo target for the probe is $\hat{V}_{-i}(x) = r^{(j)}$, a binary 0 or 1. This is an unbiased but extremely noisy estimate of $V^{\pi_\theta}(x)$ — the variance of a single binary sample is $p(1-p)$, which is at most 0.25. The probe relies on the sliding buffer to aggregate across many prompts and steps, but the per-step training signal is very weak. A GRPO-style design with $m = 8$ per prompt would give the probe (if it were used as a critic) targets of $\text{Avg@7} \in \{0, 1/7, \ldots, 1\}$, which are substantially lower-variance. POISE trades off per-prompt target quality (worse) for prompt diversity (better), but the net effect on probe convergence speed is not isolated or measured.

- **Memory overhead of paired rollouts.** During the PPO update, POISE must maintain both rollouts for each prompt in memory to compute the cross-rollout advantages (since $b^{(1)}$ depends on $\phi^{(2)}$ and vice versa). The policy forward pass already stores log-probabilities for both rollouts; the additional cost is the feature vectors $\phi^{(1)}, \phi^{(2)}$ and the baseline predictions, which are small (a few thousand floats per prompt). This is unlikely to be a bottleneck, but it is an additional memory cost that GRPO (which computes the group mean without pairwise feature exchange) does not incur.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly. The paper does not experiment with $m > 2$ in POISE (e.g., using a leave-one-out target across a larger group while maintaining the cross-rollout advantage construction), does not compare probe convergence speed with binary vs. multi-sample targets, and does not discuss the constraints $m = 2$ imposes on batch composition. The argument that $m = 2$ is optimal rests entirely on Proposition 1, which assumes the baseline is fixed. In practice, the baseline *quality* improves with larger $m$ (more accurate Monte Carlo targets for probe training), creating a tradeoff between baseline accuracy and prompt diversity that the theoretical analysis does not capture.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats $m = 2$ as the natural and optimal choice, supported by the variance decomposition, without acknowledging or exploring the countervailing benefit of larger $m$ for probe training signal quality. This is a genuine tradeoff that a practitioner would need to understand — in domains where the between-prompt variance $\Sigma_b$ is small relative to the within-prompt variance $\Sigma_w$, the Proposition 1 penalty for larger $m$ is minor, and the improved probe training signal from multi-sample leave-one-out targets might dominate, making $m = 4$ or $m = 8$ preferable.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

POISE introduces a conceptual shift in how the RLVR community thinks about baseline estimation: **the value function can be extracted from the policy model's own forward pass rather than supplied by an external critic or empirical group statistics.** This is not an incremental refinement of GRPO's sampling efficiency or PPO's critic architecture — it is a reframing of *where the value signal lives*. The paper's central empirical result (Figure 1) makes this reframing concrete: a linear probe over the policy's hidden states achieves *better* value prediction (Pearson r = 0.870) than a separately trained LLM-scale critic (r = 0.676), despite adding negligible computation. If this result transfers across model families and scales, it suggests that the internal representations of large pretrained models are not merely encoding information *about* the task — they are encoding information *about the model's own expected performance on the task*, which is precisely what a value function should capture.

Prior to this work, the field's implicit assumption was that value estimation requires *external* computation — either training a second model (PPO, SPPO) or collecting repeated samples (GRPO, DAPO). The underlying justification was that the policy model, by definition, produces actions, not evaluations of those actions. POISE challenges this assumption by demonstrating that the boundary between "actor" and "critic" is blurred in large pretrained models: the same hidden states that generate reasoning tokens also encode whether that reasoning is likely to succeed. The practical consequence is that **a whole class of RLVR overhead — auxiliary critic models, group sampling, degenerate-prompt filtering — becomes avoidable in principle**, not just optimizable in practice.

The paper also resolves a latent tension in the GRPO literature. GRPO's group-mean baseline was originally motivated as a way to eliminate the critic while maintaining a prompt-conditional baseline. But subsequent work (Gao et al., 2025; this paper's Proposition 1) revealed that group sampling introduces a new cost: reduced prompt diversity inflates gradient variance. The field's response has been to develop workarounds — dynamic sampling (DAPO), selective rollouts (Zheng et al., 2025), prefix reuse (PROS), non-uniform prompt filtering (Xu & Ding, 2025) — each of which mitigates but does not eliminate the underlying tension. POISE reframes the problem entirely: rather than asking "how can we make group sampling cheaper?", it asks "can we avoid group sampling altogether by reading the baseline from the policy's own state?" The affirmative answer means that future work on baseline estimation has a third option beyond the critic-group tradeoff — an option that preserves prompt diversity (m = 2 rollouts per prompt, versus m = 8 for GRPO) without training a separate model.

**Which research directions become more attractive.** The paper makes the study of *internal representations as optimization signals* a legitimate subfield of RLVR research, rather than a curiosity of the interpretability community. This opens several lines of inquiry: which hidden states are most value-predictive across architectures and tasks? Can the probe be replaced with a parameter-free heuristic (e.g., entropy alone achieves r = 0.780 in Table 3)? Can internal state value estimates inform other stages of the training pipeline, such as data filtering, prompt curriculum design, or early stopping? The probabilistic interpretation of hidden states as encoding the model's own capability boundaries — an idea Zhu et al. (2025) introduced diagnostically — can now be studied as an *operational tool* for training efficiency.

**Which directions become less central.** The paper's results diminish the urgency of research on making GRPO's group sampling more efficient. If a two-rollout learned baseline from internal states matches the performance of eight-rollout group-relative baselines, the marginal benefit of further optimizing dynamic sampling, selective rollouts, or prefix reuse is bounded — the ceiling is what POISE already achieves without those mechanisms. Similarly, research on lightweight critic architectures (e.g., smaller value heads, distilled critics) becomes less pressing if the policy's own hidden states provide a critic-quality signal at negligible cost. The paper does not *obsolete* these lines of work — GRPO remains dominant in production systems, and POISE's scaling properties are untested at large model sizes — but it redirects attention toward probing and exploiting the policy's internal value representations, which were previously invisible to the RLVR optimization literature.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Multi-seed replication with confidence intervals on the main RL results.** The paper's central empirical claim — that POISE matches DAPO in final accuracy while reducing wall-clock time by 25–27% — rests on single training runs. A direct replication across 3–5 random seeds per model per method would establish the statistical reliability of this claim and characterize run-to-run variance. The key measurement: mean and standard deviation of Avg@32 at the final checkpoint, and mean and standard deviation of wall-clock time to reach a specified accuracy threshold (e.g., 0.290 Avg@32 for the 1.5B model, 0.490 for the 4B model). If the standard deviation across seeds is, say, ±0.015 in Avg@32, then the observed 0.008 gap between POISE and DAPO on Qwen3-4B is within noise, and the performance-equivalence claim holds. If the standard deviation is ±0.003, then DAPO has a small but statistically reliable edge that the paper's single-run framing misses. This experiment is low-risk and high-value — it requires no new method development, only compute budget for repeated training runs.

**POISE training on coding (AceCoder) and instruction-following (IF-RLVR) domains.** The paper demonstrates that the internal state probe predicts expected reward well in these domains (Table 7: r = 0.612–0.642 on Qwen3-4B), but never closes the loop by running full POISE training on non-math tasks. A direct experiment would train POISE on AceCoder (coding with unit-test verification) and IF-RLVR (instruction-following with constraint-based verification) using the same Qwen3-4B backbone, and compare against DAPO baselines. The key questions: does the probe continue to track the evolving policy when the reward structure differs (multi-test pass rates vs. binary answer checking)? Does the learned baseline provide effective advantages when trajectory lengths are shorter (coding solutions are often briefer than math chain-of-thought)? Does the 30% variance reduction observed in math (Figure 4) hold in these domains, or does the value signal degrade with different verifier types? A negative result — e.g., the probe's value estimates become poorly calibrated mid-training, leading to policy collapse — would reveal boundary conditions on when internal state value estimation is viable, which is as scientifically valuable as a positive result.

**Controlled experiment isolating prompt diversity from baseline quality.** POISE claims two sources of advantage over DAPO: better prompt diversity (m = 2 vs. m = 8 rollouts per prompt, reducing between-prompt gradient variance by 4× per Proposition 1) and a learned continuous baseline (potentially more accurate than the discrete group mean). These effects are confounded in the main comparison. A controlled experiment would run three conditions at matched total generation budget: (a) POISE with m = 2 and cross-rollout learned baseline, (b) a hypothetical "GRPO-2" with m = 2 and a group-mean baseline (acknowledging that 2-sample group means are very noisy), and (c) POISE with m = 8 and a modified cross-rollout construction (using the 7 other rollouts' features to form the baseline for each rollout, with leave-one-out Monte Carlo targets for probe training). Comparing (a) vs. (b) isolates the effect of the learned baseline (holding prompt diversity constant at m = 2); comparing (a) vs. (c) isolates the effect of prompt diversity (holding the learned baseline mechanism constant). The prediction from Proposition 1 and Table 3 is that the prompt diversity effect (a vs. c) dominates on benchmarks with high between-prompt variance $\Sigma_b$ (i.e., diverse prompt sets like MATH), while the baseline quality effect (a vs. b) dominates when $\Sigma_b$ is small. This experiment would validate or refine the theoretical analysis, and identify which component of POISE drives the gains in which regimes.

**POISE at scale: value estimation quality and training stability on 8B–70B models.** The paper tests models up to 4B parameters (7B for value prediction only). At larger scales — 8B, 32B, 70B — several things change: hidden states are higher-dimensional (the probe input dimension grows linearly with d_model, potentially requiring larger buffers or dimensionality reduction), the policy's pass@1 on reasoning tasks is higher (changing the reward distribution and the value function's dynamic range), and the cost of group sampling (DAPO's m = 8 rollouts) becomes proportionally more burdensome because each rollout is longer on average. A scaling study would train POISE and DAPO on, e.g., LLaMA-3.1-8B, Qwen3-32B, or DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B on DAPO-Math, measuring: final Avg@32, wall-clock time, peak GPU memory, and the probe's online MAE and variance reduction ratio throughout training. The hypothesis is that POISE's relative advantage grows with model scale (because DAPO's group sampling cost scales with both model size and trajectory length, while POISE's probe cost scales only with feature dimension, which can be PCA-reduced). A negative result — e.g., the probe's value prediction degrades at larger hidden-state dimensions because the linear probe underfits — would motivate research on more expressive probe architectures for large models.

**Token-level value estimation from internal states.** The current POISE estimator predicts a single prompt-level scalar value V(x). The paper explicitly identifies token-level credit assignment as a natural extension in Section 8: extending the probe to predict per-token values would yield finer-grained advantages that distinguish critical reasoning steps from routine ones — an effect that Wang et al. (2026) and Lin et al. (2025) have shown is especially impactful for long reasoning trajectories. A concrete follow-up would extract hidden states at each reasoning token (not just the final 10), train a linear probe to predict a token-level value target (e.g., the final reward R(x, y) propagated backward with a discount factor, or a learned potential function), and use these per-token values as baselines in a token-level PPO update. The cross-rollout construction would need modification: per-token features of rollout j could predict per-token baselines for rollout i only if the trajectories are length-aligned or if the probe learns a position-invariant value function. This is a harder problem than prompt-level estimation — the value signal per token is sparser (most tokens are routine, with value near the prompt average) and the conditional independence constraint is more complex — but success would directly improve the gradient signal quality for long-chain-of-thought reasoning, where POISE currently sees only 30% variance reduction at the sequence level (Figure 4).

**Internal state value estimates for data filtering and curriculum design in RLVR.** POISE's value estimator provides a continuous signal of how "solvable" each prompt is under the current policy. This signal could be used outside the advantage computation: prompts with predicted values near 0.5 (maximum uncertainty, maximum gradient information) could be prioritized in the training batch, while prompts with values near 0 or 1 (the policy already knows it can't or can solve them) could be downsampled or deferred to later training stages. This is a form of automatic curriculum learning that requires no external difficulty labels — the value estimator produces them online. A concrete experiment: modify POISE's prompt sampling to be proportional to $g_f(\phi) \cdot (1 - g_f(\phi))$ (the variance of the binary reward under the predicted value), compare against uniform sampling, and measure whether this accelerates convergence to a given Avg@32 threshold. A positive result would integrate the value estimator into the data pipeline, not just the gradient computation, increasing its impact on training efficiency beyond what the current paper demonstrates.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient RLVR fine-tuning for domain-specific reasoning models.** Organizations fine-tuning open-weight reasoning models (Qwen, DeepSeek, LLaMA) on proprietary math, coding, or scientific reasoning datasets can adopt POISE to reduce GPU-hours per training run. The paper's wall-clock measurements provide concrete guidance: on a single B200 GPU, training a 1.5B reasoning model takes approximately 18 hours with POISE versus 24 hours with DAPO — a 25% reduction. For a 4B model on two B200 GPUs, the savings are 36 vs. 49 hours (27% reduction). Extrapolating: an organization training an 8B model for 5 days on 8 GPUs with DAPO could complete the same training in approximately 3.5–4 days with POISE, saving 1–1.5 days of accelerator time per run. For teams iterating on reward functions, hyperparameters, or data mixtures — where multiple training runs are needed — this compounds. The primary adoption barrier is the need to implement feature extraction hooks and the sliding-buffer value estimator (both of which the paper's code release would provide), and the need to verify that the value estimator tracks the policy reliably on the target domain (which the generalization results in Table 7 suggest is likely).

**On-device or edge RLVR fine-tuning where memory excludes a separate critic.** PPO's critic model roughly doubles GPU memory consumption during training, which can be prohibitive for fine-tuning on consumer GPUs or edge devices with limited VRAM. POISE replaces the critic with a linear probe on features already computed during the forward pass, adding negligible memory overhead — the probe's parameters are a weight matrix of size (2d_model + 3) × 1 plus a StandardScaler, totaling a few tens of kilobytes. This enables RLVR fine-tuning on hardware that cannot accommodate a second LLM-scale model in memory. Concretely: a researcher with a single 24GB GPU who cannot fine-tune a 7B model with PPO (actor + critic ≈ 28GB) could fine-tune the same model with POISE (actor ≈ 14GB + kilobytes for the probe), making RLVR accessible without model parallelism or gradient checkpointing tricks. The paper does not benchmark memory consumption directly, but the architectural difference is clear: POISE eliminates the second model entirely. The practical caveat is that the paper's experiments use B200 GPUs with 192GB of memory, so the memory-constrained use case is inferred from the method design, not demonstrated empirically.

**Rapid prototyping and hyperparameter search for RLVR reward functions.** In RLVR, the verifiable reward function often undergoes iteration — modifying the answer parser, adding partial credit, adjusting format constraints — and each change requires a new training run to evaluate its effect. The 25–27% wall-clock reduction that POISE provides directly accelerates this iteration cycle. A team exploring 10 reward function variants for a math reasoning task would spend roughly 180 hours with POISE versus 240 hours with DAPO on the 1.5B model, a savings of 2.5 days of B200 time. Beyond the direct speedup, POISE's elimination of DAPO's dynamic sampling means that the training time per step is predictable (no variable overhead from filtering degenerate groups), which simplifies resource scheduling and cost estimation. This is a practical benefit for industrial research teams where GPU allocation is budgeted in advance.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions POISE against two named alternatives — PPO (with a separate critic model) and GRPO/DAPO (with group-relative baselines) — and provides empirical evidence for specific tradeoffs. A practitioner deciding among these methods should consider:

- **Prefer POISE over DAPO/GRPO when:** (1) GPU memory constrains the ability to train a separate critic, but you want a learned baseline rather than a group-relative one; (2) your prompt distribution is diverse, making the between-prompt gradient variance penalty of group sampling (Proposition 1) large — this is typical of open-ended reasoning benchmarks where prompts vary widely in difficulty and structure; (3) wall-clock training time per step is dominated by rollout generation, and you want to eliminate the overhead of degenerate-group detection and filtering; (4) you are training a model from the Qwen or similar architecture family where the hidden-state extraction hyperparameters (layer 19, last-10-token pooling) have been validated to carry value-relevant signal. The expected benefit is 25–27% faster wall-clock convergence at matched final accuracy, based on the paper's measurements on math reasoning.

- **Prefer DAPO/GRPO over POISE when:** (1) The prompt distribution is narrow and homogeneous (most prompts have similar difficulty), making the between-prompt variance $\Sigma_b$ small — in this regime, DAPO's group sampling penalty is minor and its stronger baseline accuracy (from 8-sample empirical means vs. POISE's binary leave-one-out targets) may dominate; (2) you are training a model architecture where the optimal hidden-state extraction layer is unknown and running a layer sweep is impractical — DAPO requires no such architecture-specific tuning; (3) you need per-token baselines (DAPO normalizes advantages per-token through its group statistics) rather than the prompt-level baseline POISE currently provides; (4) your training infrastructure already has highly optimized dynamic sampling (e.g., the Zheng et al. 2025 implementation) and the degenerate-group overhead is minimal in your setting.

- **Prefer PPO with a separate critic over POISE when:** (1) You have abundant GPU memory and can afford the critic's overhead, and you value the rich per-token value signal that a token-level critic with GAE provides — POISE currently only estimates prompt-level value, which is coarser; (2) you are training on a domain where the internal state value signal has not been validated (Table 7 shows strong generalization for value prediction, but full RL training is only demonstrated on math); (3) you require KL-constrained policy updates (standard PPO implementations include a KL penalty against a reference policy) and POISE's current formulation omits this; (4) you are training models outside the Qwen/DeepSeek architecture family and prefer not to invest in architecture-specific probe hyperparameter tuning.

These recommendations are grounded in the paper's results but should be weighted by two unmeasured factors: the run-to-run variance in POISE's training stability (single-seed results limit confidence), and the untested performance of POISE on model scales beyond 4B parameters, where the compute savings from avoiding group sampling are largest but where the linear probe's adequacy is unverified.
