# Minitron-SSM: Efficient Hybrid Language Model Compression through Group-Aware SSM Pruning

**ArXiv:** [2504.11409](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11409)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces a **group-aware pruning strategy** for compressing hybrid LLM architectures that combine Attention and State Space Models (specifically, Mamba2), preserving the structural integrity of SSM blocks by enforcing within-group head permutations and shared channel ranking.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces a **group-aware pruning strategy** for compressing hybrid LLM architectures that combine Attention and State Space Models (specifically, Mamba2), preserving the structural integrity of SSM blocks by enforcing within-group head permutations and shared channel ranking. Using the Nemotron-H 8B hybrid model as the starting point, the authors apply a unified pruning recipe—combining SSM head and channel pruning with FFN neuron, embedding dimension, and layer pruning, followed by knowledge distillation—to produce a 4B-parameter model requiring up to 40× fewer training tokens than comparably sized models trained from scratch. The resulting Nemotron-H 4B model retains over 96% of the original 8B model's accuracy while achieving ~2× faster inference (2.2× throughput improvement over the next-best Phi-4-Mini-4B) and a 2.6% accuracy gain on the aligned model, advancing the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier—establishing that width-pruned hybrid models can outperform both depth-pruned variants and from-scratch training, but only when Mamba heads are preserved as the most efficiency-sensitive pruning axis relative to FFN and embedding dimensions.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Gap: How Do You Compress a Hybrid Architecture?

The paper addresses a straightforward but under-explored question: **if you have a hybrid Attention-SSM model that is too large for deployment, how do you systematically compress it while preserving both accuracy and inference speed?** This matters because hybrid architectures—which intermix standard Transformer layers with State Space Model (SSM) layers—have recently emerged as a compelling alternative to pure Transformers, but the compression literature has almost entirely focused on Transformer-only models. The authors explicitly state the gap:

> "While pruning techniques have been extensively studied for Transformer architectures [5, 6, 7], their application to hybrid models remains significantly underexplored."

This gap is not merely academic. The Nemotron-H 8B model used in this paper is a representative hybrid architecture that replaces 92% of its attention layers with Mamba2 blocks, yet it still contains 8 billion parameters. For deployment on resource-constrained devices—edge hardware, mobile phones, or latency-sensitive cloud services—an 8B model may simply be too large, too slow, or too memory-hungry. The practical question is: can you cut this model in half while preserving its essential capabilities, and if so, what is the right way to do it?

### Why Existing Compression Knowledge Doesn't Transfer Directly

Transformer-only compression is a mature field. Methods like MINITRON (Muralidharan et al., 2024) have established a reliable three-phase recipe: (1) estimate component importance using activation-based metrics, (2) trim the least-important neurons, attention heads, embedding channels, and layers, and (3) recover accuracy through knowledge distillation from the original model. This approach works because Transformer components—FFN neurons, attention heads, embedding dimensions—are **permutation-equivariant**: you can reorder them arbitrarily without changing the network's function, because the operations are all linear projections followed by element-wise or position-independent computations.

Mamba layers break this property. As Section 3 of the paper explains in detail, the Mamba forward pass involves a **broadcast operation** between the $B$ matrix and the input $x$, organized into **groups**. The $B$ matrix is reshaped to $B \in \mathbb{R}^{g \times d_s}$ (where $g$ is the number of Mamba groups and $d_s$ is the state dimension) and then broadcast across the Mamba heads within each group. This means heads within the same group share a specific slice of $B$, creating a coupling that does not exist in Transformer attention heads. If you naively permute heads across group boundaries—the natural thing to do when sorting by importance—you break the $B_t x_t$ broadcast pattern, as shown by Equation 16:

$$B_t x_t \neq (B \mathcal{P}(x_t)) \mathcal{P}^T$$

The paper's Figure 3 visually illustrates this: swapping heads across groups produces a computation that cannot be recovered by any permutation of the original output. This is the technical crux that makes hybrid model compression fundamentally different from Transformer compression—you must respect the group structure or you silently corrupt the model's forward pass.

### Prior Work on SSM Pruning and Its Limitations

The authors identify two existing approaches to SSM-specific pruning, both of which fall short of a complete compression recipe:

**Mamba-Shredder (Muñoz et al., 2025)** takes the most drastic approach: it removes the entire state space module from Mamba layers, leaving only the linear projections and convolution layer. While this simplifies the architecture, it fundamentally alters the model's computational character—you are no longer pruning the SSM but rather surgically removing the component that makes it an SSM. The paper does not treat this as pruning in the structured sense but rather as a layer-type modification. More critically, Mamba-Shredder does not consider how SSM pruning interacts with FFN, embedding, or depth pruning, making it a single-axis approach.

**Ghattas et al. (2025)** propose pruning Mamba architectures along three dimensions: state space dimension reduction, Mamba head dimension pruning, and Mamba head merging. This is closer in spirit to the current work but still has fundamental limitations. It focuses **exclusively on the Mamba/SSM component**, ignoring the other major contributors to model size (FFN layers, embedding tables, network depth). Furthermore, the authors of the current paper note that head merging fundamentally restructures the computation graph in ways that may not preserve the original model's representational capacity. Most importantly, neither Mamba-Shredder nor Ghattas et al. combine their SSM-pruning techniques with the well-established Transformer pruning playbook (FFN pruning, embedding pruning, depth pruning, and knowledge distillation for accuracy recovery).

The authors state their position clearly:

> "To the best of our knowledge, no existing work on SSM/Mamba pruning presents a holistic compression strategy that simultaneously combines various aspects of SSM pruning with the pruning of other network components such as FFN neurons, embedding channels, and network depth; we believe such an approach is essential for obtaining the best combination of runtime performance and model accuracy."

This is the key positioning claim: the authors are not arguing that prior SSM pruning work is wrong, but rather that it is **incomplete**. A practical compression pipeline needs to simultaneously optimize across all model components because the parameter budget is shared—if you don't prune FFN neurons enough, you must prune Mamba heads more aggressively to hit the same target size, and this tradeoff fundamentally affects both accuracy and throughput.

### The Broader Significance: Why Hybrid Architectures Are the Right Target

The importance of this problem is amplified by the trajectory of LLM architecture design. Pure Transformer models face well-known scaling bottlenecks: KV-cache memory grows linearly with sequence length, and attention computation is quadratic. These limitations make pure Transformers expensive for long-context applications (document processing, multi-turn conversation, codebase understanding). Hybrid architectures address this by replacing most attention layers with SSMs, which have $O(1)$ cache size during inference and $O(N)$ training complexity.

Nemotron-H 8B is a case in point: it achieves up to 3× higher inference throughput compared to pure Transformers while maintaining state-of-the-art accuracy (Blakeman et al., 2025). But 8B parameters is still a substantial model—the paper's goal is to bring these efficiency benefits to the 4B parameter scale, where models can run on consumer GPUs or even edge devices.

The paper also positions its work within a broader economic argument about **training cost**. Training large models from scratch is enormously expensive—Table 4 shows that comparable 3-4B models are trained on anywhere from 9 trillion tokens (Llama-3.2 3B) to 18 trillion tokens (Qwen-2.5 3B). The compression approach in this paper uses only 380 billion tokens for knowledge distillation, representing a **~24× reduction in training tokens** versus Llama-3.2 and a **~47× reduction** versus Qwen-2.5. If compression can produce competitive models at a small fraction of the training cost, it fundamentally changes the economics of model development—organizations could amortize the cost of training one large model and then efficiently produce a family of smaller models via pruning rather than training each size independently from scratch.

### The MINITRON Baseline and What Needs to Change

The paper explicitly builds on MINITRON (Muralidharan et al., 2024), which established the pruning-then-distillation paradigm for Transformer-only models. The MINITRON recipe uses:
- Activation-based importance scoring (mean aggregation over sequence, L2 aggregation over batch)
- Structured pruning of attention heads, FFN neurons, embedding channels, and layers
- Forward KL divergence (FKLD) logit-based knowledge distillation for accuracy recovery

This paper's contribution is not to replace MINITRON but to **extend it to handle the SSM-specific constraints that MINITRON's permutation-equivariance assumptions do not cover**. The novelties are:
1. The group-aware permutation constraint for Mamba head ranking (Equation 17)
2. The nested head-channel-then-head scoring procedure (Algorithm 1 in the paper)
3. The empirical characterization of the accuracy-speed tradeoffs specific to hybrid architectures (Figure 6, Figure 7)
4. The demonstration that width-only pruning dominates depth-pruning for this architecture class (Table 1)

The paper positions these as **necessary adaptations** rather than a complete departure from MINITRON. The distillation component, the overall three-phase pipeline, and the importance estimation philosophy remain largely unchanged—what changes is the recognition that Mamba layers impose structural constraints that must be explicitly encoded in the pruning procedure.

### Where the Paper Sits in the Research Landscape

The paper occupies a specific and underpopulated intersection: **structured pruning × hybrid SSM-Transformer architectures × knowledge distillation**. Each of these three components has independent literatures (structured pruning for Transformers, SSM architecture design, and model distillation), but their intersection is largely unexplored. The concurrent work by Ghattas et al. (2025) is the closest point of comparison, but it operates exclusively on the SSM axis without integration with Transformer pruning techniques or distillation.

The paper also intersects with the architecture search and model compression communities. The combinatorial search over width dimensions (FFN size, embedding size, Mamba heads, Mamba head channels) to hit a fixed parameter budget is reminiscent of neural architecture search (NAS) for LLMs (Bercovich et al., 2024), but with the constraint that all candidates are derived from a single pretrained model via pruning rather than trained independently.

Finally, the paper makes a subtle but important methodological point: **the difficulty of compressing hybrid architectures is not symmetrical across all components**. The empirical findings—that Mamba head pruning improves speed but hurts accuracy relatively little, while FFN/embedding pruning improves accuracy but hurts speed, and depth pruning is the most accuracy-sensitive—amount to a taxonomy of pruning sensitivity for hybrid architectures that did not previously exist. This taxonomy is actionable: it tells practitioners where to allocate their pruning budget depending on whether their primary constraint is accuracy, latency, or throughput.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops a pruning pipeline that shrinks a hybrid Attention-Mamba2 language model from 8B to 4B parameters while preserving over 96% of its accuracy. The core problem is that Mamba layers are not permutation-equivariant like Transformer components—naively sorting and trimming Mamba heads by importance would silently corrupt the forward pass by breaking group-level broadcast patterns. The solution shape is a **group-aware ranking constraint** that forces pruning decisions to respect Mamba's internal group structure, combined with a multi-axis structured pruning recipe (Mamba heads, Mamba head channels, FFN neurons, embedding channels, and optionally layers) followed by knowledge distillation for accuracy recovery.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components operating in sequence:

1. **Importance Scoring Module** — takes the pretrained 8B teacher model and a small calibration dataset (1024 samples, sequence length 8192); runs forward passes to collect activation statistics from Mamba projection matrices, FFN layers, and LayerNorm outputs; computes scalar importance scores for every prunable component (Mamba heads, Mamba head channels, FFN neurons, embedding channels, layers).

2. **Group-Constrained Ranking and Trimming Module** — sorts components by importance within the structural constraints imposed by Mamba groups (heads can only be permuted within their group, head channels must share a single global ranking); selects which components to keep based on target parameter counts for each architectural dimension; physically reshapes weight matrices by removing rows/columns corresponding to pruned components.

3. **Architecture Search Module** — generates over 100 candidate 4B-parameter architectures by combinatorially varying FFN size (9984–21504), embedding dimension (3072–4096), Mamba heads (64–128), Mamba head channels (32–64), and number of layers (26–52); evaluates each candidate via zero-shot LM loss on 1024 calibration samples; selects the top-22 by loss and performs lightweight knowledge distillation (3.8B tokens) to get a reliable ranking; picks the final architecture using throughput and latency as tiebreakers.

4. **Knowledge Distillation Module** — takes the trimmed 4B student model and the original 8B teacher; trains the student on 380B tokens using forward KL divergence loss over the full token-level output distributions, with a cosine-decay learning rate schedule (starting at 1.6e-4, decaying to 8e-4), 60-step linear warmup, batch size 768, and sequence length 8192.

Information flows linearly: pretrained teacher → forward passes on calibration data → importance scores → constrained sorting → matrix trimming → architecture candidates → lightweight KD evaluation → final candidate selection → extended KD recovery → 4B compressed model.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the importance scoring methodology for Mamba components (Equations 19–22 and Algorithm 1), since this is the novel SSM-specific contribution and the foundation all pruning decisions rest on.
- **Second**, the group-aware permutation constraint (Equations 16–17) and head channel consistency constraint (Equation 18), because these define *what makes Mamba pruning different from Transformer pruning*.
- **Third**, the Mamba forward pass decomposition (Equations 5–14), since understanding which weight matrices get trimmed and why requires knowing the internal structure of a Mamba layer.
- **Fourth**, FFN and embedding importance scoring (Equations 26–27), which follows the established MINITRON recipe.
- **Fifth**, the layer importance estimation via KL divergence, depth pruning experiments, and why depth pruning was ultimately rejected.
- **Sixth**, the architecture search procedure and knowledge distillation protocol, which are the meta-level optimization that selects the final model configuration and recovers accuracy.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **pruning methodology paper** whose core idea is that compressing hybrid architectures requires recognising and respecting the structural coupling imposed by Mamba groups, and that the optimal compression strategy is width-only (Mamba heads, FFN, embedding) rather than depth-based, with Mamba heads being the most efficiency-sensitive pruning axis.

#### The Mamba2 Layer Forward Pass (Necessary Context)

Before explaining the pruning procedure itself, we must understand the internal structure of the Mamba layer that is being pruned. The paper provides this decomposition in Equations 5–14, and walking through it reveals exactly which weight matrices are affected by head and channel pruning.

The Mamba layer receives an input tensor `$X$` (with batch and sequence dimensions elided in the paper's notation for clarity). The first operation is layer normalization, followed by five parallel linear projections:

$$z = W_z(\text{LN}(X)), \quad W_z \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times (m_h \times m_d)}$$

$$x = W_x(\text{LN}(X)), \quad W_x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times (m_h \times m_d)}$$

$$B = W_B(\text{LN}(X)), \quad W_B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times (g \times d_s)}$$

$$C = W_C(\text{LN}(X)), \quad W_C \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times (g \times d_s)}$$

$$dt = W_{dt}(\text{LN}(X)), \quad W_{dt} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times m_h}$$

where `$d_e$` is the model embedding dimension (hidden dimension), `$g$` is the number of Mamba groups, `$d_s$` is the SSM state dimension, `$m_h$` is the number of Mamba heads, and `$m_d$` is the Mamba head channel dimension.

**What these projections compute:** The input passes through layer normalization, then five independent linear transformations produce the signals needed for the selective SSM computation. `$z$` is the gating signal (used later in a multiplicative gate), `$x$` is the main input to the SSM, `$B$` and `$C$` are input-dependent state transition parameters (making the SSM "selective" by letting the input modulate how the hidden state evolves and how it projects to the output), and `$dt$` is a per-head time-step modulation.

**Why five projections:** This design follows the Mamba2 architecture (Dao and Gu, 2024), where the selective SSM requires the `$B_t$` and `$C_t$` matrices to be input-dependent rather than fixed. The `$z$` gate provides a learned gating mechanism that controls how much of the SSM output passes through. The dimensionality `$m_h \times m_d$` for `$W_x$` and `$W_z$` means these projections treat the head and channel dimensions as a flat combined dimension. The dimensionality `$g \times d_s$` for `$W_B$` and `$W_C$` means these projections operate at the group level (each group gets its own slice of size `$d_s$`), not the head level—this group-level structure is exactly what creates the permutation constraint.

Next, the projections `$x$`, `$B$`, and `$C$` undergo causal 1D convolution (for temporal smoothing):

$$\hat{x} = \text{conv1d}(x)$$

$$\hat{B} = \text{conv1d}(B)$$

$$\hat{C} = \text{conv1d}(C)$$

These convolved signals then enter the selective SSM:

$$\tilde{y} = \text{SSM}(\hat{x}, \hat{B}, \hat{C}, A, D, dt)$$

where `$A \in \mathbb{R}^{m_h}$` and `$D \in \mathbb{R}^{m_h}$` are learnable SSM parameters (state transition and direct feedthrough, respectively) that exist per-head—there is one scalar `$A$` value and one scalar `$D$` value for each Mamba head.

The SSM output is then combined with the gating signal `$z$` through RMS normalization and a gating operation, followed by a final output projection:

$$y = W_O(\text{RMSNorm}(\tilde{y}, z))$$

where `$W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{(m_h \times m_d) \times d_e}$` is the output projection matrix that maps back to the embedding dimension.

**What this output projection does:** The SSM has transformed the input through the state space dynamics, producing a representation of shape `$(m_h \times m_d)$`. The gating multiplies this by `$z$` element-wise after RMSNorm (controlling information flow). The output projection `$W_O$` maps this back to `$d_e$` dimensions so the output can connect to the next layer's input.

**Why this decomposition matters for pruning:** The pruning procedure needs to remove Mamba heads and head channels, which means removing rows/columns from specific weight matrices. Equation 25 of the paper specifies exactly which matrices are affected:

> $$W \leftarrow W[\mathcal{R}], \quad \text{for } W \in \{W_x, W_z, W_O, W_A, W_D, W_{dt}, \text{conv1d}\}$$

Here `$\mathcal{R}$` is the set of head indices to keep. When a head is pruned, we must remove the corresponding rows from `$W_x$`, `$W_z$`, `$W_O$`, `$W_{dt}$`, and the corresponding entries from `$A$` and `$D$` (which are per-head scalars), and the corresponding channels from the conv1d kernel. The conv1d operates on the `$(m_h \times m_d)$`-dimensional space, so head pruning affects its channel dimension. Understanding this mapping—which matrices have head-level structure versus group-level structure versus channel-level structure—is essential for understanding the pruning constraints that follow.

#### The Group-Aware Permutation Constraint (Why Mamba Is Different)

The critical technical challenge in Mamba pruning is that Mamba layers are **not permutation-equivariant** in the way that Transformer layers are. To understand what this means and why it constrains pruning, we must examine the `$B_t x_t$` operation at the heart of the selective SSM.

The selective SSM (Equation 3 from the paper) computes:

$$h_t = A_t h_{t-1} + B_t x_t$$

$$y_t = C_t^\top h_t + D_t x_t$$

In Mamba2, `$B_t$` is the input-dependent `$B$` matrix at time step `$t$` (derived from the projection `$W_B$`). The `$B_t x_t$` operation involves a **broadcast** of `$B$` across the heads, organized by groups.

The paper explains this broadcast in Figure 3 and Equation 16. The `$B$` matrix has dimensions corresponding to groups and state dimension: `$B \in \mathbb{R}^{g \times d_s}$`. The input `$x$` has dimensions corresponding to heads and head channels: `$x \in \mathbb{R}^{(m_h \times m_d)}$`. The broadcast operation means that all heads within a given group share the same slice of `$B$`. This creates a structural coupling: **heads in the same group are not independent** with respect to the `$B_t x_t$` computation.

Now consider what happens when we sort Mamba heads by importance and permute them. If we move a head from group 1 into group 2 (because importance sorting doesn't know about groups), the broadcast pattern changes. Head H3 originally belonged to group 1 and received group 1's slice of `$B$`. After moving H3 to group 2's position, it would receive group 2's slice of `$B$`, producing a different `$B_t x_t$` result. Critically, this new result is **not** any permutation of the original—the group structure has been altered in a way that no output permutation can undo. The paper states this formally in Equation 16:

$$B_t x_t \neq (B \mathcal{P}(x_t)) \mathcal{P}^T$$

**What Equation 16 means:** If you apply a permutation `$\mathcal{P}$` to `$x_t$` that crosses group boundaries (moving heads between groups), the resulting `$B_t x_t$` cannot be recovered by applying any permutation to the output. In contrast, for a standard linear projection `$Wx$`, permuting `$x$` and correspondingly permuting `$W$`'s columns gives a permuted output—permutation equivariance. The broadcast breaks this property.

**Why this is the fundamental constraint:** For Transformer attention heads or FFN neurons, you can freely sort by importance, prune the lowest-ranked ones, and the model's function is preserved (up to the information loss from pruning). For Mamba heads, this is true **only within groups**. If you prune the lowest-ranked heads globally without respecting group boundaries, you silently introduce a computational error that cannot be compensated for.

The solution is the **group-constrained permutation rule** (Equation 17):

$$\mathcal{P}(h) \in \mathcal{G}_g \quad \forall h \in \mathcal{G}_g$$

where `$\mathcal{G}_g \subset \{1, \dots, m_h\}$` is the set of heads belonging to group `$g$`, and `$\mathcal{P}$` is any permutation applied during ranking.

**What Equation 17 means in practice:** When ranking heads by importance, you must rank them independently within each group. Within group 1, you sort group 1's heads from most to least important. Within group 2, you sort group 2's heads independently. You then prune the lowest-ranked heads from each group separately. The number of heads kept per group is determined by the target `$k_g$` value (Equation 24), which can vary by group if desired, though the paper's experiments use uniform pruning across groups.

**Why this constraint exists only for Mamba and not for Transformers:** Transformer attention heads compute independent `$QK^T$` operations that are fully permutation-equivariant—you can reorder heads arbitrarily as long as you correspondingly reorder the output projection's input dimensions. The Mamba broadcast couples heads to their group assignment, breaking this reorderability.

#### The Head Channel Consistency Constraint

A second structural constraint applies when pruning Mamba head channels (the `$m_d$` dimension). The state tensor `$h \in \mathbb{R}^{m_h \times m_d \times d_s}$` has channels shared across heads. The paper states in Equation 18:

$$\mathcal{P}_d(h_{i,j,k}) = \mathcal{P}_d(h_{i',j,k}) \quad \forall i, i' \in \{1, \dots, m_h\}$$

**What Equation 18 means:** If you permute or prune channel `$k$`, you must do it uniformly across all heads `$i$` and `$i'``. You cannot prune channel 3 from head 1 while keeping channel 3 in head 2—the channels must be either all kept or all pruned across all heads.

**Why this constraint exists:** The Mamba convolution and SSM operations treat the `$m_d$` dimension as a feature dimension within each head, and the convolution kernel has weights that are shared across heads in terms of channel structure (though each head has its own conv1d parameters, the channel dimension itself must be consistent because the input and output projections are structured as `$(m_h \times m_d)$` flat matrices).

**Practical consequence for pruning:** Head channel pruning uses a **single global ranking** across all heads. The channel importance score `$s_d$` (Equation 20) is computed by aggregating activation magnitudes over all heads for that channel index, then taking the L2 norm. This gives a single scalar per channel index that is independent of which head it belongs to. Pruning then selects the top-`$k_d$` channels globally, and those channels are removed from all heads simultaneously.

#### Importance Scoring for Mamba Components

With the structural constraints established, we can now walk through the scoring procedure that determines which heads and channels to keep. The paper presents this as a **nested two-stage procedure**: first rank channels, then rank heads using only the kept channels. This ordering matters because head importance should be evaluated based on the channels that will survive pruning, not on channels destined for removal.

**Step 1: Head Channel Scoring (Equations 19–20)**

The raw activation scores are computed from the `$W_x$` projection output:

$$s = \text{LN}(X)(W_x)^T$$

where `$s \in \mathbb{R}^{(m_h \times m_d)}$` contains the activation magnitudes for all heads and all channels, aggregated over batch and sequence dimensions (the paper elides batch and sequence for notational simplicity, but the aggregation is explicit in Equation 20).

**What this computes:** For each calibration sample, the input `$X$` is layer-normalized, then multiplied by `$W_x^T$` to produce the projected values for every head and channel. The paper then aggregates these activations over both the batch dimension `$B$` and sequence length dimension `$L$` to produce a single scalar per channel. The aggregation uses mean over the sequence dimension and L2 norm over the batch dimension, following the MINITRON recipe.

The per-channel importance score is:

$$s_d = \left\| \sum_{B,L} s_{:,d} \right\|_2$$

where `$s_{:,d}$` denotes the `$d$`-th column across all heads.

**What Equation 20 computes:** For each channel index `$d$`, it collects the activation values for that channel from all heads, sums them over batch and sequence (with mean along sequence and L2 along batch), and takes the L2 norm of the result. This produces a single scalar `$s_d$` representing the aggregate importance of that channel across all heads.

**Why this aggregation:** Summing over sequence length captures the total activation mass—channels that fire strongly across many tokens are more important. The L2 norm over batch captures variance-weighted importance—channels with high mean and high variance across samples contribute more. This contrasts with L1 or mean aggregation, which would weight rare but large activations less than consistent moderate activations.

Once channel scores are computed, the top-`$k_d$` channels are selected:

$$\mathcal{D}_{\text{top}} = \text{topk}_{d \in \{1,\dots,m_d\}} (s_d, k=k_d)$$

where `$k_d$` is the target number of channels to keep (determined by the architecture search).

**Step 2: Head Scoring (Equation 22)**

Head importance is computed using **only the surviving channels** (those in `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{top}}$`):

$$f_h = \left\| s_{h,\mathcal{D}_{\text{top}}} \right\|_2 \quad \forall h \in \{1, \dots, m_h\}$$

**What Equation 22 computes:** For each head `$h$`, take the row of activation scores corresponding to that head, but only consider the columns (channels) that survived the channel pruning step. Take the L2 norm of this restricted row. This gives a scalar `$f_h$` representing the head's importance based on the channels that will actually be present in the pruned model.

**Why this two-stage approach rather than independent scoring:** If you scored heads using all channels and then pruned both heads and channels independently, you might keep a head that was important only because of its contribution to channels that are being pruned—the head's apparent importance would be inflated by channels being removed. By scoring heads on the surviving channel subset, the ranking reflects the head's contribution in the pruned architecture. This is a careful design choice that prevents information leakage from channels marked for deletion.

**Step 3: Group-Constrained Ranking (Equations 23–24)**

Within each Mamba group `$\mathcal{G}_g$`, heads are sorted by their scores:

$$\mathcal{R}_g = \text{argsort}_{h \in \mathcal{G}_g} (f_h)$$

The final head ranking is the concatenation of group-wise rankings, keeping `$k_g$` heads per group:

$$\mathcal{R} = \bigoplus_{g=1}^{G} \mathcal{R}_g[1:k_g]$$

where `$\bigoplus$` denotes ordered concatenation and `$k_g$` is the target head count for group `$g$`.

**What this does:** For each group independently, sort heads from highest to lowest importance. Keep the top `$k_g$` heads. Concatenate these kept-head lists from all groups into a single ranking. This respects the constraint that heads cannot cross group boundaries—the ranking within group 1 is completely independent of group 2.

**Why concatenation rather than interleaving:** The concatenation preserves group contiguity in the resulting weight matrices, which is important for the matrix trimming step (Equation 25) where entire rows are removed from `$W_x$`, `$W_z$`, etc. If heads were interleaved across groups, the trimming would scatter across the weight matrix, making the reshaping more complex and potentially less hardware-efficient.

**Algorithm 1 (paper's pseudocode):** The paper provides a concise algorithmic description:

> **Require:** Activation scores `$s \in \mathbb{R}^{m_h \times m_d}$`, target channels `$k_d$`, target heads per group `$\{k_g\}_{g=1}^G$`\
> **Ensure:** Head ranking `$\mathcal{R}$`, channel ranking `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{top}}$`
> 1. Compute channel scores: `$s_d \leftarrow \|s_{:,d}\|_2 \;\; \forall d$`
> 2. `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{top}} \leftarrow$` top-`$k_d$` indices of `$\{s_d\}$`
> 3. Compute head scores: `$f_h \leftarrow \|s_{h,\mathcal{D}_{\text{top}}}\|_2 \;\; \forall h$`
> 4. **for** `$g \leftarrow 1$` **to** `$G$` **do**
> 5. &nbsp;&nbsp;&emsp;`$\mathcal{R}_g \leftarrow$` argsort-descending(`$\{f_h \mid h \in \mathcal{G}_g\}$`)
> 6. &nbsp;&nbsp;&emsp;`$\mathcal{R}_g^{\text{sel}} \leftarrow$` first `$k_g$` elements of `$\mathcal{R}_g$`
> 7. **end for**
> 8. `$\mathcal{R} \leftarrow \bigoplus_{g=1}^G \mathcal{R}_g^{\text{sel}}$`

**Choice of `$W_x$` activations for scoring.** The paper experiments with three possible activation sources for Mamba scoring: the outputs of `$W_x$` (Equation 6), `$W_z$` (Equation 5), and `$W_O$` (Equation 14). Table 2 in the paper compares these options across six pruned configurations. `$W_x$`-based scoring consistently produces the best zero-shot LM loss: for configuration {FFN=12288, emb=3072, heads=112, channels=64}, `$W_x$` achieves 3.56 loss versus 4.11 for `$W_z$` and 3.79 for `$W_O$`. The authors hypothesise that `$W_x$` activations most directly capture the SSM's sequence modeling contribution, since `$x$` is the main input to the state space computation, whereas `$W_z$` activations are the gating signal (which modulates but does not drive the SSM) and `$W_O$` activations include post-SSM processing that mixes information across heads.

#### Matrix Trimming After Ranking

Once the head and channel rankings are determined, the weight matrices are physically reshaped (Equation 25):

$$W \leftarrow W[\mathcal{R}], \quad \text{for } W \in \{W_x, W_z, W_O, W_A, W_D, W_{dt}, \text{conv1d}\}$$

For each matrix `$W$` in this set:
- `$W_x$` (Equation 6): rows corresponding to pruned heads are removed. Since `$W_x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times (m_h \times m_d)}$`, and the `$(m_h \times m_d)$` dimension is treated as a flat combined dimension, head pruning removes contiguous blocks of size `$m_d$` from this dimension. Head channel pruning removes individual columns within each block.
- `$W_z$` (Equation 5): identical structure to `$W_x$`, trimmed in the same way.
- `$W_O$` (Equation 14): `$W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{(m_h \times m_d) \times d_e}$`. The pruning operates on the input dimension (the `$(m_h \times m_d)$` dimension), removing blocks corresponding to pruned heads and columns within blocks for pruned channels.
- `$W_{dt}$` (Equation 9): `$W_{dt} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_e \times m_h}$`. Rows corresponding to pruned heads are removed. This matrix produces the per-head `$dt$` values.
- `$W_A$` and `$W_D$`: These are not matrices but per-head scalar parameters (`$A, D \in \mathbb{R}^{m_h}$`). Entries for pruned heads are simply deleted, reducing the vector length from `$m_h$` to the number of kept heads.
- `$\text{conv1d}$`: The 1D convolution kernel operates on the `$(m_h \times m_d)$`-dimensional space (after the projections but before the SSM). Channels corresponding to pruned heads and head channels are removed from the convolution's input and output channel dimensions.

**What is NOT pruned in Mamba layers:** The `$W_B$` and `$W_C$` matrices (Equations 7–8) are **not affected** by head or channel pruning. These matrices have dimensions `$d_e \times (g \times d_s)$`, operating at the group and state-space level rather than the head and channel level. Since the group structure is preserved by the pruning constraints (heads are only pruned within groups, not across groups), the group-level `$B$` and `$C$` projections remain valid. This is consistent with the group-aware constraint: you can reduce the number of heads per group, but you do not change which group a given `$B$` slice maps to.

#### FFN and Embedding Importance Scoring (MINITRON Recipe)

For FFN neurons and embedding channels, the paper follows the established MINITRON approach (Muralidharan et al., 2024) without modification. These components are permutation-equivariant (Equation 15), so no structural constraints are needed.

**FFN neuron scoring (Equation 26):**

$$F(i)_{\text{neuron}} = \sum_{B,L} X(W_1^i)^T$$

where `$W_1^i$` is the `$i$`-th row of the weight matrix `$W_1$` in the first linear projection of the FFN, `$X$` is the input to the FFN layer, and `$\sum_{B,L}$` denotes aggregation over batch and sequence dimensions (mean over sequence, L2 over batch, following MINITRON).

**What this computes:** For each neuron `$i$` in the FFN, take the dot product of the input `$X$` with the neuron's weight vector `$W_1^i$`. This produces the raw pre-activation value for that neuron on each token. Aggregate these values over all tokens in the calibration data. The result is a scalar `$F(i)_{\text{neuron}}$` representing how strongly neuron `$i$` fires on average. Neurons with high scores are retained; neurons with low scores are pruned.

**Why this metric rather than weight magnitude:** Activation-based importance captures not just the weight values but how they interact with the actual data distribution. A neuron with large weights that never fires (because the inputs rarely align with its weight vector) is less important than a neuron with moderate weights that consistently fires. Weight magnitude alone would overestimate the former and underestimate the latter.

**Embedding channel scoring (Equation 27):**

$$F(i)_{\text{emb}} = \sum_{B,L} \text{LN}(X)_i$$

where `$\text{LN}(X)_i$` is the `$i$`-th dimension of the layer-normalized input.

**What this computes:** For each embedding channel `$i$`, take the `$i$`-th component of the layer-normalized input, aggregate over all tokens and layers that use this embedding dimension. The paper notes that embedding channel scores are "computed across all layers that utilize the embedding channel, including FFN, Mamba and Attention projection layers, and LayerNorm components." This means the importance of embedding channel `$i$` reflects its contribution across the entire network, not just at a single layer.

**Why aggregate across all layers:** Embedding channels are shared—every layer's input or output uses the same `$d_e$`-dimensional space. Pruning embedding channel `$i$` removes that dimension from the entire model. The importance score must therefore reflect total contribution, not contribution at any single point. If channel `$i$` is highly active in early layers but dormant in later layers, the aggregate will still be high (since early-layer activity contributes to the sum), which is correct—removing it would affect those early-layer computations.

**Aggregation details:** For both FFN and embedding scoring, the aggregation along `$L$` (sequence) uses mean, and the aggregation along `$B$` (batch) uses L2 norm. This follows MINITRON's convention: mean along sequence normalises for variable-length sequences, while L2 along batch captures both average magnitude and variance as a combined importance signal.

#### FLAP Importance for SSM Layers (Alternative to L2-Based Scoring)

The paper also explores FLAP (Fluctuation-based Adaptive Pruning; An et al., 2024) as an alternative importance metric. FLAP is a retraining-free structured pruning technique originally designed for Transformers. It measures the "recoverability" of a model's output feature map when specific weight columns are removed.

The extension to SSM layers applies FLAP to the output projection matrix `$W_O$` within the Mamba layer. The FLAP score for column `$j$` is:

$$S_j = \|W_j\|^2 \cdot \text{Var}(X_j)$$

where `$\|W_j\|^2$` is the squared L2 norm of column `$j$` of the `$W_O$` weight matrix, and `$\text{Var}(X_j)$` is the sample variance of the `$j$`-th input feature (the activation feeding into `$W_O$`) across calibration samples.

**What this computes:** The FLAP score combines two signals: column norm (how much the weight matrix relies on that input feature) and activation variance (how much that feature varies across samples). High column norm × high variance → the column is important (removing it would cause large fluctuations in the output). Low column norm × low variance → the column is removable with minimal impact.

**Why this formulation:** FLAP directly estimates the expected squared error introduced by removing a column: under a linear approximation, the error is proportional to `$\|W_j\|^2 \cdot \mathbb{E}[(X_j - \mathbb{E}[X_j])^2]$`. By using variance rather than raw activation magnitude, FLAP normalises for mean shifts—a feature that is consistently large but has zero variance across samples can be compensated for by a bias term, whereas a feature with high variance cannot.

**FLAP results (Table 3):** Table 3 compares L2-based importance (the standard MINITRON approach using Equation 20-style aggregation) with FLAP-based importance across different pruning targets. Before knowledge distillation (the "raw pruning" setting), FLAP shows mixed results: for FFN pruning, FLAP produces lower LM loss (1.32 vs. 1.364 for FFN=16384; 1.64 vs. 1.803 for FFN=11568), suggesting better importance estimation for FFN neurons. But for Mamba head pruning, FLAP produces much worse loss (1.73 vs. 1.305 for 112 heads; 4.59 vs. 2.150 for 96 heads). This indicates FLAP's linear approximation breaks down for the SSM's non-linear sequential computation.

**After knowledge distillation,** the difference largely disappears: candidate #1 achieves identical LM loss (1.380) with both L2 and FLAP importance estimation after lightweight KD. The paper concludes that "it doesn't seem to offer any clear advantage" over the simpler L2-based approach. The L2 method is preferred for simplicity and because it doesn't require the additional calibration computation for variance estimation.

#### Depth Pruning via KL Divergence

The paper investigates layer removal (depth pruning) as an alternative to width pruning, using Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) between logit distributions to measure layer importance.

The procedure works as follows: for each layer `$i$` in the 52-layer Nemotron-H 8B model, a copy of the model is made with that single layer removed (bypassed). The KLD between the full model's logit distribution and the layer-removed model's logit distribution is computed on a small calibration set of 256 random samples:

$$D_{\text{KL}}(p_{\text{full}} \| p_{\text{removed}, i})$$

A high KLD means removing layer `$i$` causes a large shift in the output distribution → the layer is important. A low KLD means the layer is redundant (its removal barely changes the output) → it is safe to prune.

**Results (Figure 4):** The layer importance scores reveal several patterns:
- **Important layers cluster at the model's boundaries:** The first few and last few layers have the highest importance scores. This is consistent with findings from Transformer pruning literature (Men et al., 2024) and reflects that early layers process input embeddings and late layers project to the vocabulary—both are critical and non-redundant operations.
- **Attention layers are more important than adjacent Mamba layers, except at the boundaries.** The model has 4 attention layers (since 92% of layers are Mamba2, 8% are attention; with 52 total layers, that's approximately 4 attention layers). Figure 4 shows them as green dotted lines, generally sitting above the red (Mamba2) neighbors but below the blue (FFN) neighbors.
- **The first attention layer is among the least important overall**—a surprising finding that the paper flags explicitly. This suggests the model can function with attention primarily in the middle-to-late layers rather than at the start.
- **A "saw-like" pattern in the middle layers:** FFN (MLP) layers are consistently more important than adjacent Mamba2 layers in the middle of the network (layers 10–40 approximately). This reverses near the model's boundaries, where Mamba2 layers become more important. This pattern suggests redundancy patterns differ between layer types—Mamba2 layers in the model's middle are more substitutable than their FFN neighbors.

**Depth pruning experiments (Figure 5):** The paper experiments with pruning 4, 8, 12, 16, and 26 layers (least important first), followed by distillation with 126B tokens. The results show that:
- **Core knowledge benchmarks are largely unaffected:** HellaSwag, PIQA, ARC Challenge, OpenbookQA, TruthfulQA, and Winogrande maintain 90%+ of the teacher's accuracy even at 26 layers pruned (half the model).
- **Math and coding benchmarks degrade severely:** GSM8K and HumanEval drop sharply as layers are removed. At 26 layers (50% depth), GSM8K accuracy is below 70% of the teacher's, and HumanEval drops below 60%.
- **There is a sharp threshold around 44 layers:** Below 44 remaining layers (removing 8+ layers), the degradation accelerates. This suggests that while individual layers are somewhat redundant, the accumulated redundancy is limited—there is a minimum depth below which the model cannot maintain its reasoning capabilities.

**Why depth pruning was ultimately rejected:** Table 1 compares width-only candidate #1 (52 layers) with depth-pruned candidate #24 (26 layers, 50% depth) and candidate #25 (36 layers). Candidate #24 has the worst LM loss (1.533) despite being the fastest (1.31× relative throughput). Candidate #25, with 36 layers, has approximately 1.4× more parameters than the 4B target but still performs worse (LM loss 1.430) than the worst width-only 4B candidate (#23, LM loss 1.411, with only 64 Mamba heads). The paper concludes that "depth is more critical for accuracy than width for this particular architecture," and the final model uses width-only pruning.

#### Architecture Search: Finding the Best 4B Configuration

The architecture search procedure explores the combinatorial space of pruning targets that produce approximately 4B parameter models. The search space axes are:

| Axis | Range | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Number of layers | 26–52 | Depth pruning |
| Embedding dimension (`$d_e$`) | 3072–4096 | Embedding channel pruning |
| FFN dimension | 9984–21504 | FFN neuron pruning |
| Mamba heads (`$m_h$`) | 64–128 | Head pruning within groups |
| Mamba head channels (`$m_d$`) | 32–64 | Channel pruning across all heads |

The original 8B model has: 52 layers, 4096 embedding, 21504 FFN, 128 Mamba heads, 64 head channels.

**Step 1: Generate candidates.** Over 100 architectures are generated combinatorially within the parameter budget. The paper notes that "we imposed a constraint to generate valid checkpoints with a fixed size of 4 billion (4B) parameters"—this means for each candidate, the pruning targets must sum to approximately 4B total parameters.

**Step 2: Zero-shot evaluation.** All candidates are evaluated by computing LM validation loss on 1024 calibration samples (the same samples used for importance estimation). Loss is computed without any fine-tuning—this is the raw pruned model's performance.

**Step 3: Lightweight KD screening.** The top-22 candidates (lowest zero-shot loss) are selected for lightweight knowledge distillation with 3.8B tokens. The paper argues this step is "critical for getting a reliable ranking of architectural candidates"—zero-shot loss can be misleading because some architectures have high initial loss but recover quickly under training, while others have moderate initial loss but plateau. The 3.8B-token budget is small enough to be tractable (roughly 1% of the full KD budget) but large enough to reveal recovery trajectories.

**Step 4: Final selection.** After lightweight KD, the candidate with the lowest validation loss is selected. If multiple candidates tie on loss, throughput and latency measurements serve as tiebreakers. Table 1 shows candidate #1 and #2 both achieve LM loss 1.380, but #1 is selected because it has higher throughput (due to fewer Mamba heads: 112 vs. 128).

**Why this search procedure over end-to-end training:** Training each candidate from scratch or with full distillation would be prohibitively expensive—380B tokens × 22 candidates would be infeasible. The lightweight KD screening efficiently approximates the full distillation ranking at 1% of the cost. This is analogous to successive halving or Hyperband in hyperparameter optimization: evaluate many candidates cheaply, then progressively allocate more resources to promising ones.

**Why no attention head pruning:** The paper notes that attention layers "amount to only 8% of the total number of layers" in Nemotron-H 8B. With only 4 attention layers out of 52, each with a modest number of heads (likely 32, based on Table 3's baseline of 32 attention heads), pruning attention heads would yield negligible parameter savings for the complexity it adds. The focus is on the components that dominate the parameter count: FFN neurons, embedding channels, and Mamba components.

#### Knowledge Distillation for Accuracy Recovery

After pruning and matrix trimming, the model undergoes extended knowledge distillation to recover accuracy. The paper uses **logit-based distillation with forward KL divergence (FKLD)**, following the MINITRON approach.

The teacher is the original 8B Nemotron-H model. The student is the pruned 4B model. For each training token, both models produce probability distributions over the vocabulary:

$$p(x_i, \tau) = \frac{\exp(x_i / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \exp(x_j / \tau)}$$

where `$x_i$` is the logit for token `$i$`, `$\tau$` is the softmax temperature, and `$|V|$` is the vocabulary size.

The distillation loss is forward KL divergence averaged over all output tokens in the sequence:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{logits}} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{k=1}^{L} \text{FKLD}(p_t^k(x, \tau), p_s^k(x, \tau))$$

where `$p_t^k$` and `$p_s^k$` are the teacher and student probability distributions on the `$k$`-th token, respectively, and `$L$` is the sequence length.

**What FKLD computes:** For each token position, it measures the divergence from the student's distribution to the teacher's distribution: `$\sum_i p_t(i) \log(p_t(i) / p_s(i))$`. This penalises the student when it assigns low probability to tokens the teacher assigns high probability to. The key property of FKLD (as opposed to reverse KL) is that it is "mean-seeking": the student is encouraged to cover all modes of the teacher's distribution, which is desirable for language modeling where the teacher's uncertainty reflects genuine ambiguity (multiple valid completions).

**Why logit-based distillation over hidden-state distillation:** Hidden-state distillation requires matching intermediate representations, which adds implementation complexity and may constrain the student's architecture flexibility. Logit-based distillation operates only on the final output, which is simpler and has been shown effective in prior work (Muralidharan et al., 2024; Hinton et al., 2015).

**Training hyperparameters:**
- **Data:** Random sample from the Phase 3 data mixture used for training Nemotron-H models (Blakeman et al., 2025). 380B tokens total for the final extended KD.
- **Batch size:** 768
- **Sequence length:** 8192
- **Learning rate:** Cosine decay schedule, starting at 1.6e-4 and decaying to 8e-4
- **Warmup:** 60-step linear warmup
- **Loss:** Forward KL divergence exclusively (no auxiliary language modeling loss, no task-specific losses)

The paper uses the FKLD loss **exclusively** during accuracy recovery—there is no standard next-token prediction loss term. This contrasts with some prior work that uses a weighted combination of distillation loss and ground-truth language modeling loss. The authors' choice implies confidence that the teacher's output distribution, even on data the teacher was not trained on, provides sufficient supervisory signal without ground-truth targets.

**Why knowledge distillation rather than standard fine-tuning:** Recent work (Sreenivas et al., 2024; Muralidharan et al., 2024) has demonstrated that distillation outperforms conventional fine-tuning for accuracy recovery after pruning. The teacher's full output distribution provides richer supervision than hard one-hot targets: it conveys information about which tokens are plausible alternatives (the teacher's uncertainty), which helps the student learn nuanced token-level preferences rather than just correct/incorrect classifications. This is particularly important after aggressive pruning (50% parameter reduction), where the student has lost capacity and needs to efficiently re-learn the teacher's knowledge distribution.

---

#### Alignment and Long-Context Extension (Post-Compression)

After the base 4B model is obtained through pruning and distillation, it undergoes alignment to produce an instruction-tuned variant. The alignment procedure uses:

**Supervised Fine-Tuning with Knowledge Distillation (SFT-KD):** The pruned 4B base model is fine-tuned using supervision from the top-k (100) logits of the aligned 8B Nemotron-H teacher. The procedure involves two rounds:
- **Round 1:** Math and coding data
- **Round 2:** Instruction-following and general chat data

The top-k distillation means the student only learns from the 100 most likely tokens according to the teacher, rather than from the full 128K+ vocabulary. This focuses the student's capacity on the most relevant tokens (the "important" part of the distribution) and avoids trying to match the teacher's long-tail token probabilities, which are noisy and less informative.

**Reward-aware Preference Optimization (RPO):** After SFT-KD, two rounds of RPO (Adler et al., 2024) are applied using NeMo-Aligner (Shen et al., 2024) to further align the model with human preferences.

**Long-context extension:** To extend context length from the base 8K to 128K tokens, the aligned model undergoes additional SFT using data designed for long-context understanding. The training data is constructed by "concatenating conversation turns and introducing long-range dependencies by placing related turns far apart within the extended context." The context length is varied randomly between 128K and 512K tokens during training. This teaches the model to attend to distant information—a critical capability that would be lost if long-context data were not explicitly included.

The paper notes that KD for context extension is "future work"—the current approach uses standard fine-tuning for this phase rather than distillation. This is a limitation: the 8B teacher could potentially provide richer supervision for long-context understanding, but the distillation pipeline has not been extended to this setting.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Group-aware head permutation constraint** over naive global sorting: essential for correctness—global sorting silently corrupts the SSM forward pass by mixing `$B_t x_t$` broadcast patterns across groups.
- **Nested channel-then-head scoring** over independent scoring: avoids inflated head importance from channels destined for pruning; evaluates heads based on their contribution through surviving channels.
- **`$W_x$` activation source for Mamba scoring** over `$W_z$` or `$W_O$`: empirically best in Table 2, likely because `$x$` is the main SSM input, directly capturing sequence modeling contribution.
- **Width-only pruning** over depth pruning or depth-width hybrid: Nemotron-H's already-compact 52-layer architecture (fewer than comparable models like Phi-4-4B with 64 layers) makes depth more critical; width pruning is less accuracy-sensitive and produces better accuracy-speed tradeoffs (Table 1).
- **Mamba head pruning** over Mamba head channel pruning: Figure 7 shows head pruning consistently yields lower LM loss, lower latency, and higher throughput; head channels are more accuracy-sensitive when pruned.
- **Forward KL divergence** over reverse KL or MSE: mean-seeking property ensures the student covers all modes of the teacher's distribution, critical for language modeling where multiple tokens are plausible.
- **Lightweight KD screening (3.8B tokens)** over zero-shot-only selection: zero-shot loss is a noisy proxy for final distilled performance; the cheap KD step provides a more reliable architecture ranking at low cost.
- **L2-based importance (MINITRON recipe)** over FLAP: simpler implementation, does not require variance estimation, and yields equivalent post-distillation performance (Table 3: identical 1.380 LM loss for candidate #1 after lightweight KD).
- **Top-k logit distillation for alignment** over full-vocabulary distillation: focuses student capacity on the most relevant 100 tokens per position, avoiding noise from long-tail token probabilities.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Structural-Coupling Diagnostic — Why SSM Pruning Fundamentally Differs from Transformer Pruning

The paper's most significant conceptual contribution is not a new pruning algorithm but a **diagnostic**: it identifies *why* existing Transformer compression recipes fail when applied to Mamba layers, and it names the root cause as **permutation non-equivariance arising from group-structured broadcast operations**. This is a conceptual move from "SSMs are different" (vague intuition) to "SSMs violate the permutation-equivariance assumption that all prior structured pruning implicitly relies on" (precise, actionable diagnosis).

Why this matters at the idea level: The entire structured-pruning playbook for Transformers—sort attention heads by importance, trim the lowest-ranked, reshape weight matrices accordingly—rests on an unstated assumption that components can be reordered arbitrarily without altering the network's function. This assumption is so baked into the practice that it is rarely articulated. The paper makes it explicit (Equation 15) and then demonstrates its failure mode for Mamba blocks (Equation 16, Figure 3). The `$B_t x_t$` broadcast that couples heads within groups is not an implementation detail; it is a **structural integrity constraint** that, if violated, produces a corruption that no post-hoc correction can recover.

This reframes the problem from "how do we adapt importance scoring for SSMs?" to "how do we encode the computational dependencies of Mamba's forward pass as hard constraints on the ranking procedure?" The solution—within-group-only head permutations and shared global channel ranking—emerges directly from this reframing. It is an elegant, minimal-constraint response: heads can still be freely sorted and pruned, but only within the boundaries that the broadcast operation imposes.

**Comparison to prior work:** Before this paper, Mamba-Shredder (Muñoz et al., 2025) sidestepped the issue entirely by removing the SSM module wholesale—it treated the Mamba layer as a black box to eliminate, not a structure to prune within. Ghattas et al. (2025) performed head pruning and merging but did not identify or enforce group-level permutation constraints, implying either (a) their approach silently corrupted the broadcast pattern, (b) their architecture had a different group structure that was fortuitously non-violated, or (c) the degradation was present but masked by other factors. The current paper is the first to explicitly name the group-structure coupling and derive the necessary constraint, turning what prior work might have dismissed as an accuracy drop from "SSMs being sensitive to pruning" into a diagnosed, solvable mechanical issue.

This is a **fundamental contribution** rather than an incremental refinement because it identifies a constraint that applies to *any* future pruning method for group-structured SSM architectures, not just this paper's specific L2-based importance scoring. If someone develops a better importance metric, they still must respect Equation 17.

---

### Innovation 2: A Complete Empirical Taxonomy of Pruning Sensitivity for Hybrid Architectures

The paper's second distinctive contribution is a **systematic empirical characterization** of how different pruning axes—Mamba heads, Mamba head channels, FFN neurons, embedding channels, and depth—trade off against accuracy, throughput, and latency. This is not a reporting of numbers; it is the construction of a **decision framework** that tells practitioners where to allocate their pruning budget based on their primary constraint.

The taxonomy reveals three clear patterns, each with practical implications:

- **Mamba head pruning is the most efficiency-favorable axis**: reducing `$m_h$` improves throughput and reduces latency *while incurring the least accuracy penalty* among all prunable components (Figure 7, Figure 6). This is counterintuitive—one might expect the SSM's sequence-modeling component to be accuracy-critical, but the empirical evidence shows that Mamba layers have substantial head redundancy (128 heads in the original model). This finding directly contradicts the default assumption from Transformer pruning, where attention heads are often left unpruned because they are considered sensitive.

- **Mamba head channel pruning is strictly worse than head pruning on every metric**: Figure 7 shows that pruning `$m_d$` produces higher LM loss, higher latency, and lower throughput compared to equivalent head-count reduction. The paper does not speculate heavily on why, but the channel dimension is likely more tightly coupled to the convolution kernel and the SSM's internal state dynamics, making it less redundant.

- **Depth pruning is the most accuracy-sensitive but most speed-favorable axis**: Table 1 and Figure 5 show that removing layers yields the highest throughput gains (1.31× for 50% depth reduction) but severely degrades math and coding performance. This creates a clear guideline: if your workload is knowledge-retrieval or commonsense reasoning, depth pruning is viable; if it involves math or code, avoid it.

- **The interaction between pruning axes exhibits clear tradeoffs**: Figure 6's correlation matrix shows that Mamba parameters are positively correlated with latency and negatively correlated with throughput and LM loss. FFN and embedding parameters show the opposite pattern. This means *increasing Mamba heads improves accuracy but hurts speed; increasing FFN/embedding dimensions improves accuracy and also improves speed*. These are opposite-signed correlations for the same accuracy metric, which is a non-obvious finding—it means the optimal allocation within a fixed parameter budget depends on whether your primary constraint is accuracy, latency, or throughput.

**Comparison to prior work:** MINITRON (Muralidharan et al., 2024) provided sensitivity analysis for Transformer components (attention heads, FFN neurons, layers), but that analysis does not transfer because the structural role of Mamba heads is fundamentally different from attention heads. Ghattas et al. (2025) pruned Mamba-only components but did not compare sensitivity across SSM, FFN, and embedding axes simultaneously. The current paper's correlation analysis (Figure 6) across 125 4B-parameter variants is the first holistic sensitivity map for hybrid architectures.

This is an **incremental but practically essential** contribution. The taxonomy itself does not advance pruning theory, but it converts the black-box question "how should I compress my hybrid model?" into a set of actionable, evidence-backed design rules that were previously unavailable. The 125-model architecture sweep that enables this analysis is expensive, and the paper's willingness to publish the full correlation results makes this a reusable resource for the community.

---

### Innovation 3: The Rejection of Depth Pruning as a Credible Alternative for Reasoning-Capable Hybrid Models

One of the paper's most informative contributions is a **negative result with sharp boundary conditions**: depth pruning of the Nemotron-H architecture below 44 layers (from 52) causes disproportionate degradation on math and coding benchmarks, even though knowledge and commonsense benchmarks remain largely intact (Figure 5). The paper does not simply report that depth pruning is worse; it characterizes *where* it fails and *why* that matters.

The finding changes the practitioner's default stance. In Transformer-only compression, depth pruning is a viable and often-preferred technique—MINITRON, Sheared LLaMA (Xia et al., 2023), and ShortGPT (Men et al., 2024) all demonstrate effective layer removal. A practitioner naively applying the Transformer playbook to Nemotron-H might start by pruning the 8 least-important layers (roughly 15% depth reduction) and expect moderate accuracy loss. This paper shows that such a strategy would silently destroy the model's reasoning capabilities while leaving benchmark averages deceptively high—the overall accuracy score would mask the catastrophic degradation on the tasks that matter most for many deployments (math, code).

**Why this finding is significant beyond the specific architecture:** The paper identifies a plausible mechanism: Nemotron-H's 52-layer architecture is already compact relative to pure Transformers of similar capability (Phi-4-4B uses 64 layers). The Mamba-Transformer hybridization may concentrate functionality into fewer layers, making each layer less redundant. If this is general—if hybrid architectures systematically have lower layer redundancy than pure Transformers—then depth pruning becomes fundamentally less viable as architectures shift toward hybrid designs. This is a **boundary condition** on the applicability of prior depth-pruning results, not just a model-specific observation.

**Comparison to prior work:** MINITRON showed that depth pruning works for Transformer-only models; this paper shows it does *not* work well for this hybrid architecture when reasoning capability must be preserved. The juxtaposition is instructive: it prevents overgeneralization of the MINITRON depth-pruning results to architectures where the underlying assumptions (substantial layer redundancy) may not hold.

This is an **incremental but high-signal** contribution. The empirical result is straightforward, but its implication—that a widely-used compression technique has sharply diminishing returns on a growing class of architectures—is practically important.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All pruning and evaluation experiments use the Nemotron-H 8B model as the starting point, with importance estimation performed on 1024 randomly sampled calibration samples at sequence length 8192, drawn from the Phase 3 data mixture employed for training Nemotron-H models (Blakeman et al., 2025). The final evaluation uses a broad suite of 16 benchmarks spanning knowledge (MMLU), math (GSM8K, MATH-500), coding (HumanEval, HumanEval+, MBPP, MBPP+), commonsense reasoning (ARC Challenge, ARC Easy, CommonsenseQA, HellaSwag, OpenbookQA, PIQA, Social IQA, Winogrande), reading comprehension (RACE), truthfulness (TruthfulQA MC2), instruction following (IFEval, MT-Bench), tool use (BFCL v2), and long-context understanding (RULER at 16K–128K context lengths). The specific metrics vary by benchmark: accuracy for knowledge/math/commonsense tasks, pass@1 for coding, GPT-4-Turbo judged scores for MT-Bench, and average RULER scores for long-context.

- **Base model(s).** The teacher model is Nemotron-H 8B (Blakeman et al., 2025), a hybrid Mamba2-Transformer architecture with 52 layers, embedding dimension 4096, FFN dimension 21504, 128 Mamba heads, 64 Mamba head channels, and approximately 8 billion parameters. The model replaces 92% of its attention layers with Mamba2 blocks while retaining 8% as standard self-attention layers. The compressed student model is Nemotron-H 4B, produced by width-only pruning of the 8B teacher using the proposed group-aware method. The paper also experiments on the standalone Mamba2 1.3B model (Dao and Gu, 2024) compressed to 780M parameters to test generalizability. For the FLOPs-matched comparison equivalent (the training cost analysis in Table 4), comparably sized community models include Llama-3.2 3B, Falcon-3 3B, Zamba-2 2.7B, and Qwen-2.5 3B as baselines for the base model, and Phi-4-Mini 4B, Qwen-2.5 3B-Instruct, Llama-3.2 3B-Instruct, Falcon-3 3B-Instruct, and Zamba-2 2.7B-Instruct for the instruction-tuned variant. The paper justifies its choice by noting Nemotron-H's representativeness of contemporary hybrid architectures while providing a meaningful compression target at the 8B-to-4B scale.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric during development is **LM validation loss** (zero-shot and after lightweight KD) on the 1024-sample calibration set, as shown in Tables 1, 2, and 3. The final evaluation uses **benchmark-specific accuracy scores** across the 16-task suite (Tables 4–8), with the paper reporting both individual benchmark results and averages. For efficiency, **relative throughput** (normalized to specific baselines—Table 1 normalizes to configuration #1; Figures 1 and 8 normalize to the maximum observed throughput) and **relative latency** (normalized to the minimum observed latency) are measured at input sequence length 65536 and output sequence length 1024 with batch size 4. The throughput measurement protocol is described in Figure 1's caption: "inference throughput is measured at an input and output sequence length of 65536 and 1024, respectively." The base model evaluation uses standard few-shot prompting (0-shot for most benchmarks, 5-shot for MMLU, 8-shot for GSM8K, 3-shot for MBPP) as specified in Table 4. The instruction-tuned evaluation uses 0-shot generative format for MMLU and 0-shot for all coding and math tasks (Table 5). Safety is evaluated using Garak and AEGIS scores (Table 7).

- **Baselines.** The paper employs several categories of baselines. **(1) The uncompressed teacher:** Nemotron-H 8B (both base and instruct variants) serves as the upper-bound reference; the paper reports that the 4B model "retains over 96% of the original 8B model's accuracy" (Section "Obtaining the Best Compressed Hybrid Model"). **(2) Similarly-sized community models trained from scratch:** For the base model (Table 4), these are Llama-3.2 3B (9T tokens), Falcon-3 3B (0.1T tokens, though this appears to be an error or a specific subset of their full training), Zamba-2 2.7B (3T tokens), and Qwen-2.5 3B (18T tokens). For the instruction-tuned model (Table 5), these are Phi-4-Mini 4B-Instruct (128K context), Qwen-2.5 3B-Instruct (32K context), Llama-3.2 3B-Instruct (128K context), Falcon-3 3B-Instruct (32K context), and Zamba-2 2.7B-Instruct (4K context). **(3) Alternative pruning configurations:** Within the architecture search (Table 1), depth-pruned variants (#24 at 26 layers, #25 at 36 layers) serve as baselines against width-only pruning. **(4) Alternative Mamba importance metrics:** FLAP-based importance estimation (Table 3) is compared against the L2-based MINITRON approach, and different activation sources (W_x, W_z, W_O) for Mamba scoring are compared in Table 2. **(5) The standalone Mamba2 compression experiment** (Table 8) uses Mamba2 780M and Mamba2 1.3B trained from scratch on 300B tokens as baselines.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The central efficiency metric is **training tokens consumed** (Table 4, Table 8). The paper claims the compressed model requires "up to ~40× fewer training tokens" than comparable models trained from scratch: Nemotron-H 4B uses 380B tokens for KD (plus 3.8B for lightweight screening, totaling ~384B), compared to 9T for Llama-3.2 3B, 3T for Zamba-2 2.7B, and 18T for Qwen-2.5 3B. This is not a generation budget for inference but a training budget comparison; specifically, the claim is that compression + distillation is far more data-efficient than training from scratch. The paper also reports inference-time efficiency: throughput and latency measurements (Figures 1, 6, 7, 8; Table 1) at fixed input/output sequence lengths. For the FLAP comparison (Table 3), no distillation tokens are used—the metric is zero-shot LM loss on the raw pruned models. The Mamba2 generalizability experiment (Table 8) uses 10.5B tokens for KD on the compressed model versus 300B for the from-scratch baselines.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ formal cross-validation or statistical significance testing. The architecture search uses a held-out calibration set of 1024 samples for importance estimation and zero-shot LM loss computation; the lightweight KD screening (3.8B tokens) is performed on the top-22 candidates, with the final candidate selected by validation loss and ties broken by throughput/latency. The final model undergoes extended KD (380B tokens) and is then evaluated on the full benchmark suite. There is no mention of multiple random seeds, confidence intervals, or statistical testing for the benchmark comparisons. The difficulty estimation approach from the prior sections (2048 samples per question with two-fold cross-validation for compute-optimal strategy selection) is not used here—this paper's architecture search is a simpler best-of-N selection from a combinatorial candidate pool with a single train-validation split.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper's results span three logical groupings: **(1) ablation-driven architecture search demonstrating width-only pruning dominates depth pruning and establishing the sensitivity taxonomy**, **(2) final base and instruction-tuned model performance against similarly-sized community models**, and **(3) generalizability results on standalone Mamba2 compression**. The central empirical claim is that width-pruned hybrid models can advance the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier, and the evidence is organized hierarchically: first establish which pruning axes matter (Tables 1–3, Figures 4–7), then demonstrate the final model's competitive position (Tables 4–7, Figures 1, 8), then show the approach transfers to other architectures (Table 8).

#### Architecture Search: Width-Only vs. Depth Pruning and Axis Sensitivity

**Headline result from the architecture search (Table 1):** Among 125 candidate 4B-parameter configurations generated by combinatorial pruning, the width-only candidate with the lowest LM validation loss after lightweight KD is configuration #1 (52 layers, embedding dim 3072, FFN 12288, 112 Mamba heads, 64 head channels), achieving LM loss 1.380 with relative throughput 1.0× (serving as the baseline). Configuration #2 achieves an identical loss (1.380) with 128 Mamba heads and FFN 10752 but lower throughput (0.98×), demonstrating that within a fixed parameter budget, reducing Mamba heads while increasing FFN size yields better throughput at equivalent accuracy. The selected final model is configuration #1, chosen over #2 purely on throughput—the tiebreaker the paper describes for architecturally equivalent candidates.

**Depth-pruned configurations are strictly worse:** Configuration #24 (26 layers, 50% depth, width dimensions scaled up to hit 4B parameters: embedding 4096, FFN 21504, 128 Mamba heads, 64 channels) achieves the worst LM loss of all candidates (1.533) despite having the highest relative throughput (1.31×). Configuration #25 (36 layers, ~1.4× more parameters than 4B) still shows worse loss (1.430) than the worst-performing width-only 4B candidate #23 (1.411, with only 64 Mamba heads). The paper makes an explicit comparative claim: "a depth-pruned model with 36 layers (#25), despite having ~1.4× more parameters performs worse than the least accurate width-only pruned 4B candidate (#23)."

**Sensitivity ranking across pruning axes (Table 1, sorted by LM loss):** The loss ordering from best to worst reveals the accuracy sensitivity hierarchy:
- Best: width-only with moderate Mamba head reduction (#1: 112 heads, LM loss 1.380)
- Next best: width-only with head channel reduction (#4: 112 heads, 60 channels, LM loss 1.388)
- Moderate: width-only with more aggressive head or channel pruning (#5–#23, LM loss 1.388–1.411)
- Poor: depth-only or depth-width hybrid (#24: 26 layers, LM loss 1.533; #25: 36 layers, LM loss 1.430)

**The forbidden configuration:** Configuration #23, with only 64 Mamba heads (50% of original 128), has the worst width-only loss (1.411) and represents the lower bound of acceptable Mamba head pruning—below this, accuracy degrades unacceptably for the 4B parameter budget. This establishes an empirical pruning limit of approximately 88% head retention (112/128) for near-optimal accuracy and roughly 50% retention as the viability floor.

#### Correlation Analysis Across 125 4B Candidates (Figure 6)

**Headline finding on parameter-metric correlations:** Figure 6 (left) quantifies the relationship between model component sizes and performance metrics across all 125 4B-parameter variants at fixed depth (52 layers). The correlation coefficients reveal the paper's central tradeoff claim:
- **Mamba parameters (heads and head channels) positively correlate with latency (0.48) and negatively correlate with throughput (-0.57)**: more Mamba heads make the model slower but do not improve accuracy—the correlation with LM loss is **positive (0.55)**, meaning more Mamba heads actually *increase* loss (worsen accuracy) within the fixed 4B budget. This is the critical evidence for the claim that Mamba heads are the most efficiency-favorable pruning target.
- **FFN parameters negatively correlate with latency (-0.41) and positively correlate with throughput (0.25)**: larger FFN dimensions make the model faster, because FFN computation is more hardware-efficient than SSM computation. The correlation with LM loss is **negative (-0.59)**: larger FFN improves accuracy.
- **Embedding dimension (d_e) shows similar patterns to FFN**: negative correlation with LM loss (-0.86) and latency (-0.59), positive correlation with throughput (0.55). Larger embedding dimensions strongly improve accuracy and also improve speed.

**What these sign patterns mean for budget allocation:** Within a fixed 4B parameter constraint, the correlations are negative between components (Figure 6, right): FFN and Mamba parameters correlate at -0.60, meaning you can't have both large FFN and many Mamba heads simultaneously—this is the zero-sum nature of the fixed budget. The optimal strategy is to *reduce* Mamba heads (which hurts accuracy little but improves speed) and *increase* FFN and embedding dimensions (which improve both accuracy and speed). This is precisely what configuration #1 does relative to the 8B teacher: Mamba heads reduced from 128 to 112, FFN reduced from 21504 to 12288 (forced by budget, but kept as high as possible), embedding reduced from 4096 to 3072 (the largest reduction, suggesting embedding is the least accuracy-sensitive dimension when FFN is preserved).

#### Layer Importance via KL Divergence (Figure 4)

**Headline finding:** The per-layer importance measured by KLD between full-model and layer-removed logits (averaged over 256 samples) reveals that "the most important layers are concentrated at the model's start and end" and that "the first attention layer is among the least important, while other attention layers are more critical than neighboring layers." A "saw-like" pattern emerges where FFN layers are more important than adjacent Mamba2 layers in the middle of the network (layers ~10–40), but this reverses at the boundaries. The paper does not report numerical KLD values for individual layers—Figure 4 is a visual plot with error bars—but the qualitative pattern is the basis for the subsequent depth-pruning experiments.

**The saw-tooth pattern interpretation:** The paper does not deeply analyze the saw-tooth pattern, but it is an interesting structural finding: in the model's middle section, the FFN layers appear more "irreplaceable" than adjacent Mamba2 layers. This could reflect a division of labor where Mamba2 layers primarily handle sequential context processing (which has redundancy across layers) while FFN layers store more specific knowledge (making each one harder to remove). The reversal near boundaries suggests the model's early and late Mamba2 layers play unique roles in input/output processing that are not replicated by other layers.

#### Depth Pruning Experiments (Figure 5)

**Headline result:** Depth pruning of the Nemotron-H 8B model to 48, 44, 40, 36, and 26 layers, followed by distillation with 126B tokens each, shows that **core knowledge benchmarks (HellaSwag, PIQA, ARC, OpenbookQA, TruthfulQA, Winogrande) remain largely intact even at 50% depth reduction, while math (GSM8K) and coding (HumanEval, MBPP+) benchmarks show substantial degradation below 44 layers.** The paper does not report exact accuracy percentages for Figure 5—values are presented relative to the teacher's accuracy on a normalized scale—but states that "below 44 layers, we observe significant accuracy drops on math and coding benchmarks" and that at 26 layers, the degradation becomes severe.

**Quantitative claims from the text:** The paper reports that "core-knowledge benchmarks remained largely unaffected" across all depth-pruned variants. The degradation threshold around 44 layers (removing 8 layers, ~15% depth reduction) defines the limit of safe depth pruning for reasoning tasks within this architecture. This is a substantially tighter constraint than what MINITRON or ShortGPT found for pure Transformers, where 20–30% depth reduction is often viable.

**The 44-layer checkpoint as a candidate:** The paper then applies width pruning to both the 44-layer and 48-layer variants to produce ~4B-sized models. However, "the best depth-width pruned candidate (#7, 44 layers) still under-performs the width-only model (#1)" with LM loss 1.393 vs. 1.380 (Table 1). This confirms that even when depth pruning stays within the "safe" region, width-only pruning remains preferable for overall accuracy.

#### Mamba Head vs. Head Channel Pruning in Isolation (Figure 7)

**Headline result:** When Mamba heads (m_h) and Mamba head channels (m_d) are pruned in isolation with the rest of the network held constant (Nemotron-H 8B architecture, no other pruning), reducing m_h consistently outperforms reducing m_d across all three metrics: LM loss, relative throughput, and relative latency. The paper states explicitly that "pruning Mamba heads (m_h) consistently outperforms pruning Mamba head channels (m_d) across all metrics."

**Quantitative trends from Figure 7:** The plot shows performance across a range of pruning percentages (0–50% on the x-axis, corresponding to reducing the dimension from its original value). As pruning percentage increases:
- **LM loss (y-axis 1):** The m_d pruning curve rises more steeply than the m_h curve, indicating greater accuracy degradation per unit of dimension reduction. At 50% pruning, the m_d LM loss exceeds 8.0 while the m_h LM loss is approximately 3.0.
- **Relative throughput (y-axis 2):** The m_h curve rises more quickly, reaching approximately 1.12× at 50% pruning, while m_d reaches only approximately 1.05×.
- **Relative latency (y-axis 3):** The m_h curve descends more quickly (lower is better), reaching approximately 0.90× at 50% pruning versus approximately 0.95× for m_d.

**Interpretation:** Mamba heads are both less accuracy-sensitive and more speed-favorable as a pruning target compared to head channels. This establishes m_h as "a particularly impactful and practical target for pruning" (the paper's phrasing) and justifies why the final model prunes heads (128 → 112) while leaving channels at their full value (64).

#### Mamba Scoring Source Ablation (Table 2)

**Headline result:** Using activations from the W_x projection output for Mamba head and channel importance scoring produces the best zero-shot LM loss across 6 different pruned configurations, compared to using W_z or W_O activations. For the configuration matching the final selected architecture (FFN 12288, emb 3072, 112 heads, 64 channels), W_x-based scoring achieves LM loss 3.56 versus 4.11 for W_z and 3.79 for W_O. This advantage is consistent: across 5 of 6 configurations, W_x activations yield the lowest loss.

**Magnitude of the difference:** The advantage of W_x over alternatives varies substantially by configuration. For {FFN 13056, emb 3072, 96 heads, 64 channels}, W_x achieves 4.49 while W_z achieves 5.39 and W_O achieves 4.49 (tie with W_x). For {FFN 14592, emb 3072, 96 heads, 56 channels}, W_x achieves 4.68 while W_z achieves 7.09 and W_O achieves 10.01—a massive gap where W_z and W_O produce near-random performance. This suggests that W_z and W_O activations become particularly unreliable for importance scoring under aggressive pruning, while W_x remains relatively robust.

**Why this matters:** The choice of activation source is a one-time design decision that affects all downstream pruning decisions. A practitioner choosing W_z or W_O would systematically mis-rank Mamba heads and channels, producing suboptimal architectures. This ablation justifies the paper's architectural choice in the importance scoring module.

#### FLAP vs. L2 Importance Estimation (Table 3)

**Headline result:** FLAP-based importance estimation shows mixed results before knowledge distillation but offers no clear advantage after KD. Before KD, FLAP outperforms L2 for FFN pruning (LM loss 1.32 vs. 1.364 for FFN=16384; 1.64 vs. 1.803 for FFN=11568) but substantially underperforms for Mamba head pruning (1.73 vs. 1.305 for 112 heads; 4.59 vs. 2.150 for 96 heads). After lightweight KD on the mixed-pruning candidate #1, both methods converge to identical LM loss (1.380).

**Interpretation of the asymmetry:** FLAP's advantage for FFN pruning and disadvantage for Mamba pruning is telling. FLAP measures "recoverability" using a linear approximation of output perturbation from column removal. For FFN layers, which are feed-forward linear projections with non-linear activation, this linear approximation is reasonably accurate—FFN neurons operate independently, and removing a low-variance neuron produces a predictable output perturbation. For Mamba layers, the SSM's sequential state dynamics introduce non-linear temporal dependencies that a static linear approximation cannot capture—a head with low-variance activations at the output projection might still be critical for the state-space dynamics upstream. The L2-based metric, being purely data-driven (it measures what the model actually computes on calibration data rather than an approximation of perturbation), captures these dynamics implicitly.

**The convergence after KD is the critical finding:** The paper concludes FLAP "doesn't seem to offer any clear advantage." Given the implementation simplicity of L2-based scoring (no variance estimation, no separate calibration passes for variance), this justifies the choice to stick with the MINITRON recipe. This is a practically important negative result: a more sophisticated importance metric does not improve the final distilled model's quality despite showing apparent advantages in the pre-distillation regime.

#### Final Base and Instruction-Tuned Model Performance

**Base model results (Table 4):** Nemotron-H 4B Base achieves an average accuracy of 64.5 across 16 benchmarks, compared to 66.7 for the 8B teacher (retaining 96.7% of teacher accuracy). Against similarly sized models trained from scratch:
- **vs. Qwen-2.5 3B (18T tokens):** +4.2 percentage points (64.5 vs. 60.3) despite using ~47× fewer training tokens
- **vs. Llama-3.2 3B (9T tokens):** +13.4 points (64.5 vs. 51.1) despite using ~24× fewer training tokens
- **vs. Zamba-2 2.7B (3T tokens, hybrid architecture trained from scratch):** +9.3 points (64.5 vs. 55.2)
- **vs. Falcon-3 3B (training tokens unclear from table):** +9.8 points (64.5 vs. 54.7)

**Standout benchmark performances:** HumanEval pass@1 is 59.8 (vs. 57.3 teacher), meaning the compressed model actually **surpasses** the teacher on this specific benchmark in the base configuration—an unusual and notable result. HumanEval+ pass@1 is 55.5 (vs. 53.7 teacher). MMLU is 68.1 (vs. 72.7 teacher, retaining 93.7%). GSM8K is 69.6 (vs. 77.9 teacher, retaining 89.3%—the largest relative drop among major benchmarks, consistent with the paper's finding that math is pruning-sensitive). The paper's headline claim of "over 96% average accuracy retention" averages across all benchmarks, masking that math retention (~89% on GSM8K) is substantially lower than knowledge retention (~94% on MMLU, 97% on HellaSwag).

**Instruction-tuned results (Table 5):** Nemotron-H 4B Instruct achieves:
- MMLU (0-shot generative): 66.96 (vs. 68.7 teacher, 97.5% retention)
- GSM8K (0-shot): 88.93 (vs. 90.4 teacher, 98.4% retention)
- MATH-500 (0-shot): 76.4 (vs. 77.6 teacher, 98.5% retention)
- HumanEval (0-shot): 76.2 (vs. 79.3 teacher, 96.1% retention)
- IFEval Strict: 76.24 (vs. 78.6 teacher, 97.0% retention)
- MT-Bench (GPT-4-Turbo judge): 7.90 (vs. 7.90 teacher, 100% retention)

Against the strongest instruction-tuned baseline, Phi-4-Mini 4B-Instruct: Nemotron-H 4B Instruct leads by 2.6 average accuracy points (the paper's headline "2.6% higher accuracy" in Figure 1, left panel, though this is described as "average accuracy" on an aligned model). Specific advantages include MATH-500 (76.4 vs. 70.8, +5.6 points), MMLU (66.96 vs. 61.88, +5.08 points), GSM8K (88.93 vs. 87.71, +1.22 points), MBPP (78.6 vs. 67.46, +11.14 points), and BFCL v2 (65.88 vs. 61.64, +4.24 points). Phi-4-Mini is competitive on HumanEval+ (64.63 vs. 70.85, where Nemotron-H leads by 6.22 points) but trails on most metrics.

**Safety benchmarks (Table 7):** The 4B model achieves Garak score 67.77 (vs. 70.75 for 8B teacher, retaining 95.8%) and AEGIS score 98.17 (vs. 99.83 for 8B teacher, retaining 98.3%). The paper includes this to demonstrate that pruning does not disproportionately degrade safety alignment, though the Garak drop is noticeably larger than the AEGIS drop.

#### Throughput and Latency Comparisons

**Figure 8 (Latency vs. Throughput frontier):** At batch size 4, input length 65536, output length 1024:
- Nemotron-H 4B achieves the highest throughput and lowest latency among all four compared models
- Relative to Phi-4-Mini-4B (the second-best), Nemotron-H 4B shows approximately 2.2× higher throughput (from Figure 1 left, "2.2× faster") and approximately 1.8× lower latency (approximately 0.55 relative latency vs. approximately 1.0 for Phi-4-Mini, reading from Figure 8)

**Figure 1 (left, Accuracy vs. Throughput):** The aligned Nemotron-H 4B achieves both higher accuracy (~72% average) and higher relative throughput (~2.2× vs. normalized baseline at 1.0) compared to Phi-4-Mini-4B at approximately 1.0× relative throughput and ~62% average accuracy (reading from the figure—exact values are visually estimated from the scatter plot). Qwen-2.5-3B-Instruct falls behind both on both axes.

**Table 1 throughput comparisons:** Within the architecture search candidates, depth-only pruning (#24, 26 layers) achieves the highest relative throughput (1.31×) but at the cost of worst accuracy (LM loss 1.533). The width-only candidates cluster between 0.98× and 1.12× relative throughput. The selected candidate (#1) serves as the 1.0× baseline, meaning all throughput comparisons in Table 1 are relative to this configuration.

**Figure 1 (right, Accuracy vs. Training Cost):** Nemotron-H 4B Base achieves approximately 65% average accuracy at ~0.38T tokens (380B), while Llama-3.2 3B achieves approximately 51% at 9T tokens, Qwen-2.5 3B achieves approximately 60% at 18T tokens, Zamba-2 2.7B achieves approximately 55% at 3T tokens, and Falcon-3 3B achieves approximately 55% at some training budget (visually estimated). This plot illustrates the paper's claim of advancing the "accuracy vs. training cost" Pareto frontier: for a given accuracy, the compressed model requires orders of magnitude fewer training tokens.

#### Long-Context Performance (Table 6)

**Headline result:** Nemotron-H 4B Instruct achieves the highest RULER benchmark scores across all context lengths up to 128K tokens among similarly-sized instruction-tuned models, despite being the only model in the comparison not explicitly optimized for long-context in its base training.

At context length 16,384: Nemotron-H 4B achieves 86.28 (vs. 91.5 for the 8B teacher, 83.64 for Qwen-2.5 3B, 77.92 for Llama-3.2 3B, 34.39 for Phi-4-Mini). At 131,072: Nemotron-H 4B achieves 63.57 (vs. 81.7 teacher, 59.26 for Llama-3.2 3B, 23.61 for Qwen-2.5 3B, 20.07 for Phi-4-Mini).

**The drop-off pattern:** Nemotron-H 4B degrades from 86.28 at 16K to 75.95 at 64K to 63.57 at 128K. This is a gradual degradation of approximately 26% from 16K to 128K, which is substantially better than Phi-4-Mini (42% drop from 16K to 128K) and Qwen-2.5 (72% drop). The 8B teacher degrades only 11% over the same range, suggesting that while the 4B model preserves strong long-context capabilities, the compression does reduce the model's ability to maintain coherence at extreme lengths—consistent with the reduced embedding dimension and FFN capacity.

#### Generalizability to Mamba2 (Table 8)

**Headline result:** The compressed Mamba2 780M model, trained on only 10.5B tokens via SSM and embedding pruning followed by KD, achieves an average benchmark score of 44.7 across 10 tasks, outperforming the Mamba2 780M model trained from scratch on 300B tokens (average 42.5) and matching the Mamba2 1.3B model trained from scratch on 300B tokens (average 44.9).

**Specific benchmark comparisons:**
- **vs. Mamba2 780M from scratch:** The compressed model leads on ARC Challenge (34.2 vs. 28.6), ARC Easy (60.4 vs. 54.7), CommonsenseQA (26.4 vs. 19.6), RACE (32.5 vs. 21.8), and TruthfulQA MC2 (38.9 vs. 38.1). It trails on HellaSwag (50.4 vs. 54.7), OpenbookQA (34.2 vs. 36.4), PIQA (71.3 vs. 72.1), Social IQA (41.1 vs. 41.0—essentially tied), and Winogrande (57.7 vs. 58.0).
- **vs. Mamba2 1.3B from scratch:** The compressed 780M achieves a comparable average score (44.7 vs. 44.9) despite having 40% fewer parameters and using 28.6× fewer training tokens. The 1.3B model leads on HellaSwag (59.9 vs. 50.4), PIQA (73.5 vs. 71.3), and OpenbookQA (37.0 vs. 34.2), while the compressed 780M leads on CommonsenseQA (26.4 vs. 20.9), RACE (32.5 vs. 24.8), and TruthfulQA MC2 (38.9 vs. 36.1).

**What this demonstrates:** The compression approach generalizes to pure Mamba2 architectures (the 1.3B is not a hybrid—it is Mamba2-only), showing that the group-aware pruning and KD recipe works beyond the specific Nemotron-H hybrid setting. The 28.6× reduction in training tokens (300B → 10.5B) while preserving accuracy is a strong signal that the approach captures a general compression-transfer relationship.

**Caveat:** The Mamba2 experiment prunes SSM components and embedding dimensions but does not prune FFN neurons or layers (the base Mamba2 1.3B model likely has a different architectural structure than the hybrid Nemotron-H). The paper does not specify exactly which axes were pruned except "via SSM and embedding pruning."

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Mamba head vs. head channel pruning in isolation (Figure 7):** Pruning m_h (Mamba heads) consistently outperforms pruning m_d (head channels) across LM loss, throughput, and latency. At 50% pruning, m_h achieves LM loss ~3.0 vs. >8.0 for m_d, throughput ~1.12× vs. ~1.05×, and latency ~0.90× vs. ~0.95×. This establishes m_h as the preferred pruning axis and is a non-obvious finding—intuitively, one might expect head channels (the "width" within a head) to be more redundant than entire heads.

- **Mamba scoring activation source (Table 2):** W_x activations produce the best LM loss in 5 of 6 configurations. The advantage over W_z and W_O is configuration-dependent, ranging from marginal (4.49 vs. 4.49 vs. 4.49 for {13056, 3072, 96, 64} where W_x and W_O tie) to dramatic (4.68 vs. 7.09 vs. 10.01 for {14592, 3072, 96, 56}). This ablation justifies the paper's architectural choice and suggests that practitioners should not assume any activation source is equally informative for Mamba importance scoring—the W_x output captures the SSM's core sequence-processing contribution most directly.

- **FLAP vs. L2 importance estimation (Table 3):** Before KD, FLAP outperforms L2 for FFN pruning (1.32 vs. 1.364 for FFN=16384) but substantially underperforms for Mamba head pruning (1.73 vs. 1.305 for 112 heads). After lightweight KD on candidate #1, both methods converge to identical LM loss (1.380). This negative result (FLAP offers no advantage) is practically informative—it saves future practitioners from implementing the more complex FLAP metric when the simpler L2 approach suffices.

- **Depth pruning at varying severity (Figure 5):** Pruning 4, 8, 12, 16, and 26 layers from the 52-layer model, each distilled with 126B tokens, demonstrates a sharp degradation threshold below 44 layers for math and coding benchmarks. Core knowledge benchmarks remain resilient even at 50% depth, but this "average accuracy" preservation masks catastrophic reasoning failure. This ablation establishes a critical boundary condition: depth pruning below ~85% layer retention is unsafe for this architecture if reasoning is required.

- **Depth-width hybrid vs. width-only (Table 1, candidates #7 and #8 vs. #1):** The 44-layer width-pruned model (#7, embedding 3072, FFN 14592, 128 heads, 64 channels) achieves LM loss 1.393 vs. 1.380 for width-only #1. The 44-layer variant with larger embedding (#8, embedding 3584, FFN 10752, 120 heads, 64 channels) achieves 1.394. Both are worse than width-only, even though 44 layers is above the 44-layer degradation threshold identified in Figure 5. This suggests that even "safe" depth pruning imposes an accuracy penalty that width pruning at full depth avoids.

- **Width-only candidate ranking sensitivity (Table 1, candidates #1–#23):** Within the width-only configurations, LM loss after lightweight KD varies from 1.380 (#1) to 1.411 (#23). The ~3% loss increase from best to worst width-only candidate is smaller than the gap to the depth-pruned candidates (~13% increase for #24 at 1.533). This demonstrates that width-only architectures are relatively robust to the specific choice of pruning targets, while depth pruning introduces large and unpredictable accuracy costs. However, even within width-only pruning, the paper shows meaningful variation—configuration #23 with only 64 Mamba heads (50% of original) is substantially worse than #1 with 112 heads (87.5% retention), confirming that Mamba head count cannot be reduced arbitrarily.

- **Teacher-student accuracy retention across benchmarks (comparing Tables 4 and 5 base-to-base and instruct-to-instruct):** The 4B model retains >96% of the 8B teacher's accuracy on average, but retention varies by benchmark: MMLU retains 93.7% (68.1/72.7), GSM8K retains 89.3% (69.6/77.9), HumanEval retains 104.4% (59.8/57.3—the compressed model surpasses the teacher), HellaSwag retains 94.8% (77.0/81.2). This benchmark-level breakdown is more informative than the headline average and is not explicitly discussed in the paper—it's derived from Table 4 but the authors do not call out the GSM8K drop relative to the average.

- **Long-context degradation at scale (Table 6):** The 4B model's RULER scores drop from 86.28 at 16K to 63.57 at 128K, a 26% relative decline. The 8B teacher drops only 11% over the same range (91.5 to 81.7). This gap—the compression amplifies long-context degradation—is not discussed but is evident from the data. It suggests that the reduced embedding dimension (4096 → 3072) and FFN capacity (21504 → 12288) may disproportionately affect the model's ability to maintain positional information at long distances.

### Critical Assessment

This paper makes a central claim that **width-pruned hybrid architectures, compressed using a group-aware Mamba pruning strategy, advance the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier while requiring up to 40× fewer training tokens than from-scratch alternatives.** The experimental evidence broadly supports this claim, but with important boundary conditions and gaps that the paper does not fully address.

**Does the evidence demonstrate that group-aware pruning is necessary, or just that width pruning works?**

The paper argues that the group constraint (Equation 17) is essential to avoid corrupting the SSM forward pass. This argument is theoretically sound: the permutation non-equivariance of Mamba's broadcast operation is a structural fact, not an empirical claim. However, the paper **never runs the counterfactual experiment**: what happens if you prune Mamba heads with naive global sorting (violating the group constraint) and then distill? Would the accuracy recovery via distillation compensate for the structural corruption, or would it fail catastrophically? The theoretical argument says the corruption is irrecoverable (Equation 16 proves the output cannot be permuted back), but in practice, the model might learn to adapt during distillation—the student is not required to be a permutation of the teacher; it just needs to produce similar output distributions. A genuinely rigorous paper would include the negative control: prune without group constraints, distill identically, and show the resulting model performs worse. The absence of this experiment means the group-aware constraint's **practical necessity** is asserted rather than demonstrated. The conceptual argument is persuasive, but the empirical standard for a methods paper should be higher.

**Does the compression recipe genuinely need all four components (Mamba head, Mamba channel, FFN, embedding pruning), or would a subset suffice?**

The paper presents the unified recipe as a package deal, but the ablation structure does not fully isolate the contribution of each component. The architecture search (Table 1) varies all components simultaneously—we cannot determine from the reported data whether pruning Mamba head channels specifically matters, or whether the same accuracy could be achieved by pruning only Mamba heads + FFN + embedding (leaving channels at 64). The correlation analysis (Figure 6) shows that Mamba parameters as a group correlate with certain accuracy/speed tradeoffs, but the paper does not report an ablation where Mamba channels are left unpruned while only heads are reduced. The Ghattas et al. (2025) comparison is not a direct ablation because their method includes head merging (different from this paper's pruning) and does not include FFN/embedding pruning. The claim that the "holistic" approach is superior is plausible but incompletely isolated—a simpler recipe (heads + FFN + embedding only) might achieve equivalent results.

**Does "40× fewer tokens" represent a fair comparison?**

The training token comparison in Table 4 is the paper's most attention-grabbing efficiency claim. Nemotron-H 4B uses 380B tokens for KD. Llama-3.2 3B reports 9T tokens, yielding a 24× ratio. Qwen-2.5 3B reports 18T tokens, yielding a 47× ratio. Zamba-2 2.7B reports 3T tokens, yielding a 8× ratio. The "up to 40×" headline is technically accurate (47× rounds to "up to 40×"), but it compares **total tokens seen by each model, including the teacher's pretraining**. The 8B Nemotron-H teacher was trained on 15T tokens (Table 4): the compression approach required 15T + 0.38T = 15.38T tokens total, while Qwen-2.5 3B required 18T tokens total. The **total compute** (teacher pretraining + student distillation) is therefore comparable (15.38T vs. 18T), not 40× less. The paper's framing compares student distillation tokens against competitor total tokens, which is misleading. The efficiency argument is valid only if the teacher model is already available as a shared resource (amortized across many compressed variants), but the paper does not make this amortization argument explicit.

**Is the "2.2× faster" throughput claim measured under realistic conditions?**

The throughput and latency measurements are at a single operating point: batch size 4, input length 65536, output length 1024. This is a long-context inference setting that favors hybrid architectures (SSMs have O(1) KV-cache, Transformers have O(N) growth). The throughput advantage of Nemotron-H 4B over Phi-4-Mini-4B (a pure Transformer) is partly an artifact of the architecture at long context lengths, not purely a result of the compression method. The paper does not report throughput at short context lengths (e.g., 2048 input, 256 output) or at different batch sizes, which would isolate the compression benefit from the architectural benefit. The claim that Nemotron-H 4B is "2.2× faster" is true at the measured operating point but may not generalize to all deployment scenarios. The baseline model (Phi-4-Mini-4B) is a pure Transformer at a similar parameter count, so the throughput comparison conflates two effects: (a) hybrid vs. Transformer architecture, and (b) compressed vs. from-scratch training. A cleaner throughput comparison would be Nemotron-H 4B vs. Nemotron-H 8B (same architecture, different size via compression), which shows ~1.4× throughput improvement (the paper mentions this in the evaluation summary: "improving throughput by ~1.4×" over the 8B teacher).

**Is the 96% accuracy retention claim robust across all capability dimensions?**

The 96% figure is an average across 16 diverse benchmarks. This hides significant variance: GSM8K retention is ~89%, MMLU retention is ~94%, while HumanEval retention is ~104% (the compressed model surpasses the teacher). The average is a useful summary statistic, but a practitioner whose primary use case is mathematical reasoning would experience a ~11% relative accuracy drop, not 4%. The paper should make the variance explicit rather than burying it in Table 4's per-benchmark numbers. Additionally, the paper does not report retention on any benchmark the teacher genuinely fails at (accuracy floor effects)—all reported benchmarks have teacher accuracy well above random, so retention is measured in a regime where the teacher is competent. What happens on tasks where the teacher achieves, say, 30% accuracy? Does the student retain 96% of that (dropping to 29%), or is retention substantially lower in low-accuracy regimes? This is not tested.

**What the experiments do not test that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **Ablating the group constraint itself:** Prune with and without group-aware ranking, distill identically, compare. This would convert the theoretical necessity argument into an empirical demonstration.
- **Isolating individual pruning axes:** The architecture search varies everything simultaneously. A controlled experiment that prunes only Mamba heads (holding FFN, embedding, channels, and depth constant), then only FFN, then only embedding, then only channels, then only depth, would provide clearer causal evidence for the claimed sensitivity hierarchy. The current correlation analysis (Figure 6) is observational, not interventional.
- **Multiple random seeds for the architecture search:** With 125 candidates and a single calibration set for importance estimation, the ranking of top candidates could be sensitive to the specific 1024 samples chosen. Running the search with a different calibration split and checking whether the same candidate (#1) is selected would improve confidence.
- **Throughput at short context lengths:** The paper's throughput advantage is demonstrated only at 65K input context, where SSMs have a known architectural advantage over Transformers. Measurements at 2K–8K context lengths would clarify how much of the benefit is from compression vs. architecture choice.
- **Retention on lower-accuracy benchmarks:** Testing benchmarks where the 8B teacher achieves 20–40% accuracy would reveal whether the 96% retention holds across the difficulty spectrum or only in the high-accuracy regime.
- **Head-only vs. channel-only vs. combined pruning ablation within the 4B budget:** Within the fixed parameter budget, is it better to prune heads more aggressively and leave channels at 64, or prune channels moderately and leave heads at 128? The current results suggest heads are the better target (Figure 7, but that ablation holds the rest of the network at 8B size). At the 4B scale with FFN and embedding also pruned, this tradeoff might shift.
- **Statistical significance or confidence intervals for benchmark comparisons:** The paper reports point estimates for accuracy on each benchmark without any measure of variability. With 500-question MATH-500, the difference between 76.4 and 70.8 (5.6 points) is likely significant, but without standard errors, this is an assumption. The paper does not discuss test-set size for any benchmark or how many evaluation samples are used.
- **Comparison to distillation-only compression (no pruning):** Could the same accuracy be achieved by distilling the 8B teacher into a randomly initialized 4B hybrid architecture (same architecture as the pruned model) using the same 380B token budget? This would isolate the benefit of structured pruning from the benefit of distillation itself. If distillation-only achieves comparable accuracy, then structured pruning is unnecessary—the same outcome could be reached without the importance estimation and ranking machinery.
- **Scaling behavior at intermediate sizes:** The paper compresses from 8B to 4B (2× reduction). How does the approach scale to 3×, 4×, or 8× reduction? Does the accuracy retention curve have an elbow where it drops sharply? The Mamba2 780M experiment (1.3B → 780M, 1.67× reduction) provides one additional data point, but a scaling curve across multiple compression ratios would characterize the method's limits more clearly.

**Summary of evidence quality:** The paper demonstrates convincingly that structured pruning with knowledge distillation can produce a competitive 4B hybrid model from an 8B teacher at substantially lower incremental training cost than from-scratch training of a comparably sized model. The evidence for the specific *group-aware* contribution is theoretical rather than empirical—the constraint is logically necessary, but its practical impact relative to naive pruning is untested. The evidence for the sensitivity hierarchy (heads > channels > FFN > embedding > depth) is correlational from the 125-model sweep, well-supported by the data, but would benefit from controlled single-axis ablations. The throughput and latency advantages are demonstrated at one favorable operating point (long context), and the training efficiency claim conflates teacher pretraining cost with student distillation cost. Overall, the paper makes a solid practical contribution to the hybrid model compression toolkit, but the experimental design leaves several important counterfactuals unexplored, particularly regarding whether the group constraint is practically necessary and whether the individual components of the unified recipe contribute additively or redundantly.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Group-Aware Constraint's Practical Necessity Is Asserted, Not Demonstrated

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire Mamba pruning methodology rests on the claim that head permutations must respect group boundaries (Equation 17) to avoid corrupting the SSM forward pass (Equation 16). The paper provides a theoretical argument for why violating this constraint produces an irrecoverable computation—the `B_t x_t` broadcast pattern cannot be restored by output permutation—but this argument addresses mathematical structure, not empirical outcomes under the full pruning-plus-distillation pipeline.

**The consequence.** The paper never runs the direct counterfactual experiment: prune Mamba heads with naive global sorting (ignoring groups), apply the identical distillation protocol, and compare the resulting model's accuracy against the group-constrained version. If distillation can partially compensate for the structural corruption—by learning to route information differently through the remaining heads—the practical accuracy gap might be smaller than the theory suggests, or even negligible. Conversely, if naive pruning produces catastrophic degradation that distillation cannot recover, the group constraint is genuinely essential. Without this experiment, the paper's central methodological contribution—the group-aware constraint—remains **theoretically justified but empirically unvalidated** in its practical significance. A practitioner implementing the method cannot know whether the additional implementation complexity (group-aware sorting logic, per-group head budget allocation) is worth the engineering cost.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no ablation of the group constraint itself. Equation 16 proves the `B_t x_t` output cannot be permuted back after cross-group head swapping, but this is a pointwise structural claim, not a demonstration that the pruned-and-distilled model's benchmark accuracy degrades. The architecture search (Table 1) and Mamba scoring ablations (Table 2) all use the group-constrained ranking; there is no "unconstrained ranking" baseline anywhere in the experimental results. The paper's Figure 3 (in the Background section) visually illustrates the broadcast violation, but this is a pedagogical diagram, not an experimental result.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the group constraint as a correctness requirement derived from the forward pass mathematics, which is logically valid but does not answer the empirical question of whether violating it produces measurably worse downstream models. The authors do not flag this gap as a limitation or call for future work on it. A simple experiment—prune 112 Mamba heads (the final configuration) with and without group constraints, distill with 3.8B tokens (lightweight KD), compare LM loss—would have resolved this with minimal additional compute.

### The "40× Fewer Training Tokens" Claim Conflates Incremental Cost with Total System Cost

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline efficiency claim—"up to 40× fewer training tokens"—compares the 380B tokens used for knowledge distillation of the 4B student against the 9T–18T tokens used to train comparably sized models from scratch (Table 4). This accounting excludes the 15T tokens already spent pretraining the 8B Nemotron-H teacher (visible in Table 4's "Tokens" row for Nemotron-H 8B Base). The implicit assumption is that the teacher model is a **sunk cost**—already available and amortized across many uses—making the marginal cost of producing the 4B model equal to only the distillation budget.

**The consequence.** If a practitioner does not already have a high-quality 8B hybrid teacher available, the total compute required to produce the 4B model is approximately **15T (teacher pretraining) + 0.38T (distillation) = 15.38T tokens**, which is comparable to Qwen-2.5 3B's 18T tokens and substantially *more* than Zamba-2 2.7B's 3T tokens or Falcon-3 3B's reported token budget. The "40× fewer" framing is therefore valid only in the regime where the teacher is already available—for an organization starting from scratch, the compression approach offers minimal or negative total training cost savings. The paper conflates **incremental cost** (distillation only) with **total cost** (teacher pretraining + distillation), and the headline figure is the former while the comparison baseline (from-scratch training) represents the latter. This is a framing asymmetry that substantially overstates the method's practical efficiency advantage.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 reports 15T tokens for Nemotron-H 8B in the "Tokens" row, directly alongside the 0.38T for Nemotron-H 4B and 9T/3T/18T for the competitor models. The asymmetry is visible to careful readers, but the paper's text never acknowledges that adding teacher cost changes the comparison. The "up to ~40× fewer training tokens" language (Section "Obtaining the Best Compressed Hybrid Model" and Conclusions) consistently refers only to distillation tokens. The paper does not make the amortization argument explicitly—it does not say "assuming the teacher already exists"—which would clarify the scope of the claim.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not flag this as a caveat, does not report total system cost (teacher + student), and does not discuss the amortization assumption. This is a framing choice rather than a technical error—the numbers in Table 4 are correct—but it is a consequential framing choice for a practitioner evaluating whether to adopt the method. The paper could strengthen its claim by either (a) making the amortization argument explicit and quantifying how many compressed variants would need to be produced to break even on total cost, or (b) reporting total cost alongside incremental cost in the headline comparison.

### Throughput Advantages Are Demonstrated at a Single Operating Point That Favors the Architecture, Not the Compression Method

**The assumption or constraint.** All throughput and latency measurements in the paper (Figures 1, 6, 7, 8; Table 1) are reported at a single operating point: batch size 4, input sequence length 65,536, output sequence length 1,024. This is a long-context inference regime where the architectural advantage of SSMs over Transformers is maximized—SSMs have O(1) KV-cache size regardless of context length, while Transformers have O(N) growth, making SSM-based architectures disproportionately faster at long contexts compared to short contexts. The paper does not report throughput at short context lengths (e.g., 2,048 input, 256 output) or at different batch sizes.

**The consequence.** The paper's headline "~2.2× faster" claim (Figure 1, left panel), which compares Nemotron-H 4B against Phi-4-Mini-4B, conflates two effects: (1) the speedup from reducing model size (8B → 4B, a genuine compression benefit), and (2) the speedup from using a hybrid SSM-Transformer architecture versus a pure Transformer (an architectural choice independent of compression). A practitioner deploying the model for short-context tasks (conversational AI, standard Q&A, code completion with short context) would not experience the full 2.2× throughput advantage because the SSM's O(1) cache benefit is less pronounced at short lengths. The compression-specific speedup—the improvement over the 8B teacher at the same context length—is reported as ~1.4× (Section "Evaluation Summary"). The 2.2× figure is therefore an **architecture-plus-compression** advantage, not a **compression-only** advantage, but the paper's presentation blurs this distinction.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The throughput measurement protocol is specified only in figure captions: "Relative throughput and latency are measured for an input and output context length of 65536 and 1024, respectively" (Figure 8 caption). The paper never reports throughput at shorter contexts, never compares Nemotron-H 4B against a compressed Transformer 4B model of similar parameter count at multiple context lengths, and never isolates the compression benefit from the architectural benefit. The ~1.4× improvement over the 8B teacher is mentioned once in the evaluation summary text, not in the figures or tables that headline the 2.2× claim. The comparison models (Phi-4-Mini-4B, Qwen-2.5-3B) are both pure Transformers, making the throughput comparison inherently architecture-conflated.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the context-length dependence of the throughput advantage, does not report measurements at multiple sequence lengths, and does not acknowledge the architecture-vs-compression confound. The 1.4× teacher-relative speedup is not highlighted in any figure or table—it exists only in the evaluation summary prose—while the 2.2× architecture-conflated number dominates Figures 1 and 8. The paper would benefit from a throughput-vs-context-length ablation curve showing both the 8B teacher and 4B student, which would cleanly separate the compression benefit from the architectural benefit across the context-length spectrum.

### The Architecture Search Relies on a Single Calibration Split with No Robustness Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire pruning pipeline—importance scoring, head/channel ranking, architecture candidate generation, and final candidate selection—depends on the activation statistics collected from 1,024 calibration samples (sequence length 8,192) drawn from the Phase 3 data mixture. The assumption is that these 1,024 samples are representative enough that the importance rankings they produce would be stable under different calibration data draws, and that the selected architecture (#1: 112 heads, FFN 12,288, embedding 3,072) would rank highest consistently rather than being an artifact of the specific calibration split.

**The consequence.** If the importance rankings are sensitive to the calibration data composition, a different random draw of 1,024 samples could produce different head/channel rankings, leading to a different pruned architecture being selected. This is particularly concerning for the architecture search's final stage, where candidate #1 and #2 have identical lightweight-KD LM loss (1.380) and the tie is broken by throughput—if the loss ordering between #1 and #2 were reversed under a different calibration split, the final model would have 128 Mamba heads and lower throughput (0.98× vs. 1.0×). The 125-model sweep (Figure 6) shows that Mamba parameters have measurable correlation with LM loss (0.55), so shifting the selected architecture by even a few heads could meaningfully change accuracy. The paper provides no evidence that the importance scores—and hence the architecture ranking—are stable.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no robustness analysis for the calibration data: no multiple random seeds for importance estimation, no variance estimates on head/channel scores, no sensitivity analysis showing how the top architecture ranking changes under different calibration splits, and no discussion of how many calibration samples are necessary for stable importance estimation. The calibration set size (1,024 samples) is specified but never justified. The selection of #1 over #2 (identical loss, throughput tiebreaker) is a single-point decision with no uncertainty quantification. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the LM validation loss used to rank candidates, making it impossible to assess whether the difference between candidate #1 (1.380) and candidate #3 (1.384) is statistically meaningful or within estimation noise.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not flag calibration-data sensitivity as a limitation, does not run multiple calibration splits, and does not discuss the stability of the architecture ranking. This is a significant gap for a method whose primary output is a specific architectural configuration recommended as optimal. The fix is straightforward: run importance estimation on 3–5 different calibration splits, compute the variance in head/channel rankings, and report whether the same architecture is consistently selected. The additional compute cost would be modest (5 × 1,024 forward passes for importance estimation, plus 5 × 3.8B tokens for lightweight KD if re-running the full pipeline, or just re-ranking the pre-computed candidate list under different importance orderings).

### The Method Is Demonstrated on a Single Architecture Family with No Evidence for Broader Transfer

**The assumption or constraint.** All primary experiments use the Nemotron-H 8B model—a specific hybrid architecture with 52 layers, 128 Mamba heads, 64 head channels, 21,504 FFN dimension, and 4,096 embedding dimension, with attention layers comprising only 8% of total layers. The paper assumes (or at least implies by its framing) that the findings—width-only pruning dominating depth pruning, Mamba heads being the most efficiency-favorable pruning axis, the 88% head retention sweet spot—generalize to other hybrid architectures with different configurations (different ratio of Mamba to attention layers, different head counts, different group structures).

**The consequence.** A practitioner applying this pruning recipe to a different hybrid architecture—say, Jamba (Lieber et al., 2024) with its mixture-of-experts modules, or Zamba (Glorioso et al., 2024) with shared global attention and low-rank projections, or a custom hybrid with a 50/50 attention-to-Mamba split rather than 8/92—cannot know whether the sensitivity hierarchy (heads least sensitive, channels more sensitive, depth most sensitive) transfers. The finding that "the first attention layer is among the least important" (Figure 4) is specific to Nemotron-H's particular layer arrangement. The optimal Mamba head retention ratio (~88% for near-optimal accuracy) is likely a function of the original head count (128) and the compression ratio (2×); a model with 64 heads being compressed 2× would have a different viable retention floor. The 44-layer depth threshold for math/coding degradation (Figure 5) is specific to this 52-layer architecture.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The Mamba2 780M experiment (Table 8) provides one additional data point, compressing a pure Mamba2 1.3B model to 780M parameters (1.67× reduction) using SSM and embedding pruning. This demonstrates that the pruning-plus-distillation approach generalizes beyond the Nemotron-H hybrid architecture—the compressed 780M model matches the 1.3B from-scratch model's average score (44.7 vs. 44.9) despite using 28.6× fewer tokens. However, this experiment does not test whether the specific findings about pruning axes transfer: the Mamba2 1.3B has no FFN layers to prune, no attention layers to skip, and a different group structure. The experiment shows the *approach* generalizes but does not validate the *design guidelines* (head vs. channel preference, depth vs. width tradeoff, optimal retention ratios). The paper provides no experiments on Jamba, Zamba, or any other hybrid architecture.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed. The Mamba2 780M experiment is a good-faith attempt to demonstrate generalizability, and it succeeds in showing that the compression approach produces competitive models on a different architecture. However, the paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation—there is no discussion of how the specific pruning recommendations (head over channel pruning, width over depth, attention head non-pruning) might change for architectures with different Mamba-to-attention ratios, different absolute head counts, or additional components like MoE modules. The conclusions section makes no claims about breadth of applicability, which is appropriately conservative, but the introduction's framing ("To the best of our knowledge, no existing work...") implies a general solution for hybrid architectures that the single-architecture evidence does not fully support.

### Math and Coding Capabilities Degrade Disproportionately Under Compression, but This Is Averaged Away in Headline Metrics

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline claim—"retains over 96% of the original 8B model's accuracy"—is an average over 16 benchmarks spanning knowledge, reasoning, commonsense, math, coding, and reading comprehension tasks (Table 4). This average treats all benchmarks as equally weighted and equally important, implicitly assuming that a practitioner values a 1% drop on HellaSwag identically to a 1% drop on GSM8K or HumanEval.

**The consequence.** The average masks substantial heterogeneity in retention across capability dimensions. For the base model (Table 4), GSM8K retention is 89.3% (69.6/77.9—a 10.7% relative drop), while HumanEval retention is 104.4% (59.8/57.3—the compressed model surpasses the teacher), HellaSwag retention is 94.8% (77.0/81.2), and MMLU retention is 93.7% (68.1/72.7). A practitioner deploying the compressed model primarily for mathematical reasoning applications would experience an ~11% effective accuracy regression, not the 4% implied by the headline. This is not a failure of the method—compression necessarily loses information, and math reasoning is shown throughout the paper to be more pruning-sensitive than knowledge retrieval—but the headline framing obscures this predictable, task-dependent degradation. The instruction-tuned model shows better math retention (GSM8K: 98.4%, MATH-500: 98.5% in Table 5), suggesting the alignment process partially recovers math capability, but this recovery is not discussed or explained.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The per-benchmark numbers in Tables 4 and 5 make this heterogeneity visible, but the paper's text never calls it out. The depth pruning experiments (Figure 5) explicitly show that math and coding degrade more than knowledge benchmarks under layer removal, and the Mamba correlation analysis (Figure 6) shows that pruning SSM components affects certain capabilities more than others, but the final model's evaluation does not connect these findings to the benchmark-level retention pattern. The paper reports the average and moves on. There is no discussion of *why* math retention is lower than average, whether this is expected, or what practitioners should do about it.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed, and arguably obscured. The paper presents the 96% average as the primary accuracy-retention figure without discussing variance across benchmarks. The instruction-tuned results (Table 5), which show better math retention, could be cited as evidence that alignment mitigates the math degradation, but the paper does not make this argument. The paper would benefit from (a) breaking retention down by task category (knowledge, math, code, commonsense), (b) explicitly noting that math/code are more compression-sensitive, and (c) advising practitioners to weight these benchmarks more heavily when evaluating whether the compression ratio is acceptable for their use case. This is not an error in the data but a limitation of the framing—the headline number is technically correct but practically misleading for math-heavy deployment scenarios.
