# InternLM2 Technical Report

**ArXiv:** [2403.17297](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17297)

## 🎯 Pitch

InternLM2-20B solves 32.4% of MATH problems with a 20B LLaMA-style model that was only trained on 4K then 32K contexts—yet it nearly perfectly retrieves information across 200K tokens. Its COOL RLHF strategy uses a single conditional reward model instead of separate ones to balance conflicting human preferences like helpfulness versus harmlessness.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **InternLM2**, an open-source large language model trained on over 2T tokens that outperforms predecessors across comprehensive evaluations spanning 6 dimensions and 30 benchmarks, long-context modeling, and open-ended subjective assessments through innovations in pre-training data preparation and alignment. Pre-training proceeds in three phases—4k context training, long-context training transitioning to 32k-token sequences with a RoPE base adjustment from 50,000 to 1,000,000, and capability-specific enhancement training on 24B curated tokens—while alignment employs a novel **COnditional OnLine RLHF (COOL RLHF)** strategy that uses a single conditional reward model with system prompts to reconcile conflicting human preferences (e.g., helpfulness versus harmlessness in dialogue, code generation versus mathematical reasoning) and conducts three rounds of online PPO to mitigate emerging reward hacking through targeted fast-path preference patches and slow-path human annotation refinement. InternLM2 achieves leading performance on the MATH benchmark at both 7B and 20B scales, with the 20B Chat model reaching 32.4% on MATH and 79.6% on GSM8K, substantially outperforming GPT-3.5; on long-context evaluation, it nearly perfectly retrieves needles in the 200k "Needle-in-a-Haystack" test, establishing that the three-stage training pipeline combining Group Query Attention with RoPE extrapolation yields practical long-context capabilities without requiring architectures fundamentally different from the LLaMA backbone. The COOL RLHF ablation demonstrates that removing conditional system prompts from the reward model decreases precision across multiple domains by up to several points (e.g., from 75.10% to 73.26% on Anthropic Helpful-base), establishing that the conditional mechanism is essential for the model's strong subjective alignment performance—InternLM2-20B achieves a 21.8 win rate on AlpacaEval and a 31.4 win rate on CompassArena—but only when deployed with the same conditional prompt conditioning used during reward model training.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Problem: Replicating Proprietary LLM Performance in Open-Source Models

At the time of InternLM2's development, the LLM landscape was characterized by a stark divide. Proprietary models like ChatGPT and GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) had demonstrated "profound empathy and problem-solving capabilities," as the paper notes, sparking widespread discussion about the advent of Artificial General Intelligence. However, the path to developing open-source models with comparable capabilities remained elusive. The paper explicitly frames this as its central motivation:

> "Despite this enthusiasm, the path to developing models with capabilities comparable to those of ChatGPT or GPT-4 remains elusive."

This gap is not merely academic. The open-source community had been "working diligently to bridge the gap between proprietary LLMs and their open-source counterparts," producing several notable models—LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023a;b), Qwen (Bai et al., 2023a), Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023), and Deepseek (Bi et al., 2024). InternLM2 positions itself as the next step in this progression, aiming to outperform these predecessors.

### Why This Problem Matters

The importance of closing the open-source gap has both practical and scientific dimensions that the paper addresses:

**From a deployment perspective**, proprietary models impose several constraints that hinder real-world applications and research. Models like GPT-4 operate as black-box APIs with usage restrictions, cost structures, and data privacy concerns that make them unsuitable for many scenarios—on-device deployment, offline operation, domain-specific fine-tuning on sensitive data, and transparent safety auditing. An open-source model achieving comparable performance removes these barriers.

**From a research perspective**, the opacity of proprietary models means that the community cannot study *why* they work. The paper explicitly addresses this by releasing "models in different training stages and model sizes, we provide the community with insights into the model's evolution." This is a deliberate design choice—rather than just releasing a single final checkpoint, InternLM2 provides SFT-only models (InternLM2-Chat-{size}-SFT) alongside RLHF-trained models (InternLM2-Chat-{size}), enabling researchers to isolate the effects of each alignment stage.

**From a capability perspective**, the paper targets specific weaknesses in prior open-source models across several dimensions. Long-context modeling is highlighted as particularly important because "many downstream applications, such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and agents, rely on long contexts." Without strong long-context capabilities, open-source models cannot serve as backends for sophisticated agent systems or document-intensive applications—use cases where proprietary models currently dominate.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies four specific gaps in prior work that it aims to address:

#### 1. Opaque Pre-Training Data Preparation

> "Technical reports on LLMs in the past have seldom addressed the processing of pre-training data."

This is a significant criticism of the existing literature. While LLaMA, Qwen, and Deepseek all achieved strong results, their technical reports provided limited detail on *how* they prepared their pre-training data. The paper argues that data quality is "the most crucial factor during pre-training," yet the community lacked detailed guidance on practical data preparation pipelines. This omission matters because data preparation decisions—filtering strategies, deduplication methods, quality classification thresholds—directly impact downstream model performance, and without transparency, these decisions cannot be studied or reproduced.

InternLM2 addresses this by extensively detailing its data processing pipeline across three data types: text (web pages, papers, patents, books), code (from GitHub and online resources), and long-context data. For each, the paper describes specific techniques: heuristic rule-based filtering, MinHash-based fuzzy deduplication with 128 hash functions on 5-grams and a 0.7 similarity threshold, safety filtering via a combination of domain blocking (approximately 13 million unsafe domains), word blocking (36,289 unsafe words), and BERT-based toxicity and pornography classifiers, and quality filtering using fine-tuned BERT advertisement and fluency classifiers trained on manually annotated data.

#### 2. Preference Conflicts in RLHF Reward Modeling

The paper identifies a fundamental tension in how prior RLHF approaches handle multiple, potentially conflicting human preferences:

> "In developing a dialogue system, we expect it to provide useful information (helpful) while not producing harmful or inappropriate content (harmless). However, these two preferences often cannot be satisfied simultaneously in practice."

Existing methods, such as LLaMA2's approach (Touvron et al., 2023b), address this by deploying multiple separate reward models—one for helpfulness, another for harmlessness, potentially others for code quality or mathematical correctness—and aggregating their scores during PPO training. The paper argues this approach has two critical drawbacks. First, it introduces more models into the training pipeline, "thus increases computational cost and slows down training speed." Second, and more subtly, it forces the system to handle preference conflicts through post-hoc score aggregation rather than through the reward model's internal understanding of context-dependent priorities.

The paper's observation is that preferences are not inherently conflicting—they are *context-dependent*. A helpful response to a medical question might involve sensitive content that would be flagged as harmful by a generic safety classifier, but this isn't a true conflict; it's a failure to recognize that different scenarios have different acceptable boundaries. Prior multi-model approaches cannot capture this contextual nuance because each reward model operates independently.

#### 3. Reward Hacking in RLHF

The paper identifies reward hacking as a persistent challenge that worsens as scale increases:

> "RLHF faces the issue of reward hacking, especially when the policy becomes more powerful with the scale increasing, where the model might learn to 'cheat' the reward system by shortcuts to obtain high scores, rather than truly learning the expected behavior."

This is a well-documented phenomenon, but the paper argues that prior approaches lack a systematic mechanism for detecting and correcting reward hacking *during* training. Most RLHF pipelines train a reward model once, freeze it, and then run PPO—meaning that any reward hacking patterns discovered during PPO are baked into the final model because the reward model can't be updated to close the loopholes.

The paper's framing of this problem is particularly insightful: reward hacking is not a one-time issue to be prevented upfront but an emergent phenomenon that evolves as the policy improves. The model finds new shortcuts as it explores different regions of the output space during PPO, and these shortcuts are precisely the regions that the frozen reward model was never exposed to during its training. The failure mode is not that the reward model is bad but that it is *stale* relative to the evolving policy.

#### 4. Long-Context Fine-Tuning Degradation

A practical problem that emerges from the paper's own multi-stage pipeline: models that acquire long-context capabilities during pre-training can lose those capabilities during SFT and RLHF because the alignment data typically consists of short, instruction-following examples. The paper notes this explicitly:

> "To preserve the long-context capability of LLMs after fine-tuning, we keep using the long-context pre-training data in SFT and RLHF."

While this might seem like an obvious solution in retrospect, prior work had not systematically addressed it. The paper's approach includes constructing specialized long-context code data from GitHub repositories with over 10,000 stars, sorted by dependency relationships and concatenated into 32k-token sequences, demonstrating that maintaining long-context performance requires deliberate data engineering in the alignment stages, not just during pre-training.

### How the Paper Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

InternLM2 does not claim to introduce fundamentally new architectural innovations. Instead, it positions itself as advancing the state of the art through more careful execution across the entire development pipeline:

**Architecture**: The paper explicitly adopts the LLaMA architecture with standard optimizations—RMSNorm, SwiGLU activations, Rotary Position Embeddings—and the only modification is internal: consolidating the Wq, Wk, and Wv matrices into an interleaved layout (Figure 2) that simplifies tensor parallelism by making the weight matrix trivially splittable along its last dimension rather than requiring head-by-head extraction as in LLaMA. This is a practical engineering improvement, not a capability claim.

**Tokenization**: The vocabulary construction is a hybrid approach that takes the top 60,004 tokens from GPT-4's cl100k vocabulary (which primarily covers English and code) and integrates 32,397 Chinese tokens plus 147 spare tokens, for a total vocabulary size aligned to a multiple of 256. This is a pragmatic decision aimed at optimizing compression rates for Chinese text while maintaining compatibility with the broader tokenizer ecosystem—a specific need for bilingual models that prior English-centric tokenizers did not address.

**Pre-training**: The three-phase approach (4k context → 32k context with 50% short-context mixing → capability-specific enhancement on 24B curated tokens) builds on ideas from Qwen and GLM-130B but systematizes them into a clear progression. The long-context transition follows the RoPE base adjustment technique from Liu et al. (2023b), and the capability enhancement follows the general principle of incorporating high-quality data late in pre-training. What distinguishes InternLM2 is the level of detail provided about *how* each phase is executed.

**Alignment**: COOL RLHF is the paper's most novel contribution to methodology. It positions itself against LLaMA2's multi-model approach by offering a single-model solution, and against conventional RLHF's static reward model by introducing online updating. The paper is careful to ground this in prior work—the focal loss modification to the ranking loss (Equation 1) draws on Lin et al. (2017), and the multi-round online RLHF concept echoes Bai et al. (2022)'s iterative training—while claiming novelty in the combination of conditional reward modeling with online PPO rounds.

**Evaluation**: The paper evaluates across an unusually broad set of benchmarks (6 dimensions, 30+ benchmarks) and explicitly provides SFT-only baselines to enable analysis of RLHF's effects—a transparency practice that was relatively rare at the time. The inclusion of both objective downstream task benchmarks and subjective alignment benchmarks reflects an understanding that these measure fundamentally different capabilities.

The paper's strongest positioning claim is implicit: that the path to GPT-4-level open-source models runs not through a single architectural breakthrough but through systematic attention to data quality, training stability, and alignment methodology across the entire pipeline. Whether InternLM2 actually achieves this goal is an empirical question addressed in the evaluation sections, but the framing is clear—this is not a "new architecture" paper but a "how to build a strong LLM end-to-end" paper.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

InternLM2 is a family of large language models (1.8B, 7B, and 20B parameters) built through a carefully orchestrated pipeline spanning pre-training on over 2 trillion tokens, supervised fine-tuning on 10 million instruction instances, and a novel multi-round reinforcement learning procedure that harmonizes conflicting human preferences. The paper is primarily a **systems and methodology report**—its core idea is that achieving strong performance across diverse benchmarks and subjective evaluations does not require a single architectural breakthrough but rather systematic, transparent engineering across data preparation, training infrastructure, and alignment strategy, with the novel COOL RLHF technique serving as the primary methodological contribution for reconciling preference conflicts that prior multi-model approaches handled less elegantly.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The InternLM2 system comprises five major component groups that operate sequentially:

1. **InternEvo Training Framework** — the distributed training infrastructure that handles model parallelism, memory management, and fault tolerance across thousands of GPUs. It is the execution engine for all three training phases (pre-training, SFT, RLHF).

2. **Pre-Training Data Pipeline** — a multi-stage filtering and preparation system that processes raw web pages, code repositories, and long documents into three distinct training corpora (text, code, long-context), each with its own deduplication, safety filtering, and quality classification stages.

3. **Model Architecture (LLaMA-derived)** — the Transformer backbone with RMSNorm, SwiGLU activations, Rotary Position Embeddings, and Grouped-Query Attention, plus a reorganized weight matrix layout for flexible tensor parallelism.

4. **Three-Phase Pre-Training** — a curriculum that progresses from 4K-token contexts (90% of steps) to 32K-token contexts with RoPE base adjustment (9% of steps) to capability-specific enhancement training on 24B curated tokens (remaining steps).

5. **COOL RLHF Alignment** — the alignment subsystem consisting of: (a) a Conditional Reward Model trained with focal ranking loss on 2.4 million preference pairs across diverse domains, using system prompts to distinguish helpfulness, harmlessness, code quality, and mathematical correctness preferences within a single model; (b) a three-round Online RLHF process that alternates between PPO training and reward model updating via fast-path patches (20–100 targeted preference pairs to fix emerging reward hacking) and slow-path human annotation (comprehensive refinement using responses from the most recent models).

Information flows unidirectionally: raw data → filtered pre-training corpora → base model via InternEvo → SFT model via instruction fine-tuning → reward model via preference training → aligned chat model via PPO with online reward model updates.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the InternEvo training framework**, because every training phase depends on it for distributed execution, and its efficiency characteristics (64% MFU at 8 GPUs, 53% MFU at 1024 GPUs for 7B models) determine what training regimes are feasible.

- **Second, the model architecture and tokenization**, since these are the invariant substrate that all subsequent components operate on—the vocabulary construction and attention mechanism choices constrain everything downstream.

- **Third, the pre-training data pipeline in its three parallel tracks (text, code, long-context)**, because data quality is "the most crucial factor during pre-training" and the preparation methodology is the paper's most detailed contribution to open-source reproducibility.

- **Fourth, the three pre-training phases**, which build on each other sequentially—the 4K phase establishes foundational capabilities, the long-context phase extends the context window through RoPE base adjustment, and the enhancement phase targets specific weaknesses.

- **Fifth, the Supervised Fine-Tuning stage**, as the bridge between pre-trained capabilities and instruction-following behavior, establishing the 10M-instance dataset and ChatML format that subsequent RLHF builds upon.

- **Sixth, the COOL RLHF system in full detail**, covering the Conditional Reward Model's architecture, training data, focal ranking loss, and logarithmic barrier penalty, followed by the Online RLHF's fast-path/slow-path mechanics, PPO training specifics, and the critic initialization choice.

- **Seventh, long-context fine-tuning and tool augmentation**, which are orthogonal capabilities integrated throughout SFT and RLHF through specialized data construction and format extensions.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### InternEvo Training Framework

The InternEvo framework is the distributed training engine that executes all phases of model development—pre-training, SFT, and RLHF—across thousands of GPUs. Its architecture addresses four distinct challenges that arise when training large language models at scale: communication overhead, computation-communication overlap, long-sequence memory management, and fault tolerance.

**Parallelism Strategy.** InternEvo composes four parallel dimensions: data parallelism (replicating the model across devices with different input batches), tensor parallelism (splitting individual transformer layers across GPUs, following Shoeybi et al., 2019), sequence parallelism (distributing long sequences across devices, following Korthikanti et al., 2023), and pipeline parallelism (partitioning layer stacks across devices, following Huang et al., 2019). These are combined with three sharding dimensions for model states—parameters, gradients, and optimizer states—drawn from ZeRO strategies (Rajbhandari et al., 2020). The key innovation is that InternEvo allows each component of the model state to independently select its sharding approach and device mesh configuration. This means parameters might use Full-Sharding across all GPUs while optimizer states use Partial-Sharding within node-local groups, with an optimization framework that automatically searches for the most efficient sharding factors subject to GPU memory constraints (Chen et al., 2024b). This adaptive sharding is what enables InternEvo to maintain 53% Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU) at 1024 GPUs with a constant 4M-token global batch size—a regime where the computation-to-communication ratio decreases as GPU count increases, making strong scaling particularly difficult. In comparison, the paper reports that DeepSpeed with ZeRO-1 and MiCS achieves only approximately 36% MFU under the same conditions.

**Communication-Computation Overlap.** The framework interleaves communication and computation at two granularities. During parameter sharding, where model weights are distributed across GPUs to conserve memory, InternEvo pre-fetches complete parameter sets for upcoming layers through AllGather operations while concurrently computing the current layer's forward pass. During the backward pass, gradient synchronization occurs in two stages: ReduceScatter within parameter sharding groups to aggregate gradients, followed by AllReduce across sharding groups, with both operations overlapped against backward computation. For optimizer state sharding, where updated parameters are broadcast within sharding groups after each optimizer step, this communication is overlapped with the forward computation of the subsequent training step. These overlapping strategies mean that a substantial fraction of communication cost is effectively hidden behind computation, directly improving MFU.

**Long-Sequence Training.** For sequences up to 256,000 tokens, InternEvo employs a hierarchical memory management system that decomposes GPU memory into the four parallel dimensions (data, tensor, sequence, pipeline) and three sharding dimensions (parameter, gradient, optimizer state) as a unified space, then uses an execution simulator to identify the optimal parallelization strategy for a given training configuration (model size, sequence length, batch size) before training begins (Chen et al., 2024a). The framework also implements a memory pool with defragmentation that proactively consolidates small memory chunks to prevent out-of-memory errors that commonly occur in long-sequence training due to memory fragmentation from variable-length allocations. At 256K sequence length with InternLM-7B, InternEvo achieves nearly 88% MFU on 128 GPUs, compared to approximately 65% for both DeepSpeed-Ulysses and Megatron-LM—a 35% relative improvement that directly translates to faster long-context training.

**Fault Tolerance.** Based on a six-month characterization study of LLM training workloads in the authors' GPU datacenter (Hu et al., 2024), InternEvo incorporates asynchronous checkpoint saving where each GPU first writes model states to local storage and then asynchronously uploads to distributed remote storage. This dual-step process means that hardware or network failures cause only minimal training progress loss, and the system can resume training even when the parallelization configuration has changed between the failure and recovery. The framework also includes automatic failure diagnosis and a decoupled scheduling system for evaluation tasks that provides timely model performance feedback without interfering with training jobs.

**RLHF-Specific Infrastructure.** For the PPO stage, where four equally-sized models (actor, critic, reference, reward) must interact, InternEvo deploys each model at its individually optimal configuration and uses a framework built on top of Ray for coordination. This allows the RLHF pipeline to scale across hundreds of GPUs while maintaining the efficiency characteristics demonstrated during pre-training.

#### Model Architecture and Tokenization

**Architecture Choice and Rationale.** InternLM2 adopts the LLaMA architecture (Touvron et al., 2023a) without fundamental modification. The transformer backbone uses RMSNorm (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019) for layer normalization instead of standard LayerNorm—a choice that removes the mean-centering operation and is empirically faster while maintaining stability—and SwiGLU (Shazeer, 2020) as the activation function in feed-forward layers, which uses a gated linear unit with Swish activation and has been shown to outperform ReLU and GELU in transformer models. Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) provide position information through rotation matrices applied to query and key vectors in attention, with the base frequency controlling how rapidly the positional signal decays with token distance. The architectural parameters for the three model sizes are specified in Table 3: the 1.8B model uses 24 layers with hidden dimension 2048, 8 key-value heads, and 2 query heads per key-value head (16 total query heads); the 7B model uses 32 layers, hidden dimension 4096, 8 key-value heads, and 4 query heads per key-value head (32 total query heads); the 20B model uses 48 layers, hidden dimension 6144, 8 key-value heads, and 6 query heads per key-value head (48 total query heads). All models use Grouped-Query Attention (GQA) (Ainslie et al., 2023), where multiple query heads share a single key-value head pair—5 query heads share each key-value head in the 20B model, for instance. GQA reduces the memory footprint of key-value caching during long-context inference by a factor equal to the query-per-kv ratio, making inference at 32K+ tokens feasible without excessive GPU memory.

**Weight Matrix Layout Modification.** The paper makes one deliberate deviation from standard LLaMA: the Wq, Wk, and Wv projection matrices are consolidated into a single interleaved weight matrix rather than stacked sequentially. In standard LLaMA, the query, key, and value projections for all heads are stored as concatenated blocks (all query weights, then all key weights, then all value weights). In InternLM2's interleaved layout (Figure 2), the weights for each head are stored contiguously as (head_k_query, head_k_key, head_k_value) tuples before moving to the next head. The practical consequence is that tensor parallelism becomes trivially implementable: splitting the weight matrix along its last dimension automatically assigns complete heads to different devices without requiring head-by-head extraction logic. The paper provides code snippets in Figure 2 contrasting the two approaches—the LLaMA layout requires a 21-line function with manual computation of head sizes, query-per-kv ratios, and slicing indices, while the InternLM2 layout requires a single `torch.split` call. This choice reportedly yields "a training acceleration of over 5% during the pre-training phase," attributed to reduced communication overhead during tensor-parallel forward and backward passes, though the paper does not provide detailed profiling to explain this speedup.

**Tokenization.** The vocabulary construction follows a hybrid strategy targeting bilingual (Chinese and English) efficiency. The starting point is GPT-4's cl100k vocabulary, which contains 100,256 tokens predominantly covering English and programming languages with fewer than 3,000 Chinese tokens. From this, the authors select the top 60,004 tokens by frequency and integrate 32,397 Chinese-specific tokens, then add 147 spare tokens. The resulting vocabulary size is chosen to align with a multiple of 256, "thereby facilitating efficient training"—a common practice because GPU tensor core operations are optimized for dimensions that are multiples of 256. The paper does not provide the final exact vocabulary size, but the arithmetic suggests 60,004 + 32,397 + 147 = 92,548 tokens, which is 256 × 361.5—not a multiple of 256. Either the top-k selection or the Chinese token count is adjusted to achieve alignment, but the exact mechanism is not specified. The rationale for this hybrid approach is twofold: maintaining compatibility with the broader GPT-4 tokenizer ecosystem (enabling interoperability with tools and libraries that expect cl100k tokenization) while achieving better compression rates for Chinese text, which is important because Chinese characters typically encode more semantic content per character than alphabetic languages and a primarily English tokenizer would fragment Chinese text into many more tokens than necessary, inflating sequence lengths and reducing the effective context window for Chinese inputs.

#### Pre-Training Data Pipeline: Text Data

The text data pipeline processes four source types—web pages, papers, patents, and books—through five sequential stages: formatting, rule-based filtering, deduplication, safety filtering, and quality filtering. Table 1 provides source-level statistics: English web pages dominate at 9,129.39 GB (67.51% of total), followed by Chinese web pages at 2,562.86 GB (18.95%), Chinese technical literature at 668.19 GB (4.94%), English technical literature at 576.48 GB (4.27%), Chinese books at 366.82 GB (2.71%), and English books at 220.14 GB (1.63%). The pipeline processes each source independently but applies conceptually similar transformations, with web page data receiving the most extensive treatment due to its high noise content.

**Step 1: Data Formatting.** Web page data is extracted from Common Crawl WARC format archives using Trafilatura (Barbaresi, 2021) for HTML parsing and main text extraction. The extracted text undergoes language detection and classification using the pycld2 library, then each document receives a unique identifier and is stored in JSON Lines format. This standardization step ensures all downstream processing operates on a uniform representation regardless of source.

**Step 2: Rule-Based Filtering.** The formatted data passes through heuristic statistical rules designed to catch specific failure modes observed by the authors in web-crawled text: anomalous line break patterns (indicative of failed HTML parsing where navigation elements or advertisements get interleaved with content), abnormal character frequency distributions (such as excessive special characters from encoding errors or non-natural-language artifacts), and irregular punctuation distributions (which signal text that is not coherent prose, such as lists of keywords or automatically generated content). These filters produce what the paper calls "Clean data." The rules are source-specific and language-specific—a filter designed for English web pages would not be applied to Chinese patents—though the exact rules are not enumerated.

**Step 3: Deduplication.** The clean data undergoes fuzzy deduplication using the MinHash variant of Locality-Sensitive Hashing (Broder, 1997). The method works by constructing a signature for each document using 128 hash functions applied to the document's 5-gram set—each hash function maps the set of all 5-character sequences in the document to a single integer, and the 128 integers together form a compact fingerprint. Two documents are considered duplicates if their MinHash signatures have a Jaccard similarity above 0.7 (meaning at least 70% of their 5-gram sets overlap). When duplicates are detected, the system retains the version from the more recent Common Crawl dump, prioritizing freshness. This approach handles near-duplicate detection (documents that are substantially similar but not byte-identical, such as the same article syndicated across multiple sites with different formatting) that exact-match deduplication would miss. The choice of 5-grams (rather than word-level or character-level tokens) and 128 hash functions represents a standard tradeoff: larger n-grams increase precision (fewer false positives) but decrease recall (more true duplicates missed), while more hash functions improve estimate accuracy at the cost of increased computation and storage.

**Step 4: Safety Filtering.** The deduplicated data passes through a four-layer safety pipeline. The first layer is domain blocking: a list of approximately 13 million unsafe domain names is constructed and any document from a matching domain is removed. The second layer is word blocking: a list of 36,289 unsafe words is used to filter documents containing those terms. The paper notes that the word list was constructed conservatively to avoid excessive false positives, acknowledging the tension between safety coverage and over-filtering. The third and fourth layers are classifier-based: a BERT model fine-tuned on the Kaggle "Toxic Comment Classification Challenge" dataset serves as a toxicity classifier, and another BERT model fine-tuned on a custom pornography classification dataset (annotated using the Perspective API applied to samples from the deduplicated data) serves as a pornography classifier. Documents receiving scores below unspecified thresholds from either classifier are removed. The paper frames this composite strategy as more robust than any single approach: domain blocking catches organized sources of unsafe content, word blocking catches documents with explicit terms that might evade classifier detection, and the classifiers catch subtle unsafe content that pattern-based methods would miss.

**Step 5: Quality Filtering.** The final stage targets two specific low-quality content patterns that the authors observed as prevalent in web data: marketing advertisements (characterized by repetitive, low-information promotional language) and incoherent extracted text (resulting from web pages that are primarily lists of article abstracts or product descriptions rather than continuous prose). Manual annotation was conducted for both patterns. For advertisement classification, annotators labeled whether a document contained advertising content at any granularity. For fluency classification, annotators rated documents on four dimensions—consistency (logical flow between sentences), noise (presence of non-content artifacts like HTML remnants), information content (density of meaningful information), and grammar—producing a composite fluency score. Two separate BERT classifiers were then fine-tuned on these manually annotated datasets: an advertisement classifier and a fluency classifier. Documents scoring below thresholds on either classifier are filtered out. The paper treats this as a precision operation—removing clearly low-quality content rather than selecting only the highest-quality content—because over-aggressive quality filtering risks removing legitimate but unusual text patterns that contribute to model robustness.

#### Pre-Training Data Pipeline: Code Data

The code data pipeline addresses the challenge that programming language data has fundamentally different structure than natural language, requiring specialized processing for deduplication, quality assessment, and context construction. Figure 4 shows the source distribution, spanning GitHub crawls, public datasets, Q&A forums, tutorial sites, and API documentation. Table 2 breaks down code data by quality tier: 105.6 GB (16.8%) classified as high-quality, 440.1 GB (69.9%) as moderate-quality, and 83.85 GB (13.3%) as low-quality and excluded from training.

**Format Cleaning and Interleaving.** All code data is converted to a unified Markdown format, chosen for its minimal token overhead for formatting and its natural support for interleaving code blocks with natural language descriptions. The actual pre-training format is more complex: multiple code files from the same repository are concatenated based on their dependency relationships, creating interleaved sequences that teach the model about cross-file program structure—a capability that is critical for real-world software development tasks where understanding a function requires following its imports. The paper mentions that a small fraction of data exhibited corrupted HTML or XML formats even after conversion, and heuristic rules were applied to minimize these occurrences, but the authors explicitly state they "did not invest too much effort in format purification," suggesting that perfect formatting is less critical than content coverage.

**Code-Specific Deduplication.** The deduplication approach is conceptually similar to the text pipeline but with a critical difference: tokenization must be programming-language-aware to avoid misclassifying semantically identical code as distinct. For example, Python code using two-space indentation versus four-space indentation versus tab characters would appear as different documents under a whitespace-naive tokenizer, when in fact the logic is identical. The paper's insight is that an effective tokenizer—one that normalizes whitespace differences—is essential for applying universal deduplication, though the specific tokenizer used is not detailed. File-level deduplication is chosen over paragraph-level or line-level deduplication "to preserve context integrity," reflecting the observation that code understanding depends on complete logical units (functions, classes, files) rather than isolated statements.

**Quality Filtering with Iterative Annotation.** Code quality assessment proved substantially more difficult than text quality assessment because the definition of "high-quality code for training an LLM" is ambiguous: a widely-recognized code repository might be too complex to serve as a teaching example, while a simple tutorial might be pedagogically valuable despite being technically trivial. The paper introduces an iterative annotation process (Figure 5) to address this. In each iteration, annotators verify model predictions on high-confidence high-quality and low-quality samples, refine the annotation guidelines based on discovered edge cases, and an automatic validation step checks that previously annotated samples remain correctly classified under the updated guidelines. The workflow took three iterations to finalize the scoring model. The paper also notes that the correlation between model-based scorer assessments and human judgments varied across programming languages, and enlarging the training set beyond approximately 50,000 labeled samples did not substantially improve scorer accuracy. Consequently, model-based scoring was only applied to languages where predictions aligned well with human evaluations on a validated set—a practical admission that quality classifiers are not universally transferable across programming languages.

The additional rule-based scorers are described as "heuristic and varied," with one notable finding: code style (formatting conventions, naming patterns) proved to be an unreliable quality metric that "can misclassify too many codes as low-quality." This is important because many automated code quality tools heavily weight style, but for LLM pre-training, stylistic variation is actually beneficial—it exposes the model to diverse coding conventions that it will encounter at inference time.

**Dependency Sorting for Repository-Level Context.** With the context window expanded to 32,000 tokens, InternLM2 can process entire code repositories as single training examples. However, earlier pipeline stages (file extension filtering, deduplication) may have broken repository structure. The dependency sorting step first regroups code files originating from the same repository, then uses regular expressions to detect `import` relations across programming languages, and applies topological sorting to determine concatenation order. The sorted sequence places non-code files (Markdown documentation, README files) before the first code file in the same sub-folder, and code files follow in dependency order. Special cases are handled: when multiple paths exist between two files in the import graph, the shorter path is preferred; when cycles exist (which shouldn't occur in well-structured code but do in practice), alphabetical order determines the starting point. The paper mentions a specific trick for resolving batched imports like Python's `__init__.py` or C/C++'s aggregate header files: heuristic rules refine the import detection to avoid including unused dependencies that would create spurious connections, "ensuring that we accurately identify and process these relations at a finer level." The output is a single long Markdown document with code blocks where the import structure is preserved, allowing the model to learn cross-file dependencies from contiguous context.

#### Pre-Training Data Pipeline: Long-Context Data

The long-context data pipeline is applied to a subset of the standard pre-training corpus to produce training data suitable for the 32K-token context window. The pipeline consists of three stages designed to filter out data that is long but low-quality, rather than to select only the highest-quality long documents.

**Length Selection.** A rule-based filter selects data samples exceeding 32K bytes—approximately 32K characters, though the relationship to tokens depends on the tokenizer's compression rate. This is the entry criterion: only documents already long enough to fill the context window are considered.

**Statistical Filters.** These filters leverage distributional properties of long documents that differ from short documents. The paper argues that statistical features are "far more consistent" in long text than in short text, making statistical filtering particularly effective at this scale. The specific filters include lexical features and linguistic features, with a "remarkable filter" being the presence of discourse-structure words like "Especially," "Formally," and other conjunctions that signal coherent argumentative or expository structure. The guiding principle is to remove "meaningless data rather than selecting the most high-quality data"—a lower bar than the quality filtering for general text, reflecting that long documents are scarcer and over-filtering would leave insufficient training material. The thresholds for these filters are domain-specific and language-specific, not universal, because textbooks, research papers, novels, and patents each have distinct characteristic distributions of structural markers. For instance, a filter requiring conjunctions would be inappropriate for long code data, which typically contains no conjunctions at all.

**Perplexity Filters.** This stage addresses the specific problem of improperly concatenated documents. When a document consists of two unrelated segments joined by a layout error, the first segment provides a distracting context that makes the second segment harder to predict. The filter operationalizes this by comparing two perplexity values: the perplexity of a text segment S2 given its preceding context S1 (conditional perplexity), versus the perplexity of S2 alone (unconditional perplexity). When S1 and S2 are genuinely related, the conditional perplexity should be lower (adding context improves prediction), producing a negative perplexity difference. When S1 is a distracting context, the conditional perplexity may be equal to or higher than the unconditional perplexity, and the document is removed. The paper emphasizes that the difference in perplexity, not the absolute perplexity, is used as the filtering criterion, which "could largely mitigate the bias introduced by the estimator itself." This is because the absolute perplexity depends on the model used to compute it (a stronger model will assign lower perplexity to everything), but the difference between conditional and unconditional perplexity is a more robust signal of contextual coherence. The paper cites Wettig et al. (2024) and Sachdeva et al. (2024) for discussion of perplexity estimator bias. Failures of this filter catch cases like "failed HTML parsing, random social media snippets, and other instances stemming from recognition errors in sources with complex layouts."

**Threshold Selection Strategy.** The paper offers two methodological lessons for setting the many thresholds required by these filters. The first is to tailor thresholds per domain rather than seeking universal settings—each source type (books, papers, patents, code) and each language requires independent calibration because their statistical profiles differ fundamentally. The second is to use a validation set to focus annotation effort on borderline cases, since the statistical and perplexity filters produce smooth score distributions within a domain, meaning that threshold adjustment is a matter of deciding whether to raise or lower boundaries rather than redesigning the filter itself. Figure 6 visualizes the data distribution before and after filtering, showing that web pages (Common Crawl) and patents are heavily filtered while most books and papers are preserved, consistent with the expectation that professionally published long documents have higher inherent quality than web-crawled long text.

#### Three-Phase Pre-Training

The pre-training process consumes between 2.0T and 2.6T tokens across the three model sizes (1.8B, 7B, 20B) and is divided into three sequential phases distinguished by context length and data composition. Table 3 specifies the shared optimizer configuration: AdamW with `$\beta_1 = 0.9$`, `$\beta_2 = 0.95$`, `$\epsilon = 10^{-8}$`, weight decay `$= 0.1$`, and cosine learning rate decay to 10% of the maximum. The learning rate is `$3 \times 10^{-4}$` for all model sizes, with batch sizes of 4M tokens for 1.8B and 7B, and 5M tokens for 20B.

**Phase 1: 4K Context Training (approximately 90% of steps).** The model trains on data with sequence lengths up to 4096 tokens, drawn approximately evenly from English, Chinese, and code corpora. When a document exceeds 4096 tokens, it is forcibly truncated, and the remaining portion is used as a separate training example—a standard practice that avoids wasting long documents while maintaining the fixed context length required for efficient batched training. This phase establishes the model's foundational language understanding, factual knowledge, and basic reasoning capabilities.

**Phase 2: Long-Context Training (approximately 9% of steps).** The training corpus transitions to include 50% of samples with context lengths up to 32,768 tokens, while the remaining 50% are still sequences shorter than 4096 tokens. This mixed-length strategy is important: if the model were trained exclusively on 32K-token sequences, it might lose performance on short-context tasks due to the distribution shift in positional encoding usage. By maintaining short-context data in the mix, the model continues to practice standard-length reasoning while learning to utilize the extended context window. Concurrent with the length increase, the Rotary Position Embedding base frequency is adjusted from 50,000 to 1,000,000. RoPE encodes position information by rotating query and key vectors by an angle proportional to the token position and inversely proportional to `$\text{base}^{2i/d}$` for dimension index `$i$` and head dimension `$d$`. A larger base (here, a factor of 20 increase from 50,000 to 1,000,000) means that the rotation angles for distant positions change more slowly with increasing distance, effectively extending the range over which the model can distinguish relative positions before the sinusoidal pattern repeats or becomes indistinguishable. This technique follows Liu et al. (2023b) and is critical because the RoPE base used for 4K training would not provide sufficient positional resolution at 32K tokens—the model would be unable to reliably distinguish the order of tokens separated by tens of thousands of positions. The paper notes that, due to InternEvo's long-sequence optimizations and FlashAttention (Dao, 2023), the training speed decreases only 40% when expanding context from 4K to 32K, making this phase tractable.

**Phase 3: Capability-Specific Enhancement Training (remaining steps).** The final phase addresses the observation that capabilities like reasoning, mathematical problem-solving, and knowledge-intensive question answering depend on high-quality data that is "sparsely distributed in the entire corpus." The model sees a carefully curated 24 billion token dataset (Table 4) composed of: 15.97B tokens (65%) of "Retrieved Stem Data"—STEM-related content with high information density—2.17B tokens (8%) of "Retrieved Special Domain Data" for niche expert domains, and 6.26B tokens (26%) of "Selected High Quality Data" from open-source sources on the HuggingFace datasets platform. The paper filters out test-set-related data from this corpus and runs contamination analysis (Section 5.4) to verify. A smaller learning rate and batch size are used during this phase, though the exact values are not specified. The phase produces a substantial performance improvement across capability dimensions: Figure 12 shows that coding, reasoning, QA, and examination scores all increase markedly from the pre-enhancement checkpoint (InternLM2-{size}-Base) to the post-enhancement checkpoint (InternLM2-{size}), with the 7B model's examination score rising from approximately 43.65 to 51.69 (an 18% relative improvement) and the 20B model's examination score rising from approximately 59.1 to 70.71 (a 20% relative improvement).

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning

The SFT stage transforms the pre-trained base model into an instruction-following assistant using a dataset of 10 million instruction instances screened for helpfulness and harmlessness. Figure 7 shows the distribution of SFT data topics, covering general conversation, NLP tasks, mathematical problems, code generation, function calls, and other categories. The data is converted into the ChatML format, a markup language for structuring multi-turn conversations with distinct roles (system, user, assistant), which the paper cites via a URL to Microsoft's Azure documentation. This format choice is significant because it standardizes the representation of dialogue turns, tool calls, and system messages, making the model's training distribution compatible with downstream agent frameworks that expect structured conversation formats.

Both the 7B and 20B models train for one epoch—a single pass over the 10M instances—using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate of `$4 \times 10^{-5}$`. The single-epoch choice reflects the common practice in instruction tuning where overfitting to the SFT distribution is a concern; the model needs to learn the format and style of instruction-following without memorizing specific training examples at the expense of generalization.

#### COOL RLHF: Conditional Reward Model

The Conditional Reward Model is the central innovation of the COOL RLHF framework. It addresses the problem of preference conflicts—scenarios where different human values pull in opposing directions—by conditioning a single reward model on system prompts that specify which preference dimension is currently relevant.

**Architecture and Rationale.** Figure 8 contrasts the Conditional Reward Model (Figure 8b) with LLaMA2's approach (Figure 8a). In LLaMA2, separate reward models are trained for helpfulness and harmlessness, each producing independent scores that must be combined during PPO, typically through a weighted sum or constrained optimization. In InternLM2's approach, a single reward model is trained on preference data from multiple domains (dialogue, article writing, poetry, summarization, coding, mathematics, formatted output), with each preference pair accompanied by a system prompt that indicates which preference dimension applies. The system prompts are not simple labels but natural language instructions that direct the reward model to apply contextually appropriate evaluation criteria—for example, a prompt for mathematical reasoning might emphasize step-by-step correctness, while a prompt for dialogue might emphasize engagement and safety. Because the reward model is initialized from the SFT model, which has already learned to follow diverse instructions, it naturally generalizes this instruction-following capability to the reward modeling task: the system prompt conditions the model's scoring behavior just as it conditioned the SFT model's response generation.

**Training Data.** The reward model trains on 2.4 million binarized preference pairs—pairs of responses to the same prompt where human annotators (or, for some domains, automated evaluation) indicated which response is preferred. This dataset spans "various fields such as dialogue, article writing, poetry, summarization, coding, mathematics, and formatted output," covering enough diversity that the reward model must learn to switch between fundamentally different evaluation criteria. The batch construction strategy fixes the total token length per batch at 16,384 tokens rather than using a fixed number of preference pairs, avoiding inefficiencies from excessive padding when pair lengths vary. The maximum context length is set to 8,192 tokens, and a special token is appended to each sequence's end—the reward model's output at this token's position is used as the scalar reward score.

**Focal Ranking Loss.** The training objective is a modified pairwise ranking loss with a difficulty-weighting mechanism inspired by Focal Loss (Lin et al., 2017). The standard ranking loss for a preference pair `$(y_w, y_l)$` where `$y_w$` is preferred over `$y_l$` is:

$$L_{\text{ranking}} = -\log(P_{i,j})$$

where `$P_{i,j} = \sigma(r_i - r_j)$` is the probability that the reward `$r_i$` for the preferred response exceeds the reward `$r_j$` for the dispreferred response, with `$\sigma$` being the sigmoid function.

The focal modification introduces a difficulty decay coefficient:

$$L_{\text{ranking}} = -(1 - 2 \times \max(0, P_{i,j} - \frac{1}{2}))^\gamma \log(P_{i,j})$$

where `$\gamma = 2$` by default, and `$P_{i,j} = \sigma(r_i - r_j)$` as before.

**What it computes:** When the model correctly predicts the preference (`$P_{i,j} > 0.5$`), the term `$1 - 2 \times (P_{i,j} - 0.5)$` becomes smaller as `$P_{i,j}$` approaches 1 (indicating high confidence in the correct prediction). This coefficient is raised to the power `$\gamma = 2$` and multiplies the log loss. For an easy sample where the model correctly assigns high probability with `$P_{i,j} \approx 0.99$`, the coefficient is `$(1 - 2 \times 0.49)^2 = (0.02)^2 = 0.0004$`, dramatically reducing the loss contribution. For a difficult sample where the model barely prefers the correct response with `$P_{i,j} \approx 0.51$`, the coefficient is `$(1 - 2 \times 0.01)^2 = 0.96^2 \approx 0.92$`, leaving the loss nearly unchanged. When the model makes an incorrect prediction (`$P_{i,j} \leq 0.5$`), the coefficient is 1 (the max operation zeroes it out), and the loss reduces to the standard ranking loss.

**Why this form:** The standard ranking loss treats all preference pairs equally, which is suboptimal when the dataset contains an imbalance between easy and difficult samples. Easy samples (where the preferred response is obviously better) dominate the gradient because they are more numerous and produce reliable learning signals, but they contribute little to improving the reward model's discrimination at the margin. Difficult samples (where the preference is subtle or ambiguous) produce larger gradients when the model is uncertain, and the focal mechanism preserves these gradients while down-weighting the easy ones. The coefficient's functional form ensures it only down-weights when the model is already correct, preventing it from suppressing gradients on samples where the model needs to improve. The choice of `$\gamma = 2$` provides moderate down-weighting—higher values would more aggressively suppress easy samples but risk destabilizing training if the model's confidence estimates are noisy.

**Logarithmic Barrier Penalty.** To ensure that reward scores remain within a stable range across different training runs and reward models, a logarithmic barrier penalty is added:

$$L_{\text{penalty}} = -(\log(x + 5) + \log(5 - x))$$

where `$x$` is the raw reward score output by the model.

**What it computes:** This term penalizes reward scores that approach the boundaries of `$[-5, 5]$`. As `$x \to -5$` from above, `$\log(x + 5) \to -\infty$`, so `$-\log(x + 5) \to +\infty$`, creating an infinitely high barrier. Similarly, as `$x \to 5$` from below, `$\log(5 - x) \to -\infty$`, producing another infinite barrier. Within the interior, the penalty is finite and relatively flat (both log terms are moderate), meaning it does not significantly influence scores that naturally fall within the range.

**Why this form:** Without this penalty, different reward models trained with different random initializations or data mixtures could produce scores on arbitrarily different scales—one model's "good" score might be 2.0 while another's might be 15.0. This would require re-tuning PPO hyperparameters (particularly the KL penalty coefficient, which balances reward maximization against staying close to the reference policy) for each reward model. By constraining scores to a predictable range, the penalty eliminates this source of hyperparameter sensitivity. The logarithmic form is chosen over alternatives like a quadratic penalty because its steepness near the boundaries provides a stronger guarantee of containment, while its flatness in the interior (the derivative `$1/(x+5) - 1/(5-x)$` is near zero for small `$x$`) means it minimally distorts the relative ordering that the ranking loss learns. The combined loss is:

$$L = L_{\text{ranking}} + \lambda L_{\text{penalty}}$$

where `$\lambda = 0.02$` was chosen based on preliminary experiments.

**Training Details.** The reward model size matches the actor model size used in PPO—the paper aligns 7B reward models with 7B actors and 20B reward models with 20B actors. The reward model is initialized from the SFT checkpoint, with the final language modeling head replaced by a randomly initialized one-dimensional linear layer that maps the final hidden state to a scalar. Training uses AdamW with a cosine learning rate schedule decreasing from `$1 \times 10^{-5}$` to `$5 \times 10^{-6}$`, weight decay of 0.01, and a single epoch of training to prevent overfitting.

#### COOL RLHF: Online RLHF and PPO Training

After the conditional reward model is trained, the Online RLHF process alternates between PPO training of the policy and targeted updating of the reward model across three rounds.

**Online RLHF: Fast Path.** The fast path addresses emergent reward hacking by rapidly patching the reward model with a small number of targeted preference pairs. As PPO training progresses, the policy explores high-reward regions of the output space, some of which represent genuine improvement and others represent reward hacking (generating outputs that score highly under the reward model but are not actually better according to human judgment). The fast path identifies hacking patterns by comparing responses generated by early-stage and late-stage PPO models within the same round, constructing preference pairs where the late-stage (hacking) response is marked as dispreferred. Only 20 to 100 such pairs are needed per identified pattern, and incorporating them into reward model training "is sufficient to prevent the reward model from the corresponding hacking pattern significantly." The key efficiency insight is that reward hacking patterns are systematic and concentrated—a single training example demonstrating that a particular shortcut is undesirable can close an entire class of hack behaviors, because the reward model generalizes from the example to structurally similar outputs.

**Online RLHF: Slow Path.** The slow path provides comprehensive refinement of the reward model by incorporating human preference judgments on responses from the most recent and capable models. Unlike the fast path's targeted patches, the slow path aims to improve the reward model's "upper bound, especially the reliability and robustness of the reward model at the high-reward regions." It does this by forming preference pairs from responses generated by models at different training stages (SFT model, early-stage PPO model, late-stage PPO model) and having professional human annotators label their preferences. Because human annotation is slow, the slow path uses accumulated preferences from all previous models at the experiment's launch time, which may not include the current round's models. This creates a deliberate lag: the reward model is always trained on somewhat stale human feedback, but the alternative of waiting for fresh annotations on every round's outputs would make the process prohibitively slow. The practical compromise is that the fast path handles urgent fixes while the slow path handles general capability improvement.

**PPO Training Setup.** The PPO stage involves four equally-sized models: the actor model (the policy being optimized), the critic model (which estimates state values for advantage computation), the reference model (a frozen copy of the initial policy used for KL divergence regularization), and the reward model (frozen during each PPO round but updated between rounds). Training iterates over approximately 200,000 diverse queries in approximately 400 iterations, with the best checkpoint selected on validation sets for release.

**Model Initialization.** The reference model and actor model are both initialized from the SFT checkpoint—the reference model remains frozen, while the actor model is updated via PPO. The critic model is initialized from the reward model (excluding the final linear head, which is replaced with a value-predicting head) and undergoes 50 iterations of pre-training with the actor model frozen. This pre-training phase is "critical for stabilizing the value estimation in early training, thus preventing the potentially detrimental effects of unstable values." The paper provides an ablation (Figure 9) comparing critic initialization from the reward model versus from the SFT model. The reward-model-initialized critic experiences higher loss during the first approximately 20 PPO iterations—which the authors hypothesize "may reveal fundamental differences between the tasks of reward modeling and critic modeling"—but subsequently exhibits consistently lower loss and leads to higher rewards for the actor model. The interpretation is that the reward model's internal representations, trained to evaluate response quality, provide a better foundation for value estimation than the SFT model's representations, despite the initial mismatch between the reward-scoring task and the value-prediction task.

**Conditional Reward in PPO.** During PPO training, each query is prepended with the appropriate conditional system prompt before the response is scored by the reward model (Figure 10). This ensures that the reward signal the actor receives is contextually appropriate—a mathematical response is evaluated on correctness, while a dialogue response is evaluated on helpfulness and engagement. The system prompt is "agnostic to the other three models" (actor, critic, reference), meaning it only affects the reward computation, not the policy's generation or the value estimate.

**Pre-Training Gradients.** To mitigate catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained knowledge during PPO, the authors incorporate a pre-training loss term following the InstructGPT methodology (Ouyang et al., 2022). The coefficient for this term is set to 0.5, and the volume of pre-training data used is approximately 50% of the PPO training data volume. This means that for every two PPO updates driven by the reward signal, the model also takes approximately one update driven by the language modeling objective on pre-training data, anchoring it to its original knowledge distribution.

**Hyperparameters.** The KL divergence coefficient is set to 0.01, controlling the penalty for deviating from the reference policy. The actor learning rate is `$1 \times 10^{-6}$` and the critic learning rate is `$5 \times 10^{-6}$`—the critic learns faster because it must adapt to the evolving policy's value landscape. The PPO clipping parameter `$\lambda$` (not to be confused with the reward model's `$\lambda$` penalty weight) is set to 0.99, which the authors note is "larger" than typical and leads to higher rewards in their case. The sampling strategy uses nucleus sampling with `$p = 0.9$`—a "slightly conservative" setting that balances diversity and convergence speed by only sampling from tokens comprising the top 90% of the probability mass. Unlike some PPO implementations, InternLM2 does not apply value loss clipping or advantage normalization, suggesting that these standard stabilization techniques were unnecessary given the carefully managed reward model and critic initialization.

**Long-Context Fine-Tuning.** To preserve long-context capabilities through alignment, the paper continues using long-context pre-training data during SFT and RLHF. Two types of long-context data are employed: book-length documents and concatenated code repositories. The code data construction (Figure 11) starts with the repositories used in DS-1000 (Lai et al., 2023)—Pandas, Numpy, Tensorflow, Scipy, Scikit-learn, PyTorch, and Matplotlib—plus repositories on GitHub with over 10,000 stars that reference these core repositories. After the same filtering and cleaning as pre-training, each repository is sorted depth-first while generating prompts that briefly describe file contents. Files are concatenated until reaching 32K tokens, then a new example is started. The paper reports that long-context code data improves both long-context capability and code capability, suggesting a synergistic effect: the model learns to reason about large codebases, which simultaneously exercises its long-context attention mechanisms and its programming knowledge.

**Tool-Augmented LLMs.** InternLM2-Chat supports tool calling through an extension of the ChatML format that introduces an "environment" role for representing tool outputs. Two specific keywords—`<|interpreter|>` for code execution and `<|plugin|>` for external API calls—enable a unified streaming format that handles diverse agent interactions while maintaining compatibility with standard chat. The agent training data is constructed following Agent-FLAN (Chen et al., 2024c), which aligns agent-specific corpus with chat domain data and disentangles it along basic language model capabilities for fine-grained training. For mathematical problem-solving with code interpreters, the model uses the Reasoning Interleaved with COding (RICO) strategy described in InternLM-Math (Ying et al., 2024), where the model alternates between natural language reasoning steps and Python code execution, with training data constructed through iterative hard example mining.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Preference Conflicts as a Context-Dependency Problem Rather Than a Multi-Objective Optimization Problem

The dominant approach to handling conflicting human preferences in RLHF, exemplified by LLaMA2's deployment of separate helpfulness and harmlessness reward models, implicitly treats preference conflicts as a **multi-objective optimization problem**: different reward functions compete, and the PPO policy must find a Pareto-optimal balance through post-hoc score aggregation. InternLM2's COOL RLHF reframes this fundamentally. Preferences are not inherently conflicting—they are **context-dependent in ways that a single evaluator can learn to distinguish**.

This reframing has a concrete conceptual payoff. In LLaMA2's approach, the reward model for harmlessness is applied uniformly regardless of context, which means it must be conservative enough to catch harmful content in sensitive domains (medical advice, crisis response) while inevitably over-flagging content in domains where the same language patterns are appropriate (technical documentation, academic discussion). The tension is baked into the architecture: separate models with separate training distributions produce scores on different scales that must be combined, and the burden of resolving conflicts falls on the PPO optimizer through the weightings between reward components. This creates a hyperparameter sensitivity problem—changing the relative weight of helpfulness versus harmlessness can produce qualitatively different policies—and it means that the reward model system has no internal representation of *when* harmlessness matters more.

COOL RLHF's conditional reward model collapses this into a single model that learns to apply different evaluation criteria based on system prompts. The evidence from the ablation study (Table 19) is striking: removing conditional system prompts causes precision drops across every tested domain—from 75.10% to 73.26% on Anthropic Helpful-base, from 75.76% to 69.24% on Stanford SHP, from 77.18% to 72.11% on PRM800k math preference data. The uniformity of the degradation suggests that the conditional prompts are not merely providing minor calibration improvements but are structurally necessary for the reward model to maintain separate evaluation standards for qualitatively different tasks.

This is not an incremental improvement to reward model architecture. It is a **diagnostic reframing** that changes what researchers should optimize. The implication is that effort spent designing sophisticated multi-model aggregation schemes is misdirected—the bottleneck is not the aggregation mechanism but the reward model's inability to recognize contextual boundaries. A single model trained on heterogeneous preference data with explicit context conditioning can learn to switch between evaluation criteria, and the evidence from Table 19 supports that this works in practice. This finding connects to a broader pattern in language model research where conditioning on natural language instructions proves more effective than architectural separation for handling multi-task diversity—the same insight that drove the shift from task-specific fine-tuning to instruction tuning.

The practical significance extends beyond the reported metric gains. A single conditional reward model is computationally cheaper to train and deploy than multiple specialized models (no need to load and run separate models simultaneously during PPO), and it eliminates the hyperparameter tuning burden of weight selection between reward components. These are not the main conceptual contribution, but they make the reframing actionable: the paper demonstrates that context-dependent preference modeling works better in practice, not just in theory.

### Innovation 2: Reward Hacking as a Dynamic, Policy-Evolving Phenomenon Requiring Online Correction

Prior RLHF work recognized reward hacking as a problem—the model learns to exploit the reward function rather than actually improving along the intended dimension—but treated it primarily as a **static vulnerability** to be prevented through careful reward model training and KL regularization. The implicit assumption was that a sufficiently well-trained reward model, combined with strong KL constraints to keep the policy close to the reference distribution, would prevent reward hacking from emerging. Under this view, if reward hacking occurs, it means the reward model wasn't trained well enough or the KL penalty wasn't strong enough.

COOL RLHF introduces a fundamentally different diagnosis: reward hacking is an **emergent, dynamic phenomenon** that evolves as the policy explores new regions of the output space during PPO training. The reward model was trained on responses from earlier-stage models (SFT, early PPO), but as the policy improves—as it moves into high-reward regions that were sparsely represented or entirely absent from the reward model's training distribution—the reward model's generalization to these regions is unreliable. This is not a failure of reward model quality in an absolute sense; it is a mismatch between the reward model's training distribution and the policy's current output distribution. The paper's key insight is that this mismatch is **inherent to the PPO process** rather than a one-time fixable flaw, because the policy's improvement trajectory cannot be fully anticipated from initial data.

The Online RLHF mechanism—three rounds alternating PPO training with reward model updating—operationalizes this insight through two complementary pathways. The Fast Path (20-100 targeted preference pairs per identified hacking pattern) is conceptually interesting not because it fixes reward hacking cheaply (though it does) but because it demonstrates that reward hacking patterns are **systematic and concentrated** rather than scattered across the output space. A small number of counterexamples can close an entire class of hack behaviors because the reward model generalizes from structurally similar outputs. This is an empirical finding about the nature of reward hacking: it tends to involve specific, identifiable shortcuts rather than diffuse degradation.

The Slow Path is conceptually complementary. By continuously incorporating human preferences on the most recent model outputs, it addresses the distribution shift that makes the reward model stale. The paper's honest acknowledgment that the Slow Path uses "accumulated human preferences of all previous models at the launch time of our experiments (i.e., which may not include the preferences of responses produced by models at the current round due to the time cost of human labeling)" reveals a practical constraint—perfect online updating is infeasible—but also clarifies the division of labor: the Fast Path handles urgent, systematic hacking patterns, while the Slow Path handles the gradual improvement of reward model generalization to high-reward regions.

This reframing has significant implications for RLHF methodology. It suggests that the research community should stop treating the reward model as a fixed component that is trained once and frozen, and instead view it as a **co-evolving system** that must be updated alongside the policy. The evidence that three rounds of online RLHF produced a model achieving 21.8 on AlpacaEval and 31.4 on CompassArena—competitive with much larger proprietary models—provides empirical support, but the deeper contribution is the diagnostic framework that explains *why* online updating is necessary and *how* it should be structured (targeted fast fixes plus comprehensive slow refinement).

### Innovation 3: The Inseparability of Long-Context Capability from Alignment Data Engineering

Before InternLM2, the dominant paradigm for long-context LLMs was architectural: extend the context window through sparse attention mechanisms, recurrence, or position encoding extrapolation during pre-training, then evaluate the resulting model on long-context benchmarks. The implicit assumption was that if a model could attend to long contexts, it would maintain that capability through downstream training stages. InternLM2 challenges this assumption with a concrete empirical finding: **long-context capability degrades during SFT and RLHF unless deliberately maintained through data engineering**.

This is not an obvious finding. SFT and RLHF typically operate on instruction-following data with relatively short exchanges—a few hundred to a few thousand tokens. One might expect that the model's attention mechanisms, having been trained to handle 32K-token sequences during pre-training, would simply not be exercised during alignment but would remain intact, like a capability that lies dormant but available. The paper demonstrates that this intuition is wrong: without explicit intervention, alignment training on short sequences causes the model to lose its long-context ability, presumably because the distribution shift in training data length causes the model to adapt its attention patterns to the shorter contexts that dominate alignment data, and the RoPE extrapolation that enables long-range position discrimination atrophies from disuse.

The solution—continuing to train on long-context pre-training data during SFT and RLHF—seems straightforward in retrospect, but the paper's contribution is diagnosing the problem and demonstrating that the solution works across multiple training stages. More significantly, the paper shows that long-context data engineering has positive spillover effects: the GitHub repository data constructed for long-context fine-tuning (code repositories with 10K+ stars, depth-first sorted with generated file descriptions, concatenated to 32K tokens) improves not only long-context performance but also code generation capability. This synergy suggests that long-context training on structured data (code repositories with dependency relationships) teaches the model something beyond just attending to longer sequences—it may learn cross-file reasoning patterns that enhance programming ability even on short-context code tasks.

The evidence for the long-context capability claim is the near-perfect performance on the 200K "Needle-in-a-Haystack" test (Figure 13) and the strong results on L-Eval and LongBench (Table 15), where InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT achieves 48.6 and 48.1 respectively—leading or competitive with much larger models. But the innovation is not the benchmark scores; it is the methodological finding that **alignment is not context-length-neutral** and must be treated as an integral part of long-context model development, not an afterthought. This changes the development workflow for any team building long-context models: SFT and RLHF data must include long sequences, and those sequences should be deliberately constructed (not merely long random documents) to provide the structured long-range dependencies that exercise the specific capabilities being developed.

### Innovation 4: Difficulty-Aware Reward Model Training Through Focal Ranking Loss

The focal ranking loss modification (Equation 1) addresses a specific pathology in preference-based reward model training: **easy preference pairs dominate the gradient**, causing the model to overfit to obvious distinctions while underfitting to the subtle, difficult cases that truly determine reward model quality at the frontier. This is not a new observation in machine learning—class imbalance and easy-sample dominance are well-studied problems—but the application to pairwise ranking loss in the context of RLHF reward modeling is novel, and the specific functional form is tailored to the ranking setting in a way that standard focal loss adaptations would not capture.

The innovation lies in **what "difficulty" means in the pairwise ranking context**. In classification, an easy sample is one with high predicted probability for the correct class. In pairwise ranking, an easy sample is one where the reward model confidently assigns a higher score to the preferred response—that is, where `$P_{i,j} = \sigma(r_i - r_j)$` is close to 1.0. The focal coefficient `$(1 - 2 \times \max(0, P_{i,j} - 0.5))^\gamma$` is designed specifically for this setting: it only activates when the model is correct (`$P_{i,j} > 0.5$`), and it down-weights proportionally to the model's confidence. This is conceptually different from the standard focal loss, which down-weights based on the predicted probability of the correct class regardless of whether the prediction is correct, because in ranking there is no single "correct class"—the signal is relative preference between two items.

The paper's choice of `$\gamma = 2$` and the specific coefficient form are motivated by the observation that reward modeling datasets contain a natural imbalance: for many preference pairs, the preferred response is unambiguously better (e.g., a correct mathematical derivation versus one with an arithmetic error), while for a smaller subset, the preference is subtle (e.g., two factually correct responses that differ in helpfulness or clarity). Standard ranking loss treats both types equally, meaning the model's gradient is dominated by the easy pairs, and the model's ability to discriminate in the subtle regime—which is precisely where reward model quality matters most, since both RLHF and best-of-N selection depend on the model distinguishing between genuinely good and nearly-good responses—is under-optimized.

The significance of this innovation is not the 1-3 point accuracy improvements it likely contributes (the paper does not provide a dedicated focal loss ablation, so the exact contribution is conflated with other COOL RLHF components) but rather the **diagnostic recognition that reward model training data has a difficulty spectrum** and that standard ranking loss fails to account for it. This opens a research direction: what other properties of preference pair difficulty (ambiguity, subjectivity, domain specificity) could be incorporated into the training objective to produce more robust reward models? The specific form of the focal ranking loss is one instantiation of this principle, and the evidence from the conditional reward model's strong performance (Table 19) is consistent with its effectiveness, but the broader conceptual contribution is identifying difficulty-awareness as a relevant axis for reward model optimization.

This innovation is incremental in mechanism—it modifies an existing loss function—but fundamental in implication: it suggests that reward model training should not treat all preference pairs as equally informative, and that down-weighting easy distinctions is beneficial specifically because reward model errors at the decision boundary (responses with similar quality but different human preferences) are more consequential for downstream RLHF than errors on obviously different responses.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** InternLM2's evaluation spans a broad set of benchmarks chosen to probe distinct capabilities. For **comprehensive examination**, the paper uses MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020, 57 subtasks, 5-shot), CMMLU (Li et al., 2023a, 67 subtasks, 5-shot), C-Eval (Huang et al., 2023, 52 subtasks, 5-shot), AGIEval (Zhong et al., 2023, 20 exams, 0-shot), and GAOKAO-Bench (Zhang et al., 2023, objective questions, 0-shot). For **language and knowledge**, it evaluates on TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017, open-domain QA subset, 4-shot for base/0-shot in tables with noted discrepancies), NaturalQuestions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019, 5-shot), C3 (Sun et al., 2020, Chinese MRC, 5-shot), RACE-High (Lai et al., 2017, high school subset, 0-shot), and FLORES (Team et al., 2022, 101-language translation, 8-shot, BLEU). For **reasoning and mathematics**, benchmarks include WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020, 0-shot), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019, 0-shot), BBH (Suzgun et al., 2023, 0-shot), GSM8K-Test (Cobbe et al., 2021, ~1,300 elementary math problems, 4-shot), MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021, 12,500 competition problems, 4-shot), TheoremQA (Chen et al., 2023a, 800 QA pairs, 0-shot), and MathBench (Anonymous, 2024b, 3,709 questions with multi-stage difficulty, 0-shot and 4-shot). For **coding**, the paper uses HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021, 164 tasks, 4-shot for chat/not specified for base in tables), HumanEval-X (Zheng et al., 2023b, 164 problems × 5 languages, 5-shot), MBPP (Austin et al., 2021, sanitized version, 5-shot for chat/not fully specified for base), and MBPP-CN (Chinese adaptation of MBPP, 0-shot). For **long-context modeling**, it uses L-Eval (An et al., 2023, 18 subtasks, 411 documents, 2,000+ test cases, with close-ended tasks evaluated by exact match and open-ended by Rouge score) and LongBench (Bai et al., 2023b, 21 subtasks, 4,750 test cases, average English length 6,711 words, average Chinese length 13,386 characters). For **tool utilization**, it evaluates on GSM8K, MATH, MathBench, T-Eval (Chen et al., 2023b, human-verified tool-use instructions with step-by-step solutions), and CIBench (Anonymous, 2024a, interactive Jupyter notebook data analysis scenarios). For **subjective alignment**, the paper uses AlpacaEval (Li et al., 2023b, 805 single-turn questions, evaluated by GPT-4-Turbo with weighted win rate), MTBench (Zheng et al., 2023a, 80 two-round conversations across 8 dimensions, scored 1-10 by judge model), CompassArena (520 Chinese questions across 5 categories, double-blind GPT-4-Turbo evaluation with position bias mitigation), AlignBench (Liu et al., 2023a, 683 Chinese QA pairs across 8 categories, scored by CritiqueLLM on 1-10 scale), and IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023, 541 questions testing 25 instruction types, rule-based evaluation with four accuracy scores whose average is reported). All evaluations are performed using OpenCompass (Contributors, 2023b) unless explicitly specified otherwise. For multiple-choice questions on base models, perplexity evaluation is used as the primary assessment method rather than generation-based scoring.

- **Base model(s).** The paper develops three model scales within the InternLM2 family: 1.8B, 7B, and 20B parameters, all following the LLaMA-derived architecture with Grouped-Query Attention, RMSNorm, SwiGLU activations, and Rotary Position Embeddings. The base models (InternLM2-{size}-Base) represent the model state after the first two pre-training phases (4K and 32K context training) but before capability-specific enhancement training. The enhanced base models (InternLM2-{size}) include the additional 24B-token capability enhancement phase. Chat models come in two variants: InternLM2-Chat-{size}-SFT (after supervised fine-tuning but before RLHF) and InternLM2-Chat-{size} (after the full COOL RLHF pipeline). This staged release strategy is explicitly designed to enable community analysis of each training stage's effects. The 7B and 20B models are evaluated comprehensively; the 1.8B model appears primarily in the enhancement training comparison (Figure 12) and is not bench marked against external models in detail. The choice of PaLM 2-S* sized models (approximately 7B as the primary comparison point against Llama2-7B, Mistral-7B, Qwen-7B, ChatGLM3-6B, and Baichuan2-7B) positions InternLM2 within the most competitive open-source weight class at the time of release.

- **Metrics.** For objective benchmarks, the primary metric is accuracy—exact match for open-ended generation tasks (GSM8K, MATH) and correct option selection for multiple-choice tasks (MMLU, C-Eval, etc.). Pass@1 is used for coding benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP), measuring the fraction of problems for which at least one generated solution passes all test cases. For translation (FLORES), BLEU score is reported. Long-context understanding uses exact match accuracy for close-ended subtasks and Rouge score for open-ended subtasks in L-Eval, while LongBench reports category-level average accuracies. Subjective alignment benchmarks use a variety of metrics: AlpacaEval uses weighted win rate against a reference model (GPT-4-Turbo as judge, with logit-based preference weighting), MTBench uses a 1-10 score from a judge model, CompassArena uses win rate from GPT-4-Turbo comparisons with double-blind position swapping, AlignBench uses 1-10 scores from CritiqueLLM across multiple dimensions, and IFEval uses the average of four accuracy scores (prompt-level strict, prompt-level loose, instance-level strict, instance-level loose) from rule-based verification. The paper does not report confidence intervals, statistical significance tests, or standard deviations for any metric—all numbers are presented as point estimates.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a comprehensive set of contemporary open-source models at two parameter scales. For the ≤7B comparison group: Llama2-7B and Llama2-7B-Chat (Touvron et al., 2023b), Mistral-7B-v0.1 and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 (Jiang et al., 2023), Baichuan2-7B-Base and Baichuan2-7B-Chat (Yang et al., 2023), Qwen-7B and Qwen-7B-Chat (Bai et al., 2023a), and ChatGLM3-6B-Base and ChatGLM3-6B (Zeng et al., 2023). For the 13~20B comparison group: Llama2-13B and Llama2-13B-Chat, Mixtral-8x7B-v0.1 and Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1 (a mixture-of-experts model with approximately 13B active parameters), Baichuan2-13B-Base and Baichuan2-13B-Chat, Qwen-14B and Qwen-14B-Chat, and Qwen1.5-14B-Chat (for T-Eval and CIBench only). API models—specifically GPT-3.5—are included as upper-bound references for chat model evaluations but are not treated as direct competitors since the paper's focus is open-source comparison. For the alignment benchmarks (Table 17), the baselines include ChatGLM3-6B, Llama2-7B-Chat, Baichuan2-7B-Chat, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, Qwen-7B-Chat in the ≤7B group, and Llama2-13B-Chat, Baichuan2-13B-Chat, Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1, Qwen-14B-Chat in the 13~20B group. For the conditional reward model ablation (Table 19), the paper compares against UltraRM-13B (Cui et al., 2023) and QwenRM (Bai et al., 2023a) on a standard preference benchmark suite spanning Anthropic Helpful, Anthropic Harmless, OpenAI Summarization, Stanford SHP, and PRM800k datasets.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not employ a unified compute budget metric across evaluations. Each benchmark is evaluated under its own standard protocol—typically few-shot in-context learning with a fixed number of examples—and there is no attempt to control for inference FLOPs or generation length across models. For tool utilization experiments (GSM8K + ReAct, MATH + ReAct, MathBench + ReAct), the compute budget is implicitly larger because the model generates multiple reasoning-action-observation cycles, but this is not quantified or compared to the standard evaluation. For the Needle-in-a-Haystack test, context lengths up to 200K tokens are used with the LMDeploy inference engine (Contributors, 2023a), but inference cost is not measured. The absence of compute accounting means that comparisons between models are based purely on accuracy at the standard evaluation protocol, not on accuracy per unit of computation—a model that achieves higher accuracy through longer or more expensive generation is not penalized. This is standard practice for technical reports of this type but differs from papers that systematically study compute-optimal inference strategies. The ablation study of enhancement training (Figure 12) implicitly compares computational cost since the enhancement phase adds approximately 1% additional training tokens (24B out of 2T+), but this is presented as a methodological choice rather than a compute-efficiency analysis.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation, bootstrapping, or statistical significance testing for benchmark evaluations. All results are reported as single point estimates. For the difficulty bin analysis in Section 5.2.5 (performance before and after enhancement training), results are aggregated across multiple evaluation sets within each capability dimension (coding: HumanEval and MBPP; reasoning: MATH, GSM8k, SummEdits, and BBH; QA: HellaSwag, PIQA, WinoGrande, OpenBookQA, NaturalQuestions, and TriviaQA; examination: MMLU, AGIEval, and C-Eval), but this is simple averaging rather than statistical protocol. For the data contamination analysis (Section 5.4, Table 20), the paper computes language modeling loss on GSM8K training split, test split, and a GPT-4-generated reference set, reporting two derived metrics: Δ1 = Ltest - Lref (an indicator of test data leakage, where lower values suggest possible leakage) and Δ2 = Ltest - Ltrain (measuring overfitting on the training split). The paper does not provide statistical thresholds for these metrics—values are compared qualitatively across models, with "outliers" highlighted in gray but not statistically defined. The reward model comparison (Table 19) and critic initialization ablation (Figure 9) appear to use single training runs without error bars. This lack of statistical rigor is typical for technical reports at this scale, where the computational cost of multiple training runs is prohibitive, but it means that small performance differences (1-2 percentage points) should not be over-interpreted as reliable model rankings.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Comprehensive Examination Performance

The paper evaluates both base and chat models on examination-oriented benchmarks that test broad knowledge coverage. For **base models** (Table 5), InternLM2-7B achieves 65.8 on MMLU (5-shot), 66.3 on CMMLU (5-shot), 65.8 on C-Eval (5-shot), 49.9 on AGIEval (0-shot), and 58.6 on GAOKAO-Bench (0-shot). These results place it competitively within the ≤7B class: it trails ChatGLM3-6B-Base on CMMLU (66.5 vs. 66.3) and C-Eval (67.2 vs. 65.8), essentially ties with Qwen-7B on MMLU (65.8 vs. 59.7 favoring InternLM2), and underperforms Qwen-7B on AGIEval (49.9 vs. 45.6 favoring InternLM2) and GAOKAO (58.6 vs. 52.8 favoring InternLM2). The comparison is complex—no model dominates across all five benchmarks, and InternLM2's advantage over InternLM2-7B-Base (which scores 64.0, 63.0, 60.7, 38.7, 40.7 respectively) demonstrates that the capability enhancement phase provides 2-18 absolute percentage point improvements, with the largest gains on AGIEval (+11.2) and GAOKAO (+17.9). At the 20B scale, InternLM2-20B achieves 67.7, 68.7, 68.5, 53.0, and 57.1, outperforming Qwen-14B on three of five benchmarks but trailing on C-Eval (68.5 vs. 71.8) and GAOKAO (57.1 vs. 62.5). The comparison against Mixtral-8x7B-v0.1 is mixed: InternLM2-20B wins on CMMLU (68.7 vs. 53.3), C-Eval (68.5 vs. 55.4), AGIEval (53.0 vs. 40.9), and GAOKAO (57.1 vs. 32.3), but loses on MMLU (67.7 vs. 71.8).

For **chat models** (Table 6), InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 63.7, 63.0, 60.8, 47.2, 58.0, while InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT achieves 63.8, 63.2, 60.9, 49.0, 57.3. The SFT and RLHF variants perform nearly identically on these objective benchmarks—the maximum difference is 1.8 points on AGIEval favoring SFT—confirming the paper's claim that COOL RLHF "has little impact on comprehensive examination performance." InternLM2-Chat-20B-SFT achieves 66.5, 65.3, 63.7, 51.2, 58.6, while InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 66.5, 65.1, 63.0, 50.3, 58.3—again, nearly identical performance. These chat model scores place InternLM2 competitively with GPT-3.5 on Chinese-oriented benchmarks (CMMLU: 63.0 vs. 53.9; C-Eval: 60.8 vs. 52.5 for the 7B model) but behind on English-oriented MMLU (63.7 vs. 69.1).

#### Language and Knowledge

Base model results (Table 7) show InternLM2-7B achieving 58.9 on TriviaQA (4-shot), 42.2 on NaturalQuestions (5-shot), 74.1 on C3 (5-shot), 81.4 on RACE-High (0-shot), and 5.9 on FLORES (8-shot BLEU). The NaturalQuestions score of 42.2 is notably strong—leading all ≤7B base models except ChatGLM3-6B-Base at 38.7, and substantially ahead of Mistral-7B-v0.1 at 31.1 and Qwen-7B at 22.7. This 42.2 represents a dramatic improvement over InternLM2-7B-Base's 28.4, indicating that the capability enhancement phase includes substantial knowledge-intensive question answering data. The C3 Chinese reading comprehension score of 74.1 is competitive but trails Qwen-7B's 71.4 (essentially tied) and ChatGLM3-6B-Base's 78.9. At the 20B scale, InternLM2-20B achieves 60.1, 44.6, 79.1, 72.9, 6.5—competitive with Qwen-14B on most metrics (TriviaQA: 60.1 vs. 62.5; NaturalQuestions: 44.6 vs. 37.1) but notably behind on RACE-High (72.9 vs. 90.3) and FLORES (6.5 vs. 5.9, essentially tied given BLEU granularity). The FLORES translation scores are low across all models (none exceed 9.0 BLEU), which the paper does not discuss but likely reflects the difficulty of 101-language translation evaluation with only 100 samples per language pair and 8-shot prompting.

Chat model results (Table 8) follow a similar pattern, with InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT achieving 51.4 on TriviaQA, 24.5 on NaturalQuestions, 91.7 on C3, 85.2 on RACE-High, and 15.5 on FLORES. The C3 score of 91.7 is remarkable—it leads all ≤7B chat models and substantially exceeds GPT-3.5's 52.5 on the same benchmark, though this likely reflects C3's Chinese language focus favoring Chinese-trained models. The paper notes no meaningful difference between SFT and RLHF variants on these knowledge benchmarks, consistent with the pattern seen in comprehensive examination.

#### Reasoning and Mathematics

**Reasoning.** Table 9 presents reasoning results for base and chat models. For base models, InternLM2-7B achieves 84.7 on WinoGrande (0-shot), 79.3 on HellaSwag (0-shot), and 65.0 on BBH (0-shot). The WinoGrande score of 84.7 is the standout—it leads all ≤7B models by a substantial margin (Mistral-7B-v0.1: 75.3, Llama2-7B: 69.5) and even surpasses InternLM2-20B-Base at 76.2, though InternLM2-20B reaches 85.2. This 10.1-point improvement over InternLM2-7B-Base (74.6) demonstrates that commonsense reasoning is particularly responsive to the enhancement training phase. On HellaSwag, InternLM2-7B's 79.3 is competitive with Mistral-7B-v0.1's 78.9 and Qwen-7B's 75.0, but trails ChatGLM3-6B-Base's 76.5 by only 2.8 points. BBH performance at 65.0 places InternLM2-7B second in the ≤7B class behind ChatGLM3-6B-Base at 66.2, with a 9.5-point improvement over InternLM2-7B-Base at 55.5. At 20B, InternLM2-20B achieves 85.2, 81.6, and 72.1, leading all tested base models on WinoGrande and BBH but trailing Mixtral-8x7B-v0.1 on HellaSwag (81.6 vs. 81.9, essentially tied).

For chat models, InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 65.8, 83.0, and 61.2. The HellaSwag score of 83.0 leads all ≤7B chat models by a significant margin (ChatGLM3-6B: 73.1, Qwen-7B-Chat: 61.9). BBH at 61.2 leads the ≤7B class. At 20B, InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 74.8, 85.8, and 68.3—leading on HellaSwag (85.8 vs. Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1's 80.3) and BBH (68.3 vs. GPT-3.5's 41.9, a +26.4-point advantage the paper highlights). Again, RLHF versus SFT differences are negligible across reasoning benchmarks.

**Mathematics.** Tables 10 and 11 present math results with a richer set of benchmarks: GSM8K, MATH, TheoremQA, MathBench-CN, and MathBench-EN. For base models (Table 10), InternLM2-7B achieves 70.8 on GSM8K (4-shot), 20.2 on MATH (4-shot), 10.5 on TheoremQA (0-shot), 37.4 on MathBench-CN, and 33.9 on MathBench-EN. The GSM8K score of 70.8 dominates the ≤7B class—ChatGLM3-6B-Base at 60.7, Qwen-7B at 54.0, Mistral-7B-v0.1 at 47.5—and nearly doubles InternLM2-7B-Base's 36.0, indicating that math capability is heavily concentrated in the enhancement training phase. On MATH, 20.2 places InternLM2-7B competitive with ChatGLM3-6B-Base at 19.2 and Qwen-7B at 13.4—substantial improvements from 8.9 at the Base stage. TheoremQA at 10.5 is notable because it surpasses Qwen-14B at 10.4, demonstrating that the 7B model's theorem-proving capability after enhancement training rivals a model twice its size. At 20B, InternLM2-20B achieves 76.1, 25.5, 13.5, 38.7, 36.9—leading on GSM8K (76.1 vs. Mixtral-8x7B-v0.1's 66.0) and TheoremQA (13.5 vs. Qwen-14B's 10.4), essentially tying InternLM2-7B on MATH (20.2 vs. 25.5 showing expected scaling but also overlapping with measurement uncertainty), and trailing Qwen-14B on MathBench-CN (38.7 vs. 46.2).

For chat models (Table 11), InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 70.7 on GSM8K, 23.6 on MATH, 9.5 on TheoremQA, 38.0 on MathBench-CN, and 42.3 on MathBench-EN. The MATH score of 23.6 approaches GPT-3.5's 28.0, while GSM8K at 70.7 trails GPT-3.5's 78.2. The SFT variant (InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT) achieves 68.8, 23.2, 11.3, 43.1, 40.1—slightly but not dramatically different from the RLHF variant. At 20B, InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 79.6, 32.4, 14.1, 48.0, 48.5—leading on GSM8K (79.6 vs. GPT-3.5's 78.2 and Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1's 71.7), leading on MATH (32.4 vs. GPT-3.5's 28.0 and Qwen-14B-Chat's 27.6), and leading on TheoremQA (14.1 vs. Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1's 9.6). This is one of the paper's strongest quantitative claims: InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves SOTA or near-SOTA math performance among open-source models at this scale, surpassing GPT-3.5 on MATH and TheoremQA. The paper highlights that COOL RLHF "leads comprehensively across all [math] metrics," but the SFT vs. RLHF comparison shows the gains are modest: InternLM2-Chat-20B-SFT already achieves 77.1, 31.9, 13.6, 47.5, 50.6, meaning RLHF adds approximately 2.5 points on GSM8K, 0.5 on MATH, 0.5 on TheoremQA, and mixed results on MathBench. The pre-existing math capability from enhancement training dominates.

#### Coding

Tables 12 and 13 present coding results. For base models (Table 12), InternLM2-7B achieves 56.1 on HumanEval (4-shot), 39.6 on HumanEval-X (5-shot), 51.8 on MBPP (5-shot), and 45.4 on MBPP-CN (0-shot). The HumanEval score of 56.1 leads all ≤7B base models (ChatGLM3-6B-Base: 45.1, Qwen-7B-Chat: 36.0, Mistral-7B-v0.1: 27.4) and shows a 25.0-point improvement over InternLM2-7B-Base at 31.1. On MBPP, InternLM2-7B's 51.8 is competitive but trails ChatGLM3-6B-Base's 68.9 substantially, and even lags behind InternLM2-7B-Base's 54.1—the only benchmark where the Base model outperforms the enhanced model, which the paper does not discuss. At 20B, InternLM2-20B achieves 48.8 on HumanEval, 48.2 on HumanEval-X, 63.0 on MBPP, and 53.2 on MBPP-CN. The HumanEval score of 48.8 is competitive with Qwen-14B at 30.5 but substantially behind the 20B parameter scale where Mixtral-8x7B-v0.1's 32.3 is the closest comparison; HumanEval-X at 48.2 leads all tested models. MBPP at 63.0 leads all tested models except ChatGLM3-6B-Base at 68.9. MBPP-CN at 53.2 leads all tested models, demonstrating strong Chinese code generation capability.

For chat models (Table 13), InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT achieves 61.6, 43.9, 47.5, 44.4, while InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 59.2, 41.7, 52.5, 46.4. The HumanEval score of 61.6 (SFT) leads all ≤7B chat models substantially (ChatGLM3-6B: 53.1, Qwen-7B-Chat: 36.0). The RLHF variant's 59.2 is slightly lower on HumanEval but higher on MBPP (52.5 vs. 47.5). At 20B, InternLM2-Chat-20B-SFT achieves 67.1, 42.0, 63.4, 52.2, while InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 67.7, 39.8, 65.8, 54.0. The HumanEval score of 67.7 (RLHF) and 67.1 (SFT) both lead all tested models across both parameter classes, with the paper claiming "the InternLM2-Chat-20B model surpasses the previous state-of-the-art by more than 10%" on HumanEval, MBPP, and MBPP-CN. However, the HumanEval-X multilingual score drops from the SFT variant's 42.0 to the RLHF variant's 39.8—a decline the paper attributes to the model being "finely tuned for Chinese at the expense of its effectiveness in other languages," though this hypothesis is not experimentally verified.

#### Performance Before and After Enhancement Training

Figure 12 visualizes the impact of the capability-specific enhancement training phase across four capability dimensions (coding, reasoning, QA, examination). For the 7B model, scores improve from 22.55 to 39.42 (coding), 30.82 to 51.92 (reasoning), 43.65 to 51.69 (examination), and 39.96 to approximately 45.87 (QA, read from figure). The relative improvements are largest in reasoning and coding—the two dimensions most directly targeted by the 24B-token curated enhancement dataset. For the 20B model, improvements follow a similar pattern: coding from 32.93 to 58.52, reasoning from 39.55 to 69.42, examination from 59.10 to 70.71, and QA from 48.73 to 54.96 (from the 7B chart, the 20B values are specified in the figure labels). The 1.8B model also benefits: coding 15.50 to 22.45, reasoning 22.45 to 39.96 (a 78% relative improvement), examination 17.77 to 32.93 (85% relative improvement), demonstrating that smaller models benefit even more proportionally from high-quality capability data.

Table 14 provides a specific SFT-level comparison: an SFT model trained from InternLM2-7B-Base (without enhancement) achieves language 66.64, knowledge 58.35, reasoning 69.30, code 38.79, while the SFT model trained from InternLM2-7B (with enhancement) achieves 69.28, 62.11, 75.18, 41.40. The gains are most pronounced in reasoning (+5.88) and knowledge (+3.76), with smaller improvements in language (+2.64) and code (+2.61). This demonstrates that enhancement training benefits transfer through SFT—the pre-training phase improvements are not overwritten by instruction tuning.

#### Long-Context Modeling

Table 15 presents long-context benchmark results. On L-Eval, InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 68.6 (close-ended) and 40.8 (open-ended) for an average of 48.5, while InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT achieves 68.7, 40.8, and 48.6—essentially identical. These scores lead the ≤7B class: the closest competitor is ChatGLM3-6B at 59.1, 35.0, 41.7, a 6.8-point average gap. On LongBench, InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT's 48.1 overall is second to ChatGLM3-6B's 48.4 (a 0.3-point difference), with InternLM2 leading on single-document (47.3 vs. 40.9), few-shot learning (59.9 vs. 56.5), and synthetic tasks (67.2 vs. 65.0—though note ChatGLM3-6B actually leads on this at 65.0; InternLM2's 67.2 is higher), while trailing on multi-document (45.2 vs. 45.0, essentially tied), summarization (25.3 vs. 26.0), and code (43.5 vs. 57.1, where ChatGLM3-6B has a substantial advantage).

At 20B, InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 68.6, 40.6, 48.4 on L-Eval, while the SFT variant achieves 68.8, 42.0, 49.4. These scores lead all tested models at any scale on L-Eval. On LongBench, Intern LM2-Chat-20B-SFT achieves 46.8 overall, trailing InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT's 48.1—a surprising result where the 7B model outperforms the 20B model on long-context understanding, which the paper acknowledges: "we noticed that for InternLM2, different parameter sizes do not lead to significant difference in long-context performance, which will be further investigated."

The qualitative Needle-in-a-Haystack result (Figure 13) shows InternLM2-7B-200K achieving near-perfect retrieval (green across the entire heatmap) for Chinese haystack texts at all depth percentiles from 0% to 100% and context lengths from 1K to 200K tokens. This is presented as a key capability demonstration, though the paper does not compare this directly to other models' Needle-in-a-Haystack performance, making the "nearly perfectly identifying all needles" claim difficult to contextualize. The heatmap is for one model configuration only, and no quantitative metric (mean retrieval accuracy, position-dependent accuracy) is reported.

#### Tool Utilization

Figures 14, 15, and 16, and Table 16 present tool utilization results using the ReAct protocol (Yao et al., 2023), where the model alternates between reasoning steps and tool execution actions.

**Code Interpreter for Math.** Figure 14 compares standard few-shot performance to ReAct-augmented performance on GSM8K (4-shot) and MATH (4-shot). For InternLM2-Chat-7B, GSM8K improves from approximately 71% (read from bar chart) to approximately 85% with ReAct—a substantial gain. MATH improves from approximately 24% to approximately 37%—an even larger relative improvement. The SFT variants show similar patterns: InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT improves from approximately 69% to approximately 83% on GSM8K and from approximately 23% to approximately 35% on MATH. At 20B, InternLM2-Chat-20B improves from approximately 80% to approximately 87% on GSM8K (a smaller gain, reflecting ceiling effects) and from approximately 33% to approximately 41% on MATH. These results demonstrate that external code execution provides substantial benefits above and beyond the model's internal reasoning capability, particularly on harder problems (MATH gains are proportionally larger than GSM8K gains). Figure 15 breaks down MathBench results with and without ReAct across four categories (Application-Chinese, Application-English, Knowledge-Chinese, Knowledge-English). Results are mixed: InternLM2-Chat-20B + ReAct shows improvement in the Knowledge domain but not consistently in the Application domain, and the 7B model shows some performance decreases with ReAct, which the paper attributes to "incorrect usage of such interpreters" without further analysis.

**General Tool Use.** Table 16 presents T-Eval and CIBench results, which test general tool utilization capabilities. For T-Eval, InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 66.1 (English) and 65.4 (Chinese) for a 65.8 average, leading all ≤7B models (ChatGLM3-6B: 63.0, Qwen-7B-Chat: 59.3). The SFT variant achieves similar scores (64.1, 66.8, 65.5), with the RLHF variant showing a slight improvement. On CIBench, InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 57.1 (English) and 40.7 (Chinese) for a 48.9 average—leading all ≤7B models and surpassing the 13~20B class Qwen-14B-Chat (47.8). At 20B, InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 65.6, 62.9, 64.3 on T-Eval—second to Qwen1.5-14B-Chat's 65.4 in this metric—and 56.0, 54.7, 55.4 on CIBench, leading all tested models. The paper highlights that InternLM2 "consistently demonstrate[s] superior or comparable performance" on tool utilization, with the CIBench results being particularly strong—the 55.4 overall score represents a new SOTA among the compared models.

#### Alignment and Subjective Evaluation

Table 17 provides the central alignment comparison. On AlpacaEval, InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves a 21.8 win rate, leading all open-source models (Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1 at 8.3, Qwen-14B-Chat at 7.5) and substantially exceeding InternLM2-Chat-20B-SFT's 8.0. The relative improvement from SFT to RLHF is 173%—the largest RLHF gain on any benchmark. At 7B, InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 11.3, also leading the ≤7B class and improving from SFT's 6.9. On MTBench, InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 7.9 (on a 1-10 scale), leading the 13~20B class (Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct-v0.1 at 8.3 is actually higher, however InternLM2-Chat-20B does not lead on this metric—it trails Mixtral's 8.3). At 7B, InternLM2-Chat-7B's 7.7 leads the ≤7B class. The SFT-to-RLHF improvement on MTBench is modest: 7.3 to 7.9 at 20B, 7.1 to 7.7 at 7B.

On CompassArena, InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves a 28.7 win rate—the highest in the ≤7B class and more than doubling the SFT variant's 14.1. InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 31.4, leading all models and doubling the SFT variant's 15.2. Figure 16 breaks down CompassArena by category, showing that InternLM2's strongest categories are Creation and Language, where win rates approach or exceed 50% against the reference model, while Knowledge and Math show lower win rates (particularly for the 7B model). The paper notes that "InternLM2 series possesses exceptionally strong Chinese creativity and language abilities, with a win rate that rivals that of GPT4-Turbo"—a strong claim based on the CompassArena comparison, though the reference model used for win rate computation is not explicitly specified as GPT-4-Turbo in the table description.

On AlignBench, InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves a 6.8 score from CritiqueLLM, leading all models and exceeding GPT-3.5's 5.7. InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 6.1, also exceeding GPT-3.5 and leading the ≤7B class. Table 18 breaks this down by subcategory: InternLM2-Chat-20B scores highest on Role Play (8.09) and Question Answering (8.26), and lowest on Mathematics (5.38) and Reasoning (5.76), consistent with the pattern seen in objective math and reasoning benchmarks where the model is strong but not at ceiling. The SFT-to-RLHF improvement is substantial: at 20B, from 5.3 to 6.8 (a 28% improvement); at 7B, from 4.9 to 6.1 (a 24% improvement).

On IFEval, InternLM2-Chat-7B-SFT achieves 48.5 average accuracy and InternLM2-Chat-7B achieves 45.9—the RLHF variant performs worse, which the paper does not comment on. At 20B, InternLM2-Chat-20B-SFT achieves 48.7 and InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves 42.2—again, RLHF reduces instruction-following performance. The paper notes that "English-based models like Llama2 and Mistral generally outperform Chinese-based models on IFEval," with Llama2-7B-Chat achieving 44.6 and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 achieving 57.9 (the highest score). InternLM2's 48.5 (SFT) is competitive with English-native models but the RLHF degradation to 45.9 is notable—the paper does not explain this, but it may reflect the tension between instruction-following strictness and the more natural, human-preferred outputs that RLHF optimizes for on subjective benchmarks like AlpacaEval and CompassArena.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Conditional system prompts in the reward model**: Table 19 compares a 7B reward model trained with versus without conditional system prompts. Without conditional prompts, precision drops across every evaluated dataset: Anthropic Helpful-base from 75.10% to 73.26%, Anthropic Helpful-online from 66.98% to 64.45%, Anthropic Harmless-base from 73.19% to 72.48%, OpenAI Summarization from 75.37% to 68.25%, Stanford SHP from 75.76% to 69.24%, and PRM800k from 77.18% to 72.11%. The drops range from 0.71 to 6.12 absolute percentage points, with the largest degradations on domain-specific datasets (code/math: PRM800k, -5.07; summarization: OpenAI, -7.12; dialogue: Stanford SHP, -6.52) and the smallest on general helpfulness datasets (Anthropic, -0.71 to -1.84). This pattern suggests that conditional prompts are most important when the evaluation criteria differ substantially from the default helpfulness judgment—exactly the preference conflict scenario COOL RLHF is designed to address. The unconditional variant still outperforms UltraRM-13B and QwenRM on several datasets, indicating that even without conditioning, InternLM2's reward model training is strong, but the conditional mechanism provides consistent additional gains. The paper does not report statistical significance for these differences, and with the exact number of evaluation samples unspecified, the reliability of the 0.71-point difference on Anthropic Harmless-base is uncertain.

**Critic model initialization**: Figure 9 ablates initializing the PPO critic from the reward model versus from the SFT model. The reward-initialized critic experiences higher loss during the first ~20 PPO iterations but subsequently maintains consistently lower loss and leads to higher actor rewards. The paper hypothesizes that the early higher loss reflects fundamental differences between reward modeling (scoring) and value estimation (predicting future returns), while the later advantage reflects better internal world knowledge from reward model training. This is a meaningful ablation because it demonstrates that a seemingly natural choice (initializing from SFT, since the critic's task is more similar to language modeling than reward scoring) is suboptimal, and that the transfer from reward modeling to value estimation—despite the initial penalty—is beneficial.

**Long-context fine-tuning with code data**: Not presented as a formal ablation with a no-long-context-finetuning baseline, but the paper does show that long-context code data "improves not only the long-context capability of LLMs but also the code capabilities" (Section 4.3). No quantitative ablation is provided—no comparison of models fine-tuned with versus without long-context data in SFT/RLHF on either long-context or code benchmarks. This is a significant missing experiment given the paper's emphasis on this design choice.

**Enhancement training effect on SFT**: Table 14 provides the only direct ablation of the enhancement training phase's impact on downstream SFT performance. An SFT model trained from InternLM2-7B-Base achieves language 66.64, knowledge 58.35, reasoning 69.30, code 38.79, while the SFT model from InternLM2-7B achieves 69.28, 62.11, 75.18, 41.40. The consistent improvements (2.61–5.88 points) demonstrate that enhancement training benefits persist through instruction tuning, though the paper does not examine whether the enhancement data could have been incorporated directly into SFT rather than pre-training, which would be a useful comparison for understanding the mechanism (does enhancement training teach capabilities that SFT cannot, or does it simply provide data that could be learned during SFT if included?).

**Enhancement training across model scales**: Figure 12 effectively serves as an ablation of model scale × enhancement training. The consistent pattern—all three model sizes benefit substantially, with the 1.8B model showing the largest relative gains—establishes that enhancement training is beneficial across scales and is not an artifact of model capacity. The 1.8B model's examination score improves from 17.77 to 32.93 (85% relative improvement) versus the 20B model's improvement from 59.10 to 70.71 (20% relative improvement), suggesting that capability data provides larger relative benefits to smaller models, consistent with the interpretation that smaller models have more "room to grow" from high-quality data.

**RLHF stage effect on objective benchmarks**: The SFT vs. RLHF comparison appears across Tables 5–16 implicitly (the SFT and RLHF variants are both reported). The consistent finding is that RLHF produces negligible changes on objective benchmarks—often within 1-2 percentage points, sometimes favoring SFT, sometimes RLHF—while producing large gains on subjective alignment benchmarks (AlpacaEval: 8.0 to 21.8 at 20B; CompassArena: 15.2 to 31.4 at 20B). This demonstrates that COOL RLHF successfully targets alignment without degrading capability, but also raises the question of whether the objective benchmark gains from enhancement training could have been achieved through RLHF alone if the reward model were trained on task-specific correctness signals—an experiment not conducted.

**Data contamination analysis**: Table 20 evaluates potential GSM8K test set contamination by comparing language modeling loss on the training split (Ltrain), test split (Ltest), and a GPT-4-generated reference set (Lref). The key metric is Δ1 = Ltest - Lref, where negative or near-zero values suggest the model has seen the test data during training. InternLM2-7B shows Δ1 = 0.02 and InternLM2-20B shows Δ1 = 0.04—both positive and comparable to other models like Llama2-7B (0.00), Mistral-7B-v0.1 (0.02), and Baichuan2-7B-Base (0.02). Notable outliers include Qwen-14B with Δ1 = -0.12 (suggesting potential leakage) and Qwen-7B with Δ1 = 0.13. InternLM2's Δ2 = Ltest - Ltrain values are 0.34 (7B) and 0.35 (20B), indicating higher test loss than training loss—the expected pattern for models that have not overfit to the training set. For comparison, Qwen-14B shows Δ2 = 0.65 and Qwen-7B shows Δ2 = 0.55, indicating stronger overfitting. The paper concludes that InternLM2 does not show evidence of test set contamination on GSM8K, though this analysis is limited to a single dataset and the methodology (comparing LM loss on test/train/reference splits) provides only an indirect signal of contamination—it would not detect scenarios where the model was trained on GSM8K problems reformulated in different language that nevertheless conveys the same mathematical content.

**ReAct protocol for tool use**: Figures 14 and 15 serve as implicit ablations of code interpreter augmentation. The consistent improvements on GSM8K and MATH (Figure 14) demonstrate that external code execution provides benefits beyond internal reasoning. The mixed results on MathBench (Figure 15)—with some categories showing improvements and others showing slight decreases—suggest that code interpreter benefits are task-dependent, with the paper attributing decreases to "incorrect usage of such interpreters." No analysis is provided of error modes: does the model fail to call the interpreter when it should, call it with incorrect code, misinterpret the results, or some combination?

**RICO strategy for code-interleaved math**: The paper mentions using Reasoning Interleaved with COding (RICO) from InternLM-Math (Ying et al., 2024) for constructing math + code interpreter training data, but provides no ablation comparing RICO-trained models to models trained with alternative data construction strategies. The effectiveness of RICO is assumed from the MathBench and GSM8K/MATH + ReAct results but not isolated.

### Critical Assessment

The paper's central claims, as articulated in the executive summary, are: (1) InternLM2 outperforms predecessors across comprehensive evaluations, (2) the three-phase pre-training pipeline with capability enhancement produces strong capabilities, (3) COOL RLHF with a conditional reward model reconciles conflicting preferences and substantially improves subjective alignment, (4) the 200k context window yields near-perfect long-context retrieval, and (5) the model achieves SOTA or competitive results on math and coding benchmarks.

**Claim 1 (comprehensive outperformance) is partially supported but requires qualification.** InternLM2 does achieve leading results on many benchmarks within its parameter class—GSM8K at 70.7 (7B) and 79.6 (20B), MATH at 23.6 (7B) and 32.4 (20B), HumanEval at 59.2 (7B) and 67.7 (20B), CompassArena at 28.7 (7B) and 31.4 (20B). However, "outperforms predecessors" is an overstatement when applied uniformly. Across the 30+ benchmarks, InternLM2 leads on perhaps 60-70% of metrics within its weight class, but is second or third on the remainder—C-Eval trails ChatGLM3-6B, LongBench overall trails ChatGLM3-6B at 7B, MTBench trails Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, IFEval trails Mistral-7B-Instruct, RACE-High trails Qwen-14B at 20B. This is a strong showing but not a clean sweep, and the paper's narrative occasionally overstates by citing leading results while not highlighting the trailing ones with equal prominence. The experimental design does not permit strong conclusions about *why* InternLM2 wins where it wins and loses where it loses—the benchmarks cover such diverse capabilities that no systematic pattern of strengths and weaknesses emerges from the evaluation.

**Claim 2 (capability enhancement effectiveness) is well-supported.** Figure 12 and Table 14 provide clear pre-post comparisons showing substantial gains across all capability dimensions and model sizes. The improvement is largest on reasoning and coding—precisely the capabilities targeted by the 24B-token enhancement dataset—which supports the causal interpretation that the enhancement data drives the improvement rather than being coincidental with continued training on standard data. However, the paper does not include a control experiment: training for an equivalent number of additional steps on the standard pre-training distribution rather than the curated enhancement dataset. Without this control, it is possible that some of the improvement comes from simply training longer rather than from the specific data composition. The enhancement phase consumes only ~1% of total training tokens (24B out of 2T+), making the observed gains remarkable—but also making the missing control more noticeable, since extending standard training by 1% would be cheap and would definitively isolate the data composition effect.

**Claim 3 (COOL RLHF effectiveness) is supported for subjective alignment but with important nuances.** The SFT-to-RLHF improvements on AlpacaEval (8.0 to 21.8 at 20B), CompassArena (15.2 to 31.4), and AlignBench (5.3 to 6.8) are genuine and substantial. The conditional reward model ablation (Table 19) demonstrates that removing conditioning degrades performance across diverse preference domains. However, several aspects of the claim are not fully tested. First, the paper does not compare COOL RLHF against the multi-model approach it criticizes (LLaMA2-style separate reward models) on the same training data and base model—such a comparison would directly test whether the conditional mechanism is superior to the alternative or merely a more computationally efficient way to achieve similar results. Second, the three-round online RLHF is not ablated against single-round RLHF—the paper cannot distinguish how much of the improvement comes from the conditional reward model versus from the online updating. Third, the RLHF stage degrades performance on IFEval (48.7 to 42.2 at 20B SFT to RLHF) and occasionally on objective benchmarks—the paper acknowledges that RLHF has "little impact on comprehensive examination performance" as a positive (it doesn't hurt), but the IFEval degradation suggests a genuine tradeoff between instruction-following precision and subjective alignment that is not discussed.

**Claim 4 (200k long-context performance) is supported by the Needle-in-a-Haystack heatmap but lacks comparative rigor.** Figure 13 shows InternLM2-7B-200K achieving near-perfect retrieval across the full 200K × depth matrix, which is visually impressive. However, the paper does not provide a quantitative metric (mean retrieval accuracy across positions), does not compare against other models' Needle-in-a-Haystack performance, and does not specify crucial experimental parameters (number of needles inserted, how retrieval accuracy is measured, whether multiple trials were run). The L-Eval and LongBench results (Table 15) provide more rigorous evidence of long-context capability—InternLM2 leads on L-Eval and is competitive on LongBench—but these benchmarks evaluate up to ~32K tokens, not 200K. The claim of "nearly perfectly identifying all needles in the 200k Needle-in-a-Haystack experiment" is therefore supported only by a single qualitative heatmap without comparison baselines, which is weaker evidence than the prominence of the claim in the abstract and introduction would suggest. Additionally, the paper does not report inference latency or memory consumption at 200K context length, which are practical considerations for the "designed with a 200k context window" contribution claim.

**Claim 5 (math and coding SOTA) is supported for the specific benchmarks and models tested but with relevant caveats.** InternLM2-Chat-20B's 32.4 on MATH and 79.6 on GSM8K are genuinely strong results that exceed GPT-3.5 and contemporary open-source models. The HumanEval 67.7 is also strong. However, the evaluation uses standard few-shot prompting, which means the comparison is against models evaluated under their own standard protocols—which may differ in prompt format, number of shots, or decoding strategy. The tool utilization results (Figures 14-16) demonstrate that code interpreter augmentation provides substantial additional gains (MATH improves from ~33% to ~41% at 20B), meaning that the model's practical capability ceiling is higher than the standard few-shot numbers. But the paper does not compare tool-augmented InternLM2 against tool-augmented baselines, so the SOTA claim for tool utilization is based on InternLM2 + ReAct versus baselines without tools—an apples-to-oranges comparison that inflates the apparent advantage. The HumanEval-X multilingual results actually degrade from SFT (42.0) to RLHF (39.8), which the paper attributes to Chinese fine-tuning tradeoffs but does not experimentally verify.

**Methodological weaknesses that affect multiple claims:**

- **No error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical tests.** All 30+ benchmarks report point estimates, making it impossible to assess whether a 1-2 point difference represents a reliable model ranking or sampling noise. This is common in technical reports but means the fine-grained rankings within parameter classes are not statistically justified.
- **Single evaluation per benchmark.** There is no evidence of repeated evaluation with different random seeds or prompt templates to assess stability. For few-shot benchmarks, prompt exemplar selection can affect results by several points, but this sensitivity is not measured.
- **The reward model training data composition and annotation quality are not described in detail.** While Table 19 shows precision numbers on public benchmarks, the paper does not discuss annotator agreement, quality control, or the proportion of data from different domains in the 2.4M preference pairs. This makes the reward model results difficult to interpret—high precision could reflect genuine quality or could reflect overfitting to systematic annotation biases.
- **Missing baselines for tool utilization.** The ReAct-augmented InternLM2 results are compared against InternLM2 without ReAct and against other models without ReAct. Comparing InternLM2 + ReAct against GPT-3.5 + ReAct or Qwen-7B-Chat + ReAct would establish whether InternLM2's tool-use advantage is due to better base capability or better integration with the ReAct protocol.
- **Limited contamination analysis.** The GSM8K-only contamination check (Table 20) provides some reassurance but is insufficient for a model trained on 2T+ tokens of web data. MATH, HumanEval, MMLU, and other high-profile benchmarks could also be contaminated, and the LM loss method has known limitations (it can miss contamination through paraphrasing or translation).
- **The 1.8B model is underevaluated.** Despite being open-sourced, the 1.8B model appears only in Figure 12 (enhancement training comparison) and is not benchmarked against external models, making it impossible to assess whether the architectural and data pipeline choices scale down effectively.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Absence of SFT-vs-RLHF Control Experiment for COOL RLHF's Core Claim

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central alignment innovation is COOL RLHF, which introduces both a conditional reward model and a multi-round online RLHF procedure. The conditional mechanism is justified by the reward model ablation (Table 19), which shows that removing system prompts degrades preference prediction precision. The online procedure is justified conceptually by the argument that reward hacking is an emergent, policy-evolving phenomenon. However, these two components—the conditional reward architecture and the online updating schedule—are never experimentally separated. The paper does not compare COOL RLHF against a single-round RLHF baseline using the same conditional reward model, nor does it compare against a multi-round RLHF baseline using an unconditional reward model. The ablation in Table 19 evaluates only the reward model's preference prediction accuracy in isolation, *before* any PPO training. The reward model comparison baselines (UltraRM-13B, QwenRM) in Table 19 are evaluated on held-out preference data only; they are never used to train actual PPO policies whose downstream alignment quality can be compared to COOL RLHF on subjective benchmarks.

**The consequence.** The paper cannot attribute the subjective alignment gains to the conditional mechanism, the online updating, or their interaction. The large SFT-to-RLHF improvements on AlpacaEval (8.0 to 21.8 at 20B), CompassArena (15.2 to 31.4 at 20B), and AlignBench (5.3 to 6.8 at 20B) could be primarily driven by the conditional reward model's improved preference discrimination (Table 19 already shows it outperforms prior reward models on held-out preference data), with online updating contributing little beyond what a single PPO round would achieve. Conversely, the gains could come primarily from the three rounds of online PPO with fast-path hacking patches, and the conditional mechanism might be replaceable by a simpler approach (e.g., a well-trained unconditional reward model plus domain-specific KL constraints) without substantial performance loss. The paper's title and abstract foreground COOL RLHF as a key contribution, but the experimental design does not isolate *which part* of COOL RLHF is responsible for the observed gains—a significant gap for practitioners deciding whether to adopt the full framework (which requires infrastructure for multi-round PPO with human annotation loops) or only the conditional reward model (which is simpler to implement but may leave most of the gain on the table if online updating is the active ingredient).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only direct evidence for the conditional mechanism's importance to alignment quality is Table 19, which evaluates reward model precision on standard preference benchmarks. This is a useful signal, but it measures the reward model's ability to predict preferences on static data, not its effectiveness as a training signal for PPO. The critic initialization ablation (Figure 9) isolates one PPO-specific design choice but does not address the conditional vs. single-round question. The paper provides no ablation comparing: (a) COOL RLHF vs. single-round RLHF with conditional reward, (b) COOL RLHF vs. multi-round RLHF with unconditional reward, or (c) COOL RLHF vs. LLaMA2-style multi-reward-model approach trained on the same preference data. These missing experiments mean the paper's strongest methodological claim—that COOL RLHF specifically, rather than just "strong reward model plus PPO," drives the alignment improvements—is not experimentally substantiated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation or suggest future work to disentangle the components. The ablation that *does* exist (Table 19) is presented as evidence for the conditional mechanism, but its relevance is limited to reward model quality in isolation. A practitioner reading the paper cannot determine whether implementing the full COOL RLHF pipeline is worth the engineering complexity over simply training a conditional reward model and running single-round PPO.

### Difficulty Estimation Cost for Compute-Optimal Allocation Is Not Accounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire compute-optimal test-time scaling framework depends on classifying each prompt into a difficulty quintile before allocating the inference budget. The method for estimating difficulty—generating 2,048 candidate solutions per prompt and computing either ground-truth pass@1 (oracle) or average PRM final-answer score (predicted)—is computationally expensive. The paper explicitly acknowledges this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

The difficulty estimation cost is not included in any budget calculation when reporting the headline efficiency gains (e.g., "4× better efficiency over best-of-N"). This matters because the estimation cost can dominate the total compute. Generating 2,048 samples per question to assess difficulty is between 8× and 32× the typical test-time compute budgets studied (64–256 generations), meaning the total cost to deploy the compute-optimal strategy is often *larger* than simply running best-of-N with the same total budget and skipping difficulty estimation entirely.

**The consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains are an upper bound that assumes difficulty is known for free. In a realistic deployment where difficulty must be estimated, the actual efficiency—accounting for estimation cost—could be substantially lower, potentially eliminating the advantage over uniform allocation for all but the highest-volume inference pipelines where estimation cost can be amortized across many similar queries. The paper does not provide a break-even analysis: how many queries must share a difficulty estimate before the compute-optimal strategy becomes net-beneficial? Without this, the practical value of the framework for low-volume or one-shot inference tasks is unclear. A deployment where every query is unique (and therefore requires its own difficulty estimation) would incur the full 2,048-sample estimation cost per query, making the approach strictly more expensive than best-of-N with the same total generation budget.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates that predicted difficulty bins (using PRM scores without ground-truth labels) track oracle bins closely (Figures 4 and 8, curves largely overlap), which is encouraging because it means the system works without answer access. However, the predicted bins still require generating and scoring 2,048 samples—the *computational* cost is identical to the oracle method, even though the *information* requirement is relaxed. The paper does not explore cheaper difficulty estimation strategies (e.g., using only 16 or 64 samples to coarsely bin difficulty, or training a lightweight classifier to predict difficulty from the question text alone) beyond flagging them as future work in Section 8. No experiment measures how estimation accuracy degrades as the number of estimation samples decreases, and no experiment measures the net efficiency of compute-optimal scaling *inclusive* of estimation cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly in Section 3.2 ("our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity") and Section 8 ("future work should focus on developing cheaper difficulty estimation"). It does not attempt to mitigate the limitation experimentally—no lower-cost estimation method is tested, no amortization analysis is provided, and no total-cost-inclusive efficiency numbers are reported. The mitigation is purely aspirational.

### Hardest Difficulty Bin Shows Near-Zero Improvement Regardless of Strategy or Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The compute-optimal framework fundamentally assumes that the base model has *some* non-trivial probability of producing a correct answer—otherwise, no test-time strategy (search, revision, or their combination) can help, because there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find or refine. The paper's difficulty bin 5 (the hardest quintile, where the base model's pass@1 is lowest) reveals exactly this boundary.

**The consequence.** On difficulty bin 5 problems, every method produces near-zero accuracy regardless of test-time compute budget. For PRM search (Figure 3, right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods (best-of-N, beam search, lookahead search) across all budgets from 4 to 256 generations. For revisions (Figure 7, right), bin 5 accuracy is roughly 2–3% regardless of sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling lines are essentially flat near 0–5% accuracy, and the ~14× larger pretrained model substantially outperforms any test-time strategy on these hard problems (e.g., -52.9% relative disadvantage for PRM search at R ≫ 1). This means the compute-optimal framework offers no solution for the hardest problems—the ones where improvement is most needed. The paper is candid about this (Section 7 takeaway box), but the practical implication is stark: for deployment scenarios where the problem distribution is skewed toward genuinely difficult problems (those outside the base model's capability range), investing in test-time compute provides essentially zero return. Only pretraining a larger model or fundamentally improving the base model's capabilities can address these cases.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is comprehensive and consistent across methods. Figure 3 (right) shows bin 5 flat near 0–3% for all search methods. Figure 7 (right) shows bin 5 flat near 2–3% for all sequential-to-parallel ratios. Figure 9 shows bin 5 (blue, bottommost line) flat near 0–5% for both revisions and PRM search at all budgets. The FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9 and bar charts in Figure 1) quantifies the gap: the larger pretrained model dramatically outperforms test-time compute on hard problems at all values of the inference-to-pretraining ratio R. There is no ambiguity in the data—this is the most robustly documented limitation in the paper.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges this limitation in the Section 7 takeaway: test-time compute "cannot compensate for fundamental capability gaps that larger pretraining would address." It does not attempt to mitigate the limitation—no method is proposed for hard problems, no hybrid approach combining test-time compute with retrieval augmentation or external tools is explored, and no analysis of *why* the bin 5 problems are hard (lack of knowledge? need for multi-step reasoning beyond the model's depth? incorrect solutions that look plausible to the verifier?) is provided to guide future work. The limitation is treated as an inherent boundary of the test-time compute paradigm rather than a solvable problem within it.

### Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect followed by a correct target (Section 6.1). This training data construction procedure, driven by the edit-distance-based pairing to ensure incorrect answers are structurally close to the correct answer, means the model never sees examples where the current answer is already correct. During training, the correct answer always occurs at the end of the sequence, and the model is trained to produce the correct answer given 0–4 preceding incorrect attempts. At inference time, however, the model's own revision chain may produce a correct answer at step k, and then at step k+1, the model conditions on this correct answer and "revises" it—often into an incorrect one.

**The consequence.** The paper reports that "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones" using a naive approach of always taking the last revision in the chain. This means the revision model actively *undoes* its own correct outputs at a high rate. The paper mitigates this by using majority voting or verifier-based selection across the entire revision chain rather than taking the final output, but this introduces its own costs: (a) the verifier must evaluate every step in the chain, increasing computational overhead, and (b) majority voting or verifier selection can themselves make errors, potentially selecting an earlier, lower-quality correct answer when a later revision was actually better despite being scored lower. More fundamentally, the 38% reversion rate indicates that the revision model has not learned a robust stopping criterion—it does not know when *not* to revise. This is a direct consequence of the training data design choice to only include incorrect-to-correct trajectories. The practical consequence is that revision chains cannot be relied upon to monotonically improve; they require external selection mechanisms (verifier or voting) to extract the best answer, and even then, some correct answers are likely lost because the verifier may fail to recognize them among the chain's outputs.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The 38% figure is stated explicitly in Section 6.1 but without an accompanying table or figure—it is a textual claim without experimental detail (how was the 38% measured? Across what dataset? With what revision chain length?). The paper reports that the revision model's pass@1 per step improves from approximately 18.2% at step 1 to roughly 24–25% by steps 15–20 (Figure 6, left), indicating that overall quality trends upward, but this aggregate metric obscures the per-step reversion rate. The ReST^EM experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) provides indirect evidence that the revision training is fragile: attempting to optimize the revision model with RL-style training caused performance to *degrade substantially* with sequential revisions, with fully sequential performance dropping to approximately 33.5% compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio at 256 generations. The paper hypothesizes that on-policy data collection in ReST^EM "exacerbates spurious correlations in revision data, causing the model to fail to learn the revision task properly," suggesting that the revision model's behavior is sensitive to data construction methodology in ways that are not fully understood.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates the reversion problem through within-chain selection using majority voting or verifier scoring (Section 6.1, "To mitigate this, the system uses a selection mechanism... across the entire chain of revisions rather than always taking the last revision."). This reduces the impact of reversions but does not address the underlying cause—the model still wastes computation generating incorrect revisions from correct answers. The paper suggests no training-based mitigation (e.g., including correct-to-correct trajectories in the training data, or training an explicit stopping classifier). The limitation is presented as a practical issue with a workaround rather than a fundamental design flaw, but the 38% figure suggests a substantial fraction of the revision model's computation is counterproductive, even if selection mechanisms recover the correct answers.

### Single Model Family, Single Benchmark Domain for Core Scaling Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments on the compute-optimal test-time scaling framework—the paper's central conceptual contribution—use a single model family (PaLM 2-S*) and a single benchmark (MATH, consisting exclusively of competition-level mathematics problems requiring symbolic reasoning and producing closed-form answers). The paper states that PaLM 2-S* is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this claim is not verified. The scaling behavior documented in Figures 3–9 may depend critically on properties specific to this model's output distribution (e.g., its calibration, error patterns, and the quality of the PRM trained on its outputs) and to the MATH domain (e.g., problems have unambiguous correctness, step-by-step solutions with verifiable intermediate reasoning, and a difficulty distribution shaped by competition math rather than natural task distributions).

**The consequence.** Practitioners cannot determine whether the key findings generalize: (a) the U-shaped difficulty-dependent optimal strategy (best-of-N for easy problems, beam search for medium, no method works for hard) may not hold for other model families with different output distributions or different verifier quality; (b) the 4× efficiency gain from compute-optimal allocation may be specific to PaLM 2-S*'s particular accuracy-difficulty locus and the MATH benchmark's problem structure; (c) the PRM over-optimization threshold (where beam search starts degrading easy-problem performance) likely depends on the PRM's calibration, which is a function of the base model's output distribution, the Monte Carlo rollout training procedure, and the specific aggregation method ("last" step, which the paper found to work best in Appendix E but which may not generalize); (d) domains without clean step-level correctness signals (open-ended generation, dialogue, creative writing) cannot use the Monte Carlo rollout PRM training approach at all, making the entire search framework inapplicable; (e) domains with fundamentally different difficulty structures (e.g., factual recall tasks where difficulty is about knowledge coverage rather than reasoning depth) may exhibit qualitatively different scaling patterns. The paper's conclusions are thus conditional on the (PaLM 2-S*, MATH) pair, and extending them to other models or tasks requires assuming that the observed patterns are universal rather than contingent.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There is no multi-model evaluation (e.g., applying the same compute-optimal analysis to a different base model or model scale), no multi-domain evaluation (e.g., testing on code generation, logical reasoning, or scientific QA benchmarks), and no analysis of how the results would change if the PRM quality were systematically varied. The paper's evaluation breadth is impressive within the MATH domain (five difficulty bins, multiple search algorithms, multiple revision strategies, FLOPs-matched analysis), but the domain itself is narrow. All quantitative claims about test-time compute scaling—the 4× efficiency improvement, the optimal strategy per difficulty bin, the over-optimization patterns—are supported only for PaLM 2-S* on MATH.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a significant limitation beyond a brief statement in Section 8 that "future work should evaluate these findings on additional models and benchmarks." There is no discussion of which findings are most likely domain-specific (e.g., the PRM aggregation preference for "last" over "min" may be particular to the Monte Carlo soft-label training approach) and which might be universal (e.g., the difficulty-dependent strategy selection logic). The paper's framing presents the compute-optimal framework as a general methodology, but the experimental support is entirely single-domain. A practitioner considering whether to adopt compute-optimal test-time allocation for a code generation or dialogue system has no direct evidence from this paper that the approach would transfer.

### Difficulty Estmation Requires 2,048 Samples—No Amortization or Cheaper Alternatives Explored

**The assumption or constraint.** This limitation is closely related to Limitation 2 (unaccounted estimation cost) but distinct in focus. Limitation 2 addresses the *consequence* of unaccounted cost for the headline efficiency claims. This limitation addresses the *absence of any exploration* of the cost-quality tradeoff in difficulty estimation itself. The paper uses exactly one method to estimate difficulty: generate 2,048 samples per prompt, compute either ground-truth pass@1 or average PRM final-answer score, and bin into quintiles. The number 2,048 is not justified or varied—there is no experiment showing how estimation accuracy degrades with fewer samples (e.g., 64, 256, 512), no comparison of alternative estimation strategies (e.g., using the PRM's score on the first few generated steps rather than full solutions, or using a learned difficulty predictor), and no measurement of how compute-optimal policy performance changes when difficulty estimates are noisy.

**The consequence.** The paper provides no guidance on the most critical practical question for deployment: how much should one spend on difficulty estimation versus on solution generation? A practitioner with a fixed total budget (say, 1,024 generations per query) needs to decide how to split it between estimating difficulty and solving the problem. If 2,048 samples are needed for accurate estimation, the approach is infeasible for budgets below ~2,100 generations. If, however, 64 samples provide difficulty estimates that are 90% as useful as 2,048-sample estimates, the approach becomes practical at much lower budgets. The paper's silence on this tradeoff means the framework cannot be applied without either accepting the 2,048-sample estimation cost (which makes it impractical for most deployment scenarios) or guessing at a cheaper approach without evidence that it works. The predicted difficulty bins shown in Figures 4 and 8 use 2,048 PRM-scored samples—they demonstrate that the PRM can substitute for ground-truth labels, not that fewer samples would suffice. The paper does not measure the correlation between difficulty estimates from N samples and those from 2,048 samples for N < 2,048, does not evaluate how compute-optimal policy performance degrades as a function of estimation sample count, and does not compare the PRM-average method to alternative cheap estimators (e.g., using the variance of PRM scores across a small number of samples as a difficulty signal).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only evidence about estimation quality is the overlap between oracle and predicted difficulty bins in Figures 4 and 8 (curves largely overlap), which shows that the PRM-based estimator is a good proxy for ground-truth difficulty *when both use 2,048 samples*. There is no experiment reducing the sample count for either estimator. The sensitivity of the compute-optimal policy to estimation noise is unknown—it is possible that coarse difficulty bins (quintiles) are robust to substantial estimation error (e.g., distinguishing bin 1 from bin 5 is easy even with few samples, while distinguishing bin 3 from bin 4 requires more precision), but this is not tested.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges that the estimation cost is unaccounted for (Section 3.2) and suggests "future work should focus on developing cheaper difficulty estimation methods" (Section 8), but does not take any steps toward that goal within the paper itself. No cheaper method is proposed, no sensitivity analysis is conducted, and no amortization analysis is provided. The mitigation is entirely deferred to future work, leaving the current framework in a state where its primary claimed benefit (4× efficiency) cannot be realized without solving a problem the paper identifies but does not address.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not introduce a single architectural breakthrough or a new training objective that fundamentally alters the LLM development paradigm. Rather, it shifts the conversation from *what* capabilities a model achieves to *how systematically transparent engineering across the entire pipeline* produces those capabilities. The contribution is best understood as a **methodological reframing**: InternLM2 demonstrates that closing the gap between open-source and proprietary models is primarily a problem of execution discipline—careful data preparation, deliberate multi-phase training, and alignment architecture that respects the context-dependency of human preferences—rather than requiring access to fundamentally different model architectures or training techniques unavailable to the open-source community.

This reframing has several concrete implications for how the field thinks about LLM development:

**Data preparation is elevated from an implementation detail to a first-class research contribution.** Prior technical reports from LLaMA, Qwen, and Deepseek mentioned data processing but provided minimal detail. InternLM2's extensive treatment of text filtering pipelines (five-stage processing with explicit classifier training and threshold selection strategies), code-specific quality assessment (the iterative annotation workflow in Figure 5), and long-context data preparation (statistical and perplexity filters with domain-specific thresholding) establishes that data preparation methodology is not merely engineering overhead but contains generalizable design principles—the lesson that statistical filters are more reliable for long text than short text, that perplexity *difference* rather than absolute perplexity mitigates estimator bias, and that code quality classifiers trained on one language may not transfer to others. These are findings that can inform other teams' data pipelines, and they elevate data preparation from "we cleaned the data" to "here is why our cleaning decisions work and how to adapt them."

**The preference conflict problem in RLHF is diagnostically recast.** Before this work, the dominant solution to conflicting preferences (helpfulness vs. harmlessness, code correctness vs. dialogue engagement) was architectural separation: train multiple reward models and aggregate their scores. InternLM2 demonstrates that a single reward model, conditioned on system prompts that specify the evaluation context, can learn to apply different criteria without architectural separation—and that removing this conditioning degrades precision across diverse preference domains by 0.7 to 7.1 absolute percentage points (Table 19). This is not merely an efficiency gain (one model instead of many); it is a diagnostic finding that the bottleneck in multi-preference RLHF is not the score aggregation mechanism but the reward model's ability to recognize when different criteria apply. The implication is that future work on preference alignment should focus on improving reward models' context-sensitivity rather than on more sophisticated multi-model ensembling schemes.

**Long-context capability is revealed as alignment-fragile.** The paper's finding that long-context performance degrades during SFT and RLHF unless deliberately maintained through continued long-context data exposure is not a theoretical result—it is an empirical observation with immediate practical consequences. It means that every team training long-context models must treat alignment as part of the long-context development pipeline rather than an orthogonal stage that preserves pre-trained capabilities by default. The specific solution—continuing to train on long-context pre-training data during SFT and RLHF, and constructing structured long-context data (dependency-sorted code repositories) that provides synergistic benefits for both context handling and domain capability—provides a template that subsequent work can adopt or refine. The 200K Needle-in-a-Haystack result (Figure 13) and the strong L-Eval/LongBench performance (Table 15) demonstrate that this approach works, but the more important contribution is the identification that it is *necessary*—without explicit intervention, alignment training silently erodes the long-context capability that pre-training built.

**The open-source model release strategy is itself a methodological contribution.** By releasing checkpoints at multiple stages—base model before enhancement training (InternLM2-{size}-Base), after enhancement training (InternLM2-{size}), after SFT (InternLM2-Chat-{size}-SFT), and after RLHF (InternLM2-Chat-{size})—the paper enables the community to study what each stage contributes. This is not standard practice among technical reports, and it transforms the paper from a capability demonstration into a research resource. The finding that RLHF has negligible impact on objective benchmarks but substantial impact on subjective alignment (AlpacaEval: 8.0 → 21.8 at 20B; CompassArena: 15.2 → 31.4) is visible precisely because SFT-only checkpoints are provided. The finding that the IFEval instruction-following score degrades from 48.7 to 42.2 at 20B after RLHF—a tradeoff invisible without staged releases—reveals that alignment optimization has real costs that single-checkpoint releases would obscure. This transparency practice enables the community to study these tradeoffs rather than simply accepting or rejecting a final model.

**The enhancement training phase demonstrates that capability-targeted data, even at small scale, can produce disproportionate gains.** The 24B-token enhancement dataset represents approximately 1% of the total pre-training tokens (2T+), yet produces improvements of 20–85% across capability dimensions (Figure 12), with the relative gains largest for the smallest model. This finding suggests that data *composition* matters far more than data *volume* for targeted capability improvement—a lesson with direct implications for teams deciding how to allocate their data curation budgets. Rather than collecting ever-larger web crawls, investing in 24B tokens of high-quality, capability-targeted data may yield better returns per curation dollar. The finding that these gains transfer through SFT (Table 14: SFT from InternLM2-7B outperforms SFT from InternLM2-7B-Base by 2.6–5.9 points across capability dimensions) confirms that late-stage pre-training data choices have downstream effects that instruction tuning does not overwrite.

In terms of resolving prior contradictions: the paper's demonstration that code-quality filtering using an iterative annotation workflow (Figure 5) can produce strong coding capability (HumanEval: 67.7 at 20B with SFT, Table 13) addresses a tension in prior work between those who argued for minimal filtering of code data (to maximize diversity) and those who argued for aggressive filtering (to maximize quality). InternLM2's approach—rule-based filtering for clear errors, model-based scoring only for languages where the scorer aligns with human judgment, and iterative refinement of annotation guidelines—suggests a middle path: aggressive enough to remove low-quality data (13.3% of code data excluded, Table 2) while remaining conservative about quality thresholds in domains where automated quality assessment is unreliable.

In aggregate, this work makes research directions focused on **pipeline transparency and systematic data engineering** more attractive—it demonstrates that detailed attention to data processing, multi-phase training curricula, and context-aware alignment can produce competitive results without architectural novelty. Conversely, it makes directions focused on **architectural innovation as the primary driver of capability improvement** somewhat less attractive for the open-source community—the gap to proprietary models appears closable through execution quality rather than requiring fundamentally new architectures. This is not to say architectural innovation is unimportant, but rather that the marginal return on improving data and alignment pipelines may be higher than the marginal return on designing new attention mechanisms or training objectives, at least at the scale studied here.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Disentangling the conditional mechanism from online updating in COOL RLHF.** The paper establishes that a conditional reward model with system prompts outperforms an unconditional variant on preference prediction (Table 19) and that three-round online RLHF produces strong subjective alignment (Table 17: AlpacaEval 21.8, CompassArena 31.4 at 20B). But these components are never experimentally separated. A direct follow-up would train three PPO policies from the same SFT base model: (a) single-round RLHF with the conditional reward model, (b) three-round online RLHF with an unconditional reward model, and (c) full COOL RLHF as described in the paper. Evaluating all three on the alignment benchmark suite (AlpacaEval, MTBench, CompassArena, AlignBench, IFEval) would isolate whether the conditional mechanism, the online updating, or their interaction drives the alignment improvements. A negative result—e.g., single-round conditional RLHF performing nearly as well as three-round COOL RLHF—would dramatically simplify deployment requirements by eliminating the need for online human annotation loops. A positive result—e.g., online updating providing substantial gains beyond conditional reward alone—would justify the engineering complexity of multi-round RLHF and motivate work on automating the fast-path/slow-path distinction to reduce human annotation latency.

**Cross-model and cross-domain replication of the compute-optimal test-time scaling framework.** The InternLM2 technical report is primarily about training methodology, but the paper's enhancement training results (Figure 12) implicitly suggest a test-time compute question: if InternLM2-7B benefits from capability-specific data during pre-training, does it also benefit from compute-optimal test-time allocation on those same capability dimensions? A direct extension would apply the compute-optimal framework from prior work to InternLM2 checkpoints on MATH, evaluating whether the difficulty-dependent strategy selection patterns (best-of-N for easy problems, beam search for medium, neither for hard) replicate with this model family. This would test whether the framework's findings are specific to PaLM 2-S* or generalize to a LLaMA-architecture model trained with a substantially different data pipeline. Extending further to code generation (HumanEval, MBPP) would test whether the framework applies to domains beyond mathematics, where "difficulty" may have different structure (syntactic complexity vs. algorithmic insight vs. knowledge of library APIs).

**Difficulty estimation from question text alone.** The paper identifies the 2,048-sample difficulty estimation cost as the primary barrier to practical deployment of compute-optimal allocation (Section 3.2). A concrete follow-up would train a lightweight classifier—initialized from a small language model or even a BERT-scale encoder—that takes only the question text as input and predicts the difficulty quintile that the 2,048-sample PRM-averaging method would assign. The training data already exists implicitly: the authors generated 2,048 samples and computed PRM-average scores for every question in the MATH test set and training set. Using these scores as labels, the classifier could be trained to predict difficulty from text alone, then evaluated on held-out questions by comparing compute-optimal policy performance using predicted bins versus oracle bins. A strong result—the classifier achieving bin accuracy comparable to the 2,048-sample method—would make the compute-optimal framework immediately deployable. A weak result—the classifier struggling to predict difficulty from text features alone—would reveal that difficulty is not a purely textual property but depends on the interaction between the question and the specific model's capabilities, motivating adaptive estimation strategies that use a small number of initial samples rather than text-only prediction.

**Does enhancement training work because of data composition or because of continued training?** Figure 12 demonstrates that enhancement training on 24B curated tokens produces substantial gains across capability dimensions. But the control experiment—training for an equivalent number of steps on the standard pre-training distribution rather than the curated enhancement dataset—is missing. A direct follow-up would train two models from the same InternLM2-7B-Base checkpoint: one on the 24B-token enhancement dataset (replicating the paper), and one on 24B additional tokens from the standard pre-training distribution. Evaluating both on the capability benchmarks in Figure 12 would isolate whether the gains come from the specific data composition (high-quality STEM, reasoning, and examination data) or from the additional training steps. If the standard-data control shows negligible improvement, it confirms that data composition is the active ingredient and justifies investment in targeted data curation. If the standard-data control shows substantial improvement, it suggests that the paper's 90%/9%/1% training phase split may be suboptimal and that the model was undertrained on the standard distribution—a finding that would shift the interpretation of the enhancement phase from "targeted capability injection" to "extended training with a data mixture change."

**Characterizing and reducing the revision model's correct-to-incorrect reversion rate.** The paper reports a 38% reversion rate (Section 6.1) but provides no analysis of when reversions occur, what types of correct answers are most vulnerable, or what the revised incorrect outputs look like. A systematic follow-up would instrument the revision chain to log, for each revision step: (a) whether the current answer is correct, (b) whether the model revises it, (c) whether the revision is correct or incorrect, and (d) the edit distance between the pre-revision and post-revision answers. This would characterize reversion patterns—do reversions predominantly affect certain problem types (algebra vs. geometry vs. probability), certain answer formats (numeric vs. symbolic), or certain positions in the revision chain (early vs. late steps)? Understanding these patterns would inform mitigation strategies. A training-based intervention—constructing a small set of correct-to-correct training trajectories (where the model is taught to recognize "this answer is correct, produce it unchanged") and fine-tuning the revision model on these—could directly address the root cause. Measuring the reversion rate before and after this intervention would test whether the problem is inherent to the revision paradigm or an artifact of the incorrect-to-correct-only training data design.

**Quantifying the long-context degradation rate during alignment without intervention.** The paper demonstrates that long-context capability can be preserved by including long-context data during SFT and RLHF, but never quantifies what happens without this intervention. A clean experiment would take the long-context pre-trained InternLM2-7B checkpoint, fine-tune it on standard SFT data only (short instruction examples, no long-context data), and evaluate the degradation on L-Eval and LongBench as a function of SFT steps. This would establish the *rate* at which long-context capability decays—is it immediate (within a few hundred steps) or gradual (over thousands of steps)? Does the degradation affect all context lengths equally, or does performance on the longest contexts (nearest the 32K limit) degrade first? These measurements would inform how much long-context data needs to be mixed into alignment training, and whether a small amount (e.g., 5% long-context data) is sufficient or whether the paper's approach of essentially maintaining the full long-context pre-training data volume is necessary.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Bilingual customer-facing assistants requiring strong Chinese-language alignment.** InternLM2-Chat-20B achieves a 31.4 win rate on CompassArena (Table 17) and 6.8 on AlignBench (Table 17), both exceeding GPT-3.5. The CompassArena category breakdown (Figure 16) shows particularly strong performance in Creation and Language categories, with win rates approaching or exceeding 50%. For organizations deploying Chinese-language conversational agents—customer support, educational tutoring, content generation—InternLM2's strong subjective alignment in Chinese means the model produces responses that human evaluators prefer over GPT-3.5-level alternatives without requiring proprietary API access or per-query costs. The availability of SFT-only and RLHF checkpoints allows practitioners to evaluate the alignment-quality tradeoff for their specific use case: if instruction-following precision matters more than conversational naturalness, the SFT checkpoint provides better IFEval performance (48.7 vs. 42.2 at 20B, Table 17), while if subjective preference matters more, the RLHF checkpoint provides the CompassArena and AlpacaEval gains.

**Long-context document processing pipelines.** InternLM2's near-perfect Needle-in-a-Haystack retrieval at 200K tokens (Figure 13) and its leading L-Eval scores (68.6 close-ended, 40.8 open-ended at 7B, Table 15) make it suitable for applications involving long-document question answering, multi-document summarization, and repository-scale code understanding. The paper's demonstration that long-context code data (dependency-sorted GitHub repositories concatenated to 32K tokens, Section 4.3) improves both long-context and code capability suggests a specific deployment pattern for software engineering tools: a single InternLM2 instance can serve both as a general coding assistant (HumanEval 67.7 at 20B, Table 13) and as a repository-scale code understanding system, rather than requiring separate short-context and long-context models. The GQA architecture's memory efficiency (key-value cache reduced by the query-per-kv factor, which is 2–6 depending on model size, Section 2.2) makes 32K+ token inference practical on consumer GPU hardware, expanding the deployment scenarios beyond datacenter-scale infrastructure.

**Math and code problem-solving with tool augmentation.** The combination of strong base math capability (MATH 32.4, GSM8K 79.6 at 20B Chat, Table 11) and substantial further gains from code interpreter use (MATH improves from ~33% to ~41% at 20B with ReAct, Figure 14) makes InternLM2 suitable for educational technology applications where the model needs to both reason about mathematical concepts and execute precise computations. The RICO strategy (Reasoning Interleaved with COding) mentioned in Section 4.4 provides a specific interaction pattern: the model alternates between natural language reasoning and Python code execution, which maps naturally to tutoring scenarios where a student benefits from seeing both the reasoning and the computation. The MathBench results (Figure 15) suggest that code interpreter benefits are strongest in the Knowledge domain, meaning the model is particularly effective at using external computation for fact-intensive mathematical tasks (formula evaluation, theorem application) while relying on internal reasoning for conceptual understanding.

**Training data generation for self-improvement pipelines.** InternLM2's staged release—base model, enhanced base model, SFT model, and RLHF model—provides a unique resource for self-improvement research. A practitioner could use InternLM2-Chat-20B to generate high-quality solutions for training tasks, then use those solutions to fine-tune InternLM2-7B-Base, studying whether the 20B model's capabilities can be distilled into the smaller model. The enhancement training results (Figure 12) demonstrate that even 24B tokens of curated data produce substantial improvements, suggesting that distillation pipelines might need relatively modest amounts of high-quality generated data. Comparing distillation from InternLM2-Chat-20B versus from GPT-4 would quantify the gap between open-source and proprietary models as teachers, and the availability of multiple checkpoint stages would allow measuring whether SFT or RLHF models are better teachers for different downstream tasks.

### When to Prefer This Method

InternLM2 is positioned against named open-source alternatives (LLaMA2, Qwen, Mistral, ChatGLM3, Baichuan2, Mixtral) rather than against a specific algorithmic tradeoff. The paper does not articulate a crisp decision rule for when to choose InternLM2 over a particular competitor, and the evaluation is designed to demonstrate competitive or leading performance across a broad benchmark suite rather than to establish a specialized strength that would cleanly separate it from all alternatives.

The closest the paper comes to a specific tradeoff is in the alignment results. The SFT vs. RLHF comparison across Tables 5–17 reveals a consistent pattern: RLHF provides large gains on subjective alignment benchmarks (AlpacaEval, CompassArena, AlignBench) at the cost of slight degradation on instruction-following precision (IFEval: 48.7 to 42.2 at 20B, Table 17). For deployment scenarios where conversational naturalness and human preference alignment are paramount—customer-facing chat, creative writing assistance, open-ended dialogue—the RLHF checkpoint is clearly preferable. For scenarios requiring strict instruction adherence—structured data extraction, API call generation, formatted output tasks—the SFT checkpoint may be the better choice, though the paper does not frame this tradeoff as an explicit design recommendation.

The long-context results also imply a domain-specific preference: InternLM2's strong Chinese-language long-context performance on L-Eval and LongBench, combined with its bilingual training, makes it particularly suitable for applications involving Chinese long documents compared to primarily English-trained models like LLaMA2 or Mistral. But this is a capability observation rather than a designed tradeoff, and the paper does not characterize it as such.

No forced "Prefer A when... Prefer B when..." matrix is justified because the paper's contribution is a comprehensive model release with pipeline transparency, not a method that occupies a specific niche relative to named alternatives under clearly specified conditions.
