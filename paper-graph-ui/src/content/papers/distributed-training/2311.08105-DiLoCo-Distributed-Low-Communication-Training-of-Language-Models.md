# DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2311.08105](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08105)

## 🎯 Pitch

Language models can be trained across poorly connected, geographically separate clusters with 500× less communication than synchronous training—and still match or beat its perplexity. The key is letting each 'island' of devices train independently for hundreds of steps using AdamW, then combining their progress with an outer Nesterov momentum step.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Distributed Low-Communication (DiLoCo)** training, a distributed optimization algorithm that enables language model training across poorly connected islands of devices by performing many local optimization steps between infrequent global synchronizations. The approach is evaluated on the C4 dataset using decoder-only transformers (60M–400M parameters) adapted from the Chinchilla architecture, combining AdamW as the inner optimizer with Nesterov momentum as the outer optimizer — a design choice that replaces standard Federated Averaging's SGD outer step with a momentum-based update applied to parameter deltas spanning hundreds of local steps. DiLoCo on 8 workers matches fully synchronous optimization while communicating 500 times less (once every 500 inner steps versus every step) and achieving comparable or better perplexity — for instance, 15.02 versus 15.30 for the 8× batch-size baseline — while also demonstrating robustness to non-i.i.d. data distributions, varying numbers of replicas up to 64, and dynamic changes in available compute resources over time. The paper establishes that test-time architectural equivalence (identical inference cost to a synchronously trained model) can be preserved while dramatically reducing communication, though the gains come with diminishing returns beyond 8 workers and reduced data efficiency relative to simply running more synchronous updates.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Training Large Language Models Requires Tightly Coupled Supercomputers

The fundamental tension this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **the standard recipe for training large language models assumes all accelerators are in the same building, connected by high-bandwidth cables, and synchronized at every single optimization step.** This assumption grows increasingly untenable as models scale. The paper opens by characterizing the practical reality of modern LLM training:

> "At modern scale, training via standard back-propagation poses unprecedented engineering and infrastructure challenges. To start, several thousands of devices need to be powered and be placed at the same physical location; and interconnected with high-bandwidth cables to minimize latency."

This is not merely an inconvenience — it is a structural constraint on who can train large models and how. The paper enumerates several specific failure modes of the standard paradigm:

- **Co-location requirements**: All thousands of accelerators must be in one physical facility. This limits training to organizations that can build or rent massive dedicated clusters, excludes geographically distributed resources (e.g., university clusters spread across campuses, or cloud instances across regions), and creates single points of failure for power, cooling, and networking infrastructure.

- **Synchronization fragility**: In standard data-parallel training, every device exchanges gradients at every optimization step. If one device among thousands fails or slows down, it risks halting the entire training run or introducing subtle numerical inconsistencies. The paper notes this means "the more devices that are used for each synchronous training step, the more chances there are that one of them fails."

- **Hardware heterogeneity is wasted**: Standard synchronous training assumes identical accelerators (same speed, same memory, same topology). Organizations often have access to diverse hardware — newer GPUs alongside older ones, TPUs in one region and GPUs in another — but standard parallelism cannot effectively pool these heterogeneous resources into a single training run.

- **Orchestration complexity**: The paper points to "careful software engineering ... to orchestrate the passage of gradients, parameters and intermediate states between these devices at each optimization step, keeping all devices fully utilized." This orchestration layer — handling communication scheduling, gradient all-reduce, and fault tolerance — is itself a major engineering investment that becomes more brittle as scale increases.

The consequence is not just inconvenience but a **fundamental barrier to scaling**. If the only way to train a larger model is to assemble a larger co-located cluster, then access to compute becomes binary: either you have a datacenter-scale supercomputer, or you cannot train state-of-the-art models. This is the gap DiLoCo addresses.

---

### Why This Problem Matters: Real-World Impact and Practical Significance

The paper's motivation is grounded in concrete, practical deployment scenarios rather than abstract theoretical concerns. Several use cases illustrate why relaxing the co-location assumption matters:

**Geographically distributed compute islands.** Organizations — particularly in academic and non-profit settings — often have access to multiple smaller GPU clusters spread across different locations (different buildings, different campuses, or different cloud regions). These cannot be combined for standard synchronous training because the inter-cluster bandwidth is orders of magnitude too low. DiLoCo promises to make these distributed resources fungible: instead of requiring one massive cluster, an organization could pool several smaller ones.

**Collaborative and volunteer computing.** The paper explicitly references Petals (Borzunov et al., 2022) and Diskin et al. (2021), systems where individual volunteers contribute their own devices to a shared training pool. In such settings, the number of available devices fluctuates constantly as participants join and leave, and the network between them is the public internet — high latency, low bandwidth, and unreliable. Standard synchronous training is impossible here. DiLoCo's infrequent communication and robustness to asynchronous updates make this paradigm feasible.

**Preemptible and spot instances.** Cloud providers offer cheaper compute through preemptible instances that can be revoked at any time. Budget-constrained teams want to use these, but the risk of losing a worker mid-training is catastrophic for tightly synchronized systems. The paper frames DiLoCo's robustness to worker dropout (formalized in the asynchronous communication experiments, Figure 8) as enabling cheaper training by tolerating such preemptions gracefully.

**Dynamic resource availability.** University computing clusters often use fair-share or karma-based scheduling where a user's available resources fluctuate over time. Similarly, industrial research labs may have variable access to compute depending on overall cluster utilization. DiLoCo's adaptive compute pool experiments (Figure 7) are motivated by exactly this: showing that training can continue effectively even as the number of workers ramps up or down over time, without the brittle assumptions of a fixed allocation.

**Breaking the "all-or-nothing" hardware barrier.** If DiLoCo works at scale, it lowers the barrier to entry for large model training. Instead of needing to assemble a 4,096-GPU pod, an organization might train across 8 pods of 512 GPUs each, potentially spread across different cloud providers or hardware generations (the paper's Figure 1 explicitly illustrates TPUv4, V100, TPUv5, and A100 all participating in the same training run).

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper situates itself relative to two main research lines: **local SGD / federated learning** and **linear mode connectivity / model merging**. Understanding both is essential because DiLoCo synthesizes insights from each while addressing their respective limitations at scale.

#### Local SGD and Federated Averaging (FedAvg)

Local SGD, introduced to the deep learning community by McMahan et al. (2017) as Federated Averaging, is the direct ancestor of DiLoCo. The core idea is the same: workers train model replicas locally for some number of steps $H$, then average their parameters (or parameter deltas) to produce a new shared model, and repeat. This reduces communication from every step to every $H$ steps.

However, prior local SGD work has significant limitations that DiLoCo addresses:

**Communication frequency was too high for the paper's target setting.** Most prior work considered $H \leq 20$ steps (Ortiz et al., 2021; Stich, 2019). While this reduces communication relative to standard training, it is still far too frequent for geographically distributed workers. Communicating every 20 steps across the public internet would still be dominated by latency and bandwidth constraints. DiLoCo pushes $H$ to 500–1,000 steps, reducing communication by an additional 25–50× over prior local SGD work. The paper explicitly contrasts this scale: "Most works instead consider communicating every $H \leq 20$ steps ... which is too frequent for many distributed learning applications."

**Scale was too small to be relevant for modern LLMs.** The paper acknowledges the local SGD literature but notes that previous large-scale studies (Ortiz et al., 2021) were limited to ImageNet classification with ResNets — a setting orders of magnitude smaller than modern language model training in both model size and compute requirements. When Ortiz et al. (2021) attempted to scale local SGD, they reached a pessimistic conclusion:

> "Ortiz et al. (2021) is one of the few works in federated learning / local SGD body of literature that has validated on a large-scale setting. ... they found that local SGD struggles at scale. In particular, they reported that fewer inner steps (e.g., $H = 8$), no pretraining, and a relatively large number of replicas ($\geq k = 16$) degrade generalization. Thus the authors conclude that 'local SGD encounters challenges at scale.'"

This is a critical citation because it establishes that *naive application of local SGD to larger models does not work*. DiLoCo directly contradicts this pessimistic finding, demonstrating robust operation with $H = 1,000$ (125× fewer communications than the $H = 8$ that Ortiz et al. found problematic), up to $k = 64$ replicas (4× more than the threshold where Ortiz et al. observed degradation), and even without any pretraining (which Ortiz et al. found necessary). The paper attributes this discrepancy to DiLoCo's specific design choices — AdamW inner optimizer, Nesterov outer optimizer, and the large-$H$ regime — but the exact mechanism is not fully explained.

**Outer optimization was primitive.** Standard FedAvg uses simple averaging (equivalent to SGD with learning rate 1.0) as the outer update. Reddi et al. (2021) introduced FedOpt, which generalizes the outer step to use adaptive optimizers like Adam, but their experiments were at small scale. DiLoCo systematically compares outer optimizers (Figure 6) and finds that Nesterov momentum substantially outperforms both SGD (FedAvg) and Adam (FedOpt) in the large-$H$ regime. The paper hypothesizes:

> "We hypothesize that the Nesterov's gradient correction is particularly helpful with the outer gradient that spans hundreds of training steps."

This is a non-obvious finding: Nesterov momentum's "lookahead" correction (computing the gradient at a momentum-adjusted position rather than the current position) provides a better update direction when the outer gradient is the accumulated result of many local steps, because it compensates for the fact that each worker's local trajectory has drifted during the long inner optimization phase.

**Inner optimizer mismatch.** Federated learning work predominantly used SGD as the inner optimizer, following the original FedAvg formulation. But modern transformer language models are almost universally trained with AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019). DiLoCo naturally adopts AdamW as the inner optimizer, making it directly compatible with standard LLM training recipes without requiring changes to learning rate schedules, weight decay, or other hyperparameters that practitioners have already tuned. FedMom (Huo et al., 2020) is the closest prior work — it also used Nesterov as the outer optimizer — but at dramatically smaller scale (1-layer LSTM, 2 replicas, $H = 20$) and with SGD as the inner optimizer. The paper explicitly positions DiLoCo as extending this direction to "a larger setting with up to a 400M transformer language model, across up to 64 replicas, and up to 100× less communication."

#### Linear Mode Connectivity and Model Merging

The second research line is model merging via linear interpolation in parameter space. The surprising finding from this literature (Frankle et al., 2020; Wortsman et al., 2021) is that independently fine-tuned model replicas often remain linearly connected — averaging their parameters produces a model that performs well, without a "loss barrier" between them. This has been exploited extensively for improving generalization (Wortsman et al., 2022b), combining models fine-tuned on different tasks (Ilharco et al., 2022), and merging language models (Li et al., 2022).

However, the model merging literature has two critical gaps relative to DiLoCo's goals:

**Merging happens only at the end of training.** The standard approach is to fine-tune multiple replicas independently to completion, then average their parameters once (model soups, Wortsman et al., 2022b). This is communication-efficient in the sense that no intermediate synchronization is needed, but it means the models diverge substantially during training without benefiting from each other's progress. DiLoCo instead performs merging *during* training — the outer optimization step happens repeatedly every $H$ inner steps, so workers continually share information about the loss landscape. The paper frames this distinction explicitly:

> "The majority of works on linear connectivity considers only averaging once all replicas have been fully finetuned, while we exploit the linear mode connectivity during training."

**No outer optimization beyond simple averaging.** When merging is done once at the end, "averaging" typically means uniform parameter averaging (SGD with learning rate 1.0). Prior distributed-training methods inspired by linear connectivity — such as BTM (Li et al., 2022) and PAPA (Jolicoeur-Martineau et al., 2023) — are described by the paper as "roughly equivalent to our framework but use as outer optimizer OuterOpt = SGD(lr=1.0)." This misses the benefits of momentum-based outer optimization that DiLoCo demonstrates are crucial at large $H$. Additionally, BTM communicates very little because each replica is fully fine-tuned on a task before synchronization (so $T = 1$ in DiLoCo's terms), while PAPA communicates every few steps with at most 10 replicas — neither achieves the combination of frequent-enough merging with infrequent-enough communication that DiLoCo targets.

**The pretraining-then-finetune paradigm hides the difficulty.** Most linear connectivity work starts from a pretrained model and fine-tunes replicas on specific tasks. This is an easier setting because the pretrained initialization constrains the replicas to stay in the same loss basin. DiLoCo demonstrates that the approach works even when starting from scratch or from a partially pretrained model, and over thousands of outer steps, not just a single averaging event.

### How DiLoCo Positions Itself

DiLoCo is not presented as a completely novel algorithm but rather as a **specific, carefully engineered combination of existing ideas** that together enable a scale regime where prior methods fail. The paper's positioning strategy has several dimensions:

**Unified framework for distributed training across poorly connected islands.** The paper's central thesis is that a properly designed local SGD variant can bridge the gap between the two extreme points on the communication spectrum: full synchronization (every step, maximum communication) and fully independent training (zero communication, model merging at the end). DiLoCo occupies the middle ground — communicating every $H = 500$ steps — and shows this is sufficient to match or exceed fully synchronous performance while being practical for geographically distributed workers.

**Robustness as the defining contribution, not asymptotic convergence rates.** Unlike much of the federated learning theory literature, this paper makes no theoretical claims about convergence rates or optimality gaps. Instead, it builds an empirical case that DiLoCo is *robust* — to data distribution, communication frequency, number of replicas, dynamic compute availability, and even communication failures. The paper's ablation experiments (subsection 3.1 and Appendix 6.2) systematically stress-test these dimensions, and the consistent finding is that DiLoCo degrades gracefully rather than failing catastrophically. This is a practical engineering contribution rather than a theoretical one, aimed at practitioners who need something that works reliably in messy real-world conditions.

**Direct challenge to the scaling pessimism from Ortiz et al. (2021).** The paper's positioning relative to Ortiz et al. (2021) is deliberately confrontational. Where Ortiz et al. concluded that "local SGD encounters challenges at scale," DiLoCo's experiments are designed to demonstrate exactly the opposite: that local SGD can work robustly at *larger* scale (more replicas, fewer communications, larger models) with the right design choices. The paper enumerates the specific disagreements: DiLoCo communicates 125× less ($H = 1,000$ vs. $H = 8$), uses 4× more replicas ($k = 64$ vs. $k = 16$), and works without pretraining — each point directly contradicting a finding from Ortiz et al.

**Pragmatic framing: "training across compute islands" rather than "federated learning."** The paper deliberately avoids the term "federated learning" in its title and framing, using "distributed low-communication training" instead. This is a meaningful positioning choice. Federated learning carries connotations of privacy-preserving training on decentralized user data, with heterogeneous and often small client devices (phones, edge devices). DiLoCo's target setting — training transformer language models across server-class GPU clusters — is different in important ways: workers are relatively powerful and homogeneous (each can independently train model replicas with standard parallelism), data is not private, and the goal is throughput rather than privacy. By framing the problem as "distributed training across compute islands," the paper connects to the high-performance computing and distributed systems communities rather than the privacy-focused federated learning community.

**Preserving inference-time equivalence as a silent constraint.** A design constraint that the paper mentions only briefly but that fundamentally shapes the approach: "at inference time the resulting model has the same size and speed as a model trained in fully synchronous mode." DiLoCo produces a single standard transformer model, not an ensemble, not a mixture of experts with additional routing overhead, and not a model with any architectural modifications. This is crucial because it means DiLoCo's training innovations impose zero cost at deployment — the trained model can be exported, compressed, and served using standard inference pipelines without modification. This constraint rules out many alternative distributed training approaches (e.g., training independent experts that must be combined at inference time) and motivates the outer optimization design that produces a single coherent model rather than a collection of specialized replicas.

### The Specific Gap: Making Large-H Local SGD Work for Modern Language Model Training

Synthesizing the above, the paper addresses a surprisingly specific and technically demanding gap:

**Prior local SGD work** demonstrated that reducing communication to every $H$ steps is possible, but (a) only considered small $H$ (≤20), (b) found performance degradation at scale, and (c) used optimizers (SGD) incompatible with modern LLM training practice.

**Model merging work** demonstrated that independently trained model replicas can be averaged successfully, but (a) only did this once at the end of training, (b) used simple averaging without momentum-based outer optimization, and (c) operated primarily in the fine-tuning regime starting from strong pretrained models.

**DiLoCo bridges these gaps** by showing that (a) $H = 500$–$1,000$ is feasible without catastrophic divergence, (b) AdamW + Nesterov momentum is the key combination that enables this large-$H$ regime, (c) repeated merging during training (every $H$ steps) outperforms both end-of-training merging and fully synchronous training, and (d) the approach works from scratch or partial pretraining, not just fine-tuning.

The paper does not claim to have a theoretical explanation for why this specific combination works. The cosine similarity analysis in Appendix 6.2 provides some intuition — showing that outer gradients remain reasonably correlated even at $H = 1,000$ steps — but the mechanistic understanding is incomplete. The contribution is primarily empirical: a recipe that practitioners can adopt and that works robustly across a range of practical deployment challenges.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

DiLoCo is a distributed optimization algorithm that trains a single language model across multiple groups of accelerators ("workers") that are physically separated and connected only by low-bandwidth links. The problem it solves is the fundamental tension in modern LLM training: standard back-propagation requires all devices to exchange gradients at every optimization step over high-bandwidth interconnects, making it impossible to pool geographically distributed or heterogeneous compute resources into one training run. DiLoCo resolves this by having each worker train its own model replica independently for hundreds of steps, then synchronizing parameter deltas infrequently through a momentum-based outer optimizer — reducing communication by up to 500× while producing a standard transformer model with no inference-time modifications.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The DiLoCo system has five major components arranged in a nested loop structure, as illustrated in Figure 1 and Algorithm 1 in the paper:

1. **A shared master copy of the model parameters** (`$\theta^{(t)}$`, where `$t$` indexes outer steps). This is the single authoritative model state that exists conceptually at a central coordinator, though in practice it may be stored on a CPU server connected to all workers. It is initialized either randomly or from a partially pretrained checkpoint.

2. **\`$k$\` workers** — each a group of co-located accelerators (GPUs or TPUs) capable of training a model replica independently using standard data and model parallelism. Workers need NOT be in the same location, have the same hardware type (Figure 1 explicitly shows TPUv4, V100, TPUv5, and A100 all in the same training run), or share data with other workers. Each worker `$i$` receives its own data shard `$\mathcal{D}_i$` drawn from the training dataset.

3. **An inner optimizer (AdamW)** local to each worker, responsible for updating that worker's replica parameters `$\theta_i^{(t)}$` for `$H$` steps using standard mini-batch gradient descent. AdamW is chosen because it is "the de facto standard optimizer for transformer language models," meaning practitioners can reuse their existing AdamW hyperparameter recipes (learning rate `$4 \times 10^{-4}$`, weight decay `$0.1$`, batch size 512, sequence length 1,024) without modification.

4. **An outer optimizer (Nesterov momentum)** that runs only on the central coordinator, once every `$H$` inner steps. It receives the averaged parameter delta `$\Delta^{(t)}$` computed from all workers, applies Nesterov momentum to produce a smoothed update direction, and produces the new shared parameters `$\theta^{(t)}$`. This is the only communication step — workers send their parameter deltas to the coordinator and receive the updated shared parameters in return.

5. **Data shards** `$\{\mathcal{D}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{D}_k\}$` — the training data is partitioned across workers. In the i.i.d. setting, this is a random partition. In the non-i.i.d. setting (used as the default in most experiments), the data is clustered using `$k$`-means on features from a pretrained model's penultimate layer, following Gururangan et al. (2023), so that each worker sees a semantically distinct subset of the data.

The information flow follows a strict alternation between local training and global synchronization, repeated `$T$` times for a total of `$N = T \times H$` inner training steps per worker:

- **Step 1 (Outer dispatch):** The shared parameters `$\theta^{(t-1)}$` are broadcast to all `$k$` workers, who initialize their local replicas `$\theta_i^{(t)} \leftarrow \theta^{(t-1)}$`.

- **Step 2 (Inner training):** Each worker `$i$` independently runs `$H$` steps of AdamW on its local data shard `$\mathcal{D}_i$`, updating `$\theta_i^{(t)}$` by mini-batch gradient descent. No communication occurs between workers or with the coordinator during this phase.

- **Step 3 (Outer gradient computation):** After `$H$` steps, each worker computes its outer gradient as the negative of the total parameter change: `$\theta^{(t-1)} - \theta_i^{(t)}$`. These `$k$` outer gradients are sent to the coordinator and averaged: `$\Delta^{(t)} \leftarrow \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^k (\theta^{(t-1)} - \theta_i^{(t)})$`. (For non-i.i.d. data with unbalanced shards, the paper rescales each worker's outer gradient by its shard size.)

- **Step 4 (Outer update):** The coordinator feeds `$\Delta^{(t)}$` into the Nesterov momentum optimizer to produce `$\theta^{(t)}$`. This becomes the starting point for the next cycle.

- **Repeat:** Steps 1–4 iterate `$T$` times.

After all `$T$` outer steps complete, the shared parameters `$\theta^{(T)}$` are the final trained model — a standard transformer with the same architecture, size, and inference cost as one trained fully synchronously.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First:** The formal DiLoCo algorithm (Algorithm 1) — the nested loop structure, the two optimizers, and the outer gradient computation — because this is the core mechanism and everything else is built on it.

- **Second:** The outer gradient — what exactly is being averaged, why it is a parameter delta rather than raw gradients, and how this connects to the linear mode connectivity literature. This is the key design choice that makes large `$H$` possible.

- **Third:** The inner optimizer (AdamW) and why it is left completely unchanged from standard LLM training practice, since this is what makes DiLoCo a drop-in replacement rather than a whole new training recipe.

- **Fourth:** The outer optimizer (Nesterov momentum) — the systematic comparison against SGD, SGD with momentum, and Adam, and why Nesterov's gradient correction is particularly suited to outer gradients spanning hundreds of steps.

- **Fifth:** The hyperparameter space — what was fixed (inner optimizer settings), what was swept (communication frequency `$H$`, number of replicas `$k$`, outer learning rate and momentum, number of pretraining steps), and the default values that define a standard DiLoCo configuration.

- **Sixth:** The data distribution regimes (i.i.d. vs. non-i.i.d.) — how shards are constructed, why the default experimental setting is non-i.i.d., and the weight rescaling for unbalanced shards.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical systems paper** whose core idea is that a specific combination of local SGD with a large number of inner steps, AdamW as the inner optimizer, and Nesterov momentum as the outer optimizer enables robust training of transformer language models across poorly connected compute islands. The contribution lies not in a novel algorithm but in demonstrating that a carefully engineered combination of existing techniques works in a scale regime where prior work predicted failure.

#### The DiLoCo Algorithm: Nested Optimization Loops

The algorithm is formalized in Algorithm 1 of the paper and proceeds as a two-level optimization process:

**Outer loop (over `$t = 1 \ldots T$`):** Each outer iteration begins by dispatching the current shared parameters `$\theta^{(t-1)}$` to all `$k$` workers. After all workers complete their `$H$` inner steps, their outer gradients are collected, averaged, and used to update the shared parameters to `$\theta^{(t)}$` via the outer optimizer.

**Inner loop (over `$h = 1 \ldots H$`):** Within each outer step, each worker `$i$` independently performs standard mini-batch training on its local data shard `$\mathcal{D}_i$`. For each inner step:
- A mini-batch `$x$` is sampled from `$\mathcal{D}_i$`.
- The loss `$\mathcal{L} = f(x, \theta_i^{(t)})$` is computed — in language modeling, this is the standard cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction on the sequence.
- The gradient `$\nabla \mathcal{L}$` is computed via back-propagation.
- The local parameters are updated: `$\theta_i^{(t)} \leftarrow \text{AdamW}(\theta_i^{(t)}, \nabla \mathcal{L})$`.

The crucial operational detail is that after line 3 of Algorithm 1 (`$\theta_i^{(t)} \leftarrow \theta^{(t-1)}$`), workers are completely independent — there is no synchronization barrier, no gradient all-reduce, and no parameter sharing until the outer optimization step at lines 12–14. This means that for `$H = 500$` steps, each worker is effectively training in isolation, seeing only its own data and following its own trajectory through parameter space.

**Total training budget:** A worker trains for `$N = T \times H$` inner steps total. The default configuration in the main experiments (Figure 2) uses `$T = 128$` outer steps and `$H = 500$` inner steps, giving 64,000 DiLoCo steps on top of 24,000 pretraining steps for a total of 88,000 optimization steps.

**The connection to data parallelism:** The paper notes that DiLoCo reduces to standard data-parallel training when `$H = 1$` and the inner optimizer is SGD — each worker takes a single gradient step on its local batch, then all gradients are averaged. DiLoCo generalizes this by allowing `$H \gg 1$`, decoupling communication frequency from the number of gradient steps, but at the cost of each worker computing updates on stale versions of the shared parameters (since they don't see other workers' progress during the inner phase).

**The connection to Federated Averaging:** DiLoCo is an instantiation of the FedOpt framework (Reddi et al., 2021) with specific choices for the inner and outer optimizers. When `$\text{OuterOpt} = \text{SGD}$` with learning rate 1.0, DiLoCo is exactly classical FedAvg (McMahan et al., 2017). When `$T = 1$` (only one outer step after all inner training), DiLoCo reduces to model souping (Wortsman et al., 2021). Neither of these extremes performs well in the paper's setting — FedAvg's simple averaging underperforms Nesterov (Figure 6), and `$T = 1$` provides no intermediate synchronization benefits.

#### The Outer Gradient: Parameter Deltas, Not Raw Gradients

The outer gradient computation at line 12 of Algorithm 1 is:

$$\Delta^{(t)} \leftarrow \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^k \left(\theta^{(t-1)} - \theta_i^{(t)}\right)$$

where `$\theta^{(t-1)}$` is the shared parameter vector at the start of the outer step, `$\theta_i^{(t)}$` is worker `$i$`'s parameter vector after `$H$` local AdamW steps, and `$k$` is the number of workers.

**What it computes:** For each worker, the outer gradient is the *negative* of the total parameter displacement that occurred during the inner optimization phase. If a worker's parameters moved from `$\theta^{(t-1)}$` to `$\theta_i^{(t)}$`, the outer gradient is `$\theta^{(t-1)} - \theta_i^{(t)}$`, which points from the current position back toward the starting point. Averaging these across workers produces `$\Delta^{(t)}$`, which is then fed to the outer optimizer. The outer optimizer applies this averaged displacement as an update to `$\theta^{(t-1)}$` to produce `$\theta^{(t)}$`.

**Why parameter deltas rather than raw gradients:** This is the central design choice that distinguishes DiLoCo from standard distributed training. In standard data-parallel SGD, each device computes a per-step gradient `$\nabla \mathcal{L}_i$` and these are averaged across devices. In DiLoCo, a single "outer gradient" `$\Delta^{(t)}$` represents the *accumulated effect* of `$H$` local AdamW steps, which include learning rate scheduling, weight decay, and adaptive per-parameter scaling (the AdamW first and second moment estimates). Simply averaging the raw gradients from the last inner step would lose all information about the optimization trajectory. The parameter delta captures the net movement, including the effects of AdamW's preconditioning.

**Why this form matters for linear mode connectivity:** The outer gradient is exactly the vector that, when subtracted from `$\theta^{(t-1)}$`, recovers the average of all workers' final positions: `$\theta^{(t-1)} - \Delta^{(t)} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^k \theta_i^{(t)}$`. If the outer optimizer were SGD with learning rate 1.0 (pure FedAvg), the new shared parameters would be exactly this average — the arithmetic mean of all workers' endpoints. This connects directly to the linear mode connectivity literature: the shared parameters remain in the convex hull of the individual workers' parameters, which is known to be a low-loss region when models are linearly connected (Frankle et al., 2020). The Nesterov outer optimizer generalizes this by applying momentum to the sequence of averaged deltas, effectively performing a smoothed interpolation through parameter space that converges to a region that is simultaneously close (in a linear sense) to all workers' local solutions.

**Weighted averaging for unbalanced shards:** In the non-i.i.d. setting, particularly at large `$k$` (e.g., 64 workers), the `$k$`-means clustering can produce shards of substantially different sizes. The paper describes rescaling each outer gradient by the number of examples in its shard before averaging:

$$\Delta^{(t)} \leftarrow \frac{1}{\sum_i |\mathcal{D}_i|} \sum_{i=1}^k |\mathcal{D}_i| \cdot (\theta^{(t-1)} - \theta_i^{(t)})$$

This gives more weight to workers that have seen more data during their inner optimization, preventing small shards from dominating the shared parameter trajectory. The paper notes that this rescaling is unnecessary at `$k = 4$` where shards are "quite balanced" but becomes important at `$k = 64$` where "imbalance can be striking."

#### The Inner Optimizer: AdamW (Unmodified from Standard Practice)

The inner optimizer is specified at line 8 of Algorithm 1: `$\theta_i^{(t)} \leftarrow \text{InnerOpt}(\theta_i^{(t)}, \nabla \mathcal{L})$`, with `$\text{InnerOpt} = \text{AdamW}$`. The choice of AdamW is constrained by a practical consideration: DiLoCo is meant to be a drop-in replacement for standard LLM training, and AdamW is "the most widely used optimizer for transformer language models." By keeping the inner optimizer identical to what practitioners already use, DiLoCo requires no changes to:

- **The inner learning rate schedule:** The paper uses a learning rate of `$4 \times 10^{-4}$` with 1,000 warmup steps followed by cosine decay. This is a standard LLM recipe and is applied independently within each worker's inner optimization phase. The inner learning rate is NOT shared or synchronized across workers — each worker's AdamW optimizer maintains its own learning rate schedule based on its own step count.

- **Weight decay:** AdamW decouples weight decay from gradient-based updates, applying `$0.1$` weight decay independently per worker. This means each worker's parameters experience a small inward pull toward zero during each inner step, in addition to the gradient-based update.

- **AdamW's internal state:** Each worker maintains its own first-moment (`$\beta_1 = 0.9$`) and second-moment (`$\beta_2 = 0.95$`, inferred from standard practice for this model family) estimates. The paper explicitly considered synchronizing these optimizer states during the outer step (in addition to the model parameters) but found it "did not lead to significant improvements while significantly increasing the communication cost (×3 more data to transmit)." Therefore, each worker's AdamW state is purely local and persists across outer steps — when a worker receives new shared parameters `$\theta^{(t)}$` at the start of an outer step, its AdamW moment estimates are NOT reset. This is an important implementation detail: the inner optimizer accumulates momentum across outer steps, which may help smooth the transition when the shared parameters jump to a new location.

**Inner optimizer states are NOT synchronized:** The paper's finding that synchronizing optimizer states adds 3× communication with no benefit echoes prior work in federated learning with momentum optimizers (Ortiz et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020), where a similar conclusion was reached for SGD with momentum and Nesterov inner optimizers. The intuition is that the outer gradient already captures the net effect of the inner optimization trajectory, making the precise moment estimates at the synchronization point less critical than the parameter displacement itself.

**Inner step count `$H$` is the primary communication control knob:** The number of inner steps per outer step determines communication frequency. At `$H = 500$` (the default), workers communicate once every 500 gradient steps. At `$H = 1,000$`, communication is halved again (once every 1,000 steps). The paper's Figure 4 systematically sweeps `$H \in \{50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 2000\}$` and finds diminishing returns beyond 500: perplexity degrades by only 2.9% relative when moving from `$H = 50$` to `$H = 1,000$`, despite 20× less communication. This robustness to large `$H$` is the paper's central empirical claim and directly contradicts prior work that found degradation with `$H \geq 8$`.

#### The Outer Optimizer: Nesterov Momentum

The outer optimizer at line 14 of Algorithm 1: `$\theta^{(t)} \leftarrow \text{OuterOpt}(\theta^{(t-1)}, \Delta^{(t)})$`. The paper systematically compares four outer optimizer choices (Figure 6) and finds Nesterov momentum with learning rate 0.7 and momentum 0.9 to be the clear winner. Understanding why requires examining each candidate:

**SGD (equivalent to classical FedAvg):** The update is `$\theta^{(t)} = \theta^{(t-1)} - \eta \Delta^{(t)}$`. With `$\eta = 1.0$`, this is exact parameter averaging — the new shared parameters are the arithmetic mean of all workers' endpoints. The paper found SGD performs "poorly" (Figure 6, red). The likely reason is that pure averaging, without momentum, responds immediately to every outer gradient without smoothing. When outer gradients are noisy (due to different data distributions or random seed variation across workers), the shared parameters can oscillate rather than converge smoothly.

**SGD with Momentum (SGDM):** The update maintains a velocity buffer `$v^{(t)} = \mu v^{(t-1)} - \eta \Delta^{(t)}$` and updates parameters as `$\theta^{(t)} = \theta^{(t-1)} + v^{(t)}$`. With `$\mu = 0.9$` and sweeps over `$\eta \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0\}$`. The paper's Figure 6 shows this improves over SGD but still underperforms Nesterov. Standard momentum helps by smoothing the outer gradient sequence, but it computes the gradient at the current parameter position and then applies momentum in the update direction — it lacks the "lookahead" correction that Nesterov provides.

**Adam (equivalent to FedOpt):** The paper reports that Adam "was particularly unstable with a high second-order momentum norm." The second-moment estimate in Adam normalizes each parameter's update by the root-mean-square of past gradients, which can amplify updates in directions where the outer gradient is consistently small. The paper found Adam required increasing the `$\epsilon$` parameter to 0.1 (from the typical `$10^{-8}$`) to stabilize. Despite this tuning, Adam still underperformed Nesterov. The hypothesis is that Adam's per-parameter adaptive learning rates interact poorly with the outer gradient, which represents the *accumulated* effect of hundreds of inner steps — the scale of `$\Delta^{(t)}$` in each parameter dimension may not reflect the per-parameter variance that Adam's normalization is designed to handle.

**Nesterov momentum (the chosen optimizer):** The Nesterov update follows:

$$v^{(t)} = \mu v^{(t-1)} - \eta \Delta^{(t)}_{\text{lookahead}}$$

$$\theta^{(t)} = \theta^{(t-1)} + v^{(t)}$$

where `$\Delta^{(t)}_{\text{lookahead}}$` is the outer gradient computed at the lookahead position `$\theta^{(t-1)} + \mu v^{(t-1)}$`, NOT at the current position `$\theta^{(t-1)}$`. Wait — this requires clarification because Algorithm 1 as written computes `$\Delta^{(t)}$` from `$\theta^{(t-1)}$`, not from a lookahead position. The paper's description is imprecise here. The standard Nesterov formulation applies the gradient at the momentum-adjusted point, but DiLoCo's outer gradient is always computed as `$\theta^{(t-1)} - \theta_i^{(t)}$` where workers started from `$\theta^{(t-1)}$`. So how is this Nesterov?

The resolution is that DiLoCo applies the Nesterov *update rule* to the outer gradient sequence, but the outer gradient itself is computed from `$\theta^{(t-1)}$` (not a lookahead position). The momentum state `$v^{(t)}$` accumulates past outer gradients, and the Nesterov correction effectively extrapolates the current gradient using the momentum direction. The paper's formulation is:

$$\theta^{(t)} = \theta^{(t-1)} + \mu v^{(t-1)} - \eta \Delta^{(t)}$$

where `$v^{(t)} = \mu v^{(t-1)} - \eta \Delta^{(t)}$`. This is equivalent to standard Nesterov applied to the sequence of `$\Delta^{(t)}$` values, where each `$\Delta^{(t)}$` is treated as if it were the gradient at step `$t$`. The practical effect is that the outer optimizer maintains a velocity term that smooths the outer gradient sequence and applies a correction that accounts for where the momentum would carry the parameters even before seeing the new outer gradient.

**Why Nesterov over standard momentum:** The paper hypothesizes that "the Nesterov's gradient correction is particularly helpful with the outer gradient that spans hundreds of training steps." The intuition is that during `$H = 500$` inner steps, each worker's trajectory may drift in a consistent direction (e.g., toward a local minimum of its own data distribution). Standard momentum would treat the averaged drift direction as a new gradient and apply momentum based on previous outer steps. Nesterov's correction effectively "looks ahead" along the current momentum direction and computes the update at that extrapolated point, which better compensates for the drift that occurred during the long inner phase. The result is smoother convergence and better final perplexity.

**Outer learning rate tuning:** The paper sweeps `$\eta \in \{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0\}$` and finds `$\eta = 0.7$` with momentum `$\mu = 0.9$` to be "very robust" and uses these as defaults throughout all experiments. The outer learning rate is NOT decayed — the paper considered cosine scheduling but found "similar performance," reasoning that "since we decay the inner learning rate, the outer gradient norm gets naturally smaller over the course of training, removing the need to further decay the outer learning rate." This is an elegant simplification: the inner learning rate decay automatically shrinks the magnitude of `$\Delta^{(t)}$` as training progresses, providing an implicit outer learning rate decay without additional hyperparameter tuning.

#### Hyperparameter Space and Default Configuration

The paper provides a comprehensive hyperparameter table (Table 5 in the appendix) enumerating all values swept. The default configuration for the main experiments is:

#### Data Distribution Regimes: i.i.d. vs. Non-i.i.d.

The paper evaluates DiLoCo under two data partitioning strategies:

**i.i.d. (random partitioning):** Each worker receives a uniformly random subset of the training data. All shards `$\mathcal{D}_i$` have the same marginal distribution — a token sampled from any worker's data is equally likely to come from any part of the training corpus. This is the standard setting for distributed training and the easier case for DiLoCo because all workers are optimizing toward locally similar loss landscapes.

**Non-i.i.d. (clustered partitioning):** Following Gururangan et al. (2023), the training data is clustered using `$k$`-means applied to the last-layer features of a pretrained model. Each cluster becomes one worker's data shard. This creates workers whose data distributions are semantically distinct — one worker might see mostly scientific text, another mostly conversational text, etc. This is the default setting because it is "a more challenging use case" and more representative of realistic federated learning scenarios where different devices or institutions have naturally non-overlapping data.

**Why non-i.i.d. is harder:** In the non-i.i.d. setting, each worker's local optimization converges toward a minimum of its *own* data distribution, not the global distribution. The outer gradients `$\theta^{(t-1)} - \theta_i^{(t)}$` from different workers can point in substantially different directions (as confirmed by the cosine similarity analysis in Appendix 6.2), because each worker's local solution has specialized to its own data. The outer optimizer must reconcile these diverging signals into a single parameter vector that generalizes across all data distributions. The fact that DiLoCo achieves comparable final perplexity in both i.i.d. and non-i.i.d. settings (Figure 5: both converge to similar final values) is one of the paper's most surprising results.

**Convergence speed differs despite similar final performance:** Figure 5 shows that DiLoCo "converges faster in the i.i.d. setting" early in training — the i.i.d. curve is lower (better perplexity) than the non-i.i.d. curve for most of the training run. However, "toward the end both data regimes attain similar generalization." The paper hypothesizes that the non-i.i.d. outer gradients, while more divergent early on, provide a regularization effect later in training — the tension between workers forces the shared parameters into a region that generalizes better, compensating for the initial slower convergence.

**Weighted averaging for unbalanced non-i.i.d. shards:** The `$k$`-means clustering can produce shards of very different sizes, especially at `$k = 64$`. The paper addresses this by rescaling outer gradients proportional to shard size: larger shards contribute more to the average. This is described as being important "at `$k = 64$`" where "imbalance can be striking."

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications: A Synthesis

**Why AdamW as inner optimizer rather than SGD:** Prior local SGD work (Ortiz et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2020) predominantly used SGD. DiLoCo switches to AdamW because it is the standard for transformer language models and requires no hyperparameter re-tuning. The adaptive per-parameter learning rates in AdamW may also help each worker converge more reliably during the long `$H = 500$` inner phase, since the optimizer automatically adjusts step sizes in different parameter dimensions based on gradient history. This is crucial: with SGD, a single global learning rate must work for all parameters across 500 steps of isolated training, which may lead to instability or slow convergence in some layers.

**Why Nesterov over simpler outer optimizers:** The systematic comparison (Figure 6) shows Nesterov substantially outperforming SGD, SGDM, and Adam. The paper's hypothesis — that Nesterov's gradient correction compensates for the long inner phase — is plausible but not rigorously tested (no ablation isolating the lookahead correction from standard momentum with the same hyperparameters). The practical takeaway is that Nesterov with `$\eta = 0.7, \mu = 0.9$` is a robust default that works across all experimental settings.

**Why `$H = 500$` as the default communication frequency:** The sweep in Figure 4 shows that `$H = 500$` balances communication reduction with perplexity degradation. At this setting, DiLoCo communicates 500× less than standard data parallelism while matching or beating its performance. The paper's observation that performance degrades only 2.9% at `$H = 1000$` relative to `$H = 50$` is the key evidence that large-`$H$` local SGD is viable for language models, contradicting prior work that found problems at `$H \geq 8$`.

**Why start from a pretrained model rather than from scratch:** The default experiments use 24,000 pretraining steps (single-worker, standard AdamW) before switching to DiLoCo. This is practical: a partially trained model provides a common starting point that is already in a region of low loss, reducing the risk of workers diverging into incompatible solutions during the first few outer steps. However, Figure 3 shows that DiLoCo also works from scratch — the final perplexity is "similar" to the pretrained initialization case (within approximately 0.1 PPL). This robustness to initialization is surprising and contradicts Ortiz et al. (2021), who found pretraining necessary for local SGD to work at scale. The paper does not fully explain this discrepancy but suggests it may be due to the different inner optimizer (AdamW vs. SGD) and the different outer optimizer (Nesterov vs. SGD).

**Why no outer learning rate decay:** The paper considered cosine decay but found "similar performance." The reasoning — that inner learning rate decay naturally shrinks the outer gradient norm over time, providing implicit decay — is an elegant simplification that removes one hyperparameter from the tuning burden. Whether this holds at different model scales or training durations is an open question.

**Why `$k = 8$` as the default number of replicas:** Table 3 shows that increasing replicas from 4 to 8 provides substantial improvement (15.23 → 15.02 PPL in non-i.i.d.), but 8 to 16 provides diminishing returns (15.02 → 14.91), and 16 to 64 provides almost none (14.91 → 14.96). The paper does not explore why the benefit saturates, but one hypothesis is that with more workers on fixed total data, each worker sees less data during its inner phase, and the outer gradient becomes noisier — the additional diversity from more workers is offset by the reduced data per worker. This saturation is identified as a limitation in Section 5.

**Why not synchronize inner optimizer states:** The paper tested this and found "it did not lead to significant improvements while significantly increasing the communication cost (×3 more data to transmit)." This is a pragmatic decision: the communication savings from DiLoCo come entirely from reducing the *volume* of data exchanged at each communication step, not just the frequency. Transmitting full AdamW state (first and second moment estimates for every parameter) would triple the communication payload, partially undermining the low-communication goal. The finding that this extra communication provides no benefit reinforces the design philosophy: DiLoCo communicates the minimum necessary information (parameter deltas) and lets each worker maintain its own optimization state independently.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Demonstrating That Large-H Local SGD Works for Language Models at Scale — Contradicting Prior Pessimism

The paper's most consequential move is not proposing a new algorithm but rather **establishing, through systematic empirical evidence, that local SGD with very infrequent communication (H = 500–1,000 steps) works robustly for transformer language model training at scale**. This directly contradicts the prior state of knowledge in the field, most notably Ortiz et al. (2021), who concluded that "local SGD encounters challenges at scale" after finding degradation at H = 8, k ≥ 16, and without pretraining on ImageNet. DiLoCo operates at H = 500 (62.5× more inner steps than the threshold where Ortiz et al. observed problems), with k = 64 (4× more replicas), and even from scratch — each point a direct empirical rebuttal.

This is not an incremental improvement but a **fundamental reframing of what the field believed was possible**. Prior to this work, the dominant assumption — grounded in both the experimental record (Ortiz et al., 2021) and theoretical convergence analyses that typically assume small H for local SGD — was that large communication gaps cause worker divergence that harms final model quality. DiLoCo shows that this assumption is not universally true: with the right inner optimizer (AdamW rather than SGD), the right outer optimizer (Nesterov momentum rather than simple averaging), and the right scale regime (language modeling rather than ImageNet classification), workers can drift for hundreds of steps without catastrophic divergence.

The significance lies in **opening a design space that was previously considered closed**. Before DiLoCo, a practitioner wanting to train across geographically distributed GPU clusters would likely have been told it was impossible — the literature said local SGD breaks down when H gets large. After DiLoCo, the question shifts from "can this work?" to "how do we make it work better?" This is the kind of contribution that changes research agendas: it creates permission to explore the large-H regime and shifts the burden of proof onto skeptics.

The evidence is Figure 4, which shows only a 2.9% perplexity degradation at H = 1,000 relative to H = 50 (20× less communication), and Figure 3, which demonstrates successful training from scratch with no pretraining at all. However, it is worth noting that the paper does not provide a mechanistic explanation for *why* the Ortiz et al. findings do not generalize to language models — the hypothesis that AdamW and Nesterov are the key differentiators is plausible but not isolated via ablation (e.g., no experiment showing that switching DiLoCo's inner optimizer to SGD would reproduce the Ortiz et al. degradation). The contribution is empirical demonstration rather than theoretical understanding, which leaves open the question of whether the result is specific to language modeling or generalizes to other domains.

### Innovation 2: The Outer Gradient as Parameter Delta — Reframing Distributed Optimization Through Linear Mode Connectivity

DiLoCo computes its outer gradient not as an average of raw per-step gradients (standard data parallelism) but as the parameter displacement `θ^(t-1) - θ_i^(t)` — the total movement of each worker's parameters over H local steps. This choice is deceptively simple but represents a **conceptual reframing of distributed optimization through the lens of linear mode connectivity** rather than through gradient averaging.

The standard approach in distributed training — averaging gradients across devices — is grounded in the mathematics of SGD: if each device computes an unbiased gradient estimate on its local batch, averaging produces a lower-variance gradient estimate. But this framework assumes H = 1 (or equivalently, that every step's gradient is communicated). When H ≫ 1, the raw gradient at the last inner step contains almost no information about the trajectory the worker followed to get there. The parameter delta, by contrast, captures the *net effect* of all H AdamW steps — including learning rate schedule effects, weight decay, and per-parameter adaptive scaling.

This connects DiLoCo to the linear mode connectivity literature (Frankle et al., 2020; Wortsman et al., 2021) in a way that prior distributed training methods did not. Linear mode connectivity studies whether two independently trained models can be interpolated in parameter space without encountering a loss barrier. DiLoCo's outer gradient is exactly the vector that, when applied with learning rate 1.0, produces the arithmetic mean of all workers' parameter positions — the simplest possible linear interpolation. The fact that this averaged model performs well is itself evidence of linear connectivity between the workers' endpoints. The Nesterov outer optimizer then extends this by smoothing the sequence of averaged positions over time, effectively performing repeated interpolations that keep the shared parameters in a region that is linearly connected to all workers' local solutions.

Prior distributed training methods that used parameter averaging — federated averaging (McMahan et al., 2017), model soups (Wortsman et al., 2022b), BTM (Li et al., 2022) — did so without connecting it to the linear connectivity concept or explaining why the averaged model should work at all. DiLoCo reframes parameter averaging as an *exploitation of an empirical property of neural network loss landscapes* (linear connectivity) rather than merely a communication-reduction trick. This is a conceptual advance because it provides a different success criterion: DiLoCo works when the workers' trajectories remain within the same linearly connected basin, and fails when they diverge into disconnected basins. The cosine similarity analysis in Appendix 6.2 (Figures 10–11) is a first step toward operationalizing this criterion, showing that outer gradients remain correlated even at H = 1,000 steps.

The innovation is incremental in mechanism (parameter averaging is not new) but fundamental in framing: it recasts distributed optimization as a problem of maintaining linear connectivity between workers rather than a problem of reducing gradient variance. This suggests future research directions — such as explicitly penalizing divergence in a connectivity metric, or using techniques from the model merging literature (e.g., REPAIR, Jordan et al., 2023; or TIES, Yadav et al., 2023) to improve the averaging step — that would not be obvious from the gradient-averaging perspective.

### Innovation 3: Identifying the AdamW-Nesterov Combination as the Critical Enabler for the Large-H Regime

The paper systematically compares outer optimizers (SGD, SGDM, Adam, Nesterov; Figure 6) and finds Nesterov momentum with learning rate 0.7 and momentum 0.9 to be the clear winner, while also defaulting to AdamW as the inner optimizer (matching standard LLM practice). This combination is not an arbitrary engineering choice — the paper argues it is **the specific pairing that makes large-H local SGD viable**, and the ablation evidence supports this claim.

What makes this distinctive is that prior federated learning work used either SGD (FedAvg) or adaptive optimizers like Adam (FedOpt) as the outer optimizer, and almost universally used SGD as the inner optimizer (Ortiz et al., 2021; Reddi et al., 2021; McMahan et al., 2017). The paper's outer optimizer comparison (Figure 6) shows that these standard choices severely underperform Nesterov in the large-H setting. FedMom (Huo et al., 2020) previously used Nesterov as the outer optimizer, but at tiny scale (1-layer LSTM, 2 replicas, H = 20, SGD inner optimizer) — it did not establish Nesterov's importance at scale or in combination with AdamW.

The paper hypothesizes that Nesterov's gradient correction is "particularly helpful with the outer gradient that spans hundreds of training steps." The intuition: during 500 inner steps, each worker drifts in a consistent direction toward a local minimum of its own data. Standard momentum would treat the average drift as a new gradient and add it to the momentum buffer. Nesterov's correction extrapolates along the momentum direction and effectively *anticipates* where the momentum would carry the parameters, computing the update at that lookahead position. This better compensates for the systematic drift during the long inner phase.

The innovation is **diagnostic rather than theoretical**: the paper identifies that the outer optimizer matters enormously in the large-H regime, and that the standard FedAvg/FedOpt choices are inadequate. This is a practical contribution — it gives practitioners a specific recipe (AdamW + Nesterov with η = 0.7, μ = 0.9) that works — but it also raises a deeper question that the paper does not answer: *why* does Nesterov outperform Adam as the outer optimizer? Adam's per-parameter adaptive learning rates should, in principle, handle the heterogeneous scales of outer gradient components better than a single global learning rate. The paper's finding that Adam was "particularly unstable with a high second-order momentum norm" and required ε = 0.1 to stabilize suggests that the outer gradient's statistical properties differ fundamentally from standard per-step gradients in ways that break Adam's assumptions. This is a fertile area for future theoretical work.

The insight is incremental in the sense that both AdamW and Nesterov are well-known optimizers, but it is practically significant because it identifies the specific combination that works and rules out natural alternatives (SGD, Adam) that prior work used. Without this systematic comparison, a practitioner attempting large-H local SGD might try FedOpt with Adam (the natural extension of Reddi et al., 2021) and conclude, like Ortiz et al., that local SGD fails at scale — when in fact the failure is due to the outer optimizer choice rather than the local SGD paradigm itself.

### Innovation 4: Robustness as a First-Class Design Goal — Operationalizing Graceful Degradation Across Multiple Stress Axes

The paper systematically stress-tests DiLoCo across an unusually broad set of real-world failure modes: non-i.i.d. data distributions (Figure 5), communication failures (Figure 8), dynamic changes in available compute (Figure 7, showing workers ramping up and down over time), varying numbers of replicas (Table 3, up to 64), and variable pretraining initialization (Figure 3, including from scratch). The consistent finding is not that DiLoCo is immune to these stressors but that it **degrades gracefully rather than failing catastrophically** — 2.1% perplexity degradation with 50% communication dropout in non-i.i.d., comparable final performance for proportional and inversely proportional compute schedules, minimal degradation without pretraining.

This is a **reframing of what "works" means for a distributed training algorithm**. Prior work on local SGD primarily evaluated convergence speed and final accuracy under idealized conditions (Stich, 2019; Lin et al., 2020; Reddi et al., 2021). The implicit goal was to match or exceed the performance of fully synchronous training. DiLoCo shares this goal but adds a second dimension: the algorithm should maintain performance even when real-world conditions deviate from the ideal. This is not just "better numbers" — it is a fundamentally different evaluation philosophy that recognizes distributed training deployments are messy: machines get preempted, network links go down, compute allocations change over time, and data is not uniformly distributed.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is the **breadth and systematicity of the stress testing**. Each stress axis is an ablation experiment with controlled variation (probability of communication dropout swept from 0% to 50%; number of replicas swept from 4 to 64; compute schedules tested as monotonic, ramping, and halving). The paper does not just claim robustness — it quantifies the exact degradation curve as a function of the stressor magnitude. This allows practitioners to make cost-reliability tradeoffs: for example, using preemptible instances with a 30% dropout probability might be worth a <2% perplexity increase if it halves compute costs.

The adaptive compute pool experiments (Figure 7) are particularly significant because they address a gap in how distributed training is typically conceptualized. Standard distributed training assumes a fixed number of workers from start to finish. DiLoCo shows that **the total compute matters more than when it is available** — ramping up from 1 to 8 workers achieves similar performance to ramping down from 8 to 1, and doubling or halving compute halfway through training produces comparable results to constant allocation with the same total. This decouples training progress from the instantaneous compute allocation, which is a prerequisite for volunteer computing (Petals), spot/preemptible instance usage, and shared cluster scheduling.

The innovation is primarily empirical — a demonstration rather than a new technique — but it shifts the conversation around distributed training from "what is the best algorithm under ideal conditions?" to "what is the best algorithm under realistic conditions?" This is incremental as a methodological contribution but significant as a demonstration of practical viability. The one weakness: all stress tests are applied to the 150M model on C4, so the graceful degradation curves at larger scale or on other tasks remain unknown.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the C4 dataset (Raffel et al., 2020), a large corpus derived from Common Crawl web data. The paper reports perplexity on the C4 validation set. Experiments consider both i.i.d. (random partitioning) and non-i.i.d. (clustered) data regimes, with the non-i.i.d. setting constructed by clustering the training set using `k`-means on features from a pretrained model's last layer, following Gururangan et al. (2023). The non-i.i.d. setting is used as the default in most experiments because it is "a more challenging use case."

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use decoder-only transformers adapted from the Chinchilla architecture (Hoffmann et al., 2022). Three model sizes are evaluated: 60M parameters (3 layers, hidden dim 896, 16 heads, K/V size 64), 150M parameters (12 layers, hidden dim 896, 16 heads, K/V size 64), and 400M parameters (12 layers, hidden dim 1536, 12 heads, K/V size 128). All use vocabulary size 32,000. The paper states these models were chosen to be "representative" of modern transformer LMs while being small enough for extensive ablation studies. By default, all models start from a checkpoint pretrained for 24,000 steps on the same C4 training set using standard single-worker AdamW training.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is validation perplexity (PPL) on the C4 validation set, reported against the number of training steps. The paper explicitly notes that training steps are "a good proxy for wall clock time since communication across workers is rather infrequent." Perplexity is computed in the standard way for language modeling: the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood of the validation sequences. The total number of training steps is fixed at 88,000 across all experiments (composed of some number of pretraining steps plus DiLoCo steps), enabling fair comparison between configurations that vary only in how those steps are allocated.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against four baselines (Table 2 and Figure 2):
  1. **Standard single-worker training (teal curve):** Starts from the 24,000-step pretrained model and trains for an additional 64,000 steps using the standard batch size (512). No communication overhead, baseline compute cost.
  2. **Standard training from scratch (red curve):** A model trained for the full 88,000 steps from random initialization, providing a lower bound on what is achievable without any pretraining.
  3. **8× batch size via data parallelism (purple curve, second row of Table 2):** Starts from the 24,000-step pretrained model and trains for 64,000 additional steps with batch size 4,096 (8× the standard). This requires communicating gradients at every step across 8× more devices, consuming 8× the data and compute of the single-worker baseline while keeping wall-clock time constant (1×). Communication cost: 8 × `N` (where `N` is the number of steps).
  4. **8× batch size via microbatching (third row of Table 2):** Achieves the same 4,096 effective batch size by accumulating gradients over 8 microbatches on a single worker. This requires 0 communication but increases wall-clock time 8× (since steps are serialized). Compute and data consumed: 8×.
  5. **8× number of updates (fourth row of Table 2):** The standard batch size run for 8× more steps (512,000 total), consuming 8× compute and 8× data without additional communication. This represents the upper bound on what is achievable given unlimited time and compute.

  DiLoCo (last row of Table 2) is positioned against these baselines: it uses `k = 8` workers, each running the standard batch size for 64,000 steps, with communication every `H = 500` steps. Communication cost: 8 × `N/500` (500× less than the data-parallel baseline). Time: 1× (same as the single-worker baseline, since workers run in parallel). Compute and data: 8× (matching the 8× batch-size baselines).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute and data consumption are measured in multiples of the single-worker baseline (the "1×" in Table 2). A configuration using `k` workers for `N` steps each consumes `k` × `N` total inner steps of compute and sees `k` × `N` × (batch size) training tokens — this is the "Compute & Data" column. Communication cost is measured as the total number of gradient exchanges: standard data parallelism communicates `k` × `N` gradients (one per step per worker), while DiLoCo communicates `k` × `N/H` gradients (one per outer step per worker, since only the outer gradients are transmitted). Time is measured in multiples of the single-worker wall-clock time, assuming all workers run in parallel (so `k` workers for `N` steps still takes `N` steps of wall-clock time, minus any communication overhead).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for model selection. Hyperparameters for the outer optimizer (learning rate, momentum) are tuned on the 150M model in the non-i.i.d. setting and then applied to other configurations (other model sizes, numbers of replicas, data regimes). The paper acknowledges that hyperparameters "were tuned on the 150M model, which may be sub-optimal for the other model sizes" (in the context of Table 4), implying that reported results for 60M and 400M models may underestimate performance with proper per-size tuning. No statistical significance testing or confidence intervals are reported for any experiment — results are presented as point estimates of validation perplexity.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### DiLoCo vs. Fully Synchronous Baselines

**Headline result (Figure 2, Table 2):** DiLoCo with 8 workers in the non-i.i.d. setting (blue curve) achieves validation perplexity 15.02, which is **better than the 8× batch-size data-parallel baseline (15.30, purple curve) while communicating 500× less** (once every 500 steps vs. every step) and running in the same wall-clock time (1×). It also outperforms the single-worker baseline (16.23, teal curve) but underperforms the 8× updates baseline (14.72), which achieves better perplexity at the cost of being 8× slower.

The comparison is structured around Table 2's trade-off dimensions:

- **Communication:** Baseline data parallelism communicates 8 × `N` gradients (where `N` is the total number of steps). DiLoCo communicates 8 × `N/500` gradients — a 500× reduction. This is the core communication efficiency claim.
- **Time:** DiLoCo and the data-parallel baseline both take 1× wall-clock time (8 workers running in parallel for 64,000 steps each). The microbatched baseline takes 8× time (serialized gradient accumulation). The 8× updates baseline takes 8× time (8× more sequential steps).
- **Compute & Data:** DiLoCo, the data-parallel baseline, and the microbatched baseline all consume 8× the compute and data of the single-worker baseline. The 8× updates baseline also consumes 8× compute and data but over a longer wall-clock period.
- **Perplexity:** DiLoCo at 15.02 outperforms both the standard data-parallel 8× batch-size baseline (15.30) and the microbatched 8× batch-size baseline (15.30 — same model, different implementation). It underperforms the 8× updates baseline (14.72) but achieves its result in 8× less wall-clock time.

The critical comparison is DiLoCo vs. the 8× batch-size data-parallel baseline (row 2 of Table 2): both use identical compute, data, and wall-clock time, but DiLoCo communicates 500× less *and* achieves better perplexity (15.02 vs. 15.30). This is the paper's central claim in numerical form: DiLoCo is not merely communication-efficient — it can actually outperform the synchronous equivalent at the same total compute budget.

**Interpretation note:** The 8× batch-size baseline represents what standard data parallelism can achieve when scaling to 8× the devices — it requires high-bandwidth interconnects for per-step gradient all-reduce, which DiLoCo does not. The microbatched baseline represents what a single worker can achieve with the same 8× compute budget by serializing — it takes 8× longer. DiLoCo achieves both: the speed of data parallelism (workers run in parallel) and the communication profile of something closer to microbatching (infrequent gradient exchange), while matching or exceeding the perplexity of both approaches.

#### Impact of Pretraining Steps (Figure 3)

**Headline result:** DiLoCo is robust to the number of pretraining steps. Even starting from a randomly initialized network (0 pretraining steps, teal curve), final perplexity degrades by only ~0.1 PPL relative to starting from 24,000 pretraining steps (red curve). The vertical dashed lines in Figure 3 mark the transition from pretraining to DiLoCo training.

The paper sweeps pretraining steps over `{0, 12,000, 24,000, 48,000}`, always keeping total training steps fixed at 88,000 so that fewer pretraining steps means more DiLoCo steps. Key observations:

- **No pretraining (teal):** Achieves "minimal (-0.1 PPL) degradation of model quality" relative to the 24,000-step pretrained starting point. This is notable because prior local SGD work (Ortiz et al., 2021) found pretraining necessary for convergence at scale. The paper does not quantify the exact PPL values for this condition, only the relative degradation.
- **12,000 pretraining steps (purple):** Performance is visually indistinguishable from 24,000 pretraining steps in the final phase of training, though early convergence is slightly slower immediately after the transition to DiLoCo.
- **24,000 pretraining steps (red, default):** Used for all other experiments in the paper.
- **48,000 pretraining steps (orange):** Performs similarly to 24,000 but, as the paper notes, operates in a different regime — DiLoCo has fewer outer steps to contribute since more of the total 88,000-step budget was consumed by pretraining.

The transient perplexity spikes visible after the dashed lines are attributed to "a warm-up of the inner learning rate" — when DiLoCo begins, the inner optimizer's learning rate is re-warmed from a low value. Despite these temporary spikes, the paper states this warm-up is "ultimately beneficial, as previously noted also in the continual pretraining setting by Gupta et al. (2023)."

This result supports the paper's claim that DiLoCo does not require a carefully pretrained initialization. However, the quantitative degradation (~0.1 PPL) is small enough that the practical recommendation would still be to use whatever pretraining budget is available — the benefit of starting closer to convergence is that DiLoCo's outer steps can focus on refinement rather than initial learning.

#### Communication Frequency: Varying H (Figure 4)

**Headline result:** More frequent communication improves perplexity, but with sharply diminishing returns beyond `H = 500`. At `H = 1,000`, perplexity increases by only 2.9% relative to `H = 50` (the most frequent setting tested), despite communicating 20× less.

The paper sweeps `H` ∈ `{50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 2000}` in the non-i.i.d. setting. The curves for all `H` values follow similar trajectories. The ordering is monotonic: lower `H` (more frequent communication) yields better perplexity throughout training. However, the gap narrows considerably in the highly infrequent regime.

Specific comparisons:
- **H = 50 vs. H = 500:** The paper does not report the exact numerical gap between these settings at the final step, but states that "communicating more frequently than H = 500 steps leads to diminishing returns." From the plot, H = 500 appears only slightly above H = 50 in final perplexity.
- **H = 500 vs. H = 1,000:** The paper reports that at H = 1,000, "the perplexity increases by only 2.9% relative to H = 50, despite communicating 20× less." This is the key number establishing that large-H local SGD is practically viable.
- **H = 2,000 (green curve):** Shows the largest gap from more frequent settings, with visibly worse convergence throughout training. The paper does not report a specific perplexity value for this setting.

The choice of H = 500 as the default for all remaining experiments is justified as striking "a good trade-off between generalization performance and communication cost." This is a practical engineering choice rather than a theoretically motivated optimum.

#### i.i.d. vs. Non-i.i.d. Data Regimes (Figure 5)

**Headline result:** DiLoCo converges faster in the i.i.d. setting early in training, but both data regimes attain comparable final generalization performance. This demonstrates robustness to data distribution heterogeneity that is stronger than expected.

The i.i.d. setting (red curve) tracks below the non-i.i.d. setting (blue curve) through most of training — meaning better (lower) perplexity at each step count. However, the curves converge toward the end of the 64,000 DiLoCo steps. The paper states: "Despite the latter converging faster early on in training, the final generalization performance of the two settings is comparable."

This result is non-obvious because the non-i.i.d. setting creates workers with semantically distinct data distributions (via k-means clustering). Intuitively, workers optimizing on different data distributions should produce more divergent outer gradients, making it harder for the outer optimizer to find a single parameter vector that works well for all data. The fact that final performance is comparable suggests either that (a) workers do not diverge as much as expected (supported by the cosine similarity analysis in Appendix 6.2, which shows outer gradients remain correlated even at H = 500), or (b) the outer optimizer successfully reconciles divergent signals into a model that generalizes broadly.

The paper speculates that the non-i.i.d. outer gradient diversity may act as a regularizer, helping the final shared model generalize better — essentially, the tension between workers' divergent local solutions pushes the shared parameters into a region that is simultaneously good for all data distributions. However, this hypothesis is not directly tested.

#### Number of Replicas (Table 3)

**Headline result:** More replicas improve perplexity, but with diminishing returns beyond 8 workers. At k = 64, further gains are essentially zero (14.95 i.i.d., 14.96 non-i.i.d.) compared to k = 16 (15.02 i.i.d., 14.91 non-i.i.d.).

The paper sweeps `k` ∈ `{4, 8, 16, 64}` in both i.i.d. and non-i.i.d. settings, with a fixed number of inner steps per replica (64,000 DiLoCo steps). Results from Table 3:

| Replicas `k` | i.i.d. PPL | non-i.i.d. PPL |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (baseline) | 16.23 | — |
| 4 | 15.23 | 15.18 |
| 8 | 15.08 | 15.02 |
| 16 | 15.02 | 14.91 |
| 64 | 14.95 | 14.96 |

The key pattern: moving from 1 to 4 to 8 replicas brings substantial gains (16.23 → 15.23 → 15.08 in i.i.d.). Moving from 8 to 16 brings smaller gains (15.08 → 15.02 i.i.d., 15.02 → 14.91 non-i.i.d.). Moving from 16 to 64 brings almost no gain in non-i.i.d. (14.91 → 14.96 — a slight regression) and only modest gain in i.i.d. (15.02 → 14.95).

The paper draws two contrasts with prior work:
1. Unlike Ortiz et al. (2021), who found degradation with ≥16 replicas on ImageNet, DiLoCo continues to improve (or at least not degrade) with up to 64 replicas.
2. The diminishing returns beyond 8 workers are identified as a limitation: the algorithm does not efficiently leverage additional compute beyond this point.

The paper hypothesizes two factors that might explain the continued scaling (or lack of degradation): "(1) in an overtrained setting with large amount of steps, larger models are more efficient at fitting the same amount of data, and (2) as the linear connectivity literature (Ilharco et al., 2022) suggests, larger models are less subject to interference when averaging their parameters." The second point is particularly relevant — if larger models have broader linearly connected basins, then a larger number of replicas can diverge further without encountering loss barriers during averaging.

Note that at `k = 64` in the non-i.i.d. setting, the paper applies per-shard rescaling of outer gradients (weighted by shard size) because "imbalance can be striking." It is unclear whether the slight regression at `k = 64` non-i.i.d. (14.91 → 14.96) is due to this imbalance, the rescaling strategy, or genuine saturation.

#### Model Size Scaling (Table 4)

**Headline result:** DiLoCo's improvement over the single-worker baseline is consistent across model sizes (60M, 150M, 400M), with absolute improvement of ~1 PPL for all three sizes, though relative improvement is larger for the larger models (7.49% for 400M vs. 4.33% for 60M).

Results from Table 4:

| Model Size | Relative Improvement | Absolute Improvement (PPL) |
|---|---|---|
| 60M | 4.33% | 1.01 |
| 150M | 7.45% | 1.21 |
| 400M | 7.49% | 1.01 |

Each row compares DiLoCo with 8 workers in the non-i.i.d. setting against the single-worker baseline for that model size. The absolute improvement is roughly constant (~1 PPL) across model sizes, while the relative improvement grows with model size (since the baseline perplexity is lower for larger models — the same 1 PPL improvement represents a larger fraction of the remaining error).

The paper acknowledges that hyperparameters were tuned on the 150M model, which "may be sub-optimal for the other model sizes." This means the reported improvements for 60M and 400M are likely lower bounds — per-size tuning could potentially improve these numbers.

The result suggests that DiLoCo's benefits scale with model size (contrary to the Ortiz et al., 2021 finding that local SGD "encounters challenges at scale"), providing tentative support for the paper's extrapolation that "DiLoCo might perform even better at larger scales, because there is less interference during the outer-gradient averaging step." However, this extrapolation is untested beyond 400M parameters.

#### Outer Optimizer Comparison (Figure 6)

**Headline result:** Nesterov momentum substantially outperforms SGD (FedAvg), SGD with momentum, and Adam (FedOpt) as the outer optimizer. Adam is particularly unstable, requiring ε = 0.1 to converge.

The paper sweeps four outer optimizer families:

- **SGD (red, FedAvg):** Performs "poorly" — the exact PPL is not reported, but the curve is visibly above (worse than) all others. This is equivalent to classical Federated Averaging (McMahan et al., 2017) with a tuned learning rate.
- **SGDM (standard momentum):** Improves over SGD but "underperforms Nesterov." The paper sweeps momentum = 0.9 and learning rates ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0}.
- **Adam (FedOpt, Reddi et al., 2021):** "Was particularly unstable with a high second-order momentum norm." The paper needed to increase ε to 0.1 (from the typical `10^{-8}`) to achieve stable training. Even with this fix, Adam underperforms Nesterov.
- **Nesterov momentum (the chosen optimizer):** With outer learning rate = 0.7 and momentum = 0.9, this "performs the best" and is "very robust." These values become the defaults for all experiments.

The paper also tested cosine decay of the outer learning rate but found "similar performance." The rationale: "since we decay the inner learning rate, the outer gradient norm gets naturally smaller over the course of training, removing the need to further decay the outer learning rate." This is a practically important simplification — it removes one hyperparameter (outer LR schedule) from the tuning burden.

The finding that Adam underperforms Nesterov is striking because Adam's per-parameter adaptive learning rates should, in principle, handle heterogeneous outer gradient scales better than a single global learning rate. The paper's hypothesis is that the outer gradient's statistical properties (representing the net effect of 500 AdamW steps) differ fundamentally from standard per-step gradients in ways that break Adam's second-moment normalization — large Nesterov updates in some parameter dimensions may actually be appropriate (reflecting genuine drift during the long inner phase), while Adam's normalization would shrink them.

#### Adaptive Compute Pool (Figure 7)

**Headline result:** Model quality depends on the total amount of compute used, not on when during training that compute is available. Ramping up, ramping down, doubling, and halving compute all achieve comparable final perplexity as long as the total compute budget is the same.

The paper tests six compute allocation schedules, all in the i.i.d. setting:

- **Constant Local (green):** 1 replica throughout — the single-worker baseline. Lowest total compute.
- **Constant Distributed (blue):** 8 replicas throughout — the standard DiLoCo setting. Highest total compute.
- **Doubling Compute (teal):** 4 replicas for the first half of training, 8 replicas for the second half. Total compute is intermediate (average of ~6 replicas throughout).
- **Halving Compute (purple):** 8 replicas for the first half, 4 replicas for the second half. Same total compute as Doubling Compute.
- **Ramping Up (red):** Linearly increasing from 1 to 8 replicas over the full training run.
- **Ramping Down (orange):** Linearly decreasing from 8 to 1 replicas over the full training run.

The key findings:
- **Constant Distributed performs best** because it uses the most total compute.
- **Doubling Compute and Halving Compute achieve similar final perplexity** despite the opposite allocation schedules — they have the same total compute.
- **Ramping Up and Ramping Down achieve similar final perplexity** — again, same total compute, opposite schedules.
- **Constant Local performs worst** because it uses the least total compute.

The paper concludes: "the factor determining the ultimate generalization ability of the model is the total amount of compute given to DiLoCo, but this is robust to how the budget is spread over time." This is a strong result for practical deployment: it means a training run can tolerate fluctuations in available workers without careful scheduling. If workers become unavailable mid-training, the remaining workers can continue, and the model will still reach approximately the same quality as long as enough total compute is eventually applied. Conversely, if additional workers become available late in training, they can be incorporated without disrupting convergence.

However, the paper does not test scenarios where the number of inner steps per worker varies over time (e.g., faster workers doing more steps between synchronizations). All schedules assume workers complete the same number of inner steps per outer step, just with varying numbers of workers.

#### Asynchronous Communication: Simulating Network Failures (Figure 8)

**Headline result:** DiLoCo is robust to communication failures. Even with 50% probability of dropping each worker's outer gradient, perplexity degrades by only 2.1% in the non-i.i.d. setting.

The paper simulates unreliable communication by randomly dropping each worker's outer gradient with probability `p` ∈ `{0%, 10%, 30%, 50%}`. When a worker's outer gradient is dropped, that worker continues training for the next H steps starting from its own local parameters `θ_i^(t)` rather than from the shared parameters `θ^(t)` — essentially, the worker skips one synchronization cycle and continues independently.

Results (extracted from the figure and paper text):

- In the **i.i.d. setting** (Figure 8a): Higher dropout probability leads to "more unstable learning with transient spikes in perplexity." The 0% curve (teal) is smoothest. The 50% curve (orange) shows visible spikes at the points where some workers miss synchronization.
- In the **non-i.i.d. setting** (Figure 8b): Same pattern of increased instability, but the paper reports that "even in the extreme non-i.i.d setting where each replica has 50% probability of dropping communication, the degradation of perplexity relative to perfect communication is only 2.1%."

The 2.1% degradation at 50% dropout is a striking result. It means that half of all outer gradient exchanges can fail (with affected workers continuing independently for another H steps) and the final model is only marginally worse. This has direct practical implications:
- **No synchronization barrier is strictly necessary:** Workers do not need to wait for all others to arrive at the synchronization point. If a worker is slow or temporarily disconnected, the outer step can proceed with the available workers.
- **Preemptible instances are viable:** If a worker is preempted and loses its outer gradient, it can simply continue from its local state and synchronize at the next opportunity.
- **Network reliability requirements are relaxed:** Occasional packet loss or timeout does not derail training.

The paper notes that this robustness means "the need of a synchronization barrier is less critical and thus training can be accelerated without having to wait all replicas." This is a meaningful practical advantage: in standard data-parallel training, a straggler slows down every step. In DiLoCo with asynchronous communication, stragglers only slow down their own progress, and the shared model continues improving using gradients from the available workers.

#### Accelerating a Single Worker: k = 1 with H ≫ 1 (Figure 9)

**Headline result:** DiLoCo applied to a single replica (`k = 1`) with `H = 500` inner steps provides both faster convergence and better final generalization than standard training, at zero communication cost.

In this configuration, there is no distributed computation — a single worker performs `H = 500` AdamW steps, then computes its own outer gradient `θ^(t-1) - θ_1^(t)` and applies the Nesterov outer optimizer locally to update its parameters. This is "equivalent at interpolating between the starting and end points of a phase in the parameters space" and is related to the Lookahead optimizer (Zhang et al., 2019).

The paper shows that this single-worker DiLoCo (the curve in Figure 9) converges faster and achieves better final perplexity than standard AdamW training on the same total step budget. This experiment is included as evidence that DiLoCo's outer optimization mechanism is beneficial even without distribution across workers — the Nesterov outer step on parameter deltas spanning many inner steps provides a regularization or smoothing effect that improves generalization.

The practical implication: even practitioners who cannot distribute training across multiple clusters can benefit from DiLoCo's outer optimization framework as a drop-in improvement to standard single-worker training, with no communication overhead.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Pretraining step count (Figure 3):** DiLoCo is remarkably robust to initialization quality. Starting from a randomly initialized network (0 pretraining steps) degrades final perplexity by only ~0.1 PPL relative to 24,000 pretraining steps, and all pretraining budgets from 0 to 48,000 steps achieve "similar final PPL." This directly contradicts Ortiz et al. (2021), who found pretraining necessary for local SGD convergence. The transient perplexity spikes at transition points are attributed to inner learning rate warm-up and are ultimately beneficial.

- **Communication frequency H (Figure 4):** Sweeping H from 50 to 2,000 reveals that frequent communication helps, but diminishing returns set in strongly after H = 500. At H = 2,000, performance visibly degrades but training remains stable. The paper's default H = 500 is an engineering trade-off, not a theoretically optimal point. The 2.9% relative degradation at H = 1,000 vs. H = 50 quantifies the cost of 20× communication reduction.

- **i.i.d. vs. non-i.i.d. data (Figure 5):** Final perplexity is comparable across data regimes despite non-i.i.d. workers seeing semantically distinct data distributions. Non-i.i.d. converges slower early but catches up — the paper speculates this is due to a regularization effect from diverse outer gradients. The cosine similarity analysis (Appendix 6.2, Figures 10–11) provides supporting evidence: outer gradients remain correlated even at H = 1,000, with similarity inversely proportional to H (larger H → less correlated, but still similar enough for effective averaging). In the non-i.i.d. setting, similarity variance increases late in training as the learning rate decays, suggesting workers fall into different nearby loss basins — yet averaging still works.

- **Number of replicas k (Table 3):** Gains from more replicas saturate beyond k = 8. The paper tests k = 4, 8, 16, 64 and finds that 8 → 16 provides small gains, while 16 → 64 provides essentially none (and a slight regression in non-i.i.d. from 14.91 to 14.96). This is identified as a limitation. The saturation is not explained mechanistically, though the paper suggests that larger models might scale better with more replicas due to reduced interference during averaging (Ilharco et al., 2022).

- **Model size (Table 4):** DiLoCo's absolute perplexity improvement over the single-worker baseline is roughly constant (~1 PPL) across 60M, 150M, and 400M models, while relative improvement grows (4.33% → 7.45% → 7.49%). The paper acknowledges sub-optimal tuning for non-150M models, so these are lower bounds. The result provides tentative evidence for the extrapolation that DiLoCo may perform better at larger scales.

- **Outer optimizer choice (Figure 6):** Nesterov dominates SGD, SGDM, and Adam. The failure of Adam is notable — it required ε = 0.1 for stability and still underperformed Nesterov. The paper hypothesizes this is due to outer gradient statistics differing from per-step gradient statistics, but does not provide quantitative evidence (e.g., histograms of outer gradient magnitudes). Cosine decay of the outer learning rate was tested and found to provide no benefit ("similar performance"), simplifying the hyperparameter set.

- **Outer gradient pruning (Table 6):** Sign-based pruning of up to 50% of individual outer gradient values (per-worker, before averaging) incurs negligible perplexity degradation (+0.39% at 50% pruning). At 75% pruning, degradation is +1.66%. This demonstrates that DiLoCo's communication cost can be further reduced through standard compression techniques without significant quality loss.

- **Inner optimizer state synchronization:** Tested but not included in the main results. Synchronizing AdamW's first and second moment estimates across workers during the outer step "did not lead to significant improvements while significantly increasing the communication cost (×3 more data to transmit)." This finding aligns with prior federated learning work (Ortiz et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2020) and justifies DiLoCo's design choice of communicating only parameter deltas.

- **ReST^EM revision model results:** Not applicable to this paper — no revision model experiments are conducted.

- **Rescaling outer gradients for unbalanced non-i.i.d. shards:** At k = 64 in the non-i.i.d. setting, shard sizes can be "strikingly" imbalanced. The paper applies per-shard rescaling proportional to shard size. The effectiveness of this rescaling is not isolated via ablation (e.g., comparing uniform vs. weighted averaging at k = 64).

### Critical Assessment

This paper makes one central empirical claim: **DiLoCo enables training of transformer language models across poorly connected compute islands with minimal communication overhead, matching or exceeding the performance of fully synchronous training while communicating 500× less.** The experimental evidence supporting this claim is strong but bounded — the claim is validated for a specific model family (Chinchilla-style decoders), a specific dataset (C4), a specific scale range (60M–400M parameters), and a specific task (language modeling perplexity). Whether it generalizes beyond these bounds is an open question that the paper itself flags as a limitation.

**What the experiments convincingly demonstrate:**

1. **The large-H regime is viable.** Figure 4 provides clear evidence that H = 500–1,000 works with only minor degradation (2.9% relative perplexity increase at H = 1,000 vs. H = 50). This directly contradicts the prior finding from Ortiz et al. (2021) that local SGD degrades at H ≥ 8 and k ≥ 16. The robustness is replicated across data regimes (Figure 5), replicas (Table 3), model sizes (Table 4), and initialization conditions (Figure 3). The consistency of this finding across multiple stress axes is the paper's strongest evidence.

2. **The AdamW-Nesterov combination is better than standard FedAvg/FedOpt.** Figure 6's systematic comparison of outer optimizers provides strong evidence that Nesterov momentum outperforms the SGD and Adam alternatives commonly used in federated learning. The failure of Adam is a practically important negative result that could save future practitioners from pursuing a dead end.

3. **DiLoCo degrades gracefully under realistic failure modes.** The adaptive compute (Figure 7) and asynchronous communication (Figure 8) experiments demonstrate robustness that is unusual in the distributed training literature. The 2.1% perplexity degradation at 50% communication dropout in the non-i.i.d. setting is a strong result that supports the paper's framing of DiLoCo as a practical system rather than a theoretical algorithm.

**Where the evidence is weaker or incomplete:**

1. **The 500× communication reduction claim is slightly misleading as a standalone number.** DiLoCo communicates once every 500 inner steps, but when it does communicate, it transmits full parameter deltas (the same size as the model itself). A standard data-parallel step transmits gradients (also the same size as the model). So the per-communication-step data volume is identical — the 500× reduction comes entirely from reduced frequency, not reduced per-message size. The outer gradient pruning results (Table 6) suggest that per-message size can also be reduced (up to 50% with negligible impact), but the 500× figure in the abstract and main claims is purely about frequency.

2. **The comparison against the 8× batch-size baseline may not be entirely fair.** DiLoCo with k = 8 workers consumes 8× the compute and 8× the data of the single-worker baseline. The 8× batch-size data-parallel baseline (row 2 of Table 2) also consumes 8× compute and data. But DiLoCo executes 8 independent AdamW trajectories for H = 500 steps each between synchronizations, while the data-parallel baseline synchronizes every step. In principle, the data-parallel baseline could benefit from the same 8× data by using a larger batch size with a properly tuned learning rate — the paper does not discuss whether the baseline's hyperparameters were re-tuned for the 8× batch size, which is known to require adjustments (e.g., learning rate scaling, warmup ratio changes). The baseline perplexity of 15.30 may therefore underrepresent what a well-tuned synchronous 8× batch-size run could achieve.

3. **The 8× updates baseline (14.72 PPL) handily beats DiLoCo (15.02 PPL), and the paper's framing of this as an "8× slower" tradeoff ignores that DiLoCo itself is slower than the single-worker baseline in total steps-to-solution.** The single-worker baseline completes 64,000 DiLoCo-phase steps. DiLoCo with k = 8 completes 64,000 inner steps per worker — but these are wall-clock parallel, so the training run finishes in the wall-clock time of 64,000 steps. The 8× updates baseline runs 512,000 steps sequentially and achieves better perplexity. The question is: is the extra 0.3 PPL worth 8× more wall-clock time? The paper frames this as a tradeoff, but does not quantify whether DiLoCo's 15.02 could be improved to approach 14.72 by, say, running DiLoCo for 2× or 4× more outer steps (which would still be faster than 8×). This experiment — scaling DiLoCo's total steps to match the 8× updates baseline's wall-clock time — is missing.

4. **Diminishing returns beyond k = 8 are a significant limitation that is acknowledged but not investigated.** Table 3 shows that going from 8 to 16 replicas provides only 0.06 PPL improvement in i.i.d. and 0.11 in non-i.i.d., while 16 to 64 provides essentially zero improvement (with a slight regression in non-i.i.d.). This means DiLoCo cannot efficiently leverage more than ~8 independent compute islands — a serious constraint for the "collaborative training across many small clusters" vision the paper motivates. The paper does not investigate why saturation occurs (is it the fixed total data? the fixed H? the outer optimizer?) or propose modifications to address it. This is flagged as a limitation but left entirely for future work.

5. **No experiment isolates the contribution of AdamW as the inner optimizer.** The paper attributes DiLoCo's success over prior work partly to using AdamW rather than SGD, but there is no ablation comparing AdamW vs. SGD as the inner optimizer at fixed H = 500. Without this, it is impossible to know whether the large-H viability is due to AdamW, Nesterov, both, or some other factor. The paper contrasts its results with Ortiz et al. (2021), who used SGD, but the comparison is across different datasets, tasks, and model architectures — it does not establish that AdamW is the differentiating factor.

6. **The scale is three orders of magnitude below state-of-the-art.** The largest model tested (400M parameters) is ~1,000× smaller than contemporary production language models. The paper extrapolates that DiLoCo may perform better at larger scales ("less interference during the outer-gradient averaging step"), but this is speculative. The linear connectivity literature does suggest that wider models are more linearly connected (Ilharco et al., 2022), which would support the extrapolation, but the paper does not provide any large-scale validation.

7. **Single dataset, single task.** All experiments use C4 for language modeling. There is no evidence about DiLoCo's behavior on other text corpora, other modalities (vision, speech), other architectures (encoder-decoder, diffusion models), or other tasks (fine-tuning, RLHF). The paper's claim that DiLoCo is "a robust and effective way to distribute training of transformer language models" is therefore supported only for autoregressive language modeling on web text. The failure of local SGD on ImageNet (Ortiz et al., 2021) suggests domain dependence — DiLoCo may similarly fail on vision tasks, and the paper provides no evidence either way.

8. **No statistical rigor.** All results are reported as point estimates of validation perplexity without confidence intervals, standard errors, or multiple seeds. The C4 validation set is large enough that variance should be small, but for claims about small differences (e.g., the 0.06 PPL improvement from 8 to 16 replicas in i.i.d.), it is unclear whether these differences are statistically meaningful or within noise. The two-fold cross-validation protocol used for compute-optimal strategy selection in the reference example paper is not present here.

9. **The cosine similarity analysis (Appendix 6.2) provides useful intuition but does not explain the mechanism.** Figures 10–11 show that outer gradients remain correlated at large H and that correlation decreases with more replicas in the non-i.i.d. setting. But correlation does not explain why averaging works — highly correlated gradients can still have destructive interference if their shared direction is away from the global minimum. The paper uses cosine similarity as a post-hoc diagnostic but does not use it to derive any design principle.

10. **Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:** (a) Scaling DiLoCo's total steps to match wall-clock time of the 8× updates baseline; (b) Ablation of inner optimizer choice (AdamW vs. SGD) to isolate its contribution to large-H viability; (c) Experiments on a second dataset or task to test generality; (d) Larger-scale experiments (1B+ parameters) to validate the extrapolation claim; (e) Comparison against locally-tuned outer optimizers per model size (the current results may underestimate performance at 60M and 400M); (f) Investigation of why replicas beyond ~8 provide no benefit — e.g., does reducing H or increasing total data per worker help at larger k?; (g) Combining outer gradient pruning with the asynchronous communication setting to test whether compression and unreliability interact negatively.

**Overall assessment:** The experiments support the paper's central practical claim — DiLoCo works for training small-to-medium transformer LMs on C4 with very infrequent communication — but the evidence base is narrower than the paper's framing suggests. The contribution is best understood as a **proof of concept** that large-H local SGD with the right optimizer combination can work for language modeling at moderate scale, directly contradicting prior pessimism from the vision domain. Whether this scales to production-scale LLM training (100B+ parameters, multiple datasets, diverse tasks) remains an open question that the paper identifies but does not resolve. The strongest findings — robustness to communication failure and dynamic compute — are genuinely novel and practically important, establishing design principles that future distributed training systems should adopt regardless of the specific algorithm.

The paper's value lies primarily in opening a research direction (large-H local SGD for language models) that the field had prematurely dismissed, and in providing a concrete recipe (AdamW + Nesterov, H = 500, η = 0.7, μ = 0.9) that other researchers can build on. The incomplete mechanistic understanding, the saturation at k > 8, and the small-scale validation are significant gaps, but they are gaps that the paper candidly acknowledges and that define a clear research agenda for follow-up work.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Scale: Three Orders of Magnitude Below Production Language Models

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use transformer models with 60M to 400M parameters, trained on the C4 dataset. The paper acknowledges this explicitly:

> "We have presented results at the scale of 60 to 400 million parameters. However, at the time of writing state-of-the-art language models use 3 orders of magnitude more parameters. Therefore, it would be interesting to see how DiLoCo works at larger scale."

The paper offers a speculative extrapolation: "DiLoCo might perform even better at larger scales, because there is less interference during the outer-gradient averaging step." This hypothesis draws on the linear connectivity literature (Ilharco et al., 2022), which suggests wider models have broader linearly connected basins, but DiLoCo provides no experimental evidence at scales beyond 400M.

**The consequence.** The practical question a practitioner needs answered is: *If I train a 70B or 175B parameter model using DiLoCo across 8 geographically distributed pods of GPUs, will the 500× communication reduction still hold with minimal perplexity degradation?* The paper cannot answer this. Several failure modes become more likely at scale:

- **Outer gradient divergence may increase with model size in ways not captured at 400M.** Larger models have more parameters and more complex loss landscapes. The cosine similarity analysis in Appendix 6.2 (Figures 10–11) shows outer gradients remain correlated at H = 500 for 150M models, but this correlation might break down for models 1,000× larger where the loss landscape has more degrees of freedom for workers to diverge into disconnected basins.

- **The H = 500 default was tuned at 150M.** At 100B+ parameters, the optimal communication frequency might differ substantially. The inner optimization dynamics (AdamW step size per parameter, effective distance traveled in parameter space per step) may not scale linearly with model size, so the relationship between H and worker divergence is unknown.

- **The diminishing returns beyond k = 8 replicas** (Table 3) might become more severe or less severe at scale. If larger models are indeed more linearly connected, the saturation point might shift to higher k. Conversely, if the saturation is due to each worker seeing too little data at high k (since total data is fixed), this problem persists or worsens at larger scale.

- **Communication volume grows linearly with model size.** A 175B parameter model requires transmitting 175B floating-point values (typically ~700GB in FP32) per outer gradient, per worker. While DiLoCo communicates 500× less *frequently* than standard data parallelism, each communication event transmits the full model. At production scale, this single communication burst might saturate available inter-cluster bandwidth for minutes or hours, undermining the wall-clock time advantages. The paper's outer gradient pruning results (Table 6: 50% pruning costs +0.39% perplexity at 150M) suggest potential mitigation, but this has not been validated at scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 shows consistent ~1 PPL improvement from DiLoCo across 60M, 150M, and 400M models, suggesting the benefits do not degrade from 60M to 400M. The paper frames this as tentative support for the scaling extrapolation, but a trend across a 6.7× parameter range (60M → 400M) provides weak evidence for a 250× further jump (400M → 100B). The paper explicitly acknowledges this gap and lists larger-scale validation as future work.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats large-scale validation as a direction for future work and offers only the speculative linear-connectivity-based extrapolation as comfort. A practitioner considering DiLoCo for production-scale training would need to conduct their own scaling experiments before committing.

---

### Diminishing Returns Beyond 8 Workers: The Algorithm Cannot Efficiently Leverage Large Distributed Pools

**The assumption or constraint.** DiLoCo's motivating vision includes pooling many distributed compute islands — the paper references collaborative systems like Petals (Borzunov et al., 2022) where "individual users provide their own devices to the shared compute pool." This vision requires DiLoCo to scale effectively to tens or hundreds of workers. The experimental results in Table 3 demonstrate that it does not:

> "DiLoCo exhibits diminishing returns beyond 8 workers. Another avenue of future research is to improve the algorithm to better leverage any additional compute that might be available."

The data from Table 3 shows the problem clearly: moving from 8 to 16 replicas provides 0.11 PPL improvement in non-i.i.d. (15.02 → 14.91), and moving from 16 to 64 replicas provides essentially zero improvement (14.91 → 14.96, a slight regression). The bulk of DiLoCo's benefit is captured at k = 8, and additional workers contribute almost nothing.

**The consequence.** This saturation severely limits DiLoCo's applicability to the "distributed collaborative training" scenario the paper motivates. If a research consortium pools 50 GPU clusters across 50 universities, DiLoCo with k = 50 would not perform meaningfully better than DiLoCo with k = 8 — the extra 42 clusters would consume compute and data without improving the model. This is not just inefficiency; it is a **cap on how much distributed compute DiLoCo can usefully absorb** in its current form.

Several mechanisms could explain the saturation (none are isolated in the paper):

- **Fixed total data:** All experiments keep the total training data fixed at 88,000 steps × batch size 512 × sequence length 1,024 tokens. When k increases, each worker sees proportionally less data during its inner phase (since data is partitioned across more workers). For a fixed H = 500, a worker with k = 64 sees 1/64th of the data that a worker with k = 1 would process in those 500 steps — but the 500 steps are still 500 gradient updates, just on a smaller, less representative data shard. The quality of each worker's local optimization may degrade as its data shard becomes too small to capture the global data distribution, making its outer gradient less informative.

- **Outer gradient noise increases with k.** Appendix Figure 11 shows that outer gradient similarity decreases as k increases from 4 to 8 in the non-i.i.d. setting. The paper does not show this trend for k = 16 or k = 64, but extrapolating, outer gradients likely become increasingly dissimilar as data shards become more specialized and smaller. The Nesterov outer optimizer must average increasingly divergent signals, and at some point the average becomes uninformative.

- **The outer learning rate and momentum were tuned at k = 8.** These hyperparameters may be suboptimal at k = 64. A larger k with noisier outer gradients might benefit from a smaller outer learning rate or higher momentum to smooth the increased noise — but the paper uses the same η = 0.7, μ = 0.9 across all k.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides the direct evidence. The paper also reports in Appendix 6.2 that "in this setting the averaged outer gradient's norm is inversely proportional to the square root of the number of replicas" — as k increases, the outer gradient shrinks in magnitude, which may explain why additional replicas contribute less (their individual contributions are diluted in the average). Figure 11 shows decreasing outer gradient similarity with increasing k, supporting the hypothesis that worker divergence increases with k. The paper does not provide analogous similarity analysis for k > 8.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies this as a limitation and suggests future work to "improve the algorithm to better leverage any additional compute that might be available," but proposes no specific mitigation strategies. Potential directions not explored include: scaling total data with k (so each worker maintains a minimum shard size), reducing H as k increases (more frequent communication to prevent divergence), using hierarchical outer optimization (clusters of workers synchronize internally before global synchronization), or adjusting outer optimizer hyperparameters as a function of k. The saturation at k ≈ 8 is one of the paper's most practically significant limitations and receives surprisingly little investigation given its importance to the motivating use cases.

### Data and Compute Efficiency: DiLoCo Is Less Efficient Than Simply Running More Synchronous Updates

**The assumption or constraint.** DiLoCo's headline result — matching or beating the 8× batch-size baseline while communicating 500× less — assumes a fixed wall-clock time budget. But the paper's Table 2 reveals a different comparison: the "8× updates" baseline (standard single-worker training run for 8× more steps) achieves perplexity 14.72, substantially better than DiLoCo's 15.02. The paper acknowledges this:

> "DiLoCo attains fast convergence in terms of wall-clock time. However, the distributed nature of the computation reduces the FLOP and data efficiency of the model, as shown by the 8× updates row in Table 2."

The mechanism: DiLoCo with k = 8 workers consumes 8× the compute and 8× the data of the single-worker baseline (because 8 workers each run 64,000 inner steps), but produces a model that is only ~1.2 PPL better than the single-worker baseline (16.23 → 15.02). The 8× updates baseline also consumes 8× the compute and 8× the data (by running 512,000 steps sequentially on one worker), and produces a model that is ~1.5 PPL better (16.23 → 14.72). So for the same total compute and data budget, **sequential training on a single worker produces a better model than distributed DiLoCo training**.

**The consequence.** This creates a fundamental tradeoff that the paper does not fully resolve: DiLoCo trades data/compute efficiency for wall-clock speed. If a practitioner has access to 8× the compute budget (8 workers' worth of GPUs) and wants the best possible model, they face a choice:

- **Run DiLoCo with k = 8:** Finish in wall-clock time T. Perplexity: 15.02.
- **Run standard training for 8× more steps on a single worker:** Finish in wall-clock time 8T. Perplexity: 14.72.
- **Run DiLoCo with k = 8 for 2× more outer steps:** Finishes in wall-clock time 2T (since workers still run in parallel), consuming 16× total compute and data. Perplexity: unknown — the paper does not run this experiment.

The paper's framing emphasizes the first vs. second comparison (DiLoCo is 8× faster), but the more relevant comparison for many practitioners is: *how does the Pareto frontier of (wall-clock time, perplexity) look for DiLoCo vs. standard training?* If DiLoCo for 2T achieves perplexity close to 14.72, then DiLoCo recovers the efficiency gap with acceptable slowdown. If not, then DiLoCo is genuinely less compute-efficient, and practitioners must decide whether wall-clock time savings justify the lower model quality per FLOP.

The paper frames this issue as one of "balancing wall-clock time efficiency with compute efficiency and data efficiency," but provides no systematic exploration of this tradeoff. The "8× updates" baseline is presented once (Table 2) and the efficiency gap is not analyzed further.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides the key numbers: DiLoCo 8× compute yields 15.02 PPL; 8× updates baseline yields 14.72 PPL. This is a 0.3 PPL gap representing 2% relative improvement for the baseline. The paper does not report whether this gap narrows or widens with more DiLoCo outer steps, different k values, or different H values. The diminishing returns with k (Table 3) suggest that adding more workers to DiLoCo is unlikely to close this gap, since beyond k = 8 additional workers contribute almost nothing.

**Mitigation status.** Partial. The paper identifies this as a limitation and speculates that "asynchronous variants of local SGD may allow distributed training with relatively more data-efficient updates," but provides no experiments in this direction. A natural experiment — scaling DiLoCo's outer steps to match the 8× updates baseline's wall-clock time and measuring whether perplexity approaches 14.72 — is not conducted. A practitioner cannot currently determine whether DiLoCo's efficiency gap is fundamental (inherent to the local SGD paradigm) or can be overcome by simply running DiLoCo for more outer steps.

### Single Dataset, Single Task, Single Architecture: No Evidence of Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses the C4 dataset for autoregressive language modeling with Chinchilla-style decoder-only transformers. The paper acknowledges this scope limitation:

> "We only considered a single task, namely language modeling, and a single architecture, a transformer. Other datasets, domains (e.g. vision), and other architectures (e.g., CNNs which are known to be more sensitive to linear mode connectivity (Jordan et al., 2023)) should also be considered."

This is not merely a "we didn't test X" caveat — it is a significant concern because the prior work DiLoCo most directly contradicts (Ortiz et al., 2021) found that local SGD *fails* at scale on ImageNet with ResNets. This establishes that the viability of large-H local SGD is domain-dependent: it worked poorly for vision classification with CNNs and works well (in this paper) for language modeling with transformers.

**The consequence.** A practitioner considering DiLoCo for anything other than autoregressive language modeling on web text — including fine-tuning language models on specific tasks, training encoder-decoder models (T5-style), training vision transformers or diffusion models, or applying DiLoCo to RLHF or instruction tuning — has no empirical basis for deciding whether it will work. The failure on ImageNet (Ortiz et al., 2021) provides a concrete example of domain dependence, and DiLoCo provides no evidence about where language modeling sits on the spectrum between "local SGD works robustly" (this paper) and "local SGD encounters challenges at scale" (Ortiz et al.).

Several mechanisms could cause domain dependence:

- **Loss landscape geometry:** Transformers trained on language modeling may have broader, more linearly connected basins than CNNs trained on ImageNet classification. If DiLoCo's success depends on linear connectivity between workers' endpoints (as the parameter-averaging outer step implies), then architectures or tasks with narrower basins will see workers diverge into incompatible solutions during the H = 500 inner steps, and averaging will produce a poor model.

- **Gradient noise characteristics:** Language modeling involves a large output space (vocabulary size 32,000) with sparse gradients (only the correct token receives a strong signal per position), while ImageNet classification has a smaller output space (1,000 classes) with denser gradients. These differences in gradient statistics might affect how quickly workers diverge during isolated training and how informative the outer gradient averaging is.

- **Optimal H likely differs across domains.** H = 500 was tuned for 150M transformers on C4. CNNs on ImageNet might require a different H, and the paper provides no methodology for determining the right H for a new domain without extensive experimentation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper's entire evidence base is C4 language modeling with Chinchilla decoders. The reference to Jordan et al. (2023) — showing that CNNs are more sensitive to linear mode connectivity — is the paper's own acknowledgment that generalization cannot be assumed. The appendix's cosine similarity analysis (Figures 10–11) provides a diagnostic tool that could be applied to other domains, but this is not demonstrated.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper lists this as a limitation and suggests future work on "other datasets, domains, and architectures." A practitioner considering DiLoCo for a new domain would need to replicate the paper's core experiments (communication frequency sweep, data regime comparison, replica scaling) from scratch, as the paper provides no transferable insights about which task or architecture properties predict DiLoCo's success.

### The 500× Communication Reduction Figure Overstates Practical Savings by Ignoring Per-Message Size and Latency Effects

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline claim — "communicating 500 times less" — compares DiLoCo's communication frequency (once every H = 500 steps) against standard data parallelism (every step). However, several factors make the practical communication savings less dramatic than this figure suggests:

**Per-message size is identical to standard training.** Each DiLoCo outer gradient `Δ^(t)` is the same size as the model parameters (and the same size as the gradients exchanged in standard data-parallel training). The paper notes that synchronizing inner optimizer states adds 3× more data but was avoided. The 500× reduction is purely in frequency, not in per-message volume. At each communication event, DiLoCo transmits exactly as much data as a single standard data-parallel step would transmit — but 500× less often.

**Communication events are bursty, not smoothed.** Standard data-parallel training spreads communication evenly across every step, transmitting relatively small gradient packets at high frequency. DiLoCo transmits nothing for 500 steps, then transmits the entire model's worth of outer gradients from all k workers simultaneously. For k = 8 workers, the coordinator must receive 8 full model replicas' worth of data (outer gradients), average them, and broadcast the updated parameters back. This burst can saturate network links that would handle smoothed gradient traffic without issue. The paper does not measure or model the peak bandwidth requirements of DiLoCo's communication events.

**Latency is not amortized.** In standard data-parallel training, gradient all-reduce latency is on the critical path of every training step. DiLoCo removes this latency from 499 out of 500 steps, but when communication does occur (every 500th step), the latency of transmitting full model-sized outer gradients across potentially intercontinental links is incurred all at once. The paper's wall-clock time analysis (Table 2, "Time" column) assumes workers run in parallel for the same number of steps as the baseline and that communication time is negligible. For geographically distributed workers with low-bandwidth, high-latency links, the communication burst at each outer step could take minutes or tens of minutes — this idle time is not accounted for in the wall-clock comparison.

**The consequence.** The practical communication savings depend heavily on the network topology and bandwidth between workers. In a scenario with moderate inter-cluster bandwidth (e.g., 1–10 Gbps links between university clusters), the 500× frequency reduction is partially offset by the bursty nature of the communication and the full-model-sized messages. The paper's framing suggests DiLoCo replaces the need for high-bandwidth interconnects, but the actual requirement is: enough bandwidth to transmit k full model replicas within the acceptable idle time between outer steps. For a 400M parameter model (1.6GB in FP32), k = 8 workers, and H = 500 inner steps taking perhaps 30–60 seconds total, the communication burst must complete in a small fraction of that time to avoid dominating wall-clock time. This may still be feasible; the paper provides no measurements to confirm or refute this.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 6 (outer gradient pruning) demonstrates that per-message size can be reduced — 50% pruning costs +0.39% perplexity — providing one mitigation strategy. The paper's infrastructure description (Appendix 6.1) states that "the outer optimization step is performed on a CPU server connected to the local machines" and that machines were "not necessarily co-located in the same geographic region," but provides no latency or bandwidth measurements, no wall-clock time breakdown separating computation from communication, and no sensitivity analysis of how communication latency affects total training time under different network conditions.

**Mitigation status.** Partial. The outer gradient pruning results (Table 6) show that compression can reduce per-message size with minimal quality loss, partially addressing the burst volume concern. The paper acknowledges that "when communication is required the network might get saturated, particularly when there are lots of workers, or when the model replicas are large" (Appendix 6.2), motivating the pruning experiments. However, the paper does not provide latency measurements, peak bandwidth requirements, or end-to-end wall-clock time analysis including communication overhead for geographically distributed workers. A practitioner cannot determine from the paper whether their specific inter-cluster links are sufficient without conducting their own measurements.

### The Linear Connectivity Hypothesis Is Invoked but Not Validated as the Mechanism Enabling DiLoCo

**The assumption or constraint.** Throughout the paper, DiLoCo's success is attributed to linear mode connectivity between workers' parameters — the empirical observation that independently trained neural networks can be averaged in parameter space without encountering a loss barrier (Frankle et al., 2020; Wortsman et al., 2021). The outer gradient formulation `Δ^(t) = average(θ^(t-1) - θ_i^(t))` with Nesterov momentum is essentially a smoothed linear interpolation between workers' endpoints, which only works if the averaged point lies in a low-loss region. The paper invokes this explicitly when explaining why DiLoCo scales with model size:

> "As the linear connectivity literature (Ilharco et al., 2022) suggests, larger models are less subject to interference when averaging their parameters."

And when analyzing outer gradient similarity (Appendix 6.2):

> "Since shards have a different distribution, each local optimization seems to fall in a different nearby loss basin. However, the averaging of such more orthogonal gradients grants beneficial generalization."

**The consequence.** The paper provides no direct evidence that linear connectivity is the mechanism — only that DiLoCo works, which is consistent with linear connectivity but does not prove it. This matters because linear connectivity is an empirical property that is known to hold under certain conditions (shared pretrained initialization, related tasks, specific architectures) and fail under others (CNNs, distant initializations, certain fine-tuning regimes; Jordan et al., 2023; Stoica et al., 2023). If DiLoCo's success depends on linear connectivity, then:

- DiLoCo may fail on architectures or tasks where linear connectivity does not hold (consistent with the Ortiz et al., 2021 failure on ImageNet CNNs).
- DiLoCo's robustness to non-i.i.d. data (Figure 5) is surprising if linear connectivity requires similar optimization trajectories — non-i.i.d. workers are optimizing toward different minima, which should produce endpoints that are *less* linearly connected. The paper's cosine similarity analysis (Figure 10b) shows that non-i.i.d. outer gradients have higher variance late in training, yet averaging still works. This suggests either (a) linear connectivity is more robust to trajectory divergence than expected, or (b) a different mechanism is at play (e.g., the outer optimizer's momentum smooths out barrier effects that would appear in single-shot averaging).

- The paper offers no diagnostic for predicting when DiLoCo will work on a new task or architecture. If linear connectivity is the mechanism, then a practitioner could test connectivity before committing to DiLoCo (e.g., train two replicas on different shards for H steps and measure the loss barrier when averaging). Without this validated mechanism, the practitioner must run full DiLoCo experiments to determine viability — an expensive proposition at scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The cosine similarity analysis in Appendix 6.2 (Figures 10–11) provides correlational evidence: outer gradients remain similar enough for averaging to be meaningful, and similarity decreases with H and k (as expected if workers diverge over longer isolated training). However, cosine similarity between outer gradients is not the same as linear connectivity between parameter endpoints. Two workers could have highly correlated outer gradients (they moved in similar directions) while their endpoints are separated by a loss barrier (the loss landscape has high curvature between them). Conversely, workers could have uncorrelated outer gradients while their endpoints remain linearly connected. The paper does not measure the actual loss along the linear interpolation between workers' parameters before averaging — the standard diagnostic from the linear connectivity literature (Frankle et al., 2020) — which would directly test whether connectivity is the enabling mechanism.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats linear connectivity as a motivating intuition and uses cosine similarity as a post-hoc diagnostic, but does not design experiments to test whether connectivity is necessary or sufficient for DiLoCo's success. A simple experiment — measuring the loss at the averaged parameters before applying the outer optimizer, and comparing this to the loss at each worker's individual parameters — would directly test whether averaging alone (without Nesterov momentum) preserves low loss, which is the defining property of linear connectivity. This experiment is not conducted. The paper's contribution remains primarily empirical (a recipe that works) rather than mechanistic (an explanation of why it works), and the lack of mechanistic understanding makes it harder for practitioners to anticipate failure modes or adapt DiLoCo to new settings.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around distributed training from an assumption of **tightly coupled, homogeneously provisioned supercomputers** toward a model where **geographically distributed, heterogeneous, and dynamically available compute resources are first-class training substrates**. Before this work, the dominant paradigm for scaling language model training was well-characterized: assemble an ever-larger cluster of identical accelerators in a single facility, connect them with high-bandwidth interconnects, and synchronize at every gradient step. The field's engineering investments — in interconnect topology, all-reduce algorithms, and fault tolerance — were all oriented around making this tightly coupled model work at larger scale. DiLoCo demonstrates an alternative path: relax the coupling, allow workers to drift independently for hundreds of steps, and use a momentum-based outer optimizer to reconcile their trajectories.

This is not a paradigm shift in the sense of introducing a fundamentally new algorithm — DiLoCo is explicitly a variant of Federated Averaging (McMahan et al., 2017) and FedOpt (Reddi et al., 2021). Rather, it is a **reframing of what the field should consider possible and worth pursuing**. The significance lies in directly contradicting the prior consensus, most clearly articulated by Ortiz et al. (2021), that "local SGD encounters challenges at scale." DiLoCo demonstrates robust operation at H = 500–1,000 (62.5–125× more inner steps than the threshold where Ortiz et al. observed failure), with k = 64 replicas (4× more than their problematic regime), and even without pretraining. This converts an empirical finding that had closed off a research direction into a demonstration that the direction is viable — it shifts the burden of proof from "can large-H local SGD work?" to "under what conditions does large-H local SGD work, and how do we make it work better?"

The practical consequence is a **decoupling of scaling ambition from co-location requirements**. Before DiLoCo, training a model across multiple university clusters, across cloud regions, or using a mix of GPU generations was not merely inefficient — it was considered impossible at scale. DiLoCo provides evidence that it is possible, at least at the 150M–400M parameter scale on language modeling, and provides a concrete recipe (AdamW + Nesterov, H = 500, η = 0.7, μ = 0.9) that other researchers can replicate, extend, or challenge. This reopens the possibility of collaborative, decentralized model training at a time when concerns about compute centralization and access inequality are growing.

**Resolving contradictions in the literature.** The paper provides a clean resolution to the contradictory findings between federated learning research (which showed local SGD can work at small scale and modest H) and Ortiz et al. (2021) (which showed it fails at larger scale). The resolution lies in the specific algorithmic choices: AdamW as the inner optimizer rather than SGD, Nesterov momentum as the outer optimizer rather than simple averaging or Adam, and the larger total training budget (88,000 steps of language modeling vs. typical ImageNet training durations). The paper does not fully isolate which factor is decisive — there is no ablation comparing AdamW vs. SGD as the inner optimizer at fixed H = 500 — but it provides enough evidence to shift the default assumption from "local SGD fails at scale" to "local SGD can work at scale with the right optimizer configuration."

**Research directions this work opens.** The paper makes large-H local SGD for language models a viable and empirically grounded research area. The diminishing returns beyond k = 8 workers (Table 3), the data/compute efficiency gap relative to the 8× updates baseline (Table 2), and the unresolved mechanism question (is it linear connectivity, or something else?) define clear research agendas. The cosine similarity analysis in Appendix 6.2 provides a diagnostic tool that future work can apply to other architectures, datasets, and scales. The paper also redirects attention away from certain directions: if Nesterov already outperforms Adam as the outer optimizer (Figure 6), further outer optimizer design for large-H local SGD should focus on momentum-based methods rather than adaptive per-parameter approaches, which the paper shows can be unstable.

**What the paper does NOT change.** The results are bounded to autoregressive language modeling on C4 with Chinchilla-style transformers at 60M–400M scale. The paper provides no evidence about vision, other NLP tasks (fine-tuning, RLHF, instruction tuning), or other architectures. The failure of local SGD on ImageNet (Ortiz et al., 2021) suggests domain specificity, and DiLoCo does not establish that language modeling generalizes to other domains. The paper also does not change the fundamental economics of very large-scale training: the saturation at k = 8 means DiLoCo cannot simply scale out to arbitrarily many distributed workers, and the communication bursts (full model deltas from all workers simultaneously) remain a practical bottleneck at production scales not tested here. The contribution is an existence proof and a recipe, not a universal solution.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling DiLoCo to production-scale language models (1B–100B+ parameters).** The paper's central extrapolation — that DiLoCo "might perform even better at larger scales, because there is less interference during the outer-gradient averaging step" — is entirely untested. A strong follow-up would train a 1B-parameter model (or larger, if resources permit) on C4 or a comparable corpus using DiLoCo with k = 8 workers and H = 500, and compare against both a standard synchronous baseline and a data-parallel 8× batch-size baseline at the same total compute. The key measurements would be: (a) Does the perplexity improvement over the single-worker baseline grow with model size, as Table 4 suggests it might? (b) Does the saturation at k = 8 shift to higher k for larger models, as linear connectivity theory predicts (Ilharco et al., 2022)? (c) What are the actual wall-clock times, including communication bursts over realistic inter-cluster links, for model sizes where parameter transmission takes non-negligible time? (d) Does the combination of outer gradient pruning (Table 6: 50% pruning costs +0.39% perplexity) and large model size amplify or reduce quality degradation? A negative result — DiLoCo degrades at 1B+ parameters — would be equally valuable, establishing that the mechanism does not scale and preventing wasted effort.

**Isolating the contribution of AdamW as the inner optimizer.** The paper attributes DiLoCo's success over prior local SGD work partly to the use of AdamW rather than SGD, but this is never tested directly. A controlled ablation would compare DiLoCo with identical outer optimizer (Nesterov, η = 0.7, μ = 0.9), identical H = 500, identical k = 8, and identical total steps, varying only the inner optimizer between AdamW and SGD (with appropriate per-optimizer learning rate tuning). The key question: does SGD as the inner optimizer reproduce the degradation that Ortiz et al. (2021) observed at large H, while AdamW avoids it? If AdamW is the critical enabler, the mechanism likely involves AdamW's adaptive per-parameter learning rates preventing the divergence that SGD experiences during 500 steps of isolated training. If SGD performs comparably, then the difference from prior work lies in the Nesterov outer optimizer or the language modeling domain rather than the inner optimizer — a finding that would redirect attention to outer optimization or domain properties. The experiment should also measure the distribution of per-parameter update magnitudes under both inner optimizers at the end of H = 500 steps, to quantify whether AdamW produces more "averaging-friendly" parameter deltas.

**Understanding and overcoming the k = 8 saturation.** The diminishing returns beyond 8 workers (Table 3) is DiLoCo's most practically significant limitation, directly constraining the collaborative training vision. A systematic investigation would vary k from 8 to 64 (or higher) while manipulating three potential causes of saturation: (a) **Total data per worker:** Fix the data per worker (rather than total data) as k increases, so that k = 16 sees 2× the total data of k = 8. If saturation is due to each worker's shard becoming too small to be representative, this should restore scaling. (b) **Communication frequency:** Reduce H as k increases (e.g., H = 500 at k = 8, H = 250 at k = 16, H = 125 at k = 32), hypothesizing that more workers diverge faster and need more frequent synchronization. This tests whether the optimal H is a function of k. (c) **Outer optimizer hyperparameters:** Re-tune η and μ at each k, since the outer gradient becomes noisier and smaller in norm (the paper notes outer gradient norm scales as 1/√k). A constant η = 0.7 may be too aggressive at k = 64, causing the shared parameters to overreact to noisy outer gradients. If none of these restore scaling beyond k = 8, the saturation may be fundamental — workers simply do not provide independent information beyond a certain point — and the collaborative training vision would need a different algorithmic approach (e.g., hierarchical synchronization where subsets of workers average internally before global averaging).

**Extending DiLoCo to other domains and architectures — with explicit connectivity diagnostics.** DiLoCo's success on language modeling and the known failure on ImageNet CNNs (Ortiz et al., 2021) suggest domain dependence. A systematic cross-domain study would test DiLoCo on: (a) Vision Transformers (ViT) on ImageNet — same architecture family as the paper, different data modality, to isolate whether the domain or the architecture is decisive. (b) CNNs (ResNet) on ImageNet — replicating Ortiz et al.'s negative result with DiLoCo's AdamW + Nesterov configuration, to test whether optimizer choices alone rescue local SGD on vision. (c) Encoder-decoder transformers (T5) on C4 — same data, different architecture, to test generalization across transformer variants. (d) Diffusion models or other generative architectures. For each setting, the study should include linear connectivity diagnostics: train two replicas for H = 500 steps, measure the loss at the midpoint of linear interpolation between their parameters, and compare to the loss at the endpoints. This directly tests whether connectivity is the enabling mechanism (midpoint loss ≈ endpoint loss → averaging should work) and whether domain/architecture differences in connectivity predict DiLoCo's success or failure. A finding that DiLoCo works whenever linear connectivity holds (and fails when it does not) would provide a cheap diagnostic — a practitioner could test connectivity with two short training runs before committing to a full DiLoCo deployment.

**Asynchronous DiLoCo with heterogeneous worker speeds.** The paper identifies asynchronous operation — where workers update the global parameters without waiting for all others to finish H steps — as a key future direction, motivated by real-world scenarios where workers have different hardware (Figure 1 shows TPUv4, V100, TPUv5, A100 in the same training run) and different speeds. The synchronous DiLoCo in the paper assumes all workers complete exactly H inner steps between each outer synchronization, meaning the fastest worker idles while waiting for the slowest. An asynchronous variant would allow each worker to send its outer gradient as soon as it completes H steps (or some variable number of steps), with the coordinator applying outer gradients sequentially as they arrive rather than in synchronized batches. The key design questions: (a) How to handle stale outer gradients — a worker that sends its delta based on a stale version of the shared parameters `θ` may push the model in a direction that was appropriate several outer steps ago but is now misleading. (b) Whether to scale the outer learning rate based on the staleness of each outer gradient. (c) Whether Nesterov momentum remains effective when outer gradients arrive at irregular intervals rather than in synchronized rounds. The paper's asynchronous communication experiments (Figure 8) — where 50% outer gradient dropout causes only 2.1% perplexity degradation — provide encouraging preliminary evidence that DiLoCo tolerates imperfect synchronization, but a true asynchronous design with variable H per worker and sequential outer updates is a different and harder problem.

**Closing the data/compute efficiency gap with the 8× updates baseline.** The paper shows that DiLoCo with k = 8 workers and 8× total compute achieves perplexity 15.02, while standard single-worker training for 8× more steps (also 8× total compute) achieves 14.72 — a 0.3 PPL gap. A targeted study would measure the full Pareto frontier: run DiLoCo for 2×, 4×, and 8× the default number of outer steps (varying T while keeping H = 500 fixed) and plot perplexity vs. total wall-clock time against the single-worker baseline run for 1×, 2×, 4×, and 8× steps. The key question: does DiLoCo's frontier cross the baseline's frontier at some compute multiple, or is DiLoCo Pareto-dominated at all operating points? If DiLoCo catches up to 14.72 PPL at, say, 2× outer steps (finishing in 2× the wall-clock time but still half the time of the 8× updates baseline), then the efficiency gap is not fundamental — DiLoCo simply needs more outer steps to match sequential training's data efficiency. If DiLoCo never catches up regardless of outer steps, then the local SGD paradigm genuinely trades per-FLOP efficiency for parallelism, and the decision to use DiLoCo is purely about wall-clock time vs. model quality. This experiment is straightforward to run at the paper's existing scale (150M parameters, C4) and would provide practitioners with the information needed to decide whether DiLoCo fits their time-quality tradeoff.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Pooling geographically distributed academic GPU clusters for collaborative model training.** The most direct application of DiLoCo is enabling research consortia — groups of universities, or a university with clusters across multiple campuses — to combine their compute resources for training runs that exceed what any single institution can host. With DiLoCo, a consortium with 8 member institutions each contributing a standard 8-GPU server (64 GPUs total, k = 8) could train a model using 8× the total compute of a single institution's cluster, communicating only once every 500 steps over commodity internet links. The paper's Table 2 quantifies the benefit: at k = 8, DiLoCo improves perplexity from 16.23 (single-worker baseline) to 15.02, a ~7.5% relative improvement (Table 4), while finishing in the same wall-clock time as a single institution would take for the baseline. The ~2.1% degradation at 50% communication dropout (Figure 8, non-i.i.d. setting) means the system tolerates the intermittent connectivity typical of inter-campus networks. The main practical constraint is the saturation at k = 8: consortia larger than ~8 institutions would not see proportional benefits without algorithmic improvements to address the diminishing returns.

**Training across cloud regions using preemptible or spot instances to reduce cost.** Cloud providers offer substantial discounts (often 60–90%) for preemptible instances that can be revoked at any time. Standard synchronous training cannot use these because losing a single worker halts the entire run. DiLoCo's robustness to worker dropout (Figure 8: 50% dropout probability → 2.1% perplexity degradation in non-i.i.d.) means that a training run using entirely preemptible workers — where any worker might disappear between outer steps — can continue with the remaining workers, and the affected worker can rejoin at the next outer step by resynchronizing from the shared parameters. The adaptive compute pool experiments (Figure 7) further demonstrate that the number of active workers can fluctuate over time without materially affecting final model quality, as long as total compute is sufficient. A cost-conscious team could run DiLoCo with k = 8 preemptible instances across multiple cloud regions, accepting that occasional worker preemptions will reduce effective k temporarily, and still achieve most of the perplexity benefit at a fraction of the cost of reserved instances. The paper does not provide a cost analysis, but the per-step communication savings (500× less frequent) also reduce the networking costs associated with inter-region data transfer compared to running standard data parallelism across regions.

**Enabling volunteer and decentralized computing for language model training.** Systems like Petals (Borzunov et al., 2022) and Diskin et al. (2021) allow individual volunteers to contribute their personal GPUs to a shared training pool over the public internet. DiLoCo's design — infrequent communication (once every 500 steps), robustness to asynchronous updates (Figure 8), and ability to handle dynamically changing worker counts (Figure 7) — aligns well with the requirements of such systems. A DiLoCo-based volunteer training run could operate as follows: a central coordinator maintains the shared parameters and dispatches them to any worker that joins the pool. Each worker trains locally for H = 500 steps on whatever data it has (potentially non-i.i.d., which DiLoCo handles; Figure 5) and sends back its outer gradient. The coordinator applies Nesterov momentum and updates the shared model. Workers can join and leave at any time without disrupting the run. The paper's adaptive compute experiments (Figure 7) directly model this scenario: ramping from 1 to 8 workers achieves similar results to constant 8 workers, so volunteer availability that fluctuates over time is acceptable. The main challenge is the saturation at k = 8: if hundreds of volunteers contribute, only ~8 of them contribute meaningful model improvement at any time, and the rest consume compute without substantially improving the shared model. This limits volunteer computing to scenarios where the goal is to accelerate training (8 volunteers finish 8× faster) rather than improve model quality through massive parallelism.

**Single-worker training acceleration via DiLoCo's outer optimization mechanism.** The paper's finding that DiLoCo applied to a single worker (k = 1, H = 500) improves both convergence speed and final perplexity over standard AdamW training (Figure 9) has immediate practical implications for practitioners who do not distribute training at all. The mechanism — periodically computing the net parameter displacement over H steps and applying a Nesterov outer update — is essentially a form of parameter-space smoothing that can be implemented as a wrapper around any existing AdamW training loop, with zero communication overhead. The paper reports that this single-worker DiLoCo variant converges faster and reaches better final perplexity than the AdamW baseline on the same total step budget. A practitioner could adopt this immediately by modifying their training script to: (a) save a checkpoint every H = 500 steps, (b) compute the parameter delta, (c) apply Nesterov momentum with η = 0.7, μ = 0.9 to update the model, and (d) continue training. The paper's outer optimizer comparison (Figure 6) validates that Nesterov with these hyperparameters is robust, and the learning rate warmup after each outer step (causing the transient spikes in Figure 3) is managed by standard inner LR scheduling. This is the most immediately actionable finding for practitioners who do not need distributed training but want better single-worker convergence.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions DiLoCo against several alternatives across the dimensions in Table 2, and the tradeoffs are clear enough to support explicit decision rules:

- **Prefer DiLoCo over standard synchronous data parallelism when:** You have access to multiple compute islands (k ≤ 8) that cannot be connected with high-bandwidth interconnects — for example, GPUs spread across different cloud regions, university campuses, or hardware generations (Figure 1). DiLoCo communicates 500× less frequently than data parallelism while achieving comparable or better perplexity at the same total compute (15.02 vs. 15.30, Table 2) and running in the same wall-clock time. The 2.1% perplexity degradation at 50% communication dropout (Figure 8) means DiLoCo tolerates the unreliable inter-island links that would make synchronous training impossible. Do NOT use DiLoCo if you can co-locate all your accelerators — standard data parallelism with high-bandwidth interconnects avoids the outer optimization complexity and does not face the k = 8 saturation limit.

- **Prefer DiLoCo over microbatching (gradient accumulation) when:** You need both increased compute throughput AND the same wall-clock time as single-worker training. Microbatching with an 8× effective batch size (Table 2, row 3) consumes 8× compute and data but runs 8× slower because steps are serialized. DiLoCo with k = 8 (Table 2, row 5) consumes the same 8× compute and data, achieves better perplexity (15.02 vs. 15.30), and runs in 1× wall-clock time because workers operate in parallel. If wall-clock time is not a constraint, the 8× updates baseline (row 4) achieves better perplexity (14.72) than either approach by running 8× more sequential steps — but takes 8× longer.

- **Prefer the 8× updates baseline (standard sequential training) over DiLoCo when:** Perplexity per total FLOP matters more than wall-clock time, AND you can tolerate the 8× longer training duration. Table 2 shows the 8× updates baseline achieves 14.72 PPL vs. DiLoCo's 15.02 on the same total compute budget — a 2% relative improvement that compounds if you plan to use the model as a base for further fine-tuning. If your deployment timeline is flexible and your primary constraint is compute cost per unit model quality, running a single worker for more steps is more FLOP-efficient than distributing across DiLoCo workers. Whether this gap can be closed by running DiLoCo for more outer steps is an open question.

- **Prefer single-worker DiLoCo (k = 1, H ≫ 1) over standard AdamW when:** You want improved convergence and final perplexity at zero communication cost and no distributed infrastructure. Figure 9 shows that applying DiLoCo's outer optimization to a single worker (periodic Nesterov updates on parameter deltas spanning H = 500 inner steps) improves over standard AdamW on both convergence speed and final perplexity. This is a pure algorithmic win — no additional hardware, no communication, and minimal code changes (save a checkpoint every 500 steps and apply an outer update). The only cost is the outer optimizer hyperparameter tuning (η = 0.7, μ = 0.9 is the paper's robust default). This variant should be preferred for any single-worker language model training run unless there is evidence that the periodic outer updates interact poorly with specific learning rate schedules or architecture choices not tested in the paper.
