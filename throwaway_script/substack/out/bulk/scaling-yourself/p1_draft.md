Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman post on scaling yourself (a mindset shift required for the Staff Engineer promotion):

**The Staff Engineer blocker: failing to scale yourself**

Ryan’s manager told him his main gaps in growing to Staff were all variations of the same theme: he needed to “scale himself.” This differed from everything that had helped him grow his career up to that point, which is why failure to make this shift is one of the most common blockers for promotion to Staff. The mindset change is fundamental: instead of doing everything yourself, you need to multiply your impact through others. The goal is to expand who or what can do impactful work on your behalf, allowing you to have more impact without more time investment.

**Influence and delegation**

The first mechanism is finding impactful problems and guiding others on how to solve them. This scales well because it often takes much less time than writing the code yourself. The natural anxiety here is whether you deserve credit for projects that others complete. Ryan’s test is straightforward: think about what would have happened if you weren’t there. If the work got done only because of your involvement, then you deserve credit. This is how some tech leads write little code yet still have a lot of impact.

**Knowledge sharing**

Ryan invokes Lao Tzu’s “Give a man a fish, and you feed him for a day; teach a man to fish and you feed him for a lifetime” as the operating principle. Sharing technical knowledge helps others have more impact without your continued time investment, and it scales by how many people you grow and by how much. He names four concrete channels that scale well: giving tech talks (each additional attendee costs no extra time, and recording expands reach further), writing answers in public Q&A forums or wikis (people learn without your involvement), code review (each review is an opportunity to give feedback so others write better code even when you’re not around), and hosting office hours (batching hands-on support requests so you handle them all at once rather than context-switching throughout the week).

**Building tooling**

Developer tooling scales well because it costs nothing to share with others. Ryan’s advice is that the next time you build a tool for yourself—scripts, testing tools, visualizers—you should think about how to package it so others can benefit too. The impact of your tool scales with the number of people that use it. For junior engineers specifically, he recommends focusing on knowledge sharing and tooling: don’t just solve problems for yourself; get more impact out of the time you spend by sharing the knowledge and tools that help you move faster. Scaling yourself correctly produces far more impact without investing more time, and this behavior is sustainable—which is precisely why it is required for growth to the Staff level.
