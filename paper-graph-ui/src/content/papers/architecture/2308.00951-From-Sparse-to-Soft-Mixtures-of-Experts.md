# From Sparse to Soft Mixtures of Experts

**ArXiv:** [2308.00951](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00951)

## 🎯 Pitch

By replacing hard token assignments with soft weighted averages, Soft MoE achieves over 40× parameter scaling with only a 2% latency penalty—completely avoiding the token dropping and training instability that cripple traditional sparse MoEs.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Soft MoE**, a fully-differentiable sparse Transformer layer that replaces the discrete token-to-expert assignment problem of conventional Sparse MoEs with an implicit soft assignment—each expert processes a learned convex combination of all input tokens rather than a hard subset of them. Evaluated on vision transformers (ViT-{S/16, B/16, L/16, H/14}) trained on JFT-4B with ImageNet transfer, Soft MoE sidesteps the token dropping, expert imbalance, and training instability that plague existing sparse routers (Tokens Choice and Experts Choice). Soft MoE achieves approximately 2–5.7× faster inference at matched quality and, in a training Pareto analysis, dominates dense ViTs and all other sparse MoEs on both JFT-4B precision and ImageNet 10-shot accuracy across a range of training budgets, with a Soft MoE Huge/14 using 128 experts in 16 MoE layers carrying over 40× more parameters than ViT Huge/14 at only 2% increased inference time. The paper also establishes that scaling the number of experts with one slot per expert consistently outperforms adding slots per expert—a finding that reflects the tendency of multiple slots assigned to the same expert to align and provide diminishing informational gains.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Scaling Models Without Paying the Full Computational Cost

The fundamental tension this paper addresses is one of the central dilemmas in modern deep learning: **larger models consistently perform better, but their computational cost scales linearly with their parameter count during both training and inference.** This is not merely an inconvenience—it is a binding constraint that determines what models can be practically deployed, how long training takes, and who can afford to participate in cutting-edge research.

The scaling laws literature (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022; Zhai et al., 2022a) has established that, to optimally use a given training compute budget, model size and training data must be scaled together. This means that as we invest more compute, we need models with more parameters processing more data. A dense Transformer pays for every parameter on every token: if you double the model width, you double the FLOPs per token. This linear relationship between capacity and cost creates an unpleasant tradeoff—you can have a good model or a fast one, but not both.

### Why This Matters: The Promise and Pain of Conditional Computation

The promise of **conditional computation**—activating only a subset of the model's parameters for any given input—is that it breaks this linear relationship. If a model has 100× more parameters than a dense baseline but only activates 10% of them per token, you get the representational capacity of the large model at roughly the cost of the small one. This is not just an academic curiosity; it has direct implications for:

**Deployment economics.** Inference costs dominate the total cost of ownership for production ML systems. A model that delivers the quality of a large dense model at the inference cost of a small one translates directly to reduced hardware requirements, lower latency, and lower energy consumption. The paper quantifies this concretely: Soft MoE B/16 matches ViT H/14's performance while being 5.7× faster at inference (Table 1).

**Training efficiency.** Training large dense models from scratch requires enormous compute budgets that are inaccessible to most researchers and organizations. If MoE architectures can achieve equivalent or better performance at lower training cost, they democratize access to state-of-the-art model quality. Figure 3 shows Soft MoE dominating the training Pareto frontier—better performance at any given training budget.

**Scaling beyond hardware limits.** Individual accelerators have finite memory. MoEs enable model sizes far beyond what can fit on a single device by distributing experts across many devices, with each device only processing the tokens routed to its experts. Soft MoE Huge/14 with 128 experts reaches over 40× the parameters of ViT Huge/14 (Section 1), a scale impossible with dense architectures on current hardware.

### Where Prior Sparse MoE Approaches Fall Short

Sparse MoEs are not new. The paper builds on a substantial body of prior work, but identifies specific, persistent failure modes that have limited adoption:

**The discrete assignment problem is fundamentally difficult.** At their core, Sparse MoEs solve a matching problem: which tokens should be processed by which experts? This is inherently discrete and non-differentiable. Prior work has explored a wide range of approximations:

- **Top-k or "Token Choice" routing** (Shazeer et al., 2017; Lepikhin et al., 2020; Riquelme et al., 2021): each token selects the k experts with the highest routing scores. This is intuitive but creates two problems simultaneously. First, **expert imbalance**: some experts receive far more tokens than others, wasting the capacity of underutilized experts and creating bottlenecks on overutilized ones. Second, **token dropping**: when an expert's buffer is full, tokens assigned to it that exceed capacity are simply not processed—their computation is wasted and their information is lost. The paper quantifies this: with Tokens Choice routing and K=1, C=1, the maximum dropping ratio across layers approaches 25% at 4096 experts (Figure 8, right panel; Figure 11).

- **"Expert Choice" routing** (Zhou et al., 2022): invert the problem—experts select the top-C tokens with the highest routing scores. This eliminates the expert imbalance issue (each expert processes exactly C tokens), but introduces a different problem: some tokens may be selected by multiple experts while others are selected by none. The paper shows this is severe: at 4096 experts with C=1, the maximum dropping ratio exceeds 40–50% in some layers (Figure 8, right panel; Figure 10). Tokens that are never selected are effectively dropped from the model's computation for that layer.

- **More sophisticated assignment algorithms**—linear programming (Lewis et al., 2021), Optimal Transport (Liu et al., 2022; Clark et al., 2022), reinforcement learning (Bengio et al., 2015)—attempt to find better matchings but are computationally expensive, often requiring solving optimization problems at every forward pass that add significant overhead.

All of these approaches share a common weakness: they are **discrete and non-differentiable**. The assignment decision is a hard choice—token 7 goes to expert 3, not to expert 4—and there is no gradient signal flowing through this decision. The router is typically trained via heuristic auxiliary losses that encourage balanced expert utilization and penalize unassigned tokens, but these are proxies, not the actual training objective. The gap between the auxiliary loss and true task performance creates instability and sensitivity to hyperparameters.

**Training instability and sensitivity to configuration.** The paper documents several dimensions of fragility in existing Sparse MoEs:

- **Expert count scaling is self-limiting.** Figure 6 (right) shows that for both Tokens Choice and Experts Choice, performance initially improves as the number of experts increases (more capacity), but then **degrades** beyond an optimal point. The paper explicitly notes: "there is a point at which training difficulties outweigh the benefits of additional capacity" (Section 3.5). For Tokens Choice with 4096 experts, ImageNet 10-shot accuracy crashes to 32.1%—far below the peak of 69.1% achieved with 128 experts. Soft MoE, in contrast, continues to improve monotonically with more experts.

- **Throughput degrades with expert count.** The routing operations themselves become a bottleneck. Sparse MoEs require sort or top-k operations to implement token-to-expert assignment, and these operations are "slow and typically not well suited for hardware accelerators" (Section 2.2). Figure 6 (bottom) shows training throughput for Tokens Choice dropping from 366 to 45 images/second as experts increase from 8 to 4096—an 8× slowdown entirely from routing overhead. Soft MoE throughput drops only from 379 to 132, a much more modest 2.9× reduction.

- **Buffer capacity is a brittle hyperparameter.** Both Token Choice and Expert Choice require specifying capacity multipliers (C) that control how many tokens each expert can process. Setting C too low causes excessive dropping; setting C too high wastes computation. The paper notes that even small amounts of buffer slack (C=1.125 vs. C=1.0) only modestly reduce dropping rates (Figures 9, 10) and increase step time.

**Batch-level determinism is lost.** Under capacity constraints, Sparse MoEs route tokens in groups of a fixed size and enforce balance within the group. When groups contain tokens from different sequences, these tokens compete for limited slots. The model becomes deterministic only at the batch level, not at the sequence level—the same input may be processed differently depending on what other inputs happen to be in the same batch (Section 2.2). This is problematic for reproducibility, debugging, and deployment scenarios where batch composition varies (small inference batches, novel inputs, transfer learning). The paper explicitly states these challenges "can be greater in out-of-distribution settings" (Section 1).

### How This Paper Reconciles Conflicting Design Tensions

The paper's central insight is that **the discrete assignment problem is unnecessary**. Rather than trying to solve a hard matching problem between tokens and experts, Soft MoE sidesteps it entirely by making the assignment soft and continuous.

The key observation is that what Sparse MoEs ultimately do—apply different expert functions to different parts of the input—can be achieved without hard routing. Instead of deciding that "token 7 goes to expert 3," Soft MoE computes: "expert 3 receives a weighted combination of all tokens, where token 7 contributes 30% and token 12 contributes 15%, etc." The weights are learned and content-dependent, so the model can still learn specialized experts that focus on particular types of token patterns, but the mechanism is fully differentiable and immune to the load-balancing pathologies of discrete routing.

This reframing resolves the tension between **capacity and cost** in a fundamentally different way than prior MoEs. Because every slot is a weighted average of all input tokens, there is no token dropping—every token contributes to every slot, and every expert processes exactly the same number of slots. Expert imbalance is structurally impossible because the slot count is fixed. The routing overhead is a simple matrix multiplication followed by softmax, both highly optimized on modern accelerators, eliminating the sort/top-k bottleneck.

The paper positions Soft MoE not as an incremental improvement over existing routers but as a **different paradigm** for conditional computation. The contrast is drawn explicitly in Figure 1: on the left, Sparse MoE routes individual tokens through a discrete assignment to slots; on the right, Soft MoE computes slots as learned weighted averages. The caption is explicit about the motivation: "Learning to make discrete assignments introduces several optimization and implementation issues that Soft MoE sidesteps."

### Where This Paper Fits in the Broader Landscape

The paper situates Soft MoE at the intersection of several research threads:

**Token merging and fusion approaches** (Jaegle et al., 2021; Ryoo et al., 2021; Renggli et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022) use attention-like weighted averages to reduce sequence length, typically to alleviate the quadratic self-attention cost. Soft MoE borrows the weighted average mechanism but uses it for a different purpose: not to reduce sequence length (the sequence is actually recovered after expert processing via the combine weights), but to route information through a large set of experts without discrete assignment.

**Soft parameter merging** (Yang et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2020; Muqeeth et al., 2023) combines expert parameters rather than routing examples. These approaches are also fully differentiable but suffer from different limitations: averaging large expert parameter matrices at every forward pass is expensive in both time and memory, and they cannot exploit vectorized operations as efficiently since every input effectively uses a different weighted combination of parameters.

**Multi-head attention** shares structural similarities—different heads can be interpreted as different linear "experts"—but operates on lower-dimensional projections (each head processes d/h dimensions) and uses different attention patterns per head. Soft MoE experts are full-dimensional non-linear MLPs applied to slots that aggregate information across the entire sequence.

The paper's contribution is best understood as **unifying the benefits of sparse and dense computation** without the drawbacks of either. From Sparse MoEs, it inherits the ability to scale model capacity without proportionally scaling computation. From dense architectures, it inherits differentiability, determinism, and implementation simplicity. The key enabling mechanism—learned soft assignment via content-dependent convex combinations—is what makes this unification possible.

## 3. Technical Approach

This is primarily an **architectural design paper** that introduces a new, fully-differentiable layer type—the Soft MoE layer—as a drop-in replacement for the MLP blocks in a Transformer, achieving the capacity-scaling benefits of Mixture of Experts without the discrete optimization pathologies that plague existing sparse routers.

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

**What the system is:** A Soft MoE layer is a modified Transformer block where, instead of routing individual tokens to specific experts via a hard assignment, each expert receives a learned convex combination of all input tokens (a "slot"), processes that slot through its MLP, and then the outputs are reassembled into per-token representations via another learned convex combination. **What problem it solves:** It eliminates token dropping, expert imbalance, non-differentiability, and the slow sort/top-k operations that make conventional Sparse MoEs unreliable and difficult to scale to many experts, while preserving the core benefit—increasing model capacity without proportionally increasing computation.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Soft MoE layer has four major components arranged in a sequential flow:

1. **Input Tokens (X ∈ ℝ^{m×d}):** The standard sequence of m token embeddings, each of dimension d, arriving from the preceding Transformer layer (typically after attention and layer normalization).

2. **Slot Parameters (Φ ∈ ℝ^{d×(n·p)}):** A learned matrix of d-dimensional vectors, one per slot. There are n experts, each processing p slots, for a total of S = n·p slots. These parameters are the only additional learnable weights introduced by the Soft MoE layer—they are what the layer learns to use for computing the soft assignments.

3. **Dispatch Mechanism:** All input tokens are combined into S input slots via a softmax-normalized matrix of dispatch weights D ∈ ℝ^{m×S}. Each slot is a different convex combination of all m input tokens. The weight each token contributes to each slot is content-dependent—computed from the dot product between the token embedding and the corresponding slot parameter.

4. **Expert Processing:** Each of the n experts is a standard MLP (the same architecture as the dense FFN it replaces). Expert i processes its p assigned slots independently, producing p output slots. Experts with multiple slots process them sequentially (or in parallel, depending on implementation).

5. **Combine Mechanism:** The S output slots (n experts × p slots each) are reassembled into m output tokens via a combine weight matrix C ∈ ℝ^{m×S}, again softmax-normalized and computed from the same token-slot dot products. Each output token is a different convex combination of all output slots.

Information flows: input tokens → [dot product with slot parameters] → dispatch weights → [weighted average] → input slots → [expert MLPs] → output slots → [combine weights] → [weighted average] → output tokens. The output tokens are the same shape as the input tokens (ℝ^{m×d}) and are passed to the next Transformer layer.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the dispatch mechanism (Equation 1):** how input tokens are mixed into slots, because this is the core innovation that replaces discrete routing with continuous soft assignment.
- **Second, the expert function and slot-expert mapping:** how experts process slots (including the round-robin allocation) and why one slot per expert is optimal.
- **Third, the combine mechanism (Equation 2):** how expert outputs are reassembled into per-token representations, completing the round-trip from tokens to slots and back.
- **Fourth, the time complexity analysis:** how the computational cost scales with tokens, experts, and slots, and why Soft MoE matches dense Transformer FLOPs under standard configurations.
- **Fifth, the L2 normalization trick:** a critical implementation detail that prevents softmax collapse at large model dimensions d—why it is necessary and what happens without it.
- **Sixth, the distributed model implementation:** how Soft MoE layers are sharded across devices for large expert counts, and the practical throughput implications.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

The Soft MoE layer is defined by two core operations—dispatching (tokens → slots) and combining (slots → tokens)—connected by standard expert MLPs. We walk through each step in algorithmic order, matching the structure of Algorithm 1 in the paper.

---

#### The Dispatch Mechanism: From Tokens to Slots

The dispatch operation is the heart of Soft MoE. Its job is to take m input tokens and produce S = n·p input slots, where each slot is a different convex combination of all tokens. The weights are learned and content-dependent, meaning the model can learn to route information from different types of tokens to different experts without any discrete decisions.

The computation proceeds in two steps: first, compute token-slot affinity scores (logits), then normalize them into convex combination weights.

**Step 1: Token-slot logits.** Given input tokens X ∈ ℝ^{m×d} and slot parameters Φ ∈ ℝ^{d×S} (where S = n·p is the total number of slots), compute the matrix of raw affinity scores:

$$L = X\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times S}$$

where X is the matrix of m token embeddings (each row is one token), Φ consists of S column vectors (each column is the learned parameter for one slot), and the product L_{ij} = ⟨x_i, ϕ_j⟩ is the dot-product affinity between token i and slot j.

**What it computes:** A score for every (token, slot) pair measuring how strongly token i should contribute to slot j. This is an unnormalized compatibility score—larger values mean the token and slot parameters are more aligned in the embedding space.

**Why this form:** The dot product is the simplest parameterized bilinear form that allows the model to learn content-dependent routing. A fixed (non-learned) affinity would prevent specialization—different slots would just be fixed averages of the input sequence, losing the ability to route different patterns to different experts. The parameter matrix Φ is what makes the routing adaptive: during training, each slot's parameter vector ϕ_j learns to produce high dot products with tokens that contain patterns that expert ⌊j/p⌋ is good at processing.

**Step 2: Dispatch weights.** The dispatch weights are computed by applying softmax over the token dimension for each slot independently:

$$D_{ij} = \frac{\exp((X\Phi)_{ij})}{\sum_{i'=1}^{m} \exp((X\Phi)_{i'j})}$$

where D ∈ ℝ^{m×S} has entries D_{ij} representing the weight of token i in slot j. The softmax is applied **per column**—for each slot j, the weights over all m tokens sum to 1, ensuring each slot is a convex combination of input tokens.

**What it computes:** Normalized, non-negative weights that determine how much each token contributes to each slot. For a fixed slot j, the vector D_{:,j} is a probability distribution over the m input tokens.

**Why softmax per column:** This is the key design choice that makes Soft MoE immune to token dropping. In Sparse MoEs, a token either goes to an expert or it doesn't—the assignment is a hard 0/1 decision. If an expert's buffer is full, some tokens' assignments fail and they are dropped (their information is lost for that layer). In Soft MoE, **every token contributes to every slot**—even if a token's weight D_{ij} is very small for some slot j, it still participates. No token is ever "dropped" because there is no capacity limit per slot (slots are just weighted averages, and you can always compute a weighted average regardless of how many tokens there are). This structural property eliminates the dropping pathology entirely.

**Step 3: Form input slots.** The input slots ˜X ∈ ℝ^{S×d} are computed as the matrix product:

$$\tilde{X} = D^\top X$$

where D^\top ∈ ℝ^{S×m} and X ∈ ℝ^{m×d}, so ˜X ∈ ℝ^{S×d}. Row s of ˜X is the weighted average of all token embeddings, with weights given by column s of D.

**What it computes:** S vectors of dimension d, each a different mixture of the input tokens. These are the "compressed" representations that will be processed by the experts. If a slot's dispatch weights concentrate heavily on a few tokens, that slot essentially summarizes those tokens; if the weights are more uniform, the slot is a broader summary of the whole sequence.

**Why this is not sequence compression:** Unlike Perceiver or token merging approaches, the number of slots S can be equal to (or even larger than) the number of input tokens m. When S = m, each slot is a different d-dimensional vector, and the total information from the m tokens is preserved (just redistributed). The goal is not to reduce sequence length but to route information through different expert parameters.

---

#### Expert Processing: Round-Robin Slot-to-Expert Allocation

Once the S input slots are computed, they must be assigned to the n experts. The paper uses a deterministic, fixed round-robin allocation: expert i processes slots i, i+n, i+2n, ..., i+(p-1)n. If there is one slot per expert (p=1), expert i simply processes slot i. If there are p=2 slots per expert and n=128 experts (so S=256), expert 1 processes slots 1 and 129, expert 2 processes slots 2 and 130, etc.

Each expert f_i is a standard MLP (usually with one hidden layer and a GELU activation, matching the architecture of the dense FFN it replaces). Expert i is applied independently to each of its assigned slots:

$$\tilde{Y}_s = f_{\lfloor s/p \rfloor}(\tilde{X}_s)$$

where ˜X_s is row s of the input slots matrix (a d-dimensional vector), ⌊s/p⌋ is the index of the expert that handles slot s (ranging from 0 to n-1), and ˜Y_s ∈ ℝ^d is the output of that expert for that slot. The result is the output slots matrix ˜Y ∈ ℝ^{S×d}.

**Why round-robin allocation:** Since the dispatch weights are learned, the model can learn to send appropriate token mixtures to each slot. The round-robin assignment is fixed and non-learned, but this does not constrain the model's routing flexibility because the slot parameters Φ can learn to align with the appropriate expert. If a particular type of token pattern should be processed by expert 7, the slot parameters for the slots assigned to expert 7 will learn to produce high dispatch weights for tokens containing that pattern. The fixed slot-to-expert mapping simply determines which learned routing target corresponds to which expert.

**Why one slot per expert is optimal:** Section 3.5 and Appendix I provide strong evidence that p=1 (one slot per expert) is the best configuration. Figure 6 shows that for Soft MoE, at any fixed number of total slots per token (rows), performance increases monotonically with the number of experts (columns), meaning the best configuration at each row is the model with the most experts (one slot per expert). More directly, Figures 28-30 in Appendix I reveal that when an expert has multiple slots, the slot parameter vectors for the same expert become highly aligned (their inner products approach 1.0). The paper hypothesizes: "slots that use the same expert tend to align and provide small informational gains, and a expert may lack the flexibility to accommodate very different slot projections" (Section 6). In other words, if you give one expert two slots, those two slots learn similar linear combinations and end up sending redundant information to the same MLP, wasting capacity. It is more effective to give each slot its own expert, so the expert can specialize fully to the patterns routed through that slot.

---

#### The Combine Mechanism: From Slots Back to Tokens

After expert processing, we have S output slots ˜Y ∈ ℝ^{S×d}. These must be reassembled into m output tokens Y ∈ ℝ^{m×d}—one per original input token position—so that the sequence length is preserved for subsequent Transformer layers.

The combine weights are computed from the same token-slot logit matrix XΦ used for dispatch, but normalized differently:

$$C_{ij} = \frac{\exp((X\Phi)_{ij})}{\sum_{j'=1}^{S} \exp((X\Phi)_{ij'})}$$

where C ∈ ℝ^{m×S} has entries C_{ij} representing the weight of output slot j in forming output token i. The softmax is applied **per row**—for each token i, the weights over all S slots sum to 1.

**What it computes:** Normalized weights that determine how much each output slot contributes to each output token position. This is the "routing in reverse"—for token i, the vector C_{i,:} is a probability distribution over the S output slots, determining which experts' outputs are most relevant for that token's position.

**Why softmax per row (not per column):** The dispatch weights normalized per column (each slot is a mixture of tokens), but the combine weights normalize per row (each output token is a mixture of slots). This is the crucial difference. Every output token gets to "collect" information from all experts, weighted by how relevant each expert's slot outputs are to that token's position. This means output token i can use information from experts that specialized in patterns present in other tokens (which were routed to those experts via dispatch), enabling cross-token information flow mediated by the expert MLPs. If we normalized per column instead, different output tokens would get the same mixture of slots (just scaled), losing this per-position adaptivity.

**Step 2: Form output tokens.** The output tokens are then:

$$Y = C\tilde{Y}$$

where C ∈ ℝ^{m×S} and ˜Y ∈ ℝ^{S×d}, giving Y ∈ ℝ^{m×d}. Row i of Y is the convex combination of all S output slots, with weights given by row i of C.

**What it computes:** m output token embeddings, each a different mixture of expert outputs. Output token i is a weighted sum of outputs from all experts, where the weights are determined by the compatibility (dot product) between the original input token i's embedding and each slot's parameter. This means that even though the dispatch mechanism mixed tokens across positions, the combine mechanism recovers per-position representations—every output position gets a vector in ℝ^d that is the output of the Soft MoE layer for that position.

**Why the combine weights use the original X, not the slot representations:** The combine weights depend on the original input tokens X, not on the processed slot representations. This means the routing out of the experts is determined by the same token-slot affinities that determined the routing into the experts. This is a form of symmetry: the same content-dependent mechanism that decides which tokens contribute to each slot also decides which slots contribute to each output token. There is no separate "combine router" to learn—the slot parameters Φ serve double duty for both dispatch and combine.

#### Complete Algorithm and Key Design Properties

Algorithm 1 in the paper provides the full JAX pseudo-code. We walk through it line by line to make the computation concrete (the line numbers match the pseudocode):

**Line 3 (logits):** `logits = jnp.einsum('md,dnp->mnp', X, Phi)` computes XΦ, but with the slot parameters Φ reshaped to have expert and slot-per-expert dimensions. The einsum notation means: for each token m and each (expert n, slot p) pair, compute the dot product between the token embedding (size d) and the corresponding slot parameter vector. This produces a 3D tensor of shape (m, n, p) containing the logits.

**Line 4 (dispatch weights):** `D = jax.nn.softmax(logits, axis=(0,))` applies softmax over the token dimension (axis 0) while keeping the expert and slot-per-expert dimensions separate. The result is a tensor of shape (m, n, p) where, for each (expert, slot) pair, the weights over all m tokens sum to 1.

**Line 5 (combine weights):** `C = jax.nn.softmax(logits, axis=(1, 2))` applies softmax over the (expert, slot) dimensions jointly (axes 1 and 2). The result is a tensor of shape (m, n, p) where, for each token, the weights over all n×p slots sum to 1.

**Line 8 (input slots):** `Xs = jnp.einsum('md,mnp->npd', X, D)` computes the weighted average of input tokens using dispatch weights, producing the input slots tensor of shape (n, p, d)—one d-dimensional vector per (expert, slot) pair.

**Lines 10-12 (expert processing):** A Python list comprehension applies each expert function f_i to all p of its assigned input slots. The expert functions themselves are the standard MLPs (with their own parameters). The jnp.stack concatenates the results, yielding output slots Ys of shape (n, p, d).

**Line 15 (output tokens):** `Y = jnp.einsum('npd,mnp->md', Ys, C)` computes the weighted average of output slots using combine weights, producing the output tokens Y of shape (m, d)—back to the original sequence shape.

**Key design properties that emerge from this algorithm:**

1. **Fully differentiable.** Every operation—matrix multiply, softmax, weighted average, MLP—is differentiable. The gradients flow through the dispatch and combine weights back to the slot parameters Φ, so the routing is learned end-to-end via standard backpropagation. There are no auxiliary load-balancing losses because no load balancing is needed (every expert processes exactly p slots, always).

2. **No token dropping.** Because every slot is a weighted average, every token contributes to every slot (possibly with very small weight, but never zero). There is no capacity limit, no buffer overflow, no concept of a token being "too late" to get a slot. The information from all tokens is preserved through the layer, though it may be distributed unevenly across slots.

3. **No expert imbalance.** Every expert processes exactly p slots, period. There is no mechanism by which one expert could receive more work than another. The total FLOPs in the expert layers is exactly n × p × (cost of one expert forward pass), deterministic and independent of the input.

4. **Per-sequence determinism.** The dispatch and combine weights D and C are computed from the input tokens X and the learned parameters Φ. For a given input sequence, they are deterministic. There is no dependence on other sequences in the batch, no competition for expert slots across sequences. The model is deterministic at the sequence level.

5. **Features of both sparse and dense paradigms.** From Sparse MoEs, Soft MoE inherits the ability to have many experts (parameters) while only processing a subset of the input dimensionality—each slot is a single vector in ℝ^d, so the expert processes only S = n·p vectors, not m vectors. When S ≪ m, the expert computation is much cheaper than a dense FFN processing all m tokens. From dense architectures, Soft MoE inherits differentiability and the absence of discrete optimization pathologies.

#### Time Complexity Analysis

The paper provides a careful FLOPs analysis to establish when and why Soft MoE is computationally efficient relative to dense and sparse alternatives.

The cost of a Soft MoE layer decomposes into two terms:

1. **Routing cost: O(m·S·d) = O(m·n·p·d).** This comes from computing the logits XΦ (matrix multiply of shape (m,d) × (d,S) = O(m·S·d)), plus the softmax operations (O(m·S) for dispatch and combine, negligible compared to the matmul), plus the two weighted averages (D^T X is (S,m) × (m,d) = O(m·S·d) and C˜Y is (m,S) × (S,d) = O(m·S·d)). The dominant factor is the m·S·d term.

2. **Expert cost: O(S·k) = O(n·p·k).** Each of the S input slots is processed by an expert MLP. If the expert has cost k per input (for a standard Transformer MLP with hidden dimension d_ff, k = O(d·d_ff)), then the total expert cost is S·k.

The total cost is O(m·S·d + S·k). The paper notes two critical regimes:

**Regime 1: Matching dense FLOPs.** By setting S = m (total slots equals sequence length), the routing cost becomes O(m²d) and the expert cost becomes O(m·k). The m²d term is **exactly the cost of single-headed self-attention** in the same Transformer block. This means that, with S = m, a Soft MoE layer has the same asymptotic FLOPs as the dense FFN + attention it replaces—no additional overhead beyond what the Transformer already pays for attention. Specifically, when S = m and the cost of applying one expert is large (mk dominates m²d), "the overall cost of a Soft MoE layer becomes comparable to that of applying a single expert on all the input tokens" (Section 2.3).

**Regime 2: Scaling experts without cost increase.** For a fixed total number of slots S, changing the number of experts n and scaling p = S/n accordingly **does not change the FLOPs**. The routing cost depends on S·m, not on n; the expert cost depends on S·k, not on n. This is the critical property that enables extreme parameter scaling: you can increase n from 8 to 4096 (keeping total slots fixed) and the theoretical FLOPs remain identical. The only cost that increases is memory (for storing the additional expert parameters) and communication (if the experts are distributed across devices). Figure 6 (bottom) confirms this empirically: Soft MoE throughput drops from only 379 to 132 images/second when going from 8 to 4096 experts, a 2.9× reduction attributable to increased memory pressure and communication overhead, not to increased computation.

**Contrast with Sparse MoEs.** Sparse MoEs require sort or top-k operations to implement token-to-expert assignment. These operations scale poorly with the number of experts because the sorting/selection overhead grows with the number of choices. Figure 6 (bottom) shows the dramatic difference: Tokens Choice throughput drops from 366 to 45 img/s (8.1× slowdown) and Experts Choice drops from 374 to 91 img/s (4.1× slowdown) over the same expert range. The sort/top-k operations "are slow and typically not well suited for hardware accelerators" (Section 2.2). Soft MoE sidesteps this entirely by using only matrix multiplications and softmax—both highly optimized primitives on GPUs and TPUs.

---

#### The L2 Normalization Trick: Preventing Softmax Collapse

An implementation detail that proves crucial for training stability at scale: the inputs to the softmax in the dispatch and combine computations must be L2-normalized to prevent collapse to one-hot vectors as the model dimension d grows.

**The problem.** In standard Transformer architectures using pre-normalization, the inputs to the MoE layer are layer-normalized. This means the token embeddings X have been centered and scaled to unit variance across the feature dimension. However, as the model dimension d grows, the dot products in XΦ become larger (summing over d terms, each with roughly unit variance, the dot product has variance proportional to d). The softmax function is sensitive to scale: as the inputs get larger, the softmax approaches a one-hot vector (the maximum input gets weight ≈ 1, all others ≈ 0).

Appendix E provides the theoretical analysis. The output of a softmax applied to layer-normalized inputs, as d → ∞, tends to:

$$\lim_{d \to \infty} \text{softmax}(\Theta \text{LN}(x))_i = \frac{1}{|M|} \text{ if } i \in M, \text{ else } 0$$

where M is the set of indices achieving the maximum value of √d·ϑ_i - δ_i (with ϑ_i and δ_i derived from the layer norm parameters). In particular, when the maximum is unique (|M| = 1), the softmax collapses to a one-hot vector. This means the dispatch and combine weights become essentially binary—every slot is formed from exactly one token, and every output token comes from exactly one slot—which defeats the purpose of soft assignment and causes training instability.

**The solution.** The paper replaces the raw logit computation XΦ with an L2-normalized version. Specifically, in Line 3 of Algorithm 1, instead of computing `X @ Phi`, the implementation computes:

$$\text{logits} = (\text{l2\_normalize}(X, \text{axis}=1)) \cdot (\text{scale} \times \text{l2\_normalize}(\Phi, \text{axis}=0))$$

where l2_normalize(x) = x / ||x||₂ (with a small epsilon for numerical stability), and `scale` is a trainable scalar parameter. The normalization is applied per token (each row of X has unit L2 norm) and per slot parameter (each column of Φ has unit L2 norm).

**What it computes:** Cosine similarities between token embeddings and slot parameters, scaled by a learned temperature parameter. After normalization, the dot product between a token and a slot parameter is exactly their cosine similarity (since both have unit norm). The learned scale allows the model to control the sharpness of the softmax—a large scale produces more concentrated (closer to one-hot) assignments, while a small scale produces more uniform assignments.

**Why this works:** By constraining the inputs to the softmax to have bounded magnitude (each term is a cosine similarity in [-1, 1], multiplied by scale), the softmax output remains smooth regardless of d. The softmax no longer approaches a one-hot as d → ∞ because the inputs no longer grow with d. The trainable scale parameter provides a learnable temperature that adapts during training.

**Empirical validation.** Figure 13 shows the problem clearly. Without normalization (solid lines), the average maximum dispatch weight grows toward 1.0 as d increases from 384 to 1664—the softmax is collapsing to one-hot. With normalization (dashed lines), the maximum weight stays well below 1.0. Correspondingly, ImageNet 10-shot accuracy degrades significantly without normalization at large d (e.g., at d=1664, the normalized model achieves ~0.75 while the unnormalized model stays near ~0.65). Figure 14 further shows that the normalization makes training more robust to the choice of peak learning rate—without normalization, larger learning rates cause the dispatch weights to collapse more severely.

**Why standard initialization doesn't fix this.** The paper notes that modern parameter initialization techniques (Glorot, He, SELU) scale weights by 1/√d, which controls the variance at initialization. However, during training, the weights and layer norm parameters can drift, causing the dot products to grow beyond what the initialization scale would suggest. The L2 normalization enforces a hard constraint on the dot product magnitude that cannot be escaped during training, preventing the collapse regardless of how parameters evolve.

#### Distributed Model Implementation

When the number of experts becomes large (thousands to tens of thousands), the expert parameters alone may exceed the memory of a single accelerator. The paper uses standard model parallelism techniques from prior MoE work (Lepikhin et al., 2020; Riquelme et al., 2021; Fedus et al., 2022): the n experts are sharded across multiple devices, with each device storing a subset of the expert MLP parameters.

**How distributed Soft MoE works.** During the forward pass:
1. The dispatch weights D are computed on all devices (each device has a copy of X and the slot parameters Φ, or at least the shard relevant to its experts).
2. Each device computes the input slots for its assigned experts by selecting the appropriate columns of D and computing the weighted averages. This requires an all-to-all communication step to gather the relevant token embeddings, or each device can compute the weighted averages for its slots independently if it has a full copy of X (which is typically the case since X is small relative to expert parameters).
3. Each device applies its experts to its assigned input slots.
4. The output slots are communicated back (all-to-all) and the combine weights C are used to form output tokens on each device (again, C depends on X which is available everywhere).

**Practical throughput implications.** The paper is careful to report both theoretical FLOPs and actual wall-clock time (TPUv3-chip-hours) in all experiments, precisely because the distributed overhead is not captured by FLOP counting. Figure 6 (bottom) shows that even with distribution overhead, Soft MoE with 4096 experts achieves 132 img/s training throughput, which is 2.9× faster than Tokens Choice at 45 img/s with the same number of experts. The distribution overhead exists for both methods, but Soft MoE's lack of sort/top-k operations means its base computational cost is lower to begin with.

**Design choices for expert placement.** The paper replaces only a subset of the Transformer's MLP blocks with Soft MoE blocks—specifically, "the second half of MLP blocks" (Section 2.1) for most experiments. Appendix D provides a systematic study of expert placement, comparing different configurations where a fixed total of 512 experts (with one slot each) are distributed across different numbers of layers. The best configuration places the experts across the last few layers: "8, 9, 10, 11" with 128 experts each (4 layers × 128 experts = 512 total) achieves 72.8% ImageNet 10-shot, while placing all 512 experts in a single layer achieves only 70.0%. The paper attributes this to providing more layers with access to the expanded capacity rather than concentrating it all in one layer. The same pattern holds for Tokens Choice and Experts Choice (Tables 5 and 6), suggesting the optimal placement strategy is not specific to the routing algorithm.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Discrete Assignment Problem in Sparse MoEs Is Unnecessary — Soft Assignment Achieves the Same Goal Without the Pathologies

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is reframing what MoE routing *is*. Prior work treated MoE routing as a resource allocation problem: given m tokens and n experts with limited capacity, find the best hard assignment of tokens to experts. This framing led the field down a path of increasingly sophisticated discrete optimization — top-k selection (Shazeer et al., 2017), linear programming (Lewis et al., 2021), Optimal Transport (Liu et al., 2022; Clark et al., 2022), reinforcement learning (Bengio et al., 2015) — all attempting to solve the same fundamentally difficult matching problem with auxiliary losses, capacity constraints, and buffer tuning.

Soft MoE rejects the premise that hard assignment is necessary. The key insight is that what experts ultimately do — apply different parameterized functions to different parts of the input — can be achieved by having each expert process a *different learned convex combination* of all tokens, rather than a hard-selected subset. The routing problem transforms from "which tokens go to which expert?" (a combinatorial assignment) to "what weighted mixture of tokens should each expert receive?" (a continuous optimization). The former requires discrete decisions that break differentiability; the latter is a simple softmax over dot products, fully differentiable and gradient-friendly.

This is not an incremental improvement over existing routers — it is a **categorical shift in the problem formulation**. The paper shows that all the pathologies that plagued Sparse MoEs — token dropping (up to 25% for Tokens Choice and 40-50% for Experts Choice at 4096 experts, Figure 8), expert imbalance requiring heuristic auxiliary losses, training instability at scale, and the need for capacity multiplier hyperparameters — are not fundamental to the MoE concept but are artifacts of the discrete assignment framing. By eliminating the assignment problem, Soft MoE eliminates all of these pathologies simultaneously. There are no dropped tokens (every token contributes to every slot), no imbalanced experts (each expert processes exactly p slots), no auxiliary losses (the routing is trained end-to-end via the task loss), and no capacity hyperparameters to tune. Section 3.5 and Figure 6 provide the empirical evidence: Soft MoE performance improves monotonically with expert count, while both Tokens Choice and Experts Choice degrade beyond an optimum — evidence that the discrete routing itself, not the expert capacity, is the bottleneck that prevents scaling.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond the specific Soft MoE algorithm. It suggests that **many problems currently tackled with discrete routing could be reformulated as continuous soft assignment problems**, opening a design space that the field had not systematically explored because the resource-allocation framing was taken as given.

### Innovation 2: The Dispatch-Combine Symmetry Enables a Fully Reversible Token-Slot-Token Round-Trip Without Auxiliary Losses

A subtle but powerful design choice in Soft MoE is the **symmetry between dispatch and combine weights**: both are computed from the same token-slot affinity scores XΦ, differing only in the softmax normalization axis (per-column for dispatch, per-row for combine). This symmetry is not an arbitrary aesthetic choice — it is what enables the layer to function without any auxiliary losses.

To see why this matters, consider what would be needed if dispatch and combine used separate learned routers. The dispatch router would need some signal to learn which tokens to route to which experts, and the combine router would need a separate signal to learn how to reassemble expert outputs. Without a shared representation, these two routers would need to coordinate — and coordination between independently learned modules is exactly the kind of problem that auxiliary losses exist to solve in Sparse MoEs (load-balancing loss to coordinate token-to-expert assignment across the batch).

By sharing the slot parameters Φ for both dispatch and combine, Soft MoE creates an **information bottleneck** that forces the routing to be internally consistent: the same token-slot affinity that determines which tokens contribute to a slot also determines which slots contribute back to that token's output position. The gradients from the task loss flow through both directions of the round-trip, and because both paths depend on the same parameters, the model naturally learns Φ values that make the routing coherent — there is no separate "dispatch task" and "combine task" to coordinate. This is what eliminates the need for auxiliary load-balancing losses that are universally required in Sparse MoEs.

The contrast with prior work is instructive. Token Choice routing (Shazeer et al., 2017) requires an auxiliary loss to encourage balanced expert utilization because the router is only trained through the tokens that survive the discrete selection — if an expert is never selected, it receives no gradient and dies. Expert Choice routing (Zhou et al., 2022) avoids the dying expert problem but still requires an auxiliary loss to balance the per-token processing budget. Soft MoE's shared-parameter symmetry is a fundamentally different approach: rather than adding losses to fix coordination problems, **design the architecture so that coordination is structurally enforced**. Every forward pass is a round-trip through the shared Φ, and every backward pass provides coherent gradients to all slot parameters regardless of which tokens or experts were "selected" (since, soft assignment means all tokens and all experts participate in every forward pass to some degree). This is confirmed by the ablation results in Table 2: "Soft / Uniform" (learned dispatch, uniform combine) and "Uniform / Soft" (uniform dispatch, learned combine) both substantially underperform the full Soft MoE, demonstrating that the symmetry between dispatch and combine is not just a convenience — it is a significant contributor to model quality (roughly 2-3 percentage points on ImageNet 10-shot).

### Innovation 3: Empirical Evidence That Optimal Routing Strategy Is Not Algorithm-Dependent, and One Slot Per Expert Is the Universal Optimum

The paper performs systematic head-to-head comparisons of routing algorithms (Soft MoE, Tokens Choice, Experts Choice) under controlled conditions — same backbone, same training budget, same expert placement — and two striking structural findings emerge that go beyond the raw performance numbers.

**Finding 1: Soft MoE's advantage is not primarily about raw accuracy at any given expert count, but about scaling behavior as expert count increases.** Figure 6 (Section 3.5) tells a nuanced story. At modest expert counts (8-32 experts), all three routing methods perform roughly comparably — Soft MoE at 75.4% ImageNet 10-shot with 4096 slots, Experts Choice at 72.5%, Tokens Choice at 69.1%. The qualitative difference emerges as experts scale: Soft MoE continues to improve (reaching 75.4% at 4096 experts), Experts Choice plateaus and then slightly degrades (72.2% at 2048 but drops by 4096), and Tokens Choice degrades dramatically (crashes to 32.1% at 4096). This is not merely "Soft MoE is better" — it is evidence that the discrete routing overhead and training instability are the **binding constraints on scaling Sparse MoEs**, not the expert capacity or the backbone architecture. Soft MoE's routing cost is independent of expert count (O(m·S·d), where S is fixed), while Sparse MoEs' sort/top-k overhead grows with expert count and eventually dominates.

**Finding 2: One slot per expert is consistently optimal, and multiple slots per expert provide diminishing returns due to slot parameter alignment.** Section 3.5 and Appendix I (Figures 28-30) show that when an expert processes multiple slots, those slots' learned parameter vectors become highly correlated — their inner products approach 1.0, indicating they learn nearly identical linear combinations. The paper's interpretation (Section 6) is that "a expert may lack the flexibility to accommodate very different slot projections" — the MLP parameters that process the slots can only represent one type of transformation well, so multiple slots converge to sending similar inputs to the same MLP, wasting the additional slots' capacity. The alternative — using more experts with one slot each — lets each expert specialize independently, and because the total FLOPs are the same (S = n·p is fixed), there is no computational penalty for splitting capacity across more experts.

These findings are not immediately obvious from the algorithm description. It would be reasonable to expect that multiple slots per expert could increase the expert's representational capacity (more inputs to process) or that Expert Choice routing with its per-expert token selection would scale better than Token Choice (which it does, but still degrades). The paper's systematic ablation provides **diagnostic clarity**: if you want to scale MoE capacity, do it by adding more experts with one slot each, not by adding slots per expert, and use a routing mechanism whose cost does not scale with expert count. This is actionable design guidance that generalizes beyond the specific Soft MoE algorithm.

### Innovation 4: The L2 Normalization Fix for Softmax Collapse Identifies a Structural Vulnerability in Any Architecture That Applies Softmax After Layer Normalization

The paper's analysis in Appendix E and the normalization fix in Section 2.3 are typically presented as an implementation detail, but they constitute a genuine conceptual contribution with implications beyond Soft MoE. The core insight is that **layer normalization followed by a softmax layer with learned parameters creates a structural tendency toward one-hot collapse as model dimension d grows**, and that standard parameter initialization (Glorot, He, SELU) does not prevent this collapse during training.

The theoretical analysis (Appendix E.1) establishes the mechanism: layer normalization centers and scales inputs to unit variance, but after applying learned parameters Θ, the dot products in Θ·LN(x) have variance proportional to d. The softmax of these growing dot products inevitably approaches a one-hot vector as d → ∞, regardless of initialization. The paper demonstrates this empirically in Figure 13: for d = 1664 (the "G" backbone size), without L2 normalization, the average maximum dispatch weight grows toward 0.8 during training, and ImageNet 10-shot accuracy is significantly degraded compared to the normalized version. Figure 14 further shows that this collapse is exacerbated by larger learning rates — a practical concern for training at scale where learning rates must be tuned.

The proposed fix — L2-normalize both the inputs and the parameter vectors before computing dot products, then scale by a learned temperature — is simple but principled. It constrains the dot products to be cosine similarities in [-1, 1], multiplied by a learned scale, so the softmax input magnitude is bounded regardless of d. The learned scale provides a trainable temperature that controls assignment sharpness.

This finding is significant because **it identifies a vulnerability that potentially affects any architecture combining layer normalization with softmax-based routing or attention**, not just Soft MoE. The paper notes that standard initialization techniques "might fix this issue" but empirically "they don't" — the collapse occurs during training as parameters drift, not just at initialization. The diagnostic — monitor the average maximum softmax weight during training as d increases — is a practical tool for detecting this pathology in other architectures. The fix — L2 normalization with learned scale — is a general recipe that could apply to attention mechanisms, token merging approaches (Jaegle et al., 2021; Renggli et al., 2022), or any module where softmax over dot products follows layer normalization. The paper frames this as specific to Soft MoE, but the analysis has broader architectural implications that the field may not have fully appreciated.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All pretraining experiments use JFT-4B (Zhai et al., 2022a), a proprietary dataset containing more than 4B images across 29k classes. Pretraining performance is evaluated via upstream validation precision-at-1 on JFT-4B and ImageNet 10-shot accuracy. Final evaluation includes ImageNet-1k validation accuracy after finetuning on the full 1.3M-image ImageNet-1k training set at 384 resolution (Section 3.1).

- **Base model(s).** The primary architecture family is Vision Transformers (ViTs) at scales S/32, S/16, S/8, B/32, B/16, L/32, L/16, and H/14 (Section 3.2). These span parameter counts from 33M (ViT S/16) to 669M (ViT H/14). Soft MoE variants replace the MLP blocks in the second half of Transformer layers with Soft MoE layers, using 128 or 256 experts at one slot per expert in most long-training experiments, yielding models from 933M (Soft MoE S/16) to 54.1B parameters (Soft MoE H/14 with 256 experts). These models are chosen because they are "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary" vision architectures and sit in a regime where capacity scaling produces meaningful accuracy improvements without saturation (Section 4, implied).

- **Metrics.** Three primary metrics are tracked: (1) **JFT-4B precision-at-1** — the fraction of validation examples where the model's top-1 prediction matches the ground-truth label, measured during pretraining; (2) **ImageNet 10-shot accuracy** — a few-shot metric where model weights are frozen and a new classification head is trained on only 10 examples per ImageNet-1k class, measuring representation quality without full finetuning; and (3) **ImageNet finetune accuracy** — standard full-dataset finetuning on ImageNet-1k at 384×384 resolution, measuring transfer performance after adaptation. Training cost is measured in both **total training exaFLOPs** and **total training TPUv3-days**, and inference cost in **GFLOPs/image** and **milliseconds/image on TPUv3** (Section 3.1, Table 1).

- **Baselines.** Four baselines are compared: (1) **Dense ViT** — standard Vision Transformer without MoE layers at matching backbone sizes (S/16, B/16, L/16, H/14); (2) **Tokens Choice routing** (Shazeer et al., 2017) with Batch Priority Routing (BPR) from Riquelme et al. (2021), using K ∈ {1, 2} selected experts per token; (3) **Experts Choice routing** (Zhou et al., 2022), where experts select the top-C tokens by routing score, with capacity multipliers c ∈ {0.5, 1.0, 2.0} controlling the total buffer size as E·C = c·T; (4) for contrastive learning experiments in Section 4, **ViT and Experts Choice** baselines serve as the frozen image tower against which Soft MoE image encoders are compared.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For the training Pareto experiments (Section 3.3), all models are trained for 300k steps with batch size 4096 at resolution 224, using a reciprocal square root learning rate schedule. Compute is reported in two units to capture both theoretical operations and practical wall-clock cost: **total training exaFLOPs** (counting all forward and backward passes) and **total training TPUv3-days** (actual elapsed time on TPUv3 accelerators, including routing overhead, communication, and distributed model overhead not captured by FLOP counting alone). For the long-training experiments (Section 3.4), models are trained for up to 4M steps (some extended to 9M steps for smaller backbones), with cooldown periods of 50k or 500k steps depending on total training duration.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** There is no explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing reported. The paper uses a fixed evaluation protocol with standard ImageNet validation sets and reports single-point accuracy numbers without confidence intervals. The Pareto frontier analysis (Section 3.3) aggregates 106 trained models across architectures, routing methods, and hyperparameters, with only non-dominated models (those on the Pareto frontier) displayed in the main figures. However, Appendix J (Table 9) and Appendix F (Figures 19-22) show all trained models, providing transparency about the full distribution of results rather than only the best-performing frontier points.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Training Pareto-Optimal Models (Section 3.3)

The central finding from the Pareto analysis is that **Soft MoE dominates all other approaches — dense ViTs, Tokens Choice MoEs, and Experts Choice MoEs — on both JFT-4B precision-at-1 and ImageNet 10-shot accuracy at every training budget** (Figures 3a and 3b). This is a strong result because it means there exists no training cost at which any alternative method outperforms Soft MoE on these metrics, within the explored space of 106 models.

**Quantitative context from Figure 3a (JFT-4B precision-at-1):** At roughly 10² total training TPUv3-days, Soft MoE achieves approximately 0.56 JFT precision-at-1. The best Experts Choice model at the same training time budget achieves approximately 0.54, Tokens Choice achieves approximately 0.53, and Dense achieves approximately 0.50. The gap of roughly 0.06 precision points between Soft MoE and Dense at this budget is substantial — it is roughly equivalent to the improvement Dense would need 3-4× more training time to achieve.

**Quantitative context from Figure 3b (ImageNet 10-shot):** At 10² training TPUv3-days, Soft MoE reaches approximately 0.78 accuracy. Experts Choice reaches approximately 0.75, Tokens Choice approximately 0.73, and Dense approximately 0.68. The 10-percentage-point gap between Soft MoE and Dense at matched training cost is large — Soft MoE achieves at ~100 TPU-days what Dense needs roughly 300-400 TPU-days to match.

**Model size trends:** The paper uses larger marker sizes in Figure 3 to indicate larger backbone models. The Pareto frontier for Soft MoE spans from S/32 (smallest, cheapest) to H/14 (largest, most expensive). Importantly, Soft MoE models of a given backbone size achieve substantially higher accuracy than Dense models of the same or even larger size — for instance, Figure 3 shows Soft MoE models with medium-sized markers (B/16 scale) outperforming Dense models with larger markers (L/16 or H/14 scale) on both metrics.

**Table 9 completeness:** Appendix J, Table 9 lists all 106 trained models with their routing type, expert count, group size, K/C values, JFT precision, ImageNet 10-shot accuracy, training exaFLOPs, and training TPU-days. This table confirms that the Pareto results are not cherry-picked — the full distribution shows Soft MoE models consistently achieving higher accuracy at lower or equal cost compared to alternative methods at similar parameter counts.

#### Long Training Durations: Inference-Optimized Models (Section 3.4)

The headline finding is that **Soft MoE models trained for extended durations achieve inference costs 2-5.7× lower than dense ViTs at matched quality, while maintaining or exceeding their accuracy** (Table 1, Figure 5).

**Soft MoE B/16 vs. ViT H/14 (the central comparison, Table 1):**
- Soft MoE B/16 with 128 experts, trained for 9M steps (500k cooldown), achieves 62.4% JFT precision, 82.9% ImageNet 10-shot, and 88.5% ImageNet finetune accuracy.
- ViT H/14 trained for 2M steps achieves 59.7% JFT precision, 83.3% ImageNet 10-shot, and 88.9% ImageNet finetune accuracy.
- Inference cost: Soft MoE B/16 uses **32 GFLOPs/image** and **1.5 ms/image**, while ViT H/14 uses **334 GFLOPs/image** and **8.6 ms/image** — a **10.4× reduction in FLOPs** and **5.7× reduction in wall-clock time** at roughly matched quality (Soft MoE B/16 actually outperforms ViT H/14 on JFT by 2.7 points, matches on ImageNet 10-shot, and lags by only 0.4 points on finetune).
- Training cost: Soft MoE B/16 required 1011.4 TPUv3-days vs. ViT H/14's 2039.8 TPUv3-days — roughly **half the training time** for a model that is 5.7× faster at inference.

**Scaling up to Soft MoE L/16:** Soft MoE L/16 with 128 experts, trained for 4M steps (500k cooldown), achieves 63.0% JFT precision, 84.3% ImageNet 10-shot, and 89.2% ImageNet finetune — outperforming ViT H/14 on all three metrics while requiring only 1355.4 training TPU-days (vs. 2039.8) and 4.8 ms/img inference (vs. 8.6 ms/img).

**Soft MoE H/14 (the largest model):** At 256 experts, trained for 2M steps, reaches 62.1% JFT precision and 84.3% ImageNet 10-shot at 342 GFLOPs/image — comparable inference cost to ViT H/14 but significantly higher accuracy (ViT H/14 at 2M steps achieves 59.7% JFT, 83.3% ImageNet 10-shot with 334 GFLOPs/image). At 128 experts, Soft MoE H/14 achieves 61.7% JFT precision and 84.2% ImageNet 10-shot at 284.6 GFLOPs/image — actually cheaper than the dense H/14 while still outperforming it.

**The "over 40× more parameters, 2% increased inference time" claim (Section 1):** Soft MoE Huge/14 with 128 experts in 16 MoE layers has over 40× more parameters than ViT Huge/14. Table 1 confirms: ViT H/14 has 669M parameters; Soft MoE H/14 with 128 experts has 27.3B parameters — a factor of 40.8×. The "2% increased inference time" refers to the comparison between Soft MoE H/14 128E (284.6 GFLOPs/img, 8.8 ms/img) and ViT H/14 (334.2 GFLOPs/img, 8.6 ms/img) — Soft MoE actually uses fewer FLOPs but slightly more wall-clock time due to routing overhead, still within a few percent of the dense model.

**Overtraining dynamics (Section 3.4, Figure 5):** The paper extends training of smaller backbones (S/16, B/16) to 9-10M steps, substantially beyond the 4M-step baseline. These "overtrained" models show continuing improvements: Soft MoE S/14 with 256 experts trained for 10M steps reaches 60.9% JFT precision and 80.7% ImageNet 10-shot at only 13.2 GFLOPs/image inference cost — a point that outperforms ViT B/16 (56.2% JFT, 76.8% ImageNet 10-shot at 35.1 GFLOPs/image) while being faster and cheaper. The paper notes that "longer cooldowns (linear learning rate decay) works well for Soft MoE," increasing cooldown from 50k to 500k steps for these extended runs (Section 3.4).

#### Number of Slots and Experts: Scaling Behavior (Section 3.5)

Figure 6 provides the most systematic analysis of how routing methods scale with model capacity. The experiment varies both the number of experts (columns, from 2 to 4096) and the number of slots per token (rows, from 1 to 16), training Soft MoE S/16 models for 300k steps.

**Key finding 1 — Soft MoE improves monotonically with expert count, Sparse MoEs do not:** For Soft MoE (Figure 6, top-left heatmap), at any fixed number of slots per token (any row), **accuracy increases monotonically as the number of experts increases** (moving right across columns). For example, at 1 slot per token (top row): 66.6% → 67.4% → 67.1% → 67.4% → 67.5% → 67.4% → 67.5% → 67.4% → 68.2% → 69.6% as experts increase from 1 to 4096 (Reading from the figure: accuracy grows from 66.6 at 1 expert to roughly 75.4 at 4096 experts with 16 slots per token). The pattern is clear: more experts = better performance, with no degradation.

For Experts Choice (middle heatmap): accuracy initially increases (65.4 → 71.8 at 128 experts) but then **degrades** at very high expert counts (69.7 at 512 experts, 70.0 at 4096). For Tokens Choice (bottom heatmap): the degradation is catastrophic — accuracy peaks at 69.1 with 256 experts but crashes to 32.1 at 4096 experts.

**Key finding 2 — The optimal configuration at any cost level is one slot per expert with many experts:** For Soft MoE, at each row (fixed slots-per-token, determining the computational cost), the highest accuracy is achieved at the rightmost column — the maximum number of experts. This means giving each expert exactly one slot and scaling total capacity by increasing the number of experts is strictly better than giving fewer experts more slots each. The theoretical FLOPs are identical in both cases (total slots = experts × slots-per-expert is fixed along each row), so this is a pure architectural win — better accuracy at identical computational cost.

**Key finding 3 — Soft MoE throughput is far more robust to expert count than Sparse MoEs:** Figure 6 (bottom) shows training throughput in images/second for the same sweep. For Soft MoE: throughput drops from 379 to 132 img/s as experts increase from 8 to 4096 — a 2.9× reduction, attributed entirely to memory pressure and communication overhead (Section 2.3). For Experts Choice: throughput drops from 374 to 91 img/s — a 4.1× reduction. For Tokens Choice: throughput drops from 366 to 45 img/s — an 8.1× reduction. The gap widens dramatically at high expert counts: at 4096 experts, Soft MoE is 2.9× faster than Tokens Choice. This is attributed to Soft MoE "completely avoiding sort or top-k operations which are slow and typically not well suited for hardware accelerators" (Section 2.2).

**Key finding 4 — Adding slots per expert provides diminishing returns:** Looking down the columns of the Soft MoE heatmap (increasing slots-per-token for a fixed number of experts), accuracy increases but with rapidly diminishing returns. For example, at 32 experts: 67.1% (1 slot) → 69.2% (2 slots) → 71.2% (4 slots) → 72.2% (8 slots) → 72.1% (16 slots). The marginal gain per doubling of slots decreases, and at the highest slot counts, accuracy may even slightly decline. Meanwhile, cost increases proportionally with slots-per-token, making this an inefficient way to scale capacity compared to adding more experts.

**Supplementary evidence on scaling (Appendix C, Figure 12):** For a fixed 32 experts, increasing slots per expert from 1 to 32 increases Soft MoE JFT precision only modestly (from ~0.51 to ~0.53) while step time increases from ~100ms to ~250ms. Experts Choice with a group size of 8 images catches up to Soft MoE at high slot counts but at substantially higher cost (step time exceeding 250ms). This confirms that the efficient scaling axis is expert count, not slots per expert.

**Figures 17 and 18 (Appendix F.2):** These provide the same scaling analysis but plotted as line graphs with normalized step time, making the Soft MoE advantage more visually apparent. Figure 17 shows Soft MoE JFT precision increasing from ~0.515 to ~0.545 as experts grow from 32 to 4096, with step time remaining roughly constant (normalized 1.0-1.1). Experts Choice performance degrades beyond 128-256 experts, and its step time explodes to 2.5× at 4096 experts. Figure 18 shows Tokens Choice struggling even more: performance peaks at 256 experts and declines thereafter, with step time reaching 4× the Soft MoE baseline at 4096 experts.

#### Ablation Studies: Routing Mechanism Components (Section 3.6)

Table 2 ablates the components of the Soft MoE routing mechanism using a Soft MoE S/14 with 256 experts trained for 300k steps:

- **Full Soft MoE**: 54.3% JFT precision, 74.8% ImageNet 10-shot. This is the full algorithm with learned dispatch weights, slot-based expert processing, and learned combine weights.
- **Soft / Uniform**: 53.6% JFT, 72.0% ImageNet 10-shot. Learned dispatch (input mixing into slots) but uniform combine (all output tokens average all expert outputs equally). Degradation of 0.7 points JFT, 2.8 points ImageNet 10-shot.
- **Uniform / Soft**: 52.6% JFT, 71.8% ImageNet 10-shot. Uniform dispatch (all slots average all input tokens equally) but learned combine. Degradation of 1.7 points JFT, 3.0 points ImageNet 10-shot.
- **Uniform Mixing**: 51.8% JFT, 70.0% ImageNet 10-shot. Both dispatch and combine are uniform averages. Expert diversity arises only from different parameter initializations. Degradation of 2.5 points JFT, 4.8 points ImageNet 10-shot.
- **Identity routing**: 51.5% JFT, 69.1% ImageNet 10-shot. Tokens are not mixed — the first token goes to the first expert, second to second, etc. (round-robin hard assignment). Degradation of 2.8 points JFT, 5.7 points ImageNet 10-shot, but still significantly outperforms the dense ViT (48.3% JFT, 62.3% ImageNet 10-shot), indicating that even fixed expert routing with no learned mixing provides substantial benefit from the additional expert capacity.
- **Dense ViT**: 48.3% JFT, 62.3% ImageNet 10-shot. The baseline without any MoE layers.

**Key takeaways from ablations:**
1. Learned dispatch mixing (the "Soft" part) is more important than learned combine mixing — Soft/Uniform (3.3 JFT points above Uniform) outperforms Uniform/Soft (2.3 JFT points above Uniform).
2. Having slots at all (any form of expert processing of different token subsets) is critical — Identity routing improves over Dense ViT by 3.2 JFT points despite having no learned mixing, indicating that the increased parameter count from experts alone provides benefit even with naive assignment.
3. The full Soft MoE routing provides 2.5 JFT points above uniform mixing and 2.8 points above identity routing, quantifying the value of content-dependent soft assignment over both naive fixed assignment and learned-but-uniform mixing.
4. Expert diversity from different initializations alone (Uniform Mixing case) provides some benefit (3.5 JFT points above Dense), but content-dependent routing (Soft MoE) roughly doubles this improvement (6.0 points above Dense).

#### Contrastive Learning: Representation Transfer (Section 4)

Table 3 evaluates whether Soft MoE representations transfer to image-text contrastive learning, following the LiT protocol (Zhai et al., 2022b): the image tower is pretrained on JFT classification and frozen, while a text encoder is trained from scratch on WebLI (10B image-text pairs, ~5 epochs).

**Main finding:** Soft MoE consistently outperforms ViT at matched backbone sizes on zero-shot transfer:

- **Soft MoE S/16 (128E) vs. ViT S/16**: ImageNet 0-shot improves from 74.2% to 81.2% (+7.0 points), Cifar-100 0-shot from 56.6% to 67.2% (+10.6 points), Pet 0-shot from 94.8% to 96.6% (+1.8 points).
- **Soft MoE B/16 (128E) vs. ViT B/16**: ImageNet 0-shot from 79.6% to 82.5% (+2.9 points), Cifar-100 from 71.0% to 74.4% (+3.4 points).
- **Soft MoE L/16 (128E) vs. ViT L/16**: ImageNet 0-shot from 82.7% to 83.8% (+1.1 points), Cifar-100 from 77.5% to 79.9% (+2.4 points).
- **Soft MoE H/14 (256E) vs. ViT H/14**: ImageNet 0-shot from 83.8% to 84.6% (+0.8 points), Cifar-100 from 84.7% to 86.3% (+1.6 points). However, COCO retrieval metrics degrade slightly (Img2Text: 62.7 → 61.0; Text2Img: 45.2 → 44.8).

**Interpretation:** The benefits are largest at smaller backbone sizes (S, B) and narrow at larger sizes (L, H), which is consistent with Soft MoE providing a larger relative capacity increase over the dense baseline when the backbone is small. The paper notes that "the improvement on COCO retrieval are modest, and likely reflects the poor alignment between features learned on closed-vocabulary JFT and this open-vocabulary task" (Section 4).

**LAION-400M results (Appendix F.1, Figure 15):** Training both towers from scratch on the publicly available LAION-400M (275M usable images after filtering) shows the same pattern: Soft MoE outperforms both ViT and Experts Choice on ImageNet 10-shot, ImageNet 0-shot, and Oxford-IIIT Pet 0-shot accuracy. Additionally, Soft MoE benefits from data augmentation ("Inception crop"), while neither ViT nor Experts Choice does — the paper hypothesizes this is because "Soft MoE can better utilize the expert parameters," consistent with the finding that Soft MoE scales better with expert count (Section 3.5).

#### Expert Placement Strategy (Appendix D)

Tables 4, 5, and 6 ablate how experts should be distributed across Transformer layers, fixing a total of 512 experts (with one slot each) and varying their placement within a 12-layer ViT S/16.

**For Soft MoE (Table 4):** The best configuration places 128 experts in each of the last 4 layers (layers 8-11), achieving 72.8% ImageNet 10-shot and 53.2% JFT precision. Placing all 512 experts in a single layer (layer 11) achieves only 70.0% ImageNet 10-shot — a 2.8 point degradation. Spreading experts across non-consecutive layers ("2, 5, 8, 11" with 128 each) achieves 71.1%, intermediate between single-layer and consecutive-late-layer placement. The takeaway: distributing a fixed expert budget across multiple later layers is better than concentrating it, and placing those layers consecutively at the end of the network is marginally better than interleaving them.

**For Tokens Choice (Table 5) and Experts Choice (Table 6):** The same pattern holds — the best configuration for both is "4:11" (64 experts in each of the last 8 layers) or "8, 9, 10, 11" (128 experts in each of the last 4 layers). This suggests the optimal expert placement strategy is **independent of the routing algorithm** — it is a property of the Transformer architecture rather than the MoE mechanism.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Dispatch vs. combine weight symmetry:** Table 2 shows that asymmetric configurations (learned dispatch with uniform combine, or uniform dispatch with learned combine) degrade performance by 0.7-1.7 JFT points and 2.8-3.0 ImageNet 10-shot points relative to symmetric Soft MoE. The learned dispatch is slightly more important (Soft/Uniform: -1.7 JFT) than learned combine alone (Uniform/Soft: -2.3 JFT), but the full symmetric system outperforms both asymmetric variants, confirming that the information bottleneck created by shared Φ parameters is a meaningful design choice rather than an arbitrary convenience.

**L2 normalization stability (Appendix E, Figures 13 and 14):** Figure 13 tracks three metrics during training of a Soft MoE S/14 (3 dense + 3 MoE layers, 256 experts) at model dimensions d ∈ {384, 768, 1024, 1280, 1664}. Without L2 normalization, the average maximum dispatch weight per slot grows from ~0.3 to ~0.8 as d increases from 384 to 1664, and the average maximum combine weight grows from ~0.5 to ~0.9. With normalization, dispatch weights stay at ~0.2-0.3 and combine weights at ~0.3-0.5 across all d. Correspondingly, ImageNet 10-shot accuracy without normalization degrades at large d (d=1664: ~0.65 with vs. ~0.55 without at 300k steps), while normalized models maintain consistent accuracy across d. Figure 14 shows this is exacerbated by learning rate: without normalization, peak learning rates of 0.003-0.006 cause dispatch weights to reach 0.8-1.0 (near-complete collapse) and accuracy to drop to ~0.2-0.3, while normalized models maintain ~0.75 accuracy across learning rates from 0.0003 to 0.006.

**Multiple slots per expert — slot correlation analysis (Appendix I, Figures 28-30):** The paper analyzes the inner product between normalized slot parameter vectors for models with 1, 4, and 16 slots per expert. Figure 28 (1 slot/expert, 32 experts): no systematic correlation between slots — the off-diagonal inner products show a roughly uniform distribution from -0.2 to 0.8, indicating independent specialization. Figure 29 (4 slots/expert, 32 experts, 128 total slots): clear block-diagonal structure emerges — within each group of 4 consecutive slots (assigned to the same expert), the inner products approach 1.0 (bright yellow blocks along the diagonal), while between-expert correlations remain low. Figure 30 (16 slots/expert, 32 experts, 512 total slots): the block-diagonal structure is even more pronounced with larger blocks. This confirms that slots assigned to the same expert learn nearly identical linear combinations, providing diminishing informational gain and explaining why one-slot-per-expert is optimal.

**Token dropping analysis for Sparse MoEs (Appendix B, Figures 8-11):** These figures characterize the token dropping pathology that Soft MoE avoids. For Experts Choice with C=1 and increasing experts from 4 to 1024 (Figure 8), the maximum dropping ratio across layers grows from ~0.05 to ~0.45 — nearly half of tokens are never processed by any expert at 1024 experts. Performance degrades accordingly: ImageNet 10-shot drops from ~0.725 to ~0.55. For Tokens Choice, the dropping ratio grows from ~0.05 to ~0.25 at 1024 experts, with a milder performance decline. Adding buffer slack (C=1.125; Figures 9-10) reduces dropping rates by ~5 percentage points but does not fully eliminate the problem. Batch Priority Routing (BPR; Figure 11) significantly improves Tokens Choice performance by intelligently selecting which tokens to drop, but the fundamental pathology remains.

**Increasing slots for fixed experts (Appendix C, Figure 12):** For Soft MoE S/16 with 32 experts, increasing slots per expert from 1 to 32 improves JFT precision from ~0.50 to ~0.53 and ImageNet 10-shot from ~0.66 to ~0.72, but training step time increases from ~100ms to ~250ms — a 2.5× cost increase for a 3-point accuracy gain. Experts Choice with group size 8 shows stronger scaling (from ~0.49 to ~0.53 JFT) but at a much higher baseline cost (step time exceeds 250ms at 32 slots). The cost-efficiency of adding slots is poor relative to simply using those compute resources to add more experts.

**Contrastive learning on public data with and without augmentation (Appendix F.1, Figure 15):** Training image-text models from scratch on LAION-400M confirms that Soft MoE outperforms ViT and Experts Choice across ImageNet 10-shot, 0-shot, and Pet 0-shot accuracy. The finding that **only Soft MoE benefits from data augmentation** (Inception crop: solid vs. dashed lines in Figure 15) is non-obvious and consistent with the paper's hypothesis that Soft MoE makes better use of additional parameters — data augmentation effectively increases the diversity of the training distribution, and Soft MoE's greater capacity allows it to absorb this diversity where dense models and Experts Choice saturate.

**Pareto frontier completeness (Appendix F, Figures 19-22):** These figures show all 106 trained models (not just Pareto-optimal ones), color-coded by method. Figures 19-22 progressively filter to show only Soft MoE vs. all methods, Soft MoE vs. Dense only, Soft MoE vs. Experts Choice only, and Soft MoE vs. Tokens Choice only. The key takeaway is that Soft MoE models consistently appear in the upper-left region of the plots (high accuracy, low cost) while other methods' models cluster in the lower-right (lower accuracy or higher cost). The Pareto dominance is not an artifact of selective display — the full distribution confirms the ranking.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "Soft MoE strongly outperforms dense ViTs and popular MoEs on training Pareto frontiers."** This claim is well-supported by Figures 3a and 3b and the comprehensive model sweep in Table 9. The experimental design is thorough: 106 models spanning multiple backbone sizes, three routing methods, and various hyperparameter configurations, all trained under identical conditions (300k steps, batch 4096, reciprocal square root schedule). The Pareto frontier analysis is appropriate and the full data in Appendix J provides transparency. However, there are caveats:

- **Single dataset (JFT-4B).** All pretraining is on JFT-4B, a proprietary dataset with 29k classes. The results may not transfer to other pretraining distributions, though the LAION-400M contrastive learning results (Appendix F.1) and the LiT-style transfer to WebLI (Section 4) provide some evidence of cross-dataset robustness.
- **Single model family (ViT).** All experiments use Vision Transformers. Whether Soft MoE provides similar benefits for other architectures (CNNs, MLP-Mixers, hierarchical ViTs) is untested.
- **Single training regime (300k steps for Pareto, up to 9M for long runs).** The Pareto curves may shift at different training durations — a model that is Pareto-optimal at 300k steps might not be at 1M steps. The longer training runs partially address this but only for selected configurations.
- **The "Pareto frontier" is constructed post-hoc** from discrete model configurations, not by actually finding the optimal hyperparameters for each method at each cost point. There might exist configurations of Tokens Choice or Experts Choice that would outperform the tested ones if the hyperparameter search were more exhaustive.

**Claim 2: "Soft MoE B/16 matches ViT H/14 quality while being 5.7× faster at inference."** This claim is well-supported by Table 1 and Figure 5. The comparison is fair: both models are trained to convergence (Soft MoE B/16 for 9M steps, ViT H/14 for 2M steps), and inference costs are measured in both FLOPs and wall-clock time on the same hardware. The numbers are precise: 32 vs. 334 GFLOPs/image (10.4×) and 1.5 vs. 8.6 ms/image (5.7×). However:

- **Training cost comparison:** The Soft MoE B/16 required 1011.4 TPU-days vs. ViT H/14's 2039.8 TPU-days (roughly half). But Soft MoE B/16 was trained for 9M steps vs. ViT H/14's 2M steps. The training cost advantage depends on the assumption that ViT H/14 could not have been improved by longer training — ViT H/14 trained for 2M steps achieves 59.7% JFT; it is possible that training it longer would close some of the gap, though the inference cost advantage would remain.
- **The "matched quality" varies by metric:** Soft MoE B/16 outperforms ViT H/14 on JFT (62.4 vs. 59.7), slightly underperforms on ImageNet 10-shot (82.9 vs. 83.3), and matches on finetune (88.5 vs. 88.9). The claim holds on average but the specific metric matters.
- **Finetuning protocol:** Soft MoE models use the same number of slots (128) at finetuning time, even though the number of input tokens increases to 576 (16×16 patches at 384 resolution). The paper notes this makes "Soft MoEs become cheaper than ViT, as the number of input tokens grows to 576 but the number slots is fixed to a much smaller number" (Table 8 caption). This is a deliberate design choice that contributes to the inference speed advantage, but it means the comparison is between a Soft MoE that processes 128 slots and a ViT that processes all 576 tokens — the Soft MoE is fundamentally doing less computation in its MoE layers.

**Claim 3: "Soft MoE scales well: more experts consistently improve performance without the training difficulties that plague Sparse MoEs."** This claim is supported by Figure 6 (top-left heatmap) and Figure 23 (Appendix F.2). Soft MoE accuracy monotonically increases with expert count from 2 to 4096 experts, while both Tokens Choice and Experts Choice degrade beyond an optimum. This is one of the paper's strongest empirical findings. However:

- **The monotonic scaling is demonstrated at fixed total training steps (300k).** It is possible that with more training, the benefit of additional experts would saturate or that the optimal expert count would shift. The claim should be interpreted as "at fixed training budget, more experts help," not "more experts always help regardless of training duration."
- **The 4096-expert Soft MoE achieves 75.4% ImageNet 10-shot with 16 slots per token** (Figure 6, bottom-right of top-left heatmap). This is the best result in the heatmap, but it uses 16× more expert computation than the 1-slot-per-token configurations. The throughput drops to 132 img/s at this setting. The "scales well" claim is about expert count scaling (columns), not slots-per-expert scaling (rows).
- **Memory scaling is not addressed in depth.** A Soft MoE with 4096 experts has ~9.7B parameters (Appendix F, Figure 23 caption). The paper acknowledges this in Section 6 ("the memory requirements of the model can grow large") but does not quantify the memory cost relative to a comparable-quality dense model. The 2.9× throughput drop from 8 to 4096 experts (Figure 6, bottom-left) indicates that memory/communication overhead is non-trivial at scale.

**Claim 4: "One slot per expert is consistently optimal."** This claim is well-supported by the heatmap analysis (Figure 6), the slot correlation analysis (Appendix I, Figures 28-30), and the diminishing returns shown in Figure 12 (Appendix C). The evidence is convergent: multiple slots per expert cause slot parameter alignment, and the accuracy gains from additional slots per expert are small relative to the cost increase. This is a robust and well-documented finding.

**Claim 5: "Soft MoE representations transfer to contrastive learning and outperform dense ViTs."** Supported by Table 3 (LiT-style) and Figure 15 (LAION-400M from scratch). However:

- **The LiT protocol freezes the image tower**, so the measured benefit is purely from the quality of frozen Soft MoE representations, not from the Soft MoE architecture adapting during contrastive training. This is a strength (it isolates representation quality) but also means the results don't demonstrate that Soft MoE is beneficial *during* contrastive training.
- **COCO retrieval metrics degrade for Soft MoE H/14** (Table 3: Img2Text drops from 62.7 to 61.0, Text2Img from 45.2 to 44.8). The paper attributes this to "poor alignment between features learned on closed-vocabulary JFT and this open-vocabulary task," but this explanation is not tested — it might alternatively reflect a genuine weakness of Soft MoE representations for retrieval-style tasks. The small absolute differences make it difficult to draw strong conclusions.
- **Only one text tower architecture (ViT-g) is tested** for the LiT experiments, and only one contrastive dataset (WebLI) is used. The generalization of these transfer results to other text encoders or datasets is open.

**What experiments would strengthen the paper:**

- **Language modeling or multi-modal decoder experiments.** The paper explicitly identifies auto-regressive decoding as a limitation (Section 6) and leaves it for future work. Demonstrating Soft MoE in a decoder-only or encoder-decoder setting (even on a small scale) would significantly broaden the claimed applicability.
- **Ablation on the number of MoE layers vs. number of experts per layer at fixed total parameters.** Appendix D does this for 512 total experts but only at S/16 scale. A more systematic exploration across scales (B, L, H) and total expert budgets would clarify whether the "last few layers with many experts each" strategy generalizes.
- **Inference-time scaling with sequence length.** All experiments use fixed resolutions (224 for pretraining, 384 for finetuning). The claim that Soft MoE achieves its speed advantage partly because the number of slots is fixed while dense models process all tokens (and thus become more expensive at longer sequences) could be tested by evaluating inference cost as a function of sequence length.
- **Comparison to soft parameter merging methods** (Yang et al., 2019; Muqeeth et al., 2023). The paper discusses these approaches as related work but provides no empirical comparison. A head-to-head at matched parameter counts would clarify whether Soft MoE's slot-based approach is preferable to expert parameter averaging for vision tasks.
- **Statistical significance or confidence intervals.** All reported numbers are point estimates from single training runs. Given the complexity of the training pipeline and the large number of hyperparameters swept, some estimate of variance (e.g., from multiple seeds) would help distinguish genuine improvements from noise, especially for the smaller gaps in the contrastive learning and finetuning results.

**Summary assessment:** The experimental analysis is thorough within its scope — the Pareto sweep over 106 models, the systematic scaling analysis across expert counts and slots, the long-training runs, and the component ablations together provide strong evidence for Soft MoE's advantages over existing sparse routers on image classification with Vision Transformers. The largest gap is the restriction to vision encoders — the paper's claims about "MoEs" are implicitly about vision MoEs in particular, and the extension to language or multi-modal decoders is acknowledged as a limitation (Section 6). Within the vision classification domain, the evidence is consistent and convincing: Soft MoE outperforms both dense ViTs and the two dominant sparse routing paradigms across every tested configuration, metric, and training budget.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Auto-Regressive Decoding Constraint

**The assumption or constraint.** Soft MoE computes each slot as a weighted average of all input tokens and each output token as a weighted average of all output slots. In an auto-regressive decoder, causality must be preserved — a token at position t cannot attend to tokens at positions > t. The paper acknowledges this explicitly:

> "This makes the use of Soft MoEs in auto-regressive decoders difficult, since causality between past and future tokens has to be preserved during training. Although causal masks used in attention layers could be used, one must be careful to not introduce any correlation between token and slot indices, since this may bias which token indices each expert is trained on." (Section 6)

The problem is subtler than simply applying a causal mask to the dispatch weights. If slot j is assigned to expert e, and the dispatch weights for slot j are causally masked so that only tokens up to position t contribute to slot j at timestep t, then the tokens that expert e processes change systematically with position — early positions route through experts that have only seen early tokens, while later positions route through experts that have seen the full prefix. This creates a **correlation between token position and expert index** that could bias which experts learn which patterns, since experts assigned to "early" slots are trained on different data distributions than experts assigned to "late" slots.

**The consequence.** The most immediate consequence is that Soft MoE is demonstrated only on **encoder-only vision tasks** (image classification, contrastive image-text alignment where the text encoder is a separate dense model). The paper's contributions do not directly transfer to language modeling, machine translation, or any decoder-only or encoder-decoder architecture — which constitute a substantial fraction (perhaps the majority) of MoE use cases in practice, given the prominence of MoE language models (GShard, Switch Transformer, GLaM, Mixtral). A practitioner interested in applying Soft MoE to a language model cannot simply drop it in; they would need to solve the causal masking problem, which the paper identifies as "a promising research avenue that we leave for future work."

A secondary consequence is that the comparison to prior work is incomplete. The Sparse MoE methods that Soft MoE is benchmarked against (Tokens Choice, Experts Choice) have been demonstrated successfully in both encoder and decoder settings. The paper's evidence for Soft MoE's superiority applies only to the encoder setting; whether the advantages persist in decoder settings — where the discrete routing pathologies might interact differently with causality — is unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No empirical evidence. The paper does not train any Soft MoE decoder. The limitation is identified as a conceptual challenge in Section 6 and deferred entirely to future work.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper suggests that causal masking "could be used" but does not attempt it, does not propose a specific masking scheme, and does not discuss whether the L2 normalization fix from Section 2.3 would interact differently with masked dispatch weights. This is a recognition of the problem, not a mitigation.

### Memory Consumption from the One-Slot-Per-Expert Design Principle

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's core scaling insight — one slot per expert is consistently optimal — has a hidden cost: it maximizes the number of experts for any given total slot budget, and therefore maximizes the number of expert MLP parameters that must be stored. The paper is transparent about this:

> "One slot per expert tends to be the optimal choice... Consequently, Soft MoE can leverage a large number of experts and—while its cost is still similar to the dense backbone—the memory requirements of the model can grow large." (Section 6)

To make this concrete: Soft MoE H/14 with 128 experts and one slot per expert has 27.3B parameters (Table 1), compared to ViT H/14's 669M — a **40.8× increase**. With 256 experts, it reaches 54.1B parameters. These parameters are the expert MLP weights (two linear layers per expert, each with input dimension d and hidden dimension d_ff). At one slot per expert, every additional expert adds a full MLP's worth of parameters. If the model instead used fewer experts with more slots each, the total parameter count would be lower for the same computational budget (since total slots S = n·p would be fixed, but n would be smaller, so fewer expert MLPs to store).

**The consequence.** The memory requirements create several practical problems:

- **Hardware accessibility.** A 54B-parameter model cannot fit on a single accelerator (even the largest TPUv3 or A100 has at most 80GB of memory). Distributed deployment is mandatory, adding communication overhead that the paper's analysis of "2% increased inference time" accounts for (Table 1 reports 8.8 ms/img for Soft MoE H/14 128E vs. 8.6 ms/img for ViT H/14 — roughly 2% — but this is measured in a distributed setting with sufficient devices to hold the model). For a practitioner with fewer devices, the inference cost would be higher (or infeasible) due to the need to sequentially load and unload expert parameters.

- **Training memory pressure.** During training, activations and optimizer states (e.g., Adam moment estimates) multiply the memory requirements. A Soft MoE with 4096 experts and ~9.7B parameters (Figure 23, Appendix F.2) requires substantial model parallelism to train, which the paper implements but does not benchmark in isolation. Figure 6 (bottom) shows Soft MoE throughput dropping from 379 to 132 img/s as experts increase from 8 to 4096 — this 2.9× reduction is attributed to "increased memory pressure and communication overhead" (Section 2.3) but not broken down into its components.

- **Fine-tuning and deployment on resource-constrained devices.** The paper's claim of "2% increased inference time" applies to the TPUv3 distributed setting used for the experiments. On a single GPU, a model that is 40× larger in parameters may not fit at all regardless of FLOPs, or may be bottlenecked by memory bandwidth rather than compute. The paper does not evaluate inference in memory-constrained scenarios.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The memory scaling problem is visible in the throughput data (Figure 6, bottom) — the throughput decline with expert count, while milder than Sparse MoEs, is still present and non-trivial (2.9× from 8 to 4096 experts). Table 1 reports model parameter counts alongside speed, making the tradeoff explicit: Soft MoE B/16 is 5.7× faster than ViT H/14 at inference but has 3.7B parameters vs. 669M — 5.5× larger in parameter count. The paper acknowledges this tradeoff in Section 6 but does not systematically explore how the parameter-to-speed ratio varies across model scales or hardware configurations.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged but not solved. The paper discusses it as a "current limitation" and hypothesizes that multiple slots per expert could reduce memory at the cost of performance, but the empirical evidence suggests this cost is substantial — slots assigned to the same expert align and provide diminishing returns (Appendix I, Figures 29-30). The paper does not propose any memory-reduction technique (e.g., expert parameter sharing, distillation, quantization) or quantify the memory-FLOPs tradeoff more precisely than the throughput numbers in Figure 6.

### Softmax Collapse and Training Stability at Scale Are Mitigated, Not Eliminated

**The assumption or constraint.** The L2 normalization trick (Section 2.3, Appendix E) prevents softmax collapse caused by the interaction of layer normalization and growing model dimension d. However, this fix introduces a new dependency: the **learned scale parameter** controls the temperature of the softmax, and if this scale grows too large during training, the softmax can still collapse. The paper demonstrates that with L2 normalization, training is stable across a range of model dimensions (d = 384 to 1664) and learning rates (0.0003 to 0.006), but this is a finite range of conditions — it does not guarantee stability at larger scales, longer training, or different optimization settings.

Additionally, the analysis in Appendix E establishes that without the fix, the softmax collapse is structurally inevitable as d → ∞. The fix constrains the inputs to be cosine similarities in [-1, 1], giving the softmax bounded inputs. But the learned scale can, in principle, grow without bound during training (there is no regularization on it), and if scale → ∞, the softmax again approaches a one-hot regardless of the bounded inputs. The question is whether training dynamics naturally keep the scale bounded, or whether there are regimes (very long training, very large models, distribution shift) where the scale drifts upward and causes collapse later in training.

**The consequence.** The practical consequence is uncertainty about training stability at scales beyond those tested. The largest model trained is Soft MoE H/14 with 256 experts (54.1B parameters) for up to 2M steps (Table 1). Whether training remains stable at 100B+ parameters, 10M+ steps, or with different optimizers (e.g., AdamW with different β values, or second-order methods) is unknown. The paper's evidence for stability (Figures 13, 14) covers 3 × 10⁵ training steps; the dispatch weight trajectories are still changing at that point (in some cases still increasing), so it is not guaranteed they remain well-behaved at longer horizons.

A subtler consequence is that the L2 normalization fundamentally changes the semantics of the routing. Without normalization, the dot products XΦ can grow with d, allowing the model to produce sharper assignments as needed. With normalization, the maximum sharpness is controlled by the learned scale — this is a deliberate regularization, but it may limit the model's ability to make very sharp assignments when they would be beneficial. The paper does not ablate whether the scale parameter saturates at some value or whether different tasks benefit from different sharpness levels.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figures 13 and 14 in Appendix E show that with L2 normalization, the average maximum dispatch weight stays below ~0.3-0.4 (Figure 13, middle row) and the average maximum combine weight stays below ~0.5-0.6 (Figure 13, bottom row) across all values of d tested and all learning rates tested, over 300k steps. The corresponding accuracy curves (Figure 13, top row) show consistent, near-identical trajectories for normalized models across d, while unnormalized models degrade at large d. Figure 14 shows similar stability across learning rates for d=1664. However, the trajectories in Figure 13 show that the dispatch weights for normalized models still drift upward slightly during training (from ~0.15 to ~0.25-0.30 at d=1664) — they are not flat. Whether this drift continues, accelerates, or asymptotes at longer training horizons is not shown.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed by the L2 normalization fix, which demonstrably solves the problem for the scales tested. The paper does not propose mechanisms to prevent scale parameter drift (e.g., weight decay on scale, explicit temperature scheduling), does not test stability beyond 300k steps for the normalization ablation, and does not characterize the distribution of scale parameter values across layers or during training. The fix is empirically validated but not theoretically guaranteed to prevent collapse under all conditions.

### Single Benchmark Domain: Vision Classification on Proprietary Data

**The assumption or constraint.** All core experiments — the Pareto sweep, the long-training runs, the scaling analysis, the ablation studies — are conducted on a single task family: image classification on JFT-4B, with transfer evaluated on ImageNet-1k. JFT-4B is a proprietary dataset (4B+ images, 29k classes) that is not publicly available, making exact replication impossible.

The paper acknowledges this implicitly by including contrastive learning experiments (Section 4, Appendix F.1) on both WebLI (another proprietary dataset) and LAION-400M (a public dataset), but these experiments are limited in scope: they test only a few model configurations (S/16, B/16, L/16, H/14 with 128 or 256 experts), use a frozen image tower (LiT protocol), and report only zero-shot transfer metrics. They demonstrate that Soft MoE representations *transfer* to other tasks, but they do not test whether Soft MoE *trains well from scratch* on tasks with fundamentally different characteristics — variable-length sequences (video, audio), structured outputs (detection, segmentation), or different data modalities (text, point clouds).

**The consequence.** The strongest claims in the paper — "Soft MoE strongly outperforms dense and other sparse approaches for any given FLOPs or time budget" (Section 3.3), "Soft MoE scales well" (Section 1) — are supported only for image classification. A practitioner building a speech recognition model, a video understanding system, or a language model has no direct evidence that Soft MoE would outperform alternatives in their domain.

Specific concerns about generalization:

- **Image classification uses fixed-size inputs.** Soft MoE's slot mechanism works with a fixed number of slots S independent of the input sequence length m. On variable-length tasks (e.g., sentences of different lengths, videos of different frame counts), the relationship between m and the optimal S may shift — a model configured for typical-length inputs may under-use experts on short inputs or overload them (in the soft sense of having to compress more information into each slot) on long inputs. The paper does not test variable-length inputs.

- **JFT-4B's 29k-class structure may favor capacity scaling.** A classification dataset with 29,000 fine-grained classes provides a strong signal for learning specialized features — different experts can specialize to different subsets of classes. On tasks with fewer classes or less structured outputs, the benefit of many experts may be smaller. The paper does not test tasks with substantially different output structures.

- **Proprietary data limits reproducibility and community validation.** The paper includes LAION-400M results (Figure 15) to partially address this, showing that Soft MoE outperforms baselines on public data. However, the LAION-400M experiments train only B/16 models (one backbone size), use contrastive learning rather than classification, and cover only ~275M image-text pairs (after filtering) — a much smaller scale than the 4B-image JFT-4B pretraining. The scale gap means the LAION results demonstrate feasibility but do not reproduce the paper's central scaling claims on public data.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides two types of cross-domain evidence: (1) contrastive image-text learning on WebLI (Table 3, proprietary, LiT-style with frozen image tower), showing consistent improvements over ViT baselines; (2) contrastive learning from scratch on LAION-400M (Figure 15, public, both towers trained), showing Soft MoE outperforming ViT and Experts Choice on ImageNet 0-shot/10-shot and Pet 0-shot. Both are still vision tasks, and both use the same ViT backbone architecture. No non-vision tasks, no decoder architectures, no tasks with variable-length inputs are tested.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged by implication (the paper does not claim applicability outside vision and explicitly identifies auto-regressive decoding as a limitation). The contrastive learning experiments partially address cross-task generalization but remain within the vision domain. The paper does not propose experiments or argue for domain-generality of the method beyond what is demonstrated.

### The Training Cost of Soft MoE vs. Dense Is Ambiguous at Matched Quality

**The assumption or constraint.** A central claim is that Soft MoE achieves better performance at lower training cost. However, the training cost comparison depends on how "matched quality" is defined and whether both models are trained to their respective convergence points. The paper reports Soft MoE B/16 trained for 9M steps at 1011.4 TPU-days matching ViT H/14 trained for 2M steps at 2039.8 TPU-days (Table 1) — roughly half the training cost. But Soft MoE B/16 was trained for **4.5× more steps** (9M vs. 2M). The training cost advantage comes from the fact that each Soft MoE B/16 step is much cheaper than each ViT H/14 step ($1011.4/9 \times 10^6 \approx 0.00011$ TPU-days per step vs. $2039.8/2 \times 10^6 \approx 0.0010$ TPU-days per step — roughly 9× cheaper per step). The total cost is lower despite more steps because the per-step cost advantage (roughly 9×) outweighs the step count disadvantage (4.5×).

The question this raises: is ViT H/14 truly at convergence after 2M steps, or would additional training further improve it and shift the cost-quality comparison? The paper does not train ViT H/14 beyond 2M steps, citing cost reasons ("for cost reasons," Section 3.4). This is understandable but means the comparison is between a Soft MoE model trained to near-convergence (9M steps for B/16, with a 500k-step cooldown) and a ViT model that may not be fully converged.

**The consequence.** If ViT H/14 could achieve meaningfully higher accuracy with longer training, the "half the training cost" claim would overstate Soft MoE's advantage. The relevant comparison should be: at equivalent training cost, which method achieves higher quality? The Pareto analysis in Section 3.3 addresses this for 300k-step training, showing Soft MoE dominates across budgets, but this short-training Pareto does not guarantee the same ranking at convergence. The long-training runs (Section 3.4) compare specific models at specific (different) step counts, not models at matched total compute.

A subtler issue: the training cost numbers in Table 1 include the overhead of distributing the model across multiple TPU devices. Soft MoE H/14 has 27.3B-54.1B parameters and requires model parallelism; ViT H/14 has 669M parameters and likely fits on fewer devices. The distribution overhead (all-to-all communication of slots between devices) is included in the TPU-days measurement, but its magnitude relative to useful computation is not broken out. If Soft MoE's training cost advantage partly comes from the fact that its per-step computation is inherently cheaper (fewer FLOPs) but the distribution overhead is higher (more communication), the advantage may not transfer to settings with different interconnect bandwidth or device topology.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 1 and Figure 5 provide the raw numbers. Figure 4 shows training curves (JFT precision and ImageNet 10-shot vs. training exaFLOPs) for selected long-training models, showing that Soft MoE models are above the ViT models at matched FLOPs between roughly 500 and 3000 exaFLOPs. However, the ViT H/14 curve ends at ~2000 exaFLOPs (1M steps) and ~4000 exaFLOPs (2M steps), while Soft MoE models extend further. The curves suggest that ViT H/14's JFT precision is still increasing at 2M steps (59.7% at 2M vs. 58.8% at 1M, Figure 4 middle panel), so it is not converged — additional gains are likely with more training. The Soft MoE B/16 (3.7B parameters) essentially overlaps with or exceeds ViT H/14 (669M parameters) across the full FLOPs range shown, but this is comparing a model with 5.5× more parameters (and possibly better ultimate capacity) against a smaller model that may be parameter-limited rather than compute-limited.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the cost-driven training limit ("H/14, which was trained for 2M steps for cost reasons," Section 3.4) but does not discuss its implications for the fairness of the comparison. The Pareto analysis at 300k steps (Section 3.3) provides a controlled comparison at matched training budget but at a single (relatively short) training horizon. No experiments systematically vary training duration for a fixed model to map out the full convergence trajectory and ensure comparisons are made at the compute-optimal stopping point for each method.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Soft MoE represents a **categorical reframing rather than an incremental improvement** in how the field approaches conditional computation. The paper does not propose a better discrete router — it challenges the premise that routing must be discrete at all. This shift has several cascading consequences for the research landscape.

**The discrete assignment framing is de-prioritized.** Prior work invested substantial effort in solving the token-to-expert matching problem: linear programming (Lewis et al., 2021), Optimal Transport (Liu et al., 2022; Clark et al., 2022), reinforcement learning (Bengio et al., 2015), greedy selection with auxiliary losses (Shazeer et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2022). Soft MoE demonstrates that **none of this machinery is necessary** — a simple softmax over learned token-slot dot products, with slots processed by fixed round-robin expert assignment, outperforms all of these approaches while eliminating token dropping, expert imbalance, and training instability. The field's attention should shift from designing better discrete matching algorithms toward designing better continuous mixing mechanisms. This does not render all prior MoE research obsolete — Sparse MoEs retain advantages in auto-regressive decoding (Section 6) — but it recasts discrete routing as a domain-specific solution for causality-constrained settings rather than the default MoE paradigm.

**Scaling behavior replaces point-estimate comparisons as the evaluation standard.** The paper's most revealing result is not that Soft MoE outperforms baselines at any single configuration, but that its performance improves **monotonically with expert count** while both Tokens Choice and Experts Choice degrade beyond an optimum (Figure 6, Section 3.5). This establishes a new evaluation criterion for MoE methods: not "what accuracy at N experts?" but "does accuracy continue to improve as N → large?" Any proposed MoE router should now be expected to demonstrate scaling behavior out to thousands of experts, not just peak performance at a hand-tuned count. The paper provides a concrete diagnostic — the heatmap format of Figure 6 — that researchers can adopt as a standard scaling evaluation.

**Verifier over-optimization is not the relevant bottleneck here, but a different failure mode is identified.** While the referenced prior work on test-time compute identified verifier over-optimization as the central scaling bottleneck (reward hacking under aggressive search), Soft MoE identifies a structurally different limiting factor: **slot parameter alignment** when multiple slots share an expert. Appendix I (Figures 28-30) demonstrates that slots assigned to the same expert learn nearly identical linear combinations, wasting the additional capacity. The implication for architecture design is clear: scaling capacity should proceed by adding more independent experts (each with one slot), not by giving existing experts more slots. This is a different kind of "over-optimization" — not exploitation of a learned verifier, but representational collapse due to shared parameters. The fix is architectural (one slot per expert) rather than algorithmic (regularization, early stopping).

**Softmax-after-LayerNorm collapse is now a recognized structural vulnerability.** Appendix E provides both a theoretical analysis and an empirical demonstration that applying softmax to layer-normalized inputs creates an inevitable tendency toward one-hot collapse as model dimension d grows, and that standard parameter initialization does not prevent this. The L2 normalization fix (Section 2.3) is simple but the diagnosis is general: any architecture that routes or attends using softmax over dot products following layer normalization — including standard multi-head attention, Perceiver-style cross-attention (Jaegle et al., 2021), token merging (Renggli et al., 2022), and any future soft-routing mechanism — is potentially vulnerable at scale. The paper provides a concrete monitoring tool (track average maximum softmax weight during training as a function of d) and a general recipe (L2-normalize both inputs and parameters, add learned temperature). This finding should prompt re-examination of stability in large attention-based models beyond just Soft MoE.

**The training-inference tradeoff is reframed for vision architectures.** The paper's most practically impactful finding is that Soft MoE B/16 (3.7B parameters) matches ViT H/14 (669M parameters) on ImageNet while being 5.7× faster at inference and roughly half the training cost (Table 1, Section 3.4). This is a concrete instance of a principle previously explored primarily in language modeling (Clark et al., 2022; Fedus et al., 2022): **it can be more efficient to train a smaller backbone with many cheap experts than a larger dense backbone.** For vision practitioners, this means the default scaling strategy should no longer be "widen the ViT to H/14 or beyond" — it should be "keep the backbone moderate (B/16 or L/16) and add Soft MoE layers with many experts." The paper demonstrates this across scales (S/16 → B/16 → L/16 → H/14 all benefit), making it a robust prescription rather than a one-off finding.

**A previously scattered literature is unified under a common diagnostic framework.** The paper connects several apparently distinct research threads — token merging, Perceiver-style cross-attention, soft parameter merging, and sparse MoE routing — by recognizing that they all compute weighted combinations of tokens or parameters using softmax-normalized scores. Soft MoE can be seen as a specific point in this design space: soft token mixing followed by independent expert processing, with symmetric dispatch and combine. The paper's ablation of this symmetry (Table 2, Section 3.6) quantifies the contribution of each component, providing a template for how future work might systematically explore neighboring points in the design space (e.g., asymmetric dispatch/combine with different learned parameters, multi-round mixing, or learned rather than fixed slot-to-expert assignment).

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Causal Soft MoE for auto-regressive decoders.** The paper identifies the core challenge: naively applying a causal mask to dispatch weights introduces a correlation between token position and expert index that biases expert training (Section 6). A concrete follow-up would design and evaluate a causally-masked Soft MoE layer where dispatch weights are computed as:

$$D_{ij}^{(t)} = \frac{\exp((X\Phi)_{ij}) \cdot \mathbb{1}[i \leq t]}{\sum_{i'=1}^{t} \exp((X\Phi)_{i'j})}$$

so that at each generation step t, each slot aggregates only tokens up to position t. This preserves causality but still creates the position-expert correlation the paper warns about. A strong follow-up would train a Soft MoE language model (e.g., a decoder-only Transformer on C4 or The Pile at 1B-10B scale) with this scheme and measure: (a) whether performance scales monotonically with expert count as in the encoder setting; (b) whether some experts disproportionately handle early vs. late tokens, and whether this specialization harms or helps; (c) whether the L2 normalization interacts differently with the causally-masked dispatch weights. Negative results (performance degrades with expert count, or experts become pathologically position-specialized) would clarify whether the causal constraint is fundamental or solvable with better masking schemes.

**Soft MoE for multi-modal and multi-task architectures.** The paper demonstrates transfer to contrastive image-text learning with a frozen image tower (Section 4, Table 3), but does not explore whether Soft MoE's routing learns modality-specific or task-specific expert specialization when trained end-to-end on multi-modal data. A natural follow-up would train a Soft MoE vision-language model (e.g., a ViT + text decoder) from scratch on a dataset like Conceptual Captions or WebLI, where the Soft MoE layers are placed in the vision encoder and trained jointly with the language objective. The key question: do different experts naturally specialize to different visual concepts (objects, textures, text) or different modalities (image patches that align with text tokens vs. background patches), and does this specialization improve multi-modal performance beyond what parameter count alone would predict? This could be tested by analyzing expert activation patterns (dispatch weight distributions) for different input types and comparing to a dense baseline with matched FLOPs. The paper's visualization in Figure 25 (Appendix G) shows that different slots attend to different image regions, but this is on a classification-trained model — whether semantically meaningful slot specialization emerges in multi-modal training is an open question.

**Scaling laws for Soft MoE: optimal expert count, backbone size, and training tokens.** The paper's Pareto analysis (Section 3.3) and scaling experiments (Section 3.5) demonstrate qualitative trends — more experts help, one slot per expert is optimal — but do not fit quantitative scaling laws of the form $\text{accuracy} = f(\text{backbone params}, \text{expert count}, \text{training FLOPs})$. A systematic follow-up following the methodology of Hoffmann et al. (2022) or Clark et al. (2022) would train Soft MoE models at multiple scales (varying backbone size from S to H, expert count from 8 to 2048, and training tokens from 1B to 100B+ images) on a public dataset (ImageNet-21k or LAION) and fit parametric scaling laws. Key questions: (a) At a fixed total parameter count, what is the optimal split between backbone parameters and expert parameters? (b) At a fixed training FLOP budget, what is the optimal expert count? The paper provides hints (Figure 6 suggests monotonic improvement with expert count at fixed FLOPs) but the optimal could shift at different training budgets. (c) Does the Chinchilla-style insight — that model size and data should scale together — apply differently to Soft MoE than to dense models? Answering these questions would transform Soft MoE from an empirical success to a principled engineering tool.

**Soft MoE with learned slot-to-expert assignment.** The paper uses fixed round-robin slot-to-expert mapping (Section 2.1) and argues this is not limiting because the dispatch weights can learn to route appropriate token mixtures to each slot. But this claim is untested: what if the slot-to-expert assignment were also learned? A follow-up could introduce a second set of learned parameters that softly assign slots to experts, making the entire routing pipeline — tokens → slots → experts — fully learned and content-dependent. Concretely, instead of expert i always processing slots i, i+n, ..., the model could compute a slot-to-expert affinity matrix (similar to the token-to-slot affinity XΦ) and soft-assign each slot to a mixture of experts. The concern is that this would increase computational cost (slots processed by multiple experts) and reintroduce something like expert imbalance. Testing this would clarify whether the round-robin assignment is genuinely sufficient or whether it leaves performance on the table — if learned assignment helps, it suggests a middle ground between Soft MoE's fixed assignment and Sparse MoE's per-token discrete routing; if it does not help, it validates the paper's claim that the dispatch weights are expressive enough.

**Stress-testing the L2 normalization fix at larger scales and in attention layers.** Appendix E establishes the softmax collapse problem and the L2 normalization solution for Soft MoE layers with d up to 1664 (the "G" backbone dimension). A diagnostic follow-up would test whether the same pathology affects standard multi-head attention in large Transformers — attention computes softmax over dot products of layer-normalized queries and keys, which is structurally identical to the dispatch weight computation. The experiment: train dense Transformers (ViT or language models) at increasing dimension d (up to 2048 or larger) with and without L2 normalization of queries and keys before computing attention logits, and measure (a) whether average maximum attention weight drifts toward 1.0 without normalization, (b) whether this drift correlates with training instability or accuracy degradation, and (c) whether L2 normalization in attention layers helps, hurts, or is neutral for standard benchmarks. This would determine whether the paper's stability fix is Soft MoE-specific or addresses a broader vulnerability in the Transformer architecture. The paper's claim that standard initialization "doesn't fix this" (Appendix E.2) is based on a Glorot-uniform initialization; testing He initialization, FixUp, or DeepNorm would establish the generality of the finding.

**Distillation of large Soft MoE models into small dense models.** The paper demonstrates that Soft MoE models can have 40× more parameters than dense ViTs at roughly the same inference cost (Soft MoE H/14 128E vs. ViT H/14, Table 1). This makes them ideal teacher models for knowledge distillation: the Soft MoE teacher has vastly more capacity than a dense ViT student of the same inference cost, potentially providing richer training signal than a dense teacher could. A concrete follow-up would train a Soft MoE teacher (e.g., Soft MoE L/16 with 128 experts, 13.1B parameters) and distill it into a dense ViT student (e.g., ViT B/16, 108M parameters) on JFT-4B or ImageNet-21k, comparing to a baseline where the teacher is a dense ViT of matched inference cost (e.g., ViT H/14, 669M parameters). The hypothesis is that the Soft MoE teacher's expert diversity provides more informative soft labels, and the routing patterns (which tokens/expert combinations are activated for which images) could be used to design better data augmentation or curriculum strategies during distillation. The paper's contrastive learning results (Section 4), where a frozen Soft MoE tower transfers well to a text encoder, provide indirect evidence that Soft MoE representations might be particularly effective as teacher signals.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device and edge deployment of high-quality vision models.** The inference efficiency numbers in Table 1 are directly actionable: Soft MoE B/16 achieves 82.9% ImageNet 10-shot and 88.5% ImageNet finetune accuracy at 32 GFLOPs/image and 1.5 ms/image on TPUv3. For comparison, ViT H/14 achieves 83.3% and 88.9% at 334 GFLOPs/image and 8.6 ms/image. This means a model with 5.7× lower inference latency and 10.4× fewer FLOPs matches the quality of the largest dense ViT tested. For on-device applications — smartphone photo organization, real-time augmented reality classification, accessibility applications that describe images to users — this translates to near-datacenter-quality vision on edge hardware without cloud round-trips. The 3.7B parameters of Soft MoE B/16 are larger than ViT H/14's 669M, which may present memory challenges on very constrained devices, but the computational savings (10.4× fewer FLOPs) dominate the total energy and time budget on most accelerators. A mobile deployment engineer could use these numbers to make a concrete case for Soft MoE over dense scaling: at fixed accuracy, Soft MoE delivers an order-of-magnitude reduction in per-inference FLOPs.

**Cost-efficient large-scale image classification and retrieval pipelines.** For organizations running batch inference over large image collections — e-commerce product categorization, content moderation, satellite image analysis, medical image screening — the training Pareto results (Figure 3) are directly applicable. For a given target accuracy on a classification or retrieval task, the Pareto frontier identifies the cheapest Soft MoE configuration (backbone size, expert count, training budget) that reaches that accuracy. For example, if the requirement is ~75% ImageNet 10-shot accuracy, Figure 3b shows Soft MoE achieves this at roughly 20-30 total training TPUv3-days, while dense ViTs require roughly 100 TPUv3-days — a 3-5× training cost reduction, plus the ongoing inference savings. Since JFT-4B is proprietary, the public LAION-400M results (Appendix F.1, Figure 15) provide a reference point for practitioners working with open data: even at modest scale (275M images), Soft MoE B/16 outperforms dense ViT B/16 and Experts Choice on transfer metrics. A team building a custom classification system could adopt the paper's recipe — replace the second half of ViT blocks with Soft MoE layers at one slot per expert, scale expert count to the memory budget, use L2 normalization in the dispatch/combine logits — with high confidence that it will outperform a comparably-priced dense model.

**Frozen image encoders for multi-modal and retrieval systems.** The LiT-style results in Table 3 demonstrate that Soft MoE image encoders pretrained on classification transfer effectively to image-text alignment when frozen. For systems that use a fixed image encoder with a separately trained text tower — zero-shot classification, image-text retrieval, visual question answering with a frozen vision backbone — Soft MoE offers a direct upgrade path. A team currently using ViT H/14 as a frozen encoder (83.8% ImageNet 0-shot, 62.7% COCO Img2Text recall, 45.2% COCO Text2Img recall) could switch to Soft MoE H/14 with 256 experts and achieve 84.6% ImageNet 0-shot with minimal inference cost increase (8.8 vs. 8.6 ms/image, per Table 1), though COCO retrieval metrics show a small degradation (61.0% Img2Text, 44.8% Text2Img). For smaller-scale systems, Soft MoE S/16 (128 experts) offers 81.2% ImageNet 0-shot vs. ViT S/16's 74.2% — a 7-point improvement at comparable inference cost (0.7 vs. 0.5 ms/image, Table 1 vs. Table 8). The paper does not ablate whether the COCO retrieval degradation at H/14 scale is fundamental or could be addressed with different pretraining (e.g., contrastive rather than classification pretraining of the Soft MoE tower), making this an area for practitioner experimentation.

**Training data generation and pseudo-labeling at scale.** The paper demonstrates that Soft MoE models achieve substantially higher JFT-4B precision-at-1 than dense ViTs at equivalent training cost (Figure 3a: ~0.56 vs. ~0.50 at 100 TPUv3-days). In a pseudo-labeling pipeline — where a pretrained model labels a large unlabeled dataset, and those labels are used to train a downstream model — higher teacher accuracy translates directly to better student performance. Soft MoE L/16 achieves 63.0% JFT precision and 84.3% ImageNet 10-shot (Table 1), which is 3.3 points higher JFT and 0.8 points higher ImageNet 10-shot than ViT H/14 at half the training TPU-days (1355 vs. 2039). A team generating pseudo-labels for a dataset like JFT-4B or LAION could use Soft MoE L/16 as the labeler, achieving higher-quality labels at lower training cost, and the teacher's inference cost (4.8 ms/image for Soft MoE L/16 vs. 8.6 for ViT H/14) means labeling throughput is nearly 2× higher. The paper's finding that Soft MoE benefits from data augmentation while dense models and Experts Choice do not (Appendix F.1, Figure 15) further suggests that Soft MoE teachers may be particularly robust when the unlabeled data distribution differs from the pretraining distribution — a common scenario in pseudo-labeling.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions Soft MoE explicitly against both dense ViTs and Sparse MoEs (Tokens Choice, Experts Choice), and the experimental results establish clear tradeoffs across several dimensions. The decision criteria that emerge from the paper's evidence are:

- **Prefer Soft MoE over dense ViTs when** training and inference cost is the binding constraint rather than absolute parameter count or memory. The evidence: Soft MoE B/16 matches ViT H/14 quality at 5.7× lower inference cost (Table 1); Soft MoE Pareto-dominates dense ViTs on JFT and ImageNet 10-shot across all training budgets (Figure 3). The tradeoff is memory — Soft MoE B/16 has 5.5× more parameters than ViT H/14 (3.7B vs. 669M), so single-device deployment is harder.

- **Prefer Soft MoE over Sparse MoEs (Tokens Choice, Experts Choice) when** scaling to large numbers of experts (hundreds to thousands). The evidence: Soft MoE improves monotonically with expert count while both Sparse MoEs degrade beyond an optimum (Figure 6); Soft MoE throughput at 4096 experts is 2.9× higher than Tokens Choice (132 vs. 45 img/s, Figure 6 bottom); Soft MoE eliminates token dropping (25-50% for Sparse MoEs, Figures 8-10) and expert imbalance entirely. The Sparse MoE advantage is in auto-regressive decoding, where Soft MoE's causal masking problem is unsolved (Section 6).

- **Prefer Soft MoE when** per-sequence determinism matters (small inference batches, reproducibility, transfer learning with distribution shift). The evidence: Soft MoE is deterministic at the sequence level because routing depends only on the sequence's own tokens; Sparse MoEs under capacity constraints are only batch-level deterministic (Section 2.2).

- **Within Soft MoE configurations, prefer one slot per expert with many experts over few experts with many slots per expert.** The evidence: at fixed total slots (fixed FLOPs), accuracy increases monotonically with expert count (Figure 6 top-left); multiple slots per expert cause slot parameter alignment (Appendix I, Figures 28-30), providing diminishing returns; the paper's long-training models all use one slot per expert (Table 1).

- **Prefer distributing MoE layers across the second half of Transformer blocks, placed consecutively.** The evidence: Appendix D (Tables 4-6) shows that distributing 512 experts across the last 4 layers (128 each) outperforms concentrating them in fewer layers, and this pattern holds across Soft MoE, Tokens Choice, and Experts Choice routing — it is a property of expert placement, not of the routing algorithm.
