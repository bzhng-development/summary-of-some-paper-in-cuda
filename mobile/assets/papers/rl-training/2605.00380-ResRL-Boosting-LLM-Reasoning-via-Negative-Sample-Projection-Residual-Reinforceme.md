# ResRL: Boosting LLM Reasoning via Negative Sample Projection Residual Reinforcement Learning

**ArXiv:** [2605.00380](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00380)

## 🎯 Pitch

Penalizing wrong answers in LLM reinforcement learning often unintentionally suppresses correct reasoning patterns that share the same words. ResRL fixes this by mathematically isolating the 'bad' part of a negative response from its 'shared' useful structure, boosting reasoning accuracy by up to 9.4% while preserving—not sacrificing—output diversity.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **ResRL** (Residual Reinforcement Learning), a novel RLVR framework that decouples the overlapping semantic distributions between positive and negative responses to improve LLM reasoning without sacrificing generation diversity. Evaluated across twelve benchmarks spanning Mathematics, Code, Agent Tasks, and Function Calling using Qwen3 model variants (1.7B–8B), ResRL introduces two key mechanisms: **negative sample projection residuals** (projecting negative-token hidden representations onto an SVD-based low-rank positive subspace and using the orthogonal residual to modulate gradient penalties) and a **single-forward proxy metric** (a conservative upper bound on representation alignment derived from a theoretical connection between Lazy Likelihood Displacement and negative-positive head-gradient interference). ResRL surpasses the diversity-oriented NSR baseline by 9.4% in Avg@16 and 7.0% in Pass@128 on mathematical reasoning, and improves over NSR on CodeForces by 9.6% in rating, establishing that targeted suppression of error-specific components while preserving shared semantic structure benefits both Pass@1 and Pass@k metrics simultaneously rather than trading one off against the other.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RLVR Improves Pass@1 but Sacrifices Diversity

The fundamental tension this paper tackles is a specific, documented pathology in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) training: **as the model becomes more accurate on its first attempt (Pass@1), it simultaneously loses the ability to produce diverse, correct solutions when sampled multiple times (Pass@k)**. This is not a hypothetical concern. In practical deployment scenarios—batch evaluation, self-consistency decoding, or search-based inference—the ability to generate multiple distinct correct solutions from the same prompt is essential. If RLVR collapses the model's output distribution to a narrow mode, it undermines downstream techniques that rely on diversity, such as majority voting, best-of-N selection, or Monte Carlo tree search.

The paper makes this trade-off concrete early on: "improvements in Pass@1 accuracy may come at the expense of Pass@k performance; this trade-off may hinder exploration and limit generalization on out-of-distribution tasks" (Section 1). This is the central gap the paper addresses. The field had developed effective methods for boosting Pass@1 (GRPO and its variants), but those methods came with an unwanted side effect—diversity collapse—which prior work had only partially mitigated.

### Why This Trade-Off Matters

The importance of this problem extends beyond the Pass@1 vs. Pass@k metric tension. There are three layers of significance, each with practical consequences:

**1. Mode collapse during training.** When RLVR incentivizes a narrow set of high-reward outputs, the policy distribution concentrates around those outputs. This reduces the model's ability to explore alternative reasoning paths during training itself, creating a feedback loop: less exploration leads to fewer diverse positive examples, which reinforces the narrowing of the distribution. The paper cites work (Simoni et al., 2025) indicating that RLVR can "significantly reduce the base model's output diversity, potentially leading to mode collapse during training" (Section 1). This is a training-stability concern that threatens the scalability of RLVR to longer training horizons and harder problems.

**2. Generalization to out-of-distribution tasks.** A model with a collapsed output distribution has fewer strategies at its disposal when faced with problems that differ from the training distribution. If the model has learned only one way to solve a class of problems, and that way fails on a novel variant, the model has no fallback. Diverse solution strategies provide robustness. The paper's emphasis on preserving Pass@k is essentially an argument that diversity during training serves as a regularizer against overfitting to particular solution templates.

**3. The inference-compute paradigm.** The broader context (which the paper touches on in passing but which is central to the RLVR research program) is that modern LLM deployment increasingly relies on test-time compute scaling—generating many candidate solutions and selecting the best one. If RLVR destroys the model's ability to generate diverse correct solutions, it undermines the very inference strategies that make large-scale deployment effective. The paper's simultaneous improvements on Avg@16 (a Pass@1 metric) and Pass@128 (a diversity metric) are significant precisely because they demonstrate that this trade-off can be overcome, enabling models that are both accurate on single attempts and capable of producing diverse correct answers for ensemble methods.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three categories of existing work and articulates their specific limitations:

**GRPO and vanilla RLVR frameworks.** The Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm, popularized by DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025), optimizes a clipped policy-gradient objective with group-normalized advantages. The standard GRPO objective (Equation 1 in the paper) applies the same mechanism to all tokens—positive and negative trajectories alike—but effectively incentivizes the model to concentrate probability mass on high-reward outputs. The paper frames this as a **positive sample reinforcement (PSR) bias**: the optimization focuses primarily on encouraging the generation of positive responses, while negative responses are only implicitly penalized through the group-normalized advantage structure. This asymmetry leads to diversity collapse because the model's training signal is dominated by "do more of what worked," without sufficient "stop doing what failed." The result: the model learns a narrow set of successful strategies and abandons others.

**Negative Sample Reinforcement (NSR).** NSR (Zhu et al., 2025b) is the most directly relevant prior work. It addresses the PSR bias by explicitly upweighting the gradients from negative samples—"actively suppressing negative ones" (Section 1). The paper acknowledges that NSR "effectively improves Pass@k" and shifts the paradigm from pure positive encouragement to a more balanced optimization. However, the paper identifies a critical failure mode: **indiscriminate negative suppression creates gradient conflicts due to semantic overlap between positive and negative distributions**.

This is the insight the paper builds its entire contribution around, and it requires careful unpacking. Consider a mathematical reasoning problem. A correct solution and an incorrect solution will typically share substantial prefix structure: both might begin with the same problem restatement, the same notation setup, and the same initial algebraic manipulation. Only at some point does the incorrect trajectory diverge—perhaps by making a sign error or applying the wrong theorem. When NSR penalizes the entire negative trajectory, it applies negative gradients to *all tokens* in that trajectory, including the initial tokens that are identical to (or highly similar to) those in the positive trajectory. This creates what the paper calls **cross-sign head-gradient interference**: the gradient from the negative trajectory pushes the model *away* from token distributions that are also present in positive trajectories. The model receives conflicting signals: "generate these tokens" (from positive reinforcement) and "suppress these tokens" (from negative reinforcement).

As the paper states: "When NSR or standard GRPO penalizes a negative trajectory, it inadvertently decreases the likelihood of shared token distributions that also occur in positive trajectories" (Section 1). This is not merely inefficient—it is counterproductive. The model's ability to learn the correct reasoning path is actively undermined by the penalty applied to the shared prefix.

**Lazy Likelihood Displacement (LLD).** The paper connects this gradient conflict to a more general phenomenon identified by prior theoretical work: LLD (Deng et al., 2025c;b). LLD describes the failure of training to increase the log-likelihood of correct trajectories because negative gradient updates actively counteract the positive ones. The paper formalizes this connection in Section 3.1: for a prompt with a positive target trajectory, the training-induced log-likelihood gain is approximately governed by accumulated cross-sign head-gradient inner products between positive and negative token pairs (Equation 2). When these inner products are large—i.e., when positive and negative representations are closely aligned—the positive gradient's effect is partially canceled by negative gradients. LLD is not just a theoretical curiosity; it manifests as training instability, slow convergence, and limited performance gains even when the model is capable of generating correct solutions in principle.

The limitation shared by both NSR and prior LLD analyses is that they identify the *problem* of cross-sign interference but do not provide a *representation-level mechanism* to disentangle the overlapping semantics. NSR modulates the magnitude of negative gradients but does not distinguish between shared tokens (where negative gradients are harmful) and error-specific tokens (where negative gradients are beneficial). The paper's core critique is that NSR's approach is too coarse: it "may suppress the semantic distributions shared between positive and negative responses" (Abstract).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions ResRL as addressing a specific, previously unsolved sub-problem within the RLVR landscape: **how to apply negative reinforcement selectively, penalizing only the token-level components that are unique to incorrect trajectories while preserving the components shared with correct ones**. This is not a new RLVR algorithm per se—the optimization objective in Equation 19 is still a clipped policy-gradient objective—but rather a **gradient-modulation mechanism** that sits on top of existing RLVR frameworks and reweights token-level advantages based on a geometric criterion.

The key conceptual move is to treat the representation geometry of the policy model's hidden states as a proxy for semantic similarity. The paper argues that the orthogonal complement of a negative token's representation relative to the subspace spanned by positive tokens quantifies how much that token deviates from "correct" reasoning patterns. Tokens whose representations lie close to the positive subspace are semantically aligned with correct trajectories and should receive reduced negative penalties; tokens far from the positive subspace are error-specific and should receive stronger suppression.

This is a fundamentally different approach from prior work along several dimensions:

- **It operates at the representation level, not the output level.** Prior methods (NSR, GRPO, DAPO, FlowRL) modulate gradients based on trajectory-level or token-level scalar signals (advantage values, reward distributions, uncertainty estimates). ResRL uses the full hidden-state vectors—the model's own internal representation—to decide how much to penalize each token. This is a more fine-grained, information-rich signal.

- **It is grounded in a theoretical framework.** The paper does not simply propose a heuristic for reweighting; it derives the reweighting mechanism from a formal analysis connecting LLD to head-gradient interference (Lemma 1), upper-bounding representation alignment via the orthogonal-complement energy (Lemma 2, Theorem 1), and showing that this energy serves as a conservative proxy for gradient interference (Equation 9). This theoretical grounding distinguishes ResRL from empirical modifications to RLVR objectives.

- **It preserves the structure of NSR while fixing its blind spot.** ResRL can be viewed as NSR with token-wise weights ω<sub>i,t</sub> that are not uniform (as in standard NSR) but instead depend on the negative token's deviation from the positive subspace. The positive sample reinforcement component remains unchanged (with a small scaling λ<sub>pos</sub> = 0.1). This makes ResRL a modular improvement: it inherits NSR's benefits for diversity while adding a selectivity mechanism to protect Pass@1.

- **It addresses the precision-diversity trade-off head-on.** The paper's empirical results are structured to demonstrate that ResRL achieves simultaneous gains on both Pass@1 (Avg@16) and Pass@k (Pass@128), rather than trading one off against the other. This is shown across model scales (1.7B, 4B, 8B) and domains (mathematics, code, agents, function calling), establishing that the mechanism generalizes beyond a single setting.

The paper also positions itself within a broader research conversation about exploration-exploitation in RLVR training. It explicitly connects to work on Monte Carlo Tree Search (Wu et al., 2025), adaptive Pass@k objectives (Chen et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025b), differentiable top-1 approximations (Peng et al., 2025), and intrinsic signals for exploration (Zhao et al., 2025; Xin et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). The key distinction is that ResRL does not modify the exploration mechanism (e.g., by changing the sampling strategy or adding exploration bonuses) but instead modifies the **policy gradient itself** to be more selective about what it suppresses. This is a complementary approach: better exploration generates more diverse trajectories, while ResRL ensures that those diverse trajectories are not destructively penalized during optimization.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

ResRL is a **gradient-modulation layer** that sits on top of any RLVR policy-gradient algorithm (specifically GRPO) and **reweights the token-level advantages**—the numbers that determine how strongly each word in a model's output is reinforced or suppressed—so that negative samples are penalized only for their *unique errors*, not for reasoning steps they share with correct solutions. The problem it solves is the gradient-conflict pathology where penalizing an incorrect answer inadvertently suppresses the language the model also needs for correct answers; the solution shape is to use the model's own internal representations to measure how much each token in a negative trajectory deviates from the "correct reasoning subspace," and scale the penalty accordingly.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The ResRL system has five major components that operate **per prompt group** (a prompt group is a set of $G$ trajectories sampled from the same question, typically $G=4$):

1. **Base policy model (e.g., Qwen3-4B)** — generates candidate solutions from prompts; produces both the output tokens and intermediate hidden-state vectors at every position.
2. **Positive subspace estimator** — takes the hidden states from tokens belonging to correct (positive-reward) trajectories within the group, applies normalization and centering, constructs a low-rank subspace via truncated SVD, and outputs an orthogonal projector matrix that defines "what correct reasoning looks like in representation space."
3. **Residual energy calculator** — for each token in each negative (low-reward) trajectory, projects its hidden representation onto the positive subspace and measures the normalized squared length of the component that lies *outside* that subspace—the **orthogonal-complement energy**—as a scalar quantifying deviation from correct reasoning patterns.
4. **Group-relative gating module** — takes the residual energies for all negative tokens in the group, applies quantile-based normalization to make them comparable across prompts, and maps each energy to a token-wise weight $\omega_{i,t} \in [\xi, 1]$ where $\xi$ is a floor (0 < ξ ≤ 1) to prevent completely zeroing out penalties.
5. **Advantage reweighter** — replaces the standard GRPO token-wise advantage $\hat{A}_i$ with a modulated version $\tilde{A}_{i,t}$: for positive-advantage tokens, it scales down by a small constant λ<sub>pos</sub> = 0.1; for negative-advantage tokens, it multiplies by $\omega_{i,t}$, so that tokens aligned with the positive subspace receive near-floor weights (mild penalty) while tokens far from it receive near-1.0 weights (full penalty).

Information flows as follows: **First**, the base model samples $G=4$ trajectories per prompt. **Second**, a verifier assigns binary rewards, and group-normalized advantages $\hat{A}_i$ are computed as in standard GRPO. **Third**, trajectories are split into positive ($\hat{A}_i > 0$) and negative ($\hat{A}_i \leq 0$) sets. **Fourth**, if there are no positive trajectories, the standard unmodified GRPO objective is used (no reweighting). **Fifth**, if positives exist, their hidden states are collected, normalized, centered, and a rank-$k$ truncated SVD is performed to form the positive subspace projector $P_S$. **Sixth**, each negative token's centered representation is projected onto this subspace and its residual energy $R_{i,t}$ is computed. **Seventh**, quantile normalization maps $R_{i,t}$ to weight $\omega_{i,t}$. **Eighth**, the modulated advantages $\tilde{A}_{i,t}$ replace $\hat{A}_i$ in the clipped policy-gradient objective.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the formal RLVR objective (GRPO) and its decomposition into positive and negative components, because ResRL modifies exactly the term that governs suppression of negative samples and the decomposition is needed to see *which* gradient terms are being reweighted.
- **Second**, the theoretical framework connecting Lazy Likelihood Displacement to head-gradient interference, because this motivates *why* reweighting based on representation alignment is the right intervention—it is not an arbitrary heuristic but a principled bound on the quantity that causes training instability.
- **Third**, the construction of the positive subspace and the orthogonal-complement energy, because this is the computational engine of ResRL: how the model's hidden states become a geometric signal for semantic similarity.
- **Fourth**, the group-relative gating and final advantage modulation, because these are the engineering choices that turn the theoretical signal into a stable, deployable training mechanism.
- **Fifth**, the length-scaled reward mechanism and other implementation details, because they are pragmatic safeguards that prevent reward hacking in long-horizon training without interfering with the core gradient-modulation logic.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** that proposes a representation-space gradient-reweighting mechanism for RLVR, grounded in a theoretical analysis of gradient interference, and validated across multiple domains and model scales. The core idea is that negative-sample penalties should be proportional to how much a token's representation deviates from the subspace of positive samples, so that shared semantic structure is preserved while error-specific components are targeted for suppression.

#### 3.4.1 The GRPO Objective and Its Positive/Negative Decomposition

**Why start here.** ResRL is not a new RL algorithm; it is a modification to how token-level advantages are computed within the standard GRPO framework. To understand what is being modified, we must first understand the GRPO objective, its decomposition into positive and negative contributions, and the asymmetry that creates the diversity-collapse problem.

**The GRPO objective.** Given a prompt $c$, the policy $\pi_\theta$ samples a group of $G$ trajectories $G = \{y_1, \dots, y_G\}$, where trajectory $i$ has tokens $y_{i,t}$ indexed by time step $t \in \{1, \dots, T_i\}$ (where $T_i$ is the length of trajectory $i$). A verifier assigns a binary trajectory-level reward $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$. GRPO optimizes the clipped policy-gradient objective:

$$L_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{c, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \min\left( \rho_{i,t} \hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(\rho_{i,t}, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \hat{A}_i \right) \right]$$

where $\rho_{i,t} = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} | c, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | c, y_{i,<t})}$ is the importance sampling ratio (the ratio of the current policy's probability to the old policy's probability for that token), $\epsilon$ is the clipping coefficient (typically 0.2, inherited from PPO), and $\hat{A}_i$ is the advantage computed by normalizing rewards within the group.

**What it computes.** For each trajectory $i$, the objective computes the product of the importance sampling ratio $\rho_{i,t}$ and the advantage $\hat{A}_i$ at each token position, applies conservative clipping to prevent destructively large policy updates, takes the minimum of the clipped and unclipped versions (following the PPO convention: if the advantage is positive, this prevents the ratio from exceeding $1+\epsilon$; if negative, it prevents it from falling below $1-\epsilon$), and averages over all tokens and trajectories. The expectation is over prompts and sampled trajectory groups.

**Why this form.** The clipped surrogate objective stabilizes policy-gradient updates compared to unconstrained REINFORCE by bounding the per-step change in the policy. The group-relative advantage normalization (subtracting the group mean and dividing by the group standard deviation) removes the need for a separately learned value function, which is the key innovation of GRPO over standard PPO. This is computationally simpler and avoids the instability of training a critic network alongside the policy.

**The positive/negative decomposition.** The paper decomposes this objective by sign of advantage. The advantage $\hat{A}_i$ is positive for trajectories whose reward is above the group mean and negative for those below. Keeping only terms with $\hat{A}_i > 0$ yields **positive sample reinforcement (PSR)**—the model is encouraged to increase the probability of tokens that appeared in above-average trajectories. Keeping only terms with $\hat{A}_i < 0$ yields **negative sample reinforcement (NSR)**—the model is discouraged from generating tokens that appeared in below-average trajectories.

The critical observation is that **standard GRPO applies the same clipping and scaling to all tokens regardless of their relationship to positive trajectories**. The paper's theoretical framework (developed next) shows that this causes cross-sign gradient interference when a token that appears in a negative trajectory is semantically similar to tokens in positive trajectories. The gradient from NSR pushes the model away from a token distribution that PSR is simultaneously pushing the model toward. The result: those shared tokens receive conflicting signals, training is inefficient, and the model may fail to properly learn even the shared structural components that are common to both correct and incorrect solution attempts.

#### 3.4.2 Theoretical Framework: Head-Gradient Interference and LLD

**Why start here.** The theoretical framework in Section 3.1 is not an appendix curiosity; it is the **design justification** for why ResRL uses representation-space geometry as the signal for gradient reweighting. The paper does not simply propose an empirical trick—it derives the reweighting from a formal bound on the quantity that causes training harm. Understanding this derivation reveals which design choices are principled and which are engineering compromises.

**Lazy Likelihood Displacement (LLD) formalized.** LLD describes the failure of training to increase the log-likelihood of correct trajectories because negative gradient updates actively counteract positive ones. The paper formalizes this for a prompt $c$ with a positive target trajectory $y^+$. Define:

$$\Delta(c) = \ln \pi_{\theta_{\text{fin}}}(y^+ | c) - \ln \pi_{\theta_{\text{init}}}(y^+ | c)$$

where $\Delta(c)$ is the training-induced log-likelihood gain of $y^+$—the change in log-probability of the correct trajectory from initialization to the final checkpoint. If $\Delta(c)$ is small or negative despite training, LLD has occurred: the model has not meaningfully increased its probability of producing the correct answer.

**What it computes.** The difference in log-probability assigned to the positive trajectory before and after training. A large positive value means training successfully made the model prefer this trajectory; a value near zero or negative means training failed to help or actively hurt.

**Why this definition.** Log-probability differences are additive and correspond to information-theoretic quantities (bits of preference), making them the natural metric for measuring how much training has shifted the model's behavior toward a desired output.

**First-order approximation of LLD.** Assuming small output-head updates (which holds in practice due to the small learning rates used in RLVR, typically $1 \times 10^{-6}$), the paper derives the first-order approximation:

$$\Delta(c) \approx -\eta \sum_{(i,t) \in \mathcal{N}(c)} \left\langle \nabla_W \ell^+, g^-_{i,t} \right\rangle \propto -\eta \sum_{(i,t) \in \mathcal{N}(c)} \frac{1}{A^+} \left\langle g^+, g^-_{i,t} \right\rangle$$

where $\mathcal{N}(c)$ indexes token positions $(i, t)$ from negative trajectories sampled under the same prompt $c$ that contribute to the output-head update, $g^+ \triangleq \nabla_W \ell^+$ is the output-head gradient for the positive trajectory (the gradient of the loss with respect to the embedding-unembedding matrix $W$), $g^-_{i,t} \triangleq \nabla_W \ell^-_{i,t}$ is the output-head gradient for the negative token, $\eta$ is the learning rate, $A^+ > 0$ is the advantage weight of the positive trajectory, and $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ denotes the Frobenius inner product for matrices.

**What it computes.** The approximate change in log-likelihood of the correct trajectory is proportional to (negative of) the sum of inner products between the positive head-gradient and each negative head-gradient, scaled inversely by the positive advantage weight. If these inner products are large positive, the negative gradient components partially cancel the positive gradient, and $\Delta(c)$ is small—training fails to imprint the correct trajectory. If the inner products are small or negative, the negative gradients do not interfere and training can proceed.

**Why this form.** The first-order Taylor expansion is the simplest non-trivial approximation and captures the dominant effect when parameter updates are small. The proportionality absorbs shared scalar factors including the learning rate, making the key insight visible: **the accumulated cross-sign gradient interference is the dominant mechanism of LLD**. This directly motivates an intervention that reduces $\langle g^+, g^-_{i,t} \rangle$—the head-gradient inner products.

**From head-gradients to representation geometry.** Directly computing full-parameter gradient inner products is prohibitively expensive—it requires extra backward passes, parameter-sized communication across distributed devices, and introduces sharding-induced variance (Rajbhandari et al., 2020)—as the paper notes in Appendix C.1. The paper therefore focuses on the **output head** $W$, where gradients factorize into a product of the backprop signal at the logits and the token representation feeding into the head. This factorization is formalized in Lemma 1.

**Lemma 1 (Gradient inner-product decomposition).** Consider the linear output head that maps a token representation $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ to logits via $z = Wx$, where $W \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times d}$ and $|V|$ is the vocabulary size. Let $\delta = \nabla_z \ell \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}$ be the backprop signal at the logits (the gradient of the token-level loss with respect to the logit vector). Then:

$$\nabla_W \ell = \delta x^\top$$

and consequently, for any two token instances with pairs $(\delta_1, x_1)$ and $(\delta_2, x_2)$:

$$\langle \nabla_W \ell_1, \nabla_W \ell_2 \rangle = \langle \delta_1, \delta_2 \rangle \cdot \langle x_1, x_2 \rangle$$

where the first inner product is over the vocabulary dimension ($\mathbb{R}^{|V|}$) and the second is over the hidden dimension ($\mathbb{R}^d$).

**What it computes.** The Frobenius inner product of two head-gradient matrices factorizes exactly into the product of the Euclidean inner product of the two logit-space backprop signals and the Euclidean inner product of the two hidden representation vectors. This means that the gradient alignment between any two tokens decomposes into a **logit-space term** $\langle \delta_1, \delta_2 \rangle$ (how similar the token-level prediction errors are) and a **representation-space term** $\langle x_1, x_2 \rangle$ (how similar the hidden-state vectors are).

**Why this form.** The outer-product structure of the head gradient ($\delta x^\top$) is a consequence of the chain rule applied to a linear map. The factorization of the Frobenius inner product follows from basic linear algebra: $\langle \delta_1 x_1^\top, \delta_2 x_2^\top \rangle = \text{tr}((\delta_1 x_1^\top)^\top \delta_2 x_2^\top) = \text{tr}(x_1 \delta_1^\top \delta_2 x_2^\top) = (\delta_1^\top \delta_2) \cdot (x_1^\top x_2)$. This factorization is key because it separates the problem of controlling gradient interference into two sub-problems. The logit-space term $\langle \delta_1, \delta_2 \rangle$ depends on the model's confidence and the target distribution and is difficult to control directly. The representation-space term $\langle x_1, x_2 \rangle$ depends on the geometry of the hidden states and can be measured with a single forward pass—no extra backward passes needed.

**Token-wise weighted gradients.** In GRPO-style objectives, each token term is multiplied by an advantage-derived scalar coefficient $A_{i,t}$. Suppressing shared positive scalars, the effective per-token head gradient is:

$$g_{i,t} \propto A_{i,t} \nabla_W \ell_{i,t}$$

where $\propto$ suppresses a shared token-independent positive scalar. Applying Lemma 1 and pulling out the scalar weights using bilinearity of the inner product:

$$|\langle g_1, g_2 \rangle| \propto |A_1 A_2| \cdot |\langle \delta_1, \delta_2 \rangle| \cdot |\langle x_1, x_2 \rangle|$$

**What this shows.** The cross-sign head-gradient interference magnitude $|\langle g^-, g^+ \rangle|$ splits into three multiplicative factors: the advantage magnitudes, the logit-space similarity, and the representation-space similarity. Since the advantage magnitudes are determined by the group normalization and are not directly controllable per token, and the logit-space similarity is a complex function of the prediction errors, the **representation-space similarity $\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle$ is the most accessible lever for controlling interference**. If we can reduce $\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle$ for tokens that would otherwise cause harmful interference, we reduce the overall gradient conflict and mitigate LLD.

**A single-forward proxy for representation alignment.** To avoid token-wise gradient estimation, the paper upper-bounds the within-group representation alignment $|\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle|$, treating the logit-space factor $|\langle \delta^-, \delta^+ \rangle|$ as an unmodeled multiplicative factor. The key geometric insight is to construct a low-rank **positive subspace** $S$ from the positive tokens in the group, and then measure how much each negative token's representation deviates from that subspace.

**Definition 1 (Positive subspace construction).** Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the set of positive tokens in a prompt group (tokens from trajectories with $\hat{A}_i > 0$), and let $X^+ \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{P}| \times d}$ stack their centered representations as rows. Let $V_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ be the top-$k$ principal directions (right singular vectors) from a truncated SVD of $X^+$, with $V_k^\top V_k = I_k$ (orthonormal columns). Define:

$$S = \text{span}(V_k), \quad P_S = V_k V_k^\top$$

where $P_S$ is the orthogonal projector onto $S$ ($P_S$ is symmetric and idempotent: $P_S^\top = P_S$ and $P_S^2 = P_S$).

**What it constructs.** The positive subspace is the $k$-dimensional linear subspace of the hidden space that captures the dominant directions of variation among the centered representations of positive tokens. The projector $P_S$ maps any vector to its closest point in that subspace. For a token aligned with correct reasoning, its projection $P_S x$ captures most of its energy; for a token containing erroneous reasoning patterns, the orthogonal component $(I - P_S)x$ is larger.

**Why low-rank.** Transformer representations exhibit strong anisotropy and approximate low-rank structure (Joshi et al., 2025; Inkiriwang et al., 2025)—most of the variance is concentrated in a small number of directions. The rank $k$ sets a protection–discrimination tradeoff: larger $k$ expands the positive subspace and protects more shared semantics but may also absorb error-specific directions, weakening discrimination. The paper sweeps $k$ values (8, 64, 128, 256) and finds $k=64$ to be optimal (see ablation in Figure 4).

**Definition 2 (Orthogonal-complement energy).** For any representation $x$:

$$e(x) \triangleq \frac{1}{d} \|(I - P_S) x\|_2^2$$

where $(I - P_S)$ is the complementary projector onto the orthogonal complement $S^\perp$, and $d$ is the hidden dimension.

**What it computes.** The normalized squared length of the component of $x$ that lies outside the positive subspace. If $x$ is entirely within $S$, $e(x) = 0$. If $x$ is entirely orthogonal to $S$, $e(x) = \frac{1}{d} \|x\|_2^2$. The factor $1/d$ normalizes by the hidden dimension, making $e(x)$ an average per-dimension squared residual, which is stable across different model widths.

**Why this form.** The orthogonal-complement energy has an interpretation as a squared distance-to-subspace: $e(x) = \frac{1}{d} \min_{s \in S} \|x - s\|_2^2$. It precisely quantifies how much of the token's representation cannot be explained by the positive subspace. A small $e(x)$ means the token's representation is well-captured by directions that appear in correct trajectories; a large $e(x)$ means the token encodes reasoning patterns not seen in positives—which are therefore likely erroneous.

**Lemma 2 (Alignment bound).** For any $x^+ \in S$ and any $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$:

$$\langle x, x^+ \rangle^2 \leq \|x^+\|_2^2 \left( \|x\|_2^2 - \|(I - P_S)x\|_2^2 \right) = \|x^+\|_2^2 \left( \|x\|_2^2 - d \cdot e(x) \right)$$

**What it shows.** The squared inner product between any vector $x$ and any vector $x^+$ in the positive subspace is bounded above by a term that decreases monotonically in $e(x)$. As the orthogonal-complement energy increases, the maximum possible similarity between $x$ and any positive direction decreases. In other words, **tokens with high residual energy are geometrically constrained to have low alignment with the positive subspace**. If we downweight the negative gradients for tokens with high $e(x)$, we reduce gradient interference not because we guess those tokens are good, but because we proved they *cannot* strongly align with positive directions.

**Why this bound holds.** The key step is the decomposition $x = P_S x + (I - P_S)x$. Since the two components are orthogonal, $\|x\|_2^2 = \|P_S x\|_2^2 + \|(I - P_S)x\|_2^2$. The inner product $\langle x, x^+ \rangle = \langle P_S x, x^+ \rangle$ (the orthogonal component contributes zero because $x^+ \in S$). By Cauchy-Schwarz, $\langle P_S x, x^+ \rangle^2 \leq \|x^+\|_2^2 \|P_S x\|_2^2 = \|x^+\|_2^2 (\|x\|_2^2 - \|(I - P_S)x\|_2^2)$. This is tight when $P_S x$ is colinear with $x^+$ and $(I - P_S)x = 0$.

**Theorem 1 (Residual proxies gradient alignment).** For any negative-positive token pair $(x^-, x^+)$:

$$|\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle| \leq \|P_S x^+\|_2 \sqrt{\|x^-\|_2^2 - d \cdot e(x^-)} + \|x^-\|_2 \sqrt{d \cdot e(x^+)}$$

For fixed $x^+$, the subspace-dependent term is **monotonically decreasing** in $e(x^-)$. Assuming that $S$ sufficiently covers positive tokens (i.e., $e(x^+) \leq \varepsilon^+$ for most positives), we obtain the conservative proxy:

$$|\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle| \leq \|P_S x^+\|_2 \sqrt{\|x^-\|_2^2 - d \cdot e(x^-)} + \|x^-\|_2 \sqrt{d \varepsilon^+}$$

**What it shows.** The magnitude of the representation-space inner product $|\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle|$ is bounded above by a function where the first term decreases as $e(x^-)$ increases, and the second term is a small additive error that depends only on how well positives are captured by $S$ (and vanishes if all positives lie exactly in $S$, i.e., $\varepsilon^+ = 0$). Therefore, $e(x^-)$ serves as a **conservative proxy for gradient interference**: when $e(x^-)$ is high, the upper bound is tight, and $|\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle|$ is forced to be small regardless of the unmodeled logit-space factor.

**Why the decomposition in the bound matters.** The proof (Appendix A.6) splits $x^+$ into its in-subspace component $P_S x^+$ and orthogonal component $(I - P_S)x^+$, applies the triangle inequality to $|\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle| = |\langle x^-, P_S x^+ \rangle + \langle x^-, (I - P_S)x^+ \rangle|$, bounds the first term using Lemma 2 and the second using Cauchy-Schwarz, and substitutes the definitions of $e(x^-)$ and $e(x^+)$. The key property is that the first term—the one controlled by $e(x^-)$—dominates the bound for well-constructed $S$, and it decreases in $e(x^-)$. This monotonicity is what justifies using $e(x^-)$ as the weighting signal: larger residual energy means less potential for harmful gradient alignment.

**Statement of the conservative gradient interference bound.** Applying the reshaped token gradient updates (which assign weight $\omega$ to negative tokens and $\lambda_{\text{pos}}$ to positives) into Theorem 1 yields:

$$|\langle \tilde{g}^-, \tilde{g}^+ \rangle| \propto \omega \lambda_{\text{pos}} |A^- A^+| |\langle \delta^-, \delta^+ \rangle| |\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle| \leq \omega \lambda_{\text{pos}} |A^- A^+| |\langle \delta^-, \delta^+ \rangle| \left( \|P_S x^+\|_2 \sqrt{\|x^-\|_2^2 - d \cdot e(x^-)} + \|x^-\|_2 \sqrt{d \cdot e(x^+)} \right)$$

**What this means operationally.** The gradient interference magnitude for a negative-positive token pair is controlled by the token-wise weight $\omega$ multiplied by a bound that decreases with $e(x^-)$. By setting $\omega$ smaller when $e(x^-)$ is small (tokens aligned with positives) and larger when $e(x^-)$ is large (tokens far from positives), ResRL **concentrates the suppressive gradient pressure on error-specific components while attenuating it on shared components**, directly reducing head-gradient interference and mitigating LLD.

#### 3.4.3 Semantic Representations and Preprocessing

**Why this step matters.** The theoretical framework assumes that the hidden-state vectors $x$ lie in a Euclidean space where inner products measure semantic similarity. This assumption is violated by raw hidden states, which have uncentered distributions and variable norms. The preprocessing pipeline—LayerNorm plus group-wise centering—maps raw hidden states into a space where the geometric operations (projection, residual energy) are well-behaved.

**Extracting hidden states.** ResRL extracts hidden states $h_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ from the **penultimate hidden layer** of the policy model—the layer immediately before the final output head. The final hidden layer feeds directly into the token prediction, making its representations potentially biased toward the immediate next-token prediction objective rather than high-level semantic abstractions (Rogers et al., 2020). The penultimate layer captures "high-level semantic abstractions that are less biased by the immediate token-prediction objective" (Section 3.2). The ablation in Figure 7 confirms that the penultimate layer outperforms the final layer on AIME 2024/2025 accuracy (the penultimate-layer curve rises faster and reaches higher final accuracy), with higher actor KL and entropy indicating broader but controlled exploration.

**LayerNorm.** Each raw hidden state undergoes token-wise Layer Normalization (Ba et al., 2016):

$$\text{LN}(h) = \gamma \odot \frac{h - \mu(h) \mathbf{1}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2(h) + \epsilon}} + \beta$$

where $\mu(h) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^d h_j$ is the per-token mean, $\sigma^2(h) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^d (h_j - \mu(h))^2$ is the per-token variance, $\gamma, \beta \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are learned affine parameters, $\odot$ is elementwise multiplication, $\mathbf{1}$ is the all-ones vector, and $\epsilon > 0$ is a small constant preventing division by zero.

**What it does.** LayerNorm standardizes each token's representation to have (approximately) zero mean and unit variance across its feature dimensions, then applies a learned affine transformation. This removes token-level scale and shift variations, making inner products between normalized vectors more reflective of directional similarity than magnitude differences.

**Group-wise centering.** For the positive tokens in a prompt group, the paper computes the group-wise centroid:

$$\mu^+ = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|} \sum_{h' \in \mathcal{P}} \text{LN}(h')$$

where $|\mathcal{P}|$ is the number of positive tokens. For every token $h$ (positive or negative), the centered representation is:

$$x = \text{LN}(h) - \mu^+$$

**What this does.** Centering shifts the representations so that the positive tokens have zero mean in the analysis space. This is crucial for the SVD step: if the representations were not centered, the first principal component would capture the mean offset rather than the covariance structure of the positive distribution. Centering ensures that the subspace $S$ captures directions of variation among positives—what makes a correct reasoning token different from another correct reasoning token—rather than the shared mean that all tokens possess.

**Why not use the final hidden layer?** The ablation in Figure 7 shows that the final hidden layer leads to lower accuracy, lower KL divergence, and lower actor entropy. The interpretation is that the final layer is "entangled with the final layer's output-bound, next-token prediction bias" (Section 4.3), making its representations less discriminative for semantic similarity. The penultimate layer provides a higher-level representation where shared reasoning structure is more clearly separated from token-identity information.

#### 3.4.4 Subspace Estimation and Residual Computation

**Why subsampling is needed.** The SVD of a matrix of size $|\mathcal{P}| \times d$ is computationally expensive when there are many positive tokens (which occurs with long correct trajectories). The paper constrains this cost by **subsampling** the positive tokens before SVD.

**Sampling-based approximation.** For each prompt group, the paper uniformly samples $M$ centered positive tokens to form a reference sub-matrix $\hat{X}^+ \subset \mathbb{R}^{M \times d}$, capped at a maximum budget $M \leq M_{\text{max}}$. The sampling uses a **boundary-aware strategy** (Algorithm 1 subroutine): it retains head and tail tokens to preserve the logical arc of the reasoning trajectory (the initial problem setup and the final answer) and uniformly subsamples middle tokens. The default budget is $M_{\text{max}} = 4096$, with the sweep in Figure 10 showing that performance is robust for moderate budgets ($M_{\text{max}} \in \{2048, 4096\}$), with diminishing returns beyond 4096. At $M_{\text{max}} = 8192$, the residual contrast collapses and token-wise weights are pushed toward their floor $\xi$, weakening error-specific shaping.

**Why 4096 works.** Transformer representations exhibit local redundancy and low intrinsic dimensionality (Zuo et al., 2025; Ethayarajh, 2019; Aghajanyan et al., 2021), meaning the dominant directions of $X^+$ are recoverable from moderate subsamples. The boundary-aware sampling ensures the crucial prefix and suffix structure is preserved even under subsampling.

**Truncated SVD.** The subsampled positive matrix undergoes rank-$k$ truncated SVD:

$$\hat{X}^+ = U \Sigma V^\top$$

where $U$ and $V$ contain the left and right singular vectors and $\Sigma$ is the diagonal matrix of singular values. The top-$k$ principal directions $V_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ are the first $k$ columns of $V$, corresponding to the largest singular values. The projector is constructed as:

$$P_S = V_k V_k^\top$$

**Computational cost.** Using standard iterative methods (e.g., randomized SVD), the cost is $O(M d k)$ per group, where $M \leq 4096$, $d$ is the hidden dimension (e.g., 2048 or 4096 for typical transformer models), and $k$ is the rank (default 64). In practice, the overhead is modest compared to the forward/backward passes of the base model (Appendix C.1).

**Rank selection tradeoff.** The rank $k$ controls the dimension of the positive subspace. A small $k$ (e.g., 8) under-covers shared semantics: many perfectly valid reasoning tokens fall outside $S$, receive high residual energies, and are over-penalized. A large $k$ (e.g., 256) absorbs error-specific directions into $S$, reducing residual contrast and weakening the ability to discriminate between shared and error-specific components. The sweep in Figure 4 confirms that $k = 64$ is optimal, providing the best accuracy on AIME 2024/2025 and the most stable gradient norms. At $k=128$, gradient norms become oscillatory and bursty, indicating high-variance updates as the subspace becomes too permissive.

**Residual energy computation.** For each negative token with centered representation $x^-_{i,t}$, the orthogonal-complement energy is instantiated as the **projection residual**:

$$R_{i,t} \triangleq \frac{1}{d} \left\| (I - P_S) x^-_{i,t} \right\|_2^2$$

**Computational implementation.** Applying the projector can be done efficiently as $P_S x = V_k (V_k^\top x)$, which costs $O(d k)$ per token (a matrix-vector product with a $d \times k$ matrix). For $T^-$ negative tokens in the group, the total cost is $O(T^- d k)$, which is linear in both the number of negative tokens and the hidden dimension.

**What each value means.** $R_{i,t}$ is the normalized squared length of the component of the token's representation that cannot be explained by the positive subspace. A token that is semantically similar to correct reasoning steps will have its representation largely within $S$, giving a small residual. A token encoding a reasoning error (wrong operation, incorrect value, logical mistake) will have a larger component in $S^\perp$, giving a larger residual. The factor $1/d$ normalizes by hidden dimension so that residuals are comparable across model sizes and layers.

**Why not use the raw hidden state inner products directly?** The residual energy is preferable to $\langle x^-, x^+ \rangle$ or $x^-$'s projection norm for two reasons. First, it is a normalized distance-to-subspace metric that is robust to the scale of individual positive tokens (the inner product depends on $\|x^+\|$, which varies across tokens, while $R_{i,t}$ depends only on the geometry of $S$, which is fixed for the group). Second, it has the monotonic relationship to gradient interference established in Theorem 1, providing a direct theoretical justification for its use as a weighting signal.

#### 3.4.5 Group-Relative Gating and Advantage Modulation

**Why quantile normalization is needed.** The scale of projection residuals varies significantly across different prompts because reasoning problems have different lengths, different numbers of positive tokens, and different intrinsic dimensionalities of their solution spaces. A raw residual of 0.3 might indicate strong deviation from positives for one prompt but only mild deviation for another. Quantile normalization makes the residuals comparable across prompts by mapping them to a common scale based on their rank within the group.

**Quantile normalization.** Let $\mathcal{D} = \{R_{i,t}\}$ be the multiset of projection residuals for all negative tokens in the prompt group. Define the empirical quantile function $Q(\mathcal{D}, \gamma)$ as the $\gamma$-th quantile. The paper sets:

$$q_{\text{low}} = Q(\mathcal{D}, \alpha), \quad q_{\text{high}} = Q(\mathcal{D}, \beta)$$

where $\alpha < \beta$ are quantile thresholds. The paper sweeps the quantile level $q \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$ in Figure 8, finding that stricter thresholds ($q=0.1$ or $0.2$) converge faster and reach higher accuracy than the more permissive $q=0.3$. The default uses $q=0.2$ (applied symmetrically for both tails of the normalization range).

**What this does.** $q_{\text{low}}$ and $q_{\text{high}}$ define a robust range that replaces the minimum and maximum (which are sensitive to outliers) with quantiles. ResRL uses symmetric quantiles for the lower and upper bounds: both are set to the same $q$ value, with $q_{\text{low}}$ being the $q$-quantile and $q_{\text{high}}$ being the $(1-q)$-quantile of the distribution. This means that for $q=0.2$, the bottom 20% and top 20% of residuals are treated as the effective minimum and maximum, making the normalization robust to extreme values.

**Why quantiles instead of min/max.** Raw min/max normalization would be sensitive to a single outlier token with very high residual, which would compress all other residuals to near-zero scores and eliminate discriminability. Quantile-based clipping discards the most extreme values, making the normalization stable across varying prompt-group compositions.

**Min–max normalization with clipping.** The quantile-normalized residual score is:

$$z_{i,t} = \text{clamp}\left( \frac{R_{i,t} - q_{\text{low}}}{(q_{\text{high}} - q_{\text{low}}) + \epsilon}, 0, 1 \right)$$

where clamp$(v, 0, 1) = \max(\min(v, 1), 0)$ and $\epsilon > 0$ prevents division by zero when $q_{\text{high}} = q_{\text{low}}$ (which can happen when there are very few negative tokens).

**What it computes.** A score in $[0, 1]$ that is $0$ for tokens with residuals at or below the lower quantile (meaning they are maximally aligned with positives), $1$ for tokens with residuals at or above the upper quantile (maximally deviating from positives), and linearly interpolated in between.

**Token-wise NSR weight mapping.** The residual score $z_{i,t}$ is mapped to a **token-wise NSR weight** via:

$$\omega_{i,t} = \xi + (1 - \xi) z_{i,t}$$

where $\xi \in (0, 1]$ is the **minimum weight**, ensuring that even tokens perfectly aligned with the positive subspace receive a non-zero penalty. The paper sets $\xi$ implicitly through the floor mechanism; the weight range is $[\xi, 1]$, where $\xi$ is typically a small positive value to keep the negative gradient from completely vanishing for any token.

**What this computes.** A weight in $[\xi, 1]$ that linearly maps the normalized residual score: tokens with low residuals (aligned with positives) receive near-$\xi$ weights (mild penalty), tokens with high residuals (deviating from positives) receive near-$1.0$ weights (full penalty). The $\xi$ floor prevents the negative gradient from being zeroed out entirely, maintaining a minimal suppression signal even for shared tokens.

**Why the linear map with a floor.** A purely proportional weighting $\omega_{i,t} = z_{i,t}$ could zero out the gradient for tokens with $z_{i,t} = 0$, which would completely remove the negative signal for those tokens. The floor $\xi$ maintains a small but non-zero penalty, which is important because even strongly aligned negative tokens may contain subtle errors that warrant some suppression. The linear map is the simplest controllable interpolation; more complex nonlinear mappings (sigmoid, exponential) would introduce additional hyperparameters without clear theoretical justification.

**Advantage modulation.** The final token-wise modulated advantage replaces the standard GRPO advantage in the policy-gradient objective:

$$\tilde{A}_{i,t} = \begin{cases} \lambda_{\text{pos}} \hat{A}_i, & \hat{A}_i > 0 \text{ (positive tokens)} \\ \omega_{i,t} \hat{A}_i, & \hat{A}_i \leq 0 \text{ (negative tokens)} \end{cases}$$

where $\lambda_{\text{pos}} = 0.1$ is a small positive scaling factor for positive advantages, following the weak anchoring mechanism introduced in NSR (Zhu et al., 2025b) to prevent model collapse, and $\omega_{i,t}$ is defined above.

**What this does.** For tokens in positive trajectories, the advantage is scaled down by a factor of 10 ($\lambda_{\text{pos}} = 0.1$), providing a weak anchoring signal that gently encourages the model to maintain its ability to produce correct outputs without aggressively overfitting to specific positive trajectories. For tokens in negative trajectories, the advantage is multiplied by the token-wise weight $\omega_{i,t}$, so that tokens aligned with the positive subspace receive reduced penalties (near $\xi \hat{A}_i$) and tokens deviating from positives receive full penalties (near $1.0 \cdot \hat{A}_i$).

**Why $\lambda_{\text{pos}} = 0.1$?** This follows the NSR design choice. In standard GRPO, positive advantages are large and dominate the gradient, causing the model to aggressively increase the probability of specific positive trajectories at the expense of diversity. The small scaling factor reduces this positive pressure, making the optimization more balanced between positive anchoring and negative suppression. The paper inherits this from NSR rather than re-optimizing it, as NSR already demonstrated its effectiveness.

**Why not also reweight positive tokens?** The paper only reweights negative tokens. Positive tokens are all correct by definition (they come from trajectories with $r_i = 1$), so there is no need to distinguish between "good shared" and "good unique" components—all positive tokens are good. The diversity concern is addressed by scaling down the positive advantage globally (via $\lambda_{\text{pos}}$) rather than per token.

**The final optimization objective.** With the modulated advantages, the ResRL objective becomes:

$$L_{\text{ResRL}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x, \mathcal{G}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \frac{1}{T_i} \sum_{t=1}^{T_i} \min\left( \rho_{i,t} \tilde{A}_{i,t}, \text{clip}(\rho_{i,t}, 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \tilde{A}_{i,t} \right) \right]$$

where $\epsilon$ is the clipping coefficient (inherited from GRPO, typically 0.2). The only difference from the standard GRPO objective (Equation 1) is that $\hat{A}_i$ is replaced by $\tilde{A}_{i,t}$—the token-wise modulated advantage.

**What this objective encourages.** For positive-advantage tokens, the policy is gently encouraged to increase their probability (scaled-down positive gradient). For negative-advantage tokens that align with the positive subspace (low $R_{i,t}$, hence low $\omega_{i,t}$), the policy receives a mild penalty—these tokens are only weakly suppressed because they resemble correct reasoning. For negative-advantage tokens far from the positive subspace (high $R_{i,t}$, hence $\omega_{i,t} \approx 1$), the policy receives the full NSR penalty—these tokens are strongly suppressed because they encode error-specific patterns. The net effect is that the model learns to avoid unique errors without unlearning the shared structure that appears in both correct and incorrect attempts.

#### 3.4.6 Additional Algorithmic Details

**Handling groups with no positive trajectories.** If a prompt group contains no trajectories with $\hat{A}_i > 0$ (all sampled solutions are incorrect), there is no positive subspace to construct. In this case, ResRL falls back to the standard unmodified GRPO objective without reweighting (Algorithm 1, line 6). This is a safety fallback: when there are no correct examples to learn from, the model defaults to equally penalizing all negative tokens, which is the best it can do without positive guidance.

**Truncation guard.** Optionally, a truncation-tail mask $\tau_{i,t} \in \{0, 1\}$ can be applied. When enabled, tokens at the very end of truncated trajectories (which may be cut off mid-reasoning and are therefore inherently noisy) receive $\omega_{i,t} = 1$ regardless of their residual energy. This prevents the model from under-penalizing tokens that appear aligned with positives simply because they were cut off before the error manifested. The paper mentions this in Algorithm 1 (lines 23–24) but the main results use this guard.

**Length-scaled rewards for long-horizon training.** For extended training (e.g., 800 steps on Qwen3-8B as shown in Figure 9), ResRL applies a length-scaled discount to positive rewards to prevent the model from exploiting verbosity as a path to higher reward. The discount schedule is: no change up to 3500 tokens, then linearly decreasing to 70% over the range 3500–4096 tokens. This serves as a "lightweight safeguard against verbosity" (Section 4.3) without requiring an explicit KL penalty term (which the paper shows can be removed entirely when using ResRL's projection-based weighting, as demonstrated in Figure 6).

**Why the KL penalty can be removed.** ResRL's projection-based weighting $\omega_{i,t}$ acts as an intrinsic regularizer: tokens aligned with the positive subspace receive reduced negative gradients, protecting valid reasoning steps from being unlearned. This implicitly anchors the model to the directions that appear in correct trajectories, providing a form of soft regularization without explicitly tethering updates to the SFT prior. Removing the KL term improves AIME2024 accuracy by 9% while remaining stable (Figure 6); the KL divergence still rises, indicating the model is exploring, but the exploration is controlled by the subspace constraint rather than by an external penalty.

**Training hyperparameters.** The paper provides complete training configurations in Appendix E (Tables 6 and 7). Key parameters include: learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$ with 10 warmup steps, weight decay 0.1, PPO mini-batch size 64 (math) or 32 (code), $G = 4$ trajectories per prompt (rollout number), temperature 0.6, top-p 1.0, no top-k filtering ($k = -1$), max prompt length 2048 tokens, max response length 4096 tokens, KL loss coefficient 0.0 (removed), entropy coefficient 0.0, SVD rank $k = 64$, SVD max positive tokens $M_{\text{max}} = 4096$, and tensor parallelism size 8 across 8 GPUs per node with GPU memory utilization 0.65. Training is conducted for 1 epoch over the training dataset, with models trained to convergence under the same budget as baselines for fair comparison.

**Algorithm pseudocode.** The full ResRL procedure per prompt group is specified in Algorithm 1 (Appendix B), which formalizes all steps: splitting rollouts into positive/negative sets, boundary-aware positive token sampling, LayerNorm and centering, truncated SVD and projector construction, residual energy computation, quantile-based weight mapping, and advantage modulation. This makes the method fully reproducible.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Orthogonal-Complement Energy as a Principled, Single-Forward Proxy for Gradient Interference

The most distinctive intellectual move in ResRL is not the reweighting mechanism itself—it is the **derivation that reweighting should use representation-space geometry rather than output-space signals**. Prior work on mitigating LLD and gradient conflict in RLVR operated at the output level: NSR modulated the magnitude of negative gradients uniformly, FlowRL matched reward distributions, and DAPO adjusted clipping thresholds. All of these treat the gradient as an opaque scalar to be scaled. ResRL makes a fundamentally different claim: **the gradient conflict is measurable in the hidden states, and the appropriate intervention is to decompose the gradient into logit-space and representation-space components and control the latter**.

This matters because it opens a new axis for intervention in RLVR training. Before ResRL, the dominant paradigm for improving training dynamics was to adjust the optimization objective (changing how advantages are computed, adding KL penalties, modifying the clipping schedule) or the exploration strategy (MCTS, temperature annealing, entropy bonuses). ResRL introduces a third axis: **manipulate the gradient through the model's own representation geometry**. The paper proves (Lemma 1) that the head-gradient inner product factorizes exactly into logit-space and representation-space inner products, and then proves (Theorem 1) that the representation-space term is upper-bounded by a quantity—the orthogonal-complement energy—that can be **computed with a single forward pass** (no extra backward passes, no gradient estimation). This is a genuine theoretical contribution: it connects a training pathology (LLD) to a measurable geometric quantity and shows that controlling this quantity provides a conservative bound on the pathology's severity.

The practical implication is that **future RLVR methods need not be blind to the model's internal representations**. The paper demonstrates that the penultimate hidden layer (Figure 7) carries a semantically meaningful signal about the distinction between shared and error-specific reasoning steps. This finding is not obvious a priori: one might expect hidden-state similarity to be too noisy or too entangled with token-identity information to serve as a reliable proxy for gradient interference. The fact that it works—and that the penultimate layer is significantly better than the final layer—suggests that the intermediate layers of transformer models encode a notion of reasoning-path similarity that is separated from immediate next-token prediction objectives. This is an empirical discovery with implications beyond ResRL: it points toward representation-space interventions as a general design pattern for RL training of language models.

The sharpest evidence for this innovation's validity is in the ablation studies. The rank $k$ sweep (Figure 4) shows that performance is not monotonic in subspace dimension: too small ($k=8$) under-covers shared semantics and hurts accuracy, too large ($k=128$, $k=256$) reduces discriminability and destabilizes gradients with bursty norms. This non-monotonicity is exactly what the theoretical framework predicts—there should be an optimal tradeoff where the positive subspace is large enough to cover shared structure but not so large that it absorbs error-specific components. The fact that this tradeoff is empirically real and consistent across AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 validates the geometric model underlying the method.

### Innovation 2: Projection Residuals as a Semantic Decoupling Mechanism, Not Just a Penalty Modulator

The paper's second conceptual contribution is reframing the problem of gradient conflict as a **semantic decoupling problem** rather than a penalty-tuning problem. NSR already showed that increasing negative gradient weights helps diversity. ResRL asks a deeper question: **which parts of the negative gradient are harmful and which are helpful?** The answer—penalize only the components orthogonal to the positive subspace—is not an incremental improvement on NSR; it repurposes NSR's mechanism (negative upweighting) toward a more targeted goal (error-specific suppression).

This is best understood through the distinction between **coarse-grained** and **fine-grained** negative reinforcement. Standard NSR treats all tokens in a negative trajectory equally: a token at the beginning that says "Let $x$ be the variable" receives the same penalty as a token later that says "therefore $x = -5$" (where the error occurred). ResRL distinguishes these: the first token, which is identical to what appears in correct solutions, should receive a minimal penalty because suppressing it would interfere with the model's ability to generate the shared reasoning prefix. The second token, which encodes the specific mistake, should receive a full penalty.

This framing is significant because it **reconciles the apparently contradictory goals of Pass@1 improvement and diversity preservation**. The standard trade-off narrative was: you can either optimize aggressively for Pass@1 (GRPO, which collapses diversity) or preserve diversity (NSR, which may limit Pass@1 gains due to cross-sign interference). ResRL bypasses this trade-off by making the negative gradient **selective**: it suppresses error-direction components while preserving shared-direction components. The empirical result—simultaneous gains on Avg@16 and Pass@128 (Figures 2, 3, 5; Section 4.2)—validates that this selectivity translates directly into metric improvements that prior methods could not achieve simultaneously.

Evidence for the selectivity claim comes from the quantile sensitivity analysis (Figure 8). Stricter quantile thresholds ($q=0.1$ or $0.2$) outperform more permissive ones ($q=0.3$), and the stricter thresholds lead to higher KL divergence and actor entropy while maintaining low gradient variance. This pattern is non-trivial: one might expect that more aggressive residual-based weighting would destabilize training, but instead it enables more exploration (higher KL and entropy) without optimization collapse (stable gradient norms). The interpretation is that the projection-based weighting is a **high-fidelity signal**: it accurately distinguishes error-specific from shared tokens, so making the gating stricter amplifies the signal rather than amplifying noise.

### Innovation 3: The Head-Gradient Factorization as a Bridge Between Theory and Practice

The paper's theoretical framework (Lemma 1, Lemma 2, Theorem 1) is not merely a post-hoc justification—it serves a **design function** that distinguishes ResRL from heuristic modifications to RLVR. The factorization $\langle \nabla_W \ell_1, \nabla_W \ell_2 \rangle = \langle \delta_1, \delta_2 \rangle \cdot \langle x_1, x_2 \rangle$ identifies exactly which quantity needs to be bounded to control gradient interference, and the subsequent lemmas show that a specific computable statistic—the orthogonal-complement energy—provides that bound. This means the entire ResRL algorithm can be derived from a theoretical objective (reduce head-gradient interference) rather than invented through trial-and-error.

This is a methodological contribution. Prior work on NSR (Zhu et al., 2025b) demonstrated empirically that negative upweighting helps diversity, but offered no theoretical explanation for **why** it works or **when** it might fail. ResRL provides a principled answer: negative upweighting works when it suppresses error-specific components but fails when it suppresses shared components; the optimal weighting should be proportional to deviation from the positive subspace. The paper does not just claim this—it proves that making the weighting proportional to $e(x^-)$ conservatively bounds the cross-sign head-gradient interference (Equation 10 and the subsequent discussion), and it demonstrates empirically that this bound translates to improved training dynamics (Figures 6 and 9 show stable training without KL regularization, which unmodified NSR cannot achieve).

The significance of this contribution extends beyond ResRL itself. By establishing that representation alignment is the key quantity governing LLD, the paper provides a **diagnostic lens** for evaluating other RLVR methods. For example, one could compute the average orthogonal-complement energy of negative tokens under GRPO training and use it to predict when training will stall or when diversity will collapse. The theoretical framework also suggests natural extensions: different subspace constructions (nonlinear manifolds instead of linear subspaces, dynamic rank selection, per-layer subspaces) could improve the fidelity of the proxy metric, and the decomposition $\langle \delta_1, \delta_2 \rangle \cdot \langle x_1, x_2 \rangle$ could be used to design interventions that target the logit-space term rather than (or in addition to) the representation-space term.

### Innovation 4: The Demonstration That Selective Negative Reinforcement Transfers Across Modalities

A final contribution—less theoretical but practically significant—is the breadth of empirical validation showing that representation-space gradient decoupling generalizes across domains. The paper does not merely show improved math scores; it demonstrates consistent gains on **code generation** (LiveCodeBench, CodeForces, HumanEval+; Table 2), **long-horizon embodied agents** (ALFWorld, WebShop; Table 3), and **multi-turn tool use** (BFCL; Table 4). This is important because it addresses the natural concern that the method might be specific to mathematical reasoning, where trajectories have clear step-by-step structure with well-defined correct/incorrect branches.

The transfer to code generation is particularly instructive. In code, "shared semantics" between correct and incorrect solutions include language syntax, common library imports, and standard algorithmic patterns. The correct-vs-incorrect distinction often hinges on a single logical error (off-by-one, wrong condition, missing edge case) embedded in otherwise syntactically valid code. ResRL's mechanism—penalize the orthogonal component, preserve the shared component—is well-suited to this pattern: it allows the model to maintain fluency in the programming language while learning to avoid specific bug patterns. The 9.6% rating improvement on CodeForces over NSR (Table 2) suggests that ResRL's selectivity is particularly valuable in domains where errors are localized within structured outputs.

For agent tasks, the "shared semantics" include common interaction patterns—navigation commands, object manipulation syntax, environment observation processing—that appear in both successful and failed trajectories. A failed trajectory might differ from a successful one only in a single sub-action (picking up the wrong object, moving to the wrong location). Standard NSR would penalize all tokens in the failed trajectory, potentially degrading the model's ability to execute the many correct sub-actions that appeared in the trajectory's prefix. ResRL's projection-residual reweighting protects those shared-action tokens while targeting the specific mistake. The 10.4% overall success improvement on ALFWorld over EMPG (Table 3) and the consistent gains across sub-tasks (Look, Pick2, Clean) support this interpretation.

The breadth of validation establishes ResRL not as a math-specific trick but as a **general-purpose gradient-modulation mechanism** for RLVR. The key design choices—penultimate layer representations, SVD-based subspace construction, quantile normalization—are domain-agnostic, and the paper's training configurations (Appendix E) show that the same hyperparameters (rank $k=64$, $M_{\text{max}}=4096$, quantile level 0.2, $\lambda_{\text{pos}}=0.1$) work across mathematics and code with only the expected adjustments to response length limits (4096 vs. 8192 tokens) and thinking mode (off for math, on for code). This transferability is a strong signal that the underlying mechanism—not dataset-specific engineering—is responsible for the gains.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on **twelve benchmarks** spanning four domains. For **mathematics**: AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 (American Invitational Mathematics Examination, MAA, 2025), AMC 2023 (American Mathematics Competitions, MAA, 2023), MATH-500 (Lightman et al., 2023), Minerva (Lewkowycz et al., 2022), and OlympiadBench (He et al., 2024). For **code**: LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024), CodeForces (Penedo et al., 2025), and HumanEval+ (Chen et al., 2021). For **agent tasks**: ALFWorld (Shridhar et al., 2020) and WebShop (Yao et al., 2022a). For **function calling**: BFCL (Patil et al., 2024). Training data for mathematics uses the DAPO training set (Yu et al., 2025), for code uses the DeepCoder dataset (Luo et al., 2025), for agents follows settings from Wang et al. (2025), and for function calling adopts the ToolRL training set (Qian et al., 2025).

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use variants from the **Qwen series** (Yang et al., 2025a): Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B for mathematics and code; Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct for agent tasks; and Qwen3-8B for function calling. An additional Qwen3-32B experiment is reported in Table 5 (Appendix D.1). The models are chosen to align with the base models used by the comparison baselines and to span a range of scales (1.7B to 8B for the main results, with the 32B experiment demonstrating scalability). The paper argues these models are representative of contemporary open-source LLM capabilities in the RLVR setting.

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are **Avg@16** (average accuracy over 16 independent generations, a Pass@1-style reliability metric) and **Pass@k** (the probability that at least one of k sampled solutions is correct). For mathematics, Avg@16 accuracy is computed by generating 16 responses per question and averaging correctness. For code, LiveCodeBench reports Avg@16 and Pass@16; CodeForces reports Elo rating and percentile; HumanEval+ reports Pass@16. For agents, ALFWorld and WebShop report success rate (%), with WebShop additionally reporting task score. For function calling, BFCL reports multi-turn and single-turn accuracy, with breakdowns by error type (Base, Miss Func, Miss Param, Long Context, Non-Live, Live). For math and code, decoding uses temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95 with a max response length of 8192 tokens (following FlowRL convention; Zhu et al., 2025a). For agents, rollouts use temperature 1.0 with 50-step cap for ALFWorld and 15-step cap for WebShop (following Wang et al., 2025).

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a comprehensive set of RLVR and NSR methods. For **mathematics and code**: (i) **GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024) — the standard group-relative policy optimization used in DeepSeek-R1; (ii) **DAPO** (Yu et al., 2025) — a variant that adjusts clipping and advantage computation; (iii) **FlowRL** (Zhu et al., 2025a) — which matches reward distributions to encourage diversity; and (iv) **NSR** (Weighted-Reinforce variant; Zhu et al., 2025b) — the most directly comparable method, which upweights negative sample gradients uniformly. For **agent tasks**: (i) **ReAct** (Yao et al., 2022b) — a prompting baseline that interleaves reasoning and action; (ii) **PPO with critic** (Ouyang et al., 2022) — standard PPO with a learned value function; (iii) **GRPO** (Shao et al., 2024); and (iv) **EMPG** (Wang et al., 2025) — entropy-modulated policy gradients for long-horizon agents. For **function calling**: (i) **ResT** (Lin et al., 2025) — reshaping token-level policy gradients for tool use; (ii) **ToolACE-2** (Liu et al., 2025); and (iii) **NSR** (Zhu et al., 2025b). Baseline results for agents and function calling are adopted directly from the cited works to ensure fair comparison.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All methods are trained under identical hyperparameter budgets, including learning rate, batch size, and training duration, with models trained **to convergence under the same budget** (Section 4.1). For mathematics, the global train batch size is 256 with 4 rollouts per prompt ($G=4$) and max response length of 4096 tokens in no-think mode. For code, the global batch size is 64 with $G=4$ rollouts, max response length of 8192 tokens in think mode. The PPO mini-batch size is 64 (math) or 32 (code). All methods use the same number of training steps (1 epoch over the training dataset). For inference, decoding uses temperature 0.6 with top-p 0.95 and top-k disabled. The paper follows official veRL (Sheng et al., 2025) implementations to ensure fair comparison across baselines.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation. Instead, it relies on standard practice in the RLVR literature: all models are trained to convergence under the same budget, and evaluation is performed on the held-out test sets of each benchmark. For agent tasks, results are averaged over 3 random seeds (Table 3). The Pass@k curves (Figures 2, 3, 5) report performance at discrete values of k (typically $k = 2^0$ through $k = 2^7$, i.e., 1 to 128 samples). Statistical significance testing is not reported, which is consistent with the computational cost of RLVR training (training each model variant once is already resource-intensive).

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper's central empirical claim is that ResRL achieves simultaneous gains on both Pass@1-style reliability (Avg@16) and diversity (Pass@128), outperforming strong baselines including GRPO and NSR across multiple domains. The results are organized by domain and model scale.

#### Mathematics: Avg@16 and Pass@k Across Model Scales

**Table 1** reports Avg@16 on six mathematical reasoning benchmarks across three Qwen3 model sizes (1.7B, 4B, 8B). The headline finding is that ResRL achieves the best overall average accuracy at all three scales.

On **Qwen3-1.7B**, ResRL achieves an overall average of **48.6%**, compared to NSR at 47.5%, FlowRL at 42.0%, and GRPO at 35.9%. The improvement is concentrated on the hardest benchmarks: on AIME24/AIME25, ResRL reaches 26.9/21.3 vs. NSR's 27.0/20.4 (approximately equivalent on AIME24, +4.4% on AIME25), and substantially outperforms FlowRL (21.6/15.8). On the more saturated MATH500, ResRL achieves 84.4 vs. NSR's 83.5 and FlowRL's 76.9.

On **Qwen3-4B**, the gains are more dramatic. ResRL achieves an overall average of **57.0%**, improving over NSR (52.1%) by **9.4%** relative, over FlowRL (53.6%) by 6.3%, and over GRPO (53.1%) by 7.3%. The largest improvements are on AIME24 (45.2 vs. NSR's 38.5, a 17.4% relative gain) and AIME25 (38.6 vs. NSR's 33.1, a 16.6% relative gain). On AMC23, ResRL reaches 89.4, substantially above NSR's 79.8. Notably, DAPO (46.2 average) performs worse than GRPO at this scale, suggesting its modifications are less effective for this model size.

On **Qwen3-8B**, ResRL achieves an overall average of **64.7%**, compared to FlowRL at 62.1%, NSR at 61.9%, and GRPO at 56.2%. The pattern shifts slightly: NSR outperforms ResRL on AIME24 (55.4 vs. 50.8), but ResRL leads on AIME25 (41.1 vs. 38.5), MATH500 (92.7 vs. 87.3), and Olympiad (68.1 vs. 60.6). The paper notes that NSR's advantage on AIME24 at this scale "suggesting that diversity-centric penalties may still expand high-variance search on a subset of problems, but ResRL yields the strongest overall profile" (Appendix D.2).

On **Qwen3-32B** (Table 5, Appendix D.1, trained in think mode with 8192 max tokens), ResRL achieves an overall average of **68.3%** vs. NSR's 64.4%, with the largest contributions from AIME24 (60.9 vs. 54.7, +11.3%) and MATH500 (94.5 vs. 88.1). This demonstrates that the method scales to larger models.

**Pass@k curves** are shown in Figures 2 (Qwen3-4B), 3 (Qwen3-1.7B), and 5 (Qwen3-8B). The key pattern across all scales is that ResRL **dominates the low-to-mid sampling regime** ($k \leq 64$) while remaining competitive at high $k$. On Qwen3-4B (Figure 2), ResRL outperforms all baselines across the full range of $k$ on AIME24 and AIME25. Averaged over AIME24, AIME25, and AMC23 at $k=128$, ResRL improves Pass@128 by **7.0%** over NSR. On Qwen3-1.7B (Figure 3), ResRL shows consistent superiority on the challenging AIME datasets across all sampling budgets ($k = 2^0$ to $2^7$), and on AMC23 leads in low-sample regimes before converging with baselines at high $k$ due to task saturation. On Qwen3-8B (Figure 5), ResRL dominates at $k \leq 64$ and remains competitive at $k=128$.

The paper interprets these Pass@k patterns as evidence that ResRL "optimizes the precision–diversity trade-off, securing reliable reasoning without relying on the high-variance 'brute-force' exploration of unconstrained methods" (Appendix D.2). The consistent advantage at low-to-mid $k$ is particularly important for practical deployment where sampling budgets are limited, while remaining competitive at high $k$ shows that the method does not sacrifice diversity.

#### Code Generation

**Table 2** reports results on three code benchmarks for Qwen3-4B. ResRL achieves the best performance on all metrics.

On **LiveCodeBench**, ResRL reaches **43.2 Avg@16** and **59.9 Pass@16**, compared to FlowRL (42.4/58.7), DAPO (41.0/52.3), GRPO (39.5/55.1), and NSR (32.8/52.3). The gap over NSR is particularly large for Avg@16 (43.2 vs. 32.8, a 31.7% relative gain), indicating that NSR's uniform negative upweighting severely degrades Pass@1-style performance on code, while ResRL's selective suppression preserves it.

On **CodeForces**, ResRL achieves an Elo rating of **1469.5** with a percentile of **78.9**, compared to NSR at 1340.9 (69.3 percentile), FlowRL at 1333.7 (68.7 percentile), and GRPO at 1267.9 (63.1 percentile). The 9.6% rating improvement over NSR and the 13.9% percentile increase are the largest margins on this benchmark, which the paper highlights as the "open-ended, distribution-shifted setting" where "suppressing overlap-induced interference improves robustness beyond in-distribution Pass@k gains" (Appendix D.2).

On **HumanEval+**, ResRL reaches **97.0 Pass@16**, marginally above NSR (96.9) and the other baselines (all at 95.7). The near-saturation on this benchmark makes the gains small, but ResRL maintains the best performance.

#### Long-Horizon Agent Tasks

**Table 3** reports results on ALFWorld and WebShop using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. ResRL achieves the highest overall success rates on both benchmarks.

On **ALFWorld**, ResRL achieves an overall success rate of **86.7%**, compared to PPO at 80.4%, EMPG at 78.5%, GRPO at 74.8%, and the prompting baseline (ReAct) at 31.2%. The improvement is distributed across sub-tasks: Look (85.5 vs. EMPG's 75.2), Clean (98.0 vs. PPO's 92.5), and Pick2 (84.2 vs. PPO's 68.8). The 10.4% improvement over EMPG and the 7.8% improvement over PPO are substantial for this benchmark, which requires long-horizon planning with shared action prefixes across successful and failed trajectories.

On **WebShop**, ResRL achieves a success rate of **71.5%** with a task score of **81.2**, compared to EMPG at 69.3% success (81.0 task score), GRPO at 65.6% (77.8), and PPO at 68.7% (81.4). The paper notes that ResRL maintains a competitive task score while improving success rate, indicating "improved completion reliability without sacrificing reward-bearing behaviors that require exploration" (Appendix D.2).

The paper contextualizes these results within the long-horizon setting: "successful and failed trajectories often share substantial prefixes, so naive negative reinforcement can corrupt reusable sub-policies; ResRL mitigates this by protecting shared directions and concentrating suppression on prefix-divergent components" (Appendix D.2).

#### Function Calling

**Table 4** reports results on the BFCL benchmark using Qwen3-8B. ResRL achieves the best overall multi-turn and single-turn accuracy among the open-weight models.

On **Multi-Turn OA** (the primary metric for multi-step tool use), ResRL achieves **41.25%**, compared to ResT-8B at 40.13, ToolACE-2-8B at 37.00, and NSR at 36.37. The 2.8% gain over ResT (the previous best) is notable in this challenging setting. The improvement is concentrated on error-sensitive subsets: Miss Func improves to **47.0** (vs. ResT's 45.0 and NSR's 41.0) and Miss Param to **34.0** (vs. ResT's 32.0 and NSR's 29.0), representing gains of 4.4% and 6.3% over ResT respectively.

On **Single-Turn OA**, ResRL achieves **68.95%**, the highest among open models, compared to ResT at 68.76, ToolACE-2 at 66.65, and NSR at 67.80. For comparison, GPT-5-2025-08-07 achieves 52.65, Grok-4-0709 achieves 64.56, and Qwen3-235B-A22B achieves 67.69, placing ResRL-8B ahead of much larger models.

The paper notes that Long Context remains substantially harder (ResRL at 35.5), "suggesting that the observed improvements arise primarily from mitigating localized decision errors and stabilizing multi-step tool planning, rather than extending context capacity per se" (Appendix D.2). This is consistent with the mechanism: ResRL protects shared action patterns while suppressing specific tool-selection and parameter-assignment errors.

#### Cross-Domain Consistency

The paper emphasizes that the gains are not domain-specific. Across mathematics (Table 1), code (Table 2), agents (Table 3), and function calling (Table 4), ResRL consistently improves over both GRPO (which primarily optimizes Pass@1) and NSR (which primarily optimizes diversity), achieving simultaneous gains on both metric types. The Appendix D.2 analysis provides a unified interpretation: "the largest gains appear in regimes where shared partial structure between positives and negatives is prevalent (AIME and long-horizon trajectories), and improvements concentrate in reliability-centric metrics (Avg@16 and low-k Pass@k), while remaining competitive at high k." This supports the paper's central claim that representation-space decoupling addresses a fundamental pathology (cross-sign gradient interference) rather than a dataset-specific optimization issue.

#### Training Stability Without KL Regularization

**Figure 6** (Appendix D.1) ablates the KL penalty term using Qwen3-8B. Removing the explicit KL penalty (which is standard in GRPO to prevent policy collapse) **improves** AIME2024 accuracy (reaching approximately 0.50 vs. 0.42 at step 200) and AIME2025 accuracy (approximately 0.40 vs. 0.32). The KL divergence rises over training (reaching approximately 0.12 at step 200) but training remains stable—gradient norms are low and entropy remains healthy. This is a striking result: ResRL's projection-based weighting acts as a sufficient intrinsic regularizer, making the explicit KL constraint redundant. The paper interprets this as evidence that "ResRL's projection-based weighting acts as a sufficient intrinsic regularizer, rendering the strict SFT constraint redundant" (Figure 6 caption). This finding is practically significant because KL penalty tuning is a major source of complexity in RLVR pipelines; removing it simplifies training while improving performance.

**Figure 9** (Appendix D.1) extends this to long-horizon training (800 steps on Qwen3-8B without KL penalty). ResRL continues improving on AIME 2024/2025 throughout the extended training, with stable gradient norms, non-degenerate actor entropy, and bounded KL divergence, while mean response length remains flat. The length-scaled reward discount (no change up to 3500 tokens, linearly decreasing to 70% over 3500–4096) serves as a lightweight safeguard against verbosity. The paper concludes that "projection-based weighting stabilizes learning without KL, and length scaling serves as a lightweight safeguard against verbosity" (Section 4.3).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper conducts extensive ablation studies (Section 4.3, Appendix D.1) on the key hyperparameters and design choices of ResRL. Each ablation uses Qwen3-8B trained on mathematics unless otherwise noted.

- **Rank selection (k)**: Sweeping $k \in \{8, 64, 128, 256\}$ (Figure 4), the paper finds that $k=64$ is both the most accurate and the most stable. At $k=8$, the subspace under-covers shared semantics, over-penalizing shared-but-negative tokens and reducing accuracy on AIME2024 and AIME2025. At $k=128$ and $k=256$, residual contrast collapses for many negatives (more tokens receive small $R_{i,t}$ after normalization), leading to oscillatory updates and bursty gradient norms. The paper interprets this as a "protection–discrimination tradeoff: larger $k$ expands the positive subspace $S$ and reduces residual energies $R_{i,t}$, but overly large $S$ can also absorb error-specific directions and weaken discrimination" (Section 4.3). The non-monotonicity validates the theoretical prediction that there exists an optimal subspace dimension balancing coverage of shared structure against discriminability of error-specific components.

- **Hidden layer selection**: Comparing the penultimate hidden layer versus the final hidden layer for representation extraction (Figure 7), the penultimate layer consistently achieves higher accuracy on AIME 2024/2025 (reaching approximately 0.40 vs. 0.35 on AIME2024 at step 150). The penultimate layer also exhibits higher actor KL and entropy, indicating broader but controlled exploration, while the final layer shows lower exploration and lower final accuracy. The paper concludes that "the penultimate layer captures high-level semantic abstractions while mitigating the immediate token-prediction bias inherent to the final layer, thereby preventing premature convergence to suboptimal reasoning paths" (Figure 7 caption).

- **Quantile hyperparameter (q)**: Sweeping $q \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.3\}$ in the quantile-based normalization range (Figure 8), stricter thresholds ($q=0.1$ or $0.2$) converge faster and reach higher accuracy than the more permissive $q=0.3$ (approximately 0.42 vs. 0.35 on AIME2024 at step 200). Lower $q$ also increases actor KL and entropy, indicating broader exploration. Critically, $q=0.1$ maintains low gradient variance (stable gradient norms) despite this exploration, indicating that the projection residual is a "high-fidelity signal for error suppression, thus justifying a stricter gating mechanism" (Figure 8 caption). This is a non-trivial finding: one might expect aggressive gating to amplify noise and destabilize training, but instead it amplifies the signal.

- **SVD subspace budget ($M_{\text{max}}$)**: Sweeping $M_{\text{max}} \in \{2048, 4096, 6144, 8192\}$ (Figure 10) under group rollouts ($G=4$) and max response length 4096, the paper finds that performance is robust for moderate budgets, with diminishing returns beyond 4096. $M_{\text{max}}=4096$ is consistently strong on AIME2024/2025 and yields stable optimization. $M_{\text{max}}=2048$ slightly lags, consistent with noisier subspace estimates. $M_{\text{max}}=8192$ lowers KL but slows accuracy gains, as very large budgets compress residual contrast and push token-wise weights toward their floor $\xi$, weakening error-specific shaping. $M_{\text{max}}=6144$ appears more susceptible to drift without accuracy benefit. The paper uses $M_{\text{max}}=4096$ by default, noting that "owing to local redundancy and low intrinsic dimensionality, the dominant directions of $X^+$ are recoverable from moderate subsamples" (Section 4.3).

- **LayerNorm mechanism**: Removing the LayerNorm and centering preprocessing stage (Figure 11) sharply degrades reasoning accuracy on AIME 2024/2025 (final accuracy drops by roughly half) and destabilizes optimization, with high-variance spikes in actor gradient norms and irregular KL behavior. This confirms that "normalizing the representation geometry is essential for deriving reliable residual signals, thereby preventing erratic policy updates and optimization collapse" (Figure 11 caption).

- **KL penalty**: As discussed in the Main Results (Figure 6), removing the explicit KL penalty term improves AIME2024 accuracy by approximately 9% (reaching approximately 0.50 at step 200 with the KL penalty removed versus approximately 0.42 with it retained) while remaining stable. The KL divergence still rises smoothly over training, indicating controlled exploration without destructive gradient conflicts. This demonstrates that "ResRL's projection-based weighting acts as a sufficient intrinsic regularizer" (Section 4.3).

- **Length-scaled rewards for long-horizon training**: Training ResRL (Qwen3-8B) for 800 steps without KL penalty but with length-scaled reward discounts (Figure 9) shows continuous performance gains on AIME 2024/2025 with stable optimization (non-degenerate actor entropy, bounded and low-variance gradient norms). KL increases smoothly while mean response length remains flat, suggesting the discount curbs length-based reward exploitation. The paper concludes that "projection-based weighting stabilizes learning without KL, and length scaling serves as a lightweight safeguard against verbosity" (Section 4.3).

- **Negative result with ReST$^{EM}$**: The paper mentions (Section 2, but results are not shown in a dedicated figure) that "an attempt to further optimize the revision model using ReST$^{EM}$ backfires: additional sequential revisions substantially hurt performance." This negative result highlights the sensitivity of revision training to the data generation procedure, but the paper does not include this ablation for ResRL itself—it is mentioned in the context of prior work and the broader RLVR landscape.

### Critical Assessment

The experimental evaluation is comprehensive in its domain coverage (12 benchmarks across 4 domains) and model scales (1.7B to 8B, with one 32B experiment). The results consistently show ResRL outperforming baseline methods, with particularly strong evidence on the hardest benchmarks (AIME, CodeForces, ALFWorld) where the shared-structure problem is most pronounced. However, several limitations and open questions emerge from the experimental design.

**The claim of simultaneous Pass@1 and Pass@k improvement is well-supported but the mechanism's attribution is indirect.** The paper demonstrates that ResRL achieves higher Avg@16 (a Pass@1 proxy) and higher Pass@128 than NSR. However, the causal chain—that this improvement is *due to* reduced cross-sign gradient interference specifically—is supported by theoretical derivation (Theorem 1) and the ablation showing the penultimate layer is better than the final layer (consistent with the theory requiring high-level semantic representations), but there is no direct measurement of gradient interference reduction during training. An experiment that tracked the average head-gradient inner product $\langle g^+, g^- \rangle$ over the course of training for ResRL vs. NSR would provide direct evidence for the claimed mechanism. Without this, the empirical results are consistent with the theory but do not uniquely verify it—alternative explanations (e.g., that the reweighting simply acts as a regularization that slows down negative gradient updates for all tokens in a helpful way) are not ruled out.

**The single model family (Qwen) limits claims of generality.** All experiments use Qwen3 or Qwen2.5 variants. The paper does not evaluate on models with different architectures (e.g., LLaMA, Mistral, DeepSeek) or different pretraining distributions. While the consistent results across model scales (1.7B, 4B, 8B, 32B) and domains (math, code, agents, function calling) are encouraging, they do not demonstrate that ResRL's mechanism is architecture-agnostic. The penultimate-layer representation quality, the low-rank structure of the positive subspace, and the quantile normalization all depend on properties of the Qwen model family's hidden states. A model with substantially different representation geometry might require different hyperparameters or might not benefit from projection-based reweighting at all.

**The baselines, while comprehensive, are not all equally tuned.** The paper follows official implementations (veRL; Sheng et al., 2025) with identical hyperparameters for fair comparison. However, methods like DAPO and FlowRL have their own optimal hyperparameter configurations that may differ from the shared settings used here. It is possible that these methods would perform better with method-specific tuning. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by training all models "to convergence under the same budget" (Section 4.1), but convergence under a fixed hyperparameter set does not guarantee optimality for each method individually. This is a common limitation in RLVR comparisons and not unique to this paper, but it means the reported gaps should be interpreted as performance under standardized settings rather than the maximum achievable performance of each baseline.

**The compute-matched comparison is generous to ResRL by including only one training run per method.** The paper does not report multiple training runs with different random seeds for the main mathematics and code results. For agent tasks, results are averaged over 3 random seeds (Table 3), but this is the exception. RLVR training is known to be high-variance, and single-run results can be misleading. Confidence intervals or standard deviations across runs would strengthen the reliability of the reported gaps. The 9.4% Avg@16 improvement on Qwen3-4B (Table 1) is substantial, but without variance estimates, it is difficult to assess whether this difference is statistically reliable or could be partly attributed to training noise.

**The ablation studies use Qwen3-8B on mathematics exclusively.** The rank selection (Figure 4), hidden layer (Figure 7), quantile level (Figure 8), SVD budget (Figure 10), LayerNorm (Figure 11), KL penalty (Figure 6), and length-scaled reward (Figure 9) ablations are all conducted on AIME 2024/2025 with Qwen3-8B. The optimal hyperparameters (rank $k=64$, $M_{\text{max}}=4096$, quantile level $q=0.2$) are transferred to other model scales and domains without re-validation. While the strong results across domains suggest these hyperparameters transfer well, it is possible that different model scales or domains would benefit from different settings (e.g., larger models might have higher intrinsic dimension in their positive subspaces and benefit from larger $k$; code might have different residual energy distributions requiring different quantile thresholds). The paper does not explore this sensitivity.

**The Pass@k evaluation uses a consistent decoding scheme (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95) at all values of k.** This is standard practice, but it means that Pass@k at high $k$ (e.g., $k=128$) is measured with the same relatively low temperature as low $k$. In practice, high-$k$ sampling often uses higher temperatures to increase diversity, and ResRL's advantage in low-$k$ regimes might partly reflect its compatibility with this specific temperature setting rather than an inherent property of the method. An ablation over decoding temperatures would clarify whether ResRL's Pass@k advantage persists under the temperature settings that practitioners would actually use for large-$k$ sampling.

**The agent task evaluation (Table 3) reports baseline results adopted from prior work rather than re-running them under identical conditions.** The paper states that "baseline results are adopted from Wang et al. (2025)" for ALFWorld and WebShop. This means the baselines were potentially run with different infrastructure, different random seeds, or different data preprocessing than ResRL. The paper's efforts to ensure fair comparison for math and code (following official veRL implementations) do not extend to the agent domain, where the comparison is less controlled.

**The BFCL function calling results (Table 4) show ResRL-8B outperforming much larger models (Qwen3-235B-A22B, GPT-5, Grok-4).** While impressive, this comparison is across model families and scales, and the larger models were not trained with the same RLVR pipeline. The claim that ResRL's mechanism is responsible for this cross-scale advantage is plausible but not directly tested—a ResRL-trained larger model would be needed to establish that the method contributes beyond what scaling alone provides.

**The removal of the KL penalty (Figure 6) is a finding rather than an ablation in the strict sense.** The paper presents this as evidence that ResRL's intrinsic regularization is sufficient, but it does not compare against other methods' behavior without KL penalty. NSR without KL penalty might also improve (or might collapse entirely)—the paper does not test this. The claim that ResRL uniquely enables KL-free training is not supported by a controlled comparison; it is supported only by the observation that ResRL trains stably without KL, which is encouraging but not comparative.

**The negative result with ReST$^{EM}$ is not shown.** The paper mentions in Section 2 that "an attempt to further optimize the revision model using ReST$^{EM}$ backfires," but this result is not presented in any figure or table. It is unclear whether this negative result is for a ResRL-trained revision model or for revision models in general, and without data, the severity and nature of the failure cannot be assessed.

**Generalization to tasks without clear structure.** ResRL's mechanism relies on the existence of a positive subspace that captures shared structure between correct and incorrect trajectories. This works well for mathematics, code, agents, and function calling—all domains where correct and incorrect responses follow similar templates and differ in localized ways. It is unclear whether ResRL would benefit tasks where correct and incorrect responses are structurally dissimilar (e.g., open-ended creative writing, where an "incorrect" response might differ from a "correct" one in its entire thematic structure rather than in localized errors). The paper does not evaluate on any such task, limiting the scope of the claimed generality.

**Overall assessment.** The experimental results strongly support the paper's central claim: ResRL achieves simultaneous improvements on Pass@1 and Pass@k metrics across multiple domains and model scales, outperforming existing RLVR and NSR baselines. The ablation studies validate the importance of the key design choices (penultimate layer, rank selection, LayerNorm, quantile normalization) and demonstrate training stability without KL regularization. However, the mechanism's attribution to reduced gradient interference is supported theoretically but not directly measured, the single model family limits architecture-generality claims, and the absence of multi-seed variance estimates for the main results introduces uncertainty about the reliability of the reported gaps. The experiments that would most strengthen the paper—direct measurement of gradient interference reduction, cross-architecture validation, multi-seed statistics—remain as future work.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 1. The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in the Reported Efficiency Gains

The compute-optimal framework's foundational requirement is that prompt difficulty can be estimated before the inference budget is allocated. The paper's method for doing so—generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness or PRM final-answer scores—is extraordinarily expensive. At 2048 samples per question, the difficulty estimation step alone consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). The authors acknowledge this explicitly in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

**Consequence.** The reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former could dominate the latter. The 4× figure should therefore be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** rather than a realized deployment gain. A practitioner deploying this system would find that the difficulty estimation step costs more than the problem-solving step itself, potentially negating or even reversing the efficiency gains.

**Evidence.** The cost acknowledgment appears in Section 3.2, and the 4× figure is reported in Figures 4 and 8, where difficulty is already known at strategy-selection time. The paper never reports total end-to-end cost including difficulty estimation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests pretraining or fine-tuning models to directly predict difficulty from question text, or adaptive difficulty estimation that amortizes cost into the problem-solving process. No such method is developed or evaluated. Until this gap is closed, the compute-optimal framework is a valuable conceptual demonstration but not a deployable system.

### 2. Single Benchmark (MATH) and Single Model Family (PaLM 2) Limit Generality Claims

All experiments reported use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this claim is unverified. Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific.

**Consequence.** The difficulty-dependent patterns that are the paper's central empirical contribution—beam search degrading easy-problem performance due to verifier over-optimization, sequential revisions dominating on easy problems but balanced strategies being optimal on hard ones, the specific difficulty thresholds where strategies should switch—may not generalize. A model with different calibration properties (affecting PRM over-optimization behavior), different in-context learning capabilities (affecting revision model training), or different output distributions would produce different difficulty-dependent scaling curves. Similarly, the MATH benchmark consists exclusively of competition-level mathematics problems requiring symbolic reasoning. It is unknown whether the difficulty-dependent patterns generalize to code generation, logical reasoning, factual QA, or open-ended generation—all domains where "difficulty" may have fundamentally different structure.

**Evidence.** All experimental results (Figures 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9; Tables 1) use MATH with PaLM 2-S*. The paper does not evaluate on any other reasoning benchmark or model family. The test set size is 500 questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each, then further split by two-fold cross-validation, meaning the compute-optimal policy is selected based on ~50 questions per fold per bin—a sample size that may introduce substantial variance in the selected strategies.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges the scope limitation only implicitly and provides no cross-benchmark or cross-model validation. A practitioner applying these methods to a different model family or reasoning domain would need to re-derive the difficulty bins, re-tune the strategy selection, and re-validate the difficulty-dependent scaling curves from scratch—the paper's specific quantitative findings (e.g., beam search with M=4 being optimal on bin 3) are unlikely to transfer directly.

### 3. The 14× Larger Model Baseline is Not Compute-Optimally Trained

The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 scales model parameters while holding training data fixed, following the LLaMA paradigm (Touvron et al., 2023). The authors explicitly acknowledge this departure from compute-optimal pretraining:

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work." (Section 7)

**Consequence.** A Chinchilla-optimal model trained with 14× more total FLOPs (scaling both data and parameters equally, as in Hoffmann et al., 2022) would likely outperform a parameter-only-scaled model. The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining—e.g., +27.8% on easy questions at R ≪ 1 in Figure 1—may shrink or reverse against a properly compute-optimal larger model. The comparison is therefore systematically biased in favor of test-time compute. Additionally, the 14× larger model uses only greedy decoding with no test-time compute augmentation of its own—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no search. Giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (say, best-of-8) would create a much stronger baseline that is never tested.

**Evidence.** The FLOPs accounting is described in Section 7, and the results are shown in Figure 9 and the bar charts in Figure 1. The paper is transparent about the parameter-only scaling choice but does not acknowledge how this biases the comparison. The absence of any test-time compute augmentation for the larger model is not discussed.

**Mitigation status.** The paper notes this as a direction for future work but does not provide Chinchilla-optimal baseline comparisons. The headline finding that test-time compute can outperform a 14× larger model should be understood as conditional on the larger model being sub-optimally trained and not augmented with its own test-time compute. For a practitioner deciding between scaling pretraining and scaling inference, the comparison provides a lower bound on what pretraining can achieve, not a definitive superiority claim.

### 4. Verifier Over-Optimization Is a Hard Ceiling, Not a Solved Problem

The paper documents verifier over-optimization as a central limiting factor: beam search degrades easy-problem performance at high budgets (Figure 3, right), lookahead search—the strongest optimizer—paradoxically performs worst overall (Figure 3, left), and qualitative examples show degenerate outputs (repetitive steps, overly short 1–2 step solutions; Appendix M, Figures 29+, not quoted directly but referenced). The compute-optimal policy *mitigates* this by routing easy problems away from aggressive search, but it does not *solve* the underlying problem. On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is deployed, over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling—the beam search curves in Figure 3 flatten and sometimes decline before the budget is exhausted.

**Consequence.** The compute-optimal framework is fundamentally bounded by verifier quality. As inference budgets increase, the optimal strategy will eventually be to use increasingly conservative search methods to stay below the over-optimization threshold, which means the approach cannot scale to arbitrarily large test-time compute. Improving the PRM (e.g., through better training data, adversarial robustness against search optimization, ensemble methods) would likely shift the difficulty thresholds and change the optimal allocation policy. A practitioner with a weaker verifier than the paper's Monte Carlo rollout-trained PRM would see the over-optimization threshold at even lower budgets, reducing or eliminating the window where search helps. Conversely, a practitioner with a stronger verifier might see search dominate across a broader difficulty range.

**Evidence.** Figure 3 shows the over-optimization pattern: beam search performance on easy problems (bins 1–2) decreases with increasing budget. The degradation of lookahead search is attributed to verifier exploitation in Section 5.2. The paper notes this as a key limitation in Section 8.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed by the compute-optimal policy, which avoids aggressive search on problems where over-optimization is likely (easy bins). Not addressed at a fundamental level—the paper does not propose methods for training more robust verifiers, detecting over-optimization during search, or constraining search to prevent verifier exploitation. The paper explicitly flags verifier improvement as the key bottleneck for future work (Section 8).

### 5. Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved

Across all methods—search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations—the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) show near-zero improvement regardless of compute budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all methods and all budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%.

**Consequence.** Test-time compute amplifies existing capability but cannot create it from nothing. If the base model's pass@1 is near zero on a problem class, no amount of search or revision will help—there are no correct solutions in the proposal distribution to find, verify, or refine. This means the paper's entire framework offers **no path forward for genuinely novel or out-of-distribution reasoning** that exceeds the base model's training distribution. For such problems, pretraining (or fundamentally different post-training methods, such as instruction tuning with human demonstrations) remains the only viable path. A practitioner deploying the compute-optimal framework on a task where the base model rarely or never produces correct answers should expect near-zero benefit regardless of test-time compute investment.

**Evidence.** The near-zero bin 5 performance is visible in Figures 3 (right), 7 (right), and 9. The paper is explicit about this limitation: the Section 7 takeaway box notes that test-time compute is ineffective when problems are outside the base model's capability range.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about this bound and does not claim to solve it. The limitation is presented as a fundamental characteristic of test-time compute scaling rather than a flaw in the method. This does limit the practical scope of the approach: it is only applicable to problems within the base model's latent capability envelope, which may be narrower than a deployment's full problem distribution.

### 6. The Revision Model Has a ~38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate, and Revisions/Search Are Not Combined

**The reversion problem.** Approximately 38% of correct answers produced during a revision chain get "revised" back to incorrect answers in the subsequent step. This is a direct consequence of the training data construction: the model only sees incorrect-to-correct trajectories during training, so it has no signal for what to do when the current answer is already correct—it may "revise" a correct answer into an incorrect one simply because it has never been trained to recognize that no revision is needed.

> "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones using a naive approach" (Section 6.1)

**Consequence.** The sequential revision chain is not monotonic: accuracy does not strictly increase with revision depth. This forces the use of across-chain selection mechanisms (majority voting or verifier-based selection) to pick the best answer from any point in the chain rather than taking the final revision as the output, which adds complexity and depends on selection quality. More fundamentally, it means the revision model is operating in a regime it was never trained for (encountering correct in-context answers) and is actively harmful in a substantial fraction of cases. A practitioner deploying sequential revisions must implement the across-chain selection mechanism or accept that each additional revision step risks corrupting a previously correct answer.

**Evidence.** The 38% figure is reported in Section 6.1. Figure 6 (left) shows that per-step accuracy fluctuates around 23–25% after the initial increase, consistent with correct answers being converted back to incorrect ones at approximately the rate new correct answers are generated.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed by best-of-N weighted selection or majority voting across the entire revision chain (described in Section 6.1), which picks the best answer from any point rather than always taking the last revision. These are patches, not solutions—they add computational overhead and depend on having a reliable verifier or sufficient within-chain diversity for majority voting to work. A more principled solution, such as training the model with examples where the correct answer is already in context and no revision is needed, is not explored.

**The missing combination.** The paper studies PRM tree-search and iterative revisions as independent mechanisms but explicitly notes they were never combined:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions" (Section 8)

**Consequence.** The complementary strengths of the two mechanisms—revisions improve the proposal distribution, PRM search improves candidate selection—are never jointly exploited. Applying beam search to revision model outputs could yield gains beyond either method alone, especially on medium-difficulty problems where both mechanisms show individual strength. The current results therefore represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. A practitioner implementing a combined revisions+search pipeline would be navigating uncharted territory, with no guidance from the paper on whether the combination is synergistic or whether the PRM's reliability degrades when scoring revision-generated outputs.

**Evidence.** The independent study of search and revisions appears in Sections 5 and 6 respectively, with no combined experiments anywhere in the paper. The acknowledgment is in Section 8.

**Mitigation status.** Explicitly flagged as future work. Not addressed in the current paper.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

ResRL introduces a **representation-space axis for gradient modulation** in RLVR training—an intervention paradigm that did not previously exist as a first-class design choice. Before this work, the RLVR community optimized along two primary axes: modifying the objective function (adjusting advantage computation, clipping schedules, KL penalties) and modifying the exploration strategy (MCTS, temperature annealing, entropy bonuses). ResRL demonstrates a third axis: using the model's own hidden-state geometry at the penultimate layer to decide *which parts of the gradient are harmful and which are helpful*, and reweighting accordingly.

This is not an incremental refinement of NSR or GRPO. It is a **conceptual reframing** of the gradient-conflict problem from a penalty-tuning problem to a **semantic decoupling problem**. Where NSR asked "how much should we penalize negative samples?", ResRL asks "which components of negative samples encode shared reasoning and which encode unique errors?" The answer—penalize only the orthogonal complement of negative representations relative to the positive subspace—is operationally simple but theoretically motivated by the head-gradient factorization (Lemma 1) and the alignment bound (Theorem 1). The practical consequence is that the trade-off between Pass@1 improvement and diversity preservation, which prior work treated as inevitable, is shown to be breakable.

The magnitude of this shift is best characterized as a **new diagnostic capability with an associated intervention mechanism**, rather than a paradigm shift. The paper does not replace GRPO or NSR; it provides a gradient-modulation layer that can sit on top of existing RLVR objectives. What makes it more than a modular trick is the theoretical framework: by proving that the head-gradient inner product decomposes into logit-space and representation-space components (Lemma 1) and that the orthogonal-complement energy upper-bounds the representation-space term (Theorem 1), the paper establishes that **hidden-state geometry is a principled signal for gradient interference**, not merely a convenient heuristic. This means future RLVR methods can—and should—consider representation-space information when designing gradient updates, rather than treating the policy gradient as an opaque scalar.

**Reconciliation of prior contradictions.** The paper directly resolves the apparent conflict between the NSR finding (negative upweighting helps diversity; Zhu et al., 2025b) and the GRPO finding (aggressive optimization collapses diversity; Simoni et al., 2025). ResRL's mechanism explains *when* negative upweighting is beneficial and *when* it is harmful: upweighting helps when it targets error-specific components (high residual energy tokens) but harms when it suppresses shared components (low residual energy tokens). NSR's uniform upweighting is a coarse approximation to the selective penalty that ResRL implements. The paper's result that ResRL outperforms NSR on Avg@16 by 9.4% on Qwen3-4B (Table 1) while also improving Pass@128 by 7.0% over NSR (Figure 2) demonstrates that this selectivity matters in practice, not just in theory.

**Research directions that become more attractive.** The paper makes representation-space interventions a credible research direction for RLVR. Specific opportunities include: using hidden states from multiple layers (not just the penultimate layer) to construct richer similarity metrics; replacing the linear subspace with nonlinear manifold learning for better separation of shared and error-specific components; and developing adaptive rank-selection methods that adjust the subspace dimension per prompt based on the intrinsic dimensionality of the positive token distribution. The paper also makes **verifier-free regularization** attractive: the finding that ResRL trains stably without KL penalty (Figure 6, +9% AIME2024 accuracy) suggests that representation-space constraints can substitute for explicit SFT anchoring, which is a significant simplification for RLVR pipelines.

**Research directions that become less attractive.** The paper's evidence that uniform negative upweighting (NSR) underperforms selective upweighting (ResRL) suggests that **further refinement of uniform penalty-tuning methods is unlikely to close the gap to representation-space methods**. Methods that modulate negative gradient magnitudes without distinguishing between shared and error-specific tokens will remain limited by the cross-sign interference that ResRL directly addresses. Similarly, the finding that lookahead search degrades due to verifier over-optimization (discussed in the paper's related work and acknowledged in Section 2, though ResRL operates in a different paradigm) suggests that **more aggressive search algorithms are not a reliable path to scaling test-time reasoning**—the bottleneck is gradient quality, not search sophistication.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Direct measurement of gradient interference reduction during training.** The paper's theoretical framework predicts that ResRL reduces the head-gradient inner products |⟨g⁻, g⁺⟩| between positive and negative token pairs. However, this quantity is never directly measured in the experiments. A follow-up study would instrument the training loop to log the average |⟨g⁻, g⁺⟩| across prompt groups for ResRL versus NSR versus GRPO at regular training intervals, using the efficient computation in Appendix C.1 (which notes that ResRL's overhead avoids token-wise gradient estimation, but direct measurement for analysis purposes is feasible on a subset of steps). The hypothesis is that ResRL shows lower average interference, and that interference reduction correlates with Pass@1 improvement on hard benchmarks. A negative result—finding that interference is not significantly lower under ResRL—would challenge the claimed mechanism and suggest the gains arise from regularization effects rather than gradient decoupling, requiring a re-evaluation of the theoretical framework.

**Cross-architecture validation on non-Qwen model families.** All experiments use Qwen3 or Qwen2.5 variants. The penultimate-layer representation quality, the low-rank structure of the positive subspace, and the optimal rank k=64 were all determined on Qwen models. A follow-up study would replicate the mathematics experiments (AIME24/25, AMC23, MATH500) on LLaMA-3, Mistral, and DeepSeek architectures at comparable scales (e.g., 7B–8B), keeping all ResRL hyperparameters fixed (k=64, M_max=4096, q=0.2). The hypothesis is that ResRL's gains transfer because the mechanism relies on general properties of transformer representations (anisotropy, approximate low-rank structure) rather than Qwen-specific features. A positive result would establish architecture-agnostic generality; a negative result would identify which representation properties (e.g., anisotropy strength, intrinsic dimensionality) are prerequisites for ResRL to work, and would motivate architecture-specific tuning of the rank and layer selection.

**Multi-seed statistical validation with variance estimates.** The main results (Tables 1, 2, 4) report single-training-run performance. RLVR training is known to be high-variance due to the stochasticity of rollout sampling and the sensitivity of policy gradients to initial conditions. A follow-up study would train ResRL, NSR, and GRPO on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B for 5 independent seeds each, reporting mean ± standard deviation of Avg@16 and Pass@128 on AIME24/25 and AMC23. This would answer: (a) whether the 9.4% gain on Qwen3-4B (Table 1) is statistically reliable, (b) whether NSR's occasional advantage on AIME24 at 8B scale (NSR 55.4 vs. ResRL 50.8) is a replicable pattern or a seed artifact, and (c) whether ResRL reduces training variance (lower standard deviation across seeds) in addition to improving mean performance—which the theory would predict if gradient decoupling stabilizes optimization. A finding that ResRL's advantage is within the noise envelope of multi-seed variance would substantially weaken the paper's claims.

**Dynamic rank selection based on prompt-group intrinsic dimensionality.** The paper fixes the SVD rank at k=64 based on a sweep over Qwen3-8B on mathematics (Figure 4). However, different prompts have different numbers of positive tokens, trajectory lengths, and intrinsic dimensionalities of their solution spaces. A follow-up study would implement adaptive rank selection: for each prompt group, compute the singular value spectrum of X⁺, estimate the effective rank using an energy-threshold criterion (e.g., retain enough singular vectors to capture 90% of the variance), and set k dynamically per group. Compare against fixed k=64 on AIME24/25 with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B. The hypothesis is that adaptive rank selection improves performance on prompts with unusually few or unusually many distinct reasoning paths, where a fixed k either over-covers (absorbing errors) or under-covers (penalizing shared structure). A null result would suggest that the fixed rank already captures the relevant variation across MATH problems, implying that prompt-level rank variation is unnecessary for mathematical reasoning.

**Nonlinear manifold extensions for the positive subspace.** The linear subspace S=span(V_k) is the simplest geometric model of the positive token distribution. However, the true positive token manifold may be curved—different correct reasoning paths may lie on different branches of a nonlinear structure where a linear subspace over-generalizes. A follow-up study would replace the linear projector P_S with a kernel-PCA or autoencoder-based projection: train a lightweight autoencoder on the centered positive token representations (within each prompt group, or across groups using a shared encoder), and use the reconstruction error as the residual energy signal. Compare against linear SVD on AIME24/25 with Qwen3-8B at k=64 (or equivalent latent dimension). The hypothesis is that nonlinear projections better separate shared-from error-specific components when the positive distribution is multi-modal (e.g., problems with multiple valid solution methods), leading to higher accuracy. A null or negative result would validate the paper's choice of linear subspaces and suggest that the dominant variation in positive tokens is adequately captured by linear PCA, consistent with the low-rank geometry reported in the literature (Joshi et al., 2025; Inkiriwang et al., 2025).

**Combining ResRL's gradient modulation with PRM-guided search for inference-time verification.** ResRL operates during training to improve the policy's gradient quality. A natural extension is to use the same positive-subspace residual energy during inference: when sampling multiple solutions for best-of-N selection, compute each token's projection residual against a reference set of correct solutions (perhaps accumulated during training or generated from a teacher model) and use the average residual across a trajectory as a confidence signal or as an additional term in the verifier score. This would connect ResRL's training-time mechanism to test-time compute scaling—a natural bridge that the paper only discusses implicitly. An experiment would add the average residual energy as a feature to a PRM trained on Qwen3-8B mathematics outputs and measure whether it improves best-of-N weighted selection accuracy on AIME24/25 over a standard PRM without residual features. A positive result would demonstrate that representation-space signals are useful not only for training but also for verification, broadening ResRL's applicability.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient RLVR training pipelines for domain-specific reasoning models.** Organizations training specialized reasoning models (e.g., for competitive programming, mathematical theorem proving, or legal reasoning) can adopt ResRL as a drop-in replacement for the advantage computation step in their existing GRPO pipelines. The concrete operational benefit is that ResRL removes the need for KL penalty tuning—a major source of hyperparameter complexity in RLVR—while simultaneously improving both Pass@1 and Pass@k. The paper shows (Figure 6) that removing KL with ResRL improves AIME2024 accuracy by ~9% on Qwen3-8B while maintaining training stability. For a training run with a budget of several thousand GPU-hours, this translates to better final model quality at the same cost, or equivalent quality with reduced engineering effort (fewer hyperparameter sweeps over KL coefficients). The SVD computation overhead is O((M + T⁻)dk) per prompt group (Appendix C.1), which is modest compared to the forward/backward passes of the base model.

**On-device deployment of small models with selective negative reinforcement.** The paper shows that ResRL-trained Qwen3-1.7B achieves 48.6 Avg@16 on mathematical reasoning benchmarks (Table 1), outperforming baseline-trained Qwen3-4B on some metrics. For on-device or edge deployments where model size is constrained, ResRL provides a way to maximize the reasoning capability of small models without the diversity collapse that plagues GRPO-trained small models. A 1.7B ResRL-trained model occupies ~3.4 GB in FP16 (vs. ~8 GB for a 4B model), making it feasible for consumer hardware, and the improved Pass@128 performance (Figure 3) means the on-device model can effectively use self-consistency decoding (generating multiple solutions and selecting by majority vote) to improve single-answer reliability—a practical inference strategy on devices with sufficient memory for batch decoding.

**Self-improvement data generation with reduced mode collapse.** When using LLMs to generate training data for themselves (as in STaR, ReST^EM, or rejection sampling fine-tuning), the quality and diversity of generated solutions are both important. The paper's finding that ResRL improves both Avg@16 and Pass@128 (Figure 2, Table 1) means that a ResRL-trained model generates both more correct first attempts and more diverse correct solutions when sampled multiple times, compared to NSR or GRPO. This makes ResRL-trained models better data generators for self-improvement loops: they produce a larger and more diverse corpus of correct solutions from the same number of sampling attempts. Concretely, if a self-improvement pipeline generates 100 candidate solutions per training question using a ResRL-trained Qwen3-4B, the expected number of distinct correct solutions in that batch will be higher than from an NSR-trained model (due to higher Pass@128), while the fraction of batches containing at least one correct solution will also be higher (due to higher Avg@16). This improves both the quantity and the coverage of positive training examples in each self-improvement iteration.

**Training verifier-free RLVR agents for long-horizon interactive tasks.** The agent task results (Table 3, ALFWorld 86.7% success) demonstrate that ResRL trains effective agents without relying on a separately learned value function or complex reward shaping. For organizations building LLM-based agents for customer support automation, robotic task planning, or game playing, ResRL offers a simpler training recipe: use GRPO with ResRL's advantage modulation and no KL penalty, train on environment interaction data with binary success/failure rewards, and the model learns to avoid error-specific actions while preserving shared interaction patterns. The 10.4% improvement over EMPG on ALFWorld and 7.8% over PPO (Table 3) suggest that ResRL's mechanism is particularly well-suited to long-horizon settings where trajectory prefixes are heavily shared across successes and failures—a common characteristic of real-world agent tasks. The absence of a KL penalty simplifies the training objective and eliminates the need to maintain and periodically update a reference policy checkpoint.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions ResRL against GRPO (which optimizes Pass@1 at the cost of diversity) and NSR (which preserves diversity but may limit Pass@1 due to cross-sign interference). The decision rubric follows directly from the empirical findings:

- **Prefer ResRL over GRPO when** you need to maintain Pass@k performance while improving Pass@1, and your task involves structured outputs where correct and incorrect responses share substantial token-level structure (prefixes, syntax, common reasoning steps). Evidence: ResRL outperforms GRPO on both Avg@16 and Pass@128 across all tested model scales (Tables 1, Figures 2–3).

- **Prefer ResRL over NSR when** Pass@1 improvement is a priority and you suspect that uniform negative upweighting is over-penalizing shared semantics. Evidence: ResRL's 9.4% Avg@16 gain over NSR on Qwen3-4B (Table 1), with simultaneous 7.0% Pass@128 improvement (Figure 2).

- **Prefer ResRL over KL-regularized RLVR methods when** hyperparameter complexity is a concern—ResRL trains stably without explicit KL anchoring (Figure 6, Figure 9), removing one of the most sensitive tuning parameters in RLVR pipelines. Evidence: +9% AIME2024 accuracy when removing KL with ResRL (Figure 6), with stable gradients and non-degenerate entropy even at 800 training steps (Figure 9).

- **Consider NSR or FlowRL as alternatives when** the positive subspace estimation is unreliable—for example, when the model rarely produces correct trajectories (very difficult tasks with near-zero pass@1), the positive subspace may be poorly estimated from a small or unrepresentative set of positive tokens, and ResRL's residual-based weighting will degenerate to near-uniform weights (Algorithm 1 line 6 falls back to unmodified GRPO). Evidence: the paper handles the no-positives case with a safety fallback but does not evaluate the quality of the subspace when positives are rare but present.
