## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **Sample-Routed Policy Optimization (SRPO)**, a unified on-policy framework that routes correct rollouts to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for reward-aligned reinforcement and failed rollouts to Self-Distillation Policy Optimization (SDPO) for dense logit-level correction, augmented by an entropy-aware dynamic weighting mechanism that suppresses unreliable high-entropy distillation targets while emphasizing confident ones. Evaluated across five benchmarks—Chemistry, Physics, Biology, Materials, and Tool Use—and two Qwen3 model scales (4B and 8B), SRPO achieves both the rapid early improvement of SDPO and the long-horizon stability of GRPO, raising the five-benchmark average on Qwen3-8B by 3.4% over GRPO and 6.3% over SDPO while simultaneously reducing per-step compute cost by up to 17.2% over long training horizons. The work further diagnoses SDPO's late-stage collapse as arising from two intrinsic flaws—self-distillation on already-correct samples introduces optimization ambiguity by imposing arbitrary logit-level preferences between reward-equivalent reasoning paths, and the self-teacher's signal reliability progressively degrades as token-level entropy rises—establishing that sample-level routing can preserve the early efficiency gains of self-distillation while maintaining reward-driven reinforcement stability only when dense corrective supervision is restricted to failed trajectories where targeted error repair provides genuine benefit.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Coarse vs. Fine-Grained Credit Assignment in LLM Post-Training

The central tension this paper addresses is a fundamental engineering dilemma in how we optimize large language models after pretraining: **reward signals tell us what is good or bad, but they operate at the wrong granularity for efficient learning.**

When training LLMs through reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), we face a credit assignment problem that is both practically significant and theoretically underexplored. Consider a model that generates a 500-token solution to a chemistry problem and arrives at the wrong answer. The scalar reward—a single number indicating failure—provides no information about *which* of those 500 tokens caused the mistake. Was it a misinterpretation of the question stem in the first few tokens? A flawed assumption about solubility constants midway through? A correct reasoning chain that stumbled on the final arithmetic? The reward signal is silent on these distinctions, yet the policy update must somehow allocate credit across the entire trajectory.

This is not merely an intellectual curiosity. **The granularity of credit assignment directly determines sample efficiency**: if every token receives an identical penalty, the gradient signal is diluted across causally irrelevant positions, slowing convergence and wasting computation on tokens that were never responsible for the failure. The practical consequence is that post-training requires more GPU hours to reach a given performance level, and the ceiling on achievable performance may be lower because the model cannot isolate and repair specific reasoning errors.

### Why This Problem Matters Now

The importance of this problem has grown in direct proportion to the scale of LLM deployment and the ambition of post-training pipelines. Three converging trends make efficient credit assignment urgent:

**1. RLVR has become the dominant post-training paradigm.** Since the release of systems like DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) and OpenAI's o1 (Jaech et al., 2024), reinforcement learning with outcome-based verifiable rewards has emerged as the standard recipe for imbuing LLMs with advanced reasoning capabilities. GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) in particular has seen widespread adoption because it eliminates the need for a separately trained critic model—advantages are estimated purely through within-group reward normalization, making it simple and scalable. But this simplicity comes at a cost: GRPO produces a single scalar advantage that is broadcast uniformly across every token in a rollout, so the method inherits all the limitations of coarse credit assignment described above.

**2. Post-training compute budgets are enormous and growing.** Organizations training frontier models now invest millions of GPU hours in post-training RL. Even modest improvements in sample efficiency—say, reaching a target accuracy in 20% fewer training steps—translate to substantial cost savings and reduced carbon footprint. Conversely, inefficiencies in credit assignment mean that a significant fraction of this enormous computational investment is effectively wasted on uninformative gradient updates.

**3. The gap between reward granularity and error granularity widens with task complexity.** As models are applied to increasingly sophisticated reasoning tasks—multi-step scientific problem solving, tool-use orchestration, complex code generation—the disconnect between coarse outcome rewards and fine-grained reasoning errors becomes more acute. A tool-use task might fail because of one incorrect parameter in a function call buried within a 200-token action sequence; a chemistry problem might go wrong because of a single misapplied equation in a chain of 10 reasoning steps. In both cases, penalizing every token equally is not just inefficient—it may actually be harmful, as tokens that contributed to correct intermediate reasoning receive the same negative gradient as the genuinely erroneous ones.

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The field has developed two broad families of solutions to this credit assignment problem, each with characteristic strengths and failure modes that the paper carefully delineates.

#### Approach 1: Sequence-Level Reinforcement (GRPO and Variants)

GRPO and related methods (PPO-based approaches, REINFORCE with group normalization) operate entirely at the sequence level. For a group of $G$ rollouts generated from the same prompt, advantages are computed as:

$$A_i^{\text{GRPO}} = \frac{r_i - \bar{r}}{\sigma_r + \epsilon}$$

This scalar advantage is then applied uniformly to every token in rollout $i$. The method has several genuine strengths: it is computationally cheap (no separate critic), it is robust (group normalization stabilizes training by adapting to reward scale), and for correct rollouts where every intermediate step logically supports the correct outcome, uniform positive reinforcement is entirely appropriate—the model should be encouraged to reproduce all the reasoning steps that led to success.

However, **the method breaks down specifically on failed rollouts**, which are precisely where fine-grained feedback would be most valuable. The paper identifies three specific mechanisms by which uniform credit assignment on failures degrades learning:

- **Dilution across causally irrelevant tokens** (Khandoga et al., 2026): When a rollout fails because of a single localized error, the negative gradient is spread across all tokens, including those that were correct. The signal-to-noise ratio of the policy update degrades with sequence length.
- **Hindered error localization in near-correct trajectories** (Kumar et al., 2026): For rollouts that are "almost correct"—perhaps a code generation that fails only one test case, or a math solution with a sign error in the final step—uniform penalization cannot direct the model's attention to the specific tokens that need correction.
- **Length-dependent bias** (Parthasarathi et al., 2025): The effective per-token gradient magnitude decreases as sequences get longer because a fixed scalar advantage is distributed over more positions. This creates an implicit bias that interacts poorly with the trend toward longer reasoning chains in advanced models.

A complementary line of work attempts to improve credit assignment through **process supervision**: training separate process reward models (PRMs) that score individual reasoning steps (Lightman et al., 2023; Setlur et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2025). These approaches provide denser, step-level signals that can localize errors more precisely. However, they introduce a significant practical burden: PRMs must themselves be trained, typically requiring expensive human annotation of intermediate reasoning steps or sophisticated automatic labeling pipelines. This creates a chicken-and-egg problem where the reward model needed for efficient credit assignment is itself expensive to obtain, partially defeating the efficiency gains it is meant to provide.

#### Approach 2: On-Policy Distillation and Self-Distillation (SDPO)

The alternative family of solutions draws from knowledge distillation, which transfers behavior from a teacher model to a student by matching output probability distributions (Hinton et al., 2015; Kim & Rush, 2016). Recent work has extended this to the on-policy setting, where the student is trained on its own generated trajectories while receiving teacher guidance on those same trajectories (Agarwal et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2023; Lu & Lab, 2025). The key advantage over pure RL is **density of supervision**: instead of a single scalar per rollout, the teacher provides a logit-level target distribution at every token position, giving the model precise information about which tokens should have higher or lower probability.

**Self-distillation removes the need for an external teacher** by conditioning the model itself on privileged information to serve as its own teacher. The lineage traces back to context distillation (Snell et al., 2022), which showed that a model can internalize behavior induced by privileged context into its parameters. SDPO (Hübotter et al., 2026) extends this to RL post-training: for each rollout, the model generates a self-teacher distribution by conditioning on auxiliary information (e.g., a successful sibling rollout from the same group), then trains the student to match these logit-level targets along its original trajectory. The procedure is on-policy because the self-teacher re-scores the student's own tokens rather than generating new trajectories.

The appeal of SDPO is immediate and substantial: it provides **dense, token-level supervision without requiring a separately trained PRM or an external teacher model**. Figure 1(a) in the paper shows that SDPO indeed achieves markedly faster early convergence than GRPO on complex scientific reasoning tasks—the precise, targeted feedback enables the model to rapidly correct specific errors that would take many more GRPO updates to resolve through the diffuse signal of sequence-level penalization.

**However, SDPO harbors a fatal instability that the paper systematically diagnoses.** As Figure 1(a) shows, SDPO's early advantage is not sustained: under prolonged training, it is consistently surpassed by GRPO and often suffers catastrophic collapse, where performance degrades sharply rather than plateauing. The paper identifies two root causes for this phenomenon, both of which trace to fundamental properties of the self-distillation mechanism rather than implementation details:

#### Cause 1: Self-Distillation on Already-Correct Samples Introduces Optimization Ambiguity

This is a subtle but devastating insight. In SDPO's standard formulation, the self-teacher is conditioned on a successful sibling rollout to provide logit-level targets. When applied to a *failed* rollout, this is clearly beneficial: the self-teacher shows the model what a correct solution looks like at each token position, enabling targeted correction of the specific deviations that caused the failure.

But when applied to an *already-correct* rollout, the situation is fundamentally different. The student's trajectory is already correct—it reached the right answer. The self-teacher, conditioned on a *different* correct sibling, provides a *different* set of correct logit targets. Forcing the student to match these targets introduces an **arbitrary preference between reward-equivalent reasoning paths**. Both sequences are correct—they both achieve the outcome reward. But they may use different intermediate reasoning steps, different orderings of arguments, different phrasings. SDPO, by forcing the student to match the teacher's specific distribution, penalizes perfectly valid variations that happened not to appear in the sibling trajectory.

This is not merely inefficient—it is a source of optimization noise that actively works against the reward objective. The policy is simultaneously receiving a positive reward signal (the outcome was correct) and a negative distillation signal (the token distribution doesn't match the sibling's), and these conflicting signals create ambiguity about what the policy should optimize. Figure 1(b) provides direct empirical evidence: restricting SDPO updates to only failed samples retains most of the benefit, while applying SDPO only to correct samples degrades performance and accelerates collapse. The correct samples are precisely where the ambiguity is most harmful.

#### Cause 2: Progressive Degradation of the Self-Teacher's Signal Quality

The second cause is more gradual but equally consequential. As training progresses and the student policy improves, the gap between the self-teacher and student narrows. This narrowing is a fundamental property of self-distillation: the student and teacher share the same parameter initialization and are updated in lockstep (the teacher is typically an exponential moving average of the student). As their distributions converge, the token-level KL divergence that drives the SDPO update shrinks, providing progressively weaker gradient signals.

Simultaneously, the self-teacher's token-level entropy rises during training, as shown in Figure 1(c). This means the teacher becomes increasingly uncertain about its own predictions, producing flatter, less informative probability distributions. The combination—weaker signal magnitude from a more uncertain source—means that in late training, "the distillation signal becomes increasingly dominated by uncertain predictions" that contribute noise rather than genuine corrective information.

The practical consequence is the collapse pattern visible in Figures 1(a) and 3: SDPO improves rapidly while the teacher signal is strong and informative (early training), then plateaus or declines as the signal degrades (late training). By contrast, GRPO suffers from none of these issues because its advantage signal is derived purely from outcome rewards, which do not systematically degrade with policy improvement—a correct answer remains correct regardless of the policy's confidence.

### How SRPO Positions Itself

The paper's central insight is that **GRPO and SDPO have complementary failure modes across the same dimension**: sample correctness. GRPO's coarse credit assignment is problematic specifically on *incorrect* samples (where fine-grained error localization would help), but is perfectly adequate on *correct* samples (where uniform reinforcement aligns with the reward objective). SDPO's dense supervision is beneficial specifically on *incorrect* samples (where targeted correction is needed), but becomes harmful on *correct* samples (where it introduces arbitrary preferences between equivalent reasoning paths) and in late training (where its signal degrades).

This complementarity is non-obvious because the failure modes operate in opposite directions. Prior work treated GRPO and SDPO as competing paradigms—reward-driven vs. distillation-driven—and chose between them. The paper instead proposes a **sample-level routing strategy** that assigns each rollout to the optimization signal best suited to its learning status:

- **Correct rollouts → GRPO**: Reward-aligned reinforcement anchors successful behaviors without introducing the ambiguity of distillation-based logit matching.
- **Incorrect rollouts with available teacher information → SDPO**: Dense logit-level correction provides targeted error repair on the samples where fine-grained feedback has genuine value.

This is not a simple averaging or interpolation of the two methods. The key distinction—which the ablation study in Section 4.3 confirms is critical—is that routing operates at the **sample level** rather than the **advantage level**. A naïve mixing strategy that combines GRPO and SDPO advantages at each token would propagate SDPO's noise into correct samples and degrade GRPO's stability on failed samples. Sample-level routing cleanly separates the two regimes: correct samples never see the potentially harmful distillation signal, and incorrect samples receive the full benefit of dense correction.

The framework further addresses SDPO's signal degradation issue through an **entropy-aware dynamic weighting mechanism** on the SDPO branch. Rather than treating all teacher predictions as equally reliable, the system reweights token-level distillation losses by the inverse of teacher entropy, emphasizing confident (low-entropy) predictions while downweighting uncertain (high-entropy) ones. This provides a natural defense against the late-stage degradation documented in Figure 1(c): as the teacher becomes more uncertain, its high-entropy predictions are automatically suppressed, allowing the framework to continue extracting useful signal from the remaining confident predictions while ignoring noise.

The dynamic weighting also interacts elegantly with the sample-level routing. Because the SDPO branch is only activated for incorrect samples, the entropy-aware weighting only needs to modulate correction signals—it never suppresses signals on correct trajectories (which are handled by GRPO anyway). This separation of concerns—routing for *which* samples get distillation, weighting for *how much* to trust each distillation target—enables SRPO to maintain the early efficiency of SDPO while achieving the long-horizon stability of GRPO, ultimately surpassing the peak performance of both.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

SRPO is a training algorithm that decides, for each generated solution attempt, whether to update the model using a coarse overall reward signal or a dense token-by-token correction signal. It solves the problem that reward signals tell you *that* an answer was wrong but not *where* the mistake occurred, while dense correction signals are excellent at pinpointing errors but become harmful when applied to already-correct solutions—by routing each attempt to the appropriate signal based on whether it succeeded or failed, SRPO captures the benefits of both while avoiding their respective failure modes.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components operating in a loop:

1. **Policy LLM (`$\pi_\theta$`)** — the Qwen3 model being trained, which generates `$G = 8$` complete solution attempts (rollouts) for a given prompt.
2. **Reward Verifier** — an automatic checker that determines whether each rollout is correct or incorrect by comparing the model's output to the ground-truth answer, producing binary correctness flags `$c_i$` and scalar rewards `$r_i$`.
3. **Self-Teacher** — the same model `$\pi_\theta$` conditioned on a successful sibling rollout as privileged context, which re-scores each incorrect rollout's tokens to produce a target probability distribution at every position, indicating what the model *should* have predicted.
4. **Routing and Weighting Module** — a decision mechanism that sends correct rollouts to the GRPO branch (which computes a sequence-level advantage from group-normalized rewards) and incorrect rollouts with available teacher information to the SDPO branch (which computes a token-level KL divergence against the self-teacher's distribution, weighted by the teacher's confidence).

Information flows through a fixed cycle per training step: a batch of prompts is sampled → the policy generates `$G$` rollouts per prompt → the reward verifier scores all rollouts → for incorrect rollouts, the self-teacher is constructed using a correct sibling's text as context → the routing module assigns each rollout to GRPO or SDPO based on correctness → both branches compute their respective losses on their assigned rollouts → the losses are summed and normalized by total token count → the policy parameters are updated via gradient descent.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the correctness flag and teacher-availability flag definitions, because these two binary indicators are the inputs to the routing decision and determine the entire information flow.
- **Second**, the sample-level routing rule itself, since it is the core architectural innovation that distinguishes SRPO from both naïve advantage mixing and pure GRPO or SDPO.
- **Third**, the GRPO branch, covering what it computes, why it is applied to correct rollouts, and what its sequence-level advantage estimator looks like.
- **Fourth**, the self-teacher construction procedure, since the SDPO branch depends on having a teacher distribution to match against and this construction involves specific design choices about sibling selection and context formatting.
- **Fifth**, the SDPO branch loss, covering the KL divergence formulation, the choice of Jensen-Shannon divergence, and how the loss translates into a token-level advantage comparable to GRPO's.
- **Sixth**, the entropy-aware dynamic weighting mechanism, which modulates the SDPO loss at the token level and is the secondary innovation that addresses late-stage signal degradation.
- **Seventh**, the combined training objective and the normalization scheme, which explains how the two branches are aggregated without introducing a mixing hyperparameter and why the effective weighting adapts automatically as training progresses.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

SRPO is a **unified on-policy policy optimization framework** whose core idea is that the optimal form of supervision—coarse and reward-aligned versus fine-grained and teacher-derived—depends on whether a given rollout is correct or incorrect, and that routing each sample to the appropriate signal avoids the interference and instability that arise when one signal type is applied to samples it is poorly suited for.

---

#### Correctness and Teacher-Availability Flags

The routing decision is driven by two binary indicators computed for each rollout `$y_i$`:

- **`$c_i \in \{0, 1\}$`**: the correctness flag, defined as `$c_i = \mathbb{1}[y_i \text{ is correct}]$`—that is, 1 if the rollout achieved the correct final answer (reward `$r_i \geq 0.5$` in the paper's implementation) and 0 otherwise.
- **`$m_i \in \{0, 1\}$`**: the teacher-availability flag, defined as `$m_i = \mathbb{1}[\text{teacher information is available for } y_i]$`—that is, 1 if a correct sibling rollout exists in the same group that can serve as the self-teacher's privileged context, and 0 otherwise.

The correctness flag is determined by the environment's reward verifier, which for the science benchmarks checks whether the model's predicted answer letter matches the ground truth, and for Tool Use checks whether the generated tool call matches the expected structured output. The teacher-availability flag is determined by examining the group of `$G = 8$` rollouts for the same prompt: if at least one rollout other than `$y_i$` itself is correct, then `$m_i = 1$`; otherwise `$m_i = 0$`. Crucially, a correct rollout is excluded from serving as its own teacher—if `$y_i$` is the *only* correct rollout in its group, then `$m_i = 0$` for that rollout despite it being correct.

**Why these two flags separately:** the correctness flag captures the binary outcome (success vs. failure), while the teacher-availability flag captures whether the infrastructure exists to construct a meaningful self-teacher. They are independent: a correct rollout may lack teacher information (if it is the sole correct one), and an incorrect rollout always has teacher information unless the entire group failed, in which case `$m_i = 0$` for all rollouts. The separation enables the routing rule to handle edge cases cleanly—when no teacher is available, the system falls back to GRPO regardless of correctness.

---

#### Sample-Level Routing Rule

The routing mask assigns each rollout to exactly one branch:

$$z_i^{\text{SDPO}} = (1 - c_i)\, m_i$$

$$z_i^{\text{GRPO}} = 1 - z_i^{\text{SDPO}}$$

where `$z_i^{\text{SDPO}} \in \{0, 1\}$` is 1 if rollout `$i$` is routed to the SDPO branch and 0 otherwise, and `$z_i^{\text{GRPO}} \in \{0, 1\}$` is the complement.

**What it computes:** a binary decision per rollout. A rollout goes to SDPO only if it is simultaneously incorrect (`$c_i = 0$`, so `$1 - c_i = 1$`) and has teacher information available (`$m_i = 1$`). All other cases—correct rollouts, incorrect rollouts with no teacher, correct rollouts with no teacher—go to GRPO. The resulting decision matrix is:

| Correct? (`$c_i$`) | Teacher avail.? (`$m_i$`) | Route |
|---|---|---|
| ✓ (1) | ✓ (1) | GRPO |
| ✓ (1) | ✗ (0) | GRPO |
| ✗ (0) | ✓ (1) | SDPO |
| ✗ (0) | ✗ (0) | GRPO (fallback) |

**Why this form:** the expression `$(1 - c_i) m_i$` is a logical AND between "is incorrect" and "has teacher." It cleanly expresses the design principle that SDPO is activated only when dense corrective supervision is both *needed* (the rollout failed) and *feasible* (a correct sibling provides informative context). The complement `$1 - z_i^{\text{SDPO}}$` ensures every rollout is assigned to exactly one branch, with GRPO serving as the default when SDPO cannot operate. This is fundamentally different from an advantage-level mixture `$\lambda A^{\text{GRPO}} + (1-\lambda)A^{\text{SDPO}}$` (explored as an ablation in Section 4.3), because routing prevents the SDPO signal from ever influencing correct rollouts, avoiding the optimization ambiguity that arises from forcing reward-equivalent trajectories to match each other's token distributions.

#### The GRPO Branch: Sequence-Level Reward-Aligned Reinforcement

For rollouts routed to the GRPO branch (`$z_i^{\text{GRPO}} = 1$`), SRPO uses the standard GRPO objective. The branch operates in three steps: advantage computation, importance-sampling ratio computation, and clipped surrogate loss.

**Step 1: Group-relative advantage computation.** For a prompt `$x$` with `$G$` rollouts and scalar rewards `$\{r_i\}_{i=1}^G$`, the advantage of rollout `$i$` is:

$$A_i^{\text{GRPO}} = \frac{r_i - \bar{r}}{\sigma_r + \epsilon}$$

where `$r_i \in \{0, 1\}$` is the binary correctness reward for rollout `$i$`, `$\bar{r} = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^G r_j$` is the group mean reward, `$\sigma_r$` is the standard deviation of rewards within the group, and `$\epsilon$` is a small constant for numerical stability.

**What it computes:** a normalized scalar that is positive for rollouts that performed better than the group average and negative for those that performed worse. If a rollout is correct and most others failed, it receives a large positive advantage; if it failed while most others succeeded, it receives a large negative advantage. The normalization by standard deviation ensures the advantage magnitude is adaptive to the group's reward spread—when all rollouts have similar rewards, advantages are small; when there is clear separation, advantages are proportionally larger.

**Why this form:** group-relative normalization eliminates the need for a separately trained value function (critic) by using the group itself as the baseline. The subtraction of the group mean `$\bar{r}$` serves as a control variate that reduces gradient variance compared to using raw rewards, while the division by `$\sigma_r$` provides adaptive scaling that stabilizes training across prompts with different reward distributions. This is the standard GRPO formulation from Shao et al. (2024), and SRPO inherits it unchanged for the GRPO branch.

**Step 2: Token-level importance sampling ratio.** For each token position `$t$` in rollout `$i$`, the importance sampling ratio is:

$$\rho_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$$

where `$\pi_\theta$` is the current policy and `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` is the policy at the start of the update (the behavior policy that generated the rollouts). This ratio measures how much more (or less) likely the current policy is to produce token `$y_{i,t}$` compared to the policy that originally generated it.

**Step 3: Clipped surrogate loss.** The GRPO loss for rollout `$i$` is:

$$\mathcal{L}_i^{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\left[\min\left(\rho_{i,t}(\theta)\, A_i^{\text{GRPO}},\; \text{clip}(\rho_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon)\, A_i^{\text{GRPO}}\right)\right]$$

where `$\varepsilon$` is the clipping threshold (set to 0.28 for the higher-side asymmetric clip following Yu et al., 2025), and the expectation is taken over valid response tokens (excluding prompt and padding tokens).

**What it computes:** a conservative policy gradient update that multiplies the sequence-level advantage by the importance sampling ratio, but clips the ratio to prevent the policy from changing too drastically in a single update. If `$A_i^{\text{GRPO}} > 0$` (the rollout was better than average), the objective encourages increasing the probability of the rollout's tokens, but only up to a ratio of `$1 + \varepsilon$`—beyond that, further increases are not rewarded. If `$A_i^{\text{GRPO}} < 0$`, the objective encourages decreasing the probability, but only down to `$1 - \varepsilon$`.

**Why this form:** the clipped surrogate is the standard PPO objective (Schulman et al., 2017) adapted to GRPO's group-relative advantages. The key property is that the advantage `$A_i^{\text{GRPO}}$` is **shared identically across all tokens** in the rollout—the `$\min$` and `$\text{clip}$` operations depend on the token-level ratio `$\rho_{i,t}$` but multiply the same scalar `$A_i^{\text{GRPO}}$` regardless of position. This is the source of GRPO's coarse credit assignment: every token receives the same directional signal (increase or decrease probability), differing only in magnitude based on how much the policy has already changed for that specific token. For correct rollouts, this uniformity is appropriate because all tokens contributed to the successful outcome. For incorrect rollouts, the uniform penalty fails to distinguish causal from incidental tokens—which is precisely why SRPO routes incorrect rollouts to the SDPO branch instead.

**SRPO-specific enhancements to GRPO.** The paper uses a "strengthened implementation" of GRPO incorporating three recent improvements from the literature (detailed in Section 4.1): asymmetric clipping with `$\varepsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$` (higher tolerance for probability increases than decreases, following Yu et al., 2025), unbiased advantage normalization (following Liu et al., 2025), and off-policy correction for distributed inference (following Yao et al., 2025). Additionally, a rollout-level importance sampling clip `$\rho \leq 2$` is applied, and the KL divergence coefficient is set to 0.0 (no explicit KL penalty in the loss). These enhancements are inherited from the baseline GRPO configuration and are not specific to SRPO.

#### Self-Teacher Construction

The SDPO branch requires a teacher distribution against which to compare the student's token predictions. In SRPO, the teacher is the model itself—the same parameters `$\pi_\theta$`—conditioned on privileged information that was not available during the original rollout generation. This section describes how that privileged information is obtained and how the teacher is invoked.

**Teacher information source: successful sibling rollouts.** For a given prompt `$x$`, the policy generates `$G = 8$` rollouts. If at least one rollout is correct, its full response text becomes the teacher information `$f_i$` for the incorrect rollouts in the same group. The selection rule is:

1. Identify the set of correct rollouts for prompt `$x$`, excluding rollout `$i$` itself.
2. If this set is non-empty, select one correct sibling rollout and use its complete response text as `$f_i$`.
3. If the set is empty (all rollouts failed, or `$y_i$` is the sole correct rollout), set `$m_i = 0$` and the SDPO branch is not activated for this sample.

The paper does not specify a particular criterion for selecting which correct sibling to use when multiple are available; any correct sibling suffices for constructing the teacher context.

**Teacher prompt formatting.** The teacher information is embedded into a structured prompt that concatenates the original question with the sibling's correct solution:

```
{question}
Correct solution:
{sibling_response}
Correctly solve the original question.
```

where `{question}` is the original prompt text and `{sibling_response}` is the full text of the selected correct sibling rollout. This enriched prompt is then used as the context for the self-teacher forward pass.

**Teacher inference: re-scoring, not re-generating.** A critical operational detail: the self-teacher does **not** generate a new response. Instead, for each token position `$t$` in the student's incorrect rollout `$y_i$`, the self-teacher processes the enriched prompt concatenated with the student's own prefix tokens `$y_{i,<t}$` and produces a probability distribution over the vocabulary:

$$q_{i,t}(v) = \pi_\theta(v \mid x, f_i, y_{i,<t})$$

where `$v \in \mathcal{V}$` ranges over the vocabulary, `$x$` is the original prompt, `$f_i$` is the teacher information (the correct sibling's text), and `$y_{i,<t}$` is the student's prefix up to (but not including) position `$t$`. The distribution `$q_{i,t}(\cdot)$` represents what the model *would have* predicted at position `$t$` had it seen the correct solution in context.

**What this produces:** for every token position in every incorrect rollout routed to SDPO, a full `$|\mathcal{V}|$`-dimensional target probability vector. This is in stark contrast to the GRPO branch, which produces a single scalar per rollout. The density of supervision is the key advantage: at each position, the student can see not just "the overall rollout was wrong" but "at this specific position, given the correct approach, the model should have assigned higher probability to this particular token (or set of tokens)."

**Why condition on a sibling rather than generating the teacher independently:** the sibling conditioning approach keeps the procedure entirely on-policy. The self-teacher's parameters `$\pi_\theta$` are the same as the student's (maintained via exponential moving average with update rate 0.05, so the teacher lags slightly behind the student), and the teacher only re-scores tokens that the student actually generated. There is no need for a separate, potentially stronger teacher model, and there is no distribution shift between teacher-generated and student-generated trajectories. The sibling provides the missing piece—knowledge of a correct reasoning path—without requiring the model to generate novel text in teacher mode.

**Edge case: when all rollouts in a group fail.** If none of the `$G$` rollouts for a prompt are correct, then `$m_i = 0$` for all `$i$`. By the routing rule, all rollouts are assigned to GRPO, even though they are incorrect. This is a deliberate fallback: without a correct sibling, the self-teacher cannot be constructed, so no dense corrective signal is available, and the system relies on GRPO's uniform penalization as the best available alternative. The frequency of this fallback is tracked in Figure 5(c), which shows that teacher-availability remains high throughout training—the fraction of samples with constructable teacher information stays above approximately 85%—so the fallback is activated only for a minority of training steps, primarily early in training before the policy generates any correct rollouts for a given prompt.

#### The SDPO Branch: Token-Level Self-Distillation Loss

For rollouts routed to the SDPO branch (`$z_i^{\text{SDPO}} = 1$`), the system computes a per-token divergence between the student's prediction and the self-teacher's target distribution.

**The base SDPO token loss (without dynamic weighting).** At each token position `$t$` of rollout `$i$`, the loss is a divergence between two probability distributions over the vocabulary:

$$\ell_{i,t}^{\text{SDPO}} = D_{\text{JS}}\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t}) \;\big\|\; \text{stopgrad}\big(q_{i,t}(\cdot)\big)\right)$$

where `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})$` is the student distribution (conditioned only on the original prompt and its own prefix), `$q_{i,t}(\cdot)$` is the self-teacher distribution defined above, `$\text{stopgrad}(\cdot)$` is an operator that prevents gradients from flowing through the teacher, and `$D_{\text{JS}}$` is the Jensen-Shannon divergence.

**The Jensen-Shannon divergence.** The JS divergence between two distributions `$P$` and `$Q$` is:

$$D_{\text{JS}}(P \parallel Q) = \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel M) + \frac{1}{2} D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel M)$$

where `$M = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$` is the mixture distribution, and `$D_{\text{KL}}$` is the standard Kullback-Leibler divergence.

**What it computes:** a symmetric measure of dissimilarity between the student's token prediction and the teacher's. Unlike the forward KL (`$D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P)$`), which penalizes the student most strongly where the teacher is confident, or the reverse KL (`$D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q)$`), which encourages mode-seeking behavior, the JS divergence is symmetric and bounded between 0 (identical distributions) and `$\log 2$` (maximally different distributions). At each position, the loss is a single non-negative scalar; the sum over positions gives the total distillation loss for the rollout.

**Why JS divergence over KL:** the paper follows the SDPO baseline (Hübotter et al., 2026) in using JS divergence. The JS divergence is less aggressive than forward KL in forcing the student to cover all modes of the teacher distribution (which could be noisy), and less prone to mode collapse than reverse KL. Its bounded range `$[0, \log 2]$` also provides natural gradient clipping at the token level, since no single token's loss can exceed this bound regardless of how far apart the distributions are. The paper does not ablate this choice—it inherits it from SDPO—but the entropy-aware weighting described below further modulates each token's contribution, partially mitigating sensitivity to the specific divergence choice.

**Top-K distillation.** The SDPO branch uses **top-K distillation** with `$K = 100$`, meaning the loss is computed only over the 100 highest-probability tokens in the teacher distribution, with all other tokens treated as having zero probability (after renormalization). This reduces the computational cost of the forward pass through the vocabulary head (which for Qwen3-4B/8B would involve tens of thousands of logits) and focuses the distillation signal on the most relevant alternatives.

**Why Top-K:** computing the full `$|\mathcal{V}|$`-dimensional KL or JS divergence at every token position is computationally expensive and includes gradient contributions from thousands of tokens that have negligible probability. Top-K distillation focuses the signal on the most plausible alternatives, which is where the corrective information is concentrated, while reducing per-step compute time. The choice of `$K = 100$` is inherited from SDPO's configuration and was not separately tuned in this work.

**The `stopgrad` operator.** The teacher distribution `$q_{i,t}(\cdot)$` is treated as a constant target during the backward pass—gradients flow only through the student distribution. This is essential because the teacher and student share parameters (the teacher is an EMA of the student); without `stopgrad`, the gradient would flow through both distributions and create a degenerate optimization where the teacher adapts to the student rather than providing a fixed target.

**Relationship to policy gradients.** The paper provides an important theoretical connection: the SDPO gradient admits a policy-gradient form:

$$-\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}^{\text{SDPO}} = \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_t \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(v \mid x, y_{<t}) \cdot A_t^{\text{SDPO}}(v)\right]$$

where the "advantage" `$A_t^{\text{SDPO}}(v)$` is logit-level—one scalar per vocabulary token per position—and is induced by the discrepancy between the teacher and student distributions under the chosen divergence. The exact form of `$A_t^{\text{SDPO}}(v)$` depends on the divergence (for JS, it involves derivatives of both forward and reverse KL terms), but the structural parallel to GRPO's `$A_i^{\text{GRPO}}$` is clear: both methods are advantage-weighted policy gradients, differing only in the granularity of the advantage (sequence-level scalar vs. logit-level vector) and its source (outcome reward vs. teacher-student discrepancy). This formal similarity is what makes sample-level routing feasible within a unified framework—both branches produce gradients in the same form, just with different advantage estimators applied to different subsets of samples.

#### Entropy-Aware Dynamic Weighting

The raw SDPO loss treats every teacher prediction as equally reliable. However, as documented in Figure 1(c), the self-teacher's token-level entropy rises during training, meaning its predictions become increasingly uncertain. To prevent noisy, low-confidence distillation targets from destabilizing late-stage training, SRPO introduces an entropy-aware weighting mechanism that modulates each token's contribution to the SDPO loss.

**Token-level teacher entropy.** For each token position `$t$` in an SDPO-routed rollout `$i$`, the self-teacher's predictive entropy is:

$$H_{i,t} = -\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} q_{i,t}(v) \log q_{i,t}(v)$$

where `$q_{i,t}(v)$` is the teacher's predicted probability for vocabulary token `$v$` at that position, and the sum ranges over the full vocabulary.

**What it computes:** the standard Shannon entropy of the teacher's output distribution at a single token position, measured in nats (or bits, depending on the logarithm base). Low entropy (`$\approx 0$`) means the teacher is confident—it assigns nearly all probability mass to one or a few tokens. High entropy (approaching `$\log |\mathcal{V}|$` for a uniform distribution) means the teacher is uncertain, spreading probability mass thinly across many tokens. A confident teacher provides a clear corrective signal ("at this position, the model should have predicted token X with high probability"); an uncertain teacher provides a diffuse, weak signal that may be dominated by noise.

**Unnormalized weight computation.** The unnormalized per-token weight is:

$$\tilde{w}_{i,t} = \exp(-\beta H_{i,t})$$

where `$\beta > 0$` is a temperature parameter controlling sensitivity to entropy differences, set to `$\beta = 1$` by default.

**What it computes:** a non-negative scalar that decays exponentially with teacher entropy. When `$H_{i,t} = 0$` (perfectly confident teacher), the weight is 1.0 (maximal). As entropy increases, the weight decreases toward 0. The rate of decay is controlled by `$\beta$`: at `$\beta = 1$`, an entropy of 1 nat reduces the weight to `$\exp(-1) \approx 0.37$`; an entropy of 3 nats reduces it to `$\exp(-3) \approx 0.05$`.

**Why exponential decay:** the exponential form has the property that the weight `$\tilde{w}_{i,t}$` is proportional to the teacher's *perplexity* at that position (since perplexity is `$\exp(H_{i,t})$`). This means the weighting is inversely proportional to the effective number of tokens the teacher considers plausible—a natural measure of uncertainty. Linear decay (e.g., `$1 - \beta H_{i,t}$`) would not capture the exponential growth of uncertainty in the vocabulary space and could produce negative weights for high entropies.

**Normalization across the SDPO branch.** The unnormalized weights are normalized over all valid SDPO tokens in the current training batch:

$$w_{i,t} = \frac{\tilde{w}_{i,t}}{\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\text{sdpo}}|} \sum_{(j,s) \in \Omega_{\text{sdpo}}} \tilde{w}_{j,s}}$$

where `$\Omega_{\text{sdpo}}$` is the set of all valid token positions routed to the SDPO branch in the current batch, and `$|\Omega_{\text{sdpo}}|$` is its cardinality.

**What it computes:** a normalized weight whose mean over the SDPO branch is exactly 1.0. This preserves the overall scale of the SDPO loss—tokens with average entropy receive weight approximately 1.0, confident tokens receive weight > 1.0 (enhanced contribution), uncertain tokens receive weight < 1.0 (suppressed contribution).

**Why normalize to mean 1.0:** without normalization, the SDPO loss magnitude would shrink as teacher entropy rises (since all weights would be less than 1.0), artificially reducing the effective learning rate for the SDPO branch over the course of training. Normalization decouples the *relative* emphasis on confident vs. uncertain tokens from the *absolute* scale of the SDPO loss, ensuring that the SDPO branch maintains a consistent effective learning rate while only modulating which tokens within the branch dominate the gradient.

**Weighted SDPO token loss.** The final per-token loss for the SDPO branch is:

$$\ell_{i,t}^{\text{DW-SDPO}} = w_{i,t} \cdot \ell_{i,t}^{\text{SDPO}}$$

where `$\ell_{i,t}^{\text{SDPO}}$` is the base Jensen-Shannon divergence defined earlier.

**What it computes:** the JS divergence loss scaled by the entropy-derived weight. Confident teacher predictions (low entropy, `$w_{i,t} > 1$`) are amplified—the model is strongly encouraged to match these clear corrective targets. Uncertain teacher predictions (high entropy, `$w_{i,t} < 1$`) are dampened—the model largely ignores these noisy targets, since the teacher itself is unsure what the correct token distribution should be.

**Why this form over alternatives:** one alternative would be to simply threshold entropy and exclude tokens above a certain entropy from the SDPO loss entirely. The soft weighting approach is more forgiving—it never completely discards information from uncertain positions, but reduces their influence proportionally to uncertainty. This is important because even a high-entropy teacher distribution contains some information (it tells the model "none of these tokens are clearly correct either"), and hard thresholding would lose this signal entirely. The soft weighting also produces smoother gradients with respect to the entropy, avoiding discontinuities at a hard cutoff.

**Interaction with training dynamics.** Early in training, the self-teacher is relatively confident (low entropy), so most weights are near or above 1.0, and the SDPO branch operates at close to full strength. As training progresses and teacher entropy rises (Figure 1(c)), the weighting automatically downweights the increasingly noisy targets while preserving the contributions from the subset of positions where the teacher remains confident. This provides a natural defense against the signal degradation identified as SDPO's second failure mode, without requiring manual scheduling or early stopping of the SDPO branch.

---

#### Combined Training Objective and Adaptive Normalization

The two branches produce token-level losses that must be aggregated into a single scalar for gradient descent. SRPO uses a weighted sum normalized by the total number of routed tokens:

$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{final}} = \frac{\sum_{i,t} z_i^{\text{GRPO}} \ell_{i,t}^{\text{GRPO}} + \sum_{i,t} z_i^{\text{SDPO}} \ell_{i,t}^{\text{DW-SDPO}}}{\sum_{i,t} z_i^{\text{GRPO}} + \sum_{i,t} z_i^{\text{SDPO}}}$$

where all summations over `$t$` are restricted to valid response tokens (excluding prompt tokens, padding tokens, and any special tokens not part of the model's generated output).

**What it computes:** the average per-token loss across all routed tokens from both branches. The numerator sums the (unweighted) GRPO token losses over GRPO-routed positions and the (entropy-weighted) SDPO token losses over SDPO-routed positions. The denominator counts the total number of token positions across both branches. The result is a single scalar that can be differentiated with respect to `$\theta$`.

**Why this normalization:** the normalization by total token count serves three purposes. First, it avoids introducing an explicit mixing hyperparameter `$\lambda$` that would need to be tuned—the branches contribute in proportion to the number of tokens they cover, which is determined entirely by the data-driven routing decision. Second, it adapts automatically to the changing composition of correct vs. incorrect rollouts during training: early in training when failures are frequent, more tokens flow through the SDPO branch, giving the distillation loss a larger effective weight; as the policy improves and more rollouts become correct, the GRPO branch naturally dominates the denominator, shifting the effective weighting toward reward-aligned reinforcement. Third, it preserves the per-token loss scale—the gradient magnitude does not grow or shrink spuriously as the batch composition changes, because the denominator tracks the total number of contributing tokens.

**What happens to the learning rate.** The paper sets the learning rate for SRPO to `$5 \times 10^{-6}$`, exactly halfway between the GRPO learning rate of `$1 \times 10^{-6}$` and the SDPO learning rate of `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`. This intermediate value reflects the fact that SRPO's effective loss is a hybrid: the GRPO branch (which was stable at `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`) and the SDPO branch (which required `$1 \times 10^{-5}$` for effective learning) are combined, so the learning rate is chosen to balance the two signal types within a single optimizer step. The paper does not perform a separate grid search over SRPO learning rates; the choice of `$5 \times 10^{-6}$` is motivated by the arithmetic midpoint and is not claimed to be optimal.

**Adaptive mixing without scheduling.** A subtle but important property of the normalization scheme: the effective mixing ratio between GRPO and SDPO is not fixed or scheduled—it emerges from the routing statistics, which in turn depend on the policy's current accuracy. This is visualized in Figure 5 (Appendix C): at the start of training, approximately 40% of samples are routed to SDPO and 60% to GRPO (Figure 5a-b); by the end of training on Chemistry (10 hours of wall-clock time), the SDPO fraction has dropped to approximately 15-20% as the policy generates more correct rollouts. The transition is smooth and automatic—no manual intervention, no decay schedule, no hyperparameter governing the mixing ratio. This is a significant practical advantage over methods that require tuning a mixing coefficient or scheduling its decay.

**Gradient flow.** Both branches update the same policy parameters `$\theta$` in the same optimizer step. The GRPO branch computes gradients with respect to `$\log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})$` for GRPO-routed tokens, while the SDPO branch computes gradients with respect to `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y_{i,<t})$` for SDPO-routed tokens. Because the routing masks `$z_i^{\text{GRPO}}$` and `$z_i^{\text{SDPO}}$` are mutually exclusive per rollout, there is no token position that receives gradients from both branches simultaneously—each token is updated by exactly one advantage estimator. This clean separation is what prevents the interference that plagues advantage-level mixing, where a single token would receive conflicting signals from the reward-derived and teacher-derived advantages.

---

#### Full Training Loop (Algorithm 1)

The paper provides a concise algorithmic description (Algorithm 1) that specifies the outer loop:

1. **Sample a prompt** `$x$` from the training dataset `$\mathcal{D}$`.
2. **Generate rollouts** `$\{y_i\}_{i=1}^G$` from the current policy `$\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$` with temperature 1.0, producing `$G = 8$` complete response sequences per prompt.
3. **Evaluate rewards** `$\{r_i\}_{i=1}^G$` by checking each rollout against the environment's verifier (exact answer matching for science benchmarks, structured output comparison for Tool Use).
4. **Construct teacher information** `$\{f_i\}_{i=1}^G$` from successful sibling rollouts: for each incorrect rollout, select a correct sibling's response text as the privileged context; for correct rollouts, `$f_i$` is not used (though it may exist for potential use by siblings).
5. **Route each rollout:** if `$y_i$` is incorrect and teacher information is available, compute the teacher distribution `$q_{i,t}(v) = \pi_\theta(v \mid x, f_i, y_{i,<t})$` and the entropy-weighted SDPO loss; otherwise, compute the GRPO loss.
6. **Aggregate losses** over all valid response tokens using the normalized sum defined above.
7. **Update parameters** `$\theta$` via gradient descent with the AdamW optimizer.

The loop repeats until convergence, measured by validation accuracy plateauing. The paper uses a total training budget of 10 hours of wall-clock time on 8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs as the long-horizon evaluation point, with intermediate snapshots at 1h and 5h.

**Batching details.** The question batch size is 32 (32 prompts per training step), each generating `$G = 8$` rollouts, for a total of 256 rollouts per step. The mini-batch size for gradient accumulation is 32 (matching the question batch size, so no gradient accumulation is used—each step processes one full batch). These hyperparameters are inherited from SDPO's configuration and were selected via grid search in the original SDPO work (Hübotter et al., 2026).

**Validation protocol.** During validation, the policy generates 16 rollouts per prompt (twice the training rollout count) with temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95, and the metric reported is `avg@16`—the average accuracy when selecting the answer via majority voting across the 16 rollouts. This protocol is identical to the SDPO baseline and ensures fair comparison.

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Sample-level routing over advantage-level mixing:** prevents the SDPO signal from contaminating correct rollouts, which is where it introduces optimization ambiguity by imposing arbitrary preferences between reward-equivalent reasoning paths. The ablation in Section 4.3 confirms that advantage mixing degrades by 3.3 points at 10h compared to sample routing.
- **GRPO for correct rollouts, not SDPO:** correct rollouts already achieved the reward; GRPO's uniform positive reinforcement is appropriate and avoids the forced logit-matching that would penalize valid variation. The diagnostic in Figure 1(b) shows that applying SDPO to correct samples actively degrades performance.
- **Entropy-aware dynamic weighting with `$\beta = 1$`:** provides a soft, automatic defense against teacher signal degradation without requiring manual scheduling. The exponential form naturally downweights high-entropy predictions while preserving confident ones, and the mean-1 normalization keeps the effective learning rate stable.
- **Jensen-Shannon divergence over KL:** bounded range `$[0, \log 2]$` prevents individual tokens from dominating the gradient, and symmetry avoids the mode-seeking vs. mode-covering bias of asymmetric KL variants.
- **Top-K distillation with `$K = 100$`:** reduces computational cost and focuses the corrective signal on plausible alternatives, where the teacher's information is concentrated.
- **No mixing hyperparameter:** the routing rule `$z_i^{\text{SDPO}} = (1 - c_i) m_i$` is purely data-driven, and the normalization by total token count lets the effective branch weighting adapt automatically to the policy's accuracy. This eliminates a significant tuning burden compared to `$\lambda$`-weighted mixtures.
- **EMA teacher with update rate 0.05:** provides a slightly delayed copy of the policy for the self-teacher, preventing the teacher from tracking the student too closely (which would collapse the distillation signal) while maintaining on-policy relevance.
- **Learning rate `$5 \times 10^{-6}$` (midpoint of GRPO and SDPO rates):** balances the different gradient scales of the two branches without requiring per-branch learning rate multipliers, keeping the optimizer configuration simple.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Correctness-Conditioned Complementarity Diagnosis — Reconciling the GRPO-SDPO Trade-off

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is not the routing mechanism itself, but the **diagnostic framework** that reveals GRPO and SDPO as complementary rather than competing paradigms, with their strengths and weaknesses cleanly partitioned by a single variable: whether the sample is correct or incorrect. This reframes the entire conversation around credit assignment in RLVR.

Prior to this work, the field treated GRPO and SDPO as alternative post-training strategies. GRPO was understood as stable but sample-inefficient due to coarse credit assignment; SDPO was understood as fast-converging but unstable. The implicit assumption was that these were competing design philosophies—reward-driven versus distillation-driven—and that practitioners must choose one and accept its characteristic failure mode. The paper by Hübotter et al. (2026) that introduced SDPO presented it as a replacement for pure GRPO, not a complement. Concurrent work by Kim et al. (2026) diagnosed SDPO's instability as arising from "suppression of epistemic verbalization," a domain-specific hypothesis about math reasoning.

SRPO's diagnostic contribution cuts deeper and is domain-agnostic. The two failure modes are not generic instabilities but **correctness-conditional**: GRPO's coarse credit assignment is problematic specifically on *incorrect* samples, where fine-grained error localization would help; SDPO's optimization ambiguity is problematic specifically on *correct* samples, where forcing logit-matching between reward-equivalent trajectories introduces arbitrary preferences. This is a non-obvious claim because it requires seeing that the two methods fail in *opposite directions* on the same correctness axis—one under-supervises failures, the other over-supervises successes.

The evidence for this diagnostic is direct and controlled. Figure 1(b) is the key experiment: "restricting SDPO updates to incorrect samples retains most of its overall benefit, whereas applying SDPO only to correct samples degrades performance and destabilizes training." This ablation doesn't just show that routing works—it isolates *why* it works. The result implies that SDPO's instability is not an intrinsic property of self-distillation, but a consequence of applying a correction signal to samples that need no correction. This explains why SDPO collapses in late training (as more rollouts become correct, the fraction of spurious distillation increases) and why GRPO plateaus (as the policy improves, the remaining errors are increasingly localized and resist coarse penalization).

This is a **fundamental conceptual reframing**, not an incremental improvement. It converts a confusing set of contradictory observations—SDPO helps early but hurts late, GRPO is slow but stable—into a single coherent picture with a clear boundary condition (sample correctness). The immediate practical implication (route by correctness) follows naturally from the diagnosis, but the diagnosis itself is the deeper contribution: it gives the field a principled vocabulary for thinking about *when* dense supervision helps versus hurts, rather than treating it as a monotonic good.

### Innovation 2: Sample-Level Routing as a Distinct Paradigm from Advantage-Level Mixing — The Clean Separation Argument

The second innovation is demonstrating that **how** two optimization signals are combined—at the sample level versus the advantage level—is not an implementation detail but a first-order determinant of training stability and final performance. This distinction was absent from prior literature, which implicitly assumed that combining GRPO and SDPO meant interpolating their advantages (as SDPO itself does with its mixing ratio `λ`, and as the ablation's "Advantage Mix" baseline formalizes).

The conceptual move is to treat the routing decision as a **hard assignment** rather than a soft blend. In an advantage-level mixture, every token receives contributions from both the reward-derived advantage and the teacher-derived advantage, scaled by a mixing coefficient. This means correct samples are still partially influenced by the distillation signal (albeit with small weight), and incorrect samples are still partially influenced by the coarse reward signal. The paper's key insight is that this *partial influence is precisely the problem*—even a small component of the "wrong" signal on a given sample introduces optimization noise that accumulates over training.

The evidence is the Advantage Mix ablation in Table 2: "Advantage Mix is slightly better at 1h (+0.7), but falls behind by 2.5 points at 5h and 3.3 points at 10h, with no further gain after 5h." The early advantage of mixing makes sense—when the self-teacher is still high-quality, even a partial distillation signal on correct samples may not be actively harmful, and the combined signal may provide richer gradients. But as training proceeds, the accumulated interference degrades performance below both pure GRPO (which stabilizes) and sample-routed SRPO (which avoids the interference entirely).

This is more than an empirical finding about a specific ablation. It establishes a **design principle**: when combining optimization signals with different reliability profiles across a conditioning variable, hard assignment along that variable is superior to soft interpolation. The principle likely generalizes beyond GRPO and SDPO to other pairs of supervision signals—for instance, combining process rewards with outcome rewards, or mixing on-policy and off-policy data. The key is that if the unreliability of one signal is concentrated on a identifiable subset of samples, excluding those samples from that signal's influence is strictly better than downweighting them.

The contribution is **fundamental** because it changes how researchers should think about multi-signal optimization. The default approach in the field—throw everything into a weighted sum and tune the coefficient—is shown to be strictly suboptimal when a clean partitioning variable exists. This shifts the design question from "what is the optimal mixing weight?" to "what is the optimal routing criterion?", which is a qualitatively different and more structured optimization problem.

### Innovation 3: Entropy as an Automatic Signal-Quality Proxy — Closing the Late-Stage Degradation Loop Without Scheduling

The third innovation addresses a practical problem that arises directly from the self-distillation mechanism: teacher signal quality degrades over training, but the degradation is gradual and variable across tokens and benchmarks, making manual scheduling (e.g., linearly decaying the SDPO weight) brittle. The paper's solution—using the self-teacher's own predictive entropy as a per-token, per-step quality estimate—is conceptually elegant because it **extracts the signal-quality estimate from the same forward pass that produces the distillation targets**, incurring no additional computation beyond the entropy calculation itself.

The intellectual contribution is not the weighting formula (`exp(-βH)`), which is straightforward, but the **closed-loop design**: the mechanism that degrades (teacher uncertainty) provides the measurement used to mitigate its own degradation. This is a form of self-calibration—the teacher doesn't just produce targets; it implicitly reports its confidence in those targets through the shape of its output distribution, and that confidence report directly modulates how strongly the targets influence learning. No external quality estimator, no validation-set monitoring, no manual decay schedule is needed.

Prior work on handling noisy teacher signals in distillation typically relies on external mechanisms: confidence thresholds determined on held-out data, ensemble disagreement as a quality proxy, or learned weighting networks. These require additional infrastructure and don't adapt per-token. The entropy-based approach is **intrinsically per-token**: a position where the teacher is sharply confident about a specific correction receives full weight; a position where the teacher is uncertain (perhaps because the correct sibling used a different but equally valid reasoning step at that point) is automatically de-emphasized. This matters because teacher uncertainty is not uniform across tokens—it clusters at decision points, reasoning transitions, and phrasings where multiple valid continuations exist. A single global weight or schedule would suppress these positions identically to genuinely uninformative ones.

The contribution is **fundamental in principle** (self-calibrating supervision) but **incremental in execution** (the weighting formula is simple and the β parameter is set to a default of 1 without tuning). The key evidence is the ablation in Table 2: "Adding dynamic weighting improves the average result by 0.4 at 1h, 0.7 at 5h, and 1.8 at 10h." The widening gain over time—from near-zero at 1h to nearly 2 points at 10h—confirms that the mechanism specifically addresses late-stage degradation, exactly as designed, without compromising early training where the teacher is already reliable. This temporal specificity is strong evidence that the mechanism targets the claimed failure mode rather than providing a generic improvement.

### Innovation 4: Routing Statistics as an Implicit Curriculum — Adaptive Mixing Without Hyperparameters

The fourth innovation is more subtle but practically significant: the observation that sample-level routing with per-token normalization creates an **implicit curriculum** that automatically shifts the effective optimization regime from distillation-dominated to reward-dominated as the policy improves, without any manual scheduling, decay coefficient, or mixing hyperparameter.

The mechanism is a direct consequence of the routing rule and the normalization scheme described in Section 3.3. Because only incorrect rollouts go to SDPO, and because the denominator normalizes by total token count across both branches, the effective weight of dense distillation naturally decreases as the policy's accuracy increases—fewer rollouts are incorrect, so fewer tokens flow through the SDPO branch. This is not a designed feature with a tunable knob; it is an emergent property of the data-driven routing.

What makes this intellectually distinctive is that it **collapses two design problems into one**: the routing decision (which samples get which signal) simultaneously determines the mixing ratio (how much total weight each signal receives). In a typical multi-signal optimization setup, these would be separate concerns: a routing criterion (e.g., sample correctness) and a mixing weight (e.g., `λ ∈ [0,1]`). SRPO shows that when the routing criterion correlates with the policy's overall competence—as correctness inevitably does—the mixing ratio becomes a derived quantity, not a free parameter.

The evidence is the routing statistics in Figure 5 (Appendix C): the SDPO fraction drops smoothly from approximately 40% to 15-20% over the course of Chemistry training. This curve is not manually specified; it emerges from the policy's improving accuracy. The practical consequence is that SRPO requires **one fewer hyperparameter to tune** than a comparable approach with explicit mixing, while simultaneously achieving better performance. For practitioners, eliminating tuning dimensions is as valuable as improving peak accuracy, because it makes the method more robust to domain shift and reduces the computational cost of hyperparameter search.

This contribution is **incremental** in that it follows naturally from the routing design rather than being a separate technical innovation, but it is **practically significant** because it addresses a genuine pain point in RLVR post-training: the brittleness of scheduled mixing ratios across tasks with different learning dynamics. A fixed schedule that works for Chemistry (where accuracy rises quickly) may be suboptimal for Tool Use (where accuracy improves slowly or even degrades in the SDPO baseline, as Figure 3(c) shows). The implicit curriculum adapts per-task automatically, which likely explains part of SRPO's robustness across all five benchmarks.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** Five benchmarks are used, following the evaluation setup of SDPO (Hübotter et al., 2026). Four are science question-answering tasks drawn from SciKnowEval (Feng et al., 2024)—Chemistry (1,890 train / 210 test), Physics (720 train / 80 test), Biology (450 train / 50 test), and Materials (841 train / 94 test)—targeting undergraduate-level scientific reasoning in multiple-choice format. The fifth is Tool Use, built from ToolAlpaca (Tang et al., 2023) with 4,046 train / 68 test examples, evaluating whether the model can map a user request and tool specification to the correct structured tool call. All splits are identical to those used by SDPO.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments use instruct-tuned Qwen3 models (Yang et al., 2025) at two scales: Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B. The 8B scale serves as the primary analysis model; the 4B scale tests whether findings generalize across model sizes. The Qwen3 family was chosen following the SDPO protocol, with the authors noting that these benchmarks were not explicitly targeted during Qwen3 fine-tuning, making them out-of-distribution downstream tasks.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **avg@16 accuracy (%)**: for each prompt, 16 rollouts are generated (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95), the final answer is selected via majority voting across rollouts, and the fraction of prompts with correct selected answers is reported. For science benchmarks, correctness is determined by exact letter matching against the ground-truth option; for Tool Use, correctness is determined by structured comparison against the expected tool call. Results are reported as the mean across all test prompts in each benchmark, and as a five-benchmark average. Wall-clock training time (1h, 5h, 10h on 8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs) is the budget axis for reporting peak performance.

- **Baselines.** Two baselines are compared: (1) **GRPO**, a strengthened implementation following recent best practices including asymmetric clipping (Yu et al., 2025), unbiased advantage normalization (Liu et al., 2025), and off-policy correction for distributed inference (Yao et al., 2025); (2) **SDPO** (Hübotter et al., 2026), which replaces reward-only supervision with self-distillation from a feedback-conditioned self-teacher using successful sibling rollouts as teacher information. Both baselines use hyperparameter configurations selected via grid search in the original SDPO paper (Hübotter et al., 2026): GRPO uses a mini-batch size of 8 and learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁶; SDPO uses a mini-batch size of 32 and learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁵.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in two complementary ways. **Wall-clock training time** (1h, 5h, 10h) on identical hardware (8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs) is the primary budget axis for comparing peak performance, following SDPO's reporting protocol—the best avg@16 achieved within each time window is reported. **Per-step compute time** (seconds per training step, averaged across five benchmarks and measured over 1h, 5h, and 10h windows) is used to compare computational efficiency independent of convergence speed. The training batch size is 32 prompts × 8 rollouts = 256 rollouts per step across all methods; SRPO uses the same question batch size, mini-batch size (32), and rollout number (8) as SDPO.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is used for strategy selection. Training and evaluation use fixed train/test splits from the SDPO repository. Training curves report 5-step rolling means with ±1 standard deviation shaded bands. The primary results table (Table 1) reports the highest avg@16 achieved within each wall-clock budget, meaning the reported numbers represent peak performance within the window rather than final performance. The paper does not report multiple random seeds or confidence intervals on final accuracy, which is a limitation for assessing statistical reliability at the ~100-example test set sizes used for some benchmarks.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Aggregate Performance Across Benchmarks and Training Budgets

Table 1 reports the highest avg@16 achieved within each wall-clock budget on all five benchmarks. On **Qwen3-8B**, SRPO reaches a 5-benchmark average of **77.4% at 10h**, compared to 74.0 for GRPO (+3.4) and 71.1 for SDPO (+6.3). On **Qwen3-4B**, SRPO reaches **74.2% at 10h**, compared to 69.7 for GRPO (+4.5) and 66.7 for SDPO (+7.5).

**The critical dynamic is the temporal pattern of improvement, not just the final numbers.** SDPO saturates early: its 5h and 10h averages are identical at both model scales (71.1 for 8B, 66.7 for 4B), confirming the collapse pattern described in Section 1. GRPO improves more steadily—from 61.8 at 1h to 72.5 at 5h to 74.0 at 10h for 8B—but the gain from 5h to 10h (+1.5) is substantially smaller than from 1h to 5h (+10.7), suggesting plateauing. SRPO is the only method that maintains meaningful improvement across all three windows: 66.9 → 75.5 → 77.4 on 8B, and 65.8 → 71.2 → 74.2 on 4B. The 5h-to-10h gain for SRPO (+1.9 on 8B, +3.0 on 4B) exceeds GRPO's at both scales, indicating that SRPO's late-stage mechanisms (entropy-aware weighting, increasing GRPO-branch dominance) are genuinely extending the effective training horizon.

Per-benchmark, the improvement over GRPO at 10h on Qwen3-8B is: Chemistry 83.0 vs. 78.9 (+4.1), Physics 78.4 vs. 73.6 (+4.8), Biology 72.8 vs. 70.6 (+2.2), Materials 81.5 vs. 77.8 (+3.7), Tool Use 71.2 vs. 69.0 (+2.2). SRPO's advantage over SDPO is universally larger—always exceeding +2.2 on Tool Use and reaching +14.3 on Biology—because SDPO's collapse is severe on benchmarks where its self-distillation signal degrades most (discussed below under training curves).

**A notable baseline ordering anomaly:** the base instruct Qwen3-4B slightly outperforms Qwen3-8B across all five benchmarks (Table 1, "Base" rows: 50.8 vs. 49.5 average). The authors address this in a footnote, attributing it to "nonmonotonic scaling on out-of-distribution downstream tasks" (citing McKenzie et al., 2023; Lourie et al., 2025). Critically, the larger 8B model achieves higher post-training performance (77.4 vs. 74.2 average) and larger total training gains (+27.9 vs. +23.4 over base), consistent with expected scaling behavior. The anomalous base ordering does not affect the relative ranking of methods within each model scale.

#### Training Curves: Two Patterns of SDPO Failure and SRPO Robustness

Figure 3 presents representative learning curves on Qwen3-8B for Chemistry, Biology, and Tool Use, plotting avg@16 against wall-clock time with 5-step rolling means and ±1 standard deviation bands. These curves reveal two qualitatively distinct failure patterns for SDPO, and SRPO's response to each.

**Pattern 1: SDPO is effective but unstable — Chemistry and Biology.** On Chemistry (Figure 3a), SDPO leads at 1h (71.6 vs. 69.2 for SRPO, 62.1 for GRPO), confirming its rapid early convergence. However, SDPO subsequently collapses: after peaking around 80.6, its performance degrades and settles at approximately 80.6 at 10h (the Table 1 value, representing the peak within the window). The training curve shows substantial variance growth in late SDPO training (widening shaded bands), consistent with the signal degradation diagnosis. SRPO tracks SDPO's early rise closely—within ~2.4 points at 1h—then continues improving through 5h and 10h to reach 83.0, exceeding both baselines. The GRPO curve rises monotonically but more slowly, crossing SDPO around 3-4h and reaching 78.9.

On Biology (Figure 3b), SDPO collapses more catastrophically: after an early peak, performance degrades substantially, stabilizing around 58.5 (its 5h and 10h values are identical). SDPO's variance is extremely high throughout, with bands spanning roughly 10-15 percentage points—far wider than GRPO or SRPO. SRPO achieves the best 1h result (55.8 vs. 52.1 for SDPO) and continues improving to 72.8 at 10h, while SDPO remains stuck at 58.5. GRPO reaches 70.6 at 10h, trailing SRPO by 2.2.

**Pattern 2: SDPO is ineffective throughout — Tool Use.** On Tool Use (Figure 3c), SDPO degrades substantially over time. The curve declines from an early peak and shows no recovery. GRPO improves steadily to 69.0 at 10h. SRPO remains stable throughout, tracking or slightly exceeding GRPO (65.2, 71.2, 71.2 vs. 64.3, 68.5, 69.0 for GRPO at 1h, 5h, 10h). This is the key demonstration of SRPO's robustness: even when self-distillation provides negligible or negative value, the GRPO branch anchors optimization to the reward objective, preventing the collapse that pure SDPO suffers. SRPO never significantly underperforms GRPO on any benchmark at any time horizon, meaning the routing mechanism successfully prevents the SDPO branch from damaging overall performance when its signal is unreliable.

The two patterns validate distinct aspects of the routing design. Pattern 1 shows that when self-distillation is useful (early Chemistry/Biology), SRPO exploits it for rapid early gains without suffering later collapse. Pattern 2 shows that when self-distillation is not useful (Tool Use, late training on all benchmarks), the GRPO branch provides a stable fallback. No manual intervention or per-benchmark tuning is required to switch between these regimes—the data-driven routing handles both automatically.

#### Response Length and Compute Time

**SRPO yields moderate response lengths between the two baselines.** Figure 4(a) tracks response length during training of Qwen3-8B on Chemistry. GRPO produces the longest responses, maintaining consistently high length throughout training. SDPO produces the shortest responses, with length dropping rapidly as training progresses—a phenomenon that Kim et al. (2026) link to the suppression of epistemic verbalization, where the self-distillation pressure inadvertently penalizes the intermediate reasoning tokens that verbalize the model's thought process. SRPO settles between the two, avoiding both the verbosity of GRPO (which inflates inference cost) and the excessive brevity of SDPO (which has been linked to degraded reasoning quality). The paper does not provide per-benchmark response length data, so the generality of this pattern across the five benchmarks is unverified.

**SRPO achieves the lowest per-step compute time over long training horizons.** Figure 4(b) reports the average seconds per training step for Qwen3-8B, averaged over the five benchmarks and measured over three time windows. The numbers tell a story of shifting computational advantage:

- **At 1h:** GRPO is fastest at 71.0 seconds/step. SDPO is slowest at 85.9 seconds/step (+21.0% over GRPO), reflecting the overhead of self-teacher forward passes. SRPO sits between at 83.4 seconds/step (+17.4% over GRPO, -2.9% under SDPO).
- **At 5h:** SRPO becomes fastest at 78.3 seconds/step, compared to GRPO at 82.4 (-4.9%) and SDPO at 83.9 (-6.7%). GRPO's per-step time has increased from 71.0 to 82.4 (+16.1%), which the authors attribute to longer generated responses requiring more forward-pass computation. SDPO's time has decreased slightly (85.9 → 83.9, -2.3%). SRPO's time has decreased more substantially (83.4 → 78.3, -6.1%).
- **At 10h:** SRPO's advantage widens further: 75.8 seconds/step vs. GRPO at 91.5 (-17.2%) and SDPO at 83.7 (-9.4%). GRPO's per-step time has continued to increase (82.4 → 91.5, +11.0%), while SRPO's has continued to decrease (78.3 → 75.8, -3.2%).

The paper's explanation for this shift has two components. First, as training progresses and the policy improves, fewer rollouts are incorrect, so the SDPO branch is activated for fewer samples—the self-teacher overhead diminishes. This is directly visible in the routing statistics (Figure 5, Appendix C): the SDPO fraction drops from ~40% early in training to ~15-20% late in training. Second, SRPO's moderate response length (shorter than GRPO's verbose outputs) reduces the total number of tokens that must be processed in both forward and backward passes per rollout.

The practical consequence is meaningful: SRPO is not just more accurate than GRPO at 10h (77.4 vs. 74.0 average), it reaches that accuracy while spending 17.2% less compute per training step. The combination of higher accuracy and lower per-step cost means SRPO's effective compute-efficiency advantage is larger than the accuracy numbers alone suggest.

The paper does not report a FLOPs-matched comparison controlling for total compute rather than wall-clock time, which is a notable omission. A reader cannot determine from the reported data whether GRPO, given 17% more training steps (to equalize total FLOPs), would close or exceed the 3.4-point accuracy gap. The wall-clock framing implicitly favors methods with lower per-step latency, which is practically relevant for practitioners but leaves open the question of whether the accuracy gains are partly attributable to simply processing more training steps within the same wall-clock budget.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

Table 2 reports ablation results on Qwen3-8B as avg@16 accuracy averaged across five benchmarks, with colored deltas measured relative to the reference row within each block.

**Mixing strategy: sample routing vs. advantage-level mixing.** The first block compares SRPO without dynamic weighting against an Advantage Mix baseline that combines GRPO and SDPO at the advantage level: `A_i,t^Mix(v) = λ A_i,t^GRPO(v) + (1-λ) A_i,t^SDPO(v)` with λ = 0.9, keeping both advantages on a comparable scale (consistent with the mixing ratio used in SDPO's original formulation). Advantage Mix achieves 67.2 at 1h (+0.7 over SRPO w/o dynamic weighting's 66.5), but falls behind at 5h (72.3 vs. 74.8, -2.5) and further at 10h (72.3 vs. 75.6, -3.3). The 5h and 10h values for Advantage Mix are identical (72.3), indicating complete stagnation after 5h—the method plateaus earlier and lower than either pure baseline.

The non-obvious finding is that advantage-level mixing provides a genuine early benefit (+0.7 at 1h) but becomes strictly harmful later. This temporal reversal is consistent with the paper's diagnosis: early in training, the self-teacher signal is high-quality enough that even partial contamination of correct samples is tolerable, and the combined gradient may be richer than pure GRPO. As training proceeds and the teacher degrades, the partial SDPO component on every token—correct and incorrect alike—introduces accumulated interference that prevents further improvement. Sample routing avoids this by completely excluding the SDPO signal from correct samples, at the cost of a smaller 1h advantage. The net effect is a 3.3-point improvement at 10h.

This ablation is the strongest evidence that "how" the two signals are combined (hard assignment vs. soft interpolation) matters at least as much as "whether" they are combined. Both Advantage Mix and SRPO w/o dynamic weighting use the same underlying loss functions (GRPO and SDPO); the only difference is the combination mechanism. The 3.3-point gap at 10h is entirely attributable to the routing decision.

**Dynamic weighting: additive contribution to sample routing.** The second block compares SRPO (with dynamic weighting) against SRPO without dynamic weighting. Adding entropy-aware weighting improves the 5-benchmark average by 0.4 at 1h, 0.7 at 5h, and 1.8 at 10h. The widening gain over time—from near-zero to nearly 2 points—is the key pattern: it confirms that dynamic weighting specifically addresses late-stage signal degradation, when teacher entropy has risen (Figure 1c) and unmodulated distillation targets would introduce increasing noise.

The magnitude of the gain (1.8 at 10h) is smaller than the gain from sample routing itself (3.3 at 10h over Advantage Mix), which is consistent with the paper's framing: "sample routing provides the stronger mixing strategy and the main source of long-horizon robustness, while dynamic weighting adds further late-stage improvement by improving the reliability of the SDPO branch." The two components are complementary—routing prevents contamination of correct samples, weighting suppresses noise within the remaining (incorrect) samples—and their contributions are additive rather than redundant. The paper does not provide an ablation that isolates dynamic weighting without sample routing (e.g., entropy-weighted SDPO applied uniformly in place of standard SDPO), so the standalone value of dynamic weighting outside the routing framework is not measured.

**Diagnostic evidence for the correctness-conditional hypothesis.** Figure 1(b) provides an ablation that is conceptually central even though it is presented in the introduction rather than Section 4.3. By restricting SDPO updates exclusively to incorrect samples or exclusively to correct samples, the experiment directly tests the paper's core claim that SDPO's benefit is concentrated on failures. "Restricting SDPO updates to incorrect samples retains most of its overall benefit"—the curve closely tracks the full SDPO performance in early training and avoids collapse. In contrast, "applying SDPO only to correct samples degrades performance and destabilizes training"—this configuration performs worse than pure GRPO and exhibits instability. This ablation does not appear in Table 2 (which focuses on SRPO variants), but it provides the foundational evidence that motivates the entire routing design.

**Routing statistics over training (Appendix C, Figure 5).** While not an ablation per se, the tracking of sample-routing composition during Chemistry training provides an important robustness check on the implicit curriculum claim. Figure 5(a-b) shows the GRPO-fraction increasing from ~60% to ~80-85% and the SDPO-fraction decreasing correspondingly, with the transition occurring smoothly over the full training duration. Figure 5(c) shows that the fraction of samples with constructable teacher information remains high (>85%) throughout, confirming that the fallback to GRPO due to teacher unavailability is infrequent. The primary driver of the routing shift is the increasing correctness of rollouts, not the absence of teacher information.

**Missing ablations of note.** Several experiments that would strengthen the paper's claims are absent. The entropy-weighting temperature `β` is set to 1 without a sensitivity analysis—there is no sweep over `β ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 5}` to show that the choice is robust or that performance degrades gracefully with suboptimal `β`. The Jensen-Shannon divergence choice is inherited from SDPO—there is no comparison against forward KL or reverse KL within SRPO, so the claim that JS is particularly suitable for the dynamic weighting context is untested. The Top-K distillation parameter `K = 100` is similarly inherited without ablation. The learning rate for SRPO (`5 × 10⁻⁶`) is set to the arithmetic midpoint of the GRPO and SDPO rates, but no sweep over alternative rates for SRPO is reported, leaving open whether a different rate would yield even stronger results.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: SRPO achieves both the rapid early improvement of SDPO and the long-horizon stability of GRPO, surpassing the peak performance of both.**

The evidence substantially supports this claim with one qualification about "rapid early improvement." At 1h on Qwen3-8B, SRPO's 5-benchmark average is 66.9 versus SDPO's 64.8 (+2.1) and GRPO's 61.8 (+5.1). On individual benchmarks, SRPO leads at 1h on Physics (69.5 vs. 67.6 SDPO), Biology (55.8 vs. 52.1), Materials (74.9 vs. 68.1), and Tool Use (65.2 vs. 64.8), but trails SDPO on Chemistry (69.2 vs. 71.6). So SRPO's early improvement is rapid—it substantially exceeds GRPO at 1h on all benchmarks and exceeds SDPO on four of five—but it does not universally match SDPO's 1h performance. The claim of "rapid early improvement of SDPO" is best interpreted as "markedly faster than GRPO, and competitive with or exceeding SDPO," which the data supports. The 5h and 10h comparisons are unambiguous: SRPO exceeds both baselines at both horizons on all five benchmarks for the 8B model (Table 1), by margins that grow with time.

**Claim 2: SRPO raises the five-benchmark average on Qwen3-8B by 3.4% over GRPO and 6.3% over SDPO.**

These specific numbers are from the 10h comparison in Table 1 and are exactly as reported. The 3.4-point gain over GRPO is substantial but should be contextualized: it represents a 4.6% relative improvement (77.4 / 74.0 = 1.046), which on a 500-total-question test set across five benchmarks translates to approximately 17 additional correct answers across all benchmarks combined. The 6.3-point gain over SDPO (9.7% relative) is larger because SDPO collapsed. The practical significance depends on deployment context—for high-stakes scientific QA, a few percentage points may matter considerably; for lower-stakes applications, the compute-efficiency gains may be more relevant than raw accuracy.

**Claim 3: SRPO reduces per-step compute cost by up to 17.2% over long training horizons.**

The 17.2% figure (Figure 4b) is the reduction relative to GRPO at 10h (75.8s vs. 91.5s). This is a wall-clock per-step measure, not a total-compute-to-reach-target-accuracy measure. Three caveats apply. First, the per-step time advantage emerges primarily because GRPO's per-step time *increases* substantially over training (71.0 → 82.4 → 91.5), while SRPO's *decreases* (83.4 → 78.3 → 75.8). If GRPO's verbosity could be controlled through a length penalty or a different KL regularization strategy, its per-step time might not increase, potentially eliminating SRPO's compute-time advantage. The paper does not investigate whether GRPO's increasing response length is an intrinsic property of the method or an artifact of the specific configuration. Second, the 17.2% reduction is at 10h—at 1h, SRPO is 17.4% slower than GRPO, so the net compute savings depend on total training duration. Third, as noted above, the paper does not provide a FLOPs-matched comparison, so we cannot determine whether GRPO given equivalent total compute would close the accuracy gap.

**Claim 4: Self-distillation on already-correct samples introduces optimization ambiguity, and the self-teacher's signal quality progressively degrades.**

The diagnostic evidence for these two failure mechanisms is compelling but incomplete. Figure 1(b) directly tests the first mechanism, showing that applying SDPO only to correct samples degrades performance, establishing the causal role of correctness in the ambiguity problem. Figure 1(c) documents rising teacher entropy, providing correlational evidence for signal degradation. However, the paper does not provide direct causal evidence that rising entropy *causes* the late-stage collapse—for instance, an experiment that artificially controls teacher entropy (by temperature scaling or by substituting a fixed teacher) and measures the effect on stability. The correlation in Figure 1(c) is consistent with the degradation hypothesis, but entropy could be rising as a consequence of some other process (e.g., the policy exploring a wider distribution) rather than as a cause of collapse. The dynamic weighting ablation in Table 2 provides indirect causal evidence—suppressing high-entropy targets improves late-stage performance—but this shows that entropy-weighted SDPO is better than unweighted SDPO, not that entropy *causes* the collapse in the unweighted case.

**What the experiments do not demonstrate.**

The paper operates entirely within a single model family (Qwen3) on a specific set of benchmarks (4 SciKnowEval science tasks + 1 ToolAlpaca tool-use task). There is no evidence about whether the correctness-conditional complementarity between GRPO and SDPO generalizes to math reasoning (e.g., GSM8K, MATH), code generation (e.g., HumanEval, MBPP), or other RLVR domains. The tool-use benchmark, where SDPO degrades most severely, is the only non-science task, and its small test set (68 examples) makes the 2.2-point SRPO-over-GRPO improvement at 10h (71.2 vs. 69.0) statistically fragile.

The teacher information is exclusively successful sibling rollouts. The paper acknowledges that "our experimental setting does not include rich environment feedback (e.g., runtime errors in coding tasks)" (Appendix B.5). This means the results are specific to settings where the only available privileged information is a correct example—a setting where the self-teacher's value is inherently bounded because it can only show the model an alternative correct path, not explain *why* the student's path was wrong. In environments with richer feedback (execution traces, unit test results, compiler errors), the self-teacher signal might be more informative and less prone to degradation, potentially altering the routing calculus.

The paper does not combine SRPO with process reward models or other denser supervision techniques beyond self-distillation. The routing framework is conceptually compatible with any pair of coarse and fine-grained signals, but the experiments only instantiate it with GRPO and SDPO. Whether sample-level routing between, say, outcome rewards and process rewards would yield similar benefits is untested.

The test sets are small: Chemistry 210, Physics 80, Biology 50, Materials 94, Tool Use 68. The five-benchmark average weights these unequally (Chemistry and Materials dominate the total question count), so the aggregate number is sensitive to performance on the larger benchmarks. With test sets of 50-94 questions, a single-digit change in accuracy can represent fewer than 5 questions, making fine-grained comparisons (e.g., the 2.2-point differences on Biology and Tool Use) fragile to sampling noise. The paper does not report bootstrap confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for any comparison.

No multiple-seed results are reported. All training curves show a single run with 1-standard-deviation bands computed over the rolling window, not over independent training runs. The stability of the reported improvements under different random seeds for initialization, data ordering, and rollout sampling is unknown. Given the well-documented sensitivity of RL training to random seeds, this is a notable omission.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Reliance on Successful Sibling Rollouts as the Sole Teacher Information Source

SRPO's SDPO branch is entirely dependent on the existence of at least one correct sibling rollout within each group of `$G = 8$` to construct the self-teacher. The routing rule `$z_i^{\text{SDPO}} = (1 - c_i) m_i$` activates dense correction only when `$m_i = 1$` — that is, when a correct sibling exists. The paper explicitly acknowledges this constraint in Appendix B.5:

> "Since our experimental setting does not include rich environment feedback (e.g., runtime errors in coding tasks), the only available source of teacher information is a correct sibling rollout from the same prompt."

**Consequence.** When all `$G = 8$` rollouts for a prompt are incorrect — which is most likely early in training or on intrinsically hard problems — `$m_i = 0$` for every rollout, and the entire group falls back to GRPO with its coarse, uniform credit assignment. This means the dense corrective signal that SRPO is designed to provide is **unavailable precisely when it would be most valuable**: on the hardest prompts where the policy cannot yet produce any correct solution. The fallback to GRPO is better than nothing, but it is exactly the regime where GRPO's coarse credit assignment is most problematic — localized errors in near-correct trajectories receive no targeted correction.

The dependence on sibling rollouts also creates a **minimum-competence threshold** for SRPO to provide any benefit over pure GRPO: the base policy must be capable enough to produce at least one correct rollout per group with non-negligible probability. Below this threshold, SRPO degenerates to GRPO with additional overhead (the teacher-availability check and the occasional self-teacher forward pass for groups where a correct sibling happens to exist). This threshold likely varies by task difficulty and model scale — the paper's experiments on Qwen3 models at 4B and 8B scales all operate above it, but smaller or weaker models on harder tasks might rarely satisfy the condition.

**Evidence in the paper.** Figure 5(c) in Appendix C tracks the fraction of samples with constructable teacher information during Chemistry training on Qwen3-8B. This fraction remains above approximately 85% throughout training, indicating that the fallback to GRPO-only updates is relatively infrequent for this model-benchmark combination. However, this is a single benchmark on a relatively capable model. For Tool Use — where SDPO degrades substantially and the base policy may struggle more — the teacher-availability fraction is not reported. The paper provides no data on how SRPO behaves when `$m_i = 0$` for a large fraction of training steps, which would be the regime for weaker models or harder tasks.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. Section 5 flags richer feedback as an "important direction for future work," noting that "in environments with richer feedback (execution traces, unit test results, compiler errors), the self-teacher signal might be more informative and less prone to degradation." This suggests that the limitation is specific to the current experimental setting (outcome-only verifiable rewards) rather than inherent to SRPO, but the framework's dependence on *some* form of teacher information — whether sibling rollouts or environment feedback — is structural. When none is available, SRPO provides no advantage over GRPO, and the paper does not quantify how often this occurs across the full benchmark suite.

---

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Incurred at Every Training Step, Not Amortized

SRPO's SDPO branch requires a self-teacher forward pass for every incorrect rollout with available teacher information. This forward pass processes the enriched prompt (original question + correct sibling text + student prefix) to produce the teacher distribution `$q_{i,t}(v)$` at every token position. The computational cost is substantial: at each position, the self-teacher must compute a full forward pass through the model to produce a `$|\mathcal{V}|$`-dimensional distribution, and this must be done for every token in every SDPO-routed rollout.

The paper reports per-step compute time in Figure 4(b), which shows that at 1h of training, SRPO is **17.4% slower** than GRPO (83.4s vs. 71.0s per step). This early-training overhead is attributed directly to the self-teacher computation:

> "Early in training, failed samples are more frequent, so the SDPO branch is activated more often and the additional self-teacher log-probs computation is more visible."

**Consequence.** The headline efficiency gains — SRPO's 17.2% per-step speed advantage over GRPO at 10h — emerge only after substantial training has already occurred. A practitioner training from scratch pays a **17.4% compute penalty in early training** in exchange for a potential speedup later. Whether the net compute is favorable depends on the total training duration and the shape of the speedup curve: if training is stopped early (e.g., at 1-2h because performance saturates), SRPO may be strictly more expensive than GRPO without delivering its full accuracy benefit. The paper's 5h and 10h comparisons capture the regime where SRPO's speed advantage has materialized, but the 1h comparison shows the method is not uniformly more efficient — it is a bet on longer training horizons paying off.

Additionally, the per-step compute time metric in Figure 4(b) measures **wall-clock time**, not FLOPs. Wall-clock time depends on implementation efficiency, GPU utilization, and memory access patterns, which can vary across methods even when total FLOPs are identical. It is possible that SRPO's self-teacher forward passes are less efficiently parallelized than the standard forward passes in GRPO, inflating the wall-clock penalty beyond what FLOP counting would suggest. The paper does not report FLOP counts, making it impossible to distinguish genuine computational overhead from implementation-specific inefficiency.

**Evidence in the paper.** Figure 4(b) provides the complete picture: 83.4s/step at 1h (+17.4% vs. GRPO), 78.3s at 5h (-4.9% vs. GRPO), 75.8s at 10h (-17.2% vs. GRPO). The transition from penalty to advantage occurs sometime between 1h and 5h, but the paper does not show finer-grained timing data to identify the crossover point. Figure 5 (Appendix C) provides the mechanistic explanation — the SDPO fraction drops from ~40% to ~15-20% over training — but the training-step-to-wall-clock mapping is not provided, so a practitioner cannot estimate when the crossover will occur for their specific setup.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not propose any mechanism to reduce the early-training overhead. The adaptive implicit curriculum (SDPO fraction decreasing as accuracy improves) means the overhead naturally diminishes, but it cannot be eliminated in early training when most rollouts are incorrect and the self-teacher is most needed. The paper acknowledges the tradeoff implicitly through the compute-time data but does not frame it as a limitation requiring mitigation. There is no exploration of cheaper alternatives to the full self-teacher forward pass — for instance, distilling only at a subset of token positions, using a smaller cached teacher distribution, or amortizing teacher computation across multiple student updates.

### Absence of Multi-Seed Results and Statistical Rigor on Small Test Sets

The paper reports all results as single training runs with 5-step rolling means and ±1 standard deviation bands computed over the rolling window (i.e., temporal variance within a single run), not over independent training runs with different random seeds. The test sets are small: Chemistry 210, Physics 80, Biology 50, Materials 94, Tool Use 68 examples. With test sets of 50-94 questions, a single-digit change in accuracy can represent fewer than 5 questions, making fine-grained comparisons fragile to sampling noise.

**Consequence.** The paper's headline comparisons — SRPO improves over GRPO by 3.4 points on the 8B 5-benchmark average, by 2.2 on Biology, by 2.2 on Tool Use — are presented without any measure of statistical reliability. A 2.2-point difference on Biology (50 test questions) corresponds to approximately 1 more correct answer out of 50. Such a difference could easily arise from sampling noise in a single training run, especially given the well-documented sensitivity of RL training to random seeds (data ordering, rollout sampling, initialization). Without multi-seed results, the reader cannot determine whether the reported improvements are robust or whether they reflect lucky draws in a single experimental run.

The problem is compounded by the reporting protocol: Table 1 reports "the highest achieved avg@16 accuracy within the corresponding wall-clock budget." This is effectively a **best-over-time** metric, not a final-performance metric. If SRPO's training is noisier than GRPO's, it might reach a higher peak within the window by random fluctuation without genuinely being a better method at convergence. The rolling-mean standard deviation bands in Figure 3 provide some indication of within-run variance, but the peak-selection protocol means the reported numbers are by construction the upper envelope of a noisy process, potentially inflating the apparent advantage of methods with higher variance.

The 5-benchmark average weights benchmarks unequally by test-set size: Chemistry (210 questions) and Materials (94) dominate the average over Biology (50), Physics (80), and Tool Use (68). A method that performs well on Chemistry and Materials but poorly on smaller benchmarks can still achieve a high average. This weighting is not acknowledged or justified in the paper. The per-benchmark breakdown in Table 1 allows readers to assess this themselves, but the aggregate "Average" column implicitly treats questions as equally weighted, which they are not when averaged across benchmarks of unequal size.

**Evidence in the paper.** The absence of multi-seed results and statistical tests is complete — there is no mention of random seeds, no confidence intervals on final accuracy, no significance tests, and no multiple-run averaging. The training curves in Figure 3 show only a single run with rolling-window standard deviation. The 1-standard-deviation bands are computed over the 5-step rolling window (i.e., local temporal variance), which is not a substitute for run-to-run variance. A reader cannot distinguish between "SRPO is genuinely better than GRPO" and "this particular SRPO training run happened to be better than this particular GRPO training run."

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation at all. No mention is made of the small test-set sizes, the statistical fragility of the comparisons, or the need for multi-seed validation. This is a significant methodological weakness that affects the strength of all quantitative claims. The two model scales (4B and 8B) provide a form of replication — SRPO outperforms baselines at both scales — but only one run per scale per benchmark is reported, and the anomalous base-model ordering (4B outperforming 8B on base instruct) undermines the cleanest interpretation of the 4B-to-8B consistency as evidence of robustness.

### Single Model Family and Task Domain — No Evidence of Cross-Architecture or Cross-Domain Generalization

All experiments use Qwen3 models (4B and 8B) on a specific set of benchmarks: four undergraduate-level science question-answering tasks from SciKnowEval and one tool-use task from ToolAlpaca. The paper does not evaluate on math reasoning (GSM8K, MATH), code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), general knowledge QA, or any other domain where RLVR post-training is commonly applied. The model family is fixed to Qwen3; there are no experiments with LLaMA, Mistral, DeepSeek, or any other architecture.

**Consequence.** Several aspects of SRPO's behavior could be model-specific or domain-specific in ways that limit generalizability:

- **The correctness-conditional complementarity between GRPO and SDPO** — the paper's central diagnostic — depends on the relationship between reward and token-level supervision. This relationship could differ for models with different pretraining data, tokenization, or architectural choices (e.g., models with stronger or weaker in-context learning abilities may benefit differently from sibling-conditioned self-distillation).

- **The entropy-based dynamic weighting mechanism** depends on the self-teacher's calibration properties. Qwen3 models may have particular uncertainty calibration characteristics that make entropy an effective quality proxy; other model families might exhibit different entropy dynamics during self-distillation, potentially requiring different weighting strategies or rendering entropy uninformative for quality estimation.

- **The Tool Use benchmark** — where SDPO degrades most severely — is the only non-multiple-choice task in the suite. Its small test set (68 examples) and structured-output format make it a weak basis for generalizing about SRPO's behavior on open-ended generation tasks. The paper's observation that "when self-distillation is ineffective, SRPO remains stable" is supported by exactly one benchmark (Tool Use) with one model family, making it suggestive rather than conclusive.

**Evidence in the paper.** The paper does not claim broader generalization; it frames the experiments as an evaluation of SRPO within the SDPO protocol. Section 4.1 states: "We follow the evaluation setup of SDPO and consider five benchmarks." The use of two model scales (4B and 8B) provides within-family consistency but does not address cross-architecture generalization. The paper does not discuss whether Qwen3 has properties (e.g., strong instruction-following from its instruct tuning) that make it particularly amenable or resistant to self-distillation.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not acknowledge the single-model-family, single-task-domain scope as a limitation, nor does it discuss what properties of the chosen models and benchmarks might affect generalizability. The "Future Work" section mentions extending to "environments with richer feedback" but not to other model families or task domains. A practitioner using a non-Qwen model on a non-science task has no evidence from this paper about whether SRPO would help, hurt, or behave differently.

### No Combination of SRPO with Process Reward Models or Other Dense Supervision Methods

SRPO's routing framework is conceptually general: it routes samples between a coarse supervision signal (GRPO's sequence-level advantage) and a fine-grained supervision signal (SDPO's token-level self-distillation). The paper acknowledges in its related work (Appendix A) that process reward models (PRMs) and process supervision represent an alternative approach to denser credit assignment, citing Lightman et al. (2023), Setlur et al. (2025), and Cui et al. (2025). However, SRPO is never combined with these methods — the SDPO branch always uses self-distillation, never a separately trained PRM.

**Consequence.** The paper positions SRPO as a general framework for combining coarse and fine-grained signals, but the experiments only instantiate one specific pair: GRPO + SDPO. This leaves several important questions unanswered:

- **Would SRPO benefit from replacing the self-teacher with a trained PRM?** A PRM might provide more reliable token-level or step-level scores than self-distillation, potentially avoiding the signal degradation problem that motivates entropy-aware weighting. Conversely, training a PRM requires additional data and compute, which might offset SRPO's efficiency gains.

- **Is the routing principle — hard assignment by correctness — specific to the GRPO-SDPO pair, or does it generalize?** The paper's argument for why routing works depends on SDPO's particular property of introducing optimization ambiguity on correct samples. A PRM-based dense signal might not share this property — a well-calibrated PRM could potentially provide useful step-level feedback on correct samples without imposing arbitrary preferences between reasoning paths, in which case routing might be unnecessary or even harmful.

- **Could SRPO combine three signals** — outcome rewards, self-distillation, and process rewards — with more sophisticated routing criteria beyond binary correctness? The paper's binary routing rule (`$z_i^{\text{SDPO}} = (1 - c_i) m_i$`) is simple, but a richer routing criterion (e.g., based on PRM confidence, trajectory length, or error localization) might yield further gains.

**Evidence in the paper.** The paper explicitly limits its scope in Section 4.1: "the SDPO branch requires teacher information `$f_i$` for each rollout `$y_i$` to construct the feedback-conditioned self-teacher distribution... Since our experimental setting does not include rich environment feedback (e.g., runtime errors in coding tasks), the only available source of teacher information is a correct sibling rollout." The related work discussion (Appendix A.1) acknowledges PRMs as a complementary approach but does not treat their absence from the experiments as a limitation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to combine SRPO with PRMs or process supervision, nor does it discuss this as a limitation requiring future work. Section 5 suggests extending to "environments with richer feedback" but frames this as making the SDPO branch more effective, not as replacing self-distillation with alternative dense signals. A practitioner interested in using PRMs for credit assignment receives no guidance from this paper on whether SRPO's routing framework would be compatible with or superior to PRM-only approaches.

### No FLOPs-Matched Comparison — Wall-Clock Efficiency Gains May Not Reflect True Compute Efficiency

The paper's primary efficiency claim — that SRPO reduces per-step compute time by up to 17.2% over GRPO at 10h — is measured in wall-clock seconds per training step on identical hardware (8 NVIDIA H20 GPUs). Wall-clock time conflates two factors: the total FLOPs required per step (determined by forward and backward pass computation) and the efficiency with which those FLOPs are executed (determined by GPU utilization, memory bandwidth, communication overhead, and implementation quality). A method can have lower wall-clock time despite higher FLOPs if it achieves better hardware utilization, or vice versa.

**Consequence.** The 17.2% wall-clock speedup at 10h could arise from several sources that have different implications for the method's true computational efficiency:

- **Reduced FLOPs due to shorter responses.** Figure 4(a) shows SRPO produces shorter responses than GRPO, which directly reduces the number of tokens processed in forward and backward passes. This is a genuine reduction in required computation.

- **Reduced FLOPs due to fewer self-teacher passes.** As the SDPO fraction decreases (Figure 5), fewer self-teacher forward passes are needed. This is also a genuine reduction.

- **Implementation efficiency differences.** The SDPO paper's original implementation used vLLM for rollout generation; SRPO uses SGLang (Appendix B.1). While the paper argues these engines "implement the same sampling algorithms... preserving a fair comparison with SDPO," the comparison between SRPO and GRPO involves different code paths (GRPO has no self-teacher logic) that may have different GPU utilization profiles. If GRPO's implementation is less optimized than SRPO's, the wall-clock advantage would overstate the true computational advantage.

Without FLOP counts, the reader cannot determine whether GRPO, given an equivalent FLOP budget (i.e., more training steps to compensate for its higher per-step wall-clock time), would match or exceed SRPO's accuracy. SRPO's per-step speed advantage at 10h (75.8s vs. 91.5s) means SRPO processes approximately 20.7% more steps than GRPO in the same wall-clock interval. If each GRPO step provides more learning progress per FLOP (e.g., because the gradient signal is cleaner without the noise of potentially degraded self-distillation targets), the FLOP-matched comparison might favor GRPO.

**Evidence in the paper.** The paper reports only wall-clock time (Figures 3, 4; Table 1) and per-step wall-clock seconds (Figure 4b). There are no FLOP measurements, no token-count-per-step data beyond the response-length curve in Figure 4(a) (which applies only to Chemistry), and no FLOPs-matched accuracy comparison. The paper's reporting protocol — "the highest achieved avg@16 within the corresponding wall-clock budget" (Table 1) — implicitly favors methods with lower per-step latency because they can complete more training steps within the budget window.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the distinction between wall-clock time and FLOPs, does not report FLOP counts, and does not provide a FLOPs-matched comparison. This is a significant omission for a paper whose central practical claims include computational efficiency. The adaptive implicit curriculum (SDPO fraction decreasing over time) means SRPO's per-step cost varies during training, making the wall-clock-to-FLOPs mapping non-trivial to reconstruct from the reported data.
