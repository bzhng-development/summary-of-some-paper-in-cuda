Here are the takeaways from this Bobby Holley interview (went from intern to CTO of Mozilla Firefox, living through the browser wars and co-evolving with Rust):

**Starting at Mozilla as an intern**

Bobby Holley arrived at Mozilla in June 2008 after a structured internship at Nvidia. The contrast was immediate. His manager, Damon Sicore—the director of platform who ran all of Gecko—embodied “the dude from the Big Lebowski in all of the best ways.” When Holley asked what to work on, the response was essentially: “I don’t know, go get on IRC and get on Bugzilla and start figuring out some things to do.” This wasn’t negligence; it was the culture. Mozilla had been an open-source project that happened largely by accident, and it selected for a particular type of person: someone who “really just needed the bare minimum and was very motivated to just go in and figure things out.” The Firefox codebase was a wild west of roughly 10 million lines of code inherited from Netscape, largely unowned. The major challenge was just dealing with all of it.

The person who embodied this culture most was Boris Zbarsky, one of Mozilla’s first distinguished engineers and Holley’s longtime hero. Boris “probably owned like half of” the codebase. Watching him simultaneously field complex technical questions across five different IRC channels—talking to senior engineers, interns, and users reporting bugs all at once—was awe-inspiring. “He was just a machine.” Holley quickly internalized the model: show up, fix something, and suddenly you own it. By the end of his first internship, he owned “quite a lot of code in the Firefox browser,” which felt like an enormous responsibility.

**The browser wars and the shifting Google relationship**

Holley arrived at a pivotal moment. The original 1990s browser wars ended when Microsoft bundled Internet Explorer for free, won the market, and—after an antitrust judgment they effectively ignored—put IE into maintenance mode. That vacuum was filled by open-source contributors who downloaded the Netscape source code, improved it, added popup blocking and tabs, and eventually stripped it down to create a standalone browser called Firefox. “Firefox just took off like wildfire because the world really wanted a better browser and it was a better browser.” Google was deeply aligned with Firefox’s success: Mozilla interns got Google campus badges, snacks appeared in the kitchen, and a search-engine deal made Mozilla financially sustainable. “There was really this sense that Google and Mozilla together were working to unlock the future of the open web.”

That peak sentiment in mid-2008 collapsed almost immediately when Google announced Chrome. A New York Times article captured the shift: “Between Mozilla and Google, Group Hugs are Getting Tricky.” What followed was what former Firefox VP Jonathan Nightingale later called “the year of a hundred Oopses”—a pattern where Google would hurt Firefox’s distribution, always claiming it was unintentional. Firefox users typing into the address bar would land on Google and see a banner saying “Download Chrome. It’s better.” Holley doesn’t believe anyone at Google was twirling a mustache; it was organizational incentives. Individual teams got their metrics wins, fixed the “mistake” a couple of weeks later, and the damage was done. Simultaneously, Firefox had legitimate architectural gaps: Chrome launched with a multi-process architecture, a better plugin model for Adobe Flash, and a fast JIT-compiled JavaScript engine—and the web was moving to YouTube, where Firefox crashed constantly due to Flash bugs Mozilla couldn’t fix because they were Adobe’s fault.

**Why people worked for free**

Before Google’s money, Mozilla’s contributors were unpaid. Holley explains the incentive structure through Boris Zbarsky’s story: as an MIT student, his choices were to VNC into a Solaris machine running Internet Explorer 5 or use a crashing Netscape build. “He got involved just because he wanted to fix something for himself.” The intellectual challenge of the vast, complicated codebase—harder than a math degree, Holley suggests—was a draw. But the deepest motivator was the Mozilla mission: the idea that “the internet is a global public resource that is open and accessible to all” was not a given in the 1990s when Microsoft was eating the world. Mozilla wasn’t just an advocacy organization arguing for change; it was “actually going out and doing something and succeeding and creating a browser that gave users choice.”

**Security and the “Slaughterhouse” project**

Holley was drawn to the hardest, gnarliest parts of the code. Early on, that was XPConnect—the binding layer between JavaScript and the DOM that was tangled up in deep security considerations. Because Firefox’s own frontend was written in JavaScript and markup, websites had to be properly isolated from touching the browser application itself. A community of external security researchers—including one pseudonymous researcher called “MozBugRa4” rumored to live in a country where security research was illegal—engaged in a weekly cat-and-mouse game, finding clever ways to slither up prototype chains and compromise the browser. “The classic thing that security researchers would do to prove that they’d pwned your machine was to launch the calculator.”

Holley realized there were categories of exploits they couldn’t fix robustly—only wallpaper over. He launched a year-plus cross-functional effort he called **Slaughterhouse** (a joke about needing to kill “chrome object wrappers”). The work was preemptive: if these exploit techniques were weaponized in the wild before a proper architectural fix, it would be a disaster. The disaster arrived anyway. A zero-day exploit was discovered in the wild on moscowtimes.com, and while disassembling it, Holley recognized the author’s distinctive “fingerprints”—the particular handwritten style of logic attacks using specific APIs and async event-loop tricks. He fired off an email to the researcher, essentially accusing him of double-crossing Mozilla. The researcher, extremely paranoid, concluded his own devices must be deeply compromised and went off the grid. An hour later, Mozilla discovered that *Bugzilla itself* had been compromised by a threat actor who had exfiltrated approximately 45 confidential security bugs. Holley tried to reach the researcher, but he’d already vanished. “It was several weeks later that we got a phone call from a bowling alley in upstate New York where this researcher had gone into hiding.”

The remarkable part: Slaughterhouse had just wrapped up. Of those ~45 exfiltrated bugs, Mozilla had already fixed all but two, thanks to the new architecture. “That really impressed upon me the importance of getting ahead of things on security and making sure that you have the right architecture, because sometimes you find yourself in a situation where you don’t have the luxury of the time to fix it.”

**No performance reviews and bottoms-up culture**

For much of its early history, Mozilla had no formal performance reviews or management structure. Quality was uneven, but “the caliber and dedication was very high.” Governance was decentralized through a module-ownership system (the access-control system was called **Despot**), and owners had near-final say over their areas. Holley sees this culture as powerful but also something that needed to evolve. His ongoing leadership challenge has been: “how do we preserve those aspects of the technical culture that make it great, while also addressing some of the shortcomings.”

**Rust adoption and the Firefox Quantum bet**

By the early 2010s, Mozilla faced two structural problems that couldn’t be solved through sheer hard work. The first was memory safety: in a C++ codebase, a large portion of defects were potentially exploitable, and dealing with them was a permanent game of whack-a-mole. The second was parallelism: as CPU core counts increased, browsers were leaving performance on the table, but multi-threaded C++ produced non-deterministic bugs that often became exploitable too. The vision was audacious: build a new programming language with compile-time guarantees against memory-safety issues *and* race conditions, then use it to build a new browser engine. That language was **Rust**; the engine prototype was **Servo**.

The strategy had two tracks. On one, Mozilla had to close the gap with Chrome on hard, discrete challenges: multi-process architecture, native video playback (to escape the crashing-Flash-on-YouTube problem), and a modern extension API. Browsers had allowed extensions to inject anything anywhere with no well-defined interfaces, which meant “anytime we changed anything, we would break an extension”—a real drag on architectural modernization. On the other track, Rust and Servo were the leapfrog bet. There was a parallel CSS engine in Servo that had never been done before. But Servo was built by a dozen people; Google had hundreds on Chromium. Every year, Servo fell further behind as hundreds of engineer-years of work accumulated in the production engine. By 2014-2015, the decision was to uplift the parallel CSS engine from Servo into Firefox.

Holley personally led that uplift, a project he calls “really the highlight of my career.” Every step of the way, people said it would never work: “How are you even gonna hook this thing up?” They had a C++ DOM and a Rust CSS engine with no tooling to make them talk; they built it all themselves. The result—shipped as part of **Firefox Quantum**—was a dramatic performance improvement: roughly a 25% reduction in rendering time on amazon.com, and it remains the fastest CSS engine in any browser. “It was a really exciting moment for everybody realizing that if we just coordinate and we do all the technical effort and we make the right bets, we can win.”

**Career progression and choosing your own title**

In the early days, Mozilla didn’t really have levels. The culture was “choose your own title.” Holley chose **Negative Entropy** as his title—describing what he wanted to do on the chaotic codebase. His favorite intern chose **Entropy**. (“We were really on the same side.”) By the time Firefox Quantum shipped, Mozilla had developed more standard career levels. Holley was a senior staff engineer; upon Quantum’s successful launch, he and the engineer who led the other half of the project were promoted to principal engineer. There were probably fewer than five principals at the time. But his long-term dream was reaching distinguished engineer (DE), like Boris Zbarsky. His model for getting there was to “contribute to as many parts of the code as possible and just be prolific” —jumping onto web render, then mobile, then multi-process, then layout, always orienting toward “whatever felt like the biggest problem.”

At one point his manager, Joe Hildebrand (who ran engineering), told him to spend one day a week *not opening his terminal*. Holley was shocked: “You’re asking me to not work for an entire day.” The point was to start thinking about impact in a much broader way than direct code contributions—a shift that did not come naturally to him.

**Should you focus on promotions?**

Holley has strong opinions: focus on impact, not the title. He acknowledges a common failure mode where soft-spoken engineers do great work but don’t get recognized—and that “brag documents” and visibility have real value. But the framing matters enormously. Approaching it as “I am trying to brag more about me” versus “I am trying to communicate and coordinate and create visibility for work that is important” produces very different outcomes. People can tell which one you’re doing. His practical advice: make updates about the work and the team, spotlight others, don’t make it about you—because that’s also how you become someone people want to work with. Ryan pushes on the “squeaky wheel” problem—that a loud, promo-focused high performer often gets rewarded faster than a quiet one. Holley acknowledges it depends on the organization, but his culture tries hard not to reward that. The best career conversations aren’t “how do I get promoted?” but “how do I grow my career and have more impact? Am I working on the most important thing?” Pinning your manager down on checkbox criteria is “just honestly extremely annoying.” On a life level, “you don’t want the people in leadership to find you to be annoying.”

**The distinguished engineer rejection**

When Mozilla finally decided to make new distinguished engineers after nearly a decade, CTO Eric Rescorla sat Holley down and told him directly: “You’re not gonna be one of them.” Holley was surprised. He had arguably more knowledge of the full Firefox codebase than almost anyone else—near-Boris-level coverage. But Rescorla explained that was the *old* model of DE. The new criteria were oriented around **industry impact**: changing how the internet works through standards, protocols, and platform-shaping technologies, not just encyclopedic codebase mastery. Rather than being upset, Holley found it inspiring. “You work out and you think that you’re really strong and you go do some Pilates move and you’re like, oh my gosh, this muscle that I have, I didn’t even know that I have—but it is extremely weak.” What mattered for an organization changes over time. Early Mozilla needed the prolific-codebase archetype; later Mozilla needed a different kind of impact. “Understanding that model of thinking about the world and that model of impact was very profound for me and really oriented how I directed my efforts.”

**Three archetypes of distinguished-engineer impact**

The three engineers promoted to DE that cycle embodied radically different shapes of world-changing IC work. **Martin Thomson** is an international standards extraordinaire—author of protocols like HTTP/2, QUIC, Web Push, WebRTC, and Geolocation. His strategic insight was to design protocols that were faster *and* more private, creating business incentives for the whole industry to adopt them and thus raising the privacy floor for everyone. **Luke Wagner** was, for a time, the single person most responsible for **WebAssembly**. He started with Emscripten (compiling C++ to naive JavaScript—technically worked, extremely slow), then pioneered asm.js (a detectable subset of JS the engine could optimize natively), and then, as that flywheel gained industry momentum, drove the creation of WebAssembly as a proper sandboxed, portable bytecode format—overcoming Google’s competing Native Client/PNaCl push and forging multi-browser consensus. Holley notes that WebAssembly’s impact now extends far beyond browsers into serverless and backend contexts. **Tim Terriberry** led the development of truly free, non-patent-encumbered codecs. When H.264’s patent thicket made it impossible for an open-source browser to ship native video playback, Terriberry’s team built the Opus audio codec and the Daala video codec, which fed into **AV1**—now the industry-standard royalty-free video format developed by the Alliance for Open Media (Mozilla, Google, Apple, Netflix, and others). These are the shapes impact can take: not building a billion-dollar business line, but changing the technical fabric of the internet itself.

**Advice for aspiring distinguished engineers: writing**

The one thing everyone can work on at any level is **writing**. Holley authored an internal memo called *Writing Things Down* that makes the case that writing isn’t just about the artifact (useful for coordination and review) but about the *process*—the process of writing *is* the process of thinking. “Clarity of thinking is one of the most important things, and you only can really get that by forcing yourself to write down your ideas.” His most pointed advice: **do not use an LLM to produce text for you**. First, it’s fairly obvious when someone does, but more importantly, “you are losing the opportunity to do the thinking yourself. And that thinking that happens when you write is one of the most important types of thinking for growth.” On writing well: keep it as short as what you have to say. If you run out of things to say, either refine your ideas more or you’re done; fluff never helps. Focus on substance over rhetoric. Non-native English speakers sometimes worry they can’t write beautifully, but “it is not about using the most flowery words. It is about clarity of thought, and clarity of thought, once you have it, is relatively easy to express in written form if you practice.”

**The AI browser wars**

Holley clarifies that the flurry of new “AI browsers” are not browsers in any engine sense—they are “different front ends, different window dressing and monetization skins on top of Chrome.” Firefox is the only independent browser with no dependency on another vendor’s engine. (Safari has WebKit; Chrome and all the Chromium derivatives—Opera, Edge, Arc, Brave—share one engine lineage.) AI is coming “like water running downhill,” and where that water flows is up for grabs. Chrome is making an aggressive play to control the AI stack by leveraging its dominance: embedding Gemini everywhere in the browser, and advancing proprietary web-platform APIs for local LLM access that are “fundamentally unspecified.” Because there’s no way to specify deterministic, interoperable LLM behavior in these APIs, websites will tune their AI experiences to whatever Gemini expects—a classic platform-capture move. Mozilla isn’t building its own foundation model, but it is fighting to keep the space open: ensuring users have choice over which AI models they integrate with, and protecting the web’s unique properties—that it’s re-interpretable content a browser can modify (enabling things like ad blockers), safe to navigate even from untrusted links, and governed by a multi-stakeholder process where no single company controls the platform’s evolution. “It is in some sense a very historical accident that we got something that is this well-aligned with the vision that Mozilla has for how the internet should work, and it’s very important to us to make sure that the changes that happen with AI do not sacrifice these properties.”

**Why Firefox builds its own engine**

Building your own browser engine is “a giant cost center” with essentially no bottom-line business justification. The strategic reason is that it gives Mozilla “a seat at the table when web standards and the future of the web is decided.” As other engines dropped off (Opera, Internet Explorer), the table narrowed to Apple and Google—two companies that don’t get along and have deeply entrenched interests. Mozilla, even with small market share, can tilt conversations back toward what’s best for users. Holley points to **Speedometer 3**, a shared cross-browser performance benchmark, as an example. A bilateral Google-Apple negotiation over a benchmark would predictably deadlock because each side would see the other angling for advantage. Mozilla’s involvement kept everyone focused on the stated mission—make the web faster, not make any particular browser look good. The result: both Firefox and Chrome roughly doubled their Speedometer scores over a couple of years, translating to about a 30% improvement in real-world page-load performance. “It is reasonable to say that having this benchmark has made the web 30% faster.”

**Biggest technical regret: WebRender and vibes-based leadership**

Holley’s biggest regret is not a technical outcome but a process that “wasn’t worth it.” After the Quantum CSS success, he was deeply enthusiastic about uplifting Servo’s graphics backend—WebRender—into Firefox. He advocated heavily for it, not based on deep graphics expertise but on a “vibes thing of this is where it’s going.” That enthusiasm caught on with management. When senior graphics engineers expressed strong doubts that the project was ready and warned it was too risky, a leader responded with “C++ is not the future.” The vibes-based push over the concerns of the people who knew the code best was “very toxic.” Several of Mozilla’s most senior graphics engineers left. The remaining team ultimately rallied and built WebRender into a fantastic, performant, safe engine. “I’m really proud of the team for what they built, but I think that pushing it on the team when the team wasn’t ready and wasn’t aligned on it had collateral damage that I regret.”

**Wide reading (and not reading)**

Holley considers himself an avid reader but admits he doesn’t actually read many whole books—it’s a depth-first approach, and he prefers breadth. “There was this book that I just read a book review of called *Good Strategy, Bad Strategy*, and I just read three pages and that has just stuck with me.” He’s been profoundly influenced by ideas from *Turn the Ship Around!* (on empowering teams) and *The Innovator’s Dilemma* (Clayton Christensen), in both cases without reading the full books. He encourages people to look wide, including outside tech. The geopolitics and national-security space, for example, contains deeply thoughtful writing about what happens on a battlefield and what goes right or wrong—and a lot of those ideas “certainly rhyme with situations in battles between big tech and how do you manage and motivate teams.”

**Advice to his younger self: humility**

“At the end of the day, to be humble is the most important.” Early in his career, he had an attitude—not strictly arrogant about himself, but about engineering—that engineers and engineering were the center of the universe. As he moved into leadership, he developed empathy for all the other functions he’d once dismissed: legal, process, the reasons organizations need structure. “Realizing that everybody has a point of view and a place that they’re coming from, and that working to understand where people are coming from is the way to make progress.” His closing message to listeners: the web is not inevitable. People treat it like water from the tap—something that just keeps getting better—but it easily could backslide into unilateral big-tech control. If you build for the web, “making sure that your experiences work in all browsers and that you don’t do the lazy path and build something that only works in Google Chrome” is the first line of defense against returning to the world of 25 years ago.

---

His career arc traces a shift from a **Negative Entropy** model of impact—prolific, encyclopedic codebase mastery oriented toward “whatever felt like the biggest problem”—to the **industry impact** model his CTO articulated, where the work changes the technical fabric of the internet through standards, codecs, and protocols. The **Slaughterhouse** project and the WebRender regret form a matched pair: one where getting ahead of the architecture saved the organization from catastrophe, and another where vibes-based enthusiasm overruled the people who knew the code best and caused talent to walk. The through-line in his advice—on promotions, on writing, on humility—is a conviction that legitimately *doing the thinking* and communicating it clearly will always outperform box-checking or self-promotion, and that people can tell the difference.
