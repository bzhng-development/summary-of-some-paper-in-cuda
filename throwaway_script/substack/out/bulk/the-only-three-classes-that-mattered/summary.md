Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman interview (went from UCLA CS grad to staff engineer at Meta, reflecting on which college courses actually matter in daily software engineering work):

**The only three classes that mattered**

Out of the 56 classes Ryan took at UCLA, only three courses directly apply to his daily work as a software engineer — and he's confident any engineer would agree. Two of them teach you how to code and pass interviews; the third transforms you from a programmer into a working engineer.

**CS31 & CS32: everything you need to pass a FAANG interview**

CS31 and CS32 are "without a doubt" the two most important courses in the UCLA CS curriculum — they're how engineering students actually learn to code. Ryan grew up without any programming classes in high school: "I didn't even know what CS was until I took CS31." That first course covers the fundamentals — how variables, loops, functions, and basic logic work. While the early assignments don't resemble industry code, "CS31 lays the foundation."

CS32 builds directly on top: object-oriented programming and data structures. Those two topics alone, Ryan says, "cover most of the programming I do." More practically, these two classes give you the toolkit to handle any FAANG interview question — though Ryan adds a caveat that actually landing those jobs "requires a lot of preparation (a topic for a future post)." Still, the conceptual baseline from CS31 and CS32 is the non-negotiable starting line for algorithmic interview performance. They taught the class how to code — but coding alone is not software engineering. That gap gets closed by one notorious follow-up course.

**CS35L: where programmers become engineers**

UCLA's CS35L is infamous — "a weekly series of time-consuming labs" that, while painful, "is what creates software engineers." In it, students learn the command line (shell commands, scripting), change management (Git), and how to use basic debugging tools. Ryan has "always loved using debuggers rather than putting print statements everywhere" because of how convenient it is to interact with a live program's runtime. These three skill areas — terminal fluency, version control, and systematic debugging — are "core to writing code in a collaborative professional environment," and he now uses them so often at work that "they've become second nature."

The course also had a memorably elegant late policy: assignments turned in \(N\) days late would lose \(2^{(N-1)}\) points. "This had a nice effect of being forgiving for slight delays but brutal for anything more than a few days late" — an exponential curve that's gentle at first and punishing quickly, encouraging students to ship rather than stall.

**Honorable mentions: algorithms, operating systems, databases**

Three other classes earned a nod of diminishing relevance. Algorithms (CS180) taught him how to do Leetcode hard difficulty questions — useful for interview prep but not a daily tool. Operating Systems (CS111) taught him "how computers work," a mental model that sits in the background. Databases (CS143) was "a nice crash course on SQL." None of the three rises to the practical, everyday utility of the core trio.

**The one thing he'd change about his education**

Looking back, Ryan wishes he'd spent less time on theoretical coursework and more on practical, tangible work — specifically open-source contributions and side projects. The signal from CS31, CS32, and CS35L is that applied, build-and-ship skills dominate what a working engineer actually does, and he would have leaned harder into that axis if he could redo it.

---

Ryan's entire framework rests on a ruthless distinction between **learning to code** and **becoming a software engineer** — CS31 and CS32 supply the former, but only CS35L's brutal lab gauntlet delivers the latter. The honorable mentions he waves aside (algorithms, operating systems, databases) are exactly the courses most students obsess over, yet they occupy a secondary tier precisely because they lack the daily collaborative tissue — version control, debugging fluency, terminal command — that his **programmer-versus-engineer gap** demands. His regret about skipping practical work in favor of theory is just the same insight applied in reverse: the classes that actually mattered were the ones that forced him to *ship* something with tools other people would also touch.
