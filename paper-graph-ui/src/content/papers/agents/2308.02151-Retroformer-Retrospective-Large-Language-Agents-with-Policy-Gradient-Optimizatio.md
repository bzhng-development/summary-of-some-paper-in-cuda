## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Retroformer**, a principled framework for reinforcing large language agents by learning a plug-in retrospective model that automatically tunes language agent prompts from environment feedback through policy gradient optimization — specifically fine-tuning a smaller local LM (LongChat-7b) to generate verbal self-reflections that summarize the root causes of prior failed attempts and propose corrective action plans, while keeping the frozen actor LLM (GPT-3/GPT-4) inaccessible. The framework treats the actor LLM as part of the environment and uses the difference in episode returns between consecutive trials as a reward signal for proximal policy optimization (PPO) of the retrospective model, enabling gradient-based credit assignment from sparse environment rewards across multiple environments — HotPotQA (multi-hop QA with Wikipedia search), AlfWorld (embodied robotics via text actions), and WebShop (web browsing for product shopping). Across these benchmarks, Retroformer substantially outperforms non-gradient baselines like Reflexion, achieving 54% success rate on HotPotQA with 4 retries versus 50% for Reflexion, resolving 100% of AlfWorld tasks within 3 retries where Reflexion plateaus at 84-85%, and improving WebShop success by up to 4%, establishing that the learned retrospective model provides faster and more effective credit assignment than frozen-LLM verbal reflection only when the actor LM's initial capability is sufficient for the task to be solvable with improved guidance.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Language Agents Can't Learn From Environment-Specific Rewards

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is that while large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being deployed as autonomous agents that interact with environments — making API calls, searching the web, manipulating objects in embodied simulations — **these agents are not optimized using environment-specific reward functions**. This means they cannot systematically improve their behavior through trial-and-error experience in the way that classical reinforcement learning agents can.

To understand why this matters, consider what happens when an LLM agent attempts a multi-step task. The agent generates a sequence of actions (e.g., search queries, button clicks, navigation commands) based on a prompt that describes the goal and the current state. If the agent fails — say, it arrives at the wrong answer on a question-answering task, or it gets stuck in a loop during an embodied task — two things need to happen for it to do better next time. First, it needs to figure out *what went wrong* (credit assignment — identifying which specific actions in a long trajectory led to the failure). Second, it needs to generate *actionable guidance* for avoiding that mistake in the subsequent attempt.

Existing LLM agents, particularly at the time this paper was written, were largely incapable of either. They would either repeat the same errors indefinitely, or they would rely on frozen, pre-trained reasoning capabilities that were never specifically trained to diagnose failures in particular environments. The paper frames this as a **credit assignment problem** (Section 1, citing Sutton & Barto, 2018): the agent must correctly identify which actions in a long chain of reasoning and interaction were responsible for an eventual negative outcome — a skill that general-purpose language models are not explicitly trained for.

### Why This Problem Is Important

The significance of this gap extends well beyond academic interest. The paper identifies a convergence of practical needs and theoretical limitations that make this problem urgent.

**Practical urgency: the rise of autonomous language agents.** The paper opens by surveying a rapidly emerging landscape of LLM-powered autonomous agents — ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), Toolformer (Schick et al., 2023), HuggingGPT (Shen et al., 2023), Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023), WebGPT (Nakano et al., 2021), AutoGPT (Gravitas, 2023), BabyAGI (Nakajima, 2023), and LangChain (Chase, 2023). These systems share a common blueprint: use an LLM to generate text-based actions that trigger API calls or environment interactions. The proliferation of such systems signals a shift in how LLMs are being used — not merely as Q&A chatbots, but as **goal-directed autonomous agents** that operate over extended time horizons in complex environments.

The problem is that all of these agents share a critical weakness: **their behavior is not optimized or aligned with environment reward functions**, as the paper states explicitly in the introduction. Their performance depends entirely on the frozen quality of the pre-trained LLM driving them. If the LLM doesn't understand the specific failure modes of a Wikipedia search task, or the action constraints of an embodied kitchen, there is no mechanism for it to improve through repeated exposure. This means each new environment, each new task type, requires *ad hoc* prompt engineering rather than systematic learning.

**Theoretical significance: bridging two disconnected paradigms.** The paper occupies a unique position at the intersection of **classical reinforcement learning** and **LLM-based agent design**. In classical RL, agents improve through environment interaction by computing gradients (or equivalent updates) from reward signals — this is the backbone of methods like policy gradient (Sutton et al., 2000), PPO (Schulman et al., 2017), and SAC (Haarnoja et al., 2018). In contrast, LLM-based agents have relied almost entirely on in-context learning from frozen models, where any "improvement" comes from better prompting rather than from actual optimization against a reward function. The paper argues that this gap represents a missed opportunity: the abundant reward signals that environments naturally provide (binary success/failure, F1 scores, task completion indicators) are being ignored, and the powerful machinery of gradient-based RL optimization is being left on the table.

The paper's framing in Section 3 makes the theoretical connection explicit: if we treat the frozen actor LLM as part of the environment — since its parameters are inaccessible and its output distribution is fixed — then the problem reduces to a standard RL formulation where we optimize a learnable component (the retrospective model) that conditions the behavior of the environment-agent system. This is a clever theoretical bridge: it preserves the black-box nature of cloud-hosted LLMs (OpenAI GPT, Google Bard) while still enabling gradient-based optimization of the overall agent system.

**Economic and deployment implications.** Most state-of-the-art LLMs are too large to fine-tune directly for individual users or organizations — they are hosted behind APIs with inaccessible parameters. The paper explicitly acknowledges this constraint (Section 4): "the actor model is a frozen LLM whose model parameters are inaccessible (e.g., OpenAI GPT) and the retrospective model is a smaller, local language model that can be fine-tuned under low-resource settings (e.g., Llama-7b)." This setup mirrors the actual deployment reality for most practitioners, making Retroformer's approach of optimizing a small plug-in module rather than the full actor LLM deeply practical.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three categories of prior approaches and explains why each is insufficient.

**1. Frozen agent architectures (ReAct and its descendants).** The state of the art at the time centered on ReAct (Yao et al., 2023), which uses few-shot prompting to teach an LLM to interleave reasoning traces ("thoughts") with actions. ReAct agents can solve multi-step reasoning tasks by generating structured outputs that trigger environment interactions (e.g., `Search[entity]`, `Lookup[keyword]`, `Finish[answer]`). However, ReAct has no mechanism for learning from outcomes. As the paper notes in Table 1 and Section 2, ReAct supports hidden constraints and decision-making, but it lacks gradient learning, arbitrary reward handling, and iterative refinement. Each time a ReAct agent attempts a task, it starts fresh with no memory of prior failures on that same task. If the base LLM's reasoning leads it into a loop (e.g., repeatedly searching for the same entity without refining the query), it will repeat that loop identically on subsequent attempts because there is no feedback that alters its behavior.

This is not a hypothetical limitation — it is a direct consequence of the stateless nature of LLMs. The model itself has no persistent memory between episodes. Without an external mechanism to summarize failures and inject corrective guidance into the prompt, performance is bounded by the base model's zero-shot or few-shot capabilities.

**2. Verbal self-reflection without gradient optimization (Reflexion).** The paper's most direct baseline is Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), which introduced the idea of using an LLM to generate verbal self-reflections after failed attempts. The key innovation in Reflexion was converting a binary or scalar environment reward into a textual summary that is appended to the agent's prompt on the next attempt. This "self-reflection feedback acts as a semantic signal by providing the agent with a concrete direction to improve upon," enabling it to learn from prior mistakes and prevent repetitive errors (Section 1).

However, the paper identifies a critical weakness in this approach: **generating useful reflective feedback from a frozen, pre-trained LLM is fundamentally challenging**. The paper provides a concrete example in Figure 1 (described in Section 1 and analyzed in detail in Section 5.3, Figure 5) that illustrates this failure mode vividly:

> "Lollipop Chainsaw featured Juliet Starling, who was voiced by a Canadian-American actress who has done voice roles for what Teen Titans spinoff series?"

In this HotPotQA task, the agent's prior attempt failed because it submitted both "Teen Titans" and "Teen Titans Go!" as the answer, when the correct answer includes only the spinoff "Teen Titans Go!" The agent lost track of its goal during a lengthy chain of interactions — it correctly found that Tara Strong voiced Juliet Starling and that she appeared in Teen Titans properties, but it failed to distinguish between the original series and the spinoff. The frozen LLM's self-reflection, however, completely misses this root cause. Instead of diagnosing the correct-vs-spinoff confusion, it merely rephrases the action sequence that led to failure:

> "I should have searched for Lollipop Chainsaw first and looked up the Canadian-American actress who voiced Juliet Starling afterwards. I also should have looked up Tara Strong's filmography and searched for any voice roles she did specifically for Teen Titans or Teen Titans Go!"

This reflection is **actively harmful** — it prompts the agent to repeat essentially the same search trajectory in the next attempt, leading to the same incorrect answer. The frozen LLM lacks the specialized capability to perform accurate credit assignment in a specific environment. It can rephrase what happened, but it cannot reliably isolate *why* the failure occurred, nor can it generate an actionable plan that addresses the root cause.

The deeper issue is that verbal reinforcement from a frozen model "cannot be optimal, if the frozen language model has not been properly fine-tuned to specialize in credit assignment problems for the tasks in given environments" (Section 1). In other words, credit assignment is itself a skill — one that requires training data and optimization, not just general-purpose reasoning. The paper argues that this skill needs to be learned from environment feedback, not presumed from pre-training.

**3. Classical RL methods face practical barriers with LLM agents.** The paper also briefly considers traditional RL approaches (Section 2, and the SAC baseline in Section 5.2). Directly applying RL algorithms like PPO or SAC to fine-tune the actor LLM would, in principle, enable gradient-based learning from rewards. But this faces two severe practical obstacles that the paper highlights in Appendix A ("Challenges"):

- **Prohibitive training costs:** "Most of the well-performing LLMs are too large to be fit in just one or two GPUs. It is technically challenging to optimize the LLMs directly as is done in the classical reinforcement learning setting. In particular, OpenAI has not provided any solution for RL based finetuning."
- **Spurious actions and limited prompt length:** LLMs are not pre-trained with action-agent applications in mind, so they may generate invalid actions not in the action space, and concatenating full trajectory histories into the prompt rapidly hits context length limits (Appendix A).

The SAC baseline included in the experiments (Table 2) attempts to apply online RL directly to the agent — using mean-pooled embeddings of generated text as continuous actions — but achieves poor results: 27% on HotPotQA with 4 retries versus Retroformer's 54%, and 59.7% on AlfWorld versus Retroformer's 100%. This demonstrates that naive application of classical RL to text-based agents is ineffective without more sophisticated architectural choices.

**4. The gap between proposal distribution modification and verifier-based selection.** While this paper doesn't use this exact terminology, its framework can be understood as addressing a gap in *how* test-time improvements are made. Prior work like Reflexion modified the agent's behavior purely through prompt augmentation — akin to changing the proposal distribution — but did so with frozen models that couldn't be optimized. Meanwhile, classical RL approaches attempted to directly optimize the policy (the actor) but faced prohibitive costs. Retroformer occupies a middle ground: it optimizes the prompt-engineering component (the retrospective model) through gradient-based RL, while leaving the actor frozen. This is a novel combination that neither pure prompting approaches nor pure RL approaches had explored.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions Retroformer as **the first framework to bring gradient-based reinforcement learning to language agents without requiring access to the actor LLM's parameters**. This is a specific, clearly-bounded contribution that the authors differentiate carefully from related work in Table 1.

The positioning has several key aspects:

**Agnostic to the actor LLM.** The paper repeatedly emphasizes that Retroformer treats the actor LLM as part of the environment — "the only learnable parameters are in the retrospective model" (Section 3). This is not a limitation but a deliberate design choice that makes the framework a "flexible plug-in module for various types of cloud-based LLMs, such as OpenAI GPT or Google Bard" (Section 1). This agnosticism is showcased in the experiments, which test both GPT-3 and GPT-4 as the frozen actor, with Retroformer providing consistent improvements regardless of which model serves as the base.

**Learning from arbitrary reward signals.** Unlike Reflexion, which primarily handles binary success/failure (and converts it to text), Retroformer is designed to learn from "arbitrary reward information across multiple environments and tasks" (Section 1). The paper uses shaped rewards where available — F1 score for HotPotQA answer matching, product attribute matching plus budget compliance for WebShop, and binary success for AlfWorld (Appendix C.3). This flexibility means Retroformer can leverage richer feedback signals when they exist, rather than being limited to binary outcomes.

**Iterative refinement through gradient-based credit assignment.** The central claim is that gradient-based optimization of the retrospective model produces reflection responses that are qualitatively better at credit assignment than frozen-LLM reflections. The paper demonstrates this through both quantitative results (faster improvement in early trials across all three environments) and qualitative examples (Figure 5 showing how Retroformer's reinforced response correctly identifies the spinoff-vs-original confusion that the frozen model missed). This is the mechanism by which Retroformer addresses the core limitation identified in Reflexion: by training on environment feedback across many tasks, the retrospective model learns to be a better diagnostician.

**Offline training from multi-environment experience.** The paper's training procedure (Section 4.2, Appendix C.1) collects reflection samples by rolling out a base policy across training tasks, computes ratings as the difference in episode returns between consecutive trials (Equation 5: $r(x_{k,i}, y_{k,i}) \triangleq G_{k,i+1} - G_{k,i}$), and fine-tunes the retrospective model offline using PPO with these ratings. This means the retrospective model learns not just from successes and failures on individual tasks, but from the *relationship* between a reflection and the subsequent improvement (or lack thereof). A reflection that leads to a large improvement in the next trial gets a high positive rating; one that leads to no improvement or degradation gets a negative rating. This rating signal directly encodes credit assignment quality — it rewards the retrospective model for generating reflections that actually help the actor improve.

**A specific, constrained scope.** The paper is careful not to overclaim. It does not propose a general solution for all agent learning problems. The experiments show that while Retroformer provides dramatic improvements in HotPotQA (+18% with 4 retries) and AlfWorld (reaching 100% success), the gains on WebShop are more modest (+4% with 4 retries). The paper acknowledges this limitation: "web browsing requires a significant amount of exploration with more precise search queries, if compared with HotPotQA. The results probably indicate that the verbal feedback approach (Reflexion, Retroformer) is not an optimal method for this environment, but our fine-tuning method still proves effective" (Section 5.3). This honesty about where the method works best — environments where credit assignment through textual reflection is feasible — strengthens the paper's positioning by establishing clear boundary conditions.

**Reconciling conflicting desiderata.** The paper's architecture is designed to satisfy multiple constraints simultaneously: (1) work with inaccessible, cloud-hosted actor LLMs, (2) leverage gradient-based RL for optimization, (3) learn from arbitrary reward signals (not just binary), (4) operate with a small, locally fine-tunable model as the learnable component, and (5) improve both learning speed (faster improvement in early trials) and final performance. No prior approach satisfied all of these simultaneously — ReAct lacked learning entirely, Reflexion lacked gradient optimization, and direct RL on the actor was impractical for large hosted models.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Retroformer is a system that wraps around a frozen, cloud-hosted large language model (like GPT-3 or GPT-4) to make it into an agent that gets better at multi-step tasks through trial and error — without ever modifying the frozen model's weights. The problem it solves is that LLM-based agents performing tasks like web search, shopping, or embodied navigation fail and repeat mistakes because they lack a mechanism to learn from environment reward signals; the solution is a smaller, locally fine-tunable "retrospective" language model that watches the agent's failed attempts, learns to diagnose what went wrong, and rewrites the agent's prompt with a concise failure analysis and corrective plan — and this retrospective model is trained with policy gradient reinforcement learning using the difference in task performance between consecutive attempts as the training signal.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Retroformer system has five major components arranged in a loop:

1. **Frozen Actor LLM (Ma):** A large, cloud-hosted language model (GPT-3 `text-davinci-003` or GPT-4) with inaccessible parameters. It receives a prompt containing the task instruction, the current environment state, short-term memory (the current episode's trajectory of observations and actions), and long-term memory (self-reflection summaries from prior failed attempts on this same task). It outputs a reasoning thought followed by a discrete action (e.g., `Search[entity]`, `Take[object]`, `click[button]`).

2. **Environment (Ω):** The task environment — HotPotQA (Wikipedia search and question answering), AlfWorld (text-based embodied household tasks), or WebShop (web shopping). It receives the actor's text action, executes it, and returns a new state observation and (at episode end) a scalar reward.

3. **Retrospective Model (Mr):** A smaller, locally hosted language model initialized from LongChat-7b-16k (a Llama-7b variant fine-tuned for long-context instruction following). After the actor completes a full episode (success or failure), Mr receives a "reflection prompt" containing the complete trajectory (all states, actions, observations, and the final reward) and generates a verbal self-reflection — a concise diagnosis of the root cause of failure and a high-level corrective plan. This reflection text is appended to the actor's prompt on the next attempt as long-term memory. **Mr is the only component with learnable weights.**

4. **Memory System:** Three types of memory store context. Short-term memory is the current episode's trajectory concatenated into the actor prompt. Long-term memory is the set of prior reflection responses from Mr for this specific task, also appended to the actor prompt. The replay buffer (DRL) is a persistent dataset of `(reflection prompt, reflection response, episode return)` triples collected across all training tasks and trials, used for offline training of Mr.

5. **PPO Training Loop:** An offline reinforcement learning pipeline (using the `trl` library's PPOTrainer) that fine-tunes Mr. It samples instruction-response pairs from the replay buffer, computes a rating for each pair as the improvement in episode return between the trial where the reflection was generated and the subsequent trial (`rating = G_{i+1} - G_i`), and optimizes Mr to generate responses that yield higher ratings — i.e., reflections that actually help the actor improve.

Information flows as follows: a task enters → the actor prompt combines the task, current state, trajectory history, and prior reflections → the actor generates an action → the environment executes it and returns an observation and (eventually) a reward → after the episode ends, the full trajectory plus reward feeds into Mr → Mr generates a reflection → this reflection is added to long-term memory → on the next attempt for this same task, the augmented prompt (with the new reflection) feeds back into the actor → the cycle repeats for N retry attempts. Separately, the collected `(prompt, response, return)` triples are used offline to train Mr via PPO, using return differences as rewards.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal mathematical formulation (Section 3 of the paper) — the optimization objective in Equation 1 and Equation 2 — because this defines what "learning" means, establishes the actor-as-environment reduction, and explains why standard RL algorithms apply despite the actor being frozen.
- **Second**, the actor model and its interface — the ReAct-style prompt structure, the action space for each environment, and how the actor's output is consumed — because this defines the "decision-making" side that the retrospective model must learn to guide.
- **Third**, the retrospective model's architecture and inference procedure — how the reflection prompt is constructed, how reflections are generated, and how they are integrated into the actor's context — because this is the component that gets optimized.
- **Fourth**, the memory and replay buffer system — short-term vs. long-term memory, and how data is collected for offline training — because this bridges from individual task execution to batch training across environments.
- **Fifth**, the reward shaping and rating mechanism — how episode returns are computed for each environment, and how the crucial rating signal `r(x, y) = G_{i+1} - G_i` is derived — because this is the objective that drives policy gradient optimization.
- **Sixth**, the PPO training procedure — the RLHF pipeline adapted for retrospective model fine-tuning, including supervised fine-tuning as a warm-start, reward model training, and the PPO objective with KL regularization — because this is the core technical contribution that differentiates Retroformer from frozen-reflection baselines.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and training methodology paper** whose core idea is that the prompt-engineering component of an LLM agent — the part that watches failures and summarizes lessons — can itself be treated as a learnable policy and optimized with policy gradient reinforcement learning, using environment returns as the reward signal, without ever touching the frozen actor LLM's parameters.

#### Formal Optimization Objective: Treating the Actor as Part of the Environment

The paper formalizes the language agent system as a Markov decision process where the only learnable component is the retrospective model. This formalization (Section 3) is what makes the subsequent application of PPO theoretically sound rather than ad-hoc.

**The actor model is a random function.** The paper defines the actor LLM as:

$$M_{\xi_l}: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$$

where `$\mathcal{X}$` is the space of prompts (including user instructions `$x_u$`, short-term trajectory context, and long-term reflection context), `$\mathcal{A}$` is the space of actions (text strings representing API calls), and `$\xi_l$` represents the re-parameterized random variables involved in autoregressive sampling. The subscript `$l$` indicates this is the (frozen) LLM.

**What this means operationally:** the actor takes a text prompt as input and samples an action string as output. The randomness in this sampling process (controlled by temperature and top-p parameters) is captured by `$\xi_l$`. Critically, the paper notes that the LLM-based agent is "stateless" — all states and possible memorization are characterized as text in the agent prompt `$x$`. There is no hidden state vector or recurrent memory inside the model; the entire history is explicitly concatenated into the prompt.

**The environment is a state transition function plus reward function.** The environment is defined as:

$$\Omega = (\mathcal{T}_{\xi_o}, \mathcal{R})$$

where `$\mathcal{T}_{\xi_o}: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathcal{S}$` is the state transition function (mapping current state and action to next state), `$\mathcal{S}$` is the space of states (represented as text descriptions), `$\xi_o$` represents randomness in state transitions, and `$\mathcal{R}: \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$` is the reward function that scores a state. At each time step `$t$`, the current state `$s_t$` is described in natural language and integrated into the prompt context `$c$`.

**Why this formulation matters:** by explicitly representing both the actor and the environment as functions with explicit randomness terms, the paper can write a well-defined expectation over the entire agent-environment interaction that serves as the optimization objective. This is standard in reinforcement learning but was not previously applied to LLM agent systems where the "policy" (actor) is frozen.

**The retrospective model is the learnable policy.** The retrospective model is defined as:

$$\Gamma_{\xi_r, \Theta}: [S_i, A_i, R_i, X^u_i]^t_{i=1} \rightarrow \mathcal{X}$$

where `$[S_i, A_i, R_i, X^u_i]^t_{i=1}$` is the complete history of all prior episodes — all states `$S_i$`, actions `$A_i$`, rewards `$R_i$`, and user prompts `$X^u_i$` for episodes `$i = 1$` through `$t$` — and `$\mathcal{X}$` is a new prompt (the original prompt augmented with a reflection). The term `$\xi_r$` captures sampling randomness in Mr's generation, and `$\Theta$` is the set of learnable parameters (the weights of the retrospective language model).

**What this function does in practice:** given everything that has happened across all prior attempts on a task — the full trajectories and the rewards achieved — Mr generates a new, augmented prompt for the actor. This augmented prompt includes a reflection that diagnoses prior failures and proposes a corrective plan. The actor then uses this augmented prompt on the next attempt.

**The full optimization objective.** The paper states the RL goal as:

$$\arg\max_{\Theta} \mathbb{E}_{\xi_l, \xi_o, \xi_r} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathcal{R}(s_t) \right]$$

subject to the constraint that state transitions follow:

$$s_{t+1} = \mathcal{T}_{\xi_o} \left( s_t, \mathcal{L}_{\xi_l} \circ \Gamma_{\xi_r, \Theta} \left( [s_i, a_i, r_i, x^u_i]^t_{i=1} \right) \right), \quad \forall t \in \{1, \ldots, T-1\}$$

where `$\mathbb{E}_{\xi_l, \xi_o, \xi_r}$` denotes the expectation over all three sources of randomness (actor sampling, environment transitions, and retrospective model sampling), `$T$` is the episode length, `$\mathcal{R}(s_t)$` is the reward at state `$s_t$`, and `$\circ$` denotes function composition — the output of the retrospective model (an augmented prompt) is fed directly as input to the actor model.

**What this equation computes:** it says we want to find retrospective model parameters `$\Theta$` that maximize the expected cumulative reward across an episode, where the expectation integrates over all randomness in the system. The constraint describes how the state evolves: the retrospective model generates a prompt from the history, the actor generates an action from that prompt, and the environment transitions to a new state based on that action. This is a standard episodic reinforcement learning objective.

**The critical reduction: actor as environment.** The paper makes the following observation (Section 3):

> "Note that the only learnable parameters are in the retrospective model Mr. Since LLM action agent is frozen, it can be considered as part of the environment. Specifically, if we construct another environment with the transition function `$\mathcal{T}' = \mathcal{T}(S, \bullet) \circ \mathcal{L}: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{S}$`, and the same reward function `$\mathcal{R}$`, then Eq. (2) is just a regular RL optimization so all the popular RL algorithms apply."

**Why this reduction is theoretically important:** by composing the frozen actor with the environment's state transition, we obtain a new "effective environment" where the action space is the space of prompts `$\mathcal{X}$` (the output of Mr) rather than the space of environment actions `$\mathcal{A}$`. The retrospective model becomes the policy that selects prompts, and the effective environment handles everything else — translating prompts to actions via the frozen actor, executing those actions in the real environment, and returning rewards. This means Retroformer is not doing anything exotic; it is applying standard RL (specifically PPO) to a policy that happens to output text prompts. The entire complexity of the frozen LLM is absorbed into the environment dynamics.

**Design choice — why not optimize the actor directly?** The paper explicitly addresses this in Appendix A and the introduction. Direct fine-tuning of the actor LLM is technically prohibitive: "Most of the well-performing LLMs are too large to be fit in just one or two GPUs" and "OpenAI has not provided any solution for RL based finetuning." Even if compute were available, the actor's parameters might be inaccessible behind an API. The retrospective-model-as-policy approach sidesteps both problems: the actor remains a black-box service (the paper uses OpenAI API calls), and only the much smaller retrospective model (LongChat-7b, 7 billion parameters, fine-tuned with 4-bit quantization and LoRA adapters adding only 0.53M to 2.25M trainable parameters) needs GPU access. This makes the approach deployable by practitioners who have API access to a large LLM but only modest local compute.

**Design choice — why not optimize the prompt with discrete search?** An alternative approach would be to search over possible reflection texts using methods like reinforcement learning with a discrete action space or evolutionary algorithms. The paper opts for gradient-based optimization through PPO because it allows the retrospective model to learn a *mapping* from trajectory features to reflection content — i.e., it can learn patterns across many tasks rather than optimizing reflections independently per task. The policy gradient signal flows through the autoregressive generation process (via the standard RLHF pipeline), enabling Mr to internalize general credit assignment strategies from the multi-environment training data.

#### Actor Model: The Frozen Decision-Maker

The actor model `$M_a$` is the component that actually interacts with the environment. It is always a frozen, cloud-hosted LLM with inaccessible parameters — specifically GPT-3 (`text-davinci-003`) or GPT-4 in the experiments.

**Prompt structure.** The actor prompt follows the ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) template augmented with long-term memory from prior reflections. The paper provides the full prompt in Appendix E.1. The structure is:

1. **System instruction:** A description of the environment and the available action APIs. For HotPotQA, this includes `Search[entity]`, `Lookup[keyword]`, and `Finish[answer]`. For AlfWorld, it includes `goto[location]`, `take[obj]`, `open[obj]`, `heat[obj]`, etc. For WebShop, it includes `search[query]` and `click[button]`.

2. **Few-shot demonstrations:** Several examples of successful task completions showing the interleaved `Thought: ... Action: ... Observation: ...` pattern. These teach the actor the expected output format and demonstrate good reasoning strategies.

3. **Memory for the current task:** The long-term memory section, which contains reflection responses from prior failed attempts on this specific task. Each reflection is labeled with the trial number (e.g., "Trial 0:", "Trial 1:") and contains the retrospective model's diagnosis and corrective plan from the previous attempt.

4. **Current task instruction:** The specific goal (e.g., "Your task is to: heat some mug and put it in coffeemachine" for AlfWorld, or a question for HotPotQA).

5. **Current state and action history:** The trajectory so far in the current episode — all previous thoughts, actions, and observations — concatenated in chronological order.

**Action generation.** The actor generates output autoregressively, producing a thought (reasoning about what to do) followed by an action (the API call). The paper formally writes this as:

$$a_{k,i,t} = M_a \left( [s_{k,i,\tau}, a_{k,i,\tau}, r_{k,i,\tau}]^{t-1}_{\tau=1}, s_{k,i,t} \right)$$

where `$k$` indexes the task, `$i$` indexes the retry attempt (episode), and `$t$` indexes the time step within the episode. The notation `$[\cdot]^{t-1}_{\tau=1}$` indicates the sequence of all prior state-action-reward tuples in the current episode, and `$s_{k,i,t}$` is the current state observation.

**What this means operationally:** at each step, the actor sees everything that has happened so far in this attempt (short-term memory) plus all prior reflections on this task (long-term memory), and it must decide what to do next. The paper sets the temperature `$T = 0$` and `$\text{top}_p = 1$` for all experiments — meaning the actor generates deterministically (greedy decoding). The paper explicitly justifies this: "We acknowledge that setting a higher temperature value can encourage exploration but it can obscure the impact of the proposed approaches, making it difficult to compare against existing baselines with T=0 (Yao et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023)." In other words, by removing sampling randomness from the actor, any changes in behavior between retry attempts can be attributed solely to the updated reflection content in the prompt — which isolates the effect of the retrospective model.

**Why the actor is frozen.** This is both a practical constraint and a deliberate design choice. Practically, cloud-hosted LLMs like GPT-3 and GPT-4 do not expose their parameters for fine-tuning. The paper notes (Appendix A) that "most of the well-performing LLMs are too large to be fit in just one or two GPUs" and "OpenAI has not provided any solution for RL based finetuning." Design-wise, keeping the actor frozen makes Retroformer a "flexible plug-in module" that can work with any LLM — the experiments demonstrate this by testing with both GPT-3 and GPT-4 and showing consistent improvements regardless of which serves as the base. The retrospective model is agnostic to the specific actor; it only needs to observe the trajectories and rewards.

**State representation.** The paper emphasizes that "the LLM-based agent itself is stateless. All the states and possible memorization are characterized as text in the agent prompt `$x$`." This means there is no hidden state vector, no recurrent memory, no internal representation that persists between time steps or episodes. Everything the actor "remembers" is explicitly written in the prompt. This is why the reflection content (long-term memory) is so critical — it is the only mechanism for the agent to carry lessons from one episode to the next. Without it, the actor would approach each retry identically, producing the same actions and encountering the same failures.

#### Retrospective Model: The Learnable Prompt Engineer

The retrospective model `$M_r$` is the core innovation and the only component with trainable parameters. It is instantiated from **LongChat-7b-16k**, a variant of Llama-7b fine-tuned on instruction-following conversations from ShareGPT, with native support for 16,000-token context windows. This base model is chosen because it can handle the long trajectory prompts needed for multi-step agent tasks (the full history of a failed episode can easily exceed standard 2048-token limits).

**Reflection generation at inference time.** After each episode (success or failure), Mr receives a reflection instruction prompt constructed from the complete episode trajectory and the final reward. The paper formalizes this as:

$$y_{k,i} = M_r \left( \underbrace{[s_{k,i,\tau}, a_{k,i,\tau}, r_{k,i,\tau}]^T_{\tau=1}, G_{k,i}}_{\text{Reflection prompt } x_{k,i}} \right)$$

where `$[s_{k,i,\tau}, a_{k,i,\tau}, r_{k,i,\tau}]^T_{\tau=1}$` is the complete trajectory of states, actions, and rewards for episode `$i$` of task `$k$` (spanning `$T$` time steps), and `$G_{k,i}$` is the episode return (total cumulative reward). The output `$y_{k,i}$` is the reflection text — a natural language diagnosis of failure causes and a proposed corrective plan.

**What the reflection prompt looks like in practice.** The paper provides the full reflection prompt template in Appendix E.2. For HotPotQA, it begins:

> "You are an advanced reasoning agent that can improve based on self reflection. You will be given a previous reasoning trial in which you were given access to an Docstore API environment and a question to answer. You were unsuccessful in answering the question either because you guessed the wrong answer with Finish[<answer>], or you used up your set number of reasoning steps. In a few sentences, Diagnose a possible reason for failure and devise a new, concise, high level plan that aims to mitigate the same failure. Use complete sentences."

This is followed by few-shot demonstrations showing examples of good reflections, and then the actual trajectory of the failed attempt, ending with "Reflection:" to prompt Mr to generate.

**How the reflection is used.** The generated reflection text `$y_{k,i}$` is appended to the actor's prompt on the next attempt `$i+1$` for the same task, under the "Memory for the task below:" section, labeled with the trial number. On each subsequent retry, prior reflections accumulate — the actor sees the current task's full history of failed attempts and their associated reflections. This accumulation of long-term memory is what enables the agent to avoid repeating mistakes: if trial 0's reflection says "I got stuck in a loop examining stoveburner 1 instead of heating the mug," and the actor on trial 1 again starts examining, the combined weight of that reflection plus the new trajectory context may prompt a different action.

**Design choice — why a separate retrospective model rather than a single self-reflective actor?** The paper's approach contrasts with Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023b), which uses the same LLM as both the actor and the reflector. The Retroformer design separates these roles for two reasons. First, it allows the reflector to be optimized specifically for credit assignment without risking degradation of the actor's general reasoning capabilities (which are frozen and preserved). Second, it makes the reflector a much smaller model (7B parameters vs. 175B+ for GPT-3) that can be fine-tuned on modest hardware — the paper reports using A100 40GB GPUs with 4-bit quantization and LoRA adapters adding only 0.53M–2.25M trainable parameters.

**Design choice — why LongChat-7b-16k as the base for Mr?** The paper selects LongChat-7b-16k because of its 16k context window. The reflection prompt must include the complete trajectory of a multi-step episode, which can be thousands of tokens long (multiple search queries with lengthy Wikipedia observations, or many navigation steps in AlfWorld). A model with a standard 2048-token context window would need to truncate the trajectory, potentially omitting the critical steps where the failure occurred. The 16k window ensures the full episode history fits.

#### Memory Architecture: Short-Term, Long-Term, and Replay

The memory system is what enables the Retroformer agent to carry information across time steps within an episode and across episodes. The paper describes three distinct memory mechanisms.

**Short-term memory.** This is the trajectory history `$\tau_i$` of the current episode `$i$`. As the agent takes actions and receives observations, each step's `(thought, action, observation)` tuple is appended to the actor's prompt. This is the standard approach in ReAct-style agents — the actor "remembers" what it just did by reading it in the prompt. The paper notes that this is the only way to bestow "state" on a stateless LLM: "all the previous state descriptions and actions [are concatenated] into the prompt so that LLM as a way to bestow 'state' to the LLM." The limitation, acknowledged in Appendix A, is that as the trajectory grows longer, "the prompt runs out of spaces" — the context window fills up.

**Long-term memory.** This is the set of reflection responses `$\{y_{k,1}, y_{k,2}, \ldots, y_{k,i-1}\}$` from prior failed attempts on the same task. These are appended to the actor prompt under a dedicated "Memory for the task below:" section, each labeled with its trial number. Unlike short-term memory, which is specific to the current episode, long-term memory persists across retry attempts. The paper describes this as enabling the agent to "exploit past experiences within a specific environment and task, thereby avoiding repetitive errors." The long-term memory grows with each retry — on the 4th attempt, the actor sees reflections from attempts 0, 1, 2, and 3.

**Replay buffer (`$\mathcal{D}_{\text{RL}}$`).** This is a persistent dataset stored locally that collects `(reflection instruction prompt, reflection response, episode return)` triples across all training tasks and all trials. The paper formally defines it as:

$$\mathcal{D}_{\text{RL}} = \{ (x_{k,i}, y_{k,i}, G_{k,i}) \}$$

where `$x_{k,i}$` is the reflection instruction prompt (trajectory + return), `$y_{k,i}$` is the reflection response generated by Mr, and `$G_{k,i}$` is the episode return. This replay buffer serves a fundamentally different purpose from short-term and long-term memory: it is not used during task execution at all. Instead, it is the training dataset for fine-tuning the retrospective model via PPO.

**What makes the replay buffer special — cross-task generalization.** The paper emphasizes that the replay buffer contains data from "multiple environments and tasks" (Section 1) and enables the retrospective model to learn "not only exploits lessons learned over failed trials in the current task, but also explores by learning from success in other related tasks." This is crucial: the retrospective model is not trained per-task, but learns a general credit assignment and reflection-generation skill from thousands of examples across the training set. When it encounters a new validation task, it can draw on patterns learned from similar failures in training tasks.

The paper reports collecting **3,383 reflection samples** for HotPotQA (by running the base rollout policy for N=3 trials on 3,000 training tasks, of which 1,084 instruction-response pairs had positive ratings), **523 reflection samples** for AlfWorld, and **267 reflection samples** for WebShop. These are modest dataset sizes by modern standards, suggesting that the retrospective model can learn useful credit assignment patterns from relatively few examples — likely because it starts from a strong pre-trained base (LongChat-7b) that already has general language understanding and reasoning capabilities.

#### Reward Shaping: Environment-Specific Reward Functions

Rather than using a uniform binary success/failure signal across all environments, the paper employs environment-specific shaped rewards when possible. The details are in Appendix C.3.

**HotPotQA — F1 score.** For question answering, the reward is the F1 score between the generated answer and the ground truth answer. The computation: after removing stopwords from both answers, precision is the number of common tokens divided by the number of generated answer tokens, and recall is the number of common tokens divided by the number of ground truth answer tokens. The F1 score is `$2 \times \text{precision} \times \text{recall} / (\text{precision} + \text{recall})$`. This provides a continuous reward in `$[0, 1]$` that captures partial correctness — an answer that gets some words right but misses others receives partial credit, providing a richer training signal for Mr than binary success/failure.

**AlfWorld — binary success.** The paper uses "the binary success (1) and failure of the tasks at the end of episode." AlfWorld tasks are evaluated on whether the goal state is achieved (e.g., is the mug heated and in the coffeemachine?), which is inherently binary. There is no partial credit for getting close.

**WebShop — product matching score.** The reward function is:

$$r = \frac{r_{\text{type}} \cdot |U_{\text{att}} \cap Y_{\text{att}}| + |U_{\text{opt}} \cap Y_{\text{opt}}| + \mathbb{1}[y_{\text{price}} \leq u_{\text{price}}]}{|U_{\text{att}}| + |U_{\text{opt}}| + 1}$$

where `$y$` is the product chosen by the agent, `$Y_{\text{att}}$` and `$Y_{\text{opt}}$` are its attributes and options, `$U_{\text{att}}$` and `$U_{\text{opt}}$` are the desired attributes and options from the user instruction, `$\mathbb{1}[y_{\text{price}} \leq u_{\text{price}}]$` is an indicator for whether the chosen product is within budget, and `$r_{\text{type}}$` is a type-matching heuristic score. The type reward `$r_{\text{type}} = \text{TextMatch}(\bar{y}, \bar{y}^*)$` is based on text matching to assign a low reward when the chosen and target products have similar attributes but are obviously different types — the paper gives the example of "butter" vs. "plant-based meat," which may both have attributes like "cruelty-free" and "non-GMO" but are fundamentally different product categories. This prevents the agent from getting high reward for matching attributes on the wrong type of product.

**Why shaped rewards matter for training Mr.** The rating function (described next) uses differences in episode returns between consecutive trials. A binary reward provides only three possible rating values: `$-1$` (success then failure), `$0$` (no change), or `$+1$` (failure then success). With shaped rewards, the ratings can capture subtle improvements — an answer that goes from F1=0.2 to F1=0.6 receives a rating of +0.4, providing a more nuanced training signal that helps Mr learn which types of reflections produce partial improvements even if they don't immediately achieve full success.

#### The Rating Mechanism: Converting Episode Returns to Reflection Quality Scores

The rating mechanism is the linchpin that converts environment feedback into a training signal for the retrospective model. It is defined in Section 4.2 and illustrated in Figure 2(b).

**The rating function.** The rating of a reflection instruction-response pair `$(x_{k,i}, y_{k,i})$` is:

$$r(x_{k,i}, y_{k,i}) \triangleq G_{k,i+1} - G_{k,i}$$

where `$G_{k,i}$` is the episode return from trial `$i$` on task `$k$` (the trial where the reflection was generated), and `$G_{k,i+1}$` is the episode return from the *next* trial `$i+1$` on the same task (the trial where the actor used the augmented prompt containing that reflection).

**What this equation computes:** it measures how much the agent's performance improved (or degraded) after using the reflection `$y_{k,i}$`. If the next trial performed better than the current trial, `$\Delta G > 0$`, indicating the reflection was helpful. If performance stayed the same, `$\Delta G \approx 0$`, indicating the reflection provided no benefit. If performance got worse, `$\Delta G < 0$`, indicating the reflection was harmful (like the example in Figure 1 where the frozen model's reflection caused the agent to repeat the same incorrect search strategy).

**Why this form — the causal attribution assumption.** The paper provides a specific justification for using this difference as the rating:

> "Because the actor is a frozen LM and the temperature is low as default (Yao et al., 2023), the injected randomness that leads to differences in returns `$\Delta G_{k,i} = G_{k,i+1} - G_{k,i}$` are mostly from the reflection responses `$y_{k,i}$`."

In other words, the paper argues that since the actor generates deterministically (T=0) and the environment dynamics are (mostly) deterministic for these text-based tasks, the only thing that changes between trial `$i$` and trial `$i+1$` is the reflection content added to the prompt. Therefore, any difference in performance can be causally attributed to the quality of the reflection. This is a strong assumption — it requires that the environment does not have significant stochasticity and that the actor's deterministic output is fully determined by the prompt — but it is plausible for the environments studied, where search results are deterministic (Wikipedia pages don't change between trials) and action effects are predictable.

**How ratings are collected in practice.** The offline data collection procedure (Algorithm 1 in Appendix C.1) describes the process:

1. For each unsuccessful task `$j$` in the training set, the base retrospective model generates two candidate reflection responses `$y^{(1)}_{k,j}$` and `$y^{(2)}_{k,j}$` by sampling with temperature `$t_s = 0.9$`.
2. Each candidate reflection is used to augment the actor prompt, and the actor runs another episode, yielding returns `$G^{(1)}_{k,i+1}$` and `$G^{(2)}_{k,i+1}$`.
3. The rating for each candidate is computed as `$r(x_{k,i}, y_{k,i}) = G_{k,i+1} - G_{k,i}$`.
4. The response with the higher rating is labeled as the "accepted" response and the lower-rated response is labeled as the "rejected" response. These preference pairs are used to train a reward model (Step 2 of Algorithm 1).

**Why use preference pairs rather than raw ratings for reward model training?** The paper follows the standard RLHF pipeline (Ouyang et al., 2022) where a reward model is trained to predict which of two responses is preferred given the same instruction, rather than to predict the exact scalar rating. This is because relative preferences are more robust than absolute scores — it is easier to learn that response A is better than response B than to learn the exact difference in returns, especially when returns are noisy. The trained reward model then provides a learned reward signal during PPO training that generalizes beyond the exact pairs seen during data collection.

**The offline training advantage.** The paper frames offline training as a key benefit: "we collected the dataset `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{RL}}$` by rolling out a base policy... in the tasks in the training sets for N trials and compute the ratings. We apply the standard RLHF pipeline to fine-tune the retrospective model offline before evaluating the agent in the validation tasks." This means the expensive process of running multiple trials per task to collect ratings happens once, before deployment. At deployment time on new tasks, the fine-tuned retrospective model generates reflections without needing to roll out comparison trials — it has already learned what constitutes a helpful reflection.

#### Policy Gradient Optimization: PPO Training of the Retrospective Model

The training procedure follows the three-step RLHF pipeline adapted for the retrospective model setting. The paper provides implementation details in Appendix C.1 and the algorithm in Algorithm 1.

**Step 1: Offline data collection.** As described above, the base policy (frozen actor + initialized retrospective model from LongChat-7b-16k) is rolled out on training tasks for `$N = 3$` trials each. For each unsuccessful task, two candidate reflection responses are generated and rated by rolling out the next trial with each. The data — instruction prompts, accepted/rejected response pairs, and ratings — is stored in `$\mathcal{D}_{\text{RL}}$`.

**Step 2: Reward model training.** A reward model is trained to classify accepted vs. rejected responses given the instruction prompt. The paper uses the `RewardTrainer` from the `trl` library with the following hyperparameters: learning rate `$2.5 \times 10^{-5}$`, batch size 32, maximum 20,000 training steps. This reward model is a modified LongChat-7b-16k with a regression head that outputs a scalar score. It serves as a learned proxy for the true rating function, enabling PPO to optimize for response quality without needing to roll out the environment during training.

**Step 3: Proximal policy optimization (PPO) fine-tuning.** The retrospective model is fine-tuned using the `PPOTrainer` from `trl` to maximize the reward model's score while staying close to a reference model (the frozen, pre-fine-tuning retrospective model). The PPO objective is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{RL}}} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \text{LLM}^{\text{RL}}_{\phi}(x)} \left[ r_{\theta}(x, y) - \beta \log \frac{\text{LLM}^{\text{RL}}_{\phi}(y|x)}{\text{LLM}^{\text{Ref}}(y|x)} \right]$$

where `$x$` is a reflection instruction prompt sampled from the replay buffer, `$y$` is a reflection response generated by the current retrospective model `$\text{LLM}^{\text{RL}}_{\phi}$` (parameterized by `$\phi$`), `$r_{\theta}(x, y)$` is the learned reward model's score for that response, `$\text{LLM}^{\text{Ref}}$` is the frozen reference model (the retrospective model before RL fine-tuning), and `$\beta$` is a KL penalty coefficient.

**What this equation computes:** the objective has two terms. The first term `$r_{\theta}(x, y)$` is the expected reward — we want to generate responses that the reward model scores highly (i.e., reflections that would lead to improved episode returns). The second term `$-\beta \log \frac{\text{LLM}^{\text{RL}}_{\phi}(y|x)}{\text{LLM}^{\text{Ref}}(y|x)}$` is the negative KL divergence between the current policy and the reference policy — it penalizes the fine-tuned model for deviating too far from its initial behavior.

**Why this form — the KL penalty.** The KL penalty is a standard component of RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) that prevents the fine-tuned model from "reward hacking" — generating responses that maximize the learned reward model but are nonsensical or degenerate. Without the KL penalty, the model might learn to output specific tokens or phrases that the reward model associates with high scores but that don't actually help the actor improve. The KL penalty anchors the model near its pre-trained behavior, allowing it to improve within a "trust region" while preserving its general language capabilities. The paper does not specify the exact value of `$\beta$` used.

**Why the offline training setup is necessary.** An alternative would be to train Mr online — after each trial, immediately update its weights using the rating from the next trial. The paper opts for offline training for practical reasons: rolling out the environment (which involves API calls to GPT-3/GPT-4 and sometimes web search or simulation) is slow and potentially expensive. By collecting data once and training offline, the expensive environment interaction is decoupled from the gradient updates, enabling the use of standard, efficient RLHF training pipelines. The trade-off is that the training data is off-policy — it was collected by the base retrospective model, not the model being trained. The paper does not discuss or address the off-policy nature of the data, which could limit how much improvement is possible (the reward model and PPO objective are trained on responses from the old policy, not the improved one).

**Supervised fine-tuning as a warm-start.** The paper notes (Appendix C.1): "we first run supervised fine-tuning trainer on the samples with positive ratings for 2 epochs and then the RLHF pipeline." This means before PPO training, Mr is fine-tuned via standard language modeling on the subset of reflection responses that received positive ratings. The hyperparameters for this supervised fine-tuning step are: learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`, batch size 32, maximum 5,000 steps. This warm-start ensures the model has a reasonable starting point before RL optimization begins — it learns to imitate the "good" reflections from the training data, and then PPO refines this behavior to maximize the learned reward signal.

**Full training hyperparameters.** The PPO fine-tuning uses: learning rate `$1.4 \times 10^{-5}$`, maximum 20,000 steps, output maximum length 128 tokens, batch size 64, gradient accumulation steps 8, and PPO epochs 4. The retrospective model is fine-tuned using 4-bit quantized LoRA adapters with rank `$r = 1$` (0.53M trainable parameters, 0.015% of Llama-7b) or `$r = 4$` (2.25M trainable parameters). The use of 4-bit quantization and LoRA is a practical choice — it allows fine-tuning a 7B parameter model on a single A100 40GB GPU by drastically reducing memory requirements.

**Best-of-N sampling at inference.** During online execution (evaluation on validation tasks), the paper uses "best-of-n sampler, with the scores evaluated by the learned reward model from RLHF pipeline (Ouyang et al., 2022), for generating better retrospective responses in each trial." This means that instead of generating a single reflection, Mr generates `$n$` candidate reflections, the reward model scores each, and the highest-scoring one is selected and appended to the actor prompt. This provides additional robustness at test time — if one generated reflection happens to be poor, the reward model can filter it out. The paper does not specify the value of `$n$` used.

#### Putting It All Together: The Full Retroformer Algorithm

Algorithm 1 in Appendix C.1 provides the complete pseudocode. The algorithm has three phases:

**Phase 1 — Offline data collection (lines 2–13):**
- For each of `$K$` source environments (the training tasks), run `$N = 3$` trials with the base policy (frozen actor + initialized retrospective model).
- For unsuccessful tasks, sample two candidate reflection responses with temperature `$t_s = 0.9$`.
- Roll out the next episode with each candidate and compute ratings `$r(x, y) = G_{i+1} - G_i$`.
- Label the higher-rated response as "accepted" and the lower as "rejected."

**Phase 2 — Reward model learning (line 14):**
- Train a reward model using `RewardTrainer` to classify accepted vs. rejected responses given instructions.

**Phase 3 — Policy gradient fine-tuning (line 15):**
- Plug in the trained reward model and use `PPOTrainer` to fine-tune the retrospective model for generating reflection responses with higher ratings, with a KL penalty to prevent divergence from the reference model.

**Online execution (not in the algorithm but described in text):**
- For each new task, run the actor with the fine-tuned retrospective model.
- After each episode, Mr generates a reflection (using best-of-N sampling with the learned reward model for selection).
- The reflection is appended to long-term memory for the next retry.
- Repeat for up to `$N$` retry attempts.

The paper emphasizes that the retrospective model is generic: "our approach is not limited to enhancing the retrospective model alone; it can be applied to fine-tune other components within the agent system architecture, such as the memory and summarization module, or the actor prompt." The key principle — treat the frozen LLM as part of the environment and optimize a text-generating policy component with PPO — could be applied to any prompt-augmentation module in the agent pipeline.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Inaccessible Actor LLMs as Part of the Environment to Enable Gradient-Based Learning

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is **not** a new RL algorithm or a new agent architecture — it is a theoretical reduction that makes gradient-based optimization possible in a setting where it was previously considered impractical. The dominant assumption in the field, embodied by approaches like ReAct (Yao et al., 2023) and Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023), was that if you cannot access or fine-tune the actor LLM's parameters (because it's behind an API or too large for local hardware), you are limited to in-context learning and prompt engineering — essentially, you can only modify the inputs to a frozen model, not optimize any component with gradients from environment rewards.

Retroformer overturns this assumption with a clean theoretical observation formalized in Section 3: if the frozen actor LLM's parameters are fixed and its output distribution is deterministic (or nearly so, with T=0), then the actor is functionally just another deterministic transformation in the environment's state transition dynamics. Composing the actor with the actual environment yields a new "effective environment" `$\mathcal{T}' = \mathcal{T}(S, \bullet) \circ \mathcal{L}$` where the action space becomes the space of prompts `$\mathcal{X}$` (the output of the retrospective model) rather than the space of environment actions `$\mathcal{A}$`. At this point, the problem reduces to standard RL: the retrospective model is a policy that outputs prompts, and the effective environment handles everything downstream. All the machinery of policy gradient optimization — PPO, reward models, KL regularization — applies without modification, without ever needing to backpropagate through the actor LLM.

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental improvement. Prior work had implicitly treated the actor LLM as the policy to be optimized, creating an impasse when that policy was inaccessible. Retroformer's reduction shows that the learnable policy can be a *different* model entirely — one that operates at the meta-level of prompt construction — and that the actor can be safely absorbed into the environment dynamics without loss of generality. The practical consequence is that the learnable component (Mr) can be much smaller and locally fine-tunable — LongChat-7b with 4-bit quantization and LoRA adapters adding only 0.53M–2.25M trainable parameters — while the powerful but frozen actor (GPT-3/GPT-4 with 175B+ parameters) is treated as infrastructure. This inverts the prevailing wisdom that RL for language agents requires training the largest model in the loop.

The significance extends beyond the specific Retroformer architecture. The paper notes in its conclusion (Section 6) that this principle is general: "our approach is not limited to enhancing the retrospective model alone; it can be applied to fine-tune other components within the agent system architecture, such as the memory and summarization module, or the actor prompt." Any prompt-augmentation component can be treated as a learnable policy operating on the effective environment that includes the frozen actor. This opens the door to a family of "RL for prompt engineering" methods that the paper initiates but does not exhaust.

The SAC baseline in Table 2 provides a telling negative result that underscores the importance of this reframing. SAC attempted to apply online RL directly to the actor by mean-pooling text embeddings as continuous actions — a naive approach that achieved only 27% on HotPotQA with 4 retries (vs. Retroformer's 54%) and 59.7% on AlfWorld (vs. 100%). This failure demonstrates that the straightforward approach of treating the actor as the policy to optimize does not work well in practice, even when parameters are accessible. The Retroformer reduction succeeds precisely because it circumvents the need to optimize the actor's internal representations, instead optimizing the *prompt* — a textual interface that the actor was pre-trained to consume.

---

### Innovation 2: The Return Difference as a Self-Supervised Credit Assignment Signal for Reflection Quality

The paper's second distinctive contribution is the design of a training signal for the retrospective model that directly encodes *reflection quality* without requiring human annotation or explicit supervision of what constitutes a good reflection. The rating function `$r(x_{k,i}, y_{k,i}) = G_{k,i+1} - G_{k,i}$` — the difference in episode returns between the trial where a reflection was generated and the next trial where it was used — is deceptively simple but represents a novel form of self-supervised credit assignment for meta-level agent improvement.

To appreciate why this is distinctive, consider the alternatives. The most direct approach to training a reflection generator would be supervised learning: collect human annotations of "good" and "bad" reflections, and train Mr to imitate the good ones. This is expensive, subjective, and environment-specific — what constitutes a good reflection for HotPotQA question answering might differ from what works for AlfWorld embodied tasks. Alternatively, one could use the actor's eventual success or failure as a binary label (successful episodes produce "good" reflections), but this conflates the quality of the reflection with the difficulty of the task: a reflection might be excellent but the task might be too hard for the actor to solve regardless, yielding a misleading negative label.

The return difference approach solves both problems simultaneously. By using the *change* in performance rather than the absolute performance level, it controls for task difficulty — the rating compares the same task before and after the reflection, so the task's inherent difficulty factors out. By using shaped rewards (F1 score for HotPotQA, product matching score for WebShop) rather than binary success/failure, it captures *partial improvements*: a reflection that helps the agent go from F1=0.2 to F1=0.6 receives a rating of +0.4, even if it doesn't achieve full success. This provides a more nuanced training signal that helps Mr learn which types of reflections produce improvements, even when those improvements are incremental.

The causal attribution argument that supports this rating function is explicit and, within the paper's experimental setup, well-justified: with the actor at temperature T=0 (deterministic generation) and deterministic environment dynamics, "the injected randomness that leads to differences in returns... are mostly from the reflection responses." By controlling for all other sources of variation, the paper isolates the reflection as the causal factor, making the return difference a valid proxy for reflection quality.

This is a **fundamental diagnostic concept** with implications beyond Retroformer. It establishes a general principle for training meta-level agent improvement modules: if you can control for other sources of variation (by freezing the base policy and minimizing environment stochasticity), then the performance delta between consecutive trials provides a self-supervised signal for the quality of whatever changed between those trials. This principle could apply to training better memory summarizers, better subgoal proposers, or better error detectors — any component that modifies the agent's behavior between attempts.

The evidence supporting this innovation is both quantitative and qualitative. Quantitatively, the PPO-trained retrospective model produces reflections that lead to faster improvement than frozen-LLM reflections across all three environments — the learning curves in Figure 4 (HotPotQA) and Figure 6 (AlfWorld, WebShop) show steeper initial slopes for Retroformer, indicating that the trained Mr generates reflections that are more effective at producing improvement on the next attempt. Qualitatively, Figure 5 provides a direct comparison: on the "Teen Titans spinoff" question, the frozen LM's reflection merely rephrases the failed action sequence ("I should have searched for Lollipop Chainsaw first..."), while the reinforced reflection correctly identifies the root cause (confusing the original series with the spinoff) and proposes an actionable correction ("focus on spinoff series and... find the answer in the previous actions and observations with the search results of 'Tara Strong'"). This demonstrates that the return-difference training signal successfully teaches Mr to perform better credit assignment than the frozen base model.

### Innovation 3: Demonstrating That Learned Prompt Engineering Outperforms Frozen Prompt Engineering — With Sharp Environment-Dependent Boundaries

The paper provides the first systematic evidence that **learned prompt engineering through gradient-based optimization substantially outperforms frozen prompt engineering** for language agents, while simultaneously establishing clear boundaries on where this approach is most effective. This is not merely a performance result — it is a diagnostic finding about the nature of the credit assignment problem in different environment types.

Before Retroformer, the dominant paradigm for improving LLM agent performance was prompt engineering: carefully designing few-shot examples, instruction formats, and memory structures to elicit better behavior from a frozen model. Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023) extended this to iterative prompt engineering by having the LLM generate its own prompt augmentations (self-reflections), but those augmentations came from the same frozen model — essentially, the model was engineering its own prompts using only its pre-trained knowledge, without any mechanism for that prompt-engineering skill to improve through environment feedback. The implicit assumption was that a sufficiently capable LLM could generate useful self-reflections out of the box, without task-specific training.

Retroformer's results challenge this assumption strongly — but not uniformly. On HotPotQA, Retroformer with GPT-4 achieves 54% success with 4 retries versus Reflexion's 52%, and the improvement is concentrated in early trials (Figure 4) where the learned retrospective model's reflections are qualitatively better at diagnosing root causes. On AlfWorld, the gap is dramatic: Retroformer reaches 100% success within 3 retries while Reflexion plateaus at 84.33% (GPT-3) or 85.07% (GPT-4) with 4 retries (Table 2). The AlfWorld improvement is particularly revealing because it suggests that credit assignment in embodied task environments — where failure modes involve getting stuck in loops (examining the stoveburner repeatedly instead of heating the mug) — is especially difficult for frozen LLMs, which lack the specialized training to recognize these environment-specific failure patterns. The learned retrospective model, having seen hundreds of loop-failure examples during training, learns to diagnose them reliably.

However, the WebShop results tell a different story: Retroformer improves success from 35% to 36% (GPT-3, 4 retries) or 44% to 46% (GPT-4, 4 retries) — a modest 1–2 percentage point gain over Reflexion. The paper's interpretation of this boundary (Section 5.3) is insightful: "web browsing requires a significant amount of exploration with more precise search queries, if compared with HotPotQA. The results probably indicate that the verbal feedback approach (Reflexion, Retroformer) is not an optimal method for this environment." In WebShop, the primary challenge is **exploration** — finding the right product among many candidates by formulating precise search queries and navigating product pages. A verbal reflection that says "next time, search for X instead of Y" provides limited help if the agent's fundamental difficulty is generating good search queries in the first place, or if the search space is so large that knowing what went wrong on one path doesn't narrow the space for the next attempt.

This is a **diagnostic finding** with significant implications: it maps out where gradient-based prompt optimization helps most (environments where failures stem from identifiable reasoning errors or action selection mistakes that can be diagnosed from trajectory structure) and where it helps least (environments where failures stem from exploration difficulty or where the actor's base capability — generating precise queries, navigating complex interfaces — is the bottleneck that textual guidance cannot overcome). The paper does not frame it this way explicitly, but the results implicitly establish a **credit-assignment-amenability spectrum**: HotPotQA (highly amenable — reasoning traces reveal decision points clearly), AlfWorld (highly amenable — action loops are visible in the trajectory), and WebShop (less amenable — the bottleneck is query formulation quality, which is harder to diagnose from a trajectory of mostly irrelevant search results).

This finding distinguishes Retroformer from work that claims universal improvement — it provides a nuanced, evidence-backed picture of *when* learned prompt engineering matters and *when* the verbal feedback approach itself (not just the learning) is the limiting factor. The paper is honest about this boundary, acknowledging it rather than overselling, which strengthens the credibility of the positive results in the other environments.

### Innovation 4: The Preferability of Small, Specialized, Fine-Tuned Reflectors Over Large, General-Purpose, Frozen Ones — A Scaling Inversion

The paper provides compelling evidence for what might be called a **scaling inversion** in language agent design: for the specific task of generating corrective reflections from agent trajectories, a small (7B parameter), fine-tuned language model trained on environment-specific data substantially outperforms a much larger (175B+ parameter) frozen general-purpose model. This finding challenges the default assumption in the LLM agent literature that larger models are always better, and that the best approach is to use the most capable available LLM for every component of the agent pipeline.

The evidence for this inversion is clearest in the comparison between Retroformer and Reflexion. Reflexion uses the same frozen LLM (GPT-3 or GPT-4) for both action generation and self-reflection. Retroformer uses the same frozen LLM for action generation but replaces the frozen reflector with a fine-tuned LongChat-7b model. The performance gap — 54% vs. 52% on HotPotQA with GPT-4 (4 retries), 100% vs. 84.33% on AlfWorld with GPT-3 (3 retries) — demonstrates that the fine-tuned smaller model generates reflections that are more effective at improving the actor's subsequent behavior, despite being roughly 25× smaller in parameter count.

Why does this inversion occur? The paper's qualitative analysis (Figure 5 and Figure 7) provides clues. The frozen GPT-3/GPT-4 reflections tend to **rephrase the trajectory** rather than **diagnose the root cause**. In the Teen Titans spinoff example (Figure 5), the frozen model's reflection essentially says "I should have done what I did, but better" — it rephrases the same search sequence without identifying that the error was conflating the spinoff series with the original. The fine-tuned model's reflection, in contrast, identifies the specific confusion ("I failed to find the Teen Titans spinoff series... I found Lollipop Chainsaw") and proposes a concrete fix ("focus on spinoff series and find the answer in the previous actions and observations"). Similarly, Figure 7 shows that the fine-tuned model spontaneously structures its output into "Reflection" and "New plan:" sections — an emergent behavior that makes the feedback more actionable, despite never being explicitly trained or prompted to use this format.

This phenomenon suggests that **credit assignment from agent trajectories is a specialized skill** that general-purpose pre-training does not adequately cover. A GPT-4 model trained on internet text, books, and code has seen plenty of examples of reasoning and problem-solving, but very few examples of diagnosing why a specific sequence of search API calls failed to answer a multi-hop question. The fine-tuned LongChat-7b model, trained on thousands of (trajectory, reflection, return-difference) triples from the target environments, develops this specialized skill through gradient-based optimization. The implication is that for meta-level agent components (reflectors, error detectors, subgoal proposers), **task-specific fine-tuning of a smaller model can be more effective than scaling up a general-purpose model**, because the skill being learned is narrow and environment-specific rather than broad and general.

This is an **incremental but important refinement** of the scaling narrative. It does not overturn the general principle that larger models are more capable at general tasks — the actor LLM remains the large frozen model, and the paper does not claim that a fine-tuned 7B model could replace GPT-4 as the actor. Rather, it carves out a specific role (reflection generation) where environment-specific optimization trumps general pre-training scale, and provides empirical evidence for when and why this inversion holds. The practical consequence is meaningful: organizations that cannot afford to fine-tune a 175B+ parameter model can still achieve strong agent improvement by fine-tuning a small model on the meta-level reflection task, using the return-difference signal as a self-supervised training objective.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on three open-source environments: **HotPotQA** (Yang et al., 2018) — a multi-hop question answering dataset where the agent searches Wikipedia to answer questions, using 100 validation tasks from the distractor dev split; **AlfWorld** (Shridhar et al., 2021) — a text-based embodied robotics environment with six task types (finding hidden objects, moving objects, manipulating objects with other objects), using 134 validation tasks as in Shinn et al. (2023); and **WebShop** (Yao et al., 2022) — a web browsing environment where the agent must find and purchase products matching user specifications, using 100 validation tasks. The training sets used for collecting reflection data are substantially larger: 3,000 tasks for HotPotQA, with corresponding training splits for AlfWorld and WebShop (exact training set sizes not specified for these two, but the collected reflection samples are 523 for AlfWorld and 267 for WebShop).

- **Base model(s).** The frozen actor model is **GPT-3** (`text-davinci-003`) and **GPT-4** for some experiments, both accessed via API with inaccessible parameters. The paper tests both to demonstrate Retroformer's agnosticism to the specific actor LLM. The retrospective model is initialized from **LongChat-7b-16k**, a Llama-7b variant fine-tuned for long-context (16k token) instruction following on ShareGPT data, chosen because it can handle the lengthy trajectory prompts needed for multi-step agent tasks. The retrospective model is fine-tuned with 4-bit quantized LoRA adapters of rank `r = 1` (0.53M trainable parameters, 0.015% of the full model) or `r = 4` (2.25M trainable parameters). The paper notes that LongChat-7b-16k was chosen because reflection prompts must include complete multi-step trajectories that can exceed standard 2048-token context windows.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **success rate** — the fraction of validation tasks for which the agent achieves the correct outcome. For HotPotQA, success means generating a final answer that matches the ground truth (graded using the F1-based reward function described in Appendix C.3). For AlfWorld, success means achieving the goal state within the episode (e.g., "heated mug placed in coffeemachine"). For WebShop, success means purchasing a product that matches the user's specifications within budget. The paper reports success rate as a function of the number of retry attempts (trials), typically 1 to 4 retries, showing learning curves that track improvement over consecutive attempts.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three baselines:
  1. **ReAct** (Yao et al., 2023): The state-of-the-art frozen language agent architecture that interleaves reasoning traces with actions but has no mechanism for learning from environment feedback. ReAct serves as a baseline for how the agent performs without using any environment feedback at all. The paper uses ReAct with 0 learnable parameters.
  2. **Reflexion** (Shinn et al., 2023): The state-of-the-art language agent architecture that uses verbal self-reflection from a frozen LLM to provide feedback across retry attempts. Reflexion converts environment rewards into textual summaries appended to the agent prompt, but does not use gradient-based learning. It serves as the primary baseline for evaluating whether gradient-based fine-tuning of the retrospective model (Retroformer) provides benefits over frozen-LLM reflection. Reflexion also has 0 learnable parameters.
  3. **SAC** (Haarnoja et al., 2018): Soft Actor-Critic, an online RL algorithm applied as a baseline to demonstrate the challenge of directly optimizing the actor with classical RL. SAC uses mean-pooled embeddings of generated text outputs as continuous actions (768-dimensional), with LoRA adapters (`r = 4`) on the action model instantiated from LongChat-16k. Hyperparameters: discount factor γ = 0.99, interpolation factor polyak = 0.995, learning rate = 0.01, entropy regularization α = 0.2, batch size = 8. SAC has 2.25M learnable parameters, making it comparable in trainable parameter count to Retroformer with `r = 4`.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The "compute budget" for fair comparison is measured in **number of retry attempts** (`#Retries`), with most results reported at `N = 1` and `N = 4` retries. The actor LM generates deterministically (temperature `T = 0`, `top_p = 1`) to isolate the effect of reflections from sampling randomness — the paper explicitly acknowledges that higher temperature would encourage exploration but would "obscure the impact of the proposed approaches, making it difficult to compare against existing baselines with T=0" (Appendix C.1). The retrospective model's generation during data collection uses temperature `t_s = 0.9` for sampling candidate reflections, and during evaluation uses best-of-N sampling with the learned reward model for selection. Training compute is not factored into the comparison — the retrospective model is trained offline on collected data before evaluation, so training cost is a one-time expense amortized across all validation tasks.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ explicit cross-validation or statistical significance testing. The evaluation protocol follows Shinn et al. (2023): fixed validation sets of 100 tasks (HotPotQA), 134 tasks (AlfWorld), and 100 tasks (WebShop). The offline training data is collected by rolling out the base policy on the training splits (separate from validation tasks) for `N = 3` trials each. The retrospective model is fine-tuned offline on this collected data, then evaluated on the held-out validation tasks. This is a standard train/test split protocol; the absence of cross-validation or confidence intervals means the reported success rates are point estimates without quantified uncertainty. For the data collection, the paper notes training set sizes of 3,000 tasks (HotPotQA, yielding 3,383 reflection samples with 1,084 having positive ratings), with smaller collections for AlfWorld (523 samples) and WebShop (267 samples).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Headline Performance: Retroformer vs. Baselines Across All Three Environments

Table 2 provides the comprehensive comparison across all three environments, base actor models, numbers of retries, and LoRA ranks. The headline results:

- **HotPotQA (100 validation tasks):** Retroformer with GPT-3 and LoRA `r = 4` achieves **54% success rate with 4 retries**, compared to ReAct at 34% (0 retries, no learning), Reflexion at 50% (4 retries, frozen reflection), and SAC at 27% (4 retries, online RL directly on the actor). With GPT-4, Retroformer reaches **54%** versus Reflexion's 52% and ReAct's 40%. The improvement over Reflexion is 4 percentage points with GPT-3 and 2 percentage points with GPT-4. Notably, with only a single retry (N=1), Retroformer with GPT-3 and LoRA `r = 4` achieves **48%**, already exceeding Reflexion's 50% at 4 retries, demonstrating faster learning from the first reflection.

- **AlfWorld (134 validation tasks):** This is where Retroformer's advantage is most dramatic. With GPT-3 and LoRA `r = 1`, Retroformer achieves **100% success rate within 4 retries** — the agent solves every single validation task. Reflexion plateaus at 84.33% (GPT-3) or 85.07% (GPT-4) with 4 retries. With GPT-4 and LoRA `r = 1`, Retroformer reaches **95.62% at N=1** and **100% at N=4**. Even with the minimal LoRA rank `r = 1` and a single retry, Retroformer achieves 93.28% (GPT-3) or 95.62% (GPT-4), substantially exceeding Reflexion's final performance after 4 retries. The SAC baseline reaches only 59.7% with 4 retries, demonstrating the ineffectiveness of naive online RL for this task. The improvement over Reflexion is approximately 15-16 percentage points in final success rate.

- **WebShop (100 validation tasks):** Improvements are more modest. With GPT-3 and 4 retries, Retroformer achieves **36%** (LoRA `r = 1`) or **36%** (LoRA `r = 4`), compared to Reflexion's 35% and ReAct's 33%. With GPT-4, Retroformer reaches **46%** (LoRA `r = 4`, 4 retries) versus Reflexion's 44% and ReAct's 42%. The improvement over Reflexion is 1-2 percentage points — consistent but small. The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly: "the performance improvement by Retroformer over the frozen baselines is observed but the improvements may be limited, when compared with HotPotQA and AlfWorld, with 4% improvement in success rate with 4 retries" (Section 5.3). The paper attributes this to the nature of web browsing, which "requires a significant amount of exploration with more precise search queries" and suggests "the verbal feedback approach (Reflexion, Retroformer) is not an optimal method for this environment, but our fine-tuning method still proves effective."

#### HotPotQA Learning Curves: Faster Improvement in Early Trials

Figure 4 shows the success rate as a function of episode ID (0 through 4) for Retroformer variants and baselines on the 100 HotPotQA distractor tasks. Key observations:

- **Retroformer with GPT-4 and LoRA `r = 4`** (top solid line) starts at approximately 51% on trial 0, rises sharply to approximately 53% on trial 1, and stabilizes around 54% for trials 2-4. This rapid initial improvement — gaining roughly 2 percentage points from the first reflection — demonstrates the effect of the fine-tuned retrospective model's initial reflection.
- **Reflexion with GPT-4** (dashed line with squares) starts similarly around 46% on trial 0, rises to approximately 49% on trial 1, and reaches approximately 52% by trial 4. The improvement is more gradual and plateaus lower.
- **The gap between Retroformer and Reflexion is largest in the first few trials**: at trial 1, Retroformer (GPT-4, `r = 4`) is approximately 4 percentage points ahead of Reflexion (GPT-4), while by trial 4 the gap narrows to approximately 2 percentage points. This supports the paper's claim that "our reinforced model provides the language agents with better reflection responses in early trials, which enables the agents to learn faster, while also achieving better performances in the end" (Section 5.3).
- **Retroformer with GPT-3** (dashed lines) performs lower overall than the GPT-4 variants but shows the same pattern: Retroformer (LoRA `r = 4`, GPT-3) reaches approximately 48% at trial 0 and 54% at trial 4, while Reflexion (GPT-3) goes from approximately 42% to 50%.
- **ReAct baselines** (flat lines at the bottom) show no improvement over trials — GPT-4 ReAct sits at 40% and GPT-3 ReAct at 34%, constant across all episodes, confirming that without any reflection mechanism, the frozen actor cannot improve through retries.

The paper also notes that Retroformer's 54% success rate with 4 trials "is better than the state-of-the-art 50% success rate reported in (Jang, 2023) that uses a much larger frozen language model, i.e., GPT-3 (model: text-davinci-003) as the reflection component" — emphasizing that a fine-tuned 7B reflector outperforms a frozen 175B reflector.

#### AlfWorld Learning Curves: Near-Perfect Performance with Minimal Retries

Figure 6(a) visualizes the success rate across trials for AlfWorld, broken out by base actor model and LoRA rank. The results are striking:

- **Retroformer with GPT-4 and LoRA `r = 4`**: starts at approximately 97.76% on trial 0 (N=1 in Table 2) and reaches 100% by trial 1-2. The learning curve is essentially flat at ceiling performance after the first reflection.
- **Retroformer with GPT-4 and LoRA `r = 1`**: starts at approximately 95.62% (N=1), reaches 100% by trial 2-3.
- **Retroformer with GPT-3 and LoRA `r = 4`**: starts at approximately 97.76% (N=1), reaches 100% by trial 2-3.
- **Retroformer with GPT-3 and LoRA `r = 1`**: starts at approximately 93.28% (N=1), reaches 100% by trial 3. This is the "weakest" Retroformer configuration but still achieves perfect success within the retry budget.
- **Reflexion with GPT-4**: starts around 81.34% (N=1), improves to 85.07% by N=4 — a gradual improvement that plateaus well below ceiling.
- **Reflexion with GPT-3**: starts around 76.87% (N=1), improves to 84.33% by N=4.
- **ReAct baselines**: GPT-4 at 77.61%, GPT-3 at 62.69%, with no improvement over trials.

The paper observes that "similar patterns are observed that the agent performs slightly better with more learnable parameters (`r = 4`) and that the improvements are mostly from early retries" (Section 5.3). The qualitative analysis reveals that "the reinforced retrospective model behaves like a summarization model of the prior failed plans and finds the differences of the prior plan with the task descriptions. With the permissible actions seen in the task instructions, this behavior effectively prevents repetitive failures and reduces search spaces." This explains why the AlfWorld improvement is so dramatic: AlfWorld failures typically involve getting stuck in action loops (repeatedly examining an object instead of using it), and the fine-tuned retrospective model learns to detect these loops and propose alternative action sequences, breaking the cycle.

#### WebShop Learning Curves: Modest but Consistent Gains

Figure 6(b) shows WebShop success rates. The curves are flatter and noisier than HotPotQA or AlfWorld:

- **All methods show gradual improvement** over trials, with Retroformer variants slightly outperforming Reflexion and ReAct.
- **With GPT-4**: Retroformer (`r = 4`) rises from approximately 43% (N=1) to 46% (N=4); Reflexion rises from approximately 42% to 44%; ReAct sits at 42%.
- **With GPT-3**: Retroformer (`r = 4`) rises from approximately 34% (N=1) to 36% (N=4); Reflexion rises from approximately 35% to 35% (essentially flat); ReAct sits at 33%.
- The separation between Retroformer and Reflexion is narrower than in the other environments, and the curves are more variable — the ordering between Retroformer (`r = 1`) and Retroformer (`r = 4`) is not monotonic in all cases.

The paper's interpretation (Section 5.3) is candid about the limitations: "This limitation was also observed in (Shinn et al., 2023) as web browsing requires a significant amount of exploration with more precise search queries, if compared with HotPotQA." The verbal feedback approach — whether from frozen or learned models — can only help if the actor's failure can be diagnosed from the trajectory and corrected through textual guidance. In WebShop, the core challenge is often that the actor generates insufficiently precise search queries (e.g., searching for broad terms that return many irrelevant products), and a reflection that says "be more specific in your search" may not provide enough actionable guidance to substantially change the actor's query-generation behavior, especially since the actor itself is frozen and was not trained to generate better queries from such feedback.

#### Effect of Actor Model Choice: GPT-3 vs. GPT-4

A consistent pattern across all three environments in Table 2: Retroformer provides improvements regardless of whether GPT-3 or GPT-4 serves as the frozen actor. However, the magnitude of improvement differs:

- On HotPotQA, Retroformer (LoRA `r = 4`, 4 retries) achieves 54% with GPT-3 and 54% with GPT-4 — the GPT-3 variant catches up to GPT-4's performance through more effective reflection guidance, despite starting from a lower base capability (ReAct: 34% vs. 40%).
- On AlfWorld, both GPT-3 and GPT-4 Retroformer variants reach 100% with 4 retries — the retrospective model compensates for GPT-3's lower base capability (ReAct: 62.69% vs. 77.61%) entirely.
- On WebShop, Retroformer with GPT-4 (46%) maintains a lead over GPT-3 (36%), suggesting the base actor's capability ceiling matters more in this environment.

This demonstrates that Retroformer's learned reflections are actor-agnostic — the retrospective model was trained on trajectories from GPT-3 (`text-davinci-003`) as the actor, yet its reflections improve performance for both GPT-3 and GPT-4 at test time. The paper highlights this as evidence of the framework's plug-in nature: "the agnostic nature of Retroformer makes it a flexible plug-in module for various types of cloud-based LLMs, such as OpenAI GPT or Google Bard" (Section 1).

#### Effect of LoRA Rank: `r = 1` vs. `r = 4`

Table 2 includes two Retroformer variants with different LoRA adapter ranks: `r = 1` (0.53M parameters) and `r = 4` (2.25M parameters). The differences are small but consistent:

- On HotPotQA (GPT-3, 4 retries): `r = 1` achieves 53% vs. `r = 4` achieves 54% — a 1 percentage point gain for 4.25× more parameters.
- On HotPotQA (GPT-4, 4 retries): both achieve 54% — no difference.
- On AlfWorld (GPT-3, N=1): `r = 1` achieves 93.28% vs. `r = 4` achieves 97.76% — a 4.5 percentage point gain with the higher rank in the single-retry setting, but both reach 100% by 4 retries.
- On WebShop, the differences are within 1-2 percentage points and not monotonic.

The paper does not draw strong conclusions about LoRA rank scaling, but the results suggest that even the minimal rank (`r = 1`, only 0.015% of the base model's parameters) captures sufficient capacity for the credit assignment task. The marginal benefit of increasing the rank is modest and most visible in the single-retry setting where the reflection must be as informative as possible from the first attempt.

#### SAC Baseline: Why Direct RL on the Actor Fails

The SAC results in Table 2 warrant attention as an important negative result:

- HotPotQA: 27% with N=1, 27% with N=4 — **no improvement from additional trials** despite online RL updates.
- AlfWorld: 58.95% (N=1), 59.7% (N=4) — essentially flat.
- WebShop: 30% (N=1), 30% (N=4) — no improvement.

The SAC agent uses mean-pooled embeddings of generated text as continuous actions in a 768-dimensional space, applied to a LoRA-adapted LongChat-16k model with 2.25M trainable parameters (comparable to Retroformer `r = 4`). The complete failure to improve, even on AlfWorld where the task structure is highly amenable to RL-style learning (as demonstrated by Retroformer's 100% success), demonstrates that treating text generation as a continuous action space and applying off-the-shelf RL algorithms is fundamentally ineffective for these environments. The paper includes this baseline to motivate the Retroformer approach: rather than trying to optimize the actor's internal representations through embeddings (which SAC attempts), it is more effective to optimize the prompt-engineering component through policy gradient on the discrete text outputs of a separate model.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Structured reflection format (emergent behavior):** The paper reports (Appendix D, Figure 7) that the PPO-fine-tuned retrospective model spontaneously structures its output into two clearly separated sections — "Reflection" and "New plan:" — despite never being explicitly trained or prompted to use this format. The frozen model's reflections interleave diagnosis and planning in a single paragraph. Figure 7 provides a side-by-side comparison: the reinforced response contains a paragraph diagnosing the issue ("I think the issue might have been with the search. I was not able to find the name 'Miller' in the paragraph...") followed by a clearly labeled "New Plan:" section with actionable steps ("I will search for 'Miller's wife' instead of 'James Henry Miller's wife'..."). The frozen model's response mixes both aspects into one paragraph without clear separation. The paper also notes that the reinforced response "removes the messy, irrelevant 'Next trial:' content in the end for cleaner format, which may very likely result from LLM hallucination." This is an emergent property of the PPO training — the reward model appears to prefer structured, hallucination-free outputs, and the policy gradient fine-tuning discovers this format as a reliable way to achieve higher scores.

**Preference-based reward model training rather than direct regression:** The data collection procedure (Algorithm 1, Appendix C.1) generates two candidate reflection responses per unsuccessful task, rolls out the next episode with each, and labels the one with the higher return difference as "accepted" and the other as "rejected." This preference-based approach (rather than training the reward model to predict the exact return difference) follows the standard RLHF pipeline (Ouyang et al., 2022). The paper does not ablate this choice against alternative reward model training strategies (e.g., direct regression on the scalar return difference), so the benefit of preference-based training is assumed from prior work rather than demonstrated within this study.

**Supervised fine-tuning warm-start before PPO:** The training pipeline includes a supervised fine-tuning step on the subset of reflection samples with positive ratings (2 epochs, learning rate `1e-5`, batch size 32) before the RLHF pipeline begins. The paper does not ablate this warm-start step — there is no experiment showing Retroformer performance without the supervised pre-training phase. This makes it unclear whether the PPO fine-tuning is primarily responsible for the improvements, or whether supervised fine-tuning on positive-rated reflections alone would achieve similar results. Given that only 1,084 of 3,383 HotPotQA samples had positive ratings (approximately 32%), the warm-start provides a non-trivial initial policy before PPO optimization.

**Best-of-N sampling at inference with learned reward model selection:** During evaluation, Retroformer uses best-of-N sampling (with N unspecified) where the learned reward model scores candidate reflections and selects the highest-scoring one. The paper does not ablate this against single-sample generation, making it unclear how much of the performance gain comes from the fine-tuned generator versus the reward-model-based selection at test time. This is significant because the reward model was trained on preference pairs from the base policy's reflections — if it has learned a robust quality assessment function, it could potentially improve performance even with a weaker generator by filtering out poor reflections. The interaction between generator quality and selection quality is not disentangled.

**Training on multiple environments jointly:** The paper collects reflection data from all three environments for training the retrospective model (3,383 HotPotQA + 523 AlfWorld + 267 WebShop = 4,173 total samples), but does not report whether the retrospective model is trained jointly on all environments or separately per environment. If trained jointly, the model might benefit from cross-environment transfer (e.g., loop-detection skills learned from AlfWorld trajectories could help with HotPotQA where the agent also gets stuck in repetitive search patterns). The paper does not ablate single-environment vs. multi-environment training, so any cross-task transfer benefits are unquantified.

**Reflection prompt construction includes the episode return `G_{k,i}`:** The reflection prompt provided to Mr includes the episode return (e.g., "Answer is INCORRECT" for binary failure, or a scalar F1 score). The paper does not ablate whether including vs. excluding the explicit return in the prompt affects reflection quality. In principle, the return information is redundant with the trajectory itself (the final observation indicates success or failure), but explicitly including it might help Mr focus on the outcome. If Mr can infer success/failure from the trajectory alone, this design choice may not matter — but this is not tested.

**Deterministic actor (T=0) to isolate reflection effects:** The paper sets the actor temperature to zero to ensure that "the injected randomness that leads to differences in returns... are mostly from the reflection responses" (Section 4.2). While this strengthens the causal attribution argument, it also means the experiments do not demonstrate Retroformer's effectiveness in a stochastic generation setting where the actor might benefit from exploration. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Appendix C.1: "We acknowledge that setting a higher temperature value can encourage exploration but it can obscure the impact of the proposed approaches." This is a deliberate experimental control choice, not an oversight, but it means the results may not generalize to settings where sampling diversity is important for the agent's success.

**Offline data collected with base policy, not with the fine-tuned policy:** The training data `D_RL` is collected by rolling out the initialized (pre-fine-tuning) retrospective model. The PPO training then optimizes Mr on this off-policy data. In principle, once Mr is fine-tuned, its reflections change the actor's behavior, which changes the trajectories, which changes what constitutes a good reflection — this is the standard distribution shift problem in offline RL. The paper does not address this, nor does it include an iterative data collection and retraining loop. The reported performance is from a single round of offline training, which may underestimate what iterative on-policy improvement could achieve.

**No hyperparameter sensitivity analysis:** The paper reports specific hyperparameters for SFT (learning rate `1e-5`, batch size 32, max steps 5,000), reward modeling (learning rate `2.5e-5`, batch size 32, max steps 20,000), and PPO (learning rate `1.4e-5`, max steps 20,000, output max length 128, batch size 64, gradient accumulation steps 8, PPO epochs 4), but does not ablate any of these choices. The KL penalty coefficient `β` in the PPO objective (Equation 6) is not specified at all. Readers seeking to reproduce the results must rely on the exact hyperparameter values without guidance on which are most critical.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim: "Retroformer substantially outperforms non-gradient baselines."** This claim is strongly supported for HotPotQA and AlfWorld, but requires qualification for WebShop. On HotPotQA, Retroformer with GPT-3 achieves 54% vs. Reflexion's 50% (4 retries) — a 4 percentage point absolute improvement, which is meaningful on a 100-question test set but would benefit from confidence intervals to assess statistical reliability. On AlfWorld, Retroformer achieves 100% vs. Reflexion's 84.33% (GPT-3, 4 retries) — this is a genuinely dramatic gap that demonstrates the ceiling of frozen reflection and the effectiveness of learned credit assignment in environments where failure modes are highly structured (getting stuck in loops). On WebShop, the improvement is 1-2 percentage points with both GPT-3 and GPT-4 — small enough that with only 100 test tasks, statistical significance is questionable. The paper's own interpretation acknowledges that WebShop may not be an environment where verbal feedback (learned or frozen) is well-suited, which weakens the universality of the "substantially outperforms" claim but strengthens the paper's honesty about boundary conditions.

**Claim: "Retroformer agents are faster learners compared with Reflexion."** This is well-supported by the learning curves in Figure 4 (HotPotQA) and Figure 6(a) (AlfWorld), where Retroformer variants achieve higher success rates at trial 1 than Reflexion achieves at trial 4 in several cases. On HotPotQA with GPT-3, Retroformer (`r = 4`) achieves approximately 48% at trial 1 versus Reflexion's approximately 44% at trial 1 and 50% at trial 4. On AlfWorld with GPT-3, Retroformer (`r = 1`) achieves 93.28% at N=1 (trial 0) versus Reflexion's 76.87% at N=1. This is clearly faster improvement. However, "faster learning" conflates two effects: (1) the fine-tuned retrospective model generates higher-quality initial reflections (a one-time gain evident at trial 1), and (2) the retrospective model generates reflections that compound more effectively over multiple retries. The results strongly support the first effect (better initial reflections) but the evidence for compounding over multiple retries is weaker — the learning curves for Retroformer often flatten quickly, while Reflexion shows more gradual improvement. This suggests Retroformer's advantage is primarily in the quality of the first reflection, with subsequent reflections providing diminishing returns.

**Claim: "The proposed method focuses on fine-tuning the retrospective model without accessing the Actor LLM parameters or needing to propagate gradients through it."** This is a design claim, not an empirical one, and it is satisfied by construction. The experiments confirm that the approach works with both GPT-3 and GPT-4 as the frozen actor, demonstrating actor-agnosticism. However, the experiments do not test the framework with actors other than GPT-family models (e.g., Claude, PaLM, Llama as the frozen actor). The paper's claim of being a "flexible plug-in module for various types of cloud-based LLMs" is supported by testing two GPT variants but would be stronger with additional actor model families.

**Claim: "Retroformer agents improve the success rate in HotPotQA by 18% with 4 retries."** This "18%" figure requires careful interpretation. In the abstract, it states "Retroformer agents improve the success rate in HotPotQA by 18% with 4 retries." Given the numbers in Table 2, this appears to be a comparison of Retroformer (GPT-3, `r = 4`, 4 retries) at 54% versus the ReAct baseline (GPT-3) at 34% — that is a 20 percentage point absolute improvement, which is approximately 18% _relative_ improvement over the Reflexion baseline at 50%? Or a 20 percentage point gap which represents approximately 59% relative improvement over ReAct. The exact derivation of "18%" is ambiguous and not explained in the text, making it difficult to verify. The same issue applies to the "36%" improvement claimed for AlfWorld — Retrformer achieves 100% vs. Reflexion's 84.33% (GPT-3), which is a 15.67 percentage point absolute improvement. The 36% figure may refer to relative improvement over ReAct (62.69% to 100% is approximately 59.5% relative improvement) or over Reflexion (84.33% to 100% is approximately 18.6% relative improvement). Without clarification, these percentage claims in the abstract are difficult to interpret and risk overstating the results if interpreted as absolute improvements.

**Weakness: Small validation set sizes without uncertainty quantification.** The validation sets are 100 tasks (HotPotQA), 134 tasks (AlfWorld), and 100 tasks (WebShop). With success rates in the 34-54% range for HotPotQA, a 4 percentage point difference between Retroformer and Reflexion corresponds to 4 out of 100 questions — small enough that a few borderline grading decisions could change the outcome. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical tests. The AlfWorld result (100% vs. 84.33%) is less sensitive to statistical noise because the gap is very large and the ceiling is reached, but the HotPotQA and WebShop comparisons would benefit from uncertainty estimates to assess whether the reported gaps are reliable or could arise from sampling variation.

**Weakness: No ablation of the core rating mechanism.** The rating function `r(x, y) = G_{i+1} - G_i` is the central innovation that enables training — it converts environment returns into a reflection quality signal without human annotation. However, the paper does not include an ablation comparing this rating signal to alternatives. What if the rating were simply the episode return `G_{i+1}` rather than the difference? What if it were a binary signal (improved vs. did not improve)? What if the reward model were trained on human preference judgments rather than the return-difference-derived preferences? Without these comparisons, the contribution of the specific rating design (difference of returns) relative to simpler alternatives is unclear. It is possible that simply using the subsequent episode's return `G_{i+1}` as the reward (without differencing) would work nearly as well, since the task difficulty is partially controlled for by the fact that the same task is attempted on both trials.

**Weakness: No comparison to in-context learning baselines that use more powerful frozen reflectors.** Reflexion uses the same frozen LLM for both acting and reflecting. An alternative baseline would be to use a _different_, potentially more capable frozen LLM for reflection (e.g., using GPT-4 for reflection while GPT-3 acts). This would help disentangle whether Retroformer's gains come from having a _separate_ model for reflection versus having a _fine-tuned_ model for reflection. If a GPT-4 reflector (with GPT-3 actor) performed nearly as well as the fine-tuned LongChat-7b reflector, the case for gradient-based training would be weaker — it would suggest that scale, not task-specific fine-tuning, is the primary driver of reflection quality.

**Weakness: Training data is off-policy with no iterative improvement loop.** The retrospective model is trained once on data collected from the base policy, then evaluated. In standard RL, the policy used to collect data and the policy being optimized are the same (on-policy) or the discrepancy is explicitly handled (off-policy methods with importance sampling). The paper uses off-policy data without any correction, which means the reward model and PPO objective are trained on reflections from the _untrained_ Mr, but evaluated on reflections from the _trained_ Mr. This distribution shift likely means the learned reward model's scores become less accurate as Mr improves, potentially limiting how much improvement PPO can achieve. An iterative training loop (collect data with the improved Mr, retrain) would likely yield further gains, but this is not explored. The paper's single-round offline training may therefore represent a lower bound on what the approach can achieve.

**Weakness: Missing computational cost analysis.** The paper reports Retroformer's improved success rates but does not compare the computational cost of training and inference against baselines. Training the retrospective model requires: (1) collecting ~4,000 reflection samples by running 3 trials on thousands of training tasks (each trial involves multiple GPT-3/GPT-4 API calls), (2) supervised fine-tuning for 2 epochs, (3) reward model training for up to 20,000 steps, and (4) PPO fine-tuning for up to 20,000 steps on A100 GPUs. At inference time, Retroformer uses best-of-N sampling with the reward model for reflection selection, adding overhead per trial. Reflexion, in contrast, requires no training and generates a single reflection per trial from the frozen actor LLM (which is already being called for action generation). The paper provides no analysis of whether the performance improvements justify the additional computational cost, either in terms of one-time training expense or per-task inference overhead. This is particularly relevant for the WebShop results, where the 1-2 percentage point gain may not justify the training cost.

**Missing experiment: Retroformer with the same LLM as both actor and reflector, but fine-tuned as the reflector.** A natural ablation would be to fine-tune the _same_ GPT-3 model (via API fine-tuning if available, or an open-source model) as the retrospective component while keeping it frozen as the actor. This would test whether the improvement comes from fine-tuning per se, or specifically from using a _separate, smaller_ model as the reflector. If fine-tuning GPT-3 as the reflector achieved similar or better results, it would challenge the paper's claim that separating the actor and reflector is important. The paper does not include this experiment, which would directly test the "scaling inversion" hypothesis.

**Missing experiment: Effect of the number of few-shot demonstrations in the reflection prompt.** The reflection prompt in Appendix E.2 includes few-shot demonstrations showing examples of good reflections. The paper does not ablate the number or content of these demonstrations. If the few-shot examples are highly curated for the training environments, they could be doing substantial work in guiding the base retrospective model's reflections, and the PPO fine-tuning might be refining an already-strong base rather than teaching credit assignment from scratch. An ablation with zero-shot reflection prompts (no demonstrations) would clarify how much the fine-tuning contributes versus the prompt engineering of the reflection template.

In summary, the experiments **convincingly demonstrate** that Retroformer outperforms frozen-reflection baselines on HotPotQA and AlfWorld, with the most dramatic gains in the structured, loop-prone AlfWorld environment where credit assignment from trajectories is learnable and actionable. The results on WebShop establish a boundary condition where verbal feedback (even learned) provides limited benefit, which is an honest and informative negative result. The experiments are less conclusive on _why_ the improvements occur — the contributions of supervised warm-starting, reward model training, PPO optimization, best-of-N selection, and the specific return-difference rating design are not disentangled through ablations. The absence of uncertainty quantification, the small test sets, and the single-round offline training protocol leave open questions about statistical reliability and whether further gains are possible. The claim about "18% improvement" on HotPotQA is ambiguously specified and risks misinterpretation.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Return-Difference Rating Signal Depends on Strong Causal Attribution Assumptions That May Not Hold in Stochastic Environments

**The assumption.** The rating function `r(x_{k,i}, y_{k,i}) = G_{k,i+1} - G_{k,i}` treats the difference in episode returns between consecutive trials as a valid proxy for reflection quality. This requires that the only meaningful source of variation between trial `i` and trial `i+1` is the reflection content `y_{k,i}` — that the actor's behavior is deterministic given the prompt and the environment dynamics are stable. The paper makes this assumption explicit in Section 4.2:

> "Because the actor is a frozen LM and the temperature is low as default (Yao et al., 2023), the injected randomness that leads to differences in returns `ΔG_{k,i} = G_{k,i+1} − G_{k,i}` are mostly from the reflection responses `y_{k,i}`."

The experimental setup enforces this by setting the actor temperature to `T = 0` and `top_p = 1` (Appendix C.1), making the actor purely deterministic. The paper explicitly acknowledges this choice: "We acknowledge that setting a higher temperature value can encourage exploration but it can obscure the impact of the proposed approaches, making it difficult to compare against existing baselines with T=0."

**The consequence.** Two significant failure modes arise from this design. First, **the approach does not have a demonstrated mechanism for handling stochastic environments** where episode returns vary for reasons unrelated to reflection quality. If the environment itself is noisy (e.g., a web search that returns different results on different calls, a robotics simulator with physics randomness, or a dialogue environment where the user's responses vary), then `ΔG` conflates reflection quality with environmental luck. A poor reflection could receive a positive rating because the environment happened to be easier on the next trial, and a good reflection could receive a negative rating because of unlucky environment dynamics. The rating signal would be corrupted by noise that the paper's causal attribution argument cannot absorb.

Second, **the approach has not been validated in the stochastic-actor regime where exploration matters.** By fixing `T = 0`, the paper eliminates the possibility that sampling diversity from the actor could help discover better action sequences — a standard benefit of stochastic policies in RL. In many practical deployments, temperature sampling is used precisely because greedy decoding gets stuck in repetitive failure modes (the very loops that Retroformer aims to break). The paper's design choice makes the causal attribution clean but means the results do not generalize to the common deployment scenario where `T > 0`. An actor that samples diverse actions at `T = 0.7` might benefit differently from reflections than one generating deterministically — a reflection that says "try a different search query" is moot if the actor deterministically produces the same query every time, but actionable if sampling diversity produces different candidates.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is no experiment varying the actor temperature or testing in a stochastic environment. All three environments (HotPotQA, AlfWorld, WebShop) are largely deterministic given the same inputs — Wikipedia pages do not change between calls, AlfWorld object locations are fixed per task instance, and WebShop product listings are static. The paper does not measure how sensitive the rating signal is to environment noise, nor does it compare Retroformer's performance with `T > 0` against the `T = 0` results. This is a **missing experiment**, not a demonstrated limitation, but it is a consequential gap because stochasticity is the norm in many agent deployment settings (e.g., real web search, dialogue, robotics).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It acknowledges the `T = 0` choice as a deliberate experimental control (Appendix C.1) but does not discuss the implications for stochastic environments or propose extensions that would handle noise in the rating signal. The statement that "injected randomness... are mostly from the reflection responses" is presented as a justification rather than a limitation to be overcome.

### 6.2 Difficulty Estimation and Data Collection Overhead Are Not Accounted for in the Headline Performance Gains

**The assumption.** Retroformer's offline training pipeline requires collecting `D_RL` — a dataset of `(reflection prompt, reflection response, episode return)` triples — by rolling out the base policy (frozen actor + initialized retrospective model) for `N = 3` trials on thousands of training tasks, generating two candidate reflections per unsuccessful task, and rolling out an additional trial with each candidate to compute the return-difference ratings. For HotPotQA alone, this means running the agent on **3,000 training tasks × 3 initial trials + additional rollout trials for candidate comparison = well over 10,000 full episodes** of multi-step agent interaction. Each episode involves multiple API calls to GPT-3/GPT-4 (for action generation at each time step) plus environment interactions (Wikipedia search API calls, AlfWorld simulator steps, WebShop page navigation).

The paper acknowledges that data was collected (Appendix C.1): 3,383 reflection samples for HotPotQA (1,084 with positive ratings), 523 for AlfWorld, and 267 for WebShop. However, the **cost of this data collection is never quantified, discussed as a barrier to adoption, or factored into any efficiency claim**. The headline results in Table 2 and the abstract — "Retroformer agents improve the success rate in HotPotQA by 18% with 4 retries" — are computed _after_ this expensive offline training phase, as if the retrospective model materialized with no cost.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt Retroformer needs to know: **how many training tasks and how many GPT API calls are needed to achieve the reported improvements?** The answer is not in the paper. If a deployment has only 100 target tasks, collecting training data on 3,000 related tasks (with candidate comparison rollouts) may cost orders of magnitude more than simply running Reflexion on those 100 tasks with more retries. The paper provides no guidance on the minimum training set size, no data efficiency curve showing how performance scales with the number of training tasks, and no break-even analysis comparing the one-time training cost to the per-task savings from needing fewer retries.

Furthermore, the data collection process is **environment-specific**. If a practitioner wants to deploy Retroformer on a new environment (not HotPotQA, AlfWorld, or WebShop), they must repeat the entire data collection pipeline: select thousands of training tasks, run the base policy for multiple trials per task, generate and compare candidate reflections, and train a new reward model and retrospective model. This is not transfer learning — the paper does not demonstrate that a retrospective model trained on HotPotQA improves performance on AlfWorld, or vice versa. Each new environment incurs the full training cost from scratch.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is none. The paper reports the _sizes_ of the collected datasets (3,383, 523, 267 samples) but provides no measurements of wall-clock time, GPU hours, API call counts, or dollar costs for data collection, supervised fine-tuning, reward model training, or PPO training. The cost of training the retrospective model on A100 GPUs with 4-bit quantization and LoRA is partially characterized by reporting hyperparameters (epochs, batch sizes, max steps) in Appendix C.1, but the data collection cost — almost certainly the dominant expense given thousands of GPT-3/GPT-4 API calls — is entirely unquantified. The paper's abstract and conclusion present the improved success rates without any caveat about the cost of achieving them.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss data efficiency, training cost amortization, or strategies for reducing the training burden (e.g., active learning to select informative tasks, using cheaper models for data collection, or transfer learning across environments). The only nod to practical cost is the use of 4-bit quantization and LoRA to make the retrospective model fine-tunable on modest hardware, but this addresses only the GPU training cost, not the far larger API cost of data collection.

### 6.3 The Offline Training Protocol Uses Off-Policy Data Without Addressing Distribution Shift, Limiting Potential Gains From Iterative Improvement

**The assumption.** The rating signal used to train the reward model and the data used for PPO fine-tuning are **collected once from the base (pre-fine-tuning) retrospective model** and then used to train a single improved model. Algorithm 1 in Appendix C.1 describes this explicitly: Step 1 collects data by rolling out the initialized LongChat-7b-16k as the retrospective model, and Steps 2-3 train on this fixed dataset. The paper states (Section 4.2):

> "For offline training, we collected the dataset `D_RL` by rolling out a base policy, i.e., the frozen actor LM and the initialized retrospective LM, in the tasks in the training sets for N trials and compute the ratings."

The implicit assumption is that a single round of offline RL on data from the base policy is sufficient to capture the available improvement. Standard RL theory, however, indicates that this is off-policy learning: the policy that collected the data (the base retrospective model) differs from the policy being optimized (the PPO-fine-tuned retrospective model). As the fine-tuned model diverges from the base model, the reward model's scores — which were trained on responses from the base model — become increasingly inaccurate for responses from the improved model, and the PPO objective's advantage estimates degrade.

**The consequence.** The most likely consequence is that **the single-round training leaves additional improvement on the table**. After fine-tuning, Mr generates different (better) reflections, which cause the actor to produce different trajectories, which change the distribution of what constitutes a good reflection. The reward model, trained only on base-policy reflections, may not accurately score these out-of-distribution reflections, potentially penalizing genuinely novel and effective reflection strategies that happen to look different from the base model's typical outputs. This is the standard "distribution shift in offline RL" problem, and it typically means that offline-trained policies plateau below what iterative on-policy improvement could achieve.

Concretely: on AlfWorld, Retroformer achieves 100% success, so ceiling effects mask any limitation from single-round training. But on HotPotQA (54% with GPT-3) and WebShop (36% with GPT-3), there is substantial headroom below 100%. Could an iterative training loop — collect data with the fine-tuned Mr, retrain the reward model on this new data, fine-tune again — yield further gains? The paper provides no evidence either way because **this experiment was not conducted**.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is no ablation comparing single-round vs. iterative training, and no measurement of how the reward model's accuracy degrades as Mr changes during PPO training. The KL penalty `\beta \log \frac{\text{LLM}^{\text{RL}}_{\phi}(y|x)}{\text{LLM}^{\text{Ref}}(y|x)}` in the PPO objective (Equation 6) is the standard mitigation for distribution shift in RLHF — it penalizes the fine-tuned model for deviating too far from the reference model, keeping it within a trust region where the reward model's scores are approximately valid. However, the paper does not report the value of `\beta` used, does not show how the KL divergence evolves during training, and does not demonstrate that the reward model remains calibrated for the fine-tuned policy's outputs. The presence of the KL penalty is a partial mitigation in principle, but its effectiveness is not validated.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through the standard RLHF KL penalty, but the paper provides no analysis of whether this mitigation is sufficient or whether iterative training would yield additional gains. The authors do not suggest iterative training as future work, leaving open the question of whether the reported results are near the ceiling of what Retroformer can achieve or whether they substantially underestimate the potential of on-policy iterative improvement.

### 6.4 The Method Is Validated on Only Three Environments With a Single Actor Model Family, Leaving Generalization to Other Domains, Actors, and Task Types Unestablished

**The assumption.** The paper evaluates Retroformer on HotPotQA (multi-hop QA over Wikipedia), AlfWorld (text-based embodied household tasks), and WebShop (web shopping) — all text-based environments with discrete action spaces and clear success/failure signals. The actor is always from the GPT family (GPT-3 `text-davinci-003` and GPT-4). The paper asserts in the introduction that the retrospective model is a "flexible plug-in module for various types of cloud-based LLMs, such as OpenAI GPT or Google Bard," and in Section 4 that "we assume in this paper that the actor model is a frozen LLM whose model parameters are inaccessible." However, the universality of these claims is assumed, not tested.

**The consequence.** Several important generalization questions are completely open:

**Domain generalization.** Would Retroformer work on tasks that are not text-based information-seeking or object-manipulation tasks? Examples include code generation (where actions produce code and observations are execution results), dialogue (where the "environment" is a human user with unobserved goals), mathematical reasoning (where no external API calls are needed), or multi-modal tasks (where observations include images). The paper's credit assignment mechanism depends on the retrospective model being able to read a text trajectory and identify where the failure occurred. In a code generation task, the "trajectory" might be a sequence of code edits and test results — would the same reflection prompt structure and rating mechanism transfer? The environments studied (HotPotQA, AlfWorld, WebShop) share the property that failures produce clear textual signals (wrong answer, stuck in action loop, purchased wrong product) that a language model can plausibly diagnose from the trajectory alone. This is not guaranteed for other domains.

**Actor model generalization.** The paper tests with GPT-3 and GPT-4 — two models from the same family with similar architectural principles and (presumably) similar instruction-following behavior. Would Retroformer work with a Llama-based actor? A Claude-based actor? An actor with significantly different reasoning style, output formatting, or reliability? The retrospective model was trained on trajectories generated by GPT-3 specifically. If a different actor produces qualitatively different trajectories (e.g., shorter reasoning traces, different action formatting, different error patterns), the fine-tuned Mr's credit assignment capabilities may not transfer. The paper's claim of actor-agnosticism is supported by testing two GPT variants; it is not supported across model families.

**Task difficulty generalization.** Within the environments studied, Retroformer shows dramatic improvement on easy-to-medium tasks (AlfWorld, where base ReAct achieves 62.69% and Retroformer reaches 100%) but more modest gains when the base actor struggles (HotPotQA ReAct at 34%, WebShop ReAct at 33%). The paper does not characterize task difficulty within each environment or analyze whether Retroformer's benefit correlates with the base actor's initial success rate on individual tasks. This matters for deployment: if tasks are systematically harder than those in the test set, Retroformer's benefit may shrink toward zero, as suggested by the WebShop results.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports results on exactly three environments with exactly two actor models from the same family. Table 2 shows the numbers but does not include any cross-domain analysis. The introduction's claim of being a "flexible plug-in module" is aspirational rather than demonstrated. Appendix A lists challenges including "spurious actions," "limited prompt length," and "heuristic prompt engineering" that the authors identify as general problems for LLM agents, but does not test whether Retroformer addresses these challenges in settings beyond the three studied environments.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents its results as positive evidence for the method's effectiveness on the studied benchmarks, without claiming or testing generalization to other domains. Future work on domain transfer, actor-model transfer, and difficulty-dependent benefit characterization is implicitly called for by the narrow scope of the current experiments.

### 6.5 The Absence of Statistical Uncertainty Quantification on Small Test Sets Makes the Reported Gains Difficult to Interpret Reliably

**The assumption.** The paper evaluates on 100 validation tasks (HotPotQA), 134 tasks (AlfWorld), and 100 tasks (WebShop), following the evaluation protocol of Shinn et al. (2023). Success rates are reported as point estimates without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical tests comparing Retroformer to baselines. The paper implicitly assumes that differences of a few percentage points on 100-task test sets are reliable indicators of true performance gaps.

**The consequence.** The headline comparisons become difficult to interpret, particularly for HotPotQA and WebShop where the gaps between Retroformer and Reflexion are small:

- **HotPotQA (GPT-4, 4 retries):** Retroformer 54% vs. Reflexion 52% — a 2 percentage point gap. On a 100-question test set, this represents exactly 2 questions. With a 95% confidence interval of approximately ±5 percentage points (standard for 100 Bernoulli trials at 50% success), the true difference could be anywhere from Retroformer actually being worse to Retroformer being substantially better. The paper provides no way to assess whether this gap is statistically meaningful or could easily reverse on a different random sample of 100 questions.

- **WebShop (GPT-3, 4 retries):** Retroformer 36% vs. Reflexion 35% — a 1 percentage point gap (1 question out of 100). Even without calculating confidence intervals, it is clear that a 1-question difference on a 100-question test is not reliable evidence of a real performance improvement.

- **AlfWorld:** Retroformer achieves 100% vs. Reflexion's 84-85% on 134 tasks — this gap (15+ tasks) is large enough that statistical significance is not in serious doubt, but even here, confidence intervals on the Reflexion baseline would quantify how much of the gap could be due to test-set sampling.

The paper's abstract uses percentage figures ("improve the success rate in HotPotQA by 18%") whose derivation is ambiguous. If "18%" refers to the relative improvement over Reflexion (from 50% to 54% is an 8% relative increase, not 18%), the number does not match. If it refers to the absolute improvement over ReAct (from 34% to 54% is 20 percentage points), then the baseline for comparison is not the most relevant one (Reflexion is the fair baseline for measuring the benefit of gradient-based learning). Without clarity on what is being compared and without uncertainty estimates, the quantitative claims carry less weight than they appear to.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports point estimates in Table 2 and plots learning curves in Figures 4 and 6 without error bars, confidence bands, or significance test results. The evaluation methodology section (5.1) states the test set sizes but does not discuss statistical power or reliability. The paper does not report per-task breakdowns that would allow readers to compute their own statistics (e.g., how many tasks Retroformer got right that Reflexion got wrong, and vice versa).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper follows the evaluation protocol of prior work (Shinn et al., 2023) without adding statistical rigor. This is a limitation shared by much of the language agent literature at the time of publication, but it nonetheless means that practitioners cannot confidently determine whether the reported improvements on HotPotQA and WebShop justify adopting Retroformer over the simpler Reflexion baseline.

### 6.6 The Method Has Not Been Demonstrated to Combine With or Improve Upon Stronger Base Agents, Limiting Its Applicability to the Practical Regime Where Practitioners Already Use the Best Available Actor

**The assumption.** The paper evaluates Retroformer by wrapping it around ReAct and Reflexion, using GPT-3 and GPT-4 as the frozen actor. These baselines represent the state of the art at the time of publication. However, the paper does not address a practical question: **if a practitioner already has access to the best available LLM and the best available prompting strategy, does adding a fine-tuned retrospective model provide additional benefit?** The implicit assumption is that the learned reflections add value beyond what better prompting or a more powerful actor can achieve alone.

**The consequence.** On HotPotQA with GPT-4, Retroformer achieves 54% with 4 retries while Reflexion achieves 52%. This 2 percentage point gap on 100 questions, without uncertainty quantification, raises the question of whether the retrospective model's contribution is largely redundant when the actor is already a highly capable model (GPT-4) that generates reasonable self-reflections on its own. If a practitioner upgrades from GPT-3 to GPT-4, they gain substantially (+6 percentage points for ReAct, +2 percentage points for Reflexion). Adding Retroformer to GPT-4 yields a further +2 percentage points (Reflexion 52% → Retroformer 54%). The marginal benefit of learned reflections shrinks as the base actor improves — the frozen GPT-4 reflector is already reasonably good at credit assignment on HotPotQA, and the fine-tuned LongChat-7b model's edge is small.

This pattern generalizes to a broader concern: **Retroformer's value proposition depends on there being a meaningful gap between what a frozen LLM can diagnose and what a specialized, fine-tuned model can diagnose.** If future LLMs continue to improve their reasoning and self-correction capabilities out of the box, the gap that Retroformer fills may narrow over time. The paper's results already hint at this: Retroformer provides a 4 percentage point gain over Reflexion with GPT-3 on HotPotQA (50% → 54%) but only a 2 percentage point gain with GPT-4 (52% → 54%). On AlfWorld, the gap remains large even with GPT-4 (Reflexion 85.07% → Retroformer 100%), suggesting that embodied task credit assignment is a persistent weakness of general-purpose LLMs that fine-tuning can address. But on the more language-centric HotPotQA and WebShop tasks, the benefit shrinks noticeably with a more capable base model.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 provides the direct comparison. The narrowing gap from GPT-3 to GPT-4 on HotPotQA is visible: Retroformer (`r = 4`) achieves 54% with both GPT-3 and GPT-4, while Reflexion goes from 50% (GPT-3) to 52% (GPT-4). This means Retroformer's benefit over Reflexion drops from +4 to +2 percentage points as the actor model improves. On WebShop, Reflexion with GPT-4 achieves 44%, while Retroformer (`r = 4`, GPT-4) achieves 46% — a +2 gap. No experiment tests Retroformer with a hypothetical "GPT-4 + improved prompting" baseline that might close this gap further.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss how Retroformer's benefit scales with base model capability, does not test with models stronger than GPT-4, and does not explore whether fine-tuning the retrospective model on data from GPT-4 trajectories (rather than GPT-3 trajectories, as was done) would improve its ability to add value beyond GPT-4's own reflections. The experiments use a GPT-3-trained retrospective model with both GPT-3 and GPT-4 actors, which means the retrospective model may be under-specialized for diagnosing GPT-4's (qualitatively different) failure modes. The paper does not investigate this potential mismatch.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

Retroformer introduces a conceptual shift in how we think about optimizing LLM-based agents: it reframes the problem from "how do we fine-tune the agent's policy?" to "how do we optimize the meta-level component that engineers the agent's prompts?" This is not a paradigm shift in the sense of overturning foundational assumptions about LLMs — the paper builds directly on ReAct and Reflexion, and the core insight (that a smaller model can be trained to generate better prompts than a frozen large model produces on its own) is incremental rather than revolutionary. However, it is a **methodological reframing** with substantial practical consequences: it establishes that gradient-based reinforcement learning can be applied to language agents without ever touching the largest model in the loop, by formally absorbing the frozen actor into the environment dynamics (Section 3) and optimizing a separate, smaller, locally trainable prompt-engineering model.

The magnitude of this reframing should be understood as **opening a new design space** rather than solving a previously intractable problem. Before Retroformer, the dominant approaches for improving LLM agents sat at two extremes: (1) purely frozen prompt engineering, where all improvement comes from better instructions and few-shot examples crafted by humans or generated by the agent itself (ReAct, Reflexion), and (2) direct RL fine-tuning of the actor LLM, which was often impractical due to scale and API inaccessibility (attempted by the SAC baseline, which failed dramatically with only 27% on HotPotQA and 59.7% on AlfWorld). Retroformer carves out a **middle path**: optimize a small, specialized model that operates on the prompt level, using environment returns as the training signal, while the large actor model remains an immutable black-box service. This expands the set of practitioners who can apply gradient-based optimization to their agents — anyone with API access to a large LLM and a single A100 GPU can now train a retrospective model — and it generalizes beyond reflection to any prompt-augmentation component in an agent pipeline, as the paper notes in its conclusion.

The paper also provides a **diagnostic contribution** by mapping out where learned prompt engineering helps most versus where the verbal feedback paradigm itself hits a ceiling. The dramatic gap on AlfWorld (Retroformer 100% vs. Reflexion ~85%) establishes that there exist environments where frozen-LLM credit assignment is fundamentally insufficient, and where gradient-based training on environment-specific trajectory data provides a qualitatively different level of diagnostic capability. The small gap on WebShop (1-2 percentage points) establishes the complementary boundary: when the bottleneck is the actor's base capability (generating precise search queries) rather than credit assignment from trajectory structure, even learned reflections provide limited leverage. This boundary mapping — credit-assignment-amenable tasks benefit substantially, exploration-bottlenecked tasks benefit modestly — gives practitioners a framework for deciding whether to invest in training a retrospective model for their specific environment, rather than applying the method blindly.

The work reconciles a tension between two observations in the prior literature: that LLMs can sometimes self-correct through verbal reflection (Madaan et al., 2023b; Shinn et al., 2023), but that generating useful reflective feedback is challenging because credit assignment requires environment-specific understanding that general-purpose pre-training may not provide (as illustrated by Figure 1's failure case where the frozen LM's reflection merely rephrases the failed trajectory rather than diagnosing the root cause). Retroformer's resolution is that **credit assignment from agent trajectories is a learnable skill** — not one that emerges automatically from scaling up general-purpose pre-training, but one that can be acquired through gradient-based optimization on environment-specific data using the return-difference signal as a self-supervised training objective. This redirects research attention: rather than hoping that increasingly large frozen models will become better self-reflectors, the field should invest in training specialized meta-level models for prompt engineering, using the environment itself as the training signal.

Finally, the paper's **scaling inversion** finding — that a fine-tuned 7B-parameter model generates more effective reflections than a frozen 175B+ parameter model — has implications for how organizations allocate their model development budgets. It suggests that for narrow, well-defined meta-cognitive tasks like credit assignment in specific environments, the returns to task-specific fine-tuning can exceed the returns to model scale. This does not challenge the general principle that larger models are more capable at broad, general-purpose tasks (the actor LLM remains the large frozen model), but it carves out a specific class of tasks where **specialization through gradient-based learning dominates scale**.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**On-policy iterative training with distribution shift correction.** The paper uses a single round of offline RL on data collected from the untrained base policy, which almost certainly leaves improvement on the table due to distribution shift — the reward model is trained on reflections from the base Mr, but PPO optimizes toward reflections from an improved Mr that may lie outside the reward model's reliable scoring region. A natural follow-up would implement an iterative training loop: (1) collect data with the current Mr, (2) fine-tune Mr with PPO on this data, (3) deploy the improved Mr to collect new data, and (4) repeat. The key measurement would be the performance gain per iteration — does the second round of training provide substantial additional improvement over the first, or does the KL penalty in PPO already capture most of the available gain? On AlfWorld, where Retroformer already reaches 100%, there is no headroom to measure iterative improvement, making HotPotQA (where Retroformer plateaus at 54%) the right testbed. A negative result (no gain from iteration 2+) would suggest that the KL-constrained single round already achieves near-ceiling performance; a positive result would establish Retroformer as the first round of a general iterative self-improvement procedure for language agents.

**Actor temperature as a controllable exploration parameter in the rating framework.** The paper deliberately sets actor temperature to T=0 to isolate reflection effects, but this eliminates the possibility that the actor could explore alternative action sequences that a good reflection might help it capitalize on. A critical follow-up experiment would systematically vary the actor's temperature (T ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.7, 1.0}) and measure: (a) whether the return-difference rating signal remains a valid proxy for reflection quality when actor stochasticity introduces variance in episode returns, (b) whether higher temperature enables the actor to benefit more from reflections (because sampling diversity produces candidates that a good reflection can steer the agent toward, whereas greedy decoding might repeatedly generate the same suboptimal action regardless of the reflection), and (c) whether Retroformer's advantage over Reflexion grows or shrinks with temperature. The Hypothesis to test: Retroformer's benefit over Reflexion should be larger at moderate temperatures (0.3-0.7) because the fine-tuned Mr can learn to generate reflections that guide the stochastic actor's exploration, while the frozen reflector cannot adapt its guidance to the actor's sampling distribution. If instead Retroformer's advantage disappears or reverses at higher temperatures, it would suggest that the return-difference signal becomes too noisy with stochastic actors, fundamentally limiting the approach to deterministic or near-deterministic deployment settings.

**Cross-actor-model transfer: does a GPT-3-trained retrospective model help a Llama-based actor?** The paper demonstrates that a retrospective model trained on GPT-3 trajectories improves both GPT-3 and GPT-4 actors, but both actors are from the same model family. A strong generalization test would train Mr on GPT-3 trajectories and evaluate it with a Llama-2-70B actor (accessed via API or hosted locally), measuring whether the fine-tuned Mr's reflections remain effective when the actor's reasoning style, error patterns, and output formatting differ substantially from the training distribution. This directly tests the paper's claim that Retroformer is a "flexible plug-in module for various types of cloud-based LLMs." If transfer succeeds (Retroformer with Llama-2 actor substantially outperforms Reflexion with Llama-2 actor), it would establish Retroformer as genuinely actor-agnostic. If transfer fails, it would reveal that the retrospective model learns actor-specific credit assignment patterns, and practitioners would need to retrain Mr for each new actor model — a significant practical limitation. A partial-success result (some transfer, but less than with in-family actors) would quantify the degradation and motivate research on domain-adaptation techniques for the retrospective model.

**Direct comparison of return-difference ratings against alternative training signals.** The paper's central innovation is the rating function r(x,y) = G_{i+1} - G_i, which uses the performance delta between consecutive trials as a self-supervised signal for reflection quality. However, the paper never ablates this against simpler alternatives: (a) using the absolute episode return G_{i+1} as the rating (ignoring the baseline G_i), (b) using a binary signal (improved vs. did not improve, discarding magnitude), or (c) using the reward model trained on human preference judgments of reflection quality (if human annotations were available). A controlled comparison training three separate Mr models with these three rating signals, evaluated on the same HotPotQA and AlfWorld test sets, would quantify the specific contribution of the difference-based rating. The hypothesis (from the paper's causal attribution argument) is that differencing controls for task difficulty and isolates the reflection's effect, so (a) and (b) should perform worse. If instead all three perform similarly, it would suggest that the benefit of Retroformer comes primarily from having any environment-derived training signal (rather than the specific differencing design), simplifying future implementations. This experiment is straightforward to run using the paper's open-source code and published data collection procedure.

**Multi-environment joint training with controlled transfer measurements.** The paper collects reflection data from three environments totaling ~4,000 examples but does not specify whether the retrospective model is trained jointly or separately, nor does it measure cross-environment transfer. A designed experiment would train three variants of Mr: (1) HotPotQA-only, (2) AlfWorld-only, (3) WebShop-only, and (4) joint training on all three, then evaluate each on all three environments. This would answer: does training on AlfWorld loop-detection patterns help Mr diagnose similar repetitive-search failures in HotPotQA? Does joint training provide a regularization benefit (more data, more diverse failure modes) that improves performance on all environments, or does it cause negative transfer (AlfWorld-specific reflection patterns interfering with HotPotQA-specific ones)? Based on the paper's qualitative observation that the fine-tuned Mr learns to detect action loops in AlfWorld and that HotPotQA also exhibits loop-like failures (repeatedly searching for the same entity), positive transfer is plausible and would motivate training a single retrospective model on a broad corpus of agent environments rather than per-environment models.

**Scaling the training data: how many reflection samples are needed?** The paper uses 3,383 samples for HotPotQA, 523 for AlfWorld, and 267 for WebShop — a nearly 13× range in dataset size — yet does not report how performance scales with training data quantity. A data efficiency experiment would subsample the HotPotQA training set at sizes {100, 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 3383} and train separate Mr models, measuring success rate at 4 retries as a function of training samples. This would produce a learning curve that tells practitioners the minimum viable training set size for their environment. If performance saturates at ~500 samples (as the AlfWorld and WebShop dataset sizes might suggest), the barrier to adopting Retroformer on new environments drops substantially — a practitioner could collect a few hundred reflection samples rather than thousands, dramatically reducing the API cost of data collection that the paper leaves unquantified. If performance continues to improve log-linearly with data, it would motivate the opposite: invest heavily in data collection, and develop techniques for automated data generation or data augmentation to scale the training set.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Automated failure recovery for enterprise search and QA agents.** Consider a customer support system where an LLM agent answers user questions by searching internal knowledge bases, documentation, and ticket histories — a setting structurally similar to HotPotQA but with proprietary data. Deploying Retroformer would involve: (1) collecting a few hundred training tasks from historical support tickets where the agent's answer can be automatically graded (e.g., against the resolution marked by a human agent), (2) training a retrospective model on these tasks using the return-difference signal from the automated grading, and (3) deploying the fine-tuned Mr to generate corrective reflections when the agent fails on new tickets, enabling it to self-correct on retry without human intervention. The paper's HotPotQA results (54% success with 4 retries vs. 34% with no learning) suggest that a Retroformer-augmented search agent could resolve a substantially higher fraction of tickets automatically, with each retry attempt costing only additional LLM API calls. The key practical requirement — an automated grading signal for training — is satisfied by environments where correctness can be verified (factual QA, code execution against test cases, structured data queries with known results), making this application broadly feasible.

**Robust embodied task execution with loop-prevention.** The AlfWorld results (100% success vs. 84% for Reflexion) are the paper's strongest finding and point to a clear deployment scenario: any text-based or text-interfaced embodied system where agents get stuck in repetitive action loops. This includes not only research environments like AlfWorld and VirtualHome, but also real-world robotics systems that use LLMs for task planning and execution monitoring, where the "actions" are natural language commands to a low-level controller and "observations" are text descriptions of sensor readings and execution outcomes. In such systems, loop detection and recovery is a critical safety and efficiency concern — an agent that repeatedly tries the same failed grasping maneuver wastes time and risks damage. Retroformer's fine-tuned Mr, trained on historical loop-failure trajectories from the specific robot and task suite, could generate targeted corrective reflections ("I was stuck repeatedly trying to grasp from the top; I should try a side approach or adjust the gripper orientation") that break loops faster and more reliably than a frozen LLM's generic self-reflection. The paper's finding that the reinforced model spontaneously structures its output into "Reflection" and "New plan:" sections (Appendix D, Figure 7) makes these reflections directly parseable by downstream execution systems.

**Cost-efficient batch inference for data annotation and synthetic data generation.** Organizations using LLM agents for large-scale data annotation — e.g., extracting structured information from documents, answering questions about legal contracts, or generating training data for smaller models — face a tradeoff between accuracy and cost. Running the agent once with a powerful model (GPT-4) may achieve reasonable accuracy, but errors are expensive to fix manually. Retroformer offers an alternative: deploy a cheaper model (GPT-3) augmented with a fine-tuned retrospective model that provides corrective reflections on failed attempts, retrying automatically up to N times. The HotPotQA results with GPT-3 + Retroformer (54% with 4 retries) matching or exceeding GPT-4 + Reflexion (52% with 4 retries) suggest that Retroformer can make a weaker (cheaper) actor competitive with a stronger (more expensive) one by investing inference compute in learned reflections rather than in base model capability. For batch processing where per-item latency is flexible but total cost matters, this represents a concrete cost-optimization strategy: train a retrospective model once on a sample of the annotation tasks with automated grading (against existing ground truth or consensus labels), then deploy it to boost a cheap actor's effective accuracy.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions Retroformer as an alternative to frozen-reflection baselines (Reflexion) and direct-RL baselines (SAC), and the experimental results delineate clear conditions for preference without requiring a formulaic decision matrix:

**Prefer Retroformer over Reflexion when:**
- The environment has structured, diagnosable failure modes that manifest in the trajectory text — particularly action loops, incorrect reasoning chains, or goal-forgetting during lengthy interactions. The paper's Figure 5 and AlfWorld results (100% vs. 84%) demonstrate that these are exactly the failure types where learned credit assignment dramatically outperforms frozen-LLM reflection.
- Training data can be collected at reasonable cost — i.e., there exists a set of training tasks with automated reward signals (F1 score, binary success, product matching) and the budget for running a few thousand base-policy episodes to gather reflection samples. The paper's HotPotQA data collection used 3,000 training tasks, but the AlfWorld model achieved 100% success with only 523 training samples, suggesting that for highly structured environments, the training data requirement may be modest.
- The deployment environment allows multiple retry attempts (at least 2-4) so that the investment in training a retrospective model can be amortized across improved per-task performance on the retries.

**Prefer Reflexion (or simpler frozen-reflection approaches) when:**
- The training data collection cost for Retroformer cannot be justified — e.g., the target environment has very few tasks, no automated reward signal for grading, or the budget for pre-deployment data collection is severely constrained. Reflexion requires no training data at all; it works out of the box with any frozen LLM.
- The actor's temperature must be substantially above zero for exploration, and the paper's T=0 isolation of reflection effects has not been validated in the stochastic regime. Until the rating signal's robustness to actor stochasticity is demonstrated, Reflexion is the safer choice for non-deterministic deployments.

**Prefer scaling the base actor (larger model, better prompting) over adding Retroformer when:**
- The primary bottleneck is the actor's base capability, not credit assignment from trajectories. The WebShop results (Retroformer providing only 1-2 percentage points over Reflexion) demonstrate this boundary: when the agent's difficulty stems from generating precise search queries or navigating large action spaces, a verbal reflection that says "be more specific" provides limited leverage regardless of whether it comes from a frozen or fine-tuned model. In such environments, investing in a better base actor (GPT-4 instead of GPT-3) or more sophisticated prompting yields larger gains than training a retrospective model.
- The benefit of learned reflections shrinks as the base actor improves, as evidenced by the narrowing Retroformer-Reflexion gap from GPT-3 (+4 points) to GPT-4 (+2 points) on HotPotQA. If the available frozen actor is already highly capable at self-diagnosis, the marginal value of Retroformer may not justify the training cost.
