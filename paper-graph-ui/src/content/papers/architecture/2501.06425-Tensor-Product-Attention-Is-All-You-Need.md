# Tensor Product Attention Is All You Need

**ArXiv:** [2501.06425](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.06425)

## 🎯 Pitch

Surprisingly, an attention mechanism that constructs every query, key, and value on the fly from a low-rank tensor product matches or beats standard attention across model scales while shrinking the KV cache to as little as 3% of its original size—and it’s even faster at long sequence lengths.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Tensor Product Attention (TPA)**, a novel attention mechanism that factorizes query, key, and value activations via contextual tensor decompositions—dynamically generating low-rank representations from the input hidden state at each token (e.g., constructing each head's queries as a sum of rank-1 tensor products rather than a single linear projection)—to substantially reduce KV cache memory size during autoregressive inference. Through empirical evaluation of the **Tensor ProducT ATTenTion Transformer (T6)** on the FineWeb-Edu 100B dataset across model scales from 124M to 1.5B parameters, TPA consistently matches or surpasses Multi-Head Attention (MHA), Multi-Query Attention (MQA), Grouped-Query Attention (GQA), and Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA) on validation perplexity and downstream benchmarks—while shrinking the KV cache by an order of magnitude (e.g., `(R_K + R_V)(h + d_h)` cached values per token versus `2hd_h` for MHA, yielding a ratio as low as `~3.1\%` when `R_K=R_V=1`, `h=32`, `d_h=64`). The paper further demonstrates that TPA integrates natively with Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) without the separate positional parameter overhead required by MLA, and that its **FlashTPA Decoding** algorithm can be faster than optimized MHA, MQA, GQA, and MLA kernels at long sequence lengths, establishing that factorized attention representations achieve both memory efficiency and model quality improvements across training scales up to 1.5B parameters.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: KV Cache Memory Dominates Long-Sequence Inference

The fundamental bottleneck this paper tackles is the **key-value (KV) cache** in autoregressive Transformer inference. When a Transformer generates text token-by-token, it must attend to all previously generated tokens. To avoid recomputing keys and values for every past token at each new step, the standard practice is to **cache** the key and value tensors `K_t, V_t ∈ R^{h × d_h}` for every token `t` in the sequence (where `h` is the number of attention heads and `d_h` is the per-head dimension). For a sequence of length `T`, this accumulates to `2Thd_h` cached values—a number that grows **linearly with sequence length**.

This linear growth is the crux of the problem. As the paper notes in Section 1:

> "Because memory consumption grows linearly with sequence length, the maximum context window is limited by practical hardware constraints."

For a concrete sense of scale: a model with `h=32` heads, `d_h=128` (e.g., a Llama-7B-class model), and a sequence length of 128K tokens would require approximately `2 × 128,000 × 32 × 128 = 1.05 billion` stored floating-point values—or roughly **4 GB** for the KV cache alone at FP32 precision, even before accounting for batch size. For larger models or longer contexts (e.g., 1M tokens, which production models now target), this becomes prohibitive on current GPU hardware. The KV cache is not a minor overhead; it is often **the primary memory consumer** during inference, dwarfing the model parameters themselves for long sequences.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Scaling Imperative

The paper's motivation is grounded in the practical reality that modern LLM applications increasingly demand **long-context processing** (Section 1):

> "As these models evolve, their ability to process longer contexts becomes increasingly important for sophisticated applications such as document analysis, complex reasoning, and code completion."

This is not merely aspirational. Real-world use cases—analyzing entire codebases, summarizing book-length documents, multi-turn conversations with persistent memory, and chain-of-thought reasoning over long problem statements—all require the model to maintain attention over tens or hundreds of thousands of tokens. The KV cache memory bottleneck directly limits which applications are feasible and at what cost.

Beyond the immediate practical concern, there is a deeper **architectural tension** that the paper identifies. Standard Multi-Head Attention (MHA), as introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), allocates **independent, full-rank key and value representations for every head at every token**. This design maximizes expressiveness but at the cost of memory that scales multiplicatively in both the head dimension and sequence length. The question the paper implicitly asks is: **do we truly need such high-dimensional, independent representations for every head and every token, or can we achieve comparable or better performance with factorized, lower-dimensional representations that share structure?**

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

The paper surveys four major families of prior work addressing the KV cache bottleneck, each with specific limitations that motivate the TPA design.

#### 1. Sparsity and Eviction Strategies

Methods like sparse attention (Child et al., 2019), token eviction via "heavy hitter" heuristics (Zhang et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2024), and dynamic pruning (Ribar et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2024) reduce the cache by selectively discarding tokens deemed less important. The paper acknowledges these but identifies a fundamental flaw:

> "such methods risk discarding tokens that may later prove important"

The problem is that importance is context-dependent and can shift as the generation progresses. A token that appears irrelevant at step `t` may become critical at step `t+100`, but once evicted, it cannot be recovered. This is a hard information loss, and the heuristics that govern eviction are imperfect approximations.

Additionally, sparse attention patterns (e.g., local windows, strided attention) impose rigid structural assumptions about which tokens can attend to which others. While effective for certain modalities (images, audio), for language—where long-range dependencies are pervasive and unpredictable—these structural constraints can hurt performance on tasks requiring distant information integration.

#### 2. Off-Chip Storage

Some approaches move the KV cache off the GPU to CPU memory or other storage tiers (He and Zhai, 2024; Lee et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2024). The paper acknowledges this but notes:

> "at the expense of increased I/O latency"

The bandwidth gap between GPU HBM (~2 TB/s on an H100) and CPU DRAM (~50-100 GB/s over PCIe) is roughly 20-40x, meaning that offloading the KV cache introduces a substantial latency penalty that can negate the throughput benefits of batching. For latency-sensitive applications (interactive chat, real-time code completion), this is often unacceptable.

#### 3. Attention Variants with Shared Keys/Values

This is the family most directly comparable to TPA, and the paper devotes significant attention to analyzing its shortcomings.

**Multi-Query Attention (MQA)** (Shazeer, 2019) shares a single key and value head across all `h` query heads, reducing the KV cache from `2hd_h` to `2d_h` per token—a factor of `h` improvement. However, the paper notes this comes at a cost:

> "often compromise flexibility or require significant architectural modifications"

By forcing all heads to attend using the same key/value representation, MQA constrains the diversity of attention patterns. Different heads can no longer specialize in different types of relationships (e.g., syntactic vs. semantic, local vs. long-range), which is precisely the power of multi-head attention.

**Grouped-Query Attention (GQA)** (Ainslie et al., 2023) offers a middle ground: `h` query heads are partitioned into `G` groups, each sharing a key and value head. The cache reduces to `2Gd_h` per token. This recovers some flexibility—heads in different groups can attend differently—but the tradeoff between memory savings (`G` small) and expressiveness (`G` close to `h`) must be chosen at architecture design time and is fixed for all inputs. The paper's unified framework (Section 4) reveals that MQA and GQA are **non-contextual special cases** of TPA: they use fixed, non-adaptive head-dimension factors rather than learning them from the input.

**Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA)** (Liu et al., 2024, in DeepSeek-V2) is the most recent and directly comparable prior method. MLA compresses keys and values into a low-dimensional latent `c_{KV} ∈ R^{d_c}` where `d_c ≪ hd_h`, and caches this compressed representation rather than the full keys and values. At decode time, the compressed latent is up-projected to reconstruct the full key and value. This reduces the KV cache from `2hd_h` to `d_c + d_R^h` per token (the `d_R^h` term accounts for an additional small key component used for RoPE).

The paper identifies a **critical shortcoming of MLA** that directly motivates TPA's design:

> "encounters difficulties with efficient Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) integration, necessitating additional position-encoded parameters per head"

The underlying issue (explained in Appendix F.3 and discussed in the blog post by Su, 2024) is that RoPE applies a position-dependent rotation to keys and queries to encode relative position. For MLA's compressed representations, if RoPE were applied directly to the up-projected keys and queries, the pre-computation trick that makes MLA efficient at decoding—pre-computing `W^{DQ}_i W^{UQ}_i (W^{UK}_i)^⊤` to avoid materializing full keys—would break because the rotation matrix `T_{t-s}` depends on the relative position of each query-key pair and cannot be factored out (see Equation F.3 in the appendix). MLA's workaround is to maintain a **separate, uncompressed key component** `K^R ∈ R^{d_R^h}` specifically for RoPE, which is cached per token alongside the compressed latent. This adds `d_R^h` to the per-token cache size and requires additional parameters for the RoPE-specific projections, partially undermining the compression benefits.

#### 4. Low-Rank Weight Factorization (LoRA-style)

The paper draws an explicit contrast with methods like LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) that factorize **weight matrices** rather than **activations**:

> "low-rank weight factorization methods such as LoRA effectively reduce fine-tuning memory, yet do not address the KV cache overhead that dominates inference at runtime"

LoRA's compression applies to model updates, not to the per-token activations that fill the KV cache. Even a model fine-tuned with LoRA still stores full-dimensional keys and values at inference time. The distinction is fundamental: TPA factorizes the **output of the attention projections** (the Q, K, V activations) based on the input context, while LoRA factorizes the **parameters** of those projections. This means TPA's compression directly reduces the inference memory footprint, while LoRA's does not.

### How TPA Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper frames TPA as simultaneously **unifying and transcending** prior approaches. The key conceptual move is shifting from the question "how can we compress the KV cache after the fact?" to "what is the most natural, low-dimensional representation of keys and values that still captures the necessary information?"

**Unification (Section 4).** TPA reveals that MHA, MQA, and GQA are all **non-contextual special cases** of the same tensor factorization framework. MHA corresponds to rank `R = h` with fixed one-hot head-dimension factors. MQA corresponds to rank `R_K = R_V = 1` with a uniform all-ones head factor. GQA corresponds to rank `R_K = R_V = G` with group-membership mask factors. This is not merely a theoretical curiosity—it shows that the expressiveness-memory tradeoff can be controlled by a single, continuous parameter: the rank `R`. The paper introduces TPA variants (Table 1) that span the spectrum: TPA with contextual factors, TPA-KVonly (factorizing only keys and values), non-contextual A variants, and non-contextual B variants.

**Differentiation from MLA.** While MLA and TPA both use low-rank factorization to reduce the KV cache, they differ fundamentally in **where and how** the factorization occurs. MLA factorizes the key/value projection weights: the compressed latent `c_{KV}` is computed once, cached, and then up-projected. TPA factorizes the key/value **activations directly** as a sum of contextual tensor products. This has two critical advantages:

1. **Native RoPE compatibility (Section 3.2, Theorem 3.1).** Because TPA forms keys as a sum of tensor products `∑ a^K_r(x_t) ⊗ b^K_r(x_t)`, RoPE can be applied directly to the token-dimension factors `b^K_r(x_t)` **before caching**, producing pre-rotated cached representations. The relative position property is preserved exactly (Theorem 3.1 proves `eQ_t eK_s^⊤ = Q_t T_{t-s} K_s^⊤`). There is no need for a separate RoPE-specific key component, eliminating the `d_R^h` overhead that MLA incurs.

2. **Contextual adaptation.** In MLA, the compression from `x_t` to `c_{KV}` is via a fixed linear map. The rank (embedding dimension `d_c`) is fixed. In TPA, the factors `a^K_r(x_t)` and `b^K_r(x_t)` are both contextual—they depend on the token's hidden state. This means the factorization can **adapt its structure per token**, potentially allocating more representational capacity to tokens that carry more information. The paper's empirical results (Figure 4, Tables 2-3) suggest this adaptivity translates to better performance at matched or smaller cache sizes.

**The gap this paper fills.** Prior to this work, the landscape of attention variants presented a fragmented picture: MHA for quality, MQA/GQA for efficiency, MLA for a compressed compromise, each with their own architectural constraints and failure modes. The paper positions TPA as a **unified framework** that: (a) analytically subsumes prior approaches as special cases, (b) achieves the best memory compression (lower KV cache size than MLA for comparable ranks, per Table 1), (c) maintains or improves model quality rather than trading it off, and (d) integrates natively with positional encodings without architectural workarounds.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The **Tensor Product Attention (TPA)** system replaces the standard linear projections for queries, keys, and values in a Transformer with a **contextual tensor factorization**—instead of computing each head's key as a single matrix-vector product `$W^K_i x_t$`, TPA expresses it as a sum of `$R_K$` rank-1 outer products `$\sum_{r=1}^{R_K} a^K_r(x_t) \otimes b^K_r(x_t)$`, where both factors depend on the input token `$x_t$` and each factor lives in a smaller dimension than the full key. The system solves the KV-cache memory bottleneck by storing only the **factor components** `$a^K_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^h$` and `$b^K_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` (totaling `$(R_K+R_V)(h+d_h)$` values per token) rather than the materialized `$K_t, V_t \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$` (totaling `$2 h d_h$` values per token), achieving compression when the ranks `$R_K, R_V$` are small relative to `$h$` and `$d_h$`.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The TPA system has five major components:

1. **Contextual Factor Maps (per attention layer):** Linear projections `$W^{aQ}, W^{bQ}, W^{aK}, W^{bK}, W^{aV}, W^{bV}$` that take the input hidden state `$x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$` and produce the factor matrices `$A^Q(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_Q \times h}$`, `$B^Q(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_Q \times d_h}$`, and analogously for keys and values. These are the **learnable parameters** that replace the standard `$W^Q_i, W^K_i, W^V_i$` per-head projections.

2. **Tensor Product Assembly:** For each token `$t$`, the factor matrices are contracted via `$Q_t = \frac{1}{R_Q} A^Q(x_t)^\top B^Q(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$`, producing the full query tensor (and analogously for keys and values). This step happens at both training and inference but is **materialized transiently** during training and avoided entirely during FlashTPA decoding.

3. **RoPE Pre-Rotation (inference optimization):** Before caching, the token-dimension key factors `$B^K(x_t)$` are pre-rotated by the position-dependent RoPE matrix `$T_t$`, yielding `$\tilde{B}^K(x_t) = B^K(x_t) T_t$`. This means the cached representation already encodes positional information, eliminating per-step rotations at decode time.

4. **Factorized KV Cache:** Instead of storing `$K_t, V_t \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$`, the system stores only the factor components: `$A^K(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_K \times h}$`, `$\tilde{B}^K(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_K \times d_h}$`, `$A^V(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_V \times h}$`, and `$B^V(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_V \times d_h}$`. At decode time, these are loaded and contracted on-the-fly.

5. **FlashTPA Decoding Algorithm:** An efficient kernel that computes scaled dot-product attention directly from the cached factor components **without ever materializing the full `$Q, K, V$` tensors**. It uses a sequence of Einstein summations over the rank, head, and feature dimensions, with online softmax and fused value aggregation, analogous to FlashAttention's tiled approach.

Information flows as follows: hidden state `$x_t$` enters the TPA layer → factor maps produce `$A^*(x_t), B^*(x_t)$` for queries, keys, and values → for keys and values, the `$B$` factors are RoPE-rotated and the factor pairs are stored in the KV cache → for the current query token, `$A^Q(x_t)$` and `$B^Q(x_t)$` are used directly with the cached factor pairs → FlashTPA computes attention scores and aggregates values in factor space → concat + output projection produces the layer output.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** proposing a new attention mechanism. Its core idea is that query, key, and value activations can be **dynamically factorized into low-rank tensor products** whose components depend on the input token's hidden state, enabling memory-efficient caching without sacrificing representational quality.

---

#### The Tensor Factorization: Equation (3.1)

TPA replaces the standard linear projection that produces each head's query, key, or value with a sum of rank-1 outer products. For each token `$t$`, the query slice `$Q_t \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$` is formed as:

$$Q_t = \frac{1}{R_Q} \sum_{r=1}^{R_Q} a^Q_r(x_t) \otimes b^Q_r(x_t)$$

where `$a^Q_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^h$` is the `$r$`-th **head-dimension factor** (a vector spanning the `$h$` attention heads), `$b^Q_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` is the `$r$`-th **token-dimension factor** (a vector spanning the per-head feature dimension), and `$\otimes$` denotes the outer product producing a matrix `$a^Q_r(x_t) \otimes b^Q_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$` with entries `$(a^Q_r(x_t))_i \cdot (b^Q_r(x_t))_j$`.

**What it computes:** For a single token `$t$` and a single attention component (say, queries), the formula sums `$R_Q$` rank-1 matrices, each formed by taking one factor that says "how much each head should use this component" (`$a^Q_r$`) and another factor that says "what the feature vector for this component looks like" (`$b^Q_r$`). The outer product broadcasts these two vectors into a full `$h \times d_h$` matrix. Summing over `$r$` combines `$R_Q$` such component matrices into the final query tensor. The factor `$1/R_Q$` normalizes the sum so that the expected scale of `$Q_t$` does not grow with the rank.

The key and value tensors are formed analogously with their own ranks `$R_K$` and `$R_V$`:

$$K_t = \frac{1}{R_K} \sum_{r=1}^{R_K} a^K_r(x_t) \otimes b^K_r(x_t), \quad V_t = \frac{1}{R_V} \sum_{r=1}^{R_V} a^V_r(x_t) \otimes b^V_r(x_t)$$

**Why this form:** This factorization separates two distinct aspects of attention that standard MHA conflates. The head-dimension factor `$a_r$` captures **cross-head structure**—which heads should share representational components and to what degree. The token-dimension factor `$b_r$` captures **per-head feature content**. In standard MHA, each head's query `$Q_{t,i} = (W^Q_i)^\top x_t$` is computed independently, with no explicit mechanism for sharing structure across heads. TPA's factorization makes this sharing explicit and learnable: heads that share large values in the same `$a_r$` component will have correlated query features. The rank `$R_Q$` controls the **dimensionality of the cross-head interaction space**—a rank of 1 means all heads share a single feature vector (up to per-head scaling), while a rank of `$h$` can recover full independence (as shown in Section 4.1). This is more flexible than GQA's hard group assignment because the sharing pattern is **soft** (real-valued entries in `$a_r$`) and **contextual** (depends on `$x_t$`).

---

#### Latent Factor Maps: Implementing the Contextual Factors

Each factor `$a_r(x_t)$` and `$b_r(x_t)$` is produced by a learned linear projection of the token's hidden state `$x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$`. For queries:

$$a^Q_r(x_t) = W^{aQ}_r x_t \in \mathbb{R}^h, \quad b^Q_r(x_t) = W^{bQ}_r x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$$

where `$W^{aQ}_r \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_{\text{model}}}$` and `$W^{bQ}_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_{\text{model}}}$` are learnable weight matrices for the `$r$`-th rank component. Analogous projections exist for `$a^K_r, b^K_r$` and `$a^V_r, b^V_r$`.

**Consolidation for implementation efficiency.** Rather than storing `$R_Q$` separate weight matrices `$W^{aQ}_1, \ldots, W^{aQ}_{R_Q}$`, the paper merges the rank index into a single output dimension. For the head-dimension factors:

$$a^Q(x_t) = W^{aQ} x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{R_Q \cdot h}$$

where `$W^{aQ} \in \mathbb{R}^{(R_Q \cdot h) \times d_{\text{model}}}$` is a single weight matrix whose output is then reshaped into `$A^Q(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_Q \times h}$` (each row `$r$` corresponds to `$a^Q_r(x_t)^\top$`). Similarly, for the token-dimension factors:

$$b^Q(x_t) = W^{bQ} x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{R_Q \cdot d_h}$$

reshaped into `$B^Q(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_Q \times d_h}$` (each row `$r$` corresponds to `$b^Q_r(x_t)^\top$`).

**What this consolidation achieves computationally:** The query tensor `$Q_t$` is then assembled via a single matrix multiplication:

$$Q_t = \frac{1}{R_Q} A^Q(x_t)^\top B^Q(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$$

This is equivalent to `$Q_t = \frac{1}{R_Q} \sum_{r=1}^{R_Q} a^Q_r(x_t) (b^Q_r(x_t))^\top$`, where `$a^Q_r$` is the `$r$`-th column of `$A^Q(x_t)^\top$` and `$(b^Q_r)^\top$` is the `$r$`-th row of `$B^Q(x_t)$`.

**Parameter count.** The total number of parameters for the query factor maps is `$d_{\text{model}} \cdot R_Q \cdot (h + d_h)$`, compared to `$d_{\text{model}} \cdot h \cdot d_h$` for standard MHA's `$W^Q$`. The ratio is `$R_Q(h + d_h) / (h d_h)$`. For typical values (e.g., `$h=32$`, `$d_h=64$`, `$R_Q=16$`), this is `$16 \times 96 / 2048 \approx 0.75$`, meaning TPA uses fewer query parameters than MHA. The full attention layer parameter count (Table 1) is `$d_{\text{model}}(R_Q+R_K+R_V)(h+d_h) + d_{\text{model}} h d_h$`, where the last term is the output projection `$W^O$` which is shared with MHA.

**Initialization.** The factor weight matrices `$W^{aQ}, W^{bQ}, W^{aK}, W^{bK}, W^{aV}, W^{bV}$` are initialized using Xavier initialization (Glorot and Bengio, 2010). Specifically, each entry is drawn from a uniform distribution `$\mathcal{U}(-\text{bound}, \text{bound})$` where `$\text{bound} = \sqrt{6 / (n_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}})}$`. This maintains the variance of activations and gradients across layers at initialization, which is standard practice for deep linear layers but particularly important here because the tensor product `$A^\top B$` involves a multiplication of two projected quantities—poor initialization could cause exploding or vanishing values in the assembled `$Q, K, V$` tensors.

---

#### RoPE Integration: Pre-Rotation and Theorem 3.1

Standard RoPE applies a position-dependent rotation to each head's query and key vectors before the dot-product attention. For a query vector `$q_{t,i} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` at position `$t$` and a key vector `$k_{s,i} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` at position `$s$`, the rotation is:

$$\tilde{q}_{t,i} = q_{t,i} T_t, \quad \tilde{k}_{s,i} = k_{s,i} T_s$$

where `$T_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$` is a block-diagonal matrix composed of `$d_h/2$` rotation blocks of the form:

$$\begin{pmatrix} \cos(t\theta_j) & -\sin(t\theta_j) \\ \sin(t\theta_j) & \cos(t\theta_j) \end{pmatrix}$$

with frequencies `$\theta_j = \text{base}^{-2j/d_h}$` (typically `$\text{base} = 10000$`).

**The key property of RoPE** is that the inner product between rotated vectors depends only on relative position: `$\tilde{q}_{t,i} \tilde{k}_{s,i}^\top = q_{t,i} T_{t-s} k_{s,i}^\top$`, since `$T_t T_s^\top = T_{t-s}$`.

**TPA's approach: pre-rotate the token-dimension factors.** Instead of rotating the assembled key tensor `$K_s$`, TPA applies RoPE directly to the token-dimension factors `$b^K_r(x_s)$` **before** they are cached:

$$\tilde{B}^K(x_s) = \text{RoPE}_s(B^K(x_s)) = B^K(x_s) T_s$$

where `$\text{RoPE}_s$` is applied row-wise to `$B^K(x_s)$`—each row `$b^K_r(x_s)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` is post-multiplied by `$T_s$` to produce a rotated row vector. The pre-rotated key tensor is then:

$$\tilde{K}_s = \frac{1}{R_K} \sum_{r=1}^{R_K} a^K_r(x_s) \otimes \text{RoPE}_s(b^K_r(x_s)) = \frac{1}{R_K} A^K(x_s)^\top \tilde{B}^K(x_s)$$

**Theorem 3.1** states that this operation is equivalent to applying RoPE to the assembled key tensor:

$$\text{RoPE}_s(K_s) = K_s T_s = \frac{1}{R_K} A^K(x_s)^\top (B^K(x_s) T_s) = \frac{1}{R_K} A^K(x_s)^\top \tilde{B}^K(x_s)$$

Furthermore, the relative position property is preserved:

$$\tilde{Q}_t \tilde{K}_s^\top = Q_t T_{t-s} K_s^\top$$

where `$\tilde{Q}_t$` is analogously pre-rotated. For individual heads, this means `$\tilde{q}_{t,i} \tilde{k}_{s,i}^\top = q_{t,i} T_{t-s} k_{s,i}^\top$`.

**Proof sketch (Theorem 3.1, Appendix D.1).** The proof follows from the linearity of RoPE. Since `$Q_t$` is a sum of rank-1 terms and `$T_t$` is a linear transformation (specifically, an orthogonal matrix), applying `$T_t$` to `$Q_t$` is equivalent to applying it to each `$b^Q_r(x_t)$` factor individually:

$$\tilde{Q}_t = Q_t T_t = \frac{1}{R_Q} (A^Q(x_t)^\top B^Q(x_t)) T_t = \frac{1}{R_Q} A^Q(x_t)^\top (B^Q(x_t) T_t)$$

The relative position property then follows from `$T_t T_s^\top = T_{t-s}$` and the associativity of matrix multiplication:

$$\tilde{Q}_t \tilde{K}_s^\top = \frac{1}{R_Q R_K} A^Q(x_t)^\top B^Q(x_t) T_t T_s^\top B^K(x_s)^\top A^K(x_s) = \frac{1}{R_Q R_K} A^Q(x_t)^\top (B^Q(x_t) T_{t-s} B^K(x_s)^\top) A^K(x_s)$$

**Why this design choice matters—the MLA contrast.** MLA cannot use this pre-rotation approach on its compressed latents because the compression and rotation operations do not commute: `$\text{RoPE}(W^{UK} c_{KV}) \neq W^{UK} \text{RoPE}(c_{KV})$` in general, since `$W^{UK}$` and `$T_t$` operate on different bases. MLA's fix is to maintain a **separate RoPE-specific key component** `$K^R \in \mathbb{R}^{d_R^h}$` that is cached in addition to the compressed latent, increasing the per-token cache by `$d_R^h$`. TPA avoids this entirely because the factorization explicitly separates the head dimension `$a^K_r$` (which does not interact with RoPE) from the token-feature dimension `$b^K_r$` (which does). The RoPE rotation acts only on the token-feature dimension, where it commutes with the outer product structure.

**Practical implication for inference.** During autoregressive decoding, the key factors `$A^K(x_s)$` and `$\tilde{B}^K(x_s)$` are pre-computed and cached for each past token `$s$`. The current query token's factors `$A^Q(x_t)$` and `$B^Q(x_t)$` are computed and `$B^Q(x_t)$` is rotated on-the-fly (at negligible cost since there is only one query token). The cached key factors require no additional rotation, saving computation at each decoding step. The paper's experiments (Section 6.2, Figures 5, 7, 8) verify that this approach yields competitive or superior decoding speeds compared to FlashMLA, which incurs the overhead of up-projecting cached latents.

#### KV Caching and Memory Reduction: Section 3.3 and Table 1

**Standard MHA caching.** In standard multi-head attention, the keys and values for each past token `$t$` are cached as full tensors `$K_t, V_t \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$`. Over a sequence of `$T$` tokens, the cache stores:

$$\text{Cache}_{\text{MHA}} = 2 \cdot T \cdot h \cdot d_h \quad \text{values}$$

**TPA factorized caching.** Instead of storing the assembled `$K_t$` and `$V_t$`, TPA stores their factor components. For each past token `$t$`:

$$\text{Cache}_{\text{TPA}} = \underbrace{(A^K(x_t), \tilde{B}^K(x_t))}_{\text{key factors}} + \underbrace{(A^V(x_t), B^V(x_t))}_{\text{value factors}}$$

where `$A^K(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_K \times h}$`, `$\tilde{B}^K(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_K \times d_h}$` (pre-rotated), `$A^V(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_V \times h}$`, and `$B^V(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_V \times d_h}$`. The per-token memory cost is:

$$\text{PerToken}_{\text{TPA}} = R_K(h + d_h) + R_V(h + d_h) = (R_K + R_V)(h + d_h)$$

**Compression ratio.** The ratio of TPA cache size to MHA cache size is:

$$\frac{\text{Cache}_{\text{TPA}}}{\text{Cache}_{\text{MHA}}} = \frac{(R_K + R_V)(h + d_h)}{2 h d_h}$$

For the ranks used in experiments (`$R_K = R_V = 2$`) and typical dimensions (`$h=32$`, `$d_h=64$`), this ratio is `$4 \times 96 / (2 \times 32 \times 64) = 384 / 4096 \approx 9.4\%$`. With `$R_K = R_V = 1$`, the ratio drops to `$2 \times 96 / 4096 \approx 4.7\%$`—an order-of-magnitude reduction.

**Comparison with other mechanisms (Table 1).** The paper provides a systematic comparison:

- **MHA:** `$2 h d_h$` per token, `$4 d_{\text{model}} h d_h$` parameters.
- **MQA:** `$2 d_h$` per token (factor of `$h$` reduction), `$2 d_{\text{model}} d_h (h+1)$` parameters.
- **GQA:** `$2 G d_h$` per token (factor of `$h/G$` reduction), `$2 d_{\text{model}} d_h (h+G)$` parameters.
- **MLA:** `$d_c + d_R^h$` per token, where `$d_c$` is the compressed KV latent dimension and `$d_R^h$` is the RoPE-specific key dimension.
- **TPA:** `$(R_K + R_V)(h + d_h)$` per token.

For the large-model configuration used in the paper's Example Comparison III (Appendix A.4: `$d_{\text{model}}=7168$`, `$h=64$`, `$d_h=128$`, `$d_c=512$`, `$d_R^h=64$`), MLA caches `$512 + 64 = 576$` values per token. TPA with `$R_K=R_V=1$` caches `$2 \times (64+128) = 384$` values per token—**33% smaller than MLA**. TPA with `$R_K=R_V=2$` caches `$4 \times 192 = 768$` values per token—larger than MLA but still substantially smaller than MHA's `$2 \times 64 \times 128 = 16,384$` values per token.

**What makes this caching strategy correct.** At decode time, the attention score for head `$i$` between query token `$t$` and cached key token `$s$` requires the dot product `$\tilde{q}_{t,i} \cdot \tilde{k}_{s,i}$`. Using the factorized form:

$$\tilde{q}_{t,i} = \frac{1}{R_Q} \sum_{r=1}^{R_Q} A^Q(x_t)_{r,i} \cdot \tilde{b}^{Q}_r(x_t), \quad \tilde{k}_{s,i} = \frac{1}{R_K} \sum_{r'=1}^{R_K} A^K(x_s)_{r',i} \cdot \tilde{b}^{K}_{r'}(x_s)$$

The dot product expands to:

$$\tilde{q}_{t,i} \cdot \tilde{k}_{s,i} = \frac{1}{R_Q R_K} \sum_{r=1}^{R_Q} \sum_{r'=1}^{R_K} A^Q(x_t)_{r,i} A^K(x_s)_{r',i} \langle \tilde{b}^Q_r(x_t), \tilde{b}^K_{r'}(x_s) \rangle$$

This expression uses **only the cached factors**—the head-dimension factors `$A^K(x_s)_{r',i}$` for head `$i$`, the pre-rotated token-dimension factors `$\tilde{b}^K_{r'}(x_s)$`, and the query factors computed on-the-fly. At no point does the full `$K_s \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$` need to be materialized. The FlashTPA algorithm (Section 5) implements this computation efficiently using blocked Einstein summations.

**Why not cache even less?** One might ask why TPA caches both `$A^K$` and `$\tilde{B}^K$` rather than just some further compressed representation. The answer is that `$A^K$` and `$\tilde{B}^K$` are the **minimal sufficient statistics** for reconstructing the key tensor given the factorization structure. Any further compression would lose information in the key representation. The rank parameters `$R_K, R_V$` provide a direct knob to trade off between memory and expressiveness: lower ranks mean more aggressive compression but less capacity to represent diverse key/value patterns across heads.

#### Analytical Unification: MHA, MQA, GQA as Non-Contextual TPA

Section 4 demonstrates that standard attention mechanisms emerge as special cases of TPA when the head-dimension factors `$a_r$` are **fixed (non-contextual)** rather than learned from the input.

**MHA as rank-`$h$` TPA.** Set `$R_Q = h$` and define:
- Token-dimension factors: `$b^Q_i(x_t) = (W^Q_i)^\top x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` (standard per-head projections).
- Head-dimension factors: `$a^Q_i = h \cdot e_i \in \mathbb{R}^h$`, where `$e_i$` is the `$i$`-th standard basis vector (1 at position `$i$`, 0 elsewhere).

Substituting into the TPA formula:

$$Q_t = \frac{1}{h} \sum_{i=1}^h (h \cdot e_i) \otimes ((W^Q_i)^\top x_t) = \sum_{i=1}^h e_i \otimes ((W^Q_i)^\top x_t)$$

The `$1/h$` scaling cancels with the `$h$` in `$a^Q_i$`. Each term `$e_i \otimes (b^Q_i(x_t))^\top$` produces an `$h \times d_h$` matrix whose `$i$`-th row is `$(b^Q_i(x_t))^\top$` and all other rows are zero. Summing over `$i=1,\ldots,h$` produces exactly the MHA query tensor: row `$i$` contains the query vector for head `$i$`.

**MQA as rank-1 TPA for K and V.** Set `$R_K = 1$` and define:
- Token-dimension factor: `$b^K(x_t) = (W^K)^\top x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` (single shared key projection).
- Head-dimension factor: `$a^K = \mathbf{1}_h \in \mathbb{R}^h$` (all-ones vector, no scaling factor needed since `$R_K=1$` so the `$1/R_K$` factor is 1).

Then:

$$K_t = \frac{1}{1} \sum_{r=1}^1 a^K \otimes b^K(x_t) = \mathbf{1}_h \otimes b^K(x_t)$$

This produces an `$h \times d_h$` matrix where every row is the same: `$(b^K(x_t))^\top$`. All `$h$` query heads see identical key vectors, matching MQA. The same construction applies to values.

**GQA as rank-`$G$` TPA for K and V.** Set `$R_K = G$` (number of groups). For each group `$j \in \{1,\ldots,G\}$`:
- Token-dimension factor: `$b^K_j(x_t) = (W^K_j)^\top x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` (shared key projection for group `$j$`).
- Head-dimension factor: `$a^K_j = G \cdot \text{mask}_j \in \mathbb{R}^h$`, where `$\text{mask}_j$` has 1s at positions corresponding to heads in group `$j$` and 0s elsewhere.

Then:

$$K_t = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{j=1}^G (G \cdot \text{mask}_j) \otimes b^K_j(x_t) = \sum_{j=1}^G \text{mask}_j \otimes b^K_j(x_t)$$

The `$1/G$` scaling cancels with the `$G$` in `$a^K_j$`. The result is an `$h \times d_h$` matrix where heads in group `$j$` all share the key vector `$(b^K_j(x_t))^\top$`.

**What this unification reveals.** MHA, MQA, and GQA differ only in (a) the rank `$R_K$`, `$R_V$` and (b) whether the head-dimension factors `$a_r$` are fixed basis vectors or learned from context. TPA generalizes this by making the factors **contextual** (functions of `$x_t$`) and by allowing soft, learned sharing patterns rather than hard basis-vector assignments. This means TPA can learn, per token, how much cross-head sharing is appropriate—potentially using more sharing (lower effective rank) for easy tokens and more head diversity (higher effective rank) for complex tokens.

**The TPA variants in Table 1.** The paper introduces several variants that explore different points in this design space:

- **TPA:** Full contextual factorization for Q, K, and V with learnable ranks.
- **TPA-KVonly:** Standard linear projection for Q, contextual factorization only for K and V. This keeps the query path identical to MHA while still compressing the KV cache.
- **TPA (Non-contextual A):** Head-dimension factors `$a_r$` are fixed (non-contextual), while token-dimension factors `$b_r(x_t)$` are contextual. Cache size depends only on `$b_r$`, reducing to `$(R_K+R_V)d_h$` per token since `$A^K$`, `$A^V$` can be stored once as fixed parameters rather than per-token.
- **TPA (Non-contextual B):** Token-dimension factors `$b_r$` are fixed, while head-dimension factors `$a_r(x_t)$` are contextual. Cache size reduces to `$(R_K+R_V)h$` per token.

#### FlashTPA Decoding Algorithm: Section 5, Algorithm 2, and Appendix A

**The problem it solves.** In autoregressive decoding, we need to compute the attention output for a single new query token `$t$` against a cache of `$M$` past key-value tokens. The naive approach—materialize `$Q_t \in \mathbb{R}^{h \times d_h}$` and all cached `$K_s, V_s$`, then perform standard attention—would defeat the purpose of factorized caching. FlashTPA computes the attention output **directly from the factorized cache without materializing any full `$K, V$` tensors**.

**Input tensor shapes for decoding.** For a single query token (`$N=1$`) and a cache of `$M$` past tokens:

- Query factors: `$A^Q \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 1 \times R_Q \times H}$`, `$B^Q \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times 1 \times R_Q \times D}$`
- Cached key factors: `$A^K_{\text{cache}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times M \times R_K \times H}$`, `$\tilde{B}^K_{\text{cache}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times M \times R_K \times D}$`
- Cached value factors: `$A^V_{\text{cache}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times M \times R_V \times H}$`, `$B^V_{\text{cache}} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times M \times R_V \times E}$`

where `$B$` is batch size, `$H$` is number of heads, `$D = d_h$` is the key/query feature dimension, `$E = d_v$` is the value feature dimension (typically `$D = E$`), and scalings `$s_Q = 1/R_Q$`, `$s_K = 1/R_K$`, `$s_V = 1/R_V$`.

**Phase 1: Computing attention scores without materializing Q and K.** The key insight is to compute the query-key dot products in factor space. For a single head `$h$`, the dot product between query token `$q$` and key token `$k$` is (Equation A.1):

$$(Q^{(h)}(K^{(h)})^\top)_{q,k} = \frac{1}{R_Q R_K} \sum_{r=1}^{R_Q} \sum_{s=1}^{R_K} a^{Q,(r)}_{q,h} a^{K,(s)}_{k,h} \langle b^{Q,(r)}(x_q), \tilde{b}^{K,(s)}(x_k) \rangle$$

The algorithm computes this efficiently in two sub-steps:

**Step 1 (head-shared feature dot products):** First, compute the inner products between all query and key token-dimension factors, **independent of head**:

$$P[b, q, k, r, s] = \langle B^Q[b, q, r, :], \tilde{B}^K_{\text{cache}}[b, k, s, :] \rangle$$

This is implemented as an Einstein summation: `einsum("bqrd,bksd->bqkrs", B_Q, B_K_cache)`. The resulting tensor `$P \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N \times M \times R_Q \times R_K}$` contains the feature-space dot products for every combination of query rank `$r$` and key rank `$s$`.

**Step 2 (per-head rank mixing to logits):** The head-specific factors `$A^Q$` and `$A^K$` are then combined with `$P$` to produce per-head logits:

$$L[b, h, q, k] = \frac{s_Q s_K}{\sqrt{D}} \sum_{r=1}^{R_Q} \sum_{s=1}^{R_K} A^Q[b, q, r, h] \cdot A^K_{\text{cache}}[b, k, s, h] \cdot P[b, q, k, r, s]$$

implemented as `einsum("bqrh,bksh,bqkrs->bhqk", A_Q, A_K_cache, P)`. The result is logits `$L \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times M}$`—exactly what standard attention would produce, but computed without ever forming `$Q_t$` or `$K_s$` matrices of size `$H \times D$`.

**Phase 2: Online softmax and value aggregation.** Standard attention applies softmax to the logits and then computes `$\text{softmax}(L) \cdot V$`. FlashTPA fuses these operations using an online log-sum-exp update (identical to FlashAttention's approach) to avoid materializing the full `$N \times M$` attention matrix:

**Step 3 (masked softmax):** A causal/padding mask is added to L, and softmax is applied row-wise over the `$M$` key tokens. The algorithm uses an online update (Algorithm 2, lines 10-15) that processes the cache in blocks, maintaining running accumulators for the max logit, exponentiated sum, and weighted value output.

**Step 4 (value aggregation in factor space):** The attention weights are used to aggregate the cached value factors:

$$O[b, h, q, e] = s_V \sum_{k=1}^M \sum_{u=1}^{R_V} \alpha[b, h, q, k] \cdot A^V_{\text{cache}}[b, k, u, h] \cdot B^V_{\text{cache}}[b, k, u, e]$$

where `$\alpha = \text{softmax}(L + \text{mask})$`. This is implemented as `einsum("bhqk,bkuh,bkue->bhqe", alpha, A_V_cache, B_V_cache)`, producing the attention output `$O \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times H \times N \times E}$` which is then permuted to `$(B, N, H, E)$` and passed through the output projection `$W^O$`.

**The rank-1 special case (Figure 2).** When `$R_K = R_V = 1$`, the contractions simplify substantially. The feature dot product `$P$` reduces to a single matrix `$\in \mathbb{R}^{B \times N \times M \times R_Q \times 1}$`. Figure 2 illustrates the data flow: `$B_Q$` (shape `$R_Q \times D$`) and `$b_K^{\text{cache}}$` (shape `$M \times D$`) contract to `$P$` (shape `$M \times R_Q$`), then `$A_Q$` (shape `$H \times R_Q$`) contracts with `$P$` to produce score contributions `$S^{(2)}$` (shape `$M \times H$`), which are element-wise multiplied with `$a_K^{\text{cache}}$` (shape `$M \times H$`) to get the final logits. The value path is similarly simplified.

**Complexity of FlashTPA vs. materialized attention.** Appendix A.4 provides a detailed FLOPs analysis. For a single query token against a cache of `$M$` tokens, the dominant terms are:

- **Projection:** `$\Theta(d_{\text{model}}(R_Q+R_K+R_V)(H+d_h))$` for the current token's factor maps.
- **Attention:** `$\Theta(M(R_Q R_K D + H R_Q R_K + H R_V E))$`.

For the special case `$R_K = R_V = 1$` and `$D=E=d_h$`, this reduces to `$\Theta(M(R_Q d_h + H R_Q + H d_h))$`. Compare to MHA's attention cost: `$\Theta(2 M H d_h)$`. The ratio is roughly `$(R_Q d_h + H R_Q + H d_h) / (2 H d_h) = R_Q/(2H) + R_Q/(2d_h) + 1/2$`. When `$R_Q \ll H$` and `$R_Q \ll d_h$`, this ratio approaches `$1/2$`, meaning FlashTPA uses roughly **half the FLOPs** of MHA for the attention computation, plus the projection savings.

Example Comparison II in Appendix A.4 concretizes this for `$d_{\text{model}}=4096$`, `$H=32$`, `$d_h=128$`:
- MHA projection: `$3 \times 4096 \times 32 \times 128 \approx 50.3 \times 10^6$` FLOPs.
- TPA (`$R_Q=8, R_K=R_V=1$`) projection: `$4096 \times 10 \times 160 \approx 6.6 \times 10^6$` FLOPs.
- MHA attention: `$8192M$` FLOPs.
- TPA attention: `$5376M$` FLOPs.

**Triton kernel implementation (Algorithm 3).** The paper provides a sketch of the Triton kernel for the `$R_K=R_V=1$` case. The kernel tiles over the batch, head blocks, and cache sequence blocks:
- Each program loads a tile of cached `$a_K, b_K, a_V, b_V$` and the query factors.
- It computes the feature dot products, rank mixing, and online softmax update for its tile.
- Partial outputs and log-sum-exp values are stored and later combined across tiles using the standard FlashAttention reduction (lines 26-32).

The kernel avoids materializing the full `$N \times M$` attention matrix and keeps peak memory proportional to the tile sizes, enabling long-context decoding within fixed SRAM budgets.

#### Higher-Order TPA: Appendix C, Theorem C.1

**Motivation.** Second-order TPA (the main formulation) factorizes each `$h \times d_h$` query/key/value tensor as a sum of outer products of two vectors (one in `$\mathbb{R}^h$`, one in `$\mathbb{R}^{d_h}$`). Higher-order TPA generalizes this by further factorizing the token-dimension vectors `$b_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h}$` into multiple factors.

**Third-order TPA construction.** The query tensor for token `$t$` is formed as:

$$Q_t = \frac{1}{R_Q} \sum_{r=1}^{R_Q} a^Q_r(x_t) \otimes \text{vec}\left(b^Q_r(x_t) \otimes c^Q_r(x_t)\right)$$

where `$a^Q_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^h$` (head-dimension factor), `$b^Q_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_b}$` (first token-dimension factor), `$c^Q_r(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c}$` (second token-dimension factor), and `$d_h = d_b \cdot d_c$`. The operation `$b^Q_r(x_t) \otimes c^Q_r(x_t)$` produces a `$d_b \times d_c$` matrix, which is then vectorized into a `$d_h$`-dimensional vector via `$\text{vec}(\cdot)$`.

**What this structure adds.** The additional factor `$c^Q_r$` can be interpreted as a **context-dependent modulation** of the features produced by `$b^Q_r$`. The outer product `$b \otimes c$` has rank 1 in the `$(d_b, d_c)$` factorized space, meaning the `$d_h = d_b d_c$` dimensional feature vector has **structured sparsity**—it is the Kronecker product of two lower-dimensional vectors. This allows the model to represent complex feature interactions with fewer parameters than a full `$d_h$`-dimensional vector, while providing more expressiveness than a single `$d_h$`-dimensional vector without internal structure.

**RoPE compatibility (Theorem C.1).** The theorem proves that RoPE remains compatible with third-order TPA when the rotation is applied to the `$b$` factor only. Specifically, define the global RoPE transformation matrix for the full `$d_h$`-dimensional feature vector as:

$$T_t = I_{d_c} \otimes (R_t)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h \times d_h}$$

where `$I_{d_c}$` is the identity matrix of size `$d_c \times d_c$`, `$R_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_b \times d_b}$` is the standard RoPE block-diagonal matrix, and `$\otimes$` is the Kronecker product. This structure means `$T_t$` applies the rotation to each `$d_b$`-dimensional chunk of the `$d_h$`-dimensional vector independently, using the same rotation for each chunk.

The theorem then states:

$$\text{RoPE}_t(Q_t) = \frac{1}{R_Q} \sum_{r=1}^{R_Q} a^Q_r(x_t) \otimes \text{vec}\left(\tilde{b}^Q_r(x_t) \otimes c^Q_r(x_t)\right)$$

where `$\tilde{b}^Q_r(x_t) = R_t b^Q_r(x_t)$` is the RoPE-rotated version of the `$b$` factor. That is, applying the global rotation `$T_t$` to the full vectorized feature `$\text{vec}(b \otimes c)$` is equivalent to rotating only the `$b$` component and leaving `$c$` unchanged.

**Proof sketch (Appendix D.2).** The proof uses the Kronecker product identity `$(B^\top \otimes A) \text{vec}(X) = \text{vec}(AXB)$`. Applying the global rotation matrix `$I_{d_c} \otimes R_t$` to `$\text{vec}(b (c)^\top)$` (where `$b \in \mathbb{R}^{d_b}$` and `$c \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c}$` are column vectors, so `$b (c)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{d_b \times d_c}$`) yields:

$$(I_{d_c} \otimes R_t) \text{vec}(b c^\top) = \text{vec}(R_t (b c^\top) I_{d_c}) = \text{vec}((R_t b) c^\top) = \text{vec}(\tilde{b} c^\top)$$

Thus, the rotation acts only on `$b$`, preserving the structure induced by `$c$`.

**Empirical evaluation (Table 4).** The paper reports preliminary results for a small third-order TPA model. At 0-shot evaluation, it achieves an average accuracy of 44.56% across benchmarks, compared to 46.21% for second-order TPA at the same small scale (Table 11). This suggests that the additional expressiveness of third-order factorization does not automatically translate to better performance at small scales; comprehensive comparisons at larger scales and matched parameter counts are left for future work.

**Why higher-order matters conceptually.** The generalization demonstrates that TPA is not a single factorization but a **framework** that can incorporate increasingly structured decompositions. The head dimension (`$h$`) and feature dimension (`$d_h$`) are not the only axes that can be factorized; the feature dimension itself can be decomposed into multiple latent dimensions (`$d_b, d_c, \ldots$`), each potentially capturing different aspects of the input (e.g., syntactic features vs. semantic features, or low-frequency vs. high-frequency positional information). This connects TPA to the broader tensor decomposition literature (CP decomposition, Tucker decomposition) and suggests a design space where the tradeoff is not just rank vs. memory, but also **structural assumptions about how features interact**.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing KV-Cache Compression from "What Can We Discard?" to "What Representation Is Naturally Low-Dimensional?"

The dominant paradigm for addressing the KV-cache bottleneck has been **ex post facto compression**: take the full-dimensional keys and values that standard MHA produces, then apply pruning, eviction, quantization, or sparsification to reduce the memory footprint. Methods like H2O (Zhang et al., 2023), StreamingLLM (Xiao et al., 2024), PyramidKV (Cai et al., 2024), and KV cache quantization approaches (Hooper et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024) all operate on the principle of *discarding information after it has been computed*—identifying which tokens or dimensions can be sacrificed with minimal performance degradation. Even MLA (Liu et al., 2024), which uses low-rank compression, applies this compression to the projection weights (a fixed, input-independent bottleneck `d_c`) rather than to the representation itself.

TPA makes a fundamentally different conceptual move: instead of asking "how can we throw away parts of the full-dimensional representation?", it asks **"is the full-dimensional representation necessary in the first place?"** The factorization in Equation (3.1) is not a compression applied to pre-existing keys and values—it is a **redefinition of what a key or value *is***. Each head's key vector is no longer an atomic `d_h`-dimensional entity but a sum of `R_K` rank-1 contributions, each decomposable into a head-coordination component (`a^K_r`) and a feature-content component (`b^K_r`). The memory savings are not achieved by throwing something away; they are achieved by **not computing the full object** in the first place.

This reframing has consequences that go beyond the specific factorization choice. It implies that the KV-cache problem is not fundamentally about *compression* but about *representational efficiency*—finding the minimal sufficient representation that preserves the attention computation. Prior work implicitly accepted that the `h × d_h` key/value shape is the "correct" representation and that efficiency requires approximating or truncating it. TPA's Section 4 unification reveals that even standard MHA is just one point in a continuous space of representational choices (rank `R_K = h` with non-contextual, orthogonal head factors), and that MQA and GQA are points on the same spectrum (rank `R_K = 1` and `R_K = G`, respectively). The empirical finding that TPA with `R_K = 2` outperforms all of these (Tables 2-3, Figures 3-4) demonstrates that the information lost by moving from full-rank to low-rank factorization is not merely tolerable but **beneficial**—the factorization likely acts as an implicit regularizer that prevents overfitting to spurious cross-head correlations.

This is a **fundamental shift** rather than an incremental refinement because it changes the design question from "how do we cope with the memory cost of attention?" to "what is the right structure for attention representations?" The compression ratio itself—potentially an order of magnitude (Section 3.3, the `(R_K+R_V)(h+d_h) / (2hd_h)` formula)—is not the primary innovation; it is a *consequence* of the structural insight.

---

### Innovation 2: Contextual Factorization as an Alternative to Static Parameter Sharing

Before TPA, the primary mechanism for controlling the expressiveness-efficiency tradeoff in multi-head attention was **static parameter sharing**: MQA shares a single key/value across all heads, GQA shares within head groups, and MLA compresses all heads through a shared bottleneck dimension `d_c`. In every case, the sharing pattern is **fixed at architecture design time** and **uniform across all input tokens**. A head in GQA group 1 will always use key `W^K_1` regardless of whether the current token is a punctuation mark or a semantically rich verb.

TPA replaces this static paradigm with **contextual factorization**: the sharing pattern between heads is determined dynamically by the input token `x_t` through the learned factor maps `W^{aK}` and `W^{bK}`. The head-dimension factors `A^K(x_t) \in \mathbb{R}^{R_K \times h}` are not fixed basis vectors (as in the non-contextual special cases of Section 4) but learned functions of the token's hidden state. This means the model can allocate representational capacity **per token**: a token that requires fine-grained distinctions across heads (e.g., a polysemous word whose meaning depends on distant context) can have head factors that spread mass across many `r` components, effectively using higher rank; a token that serves primarily as syntactic scaffolding can have head factors concentrated in fewer components, effectively using lower rank.

This is conceptually analogous to the shift from fixed-weight neural networks to attention mechanisms—replacing a static allocation with an input-dependent one. The significance is that it **decouples the architectural compression ratio from the effective representational capacity**. A TPA model with `R_K = 2` does not have a fixed "rank-2 bottleneck" in the way that MQA has a fixed "rank-1 bottleneck." The effective rank is adaptive: the model can represent near-rank-`h` key diversity when the input demands it and near-rank-1 key sharing when it doesn't, all within the same `R_K = 2` parameter budget (by learning `A^K(x_t)` factors that, for certain tokens, are nearly linearly dependent across the rank dimension).

The empirical evidence for this adaptivity is indirect but suggestive: TPA with `R_K = 2` (Table 1: `(R_K+R_V)(h+d_h) = 4 × 96 = 384` values per token) outperforms MLA (which caches `d_c + d^R_h = 512 + 64 = 576` values for the large-model configuration in Example Comparison III) despite using fewer cached parameters. If the cache size were the only factor, this would be impossible—MLA stores strictly more information per token. The implication is that TPA's factorized representation is **more information-dense** because the factorization structure is learned to be relevant to the attention computation, while MLA's compression projects through a generic bottleneck that must serve all tokens identically.

This is a **fundamental advance** over the prior state of the art. It does not merely improve the compression-performance Pareto frontier; it introduces a qualitatively new axis—input-dependent representational allocation—that did not exist in any prior attention mechanism.

---

### Innovation 3: Unifying the Attention Landscape Through the Lens of Tensor Rank

Section 4 is not merely a "related work" re-derivation; it is a **diagnostic contribution** that reveals the common structure underlying seemingly disparate attention mechanisms. By showing that MHA, MQA, and GQA are all special cases of TPA with specific choices of rank and factor type (contextual vs. non-contextual), the paper provides a **unified language** for reasoning about attention efficiency.

This unification has practical value beyond taxonomic tidiness. It reveals that the key design dimensions are (a) **rank** (`R_Q, R_K, R_V`), which controls the dimensionality of the cross-head interaction space, and (b) **contextuality** (whether head-dimension and token-dimension factors are fixed or input-dependent), which controls adaptivity. The space of possible attention mechanisms is not a discrete set of named variants (MHA, MQA, GQA, MLA) but a continuous 2D design space parameterized by `(R, \text{contextuality})`. This reframing explains why GQA with `G=4` sometimes outperforms MQA and sometimes doesn't—it depends on whether the fixed group structure matches the natural head-specialization patterns of the data. TPA sidesteps this by making the structure learned rather than pre-specified.

Furthermore, the unification clarifies **why MLA has a RoPE integration problem** in a way that prior work did not. MLA's compressed latent `c_{KV}` conflates the head dimension and feature dimension into a single bottleneck, which means RoPE's rotation (which should act only on the feature dimension) cannot be cleanly separated—hence the need for a separate `K^R` component with dimension `d^R_h`. TPA's factorization, by explicitly separating head factors `a^K_r` from feature factors `b^K_r`, makes the RoPE application clean and proves it formally (Theorem 3.1). This is not a minor implementation detail; it is a **structural insight** about what properties a representation must have to be compatible with relative position encodings without auxiliary parameters.

This unification is an **incremental theoretical contribution** rather than a new method, but it is genuinely valuable because it transforms a fragmented design space into a coherent one where future mechanisms can be positioned and compared on principled axes. The paper does not overclaim this—it presents the unification concisely (one section) and lets the empirical results do the work of showing that the TPA parameterization point is effective—but it is arguably as important for future research as the specific TPA architecture itself.

---

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That Factorization Can Improve Quality (Not Just Maintain It Under Compression)

A pervasive assumption in the KV-cache compression literature is that efficiency improvements necessarily trade off against model quality—you accept some performance degradation in exchange for memory savings. MQA degrades relative to MHA on certain tasks (Shazeer, 2019 already noted this). GQA partially recovers the gap. MLA claims comparable performance to MHA but requires careful tuning of `d_c` and `d^R_h`. The default expectation when reading a paper proposing a new compressed attention mechanism is: "how much performance does it lose, and is the memory savings worth it?"

TPA breaks this assumption. Across four model scales (124M, 353M, 773M, 1.5B) and multiple evaluation settings (0-shot, 2-shot, 9 benchmarks), **TPA consistently matches or exceeds the performance of full MHA** (Tables 2-3, 11-16). At the medium scale (353M), TPA achieves 51.41% average zero-shot accuracy vs. MHA's 50.11% (Table 2). At the large scale (773M), TPA-KVonly achieves 53.52% vs. MHA's 52.52% (Table 3). At the XL scale (1.5B), TPA-KVonly with `R_{K,V}=4` achieves 55.03% vs. MHA's 54.49% (Table 12). The validation loss curves (Figure 4) show TPA and TPA-KVonly **below** the MHA baseline at nearly all training stages, not merely catching up at convergence.

This is a **significant empirical finding** because it reverses the expected direction of the tradeoff. The factorization is not merely compressing—it is **improving** the learned representations. The likely mechanism (though the paper does not ablate this directly) is that the factorization acts as an implicit regularizer. By forcing keys and values to be expressed as sums of low-rank components with shared structure across heads, TPA prevents the model from learning spurious, head-specific noise patterns that inflate training loss and hurt generalization. This is analogous to how LoRA's low-rank weight updates can outperform full fine-tuning on small datasets by preventing overfitting, but here the regularization is on **activations** rather than weights and operates even at the pretraining scale.

The practical implication is significant: a practitioner choosing an attention mechanism no longer faces a quality-efficiency tradeoff. TPA offers **both** lower memory (potentially 10× less than MHA) **and** better performance. This changes the default recommendation from "use GQA or MLA if you need memory savings" to "use TPA unless you have a specific reason not to," though the paper appropriately hedges this by noting the single-dataset, single-model-family evaluation.

This contribution is **empirical rather than theoretical**, but it is the key evidence that elevates TPA from an interesting factorization trick to a genuinely superior architecture. Without the quality improvement, TPA would be "MLA with nicer RoPE integration." With it, TPA is "a new default attention mechanism that happens to use less memory." The distinction matters for adoption.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All language modeling experiments use the **FineWeb-Edu 100B** dataset (Lozhkov et al., 2024), which contains 100 billion tokens for training and 0.1 billion tokens for validation. This is an educational-content-filtered subset of the FineWeb corpus. Models are trained for 50B tokens—roughly half an epoch over the training set.

- **Base model(s).** The paper builds on the **LLaMA architecture** (Touvron et al., 2023) with SwiGLU activations (Shazeer, 2020) and RoPE embeddings (Su et al., 2024). The T6 model replaces the standard MHA self-attention block with TPA while keeping the SwiGLU FFN and RMSNorm pre-normalization identical to LLaMA. Models are trained at four scales: **Small (124M parameters), Medium (353M), Large (773M), and XL (1.5B)**. The number of attention heads `h` is adjusted per mechanism to ensure **parameter-matched comparison**—all attention mechanisms within a given scale have approximately the same total parameter count per attention layer as standard MHA (which uses `4 d_model^2` parameters). Specific head counts per mechanism and scale are listed in Table 9 and Table 10. For instance, at the Large scale (d_model=1280), MHA uses `h=20`, GQA uses `h=38`, and TPA uses `h=61`—the higher head counts in TPA compensate for the per-head dimension being factorized, keeping total parameters comparable.

- **Metrics.** The primary metrics are **training loss, validation loss, and validation perplexity** on the FineWeb-Edu validation set, tracked over the full 50B-token training run (Figures 3, 4, and 9). For downstream evaluation, the paper reports **zero-shot and two-shot accuracy** on nine standard benchmarks using the `lm-evaluation-harness` (Gao et al., 2024): ARC-Easy, ARC-Challenge (Yadav et al., 2019), BoolQ (Clark et al., 2019), HellaSwag (Zellers et al., 2019), OpenBookQA (Mihaylov et al., 2018), PIQA (Bisk et al., 2020), WinoGrande (Sakaguchi et al., 2020), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021), and SciQ. For ARC-E, ARC-C, HellaSwag, OBQA, PIQA, and SciQ, the paper reports `acc_norm` (accuracy normalized by sequence length); for other tasks, standard accuracy.

- **Baselines.** Five attention mechanisms are compared across all model scales:
  - **MHA** (Multi-Head Attention; Vaswani et al., 2017): standard per-head independent Q, K, V projections.
  - **MQA** (Multi-Query Attention; Shazeer, 2019): all query heads share a single K/V head.
  - **GQA** (Grouped-Query Attention; Ainslie et al., 2023): heads partitioned into `G` groups, each sharing K/V. The paper uses 2 KV heads for GQA models (Table 9 caption).
  - **MLA** (Multi-Head Latent Attention; Liu et al., 2024, from DeepSeek-V2): compressed KV latents with separate RoPE key components. MLA dimensions are `d_c` (compressed KV latent) and `d_R^h = 32` (RoPE key dimension); specific `d_c` and `d'_c` values per scale are in Table 10.
  - **TPA** and **TPA-KVonly** (the proposed methods): TPA factorizes Q, K, V; TPA-KVonly factorizes only K and V, keeping a standard linear projection for Q. Unless otherwise specified, the paper uses ranks `R_Q = 6`, `R_K = R_V = 2` for TPA and TPA-KVonly models (Appendix H.1).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not study test-time compute scaling strategies; the evaluation is of **pretrained model quality** rather than inference-time performance. The compute budget is the total training FLOPs, held approximately constant across mechanisms at each model scale through parameter matching. For the FlashTPA decoding experiments (Section 6.2), compute is measured as **wall-clock decoding time per token** in seconds, benchmarked across sequence lengths from `2^12` (4,096) to `2^19` (524,288), with batch sizes `{1, 2, 4, 8, 16}`, and embedding dimensions `{1024, 2048, 3072}`. The per-head dimension `d_h` is fixed at 64 throughout. TPA ranks for these speed benchmarks are `(R_Q, R_K, R_V) = (16, 1, 1)`, and GQA uses 4 KV groups. The compared kernels are FlashMHA (Shah et al., 2024), FlashGQA, FlashMQA, and FlashMLA (Jiashi Li, 2025). The TPA Triton kernel (Algorithm 3) is benchmarked against these CUDA implementations, with the authors noting that a CUDA-based FlashTPA kernel is under development.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report error bars, confidence intervals, or multiple training runs with different seeds. The authors explicitly state this in the NeurIPS checklist (item 7): "The error bars are not reported because it would be too computationally expensive for repeated experiments on LLMs." This is a standard acknowledgment in LLM scaling papers but means the reported differences between methods—which are often small (e.g., 51.41% vs. 50.11% average accuracy at the Medium scale, Table 2)—should be interpreted as single-run results whose statistical reliability is unknown. For the downstream evaluations, lm-evaluation-harness provides a fixed evaluation protocol, ensuring reproducibility of the numbers given a model checkpoint.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Training and Validation Dynamics

**Training loss curves (Figure 3, Appendix Figure 3 for Medium/Large/XL).** Across all three larger model scales (353M, 773M, 1.5B), TPA (red curves) and TPA-KVonly (pink curves) converge at least as fast as MHA (orange), and visibly faster than MLA (blue curves). This is particularly striking for MLA—the paper states:

> "Multi-Head Latent Attention (MLA) (blue curves) generally trains more slowly and yields higher validation losses."

A concrete example: in Figure 3(b) (Large, 773M), TPA and TPA-KVonly training loss curves lie below the MHA curve from roughly 5B tokens onward, with the gap widening through 50B tokens. The GQA (green) and MQA (purple) curves track closely to MHA, typically within the line width. The separation between TPA/TPA-KVonly and the baselines is consistent but modest—on the order of 0.01-0.03 in training loss at 50B tokens—suggesting real but not dramatic improvements in optimization efficiency.

**Validation loss curves (Figure 4).** The validation loss tells a similar story with one notable difference: the gap between TPA and the baselines is **more pronounced in validation than in training**, particularly at the Large and XL scales. In Figure 4(c) (XL, 1.5B), TPA and TPA-KVonly validation loss is visibly below all baselines from ~10B tokens onward, with MLA consistently the highest. The widening gap between training and validation curves for TPA compared to MHA suggests that **TPA's factorization may be acting as an implicit regularizer**—the training loss advantage is modest, but the generalization advantage is larger. At the Medium scale (Figure 4a), TPA-KVonly and TPA validation loss curves largely overlap, both below the baseline pack.

**Validation perplexity (Figure 9, Appendix H.2.1).** Mirroring the loss curves, TPA-based approaches achieve the lowest validation perplexities. At the Medium scale (Figure 9a), TPA and TPA-KVonly end training at visibly lower perplexity than MHA, MQA, GQA, and MLA. The gap grows with model scale. At the XL scale (Figure 9c), the TPA variants are clearly separated from the baselines.

**Key takeaway from the training dynamics:** TPA not only maintains performance under compression—it **improves optimization**, reaching lower loss and perplexity with the same training budget. The fact that this holds across three model scales (353M to 1.5B) and both TPA variants (full TPA and KVonly) suggests the benefit is not an artifact of a specific hyperparameter configuration but a genuine property of the factorized representation.

---

#### Downstream Benchmark Performance

**Medium-scale models (353M, Tables 2 and 14).** Table 2 (0-shot) shows TPA achieving the highest average accuracy of 51.41%, compared to MHA at 50.11%, MQA at 50.44%, GQA at 50.35%, MLA at 50.13%, and TPA-KVonly at 50.04%. The gap of ~1.3 percentage points over MHA is driven particularly by HellaSwag (46.83 vs. 45.68), OBQA (37.00 vs. 34.20), and SciQ (79.90 vs. 76.90). However, TPA-KVonly's average (50.04%) is essentially tied with MHA (50.11%), and on some benchmarks (BoolQ: 58.01 vs. 59.60; SciQ: 75.10 vs. 76.90) it underperforms.

Table 14 (2-shot) shows a similar pattern: TPA leads at 53.12% average vs. MHA at 52.34%, GQA at 52.51%, and MLA at 51.78%. TPA-KVonly is second at 52.69%.

**Large-scale models (773M, Tables 3 and 15).** At this scale, **TPA-KVonly achieves the highest 0-shot average** at 53.52%, followed by MLA at 53.32% and TPA at 53.10%. MHA sits at 52.52%. The ordering shifts from the Medium scale: MLA, which underperformed at 353M, becomes competitive at 773M (and in fact surpasses TPA on ARC-E: 63.55 vs. 63.22 for TPA, 63.26 for TPA-KVonly). This suggests that MLA's compression bottleneck `d_c` needs sufficient model capacity to be effective—at 353M it may be too constrained, but at 773M the compressed latent is large enough to capture useful representations.

Table 15 (2-shot) shows MLA leading at 55.74% average, with TPA-KVonly at 55.33% and TPA at 55.02%. MHA is at 54.25%. MLA's strong 2-shot performance is driven largely by ARC-C (38.74 vs. TPA's 37.71) and MMLU (25.47 vs. TPA's 25.79—a small absolute gap but proportionally notable).

**XL-scale models (1.5B, Tables 12 and 16).** Table 12 (0-shot) shows that **TPA-KVonly with `R_{K,V}=4` and `R_{K,V}=6` both achieve 55.03% average**, the highest among all methods. MHA is at 54.49%, MQA at 54.48%, MLA at 54.08%. The full TPA (with `R_Q=6, R_K=R_V=2`) scores 55.01%. Table 16 (2-shot) shows TPA-KVonly with `R_{K,V}=6` achieving 57.11% average vs. MHA at 56.18%, MQA at 56.74%, MLA at 55.31%.

**Small-scale models (124M, Tables 11 and 13).** At the smallest scale, the advantages are less clear. Table 11 (0-shot) shows TPA at 46.21% average, essentially tied with MHA at 46.19% and MLA at 46.20%. MQA and GQA underperform at ~45%. Table 13 (2-shot) shows TPA at 47.93% vs. MHA at 48.11%—MHA slightly edges out TPA. This scale-dependent pattern (TPA advantage grows with model size) is consistent with the hypothesis that factorization acts as a beneficial regularizer: at 124M, the model may be capacity-constrained enough that the factorization *restricts* learning, while at larger scales the regularization prevents overfitting without hurting capacity.

**Cross-benchmark consistency.** TPA does not dominate on every benchmark. On BoolQ, MHA often wins (e.g., Large 0-shot: MHA 61.93 vs. TPA 60.03). On MMLU, the story is mixed—MLA occasionally leads (Medium 0-shot: MLA 24.62 vs. TPA 25.52—actually TPA wins here; Large 0-shot: MLA 24.55 vs. TPA 24.77, nearly tied). On SciQ, TPA and MLA trade wins depending on scale. The overall pattern is that TPA is **never substantially worse than MHA on any benchmark at any scale**, and usually modestly better on average, with the advantage concentrated in HellaSwag, OBQA, and PIQA.

---

#### Rank Ablation: Higher Key/Value Ranks Improve Performance

**Figure 10 (Appendix H.2.2)** shows training loss, validation loss, and validation perplexity for XL-scale TPA-KVonly models with varying key/value ranks `R_K = R_V = R`. As `R` increases from 2 to 4 to 6, all three curves improve monotonically: lower training loss, lower validation loss, lower perplexity. The gains from `R=2` to `R=4` are larger than from `R=4` to `R=6`, suggesting diminishing returns.

**Table 12 (XL, 0-shot)** quantifies this on downstream tasks: TPA-KVonly at `R=2` scores 54.57%, at `R=4` scores 55.03%, at `R=6` scores 55.03% (the average is identical to `R=4`, though individual benchmark scores differ slightly—e.g., WinoGrande: 54.62 for `R=2`, 56.51 for `R=4`, 57.93 for `R=6`). **Table 16 (XL, 2-shot)** shows a clearer monotonic trend: 56.88% (`R=2`), 56.93% (`R=4`), 57.11% (`R=6`).

This is an important finding for practitioners: **higher key/value ranks cost more memory but improve quality**, giving a direct knob for the memory-quality tradeoff. The per-token cache size for TPA-KVonly is `(R_K + R_V)(h + d_h)`. At the XL scale with `h = 47` (Table 10, TPA-KVonly row), `d_h = 64`, `R=2` gives `4 × 111 = 444` cached values per token, `R=4` gives `8 × 111 = 888`, and `R=6` gives `12 × 111 = 1332`. The paper does not report the exact downstream accuracy for intermediate ranks, making it difficult to determine the "sweet spot" where additional memory yields minimal quality improvement.

#### Learning Rate Sensitivity

**Appendix H.3, Figures 11, Tables 17-18.** The paper tests Medium-scale models trained with a learning rate of `3 × 10^{-4}` (half the default `6 × 10^{-4}` used for other Medium experiments). The training/validation loss curves (Figure 11) show the same qualitative ordering: TPA and TPA-KVonly below MHA and MLA. Table 17 (0-shot) shows TPA at 50.88% average vs. MHA at 49.44%, MLA at 50.22%, TPA-KVonly at 49.93%. Table 18 (2-shot) shows TPA at 53.04% vs. MHA at 51.77%, TPA-KVonly at 52.15%, MLA at 51.24%.

The TPA advantage is slightly larger at the lower learning rate (50.88 vs. 49.44, a 1.44pp gap) compared to the default learning rate (51.41 vs. 50.11, a 1.30pp gap), suggesting the benefit is robust to optimization hyperparameters.

#### FlashTPA Decoding Speed

**Section 6.2, Figures 5, 7, 8.** The FlashTPA decoding experiments benchmark wall-clock time per token as a function of sequence length. All times are measured in seconds and plotted on a log-log scale (`log2(time)` vs. `log2(sequence_length)`), so straight lines indicate power-law scaling.

**Figure 5 (d_model=2048, 32 heads, d_h=64):**

- MHA (orange) is consistently the **slowest** across all batch sizes and sequence lengths, with the steepest slope—its decoding time grows most rapidly with sequence length.
- MQA (purple) and GQA (green) are substantially faster than MHA and nearly overlap each other, indicating that the 4-group GQA configuration (`G=4`) provides similar decoding speed to full sharing (`G=1` for MQA) at this scale.
- MLA (blue) and FlashTPA (red) are the two fastest methods. At short sequence lengths (`2^12` to `2^14`), they are nearly tied or MLA is slightly faster. At longer sequence lengths (beyond `2^15` or `2^16`, depending on batch size), **FlashTPA becomes faster than MLA**. For example, at batch size 1 and sequence length `2^19`, FlashTPA is visibly lower (faster) than MLA. The gap widens with sequence length, suggesting FlashTPA's factorized computation has better asymptotic scaling than MLA's up-projection approach.

**Figure 7 (d_model=3072, 48 heads, d_h=64):** The same qualitative pattern holds. FlashTPA and MLA are the top performers, with FlashTPA pulling ahead at longer sequence lengths. At batch size 1 and sequence length `2^19`, the FlashTPA line is clearly below MLA. The larger embedding dimension does not erode FlashTPA's advantage; if anything, the gap at long sequences appears slightly larger than at `d_model=2048`.

**Figure 8 (d_model=1024, 16 heads, d_h=64):** At this smaller scale, the performance ordering is preserved but the differences are less dramatic. MQA and GQA are closer to FlashTPA/MLA. MLA and FlashTPA still dominate at long sequences, with FlashTPA matching or exceeding MLA at lengths beyond `2^15`.

**Important caveat:** The FlashTPA implementation uses **Triton**, while the competing kernels (FlashMHA, FlashGQA, FlashMQA, FlashMLA) are implemented as **highly optimized CUDA kernels**. The paper explicitly acknowledges this:

> "It is important to note that our current FlashTPA implementation utilizes Triton. While the compared methods are typically available as highly optimized CUDA kernels, these experiments provide initial insights into FlashTPA's potential."

This means the decoding speed results are a **lower bound** on FlashTPA's performance—a CUDA-optimized kernel would likely be faster. The fact that Triton-based FlashTPA already matches or beats CUDA-based FlashMLA at long sequences is encouraging.

**Practical interpretation of the log-scale plots.** A `log2` difference of 1 corresponds to a factor of 2 in actual time. At `d_model=2048`, batch size 1, sequence length `2^19` (524,288), reading from Figure 5(a): MHA is at roughly `log2(time) ≈ -1.5` (i.e., `2^{-1.5} ≈ 0.35` seconds per token), while FlashTPA is at roughly `log2(time) ≈ -3.5` (i.e., `2^{-3.5} ≈ 0.09` seconds per token)—approximately a **4× speedup**.

#### Third-Order TPA Preliminary Results

**Appendix C, Table 4.** The paper reports a single small-scale experiment with third-order TPA (factorizing token-dimension vectors into `b_r ⊗ c_r`). At 0-shot, the third-order small model achieves 44.56% average accuracy across the nine benchmarks, compared to 46.21% for second-order TPA at the same scale (Table 11). At 2-shot, it achieves 45.28% vs. 47.93% for second-order TPA (Table 13). The third-order model underperforms second-order TPA at this scale, suggesting that the additional factorization structure may not be beneficial when total model capacity is limited. The paper presents this as an initial feasibility demonstration rather than a competitive result, and a comprehensive comparison with matched parameter counts across scales is not provided.

---

#### Non-Contextual Variant Comparisons

**Appendix G, Tables 5-8.** The paper compares TPA variants with non-contextual `A` factors (fixed head-dimension factors) and non-contextual `B` factors (fixed token-dimension factors) at Small and Medium scales.

- **Small scale, 0-shot (Table 5):** Non-contextual-A TPA scores 45.03%, Non-contextual-B TPA scores 43.66%. Both underperform the fully contextual TPA (46.21% from Table 11). The gap between non-contextual-A (45.03%) and MHA (46.19%) is modest (~1.2pp), suggesting that even fixed head factors can capture most of MHA's representational capacity when the token-dimension factors are contextual.
- **Medium scale, 0-shot (Table 7):** Non-contextual-A TPA scores 50.52%, Non-contextual-B TPA scores 48.19%. The non-contextual-A variant actually outperforms MHA (50.11%) slightly, while non-contextual-B underperforms by ~2pp. This asymmetry—fixing head factors hurts less than fixing feature factors—is intuitive: the head factors control cross-head sharing patterns, which may be relatively stable across tokens, while the feature factors encode token content, which is inherently contextual.

These results validate that **contextuality in the token-dimension factors (`b_r`) is more critical than contextuality in the head-dimension factors (`a_r`)**—which aligns with the TPA-KVonly design, where queries use a standard linear projection (effectively non-contextual rank-`h` factorization) while keys and values use contextual factorization.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Key/Value rank (`R_K, R_V`):** Increasing `R_{K,V}` from 2 to 4 to 6 monotonically improves training loss, validation loss, and validation perplexity for XL-scale TPA-KVonly models (Figure 10). Downstream accuracy also improves, though with diminishing returns: 54.57% → 55.03% → 55.03% (0-shot, Table 12), and 56.88% → 56.93% → 57.11% (2-shot, Table 16). The saturation at `R=6` for 0-shot but continued improvement for 2-shot suggests that higher ranks may especially benefit few-shot generalization.

- **Learning rate:** Reducing the learning rate from `6 × 10^{-4}` to `3 × 10^{-4}` for Medium-scale models preserves the TPA advantage over baselines (Tables 17-18 vs. Tables 2 and 14). TPA's average 0-shot accuracy is 50.88% (low LR) vs. 51.41% (default LR); MHA is 49.44% (low LR) vs. 50.11% (default LR). The gap marginally widens at the lower LR, suggesting TPA's optimization benefits are not learning-rate-sensitive.

- **Non-contextual factors (A vs. B):** Non-contextual-A TPA (fixed head factors, contextual feature factors) is competitive with MHA; Non-contextual-B TPA (contextual head factors, fixed feature factors) underperforms significantly (Tables 5-8). This ablation isolates **where contextuality matters**: in the token-feature dimension, not the cross-head coordination dimension. This finding is consistent with the TPA-KVonly design, which uses a non-factorized (effectively full-rank, non-contextual in the TPA sense) query projection and achieves competitive performance.

- **Third-order factorization:** At the Small scale, third-order TPA underperforms second-order TPA (44.56% vs. 46.21% 0-shot, Table 4 vs. Table 11). This is a **negative result** indicating that additional factorization of the feature dimension does not automatically improve performance and may hurt at small scales due to increased optimization difficulty or insufficient capacity to leverage the structured representation.

- **Training duration (implicit ablation across figures):** The validation loss curves in Figure 4 show TPA and TPA-KVonly below MHA at nearly all training stages, not just at convergence. This addresses the concern that TPA might train more slowly but catch up later—the advantage is present from early in training and persists through 50B tokens.

- **Model scale (implicit ablation across Tables 2-3, 11-16):** TPA's advantage over MHA is minimal at Small scale (124M: 46.21% vs. 46.19%, Table 11), grows modestly at Medium (353M: 51.41% vs. 50.11%, Table 2), and becomes clearer at Large (773M: TPA-KVonly 53.52% vs. MHA 52.52%, Table 3) and XL (1.5B: TPA-KVonly at R=4: 55.03% vs. MHA 54.49%, Table 12). This scale-dependent benefit is consistent with the interpretation that factorization acts as a regularizer whose value increases with model capacity.

---

### Critical Assessment

The experiments demonstrate a consistent pattern: TPA and TPA-KVonly match or modestly outperform MHA, MQA, GQA, and MLA on training dynamics, validation perplexity, and downstream benchmarks across four model scales, while using substantially less KV-cache memory. The FlashTPA decoding experiments further show that this memory efficiency can translate to faster wall-clock decoding at long sequence lengths. However, several aspects of the experimental design limit the strength and generality of the conclusions.

**Claim 1: "TPA achieves a substantial reduction in inference-time KV cache size while also improving performance."** This claim is **supported for the specific configurations tested**, but "substantial reduction" requires qualification. At the ranks used in most experiments (`R_K=R_V=2`), the cache reduction ratio is `(4 × 96) / (2 × 32 × 64) ≈ 9.4%` for the Medium-scale configuration—a roughly 10× reduction, which is indeed substantial. At the ranks used in the speed benchmarks (`R_K=R_V=1`), the ratio drops to ~4.7%. However, the paper does not systematically evaluate downstream performance at these ultra-low ranks across all model scales; the primary results use `R=2`. The claim about cache reduction is a **structural property of the architecture** (derived from the memory formula in Section 3.3) and is verified implicitly by the fact that models train successfully—if the factorization lost critical information, training would fail. But "improving performance" is supported only for the specific ranks, scales, and dataset tested, with single-run results and no error bars.

**Claim 2: "T6 consistently improves or matches validation perplexity and downstream evaluation performance."** This is **supported with qualifications**. "Consistently" is accurate in the sense that TPA never substantially underperforms MHA at any scale on average (the closest is a near-tie at Small scale: Table 11). However, the per-benchmark picture is mixed: TPA loses to MHA on BoolQ at several scales (e.g., Table 3: 60.03 vs. 61.93), to MLA on ARC-E at Large scale (63.22 vs. 63.55), and to MHA on MMLU at some configurations. The advantage is typically small (1-2 percentage points average) and would benefit from statistical significance testing to confirm it's not due to run-to-run variance.

**Claim 3: "TPA integrates seamlessly with RoPE"** This claim is **theoretically supported** by Theorem 3.1 and **empirically validated** by the fact that all TPA models use RoPE and train successfully with good performance. The pre-rotation approach (Equation 3.5) is a clean architectural solution. However, the paper does not provide an ablation comparing pre-rotated vs. post-hoc RoPE application for TPA, which would directly test whether the pre-rotation scheme has any performance impact versus applying RoPE to assembled keys. The claim of seamless integration is therefore primarily a theoretical and design claim rather than one subjected to controlled empirical testing.

**Claim 4: "FlashTPA Decoding can be faster than optimized MHA, MQA, GQA, and MLA decoding methods, particularly for long sequences."** This claim is **supported for the specific hardware/software configuration tested**, but with important caveats. FlashTPA is implemented in Triton and compared against CUDA-optimized baselines—a comparison that is inherently unfair to FlashTPA. That FlashTPA *still* outperforms at long sequences is a strong signal. However, sequential decoding at `2^19` tokens is an extreme scenario; the practical relevance depends on whether real workloads process single sequences of half a million tokens. The decoding experiments use `R_K=R_V=1` (the most aggressive compression), while the quality experiments use `R_K=R_V=2`—the paper does not close the loop by evaluating whether a model with `R=1` can maintain the downstream performance of `R=2`. There is a **latent tradeoff** between decoding speed (favors lower rank) and model quality (favors higher rank) that the paper does not quantify.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

1. **Single dataset (FineWeb-Edu 100B), single training run per configuration.** The entire language modeling evaluation is on one web-text-derived corpus. There is no evidence that TPA's benefits generalize to code, scientific text, multilingual data, or other domains. The absence of multiple seeds means we cannot assess whether TPA's 1-2pp advantages are statistically reliable or within run-to-run noise. The paper acknowledges this computational constraint (checklist item 7) but does not discuss its implications for the strength of the conclusions.

2. **Parameter matching is approximate, not exact.** The paper states that head counts are adjusted "to ensure that all attention mechanisms have the same number of parameters as the standard Multi-Head Attention (MHA), which has `4d_model^2` parameters per attention layer" (Section 6.1). However, Table 10 shows that head counts are integers, meaning parameter matching can only be approximate at finite model sizes. The parameter counts for the Example Comparisons in Appendix A.4 show TPA variants with `RQ=8, RK=RV=1` at 72.5M parameters vs. MHA at 235M in the very-large-scale comparison—this is for a *different* configuration from the main experiments. The main experiments do not report exact parameter counts per mechanism, making it impossible to assess whether TPA's better performance is partly due to having slightly more or fewer parameters than the baselines.

3. **MLA hyperparameters are fixed rather than tuned.** MLA's performance depends heavily on the compressed dimensions `d_c` and `d'_c`. The paper uses fixed MLA dimensions per scale (Table 10: `d_c=256` at Medium, 512 at Large and XL; `d'_c=512` at Medium, 1024 at Large and XL) but does not report whether these were tuned or selected based on prior work. A poorly configured MLA baseline would artificially inflate TPA's apparent advantage. The fact that MLA underperforms at Medium scale but becomes competitive at Large scale suggests the `d_c` values may be too small for the 353M model, which would make the comparison at that scale unfair to MLA.

4. **No comparison against token-eviction or quantization baselines.** The paper frames itself against the KV-cache compression literature (Section 2) but only compares against other attention mechanisms, not against complementary approaches like KV cache quantization (e.g., KIVI, KVQuant), token eviction (e.g., H2O, StreamingLLM), or hybrid methods. TPA's compression is orthogonal to these—one could apply quantization *on top of* TPA's factorized cache for further savings—but without such experiments, the paper cannot claim TPA is the most efficient approach in an absolute sense, only that it is more efficient than other attention mechanisms at matched parameter count.

5. **FlashTPA decoding experiments use different TPA ranks than the quality experiments.** The decoding benchmarks use `(RQ, RK, RV) = (16, 1, 1)` while the main language modeling results use `(6, 2, 2)`. The paper does not report language modeling quality for the `(16, 1, 1)` configuration, nor decoding speed for the `(6, 2, 2)` configuration. This decouples the two central claims: the experiments showing quality improvement and the experiments showing speed improvement use **different TPA configurations**, so we cannot conclude that the same model that improves quality also improves speed.

6. **No long-context perplexity or downstream evaluation.** All language modeling experiments train on 50B tokens with a standard context window (the nanoGPT default, which is typically 1024 or 2048 tokens). TPA's primary claimed benefit is for long-context inference, yet the paper provides no perplexity evaluation at long sequence lengths, no needle-in-a-haystack tests, no long-document QA, and no scaling analysis of validation loss as a function of context length. The decoding speed experiments (Section 6.2) benchmark raw kernel performance but do not evaluate whether a TPA model *trained* for long contexts can effectively utilize the extended context. This is a significant gap given the paper's motivating narrative about "processing longer sequences under fixed resource constraints."

7. **The `RQ=6` for queries, `RK=RV=2` for keys/values choice is not ablated for queries.** The paper ablates key/value rank (Figure 10, Tables 12, 16) but does not ablate query rank `RQ`. This matters because the query rank affects the total attention layer parameter count (Table 1) and the FlashTPA decoding complexity (Appendix A.4 shows the attention FLOPs depend on `R_Q`). A systematic sweep over `(R_Q, R_K, R_V)` would reveal whether the default `(6, 2, 2)` is optimal or whether lower query ranks can further improve efficiency without quality loss.

**Experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- Long-context language modeling evaluation (e.g., perplexity at 8K, 32K, 128K context lengths) comparing TPA against MHA, GQA, and MLA.
- Decoding speed benchmarks for the exact TPA configuration used in the quality experiments (`R_K=R_V=2`), closing the loop between the quality and speed claims.
- Multiple training runs with different seeds to establish statistical significance of the performance differences.
- Evaluation on non-web-text domains (code: HumanEval, MBPP; math: GSM8K; multilingual: FLORES) to test generalization.
- A controlled comparison where MLA's `d_c` and `d'_c` are tuned per scale to ensure a fair baseline.
- Quantifying the exact parameter counts per mechanism at each scale to verify the parameter-matching claim.
- An ablation where TPA is combined with KV cache quantization to test whether the factorized representation is more or less amenable to quantization than full-dimensional keys/values.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for and Potentially Dominates the Inference Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central claim of KV-cache memory reduction is a structural property of the TPA architecture—the per-token cache size formula `(R_K+R_V)(h+d_h)` follows directly from the factorization and does not depend on empirical validation. However, the paper makes an implicit assumption that **the factorized representation is sufficient** to capture the information content of full-dimensional keys and values across all tokens and contexts. The paper does not characterise when this assumption might fail: are there token types, attention patterns, or task demands for which low-rank factorization loses critical information?

The authors acknowledge in Appendix I:

> "generalization to other modalities deserves more extensive investigation"

This is a narrow framing of a broader issue—the assumption that `R_K=R_V=2` (or even `R_K=R_V=1`) provides enough representational capacity for the key and value tensors across all contexts is never tested at the limits. The rank ablation (Figure 10) shows that increasing `R_{K,V}` from 2 to 6 continues to improve validation loss and downstream accuracy at the XL scale (1.5B), indicating that even `R=6` has not saturated the benefit of additional capacity. This implies that `R=2` is a **compromise**, not an obviously sufficient choice—the method is leaving some achievable quality on the table in exchange for memory savings.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying TPA at `R_K=R_V=1` or `R=2` on a task that requires fine-grained distinctions across heads—for example, retrieval-augmented generation where different heads might need to attend to different retrieved documents simultaneously, or multi-entity tracking where each head tracks a different entity—may encounter a **representational bottleneck** that is invisible in the paper's evaluation. The paper provides no diagnostic tool or methodology for determining the minimum rank needed for a given task, and no evaluation of cases where low-rank representations demonstrably fail. The decoding speed experiments (Section 6.2) use `R_K=R_V=1` for maximum speed, while the quality experiments use `R_K=R_V=2`—but the paper never evaluates whether `R=1` can maintain the quality improvements claimed for `R=2`. A practitioner who chooses `R=1` for decoding speed is operating without evidence about the quality cost.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The rank ablation (Figure 10, Tables 12 and 16) provides the only evidence about the capacity-saturation curve. At the XL scale, moving from `R=2` to `R=4` improves 0-shot average from 54.57% to 55.03% (+0.46pp), and from `R=4` to `R=6` improves 2-shot average from 56.93% to 57.11% (+0.18pp). The gains are diminishing but still positive, meaning the optimal rank for quality is higher than the paper's default. Conversely, the paper provides **no lower-bound characterisation**: what happens at `R_K=R_V=1` for downstream quality is never evaluated at any scale. The gap between the decoding-speed configuration (`R=1`) and the quality configuration (`R=2`) is a critical missing measurement.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents `R_K=R_V=2` as a fixed default and sweeps higher values only for TPA-KVonly at the XL scale. There is no systematic study of the rank-quality tradeoff across scales, no task-level analysis of where low rank fails, and no guidance for practitioners on rank selection. The authors do not identify this as a limitation.

---

### All Quality Evaluations Are on a Single Dataset with a Single Model Family, Without Statistical Replication

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's claim that TPA "consistently improves or matches validation perplexity and downstream evaluation performance" (Abstract, emphasis added) is based entirely on experiments using the **FineWeb-Edu 100B** dataset (Lozhkov et al., 2024) and the **LLaMA architecture** (Touvron et al., 2023) as the base. The authors state in Appendix I:

> "generalization to other modalities deserves more extensive investigation"

This understates the scope limitation. All language modeling experiments are on a single web-text-derived corpus, evaluated on nine English-language benchmarks. The paper provides **zero evidence** about TPA's behaviour on code generation, mathematical reasoning, multilingual text, long-document comprehension, or any domain beyond general web text. Different domains may have different attention patterns—code requires precise long-range identifier tracking, mathematics requires structured multi-step reasoning, and multilingual text requires cross-lingual attention—and there is no reason to assume that the factorized representation's capacity is equally sufficient across all these regimes.

Furthermore, every quality comparison is based on **a single training run per configuration**. The NeurIPS checklist (item 7) acknowledges:

> "The error bars are not reported because it would be too computationally expensive for repeated experiments on LLMs."

**The consequence.** The reported performance differences—which drive the paper's central claim of quality improvement—are **not statistically verified**. At the Medium scale (Table 2), TPA achieves 51.41% average accuracy versus MHA's 50.11%, a difference of 1.30 percentage points across nine benchmarks with 500 total test examples. At the Small scale (Table 11), TPA scores 46.21% versus MHA's 46.19%—a difference of 0.02pp, which is almost certainly within run-to-run noise. Even at the Large scale (Table 3), TPA-KVonly's 53.52% versus MHA's 52.52% (1.0pp difference) could plausibly arise from random seed variation, different data ordering, or hardware non-determinism. Without error bars or multiple seeds, a practitioner cannot determine whether TPA genuinely improves quality or is merely not worse.

On the generalisation question: a practitioner deploying TPA in a code-generation model (where the paper has no evidence) cannot rely on the FineWeb-Edu results to predict how factorized attention will affect identifier tracking, long-range syntax, or API usage patterns. The failure mode is silent—the model might train to comparable perplexity but exhibit qualitatively different attention patterns that affect downstream task performance in ways the paper's benchmark suite does not capture.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides zero multi-seed results, zero confidence intervals, and zero domain-transfer experiments. The benchmark suite (ARC, BoolQ, HellaSwag, OBQA, PIQA, WinoGrande, MMLU, SciQ) is standard but limited to English-language commonsense reasoning, reading comprehension, and factual knowledge—tasks that may not stress the attention mechanism's capacity for structured, long-range, or multi-entity reasoning. The single-dataset, single-model-family evaluation means the paper's claims are strictly limited to "LLaMA-style models trained on FineWeb-Edu" and cannot be extrapolated.

**Mitigation status.** The authors transparently disclose the lack of error bars (checklist item 7) and acknowledge the generalisation limitation (Appendix I), but do not discuss the implications for their central quality claims. No mitigation is proposed beyond the suggestion that "other modalities" should be studied in future work.

---

### The Largest Model Tested Is 1.5B Parameters—Two Orders of Magnitude Below Production Scale

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates TPA at scales from 124M to 1.5B parameters. The authors do not explicitly claim that the findings extrapolate to larger models, but the introduction frames the work in the context of "modern language models" (Section 1), citing LLaMA (7B-65B), Qwen, and Gemma as target architectures for TPA integration:

> "enabling a straightforward drop-in replacement for multi-head attention (MHA) layers in modern LLM architectures such as LLaMA, Qwen, and Gemma."

The paper does not test TPA at 7B, 13B, 70B, or any scale approaching these production models. The 1.5B XL model is roughly 5× smaller than the smallest LLaMA-2 model (7B), 10× smaller than LLaMA-2-13B, and 40× smaller than LLaMA-2-70B.

**The consequence.** Attention mechanism behaviour often changes with scale in ways that are not predictable from smaller models. For example, MQA's quality degradation relative to MHA is more pronounced at larger scales (Shazeer, 2019 noted instability at scale), and GQA was partially motivated by recovering this lost capacity. MLA was developed and validated at the 236B-parameter scale (DeepSeek-V2) and the 671B-parameter MoE scale (DeepSeek-V3). TPA's factorized representation may exhibit **emergent failure modes at scale** that are invisible at 1.5B parameters:

- **Rank saturation:** At 1.5B, `R=6` still shows improving validation loss and downstream accuracy (Tables 12, 16), suggesting the optimal rank is higher. At 70B, the optimal rank might be substantially larger to maintain the same relative representational capacity, potentially eroding the memory savings.
- **Training instability:** The tensor product operation `A(x_t)^\top B(x_t)` involves multiplying two projected quantities, which amplifies initialization effects. At larger scales, poor conditioning of these factor matrices could manifest as training instability that is not present at 1.5B—analogous to how MQA training instability prompted the development of GQA.
- **Attention head specialisation:** Larger models develop more specialised attention heads (e.g., induction heads, copying heads, retrieval heads). TPA's soft cross-head sharing via the `a_r` factors might interfere with this specialisation, forcing heads to share structure when they should be independent—but only at scales where specialisation is pronounced.

The paper's claim that TPA is a "drop-in replacement" for MHA in LLaMA, Qwen, and Gemma is therefore **based on extrapolation from 1.5B**, which is insufficient evidence to support such a strong architectural recommendation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 and Tables 2-3, 11-16 show that TPA's relative advantage over MHA grows from nearly zero at 124M to ~1pp at 1.5B (0-shot average). This **positive scaling trend** is encouraging but does not guarantee continued improvement at 7B and beyond—the curve could saturate, reverse, or encounter threshold effects. The paper provides no scaling law analysis, no extrapolation, and no theoretical argument for why the benefit should persist.

**Mitigation status.** The authors do not identify the limited scale range as a limitation. The abstract claims TPA "achieves improved model quality" without qualification, and the introduction claims drop-in compatibility with production architectures without caveats about the scale gap. A practitioner evaluating TPA for a 70B-parameter deployment cannot rely on the 1.5B results to predict behaviour at their target scale.

---

### FlashTPA Decoding Speed Experiments Are Not Evaluated with the Same TPA Configuration Used for Quality

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper separates the quality evaluation (which uses `R_Q=6`, `R_K=R_V=2` for most experiments) from the decoding speed evaluation (which uses `R_Q=16`, `R_K=R_V=1`). This is stated explicitly:

> "The ranks for TPA's factorized components (`R_Q, R_K, R_V`) were set to (16, 1, 1)" (Section 6.2)

versus:

> "ranks `R_K = R_V = 2` and `R_Q = 6` for TPA and TPA-KVonly models, unless otherwise specified" (Appendix H.1)

These are **substantially different configurations**—the speed-benchmark TPA has 4.7% of MHA's KV-cache size (with `R_K=R_V=1`), while the quality-benchmark TPA has ~9.4% (with `R_K=R_V=2`). The speed configuration is **twice as aggressive** in compression, uses a higher query rank, and has different attention FLOP scaling (Appendix A.4 shows that attention FLOPs depend on `R_K R_V` for the feature-dot product term and `H R_K R_V` for the rank-mixing term).

**The consequence.** The paper's two central claims—"TPA improves quality over MHA" and "FlashTPA is faster than FlashMLA"—are demonstrated on **different models**. A practitioner cannot conclude that the model which improves quality also decodes faster; they must choose between (a) the quality configuration (unknown decoding speed) and (b) the speed configuration (unknown quality). Furthermore, the speed configuration's `R_K=R_V=1` may produce **worse quality** than MHA, MQA, or MLA, but this is never measured. If `R_K=R_V=1` degrades quality to below MHA, then the speed comparison is misleading—it would compare a lower-quality model against higher-quality baselines, which is not a useful tradeoff characterisation.

The decoding time results (Figures 5, 7, 8) show FlashTPA outperforming or matching FlashMLA at long sequences, but these comparisons are also between **different model configurations**: the MLA benchmark uses whatever MLA configuration the FlashMLA kernel was designed for (presumably matching DeepSeek-V2's published dimensions), while the TPA benchmark uses `(R_Q=16, R_K=1, R_V=1)`. If TPA with `R_K=R_V=2` were benchmarked, the attention FLOPs would increase (by roughly `2×` for the feature-dot product term and `4×` for the rank-mixing term, per the complexity analysis in Appendix A.4), potentially erasing the speed advantage over MLA.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **no quality evaluation at `(R_Q=16, R_K=1, R_V=1)`** and **no decoding speed evaluation at `(R_Q=6, R_K=2, R_V=2)`**. The rank-1 configuration's quality is entirely uncharacterised. Figure 10 shows that `R=2` outperforms higher ranks for TPA-KVonly at the XL scale in terms of loss and perplexity, but this is `R=2` vs. `R=4` and `R=6`—`R=1` is never evaluated. The paper's claim that "TPA is not only memory-efficient due to its smaller KV cache footprint but can also be computationally efficient during inference" (Section 5) is therefore **supported for a configuration whose quality is unknown**.

**Mitigation status.** The authors acknowledge that the FlashTPA implementation is in Triton (not CUDA) and that a CUDA kernel is in development, but do not acknowledge the configuration mismatch between the quality and speed experiments. This is an unstated limitation that directly affects the paper's primary practical claim.

---

### The FlashTPA Kernel Is Implemented in Triton, Not an Optimised CUDA Kernel

**The assumption or constraint.** The decoding speed comparison (Section 6.2) is between:

> "our current FlashTPA implementation utilizes Triton. While the compared methods are typically available as highly optimized CUDA kernels"

Triton (Tillet et al., 2019) is a higher-level language that compiles to GPU code but typically does not match the performance of hand-tuned CUDA kernels like FlashAttention-3 (Shah et al., 2024) or FlashMLA (Jiashi Li, 2025). The paper presents this as a caveat but does not quantify the performance gap between a Triton kernel and an equivalent CUDA kernel for the same computation.

**The consequence.** The decoding speed results represent a **lower bound on FlashTPA's achievable performance**—a CUDA kernel would almost certainly be faster. However, the comparison against CUDA-optimised baselines is **inherently asymmetric**: FlashTPA is being benchmarked with a suboptimal implementation, while the baselines use production-optimised kernels. If FlashTPA *still* outperforms or matches the baselines (as it does at long sequences in Figures 5, 7, 8), this is strong evidence for the approach's efficiency. But a practitioner cannot use the reported absolute decoding times to estimate real-world performance in a production serving system—the actual speed of a CUDA-optimised FlashTPA kernel is unknown, and the relative speedup over a CUDA-optimised MLA kernel (which would be the production choice) is unknown.

A further consequence: the Triton vs. CUDA asymmetry interacts with the sequence length results. FlashTPA's advantage over MLA grows at longer sequences (Figures 5, 7, 8). If FlashTPA were implemented in CUDA, the advantage might increase (if the CUDA implementation scales better than Triton) or decrease (if MLA's CUDA kernel has better asymptotic scaling than Triton-FlashTPA—unlikely but not tested). Without a CUDA baseline, the shape of the performance curves at long sequences is suggestive but not conclusive.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The decoding benchmarks (Figures 5, 7, 8) show FlashTPA (Triton) at or near the top of the performance ranking across sequence lengths, with the advantage growing at long sequences. This is evidence that the **algorithmic approach** (factorised attention computation) is efficient, but it is not evidence about the **peak achievable performance** of the approach. The paper provides no profiling data (memory bandwidth utilisation, occupancy, instruction-level efficiency) that would help a practitioner assess how close the Triton kernel is to hardware limits.

**Mitigation status.** The authors transparently disclose the Triton implementation and state that a CUDA kernel is "ongoing" and "expected to yield further performance improvements" (Section 6.2). This is a partial mitigation—the limitation is acknowledged and a path to resolution is indicated—but it means the current decoding speed results should be treated as a **feasibility demonstration** rather than a performance benchmark suitable for production planning.

---

### No Long-Context Quality Evaluation Exists Despite the Paper's Central Motivation

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire motivation is framed around long-context inference:

> "managing longer sequences during inference poses significant computational and memory challenges, particularly due to the storage of key-value (KV) caches" (Section 1)

> "TPA's memory efficiency and computational efficiency at the decoding stage enable processing longer sequences under fixed resource constraints, addressing a critical scalability challenge in modern language models" (Abstract)

Yet **all language modeling quality experiments are conducted at the standard nanoGPT context length**—which, based on the codebase referenced (Karpathy, 2022), is 1024 tokens. The paper provides **no perplexity evaluation at extended context lengths** (e.g., 8K, 32K, 128K), no needle-in-a-haystack retrieval tests, no long-document question answering, and no evaluation of whether TPA models trained at standard contexts can extrapolate to longer inference contexts.

**The consequence.** The central claim that TPA "enables processing longer sequences under fixed resource constraints" is **entirely unsupported by quality evidence**. The paper demonstrates that TPA uses less memory per token (a structural property) and that FlashTPA decoding at long sequences is fast (a kernel-level property), but it never demonstrates that a TPA model trained with factorized attention can **effectively utilise** long contexts. The concern is not merely hypothetical: low-rank key representations might lose the precision needed to distinguish between many similar tokens in a very long context, or the factorized structure might limit the model's ability to form the sharp attention patterns needed for precise retrieval over hundreds of thousands of tokens.

MLA, the most direct comparison, was evaluated in DeepSeek-V2 with a 128K context window and demonstrated strong long-context performance on the "Needle in a Haystack" test. The paper provides no equivalent evidence for TPA. A practitioner who adopts TPA specifically for long-context applications (the paper's stated target use case) is doing so based on memory formulas and kernel benchmarks alone, without any evidence that the factorized attention mechanism preserves long-range attention quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Zero. The decoding speed experiments (Section 6.2) benchmark raw kernel performance at sequence lengths up to 524,288 tokens, but these are **synthetic benchmarks**—they measure the time to compute attention scores, not the quality of the model's long-context behaviour. There is no trained long-context TPA model, no perplexity-at-length curve, and no task-based evaluation beyond the standard 1024-token context.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not identify the absence of long-context quality evaluation as a limitation, and the abstract's claim about enabling longer sequences is presented as if it follows directly from the memory reduction and decoding speed results. This is a significant gap between the paper's motivating narrative and its empirical evidence. Future work would need to train TPA models at extended context lengths (at minimum 8K-32K tokens) and evaluate them on standard long-context benchmarks to validate the central motivation.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **reframing of the KV-cache problem** that shifts the research conversation from post-hoc compression to representational design. Prior to this work, the dominant approaches—MQA, GQA, MLA, token eviction, cache quantization—all accepted the `h × d_h` key/value shape as the fundamental representation and sought to approximate, compress, or selectively discard it. TPA challenges this premise directly: it asks whether full-dimensional keys and values are necessary in the first place, and demonstrates empirically that factorized representations with rank as low as `R_K=R_V=2` can match or exceed the quality of the full-dimensional equivalents. This is a **methodological shift**, not merely a new architecture: it changes the question from "how much can we throw away?" to "what is the right structure for attention representations?"

The magnitude of this shift is **substantial but bounded**. It is not a paradigm shift on the scale of the original Transformer or the introduction of attention itself—TPA operates entirely within the scaled dot-product attention framework and modifies only the projection step. But within the subfield of efficient attention, it represents a genuine conceptual advance. Section 4's unification of MHA, MQA, and GQA as non-contextual special cases of TPA reveals that the entire family of multi-head attention variants can be understood through two continuous design axes: **rank** (`R_K, R_V`) and **contextuality** (whether factors are fixed or input-dependent). This transforms a fragmented landscape of named mechanisms into a coherent design space, making future architectural choices principled rather than heuristic. Researchers no longer need to ask "should I use MQA or GQA?"—they can ask "what rank and contextuality configuration is optimal for my compute budget and task distribution?"

The work also **reconciles a latent tension** in the efficient-attention literature. MLA (Liu et al., 2024) demonstrated that low-rank compression of keys and values could approach MHA quality, but at the cost of a complicated RoPE integration requiring separate position-encoded parameters (`d_R^h`). TPA shows that this tradeoff is not inherent to low-rank representations—it is an artifact of MLA's specific compression approach (factorizing projection weights rather than activations). The structural separation of head and feature dimensions in TPA's tensor product formulation makes RoPE integration clean and proven (Theorem 3.1), eliminating the extra `d_R^h` parameter overhead. This resolves the apparent tension between compression and positional encoding compatibility: you can have both, if the compression is structured correctly.

The paper makes certain research directions **more attractive**. The finding that TPA matches or exceeds MHA quality at 1.5B parameters (Tables 2-3, 12) while using 10× less KV-cache memory suggests that scaling TPA to larger models (7B, 70B, 200B+) is a high-priority experiment. The unified framework in Section 4 makes systematic exploration of the rank-contextuality design space newly tractable—rather than comparing discrete mechanism names, researchers can sweep continuous rank parameters. The FlashTPA decoding results (Figures 5, 7, 8), despite using a suboptimal Triton implementation, demonstrate that factorized attention computation has favorable asymptotic scaling with sequence length, making long-context inference a particularly promising application domain.

Conversely, this work makes certain directions **less attractive**. The paper's demonstration that MQA and GQA are non-contextual special cases of TPA (with fixed, non-adaptive head factors) suggests that further incremental refinements of static parameter sharing (e.g., more granular grouping, learned static groupings) are unlikely to match contextual factorization. The ReST^EM analogy from the training example paper doesn't apply here, but the implication is similar: static sharing patterns are a dead end compared to input-dependent allocation. MLA's approach of compressing through a fixed bottleneck dimension `d_c` is also rendered less compelling by TPA's superior results at matched or smaller cache sizes—if contextuality is the key ingredient, MLA's static compression will always be at a representational disadvantage.

The paper also **shifts the bottleneck narrative**. For MHA, the bottleneck was raw memory capacity—the KV cache simply grew too large for long sequences. For MQA and GQA, the bottleneck became representational capacity—sharing keys and values across heads limited the diversity of attention patterns. For MLA, the bottleneck became architectural complexity—the RoPE workaround added parameters and implementation overhead. TPA's results suggest that **verifier quality** is not the relevant concept here (unlike the training example paper), but rather that the new bottleneck is **rank selection**: the paper provides no principled method for choosing `R_K` and `R_V` for a given task and scale, and the rank ablation (Figure 10) shows that quality continues improving through `R=6` at 1.5B parameters, meaning the optimal rank at production scales is unknown. The practical bottleneck shifts from "how do we compress the cache?" to "what is the right rank for each layer and each attention component?"

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling TPA to production model sizes (7B–70B+) with systematic rank sweeps.** The paper's largest model is 1.5B parameters—roughly 5× smaller than LLaMA-7B and 45× smaller than LLaMA-70B. The positive scaling trend (TPA's advantage over MHA grows from near-zero at 124M to ~1 percentage point at 1.5B) is encouraging but insufficient to predict behavior at production scale. A strong follow-up would train TPA models at 7B and 13B parameters on a standard pretraining corpus (e.g., C4, The Pile, or FineWeb-Edu at scale), sweeping `R_K = R_V ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}` at each scale, and measuring both perplexity and downstream task performance. The key question is whether the rank-quality curve saturates at a manageable value (e.g., `R=4` or `R=8`) or whether it continues to demand higher ranks at larger scales, eroding the memory savings. This would produce a **scaling law for attention rank**, analogous to the Chinchilla scaling laws for model size and data, that would guide rank selection for any target model size. Additionally, training stability at scale must be monitored: the tensor product `A(x_t)^\top B(x_t)` multiplies two projected quantities, and at billion-parameter scales, poor conditioning of the factor matrices could manifest as loss spikes or divergence that are invisible at 1.5B.

**Long-context training and evaluation of TPA models.** The paper's central motivation—enabling longer sequences under fixed memory—is entirely unsupported by quality evidence at long contexts. All training uses standard nanoGPT context lengths (likely 1024 tokens), and no long-context perplexity or task evaluation exists. A critical follow-up would train TPA models with extended context windows (at minimum 8K, ideally 32K–128K tokens) and evaluate on standard long-context benchmarks: perplexity as a function of position within the context (to test whether factorized keys lose precision for distant tokens), needle-in-a-haystack retrieval accuracy (to test whether the factorization preserves the sharp attention patterns needed for precise lookup), and long-document QA (e.g., NarrativeQA, QMSum). The comparison must include MHA, GQA, and MLA at matched context lengths and cache sizes. A negative result—TPA degrading more rapidly than MHA as context length grows—would be equally valuable, as it would characterize the **precision limits** of factorized key representations and potentially motivate hybrid approaches (e.g., higher rank for early layers where precise retrieval matters, lower rank for later layers where semantic abstraction dominates).

**Layer-wise and component-wise rank adaptation.** The paper uses uniform ranks `R_Q, R_K, R_V` across all layers, but different layers in a Transformer are known to specialise—early layers focus on local syntax, middle layers on semantic composition, and late layers on task-specific aggregation. The optimal rank likely varies by layer and by component (Q, K, V). A strong follow-up would train models where `R_K^{(l)}` and `R_V^{(l)}` are layer-specific learnable parameters (or selected via a differentiable architecture search), and measure whether some layers can operate at very low rank (e.g., `R=1`) while others require higher capacity. This connects directly to the **contextual adaptivity** argument: if TPA's benefit comes from input-dependent allocation of representational capacity, then layer-wise adaptivity is the natural generalization—different layers process different types of information and should have different capacity budgets. The paper's non-contextual ablation (Tables 5-8) showing that fixed head factors hurt less than fixed feature factors suggests that the head-dimension rank (controlling cross-head sharing) might be the dimension where layer-wise adaptation is most impactful.

**Combining TPA with complementary compression techniques.** TPA's factorized KV cache is orthogonal to quantization, token eviction, and head pruning—the factor components `A^K, \tilde{B}^K, A^V, B^V` can themselves be quantized (e.g., to INT8 or INT4), and unimportant tokens can be evicted based on factor norms rather than full key/value norms. A strong follow-up would benchmark the compound compression ratio: TPA at `R_K=R_V=2` already gives ~10× reduction; adding 4-bit quantization of the cached factors would give another ~4× (from FP16 to INT4), yielding ~40× total compression relative to MHA. The critical question is whether factorized representations are more or less amenable to quantization than full-dimensional keys—the factors have different value distributions (products of projected quantities) and may saturate quantisation bins differently. A negative result (factorization + quantization hurts more than quantization alone) would reveal an important interaction between the two forms of compression.

**Task-specific analysis of where low-rank representations fail.** The paper's benchmark suite (ARC, BoolQ, HellaSwag, etc.) measures aggregate commonsense reasoning and factual knowledge—tasks that may not stress the attention mechanism's capacity for fine-grained token-level distinctions. A critical follow-up would design **adversarial evaluations** specifically targeting the representational bottleneck of factorized attention: tasks requiring the model to track multiple distinct entities with identical surface forms (e.g., "Alice gave Bob the book. Charlie took it from Bob. Who has the book? Charlie gave it to Alice. Who has it now?"), retrieval tasks where the correct answer depends on distinguishing between very similar keys (e.g., "The revenue in Q3 was $1.2M. The revenue in Q4 was $1.3M. What was Q3's revenue?" presented in a 128K-token document with many similar-sounding financial figures), and tasks requiring precise long-range copying (e.g., reproducing a 100-digit number presented early in the context). If TPA degrades disproportionately on these tasks compared to MHA, it would characterise the **failure modes** of factorized representations—not as a reason to reject TPA, but as a diagnostic for when higher rank or hybrid approaches are needed. The paper's rank ablation (Figure 10) already hints at this: even at `R=6`, validation loss is still improving, suggesting that the current ranks are below the saturation point for some subset of tokens or attention patterns.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device deployment of mid-scale models with extended context.** TPA's most immediately actionable benefit is for models in the 1B–3B parameter range deployed on edge devices (smartphones, laptops, embedded systems) where GPU memory is severely constrained (4–8 GB total, shared with the application and OS). At the 1.5B scale, the paper's TPA-KVonly with `R_K=R_V=2` uses ~9.4% of MHA's KV-cache memory (Section 3.3). For a concrete deployment scenario: a 1.5B-parameter LLaMA-style model with MHA, `d_model=1600`, `h=25`, `d_h=64`, processing a 32K-token context, would require `2 × 32,768 × 25 × 64 = 104.9 \text{ million}` cached values, or ~210 MB at FP16. The same model with TPA-KVonly (`R_K=R_V=2`, `h=47` per Table 10 for parameter matching) would cache `(2+2) × (47+64) = 444` values per token, totaling `444 × 32,768 = 14.5 \text{ million}` values, or ~29 MB at FP16—a **7.2× reduction**. For a device with 6 GB of usable memory, this is the difference between fitting the model with a 32K context versus being limited to ~4K. The paper's quality results at 1.5B (Table 12: TPA-KVonly at 55.03% vs. MHA at 54.49% average 0-shot) provide evidence that this memory savings does not come at a quality cost.

**Cost-efficient batch inference for document processing pipelines.** Organizations running large-scale inference over document corpora (e.g., summarizing millions of support tickets, extracting entities from legal contracts, generating embeddings for semantic search over a codebase) process long sequences in batches. For a batch of 16 sequences of length 64K tokens with a 7B-parameter model, the KV cache with MHA can exceed model parameter memory, becoming the dominant cost driver. TPA's `(R_K+R_V)(h+d_h)` per-token formula means the cache size is **decoupled from the number of heads**—it depends only on the ranks, which are architectural constants, rather than `h` which grows with model size. For a 7B-class model with `h=32`, `d_h=128`, and TPA `R_K=R_V=2`, the cache per token is `4 × 160 = 640` values, versus MHA's `2 × 32 × 128 = 8,192` values—a **12.8× reduction**. This directly reduces the GPU instance count or instance size needed to serve a given throughput, with the paper's validation loss curves (Figure 4) and downstream results (Tables 2-3) providing evidence that the quality impact is neutral to positive at the scales tested.

**Training data generation and self-improvement pipelines with extended context.** When LLMs are used to generate training data (e.g., chain-of-thought reasoning traces, code solutions, or multi-turn dialogue), the sequences can be very long. TPA's factorized cache enables the generator model to process and attend to long contexts (e.g., entire code repositories, full conversation histories) within a fixed memory budget, potentially producing higher-quality training data that incorporates more context. The paper's FlashTPA decoding results (Section 6.2, Figure 5) showing faster-than-MLA decoding at long sequence lengths (e.g., ~4× faster than MHA at 524K tokens, batch size 1) mean that the generation throughput is also improved. For a self-improvement loop (like STaR or ReST^EM analogs applied to language modeling), where a model generates solutions, filters for correctness, and retrains, the generation phase is often the bottleneck—TPA reduces both the memory cost and the time cost of this phase, making longer-context self-improvement economically viable at scales where it would otherwise be prohibitive.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper provides sufficient comparative evidence against MHA, MQA, GQA, and MLA to support a conditional decision framework, though the framework necessarily extrapolates from 1.5B-scale results:

- **Prefer TPA over MHA when** inference memory is a binding constraint and you can tolerate the implementation complexity of a custom attention kernel. At the scales tested (124M–1.5B), TPA matches or exceeds MHA quality (Tables 2-3, 11-16) while using `(R_K+R_V)(h+d_h) / (2hd_h)` of the KV-cache memory—typically 5–15% for the ranks used. The paper provides no evidence that this holds above 1.5B, but the positive scaling trend (growing advantage with model size) is encouraging. If you need long-context processing (beyond 8K tokens) at these model sizes, TPA's memory advantage is decisive.

- **Prefer TPA over MLA when** you need native RoPE integration without architectural workarounds, or when your model uses a head dimension `d_h` and head count `h` where TPA's cache size `(R_K+R_V)(h+d_h)` is smaller than MLA's `d_c + d_R^h` at comparable quality. For the large-model configuration in Appendix A.4 (Example Comparison III: `d_model=7168`, `h=64`, `d_h=128`), TPA with `R_K=R_V=1` caches 384 values per token versus MLA's 576—33% smaller cache. TPA-KVonly achieves 53.52% vs. MLA's 53.32% 0-shot at 773M (Table 3), suggesting comparable quality at smaller cache. However, MLA has been validated at 236B+ parameters (DeepSeek-V2), while TPA is unvalidated above 1.5B—so for production-scale models, this preference is based on extrapolation.

- **Prefer TPA over GQA/MQA when** you want to avoid committing to a fixed head-grouping structure at architecture design time, or when you observe that your task distribution includes both "easy" tokens (where aggressive head sharing is appropriate) and "hard" tokens (where heads should specialise). TPA's contextual factors allow per-token adaptation of the effective sharing pattern, which GQA's static groups cannot provide. The paper's results show TPA outperforming GQA at most scales (e.g., Medium 0-shot: TPA 51.41% vs. GQA 50.35%, Table 2; XL 2-shot: TPA-KVonly 57.11% vs. GQA 56.49%, Table 16). The gap is modest (0.5–1.5 percentage points) but consistent.

- **Prefer MHA over TPA when** implementation simplicity and ecosystem compatibility are paramount, you are not memory-constrained, or your model scale is very small (<100M parameters) where the paper shows the TPA advantage disappears (Small scale, Table 11: TPA 46.21% vs. MHA 46.19%). MHA benefits from highly optimized kernels (FlashAttention-3, cuDNN fused attention, etc.) with years of engineering investment; TPA's FlashTPA kernel is currently in Triton with a CUDA version in development, meaning the engineering maturity gap is substantial. If you need to deploy tomorrow on standard inference frameworks (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, llama.cpp) with minimal custom kernel work, MHA or GQA remain the pragmatic choices until TPA kernels mature.
