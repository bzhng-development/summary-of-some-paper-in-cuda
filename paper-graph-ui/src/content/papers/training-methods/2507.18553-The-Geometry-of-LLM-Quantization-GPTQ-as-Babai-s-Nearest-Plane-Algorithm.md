# The Geometry of LLM Quantization: GPTQ as Babai's Nearest Plane Algorithm

**ArXiv:** [2507.18553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18553)

## 🎯 Pitch

GPTQ, a workhorse for 4-bit LLM quantization, is secretly Babai's classic lattice-decoding algorithm in reverse—a finding that finally explains its global success and gives a sharp error bound, but only when weight clipping is dropped. The authors exploit this to build no-clipping methods that beat the original GPTQ in accuracy.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper reveals that GPTQ—a standard one-shot post-training quantization method for large language models—is mathematically identical to **Babai’s nearest plane algorithm** for the classical closest vector problem (CVP) on a lattice defined by the layer’s input Hessian, provided GPTQ executes back-to-front (from last to first dimension) without weight clipping. Working with Qwen3 and Llama model families on WikiText-2, C4, and standard zero-shot benchmarks, the authors establish this equivalence through both geometric and algebraic proofs, demonstrating that GPTQ’s error propagation step corresponds exactly to Babai’s iterative hyperplane projection (rounding coefficients along Gram-Schmidt directions). The equivalence yields a tight layer-wise error bound of $\frac{1}{4} \sum_{j=1}^c \|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j\|^2$ in the no-clipping setting and motivates two overflow-tolerant quantization methods—**Scale-adjusted SpQR (SSQR)** using per-group scales with sparse outlier capture, and **Huffman-encoded post-training quantization (HPTQ)** using a single scalar with Huffman-coded integers—that outperform original GPTQ at matched bitwidths while a custom CUDA kernel delivers approximately 2× end-to-end inference speedup over PyTorch BF16 on NVIDIA A6000, establishing that decades of lattice algorithm research directly transfer to billion-parameter model compression only when weight clipping is eliminated.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Problem: GPTQ Works but Nobody Knows Why

GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023) is one of the most widely deployed post-training quantization algorithms for large language models. It was the first method to push one-shot quantization reliably into the 4-bit regime while preserving near-baseline accuracy on billion-parameter models, and it remains state-of-the-art in certain compression regimes (Kurtic et al., 2025). The procedure itself is deceptively simple to state: pick one weight at a time in a fixed order, quantize it by rounding to the nearest representable value on a quantization grid, compute the resulting error, then "propagate" that error to the remaining unquantized weights through a carefully chosen linear update. Repeat until all weights are quantized.

Yet for all its empirical success, GPTQ comes with a fundamental gap between *what* the algorithm does and *why* it works. The method is described in its original paper as a sequence of algebraic operations—rounding, error computation, matrix-vector updates—whose correctness is verified by showing that each step optimizes a local quadratic proxy for the layer-wise output error. But this local greedy perspective leaves a dangling question: **why should locally optimal, sequentially greedy rounding decisions add up to a globally good solution?** Greedy algorithms are notorious for accumulating cascading errors that compound into poor global solutions; GPTQ somehow avoids this fate, but the original presentation offers no explanation for *why* the error propagation step effectively cancels out the damage done by quantization.

This gap is not merely an aesthetic one—the absence of a principled understanding carries concrete practical consequences:

- **Design guidance is absent.** Practitioners who want to extend GPTQ (e.g., to new quantization grids, structured sparsity patterns, or activation quantization) must work by trial and error because there is no theory telling them which modifications preserve the algorithm's desirable properties and which break them.

- **Failure modes are opaque.** When GPTQ degrades on certain layers, models, or bitwidths, there is no diagnostic framework for understanding *why*. All one sees is that perplexity spikes; whether this is caused by poor Hessian conditioning, unfavorable weight distributions, or the interaction between rounding and clipping remains unknown.

- **Worst-case guarantees are missing.** The original GPTQ paper provides no error bounds. A user deploying a 4-bit quantized model cannot bound how much the layer outputs could differ from the full-precision original in the worst case, which matters for safety-critical or numerically sensitive applications.

- **No connection to broader theory.** The quantization literature and the lattice algorithms literature have evolved largely independently, despite solving structurally similar problems. Without a bridge between them, decades of algorithmic advances in one field cannot transfer to the other.

### Why Closing This Gap Matters (Real-World and Theoretical Significance)

The practical stakes are enormous. Quantization is the *de facto* approach to making LLMs deployable on affordable hardware. A 70-billion parameter model stored at 16 bits occupies roughly 140 GB—far exceeding the memory of consumer GPUs (typically 8–24 GB). Quantization to 4 bits shrinks this to roughly 35 GB, bringing it within range of a single high-end consumer card. Without accurate quantization, the economic and environmental case for widespread LLM deployment collapses: larger models would require datacenter-scale hardware, concentrating access among the few organizations that can afford it.

But the theoretical stakes are equally significant. The paper's central insight—that LLM weight quantization is an instance of the classical **closest vector problem (CVP)**—connects two fields with fundamentally different intellectual traditions. The CVP, and lattice algorithms more broadly, has been studied for over four decades. Results include hardness proofs (CVP is NP-hard to approximate within any constant factor under polynomial-time reductions; van Emde Boas, 1981; Micciancio & Goldwasser, 2002), approximation algorithms like Babai's nearest plane heuristic (Babai, 1986), and basis reduction techniques like LLL (Lenstra et al., 1982) and BKZ (Kannan, 1987). These are not merely academic curiosities—they underpin modern cryptography (lattice-based post-quantum encryption relies on CVP hardness) and have been honed for computational efficiency. If quantization reduces to CVP, then this entire algorithmic toolkit becomes available for model compression. Better basis reduction, better approximation algorithms, and better error analysis from the lattice community could directly translate into better-quantized LLMs.

Conversely, LLM quantization poses CVP instances at an unprecedented scale—tens of millions of variables across thousands of layers, with structured covariance matrices (Hessians) derived from real data rather than worst-case random lattices. This may reveal structure in CVP instances that the lattice community has not yet studied, potentially motivating new theoretical questions.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

**Second-order compression: from Optimal Brain Damage to GPTQ.** The lineage is important for understanding why GPTQ's missing theory matters. **Optimal Brain Damage** (LeCun et al., 1989) introduced the idea that a trained neural network's Hessian matrix (second derivatives of the loss with respect to weights) identifies which weights are "least important" and can be pruned with minimal impact. **Optimal Brain Surgeon** (OBS; Hassibi et al., 1993) refined this by showing that after removing one weight, the optimal adjustment to all remaining weights—the "error propagation" that compensates for the removal—is given by a formula involving the inverse Hessian:

When you prune weight $j$, update all other weights $w \leftarrow w + \frac{H^{-1}[:, j]}{H^{-1}[j,j]} \cdot \Delta w_j$

where $\Delta w_j$ is the perturbation introduced by setting the weight to zero (or to a quantized value). This ensures that the *linearized* increase in loss is minimized at each step.

**Optimal Brain Compression** (OBC; Frantar & Alistarh, 2022) unified pruning and quantization under this same framework, with the quantization variant called **Optimal Brain Quantizer (OBQ)**. OBQ processes weights one at a time, but with a crucial refinement: at each step, it *dynamically selects* which weight to quantize next based on which choice would minimize the immediate error (Equation 1 in the paper). This dynamic ordering is computationally expensive—it requires re-evaluating the Hessian inverse for the remaining weights after each step—yielding cubic complexity in the number of remaining weights. For a standard transformer layer with $c = 4096$ input features, this is manageable ($O(c^3)$ per layer), but for LLM-scale layers with $c$ in the thousands, the per-weight dynamic re-evaluation makes OBQ impractical.

**GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023)** is the practical successor. Its key insight is to **abandon dynamic order selection** and instead process weights in a fixed, pre-determined order (defaulting to front-to-back along the input dimension). This one change has cascading computational benefits:

1. The Hessian inverse only needs to be computed **once** per layer, not updated after every weight.
2. The error propagation coefficients (the columns of the inverse Hessian) can be **shared across all output channels** of a weight matrix, since the Hessian $X^\top X$ depends only on the inputs and not on which output channel is being quantized.
3. Using LDL or Cholesky decomposition, the necessary coefficients can be pre-computed efficiently.

This reduces the complexity from $O(c^4)$ for OBQ to $O(c^3)$ for GPTQ—the difference between hours and seconds for an LLM-scale layer. The method works remarkably well in practice, but the theoretical cost is real: by abandoning OBQ's principled dynamic ordering, GPTQ loses the step-by-step optimality guarantees. The fixed order is a heuristic, and there is no a priori reason to believe it should produce globally good solutions.

**QuIP (Chee et al., 2023)** made a step toward bridging this gap by proving an error guarantee for GPTQ and proposing LDLQ as an equivalent variant. However, their analysis remained algebraic—it did not reveal the geometric structure of the problem or connect it to the CVP literature. The guarantee existed, but without geometric intuition, it was difficult to interpret or improve.

**Existing attempts at combining quantization with lattice ideas.** Some prior work invoked lattice concepts for quantization, most notably the QuIP# method (Tseng et al., 2024a) and QTIP (Tseng et al., 2024b), which use lattice codebooks (E8, for instance) to define the quantization grid. But this is a *superficial* use of lattice theory—it treats the quantization grid as a lattice (a discrete set of representable vectors) but does not recognize that the *optimization problem itself* (finding the best quantized weights) is a lattice problem. In other words, these methods use lattices as a codebook design tool, not as a problem formulation framework. The deeper connection—that the search for optimal quantized weights *is* the closest vector problem—remained unmade.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

This paper makes a specific, falsifiable claim: **GPTQ, when executed back-to-front (from the last to the first dimension), is mathematically identical to Babai's nearest plane algorithm applied to the lattice defined by the input Hessian, without LLL basis reduction.** This is not an approximate equivalence or a conceptual analogy—it is exact, down to the intermediate computations, as proven in Section 4 and Appendix C.

The positioning is precise relative to prior work:

- **Relative to GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2023):** The paper does not propose a new quantization algorithm per se; it *reinterprets* GPTQ, providing the geometric meaning the original paper lacked. The practical methods (SSQR, HPTQ) are consequences of the theory, not the main contribution.

- **Relative to OBQ (Frantar & Alistarh, 2022):** The paper shows that OBQ's dynamic dimension selection (Equation 1) has a geometric interpretation as choosing the nearest hyperplane to the current residual (Corollary 3), connecting the seemingly arbitrary algebraic formula to geometric intuition.

- **Relative to QuIP (Chee et al., 2023):** The paper goes beyond algebraic guarantees to full geometric equivalence, enabling the import of Babai's error bounds and opening the door to basis reduction techniques that QuIP did not exploit.

- **Relative to lattice-based quantization methods (QuIP#, QTIP):** The paper argues that prior work used lattices as codebooks but missed the deeper connection that the *quantization problem itself is CVP*. This distinction matters because it means lattice *algorithms* (search, reduction, approximation) can be applied, not just lattice *codebooks*.

- **Relative to the CVP literature:** The paper positions LLM quantization as a new, practically important instance of CVP that motivates revisiting classical algorithms at scale. The LLL reduction—standard in Babai's algorithm for theoretical guarantees—is shown to be incompatible with batched quantization (Section B), identifying a specific challenge for transferring lattice techniques to LLMs.

The paper explicitly connects its work to the broader training-inference tradeoff by demonstrating that its no-clipping variants (SSQR, HPTQ) outperform the original GPTQ at matched bitwidths, and that the resulting quantized models can be served with roughly 2× speedup via custom CUDA kernels. But the deeper contribution is theoretical: establishing that GPTQ is not an ad-hoc heuristic but rather an instance of a classical algorithm with decades of analysis behind it, thereby unlocking principled extensions rather than trial-and-error modifications.

One final piece of positioning deserves emphasis: the paper demonstrates that **clipping is the obstacle to theoretical guarantees.** GPTQ as originally implemented clips weights that would quantize outside the representable range (e.g., for INT4, values that would round to integers outside $\{-8, \ldots, 7\}$). This clipping breaks the Babai equivalence because it introduces non-lattice constraints—the rounding boundaries are no longer the infinite hyperplanes Babai's analysis assumes. By designing overflow-tolerant schemes (SSQR with sparse outlier storage, HPTQ with Huffman encoding) that eliminate clipping entirely, the paper shows that the CVP framework becomes directly applicable, and the theoretical guarantees hold. This insight—that the practical necessity of clipping, not any algorithmic limitation, was what prevented principled analysis—reframes the quantization design problem: rather than accepting clipping as inevitable and working around it, one should design representations that make clipping unnecessary.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

This is primarily a **theoretical analysis paper** that reinterprets an existing practical algorithm (GPTQ) through the lens of classical lattice geometry. The core idea is that GPTQ—when executed in reverse dimensional order and without clipping weights—is mathematically identical to Babai's nearest plane algorithm for solving the closest vector problem on a lattice defined by the layer's input Hessian matrix. The paper does not propose a fundamentally new quantization method; rather, it provides the missing geometric meaning for GPTQ's operations, derives error guarantees that were previously unavailable, and then designs practical quantization schemes that satisfy the theoretical assumptions (specifically, the no-clipping requirement) to inherit those guarantees.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The paper's argument proceeds in two interconnected halves—theory and application—connected by a single design constraint (no clipping):

**Theoretical Half (Section 4 and Appendices B–D):**
1. **Weight-space to activation-space mapping:** The standard linear-layer quantization objective `$\|XQ - XW\|_F^2$` is reformulated as a closest vector problem (CVP) where each output channel defines a separate lattice.
2. **GPTQ as Babai's algorithm:** The three-step algebraic proof (Appendix C) shows that GPTQ's error propagation update is Babai's hyperplane projection; when run back-to-front, the two algorithms produce identical integer solutions.
3. **Error bound derivation:** Babai's known worst-case guarantee on the squared residual norm is imported and expressed in terms of the LDL decomposition diagonal, giving a tight, computable bound for any no-clipping GPTQ run.

**Practical Half (Section 5 and Appendix E):**
1. **Overflow-tolerant quantization (SSQR):** A variant that stores clipped weights as sparse floating-point outliers, with a scale-adjustment binary search to control outlier density.
2. **Huffman-encoded quantization (HPTQ):** A variant that uses a single global scale with Huffman-coded integers, making every weight representable without clipping.
3. **CUDA inference kernel:** An optimized low-batch-latency kernel for SSQR that handles dense inliers (via tensor cores) and sparse outliers (via SIMT cores) separately on Ampere GPUs.

The binding thread: **weight clipping is the obstacle to theoretical guarantees.** Both SSQR and HPTQ eliminate clipping, making the no-clipping CVP framework directly applicable and enabling Babai's error bound to hold. The original GPTQ clips overflowed weights, which corresponds to constraining the CVP solution to a bounded subset of the infinite lattice—breaking the geometric structure that produces the error bound.

#### Linear-Layer Quantization Reformulated as CVP

The paper begins by showing that the standard per-layer quantization objective is structurally identical to the closest vector problem (CVP) from lattice theory. This reformulation is the foundation for everything that follows.

**The quantization objective.** Given a linear layer with input activations `$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}$` (batch size `$n$`, input dimension `$c$`), original weights `$W = [\mathbf{w}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{w}_r] \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times r}$` (output dimension `$r$`), and per-coordinate quantization scales `$S = [\mathbf{s}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{s}_r] \in \mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}^{c \times r}$`, the goal is to find quantized integer weights `$Z = [\mathbf{z}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{z}_r] \in \mathbb{Z}_\dagger^{c \times r}$` that minimize the L2 error on the layer output:

$$\min_{\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{Z}_\dagger^c} \| X \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i) \mathbf{z}_i - X\mathbf{w}_i \|^2 \quad \text{for all } 1 \leq i \leq r$$

where `$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$` is the quantization grid (e.g., `$\{-8, \ldots, 7\}$` for INT4 with clipping, or `$\mathbb{Z}$` for no-clipping), `$\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)$` is a `$c \times c$` diagonal matrix with the scale vector `$\mathbf{s}_i$` on its diagonal, and the dequantized weight is `$\mathbf{q}_i = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)\mathbf{z}_i$`.

**What this computes:** For each output channel `$i$`, find the integer vector `$\mathbf{z}_i$` such that when scaled by the per-coordinate factors `$\mathbf{s}_i$` and multiplied by the input matrix `$X$`, the resulting activations are as close as possible (in Euclidean norm) to the original activations `$X\mathbf{w}_i$`. The total loss is the sum over all `$r$` output channels, making this a **separable problem per channel**—each `$\mathbf{z}_i$` can be optimized independently.

**Why this form:** The per-channel separability is crucial for computational tractability. Without it, the quantization of one output channel would depend on that of another, creating a coupled optimization problem of dimension `$cr$` instead of `$r$` independent problems of dimension `$c$`. GPTQ exploits this separability implicitly by applying identical error propagation coefficients (derived from the input Hessian) across all output channels.

**The CVP equivalence.** A canonical CVP instance asks: given a lattice basis `$B = [\mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_c] \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}$` and a target vector `$\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n$`, find the integer coefficient vector `$\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{Z}^c$` that minimizes `$\| B\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{y} \|^2$`. Comparing the CVP objective with the quantization objective reveals a direct structural match: set `$B = X \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)$` and `$\mathbf{y} = X\mathbf{w}_i$`. Then finding `$\mathbf{z}_i$` for the quantization problem is exactly finding the integer vector for the CVP with basis `$B$` and target `$\mathbf{y}$`.

**The quantization-CVP dictionary (Table 1).** The paper provides an explicit mapping between quantization concepts and CVP concepts for each output channel `$i$`:

| Quantization concept | CVP interpretation |
|---|---|
| Input activation `$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}$` | Basis directions (columns) |
| Scale vector `$\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}^c$` | Basis stretches (length scaling per direction) |
| `$B^{(i)} = X \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)$` | Lattice basis (columns generate the lattice) |
| Floating-point weight `$\mathbf{w}_i \in \mathbb{R}^c$` | Coordinates on the unstretched basis |
| Integer representation `$\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{Z}_\dagger^c$` | Integer coordinates on the lattice basis |
| Dequantized weight `$\mathbf{q}_i = \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)\mathbf{z}_i$` | Dequantized coordinates on the unstretched basis |
| Target activation `$\mathbf{y}^{(i)} = X\mathbf{w}_i$` | External target vector to approximate |

**What this dictionary means operationally:** The input activation matrix `$X$` defines a set of `$c$` direction vectors in `$\mathbb{R}^n$` (one per input dimension). Multiplying by the scale diagonal stretches each direction by a per-coordinate factor. The resulting `$c$` vectors form a lattice basis: any integer combination of them produces a lattice point. The target activation `$X\mathbf{w}_i$` is the point in `$\mathbb{R}^n$` that we want to approximate using an integer combination of the stretched basis vectors. The quantized weights `$\mathbf{z}_i$` are the integer coefficients of that best-approximating combination. The dequantized weights `$\mathbf{q}_i$` convert those coefficients back to the original weight space by applying the inverse scaling.

**Hessian factor invariance (Theorem 1).** The paper proves that any factor `$\bar{X}$` of the Hessian matrix `$X^\top X$` (i.e., any matrix satisfying `$\bar{X}^\top \bar{X} = X^\top X$`) produces a CVP that is equivalent to the original under an orthogonal transformation (rotation or reflection) of both the lattice and target. Formally:

> "The CVPs using any possible factors `$\bar{X}$` of the Hessian matrix `$X^\top X$` are equivalent under an orthogonal transformation (rotation and reflection) of the lattice and external target vector."

**What this invariance means:** Two matrices `$\bar{X}$` and `$\bar{X}'$` satisfying `$\bar{X}^\top \bar{X} = \bar{X}'^\top \bar{X}' = X^\top X$` produce lattice bases `$\bar{X} \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)$` and `$\bar{X}' \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)$` that are related by an orthogonal transformation—they have the same lengths and pairwise angles. Since orthogonal transformations preserve Euclidean distances, the optimal integer solution `$\mathbf{z}_i$` and the achieved error `$\|\bar{X} \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)\mathbf{z}_i - \bar{X}\mathbf{w}_i\|^2$` are identical regardless of which Hessian factor is used.

**Why this matters computationally:** The original input matrix `$X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times c}$` has `$n$` rows (e.g., 2048 for a calibration batch). Computing with the full `$n \times c$` matrix requires `$\mathcal{O}(nc)$` work per operation. But Theorem 1 allows replacing `$X$` with any square factor `$\bar{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times c}$` (obtainable via Cholesky decomposition), reducing the dimension from `$n \times c$` to `$c \times c$`. For typical GPTQ settings where `$n \gg c$`, this is a substantial computational saving. More importantly, it allows the Babai algorithm (which works with an explicit basis) to operate on a compact `$c \times c$` representation without loss of geometric fidelity.

**Domain matching: clipping vs. no-clipping.** The CVP formulation assumes the integer solution `$\mathbf{z}_i$` can be any vector in `$\mathbb{Z}^c$` (the infinite integer lattice). The quantization problem, by contrast, restricts `$\mathbf{z}_i$` to a finite grid `$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger^c$` (e.g., `$\{-8, \ldots, 7\}^c$` for INT4). These match only when either: (a) clipping is disabled in the quantization setup (`$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger = \mathbb{Z}$`), or (b) the CVP setup is modified to restrict the integer domain. The paper focuses on option (a)—disabling clipping—because it preserves the full CVP structure and enables importing Babai's error guarantees without modification. The practical challenge then becomes: how to handle weights that would be clipped without degrading accuracy, which Section 5 addresses through overflow-tolerant representations.

#### OBQ's Geometric Interpretation: Error Propagation as Nearest-Hyperplane Projection

Before proving the GPTQ-Babai equivalence, the paper first establishes the geometric meaning of OBQ (GPTQ's slower predecessor), which uses a dynamically selected dimension order rather than a fixed one. This intermediate step builds geometric intuition and provides a template for the main proof.

**Theorem 2 (Error Propagation and Babai's Projection).** The paper proves that OBQ's error propagation step (Equation 2 in the original algorithms):

$$\Delta w_i[j'] \leftarrow \frac{(X[:, J]^\top X[:, J])^{-1}[j', j]}{(X[:, J]^\top X[:, J])^{-1}[j, j]} (q_i[j] - w_i[j])$$

is exactly Babai's projection of the target vector onto the nearest hyperplane of the chosen basis direction, without any basis reduction.

**Proof sketch (geometric).** The proof works in the activation space `$\mathbb{R}^n$` where the lattice basis columns `$\mathbf{b}_j$` live. Let `$J$` be the set of not-yet-quantized dimensions, and let `$j_1, j_2 \in J$` be two such dimensions with `$j_1 \neq j_2$`. The current residual target vector is `$\mathbf{y} = \sum_{j \in J} \zeta_j \mathbf{b}_j$`, where `$\zeta_j \in \mathbb{R}$` are the continuous coordinates to be rounded to integers. When Babai's algorithm projects `$\mathbf{y}$` onto the nearest hyperplane of basis `$\mathbf{b}_{j_2}$` (the hyperplane `$\lfloor \zeta_{j_2} \rceil \mathbf{b}_{j_2} + \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf{b}_j : j \neq j_2\}$`), the projection error `$\Delta\mathbf{y}$` is a vector orthogonal to that hyperplane. This error can be decomposed into components along the original basis directions: `$\Delta\mathbf{y} = \sum_{j \in J} \Delta\zeta_j \mathbf{b}_j$`.

The key quantity to determine is how the coefficient change `$\Delta\zeta_{j_1}$` in basis direction `$\mathbf{b}_{j_1}$` relates to `$\Delta\zeta_{j_2}$` in the projected direction `$\mathbf{b}_{j_2}$`. To isolate this relationship, the proof introduces the **inverse basis** `$N = B^{-\top} = [\mathbf{n}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{n}_c]$`, which satisfies `$\langle \mathbf{n}_j, \mathbf{b}_j \rangle = 1$` and `$\mathbf{n}_j \perp \mathbf{b}_{j'}$` for all `$j \neq j'$`. These inverse vectors are the dual basis; each `$\mathbf{n}_j$` extracts the `$j$`-th coordinate from any vector via inner product.

The proof collapses all basis directions except `$j_1$` and `$j_2$` into a single dimension (a hyperline), then projects the geometry onto the 2D plane spanned by `$\mathbf{n}_{j_1}$` and `$\mathbf{n}_{j_2}$` (the orthogonal projection plane, OPP). On this 2D plane, the relationship between `$\Delta\zeta_{j_1}$` and `$\Delta\zeta_{j_2}$` is determined by a simple trigonometric argument involving the angle `$\theta = \angle(\mathbf{n}_{j_1}, \mathbf{n}_{j_2}) = \pi - \angle(\operatorname{Proj}_{\text{OPP}}(\mathbf{b}_{j_1}), \operatorname{Proj}_{\text{OPP}}(\mathbf{b}_{j_2}))$`. Carrying through the trigonometry yields:

$$\Delta\zeta_{j_1} = \frac{\langle \mathbf{n}_{j_1}, \mathbf{n}_{j_2} \rangle}{\langle \mathbf{n}_{j_2}, \mathbf{n}_{j_2} \rangle} \Delta\zeta_{j_2}$$

Now substitute `$\langle \mathbf{n}_j, \mathbf{n}_{j'} \rangle = (B^\top B)^{-1}[j, j']$` (since `$N^\top N = (B^{-\top})^\top B^{-\top} = B^{-1} B^{-\top} = (B^\top B)^{-1}$`), and set `$B = (X \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i))[:, J]$` with `$\zeta_j = w_i[j] / s_i[j]$`. The numerator becomes `$(B^\top B)^{-1}[j_1, j_2]$` and the denominator becomes `$(B^\top B)^{-1}[j_2, j_2]$`. Multiplying by the quantization error `$q_i[j_2] - w_i[j_2] = \Delta\zeta_{j_2} \cdot s_i[j_2]$` recovers exactly OBQ's error propagation formula (Equation 2).

**What this proves geometrically:** When OBQ quantizes dimension `$j$` by rounding `$w_i[j]$` to the nearest representable value `$q_i[j]$`, the error `$q_i[j] - w_i[j]$` creates a discrepancy in the activation space. The error propagation step adjusts all not-yet-quantized weights `$w_i[j']$` by amounts proportional to the inverse Hessian entries, which is equivalent to projecting the target vector in activation space onto the hyperplane where the quantized dimension takes its rounded value. The remaining error (the distance between the original target and this projected target) lies entirely along the Gram-Schmidt direction orthogonal to the hyperplane, meaning it does not affect subsequent projections.

**Corollary 3 (OBQ Dimension Selection).** The paper further shows that OBQ's criterion for choosing which dimension to quantize next:

$$j \leftarrow \arg\min_{j \in J} \frac{(q_i[j] - w_i[j])^2}{(X[:, J]^\top X[:, J])^{-1}[j, j]}$$

can be rewritten as `$\arg\min_{j \in J} |\Delta\zeta_j| / \|\mathbf{n}_j\|$`, which is **exactly the Euclidean distance between the current residual target and the nearest hyperplane of basis direction** `$\mathbf{b}_j$`. In other words, OBQ always picks the dimension whose quantization will cause the smallest immediate displacement in activation space—it greedily minimizes the per-step error. This explains why OBQ's dynamic ordering is locally optimal: at each step, it projects the target the shortest possible distance to the nearest hyperplane, making the smallest possible perturbation to the residual.

#### The Main Equivalence: GPTQ as Babai Without Basis Reduction

With the geometric interpretation of OBQ established, the paper proceeds to the central result: GPTQ, when run in reverse dimensional order, is identical to Babai's nearest plane algorithm without LLL basis reduction.

**The directional difference.** GPTQ (Algorithm 1) processes dimensions from the first to the last (`$j \leftarrow 1$` to `$c$`), while Babai's algorithm (Algorithm 2) processes from the last to the first (`$j \leftarrow c$` to `$1$`). This is the sole superficial difference; everything else follows from the choice of iteration direction.

**Theorem 4 (GPTQ and Babai).** The equivalence is stated concisely:

> "GPTQ and Babai's algorithm without basis reduction will have the same results if we align the dimensional order of these two algorithms, e.g., running GPTQ from the last to the first dimension."

**What this means precisely:** If you take the original GPTQ algorithm, change the loop to iterate `$j \leftarrow c$` down to `$1$` instead of `$1$` up to `$c$`, keep all other mechanics identical, and compare with Babai's algorithm applied to the lattice basis `$B^{(i)} = X \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i)$` and target `$\mathbf{y}^{(i)} = X\mathbf{w}_i$` (using the same dimensional order), the resulting quantized integers `$\mathbf{z}_i$` and dequantized weights `$\mathbf{q}_i$` are identical—for every output channel `$i$` and every choice of scales `$\mathbf{s}_i$`.

**Geometric proof sketch.** The geometric proof builds directly on Theorem 2. At step `$j$` (running from `$c$` down to `$1$`), GPTQ rounds the current weight residual `$W[j, :]$` divided by the scales `$S[j, :]$` to the nearest integer, then propagates the resulting error to all preceding (not-yet-quantized) rows via `$W[j:, :] \leftarrow W[j:, :] + L[j:, j] \varepsilon$`, where `$L$` is the lower triangular matrix from the LDL decomposition of the permuted inverse Hessian. By Theorem 2, this propagation is exactly Babai's projection of the residual target onto the hyperplane orthogonal to the `$j$`-th Gram-Schmidt vector. Each subsequent iteration works with a target that has already been projected onto all later hyperplanes, so the sequence of projections matches Babai's back-to-front traversal exactly.

**Algebraic proof structure (Appendix C, Algorithms 5–8).** The algebraic proof proceeds in three steps, tracking the equivalence through progressively transformed versions of GPTQ:

**Step 1: Equivalence of relative-error and absolute-error forms.** The original GPTQ (Algorithm 5) maintains a row vector `$\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times r}$` of relative quantization errors and updates the weight matrix as `$W^{(j)} \leftarrow W^{(j-1)} + L\mathbf{e}_j \varepsilon^{(j)}$`. Algorithm 6 (GPTQ Type-2) equivalently maintains the cumulative absolute quantization error `$\Delta^{(j)} \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times r}$` defined as `$\Delta^{(j)} = \hat{Q}^{(j)} - \hat{W}^{(0)}$` (the difference between the current dequantized weights and the original floating-point weights) and updates the weight residual as `$\hat{W}^{(j)} \leftarrow \hat{W}^{(0)} - L^{-1}\Delta^{(j)}$`. The proof shows that `$\omega^{(j)} = \hat{\omega}^{(j)}$` for all `$j$`, meaning the row being quantized at each step is the same in both algorithms, and consequently the quantized integers and dequantized weights match step by step.

The key algebraic manipulation exploits the lower-triangular structure of `$L$` (which has ones on the diagonal) and its inverse `$L^{-1}$` (also lower-triangular with ones on the diagonal). Specifically, for `$j' < j$`, the entries `$\mathbf{e}_{j'}^\top L\mathbf{e}_j = 0$` and `$\mathbf{e}_{j'}^\top L^{-1}\mathbf{e}_j = 0$`, which reflects the fact that error propagation in the forward direction only affects later rows and in the backward (inverse) direction only affects earlier rows. The proof establishes that `$\varepsilon^{(k)} = \mathbf{e}_k^\top Q^{(k)} - \omega^{(k)} = \mathbf{e}_k^\top L^{-1}\Delta^{(k)}$` for all `$k \leq j$`, which connects the relative error in Algorithm 5 to the absolute error in Algorithm 6.

**Step 2: Reversing the iteration direction.** To run GPTQ back-to-front, Algorithm 7 replaces the LDL decomposition with a "UDU" decomposition: `$H^{-1} = U D_U^{1/2} D_U^{1/2} U^\top$` where `$U$` is upper triangular with ones on the diagonal. This is algebraically equivalent to taking the LDL decomposition of the anti-diagonally permuted inverse Hessian, then permuting back. The UDU decomposition ensures that when iterating `$j \leftarrow c$` down to `$1$`, the error propagation `$W^{(j)} \leftarrow W^{(c+1)} - U^{-1}\Delta^{(j)}$` correctly updates only the not-yet-quantized rows (rows `$1$` through `$j-1$`), leaving the already-quantized rows unchanged. This is the dual of the forward iteration, where `$L^{-1}$` updates only rows after the current one.

**Step 3: Equivalence with Babai's algorithm.** Algorithm 8 (Babai-Quantize) is derived from the general Babai framework with three specializations:
- The LLL basis reduction is replaced by a permutation (the quantization order) that does not depend on the output channel.
- The basis is chosen to be upper triangular (via Cholesky decomposition of the permuted Hessian), making the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization trivial (`$\Phi = I$`).
- The target vector is initialized as `$Y^{(c+1)} = A\tilde{W}$` where `$A = D_U^{-1/2} U^{-1}$` is the Cholesky factor of the permuted Hessian, and the residual is updated as `$Y^{(j)} \leftarrow Y^{(j+1)} - A\mathbf{e}_j \mathbf{e}_j^\top \tilde{Q}^{(j)}$`.

The proof shows that `$\hat{\omega}^{(j)} = \tilde{\omega}^{(j)}$` for all `$j$`, where `$\hat{\omega}^{(j)} = \mathbf{e}_j^\top \hat{W}^{(j+1)}$` is the row vector being quantized in the back-to-front GPTQ (Algorithm 7) and `$\tilde{\omega}^{(j)} = \mathbf{e}_j^\top D_U^{1/2} \tilde{Y}^{(j+1)}$` is the corresponding quantity in Babai-Quantize (Algorithm 8). The induction proceeds backward from `$j = c$` to `$j = 1$`. At each step, the equality of `$\hat{\omega}^{(j)}$` and `$\tilde{\omega}^{(j)}$` implies the rounding decisions are identical, which implies the dequantized weights are identical, which propagates to the next iteration.

A key algebraic relationship used in the proof is:
$$\tilde{\omega}^{(j)} = \mathbf{e}_j^\top \left( \tilde{W} - U^{-1} \left( \tilde{Q}^{(j+1)} - \tilde{W} \right) \right)$$

This shows that the residual target in Babai's algorithm, when expressed in the original weight space, matches the back-to-front GPTQ's residual `$\hat{W}^{(j)} = \hat{W}^{(c+1)} - U^{-1}\Delta^{(j)}$` exactly.

**Ineffectiveness of composing algorithms (Section C.4).** A seemingly natural idea is to take Babai's solution and then run an additional GPTQ-style error propagation step on top—hoping to "polish" the result further. The paper proves this is algebraically redundant: any such extra update vanishes. If we modify Babai's algorithm to apply an extra GPTQ update after each projection, the subsequent projection step `$\tilde{\omega}'^{(j-1)}$` remains unchanged because the extra update is orthogonal to all later Gram-Schmidt directions. More formally, for any `$j$`, the GPTQ-style correction `$AU\mathbf{e}_j \varepsilon^{(j)}$` (where `$U$` is the upper triangular factor) affects only the `$j$`-th coordinate in the residual, and this coordinate is completely determined by the projection onto the `$j$`-th Gram-Schmidt direction—subsequent projections onto earlier directions are unaffected. This confirms that the equivalence in Theorem 4 is tight: Babai's algorithm (equivalently, back-to-front GPTQ) already achieves the best possible result within the greedy hyperplane-projection framework; no further improvement can be squeezed out by additional error propagation.

**What the equivalence produces concretely.** The Babai perspective reframes GPTQ as an orthogonal walk through a nested sequence of affine subspaces in activation space:

1. Start with the target activation `$X\mathbf{w}_i$`.
2. Project onto the hyperplane that fixes the last dimension's (rounded) coefficient.
3. Project that projected point onto the hyperplane that fixes the second-to-last dimension's (rounded) coefficient.
4. Continue until all dimensions are fixed.

Each projection is along a Gram-Schmidt direction (the component of the basis vector orthogonal to all previously processed basis vectors), guaranteeing that each rounding decision does not interfere with subsequent decisions. The final lattice point reached is the center of the hyper-cuboid (in the Gram-Schmidt coordinate system) containing the original target—this is the Babai solution, which is not necessarily optimal but comes with a worst-case error guarantee.

#### GPTQ's Error Bound

The equivalence established in Section 4.3 provides the machinery to import Babai's classical error guarantees to GPTQ. This is the paper's second main theoretical contribution.

**The absolute error bound (Theorem 5).** Assume no clipping (`$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger = \mathbb{Z}$`), and let `$T$` be the permutation matrix encoding the (reversed) GPTQ quantization order. Let `$D$` be the diagonal matrix from the LDL decomposition of the permuted Hessian matrix `$T^\top X^\top X T$`, i.e.,

$$T^\top X^\top X T = L D L^\top$$

where `$L$` is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal and `$D$` is a non-negative diagonal matrix.

For every output channel `$i$` (`$1 \leq i \leq r$`), the squared quantization error under Babai's algorithm (equivalently, back-to-front GPTQ without clipping) satisfies the tight bound:

$$\| X \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i) \mathbf{z}_i - X\mathbf{w}_i \|^2 \leq \frac{1}{4} \left( T^{-1} \mathbf{s}_i \right)^\top D \left( T^{-1} \mathbf{s}_i \right)$$

where `$\mathbf{z}_i$` is the integer solution produced by the algorithm and the right-hand side is the quadratic form of the permuted scale vector with the LDL diagonal matrix `$D$`.

**What this computes:** For each output channel, take the scale vector `$\mathbf{s}_i$`, permute it by `$T^{-1}$` (so that its entries align with the order in which GPTQ processes dimensions), compute the weighted sum `$\sum_{j=1}^c D[j, j] \cdot (\text{permuted scale at position } j)^2$`, and multiply by `$1/4$`. This is a **computable, non-asymptotic guarantee**: you can calculate this bound before running quantization (since it depends only on the Hessian, the scales, and the order—not on the rounding decisions themselves) and know the worst-case squared error cannot exceed it for any weight vector.

**Why the factor `$1/4$`:** At each projection step, Babai's algorithm replaces the exact floating-point coefficient `$\zeta_j$` with the nearest integer `$\lfloor \zeta_j \rceil$`. The deviation `$|\zeta_j - \lfloor \zeta_j \rceil|$` is at most `$1/2$` (worst case: the coefficient lies exactly halfway between two integers). The error component along the `$j$`-th unnormalized Gram-Schmidt vector `$\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j$` therefore has norm at most `$\frac{1}{2} \|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j\|$`. Since the Gram-Schmidt vectors are orthogonal, the total squared error is the sum of the squared errors in each direction: `$\sum_{j=1}^c \left(\frac{1}{2} \|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j\|\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{j=1}^c \|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j\|^2$`. This is exactly `$\frac{1}{4} \operatorname{tr}(D^{(i)})$` where `$D^{(i)} = \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i) D \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)$`.

**Derivation of `$D^{(i)}[j, j] = D[j, j] \cdot (T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)[j]^2$`:** The permuted basis is `$A^{(i)} = X \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i) T = XT \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)$`. The squared norm of the `$j$`-th unnormalized Gram-Schmidt vector `$\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{(i)}_j$` of `$A^{(i)}$` equals `$D^{(i)}[j, j]$` (the `$j$`-th diagonal entry of the LDL decomposition of `$A^{(i)\top} A^{(i)}$`). Since `$A^{(i)\top} A^{(i)} = \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i) T^\top X^\top X T \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)$`, the LDL decomposition factors as `$L^{(i)} D^{(i)} L^{(i)\top}$` with `$D^{(i)} = \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i) D \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)$` and `$L^{(i)} = \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i) L \operatorname{diag}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)^{-1}$`. Therefore `$\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{(i)}_j\|^2 = D^{(i)}[j, j] = D[j, j] \cdot (T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)[j]^2$`, and the bound follows.

**Tightness:** The bound is **tight** in the sense that equality is attained when the target vector lies at a vertex of the hyper-cuboid constructed by the Gram-Schmidt vectors—i.e., when every coefficient `$\zeta_j$` is exactly halfway between two integers (`$\zeta_j - \lfloor \zeta_j \rceil = \pm 1/2$` for all `$j$`). In practice, the error will typically be substantially lower, but this worst-case guarantee is valuable for safety analysis.

**The relative error bound.** Babai (1986) also provides a relative error guarantee: for any `$\gamma$` satisfying

$$\| B\mathbf{z} - \mathbf{y} \| \leq \gamma \cdot \min_{\mathbf{z}' \in \mathbb{Z}^c} \| B\mathbf{z}' - \mathbf{y} \|$$

the bound on `$\gamma$` is:

$$1 \leq \gamma \leq \sqrt{1 + \max_{1 \leq j \leq c} \frac{\sum_{j'=1}^j \|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{j'}\|^2}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j\|^2}} \leq \sqrt{c + 1} \cdot \max_{1 \leq j' \leq j \leq c} \frac{\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_{j'}\|}{\|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j\|}$$

In the quantization context, setting `$d_j = \sqrt{D[j, j]} \cdot |(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)[j]|$` (the `$j$`-th Gram-Schmidt norm) gives the same structure:

$$1 \leq \gamma \leq \sqrt{1 + \max_{1 \leq j \leq c} \frac{\sum_{j'=1}^j d_{j'}^2}{d_j^2}} \leq \sqrt{c + 1} \cdot \max_{1 \leq j' \leq j \leq c} \frac{d_{j'}}{d_j}$$

**What the relative bound means operationally:** The factor `$\gamma$` tells you how much worse Babai's solution can be compared to the true optimal CVP solution (which is NP-hard to compute). If `$\gamma$` is close to 1, Babai is near-optimal; if `$\gamma$` is large, Babai could leave substantial slack. The bound depends on the ratios of consecutive Gram-Schmidt norms: if the norms drop sharply (`$d_{j'} \gg d_j$` for `$j' < j$`), the ratio `$d_{j'} / d_j$` can be large, inflating `$\gamma$`. This occurs when the basis is far from orthogonal—the later basis vectors have short Gram-Schmidt projections because they lie largely in the span of the earlier ones. Basis reduction (LLL, BKZ) aims to reduce these ratios, which is why it improves Babai's theoretical guarantee. In the GPTQ context, this bound explains why the quantization order matters: a good order makes the Gram-Schmidt norms decline more gradually, tightening the relative guarantee.

**Expected error under uniform prior (Section D.2).** If we additionally assume the (not-yet-quantized) weight offsets are uniformly distributed within Babai's hyper-cuboid (each coordinate `$u_j \sim \text{Uniform}(-a_j, a_j)$` independently, with `$a_j = \frac{1}{2} \|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j\|$`), then the expected squared error is `$\mathbb{E}[\epsilon] = \frac{1}{3} \epsilon_{\text{worst}}$`, i.e.,

$$\mathbb{E}\left[\|X \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i) \mathbf{z}_i - X\mathbf{w}_i\|^2\right] = \frac{1}{12} \sum_{j=1}^c \|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_j\|^2$$

This is a direct consequence of `$\mathbb{E}[u_j^2] = a_j^2 / 3$` for a uniform distribution on `$[-a_j, a_j]$`. The practical implication: in many realistic settings, the expected quantization error is roughly one-third of the worst-case bound derived in Theorem 5.

#### The Role of Quantization Order in GPTQ

The error bound in Theorem 5 depends on the LDL diagonal `$D$` through the quadratic form `$(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)^\top D (T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)$`. The permutation `$T$` (the quantization order) determines how the entries of `$D$` are paired with the entries of the scale vector `$\mathbf{s}_i$`. A poor order places large `$D[j, j]$` entries against large scale entries, inflating the bound; a good order minimizes this interaction.

**Independence from output channel.** For batched quantization like GPTQ, the order `$T$` must be the same for all output channels `$i$` (otherwise the Hessian decomposition would need recomputation `$r$` times). This constraint forces the order optimization to consider aggregate behavior across different scale vectors `$\mathbf{s}_i$`.

**The equal-scale approximation.** When scales are shared across groups (e.g., one scale per group of 128 weights), the entries of `$\mathbf{s}_i$` within a group are equal. If we approximate the scale as uniform across all dimensions (which is reasonable when group sizes are large), the dominant factor becomes `$\operatorname{tr}(D) = \sum_{j=1}^c D[j, j]$`. The problem then reduces to: find a permutation `$T$` that minimizes the trace of the LDL decomposition diagonal of `$T^\top X^\top X T$`.

**Finding the optimal order is NP-hard (Rose et al., 1976),** but effective heuristics exist.

**Act-order (GPTQ's heuristic).** GPTQ uses the **ascending order of Hessian diagonal entries** (the "act-order" heuristic): dimensions with smaller `$(X^\top X)[j, j]$` (activation variance) are processed earlier, and dimensions with larger variance are processed later. When applied to the back-to-front Babai iteration, this becomes the **descending order** of Hessian diagonal entries (process large-variance dimensions first, small-variance later). This heuristic is computationally cheap (it costs only a sort on the diagonal) and empirically effective, but it only considers the diagonal entries, ignoring off-diagonal correlations.

**Min-pivot order (Algorithm 3).** The paper proposes an improved heuristic: at each step of the LDL (or Cholesky) decomposition, choose the remaining dimension with the **smallest current diagonal entry** as the next pivot. The algorithm:

1. Initialize `$J \leftarrow \{1, \ldots, c\}$` (the set of not-yet-pivoted indices).
2. For `$j = 1$` to `$c$`:
   - Select `$j' \leftarrow \arg\min_{j' \in J} H[j', j']$` (the remaining index with smallest diagonal).
   - Update the Hessian by the rank-1 Schur complement: `$H \leftarrow H - H[:, j'] H[j', :] / H[j', j']$` (this eliminates the `$j'$`-th row and column's influence from the remaining submatrix).
   - Record `$j'$` as the `$j$`-th pivot and remove it from `$J$`.

**What min-pivot does geometrically:** This order corresponds to always picking the shortest remaining Gram-Schmidt residual vector as the next direction to orthogonalize. This agrees with the structure of Babai's relative error bound, where the ratio `$d_{j'} / d_j$` is minimized when later Gram-Schmidt vectors are not too short compared to earlier ones—picking the shortest vector early ensures subsequent vectors don't appear even shorter in comparison.

**Empirical comparison (Table 2).** The paper evaluates both heuristics on Qwen3-8B transformer block 18. The `$\operatorname{tr}(D)$` values are reported for each linear layer type:

| Order | Q·K·V | O | Gate·Up | Down |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| back-to-front | 1.169e8 | 1.824e8 | 1.181e8 | 1.323e9 |
| front-to-back | 1.161e8 | 1.841e8 | 1.202e8 | 1.320e9 |
| random (ave.) | 1.168e8 | 1.856e8 | 1.194e8 | 1.322e9 |
| act-order | 7.400e7 | 1.786e8 | 6.052e7 | 1.222e9 |
| min-pivot | 7.323e7 | 1.772e8 | 5.990e7 | 1.221e9 |

Act-order already reduces `$\operatorname{tr}(D)$` substantially relative to the naive orders (35–50% improvement on Q·K·V and Gate·Up layers). Min-pivot consistently achieves the lowest trace across all layers, but the marginal improvement over act-order is modest (0.9–1.0% further reduction in the problematic layers). The paper concludes that act-order is a "cheap approximation that already captures most of the benefit when the Hessian matrix is well-conditioned," and reserves min-pivot for situations demanding the tightest possible bound.

**Why front-to-back and back-to-front give similar `$\operatorname{tr}(D)$`:** The trace of the LDL decomposition diagonal is not invariant under reversal—front-to-back and back-to-front process the same set of pivots in opposite order, which generally yields different Gram-Schmidt orthogonalizations and different diagonal entries. However, for the Qwen3-8B Hessians, the difference is small (less than 1% in most layers, though the O layer shows a ~1% difference in the opposite direction). This symmetry is not theoretically guaranteed but empirically observed.

#### Babai's Algorithm for Batched Quantization (Algorithm 4)

The paper constructs a practical Batched Babai algorithm (Algorithm 4) that applies the CVP perspective directly to linear-layer quantization with per-channel scales. This algorithm is the computational realization of the theory and serves as the template for the practical methods (SSQR, HPTQ) in Section 5.

**Inputs:** Weight matrix `$W$`, scale matrix `$S$`, activation matrix `$X$`, permutation `$T$`, damping factor `$\lambda$`, and grid `$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger$`.

**Outputs:** Quantized integers `$Z$` and dequantized weights `$Q$`.

**Procedure (in operational order):**

1. **Permute and dampen the Hessian:** `$H \leftarrow T^\top (X^\top X + \lambda I) T$`. The damping term `$\lambda = \frac{1}{100c} \|X\|_F^2$` ensures `$H$` is positive definite (full rank) even if the original `$X^\top X$` is numerically rank-deficient. The permutation `$T$` applies the chosen quantization order.

2. **Cholesky factor as upper triangular basis:** `$A \leftarrow \text{CHOLESKY}(H)^\top$`. The Cholesky decomposition gives `$H = A^\top A$`, so `$A$` is the lower triangular Cholesky factor and `$A^\top$` is upper triangular. Using Theorem 1, `$A^\top$` serves as the (square, upper triangular) basis for the CVP—its rows are the basis vectors, and the upper triangular structure makes the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization trivial (`$\Phi = I$`).

3. **Permute weights and scales:** `$W, S \leftarrow T^{-1} W, T^{-1} S$`. This reorders the rows to match the pivot order.

4. **Initialize target and solutions:** `$Y, Q, Z \leftarrow A W, W, \mathbf{0}$`. The target matrix `$Y \in \mathbb{R}^{c \times r}$` contains the activation-space targets for each output channel: `$\mathbf{y}^{(i)} = A \mathbf{w}_i$`. The dequantized weights start as the original floating-point weights, and the quantized integers start as zero.

5. **Iterate `$j \leftarrow c$` down to `$1$`:**
   - `$\omega \leftarrow Y[j, :] / A[j, j]$`: extract the `$j$`-th row of the residual target and divide by the diagonal entry `$A[j, j]$` (this gives the exact coefficient along the `$j$`-th basis direction, since `$A$` is upper triangular and `$A[j, :] \cdot \mathbf{w}_i = A[j, j] \cdot w_i[j] + \sum_{k=j+1}^c A[j, k] \cdot w_i[k]$`).
   - `$\zeta \leftarrow \omega / S[j, :]$`: divide by the per-channel scales to obtain the integer coefficients to round.
   - `$Z[j, :] \leftarrow \text{ROUND}(\zeta, \mathbb{Z}_\dagger)$`: round to the nearest representable integer.
   - `$Q[j, :] \leftarrow Z[j, :] * S[j, :]$`: dequantize by multiplying by the scales.
   - `$Y \leftarrow Y - A[:, j] Q[j, :]$`: subtract the contribution of the quantized `$j$`-th dimension from the residual target.

6. **Undo permutation:** `$Z, Q \leftarrow T Z, T Q$` to restore the original dimensional order.

**What makes this batched:** The operations inside the loop broadcast naturally over the output channel dimension `$r$`: `$Y[j, :] / A[j, j]$` divides a row vector of length `$r$` by a scalar, `$\omega / S[j, :]$` is element-wise division, and `$Y \leftarrow Y - A[:, j] Q[j, :]$` is an outer product of a column vector `$A[:, j] \in \mathbb{R}^c$` with a row vector `$Q[j, :] \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times r}$`. This is exactly GPTQ's batched structure—the only difference is the iteration direction and the explicit residual target `$Y$` in activation space rather than the weight-residual `$W$` in weight space.

**Disabling basis reduction (LLL) is necessary for batching.** Theorem 6 proves that if the permutation `$T$` is used instead of an LLL transformation, the QR (or Cholesky) decomposition of the basis can be reused across all output channels. The LLL reduction is *scale-sensitive*: different scale vectors `$\mathbf{s}_i$` would produce different reduction transformations, requiring `$r$` separate LLL calls. Since LLL takes `$\mathcal{O}(c^3)$` time per call, this would make the algorithm `$r$` times slower—prohibitive for `$r$` in the thousands. The tradeoff is accepting a larger error bound (LLL reduction tightens Babai's theoretical guarantee) in exchange for computational tractability.

#### Practical Applications: Overflow-Tolerant Quantization Methods

The theoretical results assume no weight clipping (`$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger = \mathbb{Z}$`). However, standard quantization formats use a finite grid (e.g., 16 representable values for INT4), which forces clipping when a weight's magnitude exceeds the grid boundary after scaling. Simply increasing the scales to avoid clipping is counterproductive—larger scales coarsen the quantization granularity, increasing the error for inlier weights and potentially exceeding the error of a clipped scheme like MSE. The paper addresses this tension with two overflow-tolerant schemes.

**Scale-Adjusted SpQR (SSQR).** SSQR (Algorithms 9 and 10) extends the SpQR (Dettmers et al., 2024) framework. The core idea: represent each weight vector `$\mathbf{w}_i$` as `$\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_i) \mathbf{z}_i + \boldsymbol{\xi}_i$`, where `$\mathbf{s}_i$` are per-group scales (stored once per group), `$\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{Z}_\dagger^c$` are low-bitwidth inlier integers, and `$\boldsymbol{\xi}_i \in \mathbb{R}^c$` is a sparse floating-point vector (stored in CSR format) capturing the overflowed weights—those that would otherwise be clipped.

**The scale-adjustment mechanism** tunes the scales so that the outlier density matches a target rate `$\rho \in [0, 1]$` (e.g., 1%, 3%, 5%). The mechanism exploits one degree of freedom: each output channel's group scale can be proportionally adjusted (multiplied by a scalar `$s[i]$`), and the outlier rate is negatively correlated with the scale size. This reduces the search space to a one-dimensional binary search per output channel:

1. Initialize scales `$S_{\text{MSE}}$` using the MSE method (minimizing `$\|Q - W\|_F^2$` under the quantization grid).
2. Set binary search bounds `$s_{\min} \leftarrow 0^r$`, `$s_{\max} \leftarrow 2^r$` (per output channel multiplicative factors).
3. While not converged:
   - Set `$S \leftarrow S_{\text{MSE}} \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})$` (element-wise, proportionally adjust each output channel's scales).
   - Run the inner quantization procedure (Algorithm 10: GPTQ without clipping, but recording overflowed weights as sparse outliers in `$\Xi$`).
   - For each output channel, if the outlier count exceeds `$\rho c$`, the current scale is too small—set `$s_{\min}[i] \leftarrow \mathbf{s}[i]$`; otherwise, set `$s_{\max}[i] \leftarrow \mathbf{s}[i]$`.
   - Update `$\mathbf{s} \leftarrow (\mathbf{s}_{\min} + \mathbf{s}_{\max}) / 2$`.
4. The search terminates when the scale vector `$\mathbf{s}$` stabilizes.

**Inner procedure (Algorithm 10):** This is GPTQ (back-to-front, without clipping) with one modification: after rounding each weight, if the rounded value is not equal to what `$\text{ROUND}(\zeta[i], \mathbb{Z})$` would produce (i.e., the weight would overflow the grid), the overflow amount is stored in `$\Xi[j, i]$` rather than being clipped. The dequantized weight becomes `$Q[j, :] \leftarrow Z[j, :] * S[j, :] + \Xi[j, :]$`, ensuring exact representation of the overflowed weights at higher precision.

**Storage cost:** Each outlier `$\xi$` is stored in full precision (typically FP16 or BF16). The effective average bitwidth is `$b_{\text{inlier}} \cdot (1 - \rho) + 16 \cdot \rho$` plus overhead for scales and sparse indices. For example, SSQR-3% with 3-bit inliers averages `$3 \times 0.97 + 16 \times 0.03 = 3.39$` bits per weight, which the paper rounds to 3.445 bits after accounting for scale storage and sparse index overhead.

**Huffman-Encoded Post-Training Quantization (HPTQ).** HPTQ (Algorithm 11) takes a different approach: rather than separating inliers and outliers, it represents the entire weight matrix with a **single global scale** `$s \in \mathbb{R}_{\neq 0}$` and integer weights `$Z \in \mathbb{Z}^{c \times r}$`, then compresses the integers using Huffman encoding to achieve a target average bitwidth.

**Why a single scale works with Huffman encoding:** When a single scale is used, the integer magnitudes can vary widely—some weights map to small integers, others to very large integers. Huffman encoding assigns variable-length codes based on frequency: frequently occurring integer values get short codes, rare values get long codes. This naturally handles heavy-tailed weight distributions because outlier weights (large integers) occur rarely and receive long codes but contribute little to the average bitwidth, while inlier weights (small integers) occur frequently and receive short codes.

**Entropy-guided scale selection (binary search):**
1. Set `$s_{\min} \leftarrow 0$`, `$s_{\max} \leftarrow \|W\|_{\infty}$` (the absolute maximum weight magnitude).
2. Set `$s \leftarrow (s_{\min} + s_{\max}) / 2$`.
3. While not converged:
   - Set `$S \leftarrow s \cdot \mathbf{1}^{c \times r}$` (broadcast the single scale to all weights).
   - Run GPTQ (Algorithm 1) with `$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger = \mathbb{Z}$` (no clipping) to obtain integer weights `$Z$`.
   - Compute `$h' \leftarrow$` average Huffman encoding bitwidth of `$Z$` (the empirical entropy of the integer distribution).
   - If `$h' < h$` (target bitwidth not met): `$s_{\max} \leftarrow s$` (scale too small—integers are too large and too varied, needing too many bits).
   - Else: `$s_{\min} \leftarrow s$` (scale too large—quantization is too coarse, but we can afford to make it finer while still meeting the bit budget).
   - Update `$s \leftarrow (s_{\min} + s_{\max}) / 2$.

**What this search ensures:** At convergence, the scale `$s$` is the maximum possible value (finest quantization granularity) that still produces integers whose Huffman-encoded representation fits within the target bitwidth `$h$`. This maximizes the representational precision while respecting the compression budget.

**Huffman-encoded RTN (HRTN, Algorithm 12)** is a baseline that replaces GPTQ with simple round-to-nearest quantization (no error propagation). This isolates the contribution of GPTQ's error propagation: HPTQ = GPTQ + Huffman encoding, HRTN = RTN + Huffman encoding. The comparison shows whether the GPTQ improvement comes from better quantization (error propagation) or merely from the Huffman encoding itself.

**Key design decisions in both methods:**

- **No clipping anywhere.** Both SSQR and HPTQ ensure that every weight value is representable: SSQR by storing overflowed weights in full precision, HPTQ by using the infinite integer lattice `$\mathbb{Z}$` as the quantization grid. This satisfies the theoretical assumption `$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger = \mathbb{Z}$` from Theorem 5, making the error bound applicable.

- **GPTQ order is act-order for all methods.** No special order optimization is performed; act-order is used as the default because it is "a cheap approximation that already captures most of the benefit."

- **Group size 128 for RTN, GPTQ, and SSQR.** This means one scale value is shared per group of 128 consecutive weights in the input dimension. The per-group granularity enables finer scaling than a single channel-wise scale while keeping scale storage overhead manageable.

- **MSE scale initialization for RTN and GPTQ.** The initial scales before binary search are computed by minimizing the mean squared error between the dequantized weights and the original weights, assuming round-to-nearest quantization—a standard initialization in the GPTQ pipeline.

#### SSQR CUDA Inference Kernel

The paper implements a custom CUDA/C++ inference kernel for the SSQR representation targeting Ampere GPUs (NVIDIA A6000). The kernel is specialized for **low-batch-latency decoding** (batch size 1) while also supporting larger batches.

**Dual-path architecture.** The kernel separates handling of dense inliers and sparse outliers:

**Dense inlier path:**
- For batch sizes `$\geq 8$`, tensor cores are used to multiply the dense inlier weight matrix (quantized to 2–4 bits) with the activations. Tensor cores require the outer dimension to be a multiple of 8, so batch sizes below 8 are handled via SIMT cores exclusively.
- Two reordering schemes are applied:
  1. **Tensor core memory reordering:** Weights are rearranged to match the memory layout that tensor cores expect (tiled, blocked format for `mma` instructions), maximizing memory coalescing.
  2. **Batched integer conversion reordering:** The 2–4 bit packed integers are reordered to enable efficient batched conversion to FP16 operands (the format tensor cores consume). This avoids a per-weight conversion bottleneck.
- Operands are 16-bit, accumulators are 32-bit floating-point (standard for tensor core math on Ampere).

**Sparse outlier path:**
- Outliers are grouped in blocks of 16 rows (matching the outer tensor core dimension for the case where tensor cores are used).
- Within each block, non-zero values are stored in **column-major row order** with padding to account for differing non-zero counts across rows in the group. Column-major order ensures that when processing a single output token (sequence length 1), contiguous memory accesses correspond to the active columns, maximizing cache line utilization.
- Outlier contributions are computed via SIMT cores (standard CUDA cores) and added to the dense inlier result.

**Performance (Figure 5).** The paper reports layer-wise speedup relative to the PyTorch BF16 matrix multiplication kernel on NVIDIA RTX A6000, across different layer shapes in Qwen3-8B, inlier bitwidths (2, 3, 4), outlier rates (0–5%), and batch sizes (1–16):

- **Low-batch regime (batch size 1):** Speedups of up to ~4× are observed for layers with <1% outliers at 2–3 bits. The speedup decreases as the outlier rate increases (more SIMT work) and as the bitwidth increases (less compression, more memory traffic).
- **Higher batch sizes (8–16):** Speedups are more modest (roughly 1.5–2.5×), as tensor core utilization improves for both the quantized kernel and the BF16 baseline, reducing the relative advantage.
- **End-to-end speedup (Figure 4c):** When running the full Qwen3-8B model in decoding mode (generating 128 new tokens, batch size 1), the SSQR kernel achieves approximately 2× speedup over PyTorch BF16 across multiple outlier rates and inlier bitwidths, measured by the TPOT (time per output token) metric.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Quantization as a Lattice Problem — Not Just a Codebook Design Choice

The dominant conceptual shift in this paper is reframing the *entire quantization optimization problem* as an instance of the classical closest vector problem (CVP) on a lattice defined by the input Hessian, rather than treating the lattice solely as a structural property of the representable grid. This distinction is subtle but fundamental.

Prior work that invoked lattices in the context of LLM quantization — most prominently QuIP# (Tseng et al., 2024a) and QTIP (Tseng et al., 2024b) — used lattice ideas as a *codebook design tool*. The E8 lattice, for instance, was adopted because its points pack densely in high-dimensional space, providing a good set of representable vectors for vector quantization. This is an application of lattice *geometry* (point packing, covering radius) to the *output representation*, but the optimization problem — finding which codebook entry best approximates a given weight vector — was still treated as a standard quantization search. The lattice defined the *answer vocabulary*; it did not define the *problem structure*.

What this paper demonstrates (Theorem 1, Section 4.1) is that the optimization itself — the search for integer coefficients zi that minimize ∥X diag(si) zi − Xwi∥² — is structurally identical to CVP on a lattice whose basis columns are X scaled by si. The entire apparatus of CVP algorithms — not just the geometry of particular lattices, but the algorithmic strategies for navigating them — becomes applicable. The quantization-CVP dictionary (Table 1) makes this mapping explicit: the input activation matrix X becomes the set of basis directions, the scale vector si stretches those directions, the target activation Xwi is the external point to approximate, and the quantized integer vector zi is the CVP solution.

This is a reframing, not an incremental improvement — and its significance lies in what it unlocks rather than what it changes about existing practice. Before this paper, the relationship between quantization and lattice theory was surface-level: you could use lattice codebooks, but the algorithm that found the best representation was still GPTQ, OBQ, or some other quantization-specific heuristic. After this paper, the entire CVP algorithmic toolbox — Babai's nearest plane, LLL basis reduction, BKZ, enumeration algorithms, and decades of analysis on approximation factors and error bounds — sits ready for import. The fact that GPTQ itself turns out to be a known CVP algorithm (Theorem 4) was not a coincidence but a consequence of this deeper structural identity.

The paper's identification of *Hessian factor invariance* (Theorem 1) is conceptually important within this reframing: it shows that any square factor of the input Hessian produces a geometrically equivalent CVP under rotation or reflection. This means the choice of basis representation — which in practice determines computational cost (n × c vs. c × c matrices) — does not affect the geometry of the optimization. The lattice is defined by the Gram matrix X⊤X, not by the specific factor X. This is reminiscent of how the Cholesky decomposition is used in numerical linear algebra to replace an overdetermined least-squares problem with a square, triangular one — but the paper provides the geometric justification for why this substitution preserves the CVP solution.

### Innovation 2: GPTQ as Babai’s Algorithm — A Greedy Heuristic With a Proven Name

The paper's central theoretical result is not merely that GPTQ *resembles* Babai's nearest plane algorithm, but that the two are mathematically identical when the dimensional order is aligned and clipping is disabled (Theorem 4, Section 4.3). This is an exact equivalence down to the intermediate computations, proven through both geometric reasoning (Appendix C) and a three-step algebraic derivation.

Why is this identification novel, given that the algorithmic steps were already known? Because it recontextualizes GPTQ from an *ad-hoc engineering heuristic* — "pick weights in a fixed order, round them one at a time, propagate the error forward" — into an *instance of an algorithm with four decades of theoretical analysis* behind it. Babai's nearest plane algorithm (Babai, 1986) is one of the foundational approximation algorithms for CVP, studied in cryptography, coding theory, and computational number theory. It comes with tight worst-case error bounds, relative approximation guarantees, and a well-understood relationship to basis reduction techniques like LLL. By proving the equivalence, the paper gives GPTQ the theoretical pedigree it previously lacked.

The key insight about *why* this equivalence was missed is that GPTQ and Babai run in opposite directions: GPTQ front-to-back, Babai back-to-front. The original GPTQ presentation (Frantar et al., 2023) did not consider reversing the iteration order, and the quantization community had no reason to suspect that direction mattered for anything beyond implementation convenience. The paper's insight — that reversing the order reveals the algorithm's true geometric identity — is a diagnostic move rather than a design move. It didn't require changing what GPTQ computes; it required looking at the same computation through a different lens.

A subtlety that strengthens this contribution: the paper also proves that *composing* Babai's algorithm with additional GPTQ-style error propagation steps is algebraically redundant (Section C.4). Once Babai's projection has been executed, any further GPTQ correction vanishes — the algorithm has already extracted all the improvement that the hyperplane-projection framework can provide. This tightness result confirms that the equivalence is not approximate or partial; Babai's algorithm (equivalently, back-to-front GPTQ) is the fixed point of the greedy projection approach, and no amount of iterative refinement can improve it further without fundamentally changing the search strategy (e.g., through backtracking, which would correspond to enumeration algorithms in the CVP literature).

The practical consequence of this identification is significant: the paper imports Babai's error guarantees (Theorem 5) and shows that they are tight, computable, and practically relevant. Before this equivalence, GPTQ had no worst-case error bound; after it, every run of no-clipping GPTQ comes with a guarantee on the maximum possible layer-wise output distortion. This matters for safety analysis and for principled comparison between quantization schemes, but it also matters for *design*: the error bound depends on the LDL decomposition diagonal D and the scale vector si, providing a computable quantity that can guide order selection (Section 4.5) and scale optimization.

### Innovation 3: Clipping as the Obstacle to Theory — and How to Remove It

The paper makes a diagnostic contribution that is conceptually clean and practically actionable: the reason GPTQ previously lacked theoretical guarantees is not because the algorithm is intrinsically heuristic, but because the standard practice of clipping overflowed weights breaks the CVP equivalence. The CVP framework requires the integer solution domain to be Zc (the infinite lattice); clipping constrains it to Z†c for a bounded grid Z†, which is no longer a lattice. This means that Babai's hyperplane geometry — infinite parallel planes spaced at unit intervals — gets truncated into finite segments, and the error bound derivation (which relies on the worst-case rounding error of ±1/2 along each Gram-Schmidt direction, achievable when the target lies exactly halfway between planes) no longer holds.

Prior work largely treated clipping as an unavoidable consequence of finite-precision arithmetic — you have, say, 4 bits, which gives 16 representable values, and weights outside that range must either be clipped or the scale must be increased to cover them (coarsening quantization everywhere). The dominant frameworks (GPTQ, SpQR, QuIP#) all clip. The field implicitly accepted that theoretical guarantees and practical quantization were incompatible because of this constraint.

This paper rejects that premise. The core move is to recognize that clipping is a design choice — specifically, a tradeoff between per-weight precision and storage — not a mathematical necessity. The two practical methods (SSQR, HPTQ) demonstrate orthogonal ways to eliminate clipping:

- **SSQR** keeps the clipped weights as sparse, full-precision outliers, storing them separately. This preserves the infinite lattice property for the quantized inliers (the CVP problem is solved on Zc, with non-conforming weights simply stored uncompressed) while keeping the average bitwidth low through sparsity.
- **HPTQ** abandons the fixed-bitwidth-per-weight assumption entirely: use a single global scale (so that Zc is the natural grid) and compress the resulting integers with Huffman encoding. Every integer is representable; the compression budget is met by assigning variable-length codes based on frequency, not by constraining the range.

Both methods satisfy Z† = Z (the no-clipping assumption in Theorem 5), making the error bound directly applicable. The empirical results (Figure 4, Table 3) show that these no-clipping methods not only match but *outperform* the original clipped GPTQ at comparable average bitwidths — suggesting that the accuracy cost of clipping was real and avoidable. SSQR with 3–5% outliers achieves lower perplexity than GPTQ at the same inlier bitwidth, and HPTQ consistently produces the best perplexity across bitwidths for Qwen3-8B.

This is a reframing of the quantization design space: rather than asking "how do we quantize within a fixed grid?", the paper asks "how do we represent weights such that no clipping is necessary while still meeting a bit budget?" The lattice perspective provides the motivation (error guarantees), and the practical methods demonstrate that the constraint shift is feasible and beneficial. The paper's suggestion that state-of-the-art floating-point formats like MXFP4 and NVFP4 are "essentially no-clipping" because their small group sizes (32 and 16 respectively) make AbsMax per-group scaling near-optimal without clipping — is an important connection to emerging industry standards that further reinforces the practical relevance of the no-clipping framework.

### Innovation 4: The LDL Diagonal as a Quantifiable, Optimizable Surrogate for Quantization Quality

A less flashy but intellectually significant contribution is the identification of the LDL decomposition diagonal D of the permuted input Hessian as the key quantity governing quantization error in the no-clipping setting (Theorem 5). The error bound 1/4 · (T⁻¹si)⊤ D (T⁻¹si) reveals that every factor influencing the theoretical guarantee — the input data distribution (through the Hessian), the quantization scales (through si), and the dimensional processing order (through the permutation T) — converges into a single quadratic form. This provides a *computable, pre-quantization quality metric* that is independent of the specific weight values being quantized (only their scales matter) and independent of the rounding decisions themselves.

Before this result, the quantization literature lacked a clean theoretical quantity that predicted post-quantization error without actually running the quantizer. Practitioners tuned hyperparameters (group size, scale method, order heuristic) by trial and error — quantize, evaluate perplexity, adjust, repeat. The bound provides a cheap-to-compute proxy: for a given layer, compute T⊤X⊤XT, perform LDL decomposition, extract D, and compute the quadratic form with the scale vectors. This can guide design decisions (which order to use, how to set scales) without a single quantization run.

The connection between D and quantization order (Section 4.5) reveals that act-order — the ascending-order-of-Hessian-diagonal heuristic used in the original GPTQ — has a principled interpretation as a cheap approximation to minimizing tr(D). The paper's min-pivot heuristic (Algorithm 3) formalizes this: at each LDL decomposition step, pivot on the remaining variable with the smallest current diagonal entry, effectively orthogonalizing the "easiest" residual direction first. The empirical results (Table 2) show that min-pivot consistently achieves the lowest tr(D), though act-order captures most of the benefit at negligible computational cost. This is a satisfying theoretical validation of a previously heuristic design choice — act-order was *approximately* doing the right thing for reasons that were not understood at the time.

The bound also reveals an important limitation that motivates future work: the LDL diagonal D depends on the input distribution through the Hessian X⊤X, which is estimated from a calibration dataset. If the calibration data does not match the deployment distribution, the bound — and, by implication, the quantization quality — may be misleadingly optimistic. This connects to well-known issues in post-training quantization (calibration set sensitivity) but provides a theoretical language for analyzing them: distribution shift manifests as a perturbation to D, and the quadratic form's sensitivity to that perturbation can be bounded via matrix perturbation theory.

The geometric interpretation of the LDL diagonal entries as the squared lengths of Gram-Schmidt basis vectors is crucial for intuition. When D[j, j] is large, the j-th basis direction captures a large "chunk" of the input variance in its orthogonal component, meaning an error of ±1/2 in that direction's coefficient creates a large activation-space displacement. This is why good quantization orders (act-order, min-pivot) process small-D dimensions early: they lock in the directions where rounding errors are most consequential while the residual target still has freedom to compensate in other directions.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation uses **WikiText-2** and **C4** for perplexity, plus six zero-shot benchmarks for accuracy: **WinoGrande (Wino)**, **MMLU**, **PiQA**, **SciQ**, and **TruthfulQA** (reported as MC1/MC2). For WikiText-2, the entire test split is concatenated with two line breaks as separators and tokenized with each model's default HuggingFace tokenizer. For C4, documents are sampled individually from the selected shard, tokenized, and randomly extracted into 2048-token sequences. Sequences shorter than 2048 are discarded; longer ones are cropped. The calibration dataset for GPTQ's Hessian estimation is constructed from **FineWeb-Edu** (subset `sample-10BT`), streamed and shuffled with a fixed seed, with 256 sequences of length 2048 accumulated into non-overlapping windows.

- **Base model(s).** The paper primarily evaluates the **Qwen3 family** across five scales: **0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, and 14B parameters**, with detailed head-to-head comparisons on **Qwen3-8B**. Additionally, the **Llama** family is tested: **Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct**, **Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct**, and **Llama-2-7B**. The Qwen3 family is chosen as the main testbed because it represents a recent, high-performing open-source model series spanning two orders of magnitude in parameter count, enabling scaling behavior analysis (Figure 4b). The inclusion of Llama models tests generalization across different pretraining recipes and architectures.

- **Metrics.** **Perplexity** (lower is better) on WikiText-2 and C4 is the primary compression quality metric — it measures the model's next-token prediction certainty after quantization, with the BF16 baseline serving as the reference. **Zero-shot accuracy** (%) on six benchmarks (WinoGrande, MMLU, PiQA, SciQ, TruthfulQA MC1/MC2) measures downstream task preservation. **Average effective bitwidth** (bits per weight) is the compression metric, computed as `inlier_bitwidth * (1 − outlier_rate) + storage_overhead`, where storage overhead accounts for scale values (usually FP16 per group of 128) and, for SSQR, sparse outlier values (FP16 each plus CSR index overhead) and for HPTQ, Huffman codebook overhead. **TPOT** (time per output token) in milliseconds measures inference latency for the SSQR CUDA kernel, reported as speedup relative to the PyTorch BF16 matrix multiplication kernel.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five quantization methods: **(1) Round-to-Nearest (RTN)**, the simplest baseline that rounds each weight independently to the nearest grid value with per-group MSE scales (group size 128); **(2) GPTQ** (Frantar et al., 2023) with act-order, MSE scale initialization, and standard clipping (the original algorithm); **(3) Huffman-encoded RTN (HRTN)** , which mirrors HPTQ but replaces GPTQ's error propagation with simple RTN — this isolates whether gains come from Huffman encoding alone; **(4) AQLM** (Egiazarian et al., 2024); **(5) QuIP#** (Tseng et al., 2024a); and **(6) QTIP** (Tseng et al., 2024b). The last three are state-of-the-art vector/lattice quantization methods, used for comparison in Table 16 on Llama-2-7B. For all methods except HPTQ/HRTN (which use a single global scale), group size is 128.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Since this is a post-training quantization paper, there is no "generation budget" in the inference sense. Instead, the relevant computational cost is the **time and memory required to quantize the model** (not measured systematically) and the **storage budget** (the average effective bitwidth, which serves as the fairness metric — all methods are compared at matched average bitwidths). For SSQR, the outlier rate ρ controls the storage budget: more outliers mean higher precision but more storage. For HPTQ, the target bitwidth `h` controls the search. The binary search procedures in Algorithms 9 and 11 iterate until the scale converges, but the number of iterations and the cost of each GPTQ run within the search are not reported.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** No cross-validation is used for the quantization experiments, as there is no training or hyperparameter selection on the evaluation data. The calibration data (FineWeb-Edu) and evaluation data (WikiText-2, C4, zero-shot benchmarks) are entirely disjoint. For the empirical comparison of quantization orders (Table 2, Section D.3), the random order result is averaged over 100 runs to capture variance. Perplexity and zero-shot accuracy are point estimates without confidence intervals.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Perplexity on Qwen3-8B: HPTQ Consistently Outperforms All Methods

Table 3 reports the core perplexity comparison on Qwen3-8B across five methods (HPTQ, GPTQ, HRTN, RTN, SSQR) at three average bitwidths (approximately 4.125, 3.125, and 2.125 bits, corresponding to 4-bit, 3-bit, and 2-bit inlier quantization). The BF16 baseline achieves 9.73 on WikiText-2 and 13.55 on C4.

At **4.125 bits**: HPTQ achieves 9.81 on WikiText-2 (only +0.08 above BF16) and 13.64 on C4 (+0.09). HRTN scores 9.90 and 13.80 — slightly worse than HPTQ but notably better than standard GPTQ (10.10 and 13.92) and RTN (10.30 and 15.20). The key comparison is HPTQ vs. GPTQ: HPTQ reduces WikiText-2 perplexity by 0.29 (from 10.10 to 9.81) and C4 perplexity by 0.28 (from 13.92 to 13.64). SSQR with 1% outliers (4.445 bits) achieves 10.00 and 13.83 — worse than HPTQ despite higher bitwidth. At 3% outliers (5.085 bits) it improves to 9.92 and 13.76, approaching but not matching HPTQ at lower bitwidth. At 5% outliers (5.725 bits) it reaches 9.80 and 13.67, essentially matching BF16 but at nearly 2 bits higher than HPTQ's 3.125-bit result (10.34 and 14.23).

At **3.125 bits**: HPTQ achieves 10.34 on WikiText-2 (+0.61 over BF16) and 14.23 on C4 (+0.68). The degradation is mild — far better than any competing method. GPTQ reaches 12.77 and 15.61 (gap of +2.43 vs. HPTQ on WikiText-2). HRTN achieves 10.75 and 14.63 — substantially better than GPTQ but still 0.41 perplexity worse than HPTQ on WikiText-2. RTN collapses to 16.30 and 21.08. SSQR-1% (3.445 bits) achieves 10.64 and 14.71 — worse than HPTQ at both higher bitwidth and higher perplexity. SSQR-5% (3.725 bits) reaches 12.88 and 16.85 — far worse than HPTQ despite over 0.6 extra bits.

At **2.125 bits**: The separation widens dramatically. HPTQ achieves 13.97 on WikiText-2 and 16.89 on C4 — degraded but still functional. GPTQ collapses to 57.51 and 36.14 (gap of +43.5 vs. HPTQ on WikiText-2). HRTN fails catastrophically at 593.05 and 503.00, showing that simple RTN with Huffman encoding cannot handle 2-bit quantization — the round-to-nearest errors accumulate and destroy the signal. RTN diverges entirely (2e10 on both datasets). SSQR-1% (2.445 bits) yields 22.30 and 27.07 — better than GPTQ but much worse than HPTQ. SSQR-5% (3.725 bits, significantly higher bitwidth) reaches 12.88 and 16.85 — still worse than HPTQ at 2.125 bits on C4.

**The critical comparison in Table 3 is the GPTQ–HRTN–HPTQ triad.** At every bitwidth, HRTN (RTN + Huffman) outperforms GPTQ (error-propagated quantization with clipping), demolishing the assumption that GPTQ's error propagation is always beneficial. At 2.125 bits, HRTN catastrophically fails while HPTQ remains stable, proving that error propagation (GPTQ's contribution via Babai's algorithm) becomes *essential* at very low bitwidths — but only when combined with no-clipping (Huffman encoding). In other words: **at moderate compression (4-bit), a simple no-clipping scheme with frequency-aware encoding beats GPTQ's sophisticated error propagation; at extreme compression (2-bit), error propagation is necessary, but it only works when unshackled from clipping.** This directly validates the paper's central thesis: clipping is the obstacle to theoretical guarantees, and its removal enables the CVP structure to deliver benefits at scale.

#### Scaling Behavior: HPTQ Pareto Optimality Across Model Sizes

Figure 4b plots WikiText-2 perplexity against effective model size (in GB) for HPTQ across five Qwen3 model scales (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B) at three bitwidths (4.125, 3.125, 2.125 bits). Each curve connects different model sizes at a fixed bitwidth.

The Pareto optimal frontier (best perplexity per GB of storage) is traced out: at the largest model size (14B), 3.125-bit HPTQ achieves perplexity ~9 on WikiText-2 with a model size of roughly 5.5 GB — matching or beating 4-bit quantization on smaller models at lower storage cost. Specifically, the 14B model at 3.125 bits costs roughly the same storage as the 4B model at 4.125 bits (~5.5 GB) but delivers 9.06 vs. 14.26 perplexity — a massive gain from scaling parameters rather than precision. The 2.125-bit curve at 14B (11.36 perplexity, ~3.7 GB) performs comparably to the 4.125-bit curve at 4B (14.26 perplexity, ~5 GB) — more than 2× storage reduction with only a modest perplexity increase.

**The paper identifies 3.125-bit as the Pareto optimal bitwidth** across the Qwen3 family, meaning it offers the best compression-vs-quality tradeoff. This is visible in Figure 4b as the 3.125-bit curve consistently lying in the "sweet spot" — performance degrades gracefully from BF16, while 2.125-bit shows a sharper drop. Perplexity values supporting this (Table 4): at 8B, 3.125-bit scores 10.34 vs. 4.125-bit at 9.81 (acceptable trade); at 14B, 3.125-bit scores 9.06 vs. 4.125-bit at 8.76 (narrower gap as scale increases); at 1.7B, the gap is larger (19.72 vs. 18.18), suggesting Pareto optimality becomes clearer at scale.

At **3.125 bits**: The separation emerges more clearly. HPTQ achieves 66.93, 70.96, 77.53, 95.4, 36.11/54.73 — MMLU drops 2% from BF16 but all other metrics hold strong. GPTQ scores 68.35, 65.80, 75.46, 75.46, 36.11/55.21 — note the catastrophic SciQ collapse (75.46 vs. BF16's 95.7), which is a **35-point drop** that signals catastrophic quantization failure on factual/scientific knowledge, even as WinoGrande and MMLU remain reasonable. HRTN achieves 66.22, 67.85, 76.12, 93.7, 35.13/53.68 — better SciQ than GPTQ (93.7 vs. 75.46) but noticeably degraded from BF16. RTN drops to 57.93, 47.90, 70.89, 87.1, 34.03/52.76 — substantial degradation across the board, especially MMLU (47.90 vs. 73.02). SSQR must be compared at its higher bitwidths: SSQR-1% (3.445 bits) achieves 68.11, 68.46, 75.84, 95.5, 38.19/55.95 — broadly better than HPTQ at 3.125 bits but at 0.32 extra bits. SSQR-5% (3.725 bits) achieves 62.59, 58.67, 73.23, 90.8, 31.21/50.25 — substantially worse than HPTQ at 3.125 bits despite being 0.6 bits higher, showing that SSQR's outlier overhead doesn't always translate to accuracy.

At **2.125 bits**: Here the divergence is stark and supports the paper's claims dramatically. HPTQ achieves 59.19, 52.99, 72.52, 86.8, 31.09/49.01 — degraded but functional, retaining non-trivial accuracy. GPTQ collapses to 52.25, 34.25, 57.83, 57.83, 28.40/46.91 — MMLU at 34.25% is near random (25% for 4-choice), and SciQ at 57.83 is catastrophic. HRTN scores 51.22, 33.91, 65.78, 76.8, 30.48/51.78 — also collapsed but with slightly better PiQA. RTN reaches 49.08, 22.95, 51.63, 21.2, 24.11/47.33 — SciQ at 21.2% is worse than random guessing (25% for 4 choices), confirming complete model destruction.

**The key finding from Table 5:** GPTQ's standard clipping implementation fails catastrophically on knowledge-intensive benchmarks (SciQ) at moderate compression (3.125 bits), dropping from 95.7 to 75.46, while HPTQ drops only to 95.4. This is not a gradual degradation — it's a cliff, likely caused by clipped weights in layers that are sensitive to factual knowledge retrieval. The removal of clipping (via Huffman encoding in HPTQ, or sparse storage in SSQR) prevents this cliff, consistent with the paper's thesis that clipping introduces errors that violate the Babai guarantee and disproportionately harm certain model capabilities.

#### Llama Model Results: Generalization Across Architectures

Tables 11–15 extend the perplexity and zero-shot evaluations to Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, and Llama-2-7B, confirming the patterns are not Qwen3-specific.

**Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Table 11, perplexity):** BF16 baseline achieves 11.01 on WikiText-2. At 4.125 bits, HPTQ (11.27) slightly outperforms GPTQ (11.96) and SSQR-1% (11.38 at 4.445 bits). At 3.125 bits, HPTQ (12.51) substantially outperforms GPTQ (15.20) and all SSQR variants (best is SSQR-5% at 12.29 but at 4.725 bits — 1.6 extra bits). At 2.125 bits, HPTQ (22.58) vastly outperforms GPTQ (357.69) and all SSQR variants under 4 bits (best is SSQR-5% at 3.725 bits, 22.94). The same GPTQ cliff at low bitwidths appears consistently.

**Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Table 14, zero-shot):** BF16 baseline: Wino 68.75, MMLU 62.18, PiQA 76.17, SciQ 95.4. At 4.125 bits, HPTQ (68.03, 61.57, 76.55, 95.0) slightly edges GPTQ (68.11, 59.81, 75.73, 95.5) — similar with MMLU advantage for HPTQ. At 3.125 bits, HPTQ (68.35, 58.50, 74.97, 95.5) substantially outperforms GPTQ (66.06, 49.13, 72.58, 94.0) with a 9.4-point MMLU gap. At 2.125 bits, HPTQ (60.85, 42.75, 69.15, 89.9) again vastly exceeds GPTQ (50.59, 22.96, 53.65, 63.4) — an 18.8-point MMLU gap and 26.5-point SciQ gap.

**Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Table 12, perplexity):** BF16 achieves 7.20. HPTQ at 4.125 bits (7.37) beats GPTQ (7.56). At 3.125 bits (7.84 vs. 9.44), the gap widens. At 2.125 bits, the cliff is dramatic: HPTQ 11.89 vs. GPTQ 148.15. SSQR performs competitively but at higher bitwidths: SSQR-5% at 4.725 bits reaches 7.98, close to BF16, while SSQR-3% at 4.085 bits reaches 8.16.

**Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Table 15, zero-shot):** The GPTQ cliff at low bitwidths appears clearly: at 2.125 bits, HPTQ achieves 63.69 Wino, 45.01 MMLU, 90.8 SciQ, while GPTQ scores 54.93, 24.67, 75.1 — a 20.3-point MMLU gap. Notably, even at 4.125 bits, GPTQ (65.68 MMLU) lags HPTQ (67.90) and BF16 (68.31), suggesting the degradation begins earlier than for Qwen3.

**Llama-2-7B (Table 13, perplexity):** At 4.125 bits, HPTQ (5.53) slightly outperforms GPTQ (5.70). At 3.125 bits, HPTQ (5.77) more clearly exceeds GPTQ (6.75). At 2.125 bits, HPTQ (7.45) massively outperforms GPTQ (28.07). The pattern is consistent: moderate separation at high bitwidths, dramatic separation at low bitwidths.

**Across all three model families, the qualitative pattern repeats:** (1) At 4 bits, HPTQ and GPTQ are comparable with a small edge for HPTQ; (2) At 3 bits, HPTQ holds up well while GPTQ begins to deteriorate, with the gap especially pronounced on knowledge-intensive tasks (SciQ, MMLU); (3) At 2 bits, GPTQ catastrophically fails while HPTQ remains functional, albeit degraded. This consistent pattern across architectures strongly supports the paper's claim that removing clipping (HPTQ) enables the Babai framework to deliver robust quantization, while clipping (GPTQ) introduces failure modes that worsen as bitwidth decreases.

#### Comparison with State-of-the-Art Vector Quantization Methods (Table 16)

Table 16 places HPTQ and SSQR in context with the current state-of-the-art on Llama-2-7B, reporting WinoGrande and PiQA accuracies against AQLM, QuIP#, and QTIP (numbers taken from their respective papers, except HPTQ/SSQR/GPTQ which are the authors' measurements).

At **~4 bits**: All methods are tightly clustered. HPTQ at 4.125 bits scores 69.61 Wino / 77.75 PiQA, essentially matching QuIP# at 4.000 bits (67.60 / 78.40) and QTIP (67.10 / 78.40), and slightly outperforming AQLM at 4.040 bits (67.32 / 78.24). GPTQ at 4.125 bits (68.82 / 77.97) is also competitive.

At **~3 bits**: HPTQ at 3.125 bits scores 67.72 / 77.80 — competitive with QuIP# at 3.000 bits (66.50 / 77.30), QTIP at 3.000 bits (66.90 / 78.10), and AQLM at 3.040 bits (66.93 / 76.88). GPTQ at 3.125 bits (64.96 / 73.88) falls behind all methods, with a notable PiQA gap. SSQR-1% at 3.445 bits (65.43 / 77.15) trails HPTQ despite higher bitwidth.

At **~2 bits**: This is where HPTQ distinguishes itself. HPTQ at 2.125 bits scores 65.82 / 73.56 — comparable to QuIP# at 2.000 bits (64.90 / 75.10), QTIP at 2.000 bits (64.70 / 75.90), and AQLM at 2.290 bits (65.67 / 74.92) and 2.020 bits (65.67 / 74.76). GPTQ at 2.125 bits collapses to 49.64 / 56.20. SSQR-1% at 2.445 bits (50.04 / 56.15) similarly fails.

**The critical interpretation:** HPTQ — a scalar quantization method with Huffman encoding — achieves accuracy on par with sophisticated vector/lattice quantization methods (AQLM, QuIP#, QTIP) that use lattice codebooks (E8) and incoherence processing (Hadamard transforms). This is remarkable because HPTQ is conceptually simpler: a single global scale, GPTQ's error propagation, and entropy coding. It suggests that the *no-clipping* property and the Babai framework, rather than complex codebook design, may be the primary drivers of low-bitwidth robustness. The comparison also validates the paper's framing: prior lattice quantization methods used lattices as codebook structures but did not formulate the optimization as CVP with Babai's algorithm. HPTQ, by contrast, explicitly implements Babai (via GPTQ back-to-front without clipping) and achieves comparable results with a simpler representational scheme.

#### Inference Speedup (Figure 4c and Figure 5)

The SSQR CUDA kernel's end-to-end speedup (Figure 4c) measures decoding latency when generating 128 new tokens at batch size 1 on an NVIDIA RTX A6000. The kernel achieves approximately **2× speedup** over the PyTorch BF16 baseline across Inlier bitwidths (2, 3, 4) and outlier rates (0–5%). The speedup is near-constant at 2×, with slight improvement at lower inlier bitwidths and lower outlier rates.

Figure 5 provides the layer-wise breakdown. The largest speedups (up to ~4× at batch size 1) occur in the **Gate·Up** and **Down** layers — the largest matrix multiplications in the transformer block — where memory bandwidth savings from 2–3-bit inliers are most impactful. The **Q·K·V** and **O** layers show more modest speedups (1.5–2.5× at batch size 1), reflecting their smaller dimensions (attention projections are typically smaller than FFN projections). As batch size increases to 8–16, tensor core utilization improves for both the quantized kernel and the BF16 baseline, compressing the relative speedup to approximately 1.3–1.8×.

**Speedup decreases monotonically with outlier rate:** at 0% outliers, 2-bit inlier speedups approach 3.5–4.0× on the largest layers; at 5% outliers, the same layers drop to ~1.8–2.2×. This is because outliers require SIMT core processing (no tensor core acceleration) and scatter/gather memory operations that break coalescing.

There is no TPOT comparison against other quantization inference frameworks (e.g., bitsandbytes, Marlin, or QuIP#'s kernel), only against PyTorch BF16. This makes the speedup figure relative to an unoptimized baseline rather than a production-grade quantized inference baseline.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **GPTQ vs. HRTN vs. HPTQ decomposition (Table 3):** This is the central ablation for separating the contributions of error propagation (GPTQ's Babai structure) and no-clipping (Huffman encoding). RTN = baseline with neither; HRTN = baseline + Huffman encoding (no-clipping, no error propagation); GPTQ = original with error propagation and clipping; HPTQ = error propagation + no-clipping. At 4.125 bits, HRTN (9.90 WikiText-2) outperforms GPTQ (10.10), suggesting that at moderate compression, removing clipping provides more benefit than Babai's error propagation. At 3.125 bits, HPTQ (10.34) outperforms both HRTN (10.75) and GPTQ (12.77), showing that at lower bitwidths, *both* error propagation and no-clipping are needed — neither suffices alone. At 2.125 bits, the gap is enormous: HPTQ (13.97) vs. HRTN (593.05) vs. GPTQ (57.51), proving that error propagation becomes *essential* for survival at extreme compression, but only in the absence of clipping. The interaction is clear: error propagation without no-clipping (GPTQ) moderately helps at 4 bits but fails catastrophically at 2 bits; no-clipping without error propagation (HRTN) is surprisingly good at 4 bits but fails at 2 bits; the combination (HPTQ) is robust across the entire range.

- **Outlier rate vs. bitwidth tradeoff in SSQR (Table 3):** At a fixed inlier bitwidth of 2 bits, increasing outlier rate from 1% (2.445 effective bits, 22.30 WikiText-2) to 5% (3.725 effective bits, 12.88) dramatically improves perplexity but at a steep storage cost — nearly 1.3 extra bits for a 9.4-point perplexity gain. At 3.125 bits, SSQR with 3% outliers (4.085 bits, 10.42 perplexity) slightly outperforms SSQR with 1% outliers (3.445 bits, 10.64), suggesting a sweet spot at 3–5% outliers for matching HPTQ's performance but at higher bitwidth. The comparison with HPTQ at matched bitwidths is unfavorable: at ~3.4 bits, SSQR-1% (3.445 bits, 10.64) is worse than HPTQ at 3.125 bits (10.34), despite higher bitwidth; at ~3.7 bits, SSQR-5% (3.725 bits, 12.88) is substantially worse. This suggests that the sparse outlier storage format adds overhead that is not well-captured by the effective bitwidth calculation, or that the distribution of outliers is such that the storage cost per outlier (16-bit float + index overhead) is higher than reported.

- **Scaling across model sizes (Table 4, Figure 4b):** HPTQ's behavior as model size varies reveals that smaller models degrade more sharply at low bitwidths. At 2.125 bits, the 0.6B model scores 156.45 on WikiText-2 (+646% vs. BF16's 20.96), while the 14B model scores 11.36 (+31% vs. BF16's 8.65). This disproportionate degradation at small scales suggests that smaller models have less redundancy and are more sensitive to quantization error — a finding consistent with the CVP interpretation, where the Hessian of a smaller (more capacity-constrained) model may be more ill-conditioned, leading to larger error bounds. The 3.125-bit curve is Pareto optimal across all scales, meaning it offers the best perplexity-per-byte tradeoff for every model size.

- **Quantization order comparison (Table 2, Section D.3):** The comparison of `tr(D)` (the LDL diagonal trace) across five order heuristics on Qwen3-8B block 18 shows: act-order reduces tr(D) by 35–50% relative to natural (back-to-front/front-to-back) and random orders in the Q·K·V and Gate·Up layers, while offering negligible improvement in the O layer (~2% reduction). Min-pivot achieves a further 0.9–1.0% reduction over act-order in the same layers. The Down layer shows the smallest relative improvement from act-order (~7.6% reduction) and min-pivot (~7.7%), likely because the Down projection's Hessian is better-conditioned to begin with. The random order variance is small (`1.168e8` vs. `1.322e9` across 100 runs), indicating that while order matters, the effect size varies substantially across layer types.

- **Zero-shot accuracy robustness at 3.125 bits (Table 5):** The most informative ablation is the per-benchmark breakdown: HPTQ maintains SciQ at 95.4 (vs. BF16 95.7) while GPTQ drops to 75.46 — a 20-point gap on a single benchmark. MMLU shows a smaller but significant gap (70.96 vs. 65.80), while WinoGrande and TruthfulQA are nearly identical. This pattern — factual/knowledge benchmarks disproportionately affected by clipping — is consistent across Llama models (Tables 14–15) and suggests that the clipped weights specifically perturb layers involved in knowledge retrieval or fact storage. The Babai framework cannot explain this directly (the error bound is aggregate, not per-benchmark), but the empirical result validates that clipping introduces non-uniform damage.

- **Comparison with AQLM/QuIP#/QTIP (Table 16):** HPTQ achieves comparable accuracy to these specialized vector quantization methods at matched bitwidths, despite using only scalar quantization + Huffman encoding. At 2 bits, HPTQ (65.82 Wino) is within 1 point of QuIP# (64.90) and QTIP (64.70). This robustness check shows that the Babai structure *with no-clipping* can close the gap to far more complex lattice codebook methods — a strong validation of the theoretical framework's practical relevance.

### Critical Assessment

**Do the experiments genuinely support the paper's central claims?**

**Claim from the executive summary: GPTQ, run back-to-front without clipping, is mathematically identical to Babai's nearest plane algorithm.** The experiments do not test this claim — it is a theoretical result, proven in Section 4 and Appendix C. The experiments validate the *consequences* of the theory (that no-clipping methods built on the Babai framework outperform clipped GPTQ), but they do not directly verify the equivalence (e.g., by running both algorithms on the same inputs and checking bitwise identity of outputs). This is acceptable for a theory paper, but readers should understand that the experimental section validates the practical utility of the insight, not the theorem itself.

**Claim: GPTQ inherits an error upper bound from Babai under no-clipping.** The bound is derived (Theorem 5) but never empirically verified against actual quantization errors. The paper does not report a comparison between the theoretical bound `1/4 · (T⁻¹si)⊤ D (T⁻¹si)` and the observed `∥XQ − XW∥²` on quantized models. This would be a straightforward validation — compute the bound for each layer, quantize, measure the actual error, and check that it lies below the bound. Without this, the bound remains a theoretical object whose practical tightness is unknown. The expected-error refinement (Section D.2, 1/3 of the worst-case bound under uniform prior) is also unverified.

**Claim: No-clipping methods (HPTQ, SSQR) outperform original GPTQ.** This is strongly supported by Tables 3, 5, 11–15 across three model families and multiple bitwidths, with the caveats below.

**Claim: HPTQ enables Pareto optimal compression-quality tradeoffs.** Supported by Figure 4b for Qwen3 family, but only tested on perplexity (WikiText-2), not on zero-shot accuracy. The Pareto optimality claim would be stronger with a scatter plot of accuracy vs. model size across bitwidths.

**Genuine weaknesses and missing experiments:**

**1. No direct verification of the error bound.** The paper's central practical contribution — the computable error guarantee — is never checked against reality. For any given layer and quantization run, the bound `1/4 · (T⁻¹si)⊤ D (T⁻¹si)` can be computed, and the actual activation-space error `∥XQ − XW∥²` can be measured. The ratio between the two would reveal how tight the bound is in practice. Since the bound is claimed to be tight (Theorem 5: "the bound is tight" with equality when the target lies at a hyper-cuboid vertex), this verification is important for establishing whether the theoretical worst-case is ever approached in real data.

**2. No perplexity or accuracy for the "back-to-front GPTQ without clipping" baseline that the theory describes.** The practical methods (HPTQ, SSQR) add additional components (Huffman encoding, sparse outlier storage, scale binary search) beyond the basic Babai/GPTQ algorithm. To isolate the contribution of the Babai equivalence itself, the paper should have evaluated a direct implementation of Algorithm 4 or 8 (Babai-Quantize without clipping, using a fixed grid `Z` and per-group scales) and compared it to standard clipped GPTQ. Does the direction reversal alone (front-to-back vs. back-to-front) change accuracy? The theory says it shouldn't matter if the permutation order is adjusted, but this is never tested.

**3. SSQR binary search cost is never quantified.** SSQR's scale-adjustment mechanism (Algorithm 9) runs a binary search that calls GPTQ without clipping in each iteration. The number of iterations and the total quantization time relative to standard GPTQ are not reported. If the binary search requires, say, 10 GPTQ runs per layer, the quantization time is 10× longer — a significant practical cost that affects the method's deployability.

**4. HPTQ's Huffman codebook overhead is not detailed.** The effective bitwidth calculation for HPTQ should account for the Huffman codebook itself (mapping from integer values to variable-length codes), which must be stored alongside the compressed integers. For a weight matrix with `c × r` entries, the codebook size grows with the number of distinct integer values, which could be large in the no-clipping setting (since outlier weights map to large integers). If the codebook is, say, hundreds of bytes per matrix, this is negligible; if it's megabytes, it matters. The paper does not specify.

**5. SSQR's effective bitwidth calculation may undercount overhead.** SSQR stores outliers in a compressed sparse row (CSR) format, which requires column indices for each non-zero. The effective bitwidth formula `b_inlier * (1 − ρ) + 16 * ρ` accounts for the outlier values (16 bits each) but not the column indices (typically 16 bits each as well, doubling the outlier storage cost). If this overhead is significant, SSQR's reported bitwidths are optimistic, and its actual storage parity with HPTQ at matched *reported* bitwidths would shift.

**6. Only a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU is tested for inference speedup.** The kernel's performance on other Ampere cards (A100, A40) or newer architectures (Hopper H100, Blackwell) is unknown. The 2× speedup over PyTorch BF16 is promising but leaves headroom — a comparison against optimized quantized inference frameworks (like Marlin for GPTQ, or the QuIP# kernel) would contextualize whether the SSQR kernel is genuinely fast or merely faster than an unoptimized baseline.

**7. No comparison against GPTQ with the SAME no-clipping representation.** A key missing experiment: what happens if you take standard GPTQ (front-to-back, with clipping), disable clipping, store overflowed weights as FP16, and call it a day — without the Babai directional equivalence? This would test whether the theory's directional insight (back-to-front as Babai) provides any practical benefit, or whether simply removing clipping achieves most of the gain. Since all methods in Section 5 use act-order (which is symmetric — front-to-back act-order is the reverse of back-to-front act-order), the directional reversal may not actually change any results, making this missing ablation particularly important.

**8. The 3.125-bit Pareto optimality claim is based solely on perplexity.** The zero-shot results (Table 5) show that at 3.125 bits, HPTQ and GPTQ have comparable WinoGrande and TruthfulQA scores, but HPTQ has a massive SciQ advantage (95.4 vs. 75.46). This suggests perplexity alone may not capture the practical advantage — downstream task preservation is more compelling. A Pareto frontier in the accuracy-storage space (e.g., average zero-shot accuracy vs. model size) would be more informative than perplexity alone.

**9. Group size is fixed at 128 for all non-HPTQ methods.** HPTQ uses a single global scale (effectively group size = full dimension), while SSQR, GPTQ, and RTN use group size 128. This is an apples-to-oranges comparison on the scale storage axis — HPTQ's global scale saves storage but may hurt accuracy if per-group variation is important. The paper does not explore whether HPTQ's advantage comes from no-clipping, from the single-scale simplification, or from the Huffman encoding, since these three factors are confounded.

**10. No quantification of the "outlier cliff" precision.** The catastrophic failure of GPTQ at low bitwidths (SciQ dropping from 95.7 to 75.46 at 3.125 bits) is dramatic but unexplained. *Which* weights are being clipped? *Which* layers are most affected? Are there specific attention heads or FFN neurons whose quantization at 3 bits causes the factual knowledge collapse? Without this diagnostic, the finding validates that clipping is harmful but offers no guidance for *where* or *why*.

**11. Calibration data is a single dataset (FineWeb-Edu).** The paper does not report results with different calibration sets, so the sensitivity of the Hessian estimate — and thus of the Babai bound and the quantization quality — to calibration data distribution is unknown. Since Theorem 5's bound depends on the Hessian `X⊤X`, and this Hessian is estimated from calibration data, shifts in deployment distribution could alter the effective bound and the quantization quality.

The experiments convincingly demonstrate that no-clipping quantization methods (HPTQ, SSQR) substantially outperform clipped GPTQ, particularly at aggressive compression ratios, across multiple model families and benchmarks. The failure of GPTQ on knowledge tasks at 3 bits is a genuine empirical discovery that would not have been predicted by perplexity alone. However, the experiments test the practical *consequences* of the Babai equivalence rather than the equivalence itself, and several key validation experiments — direct error bound verification, a pure Babai-without-clipping baseline without Huffman/sparse extensions, and a controlled ablation of direction reversal — are absent. The paper's experimental contribution is therefore better characterized as "demonstrating that no-clipping designs enable robust low-bitwidth quantization" rather than "empirically validating the GPTQ-Babai equivalence." The theory provides the *motivation* for removing clipping; the experiments provide the *evidence* that removing clipping helps; but the causal chain from Babai's error bound to HPTQ's accuracy advantage is more correlational than directly demonstrated.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Theoretical Error Bound Is Never Empirically Verified

**The assumption or constraint.** Theorem 5 derives a tight worst-case bound `$\frac{1}{4}(T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)^\top D (T^{-1}\mathbf{s}_i)$` on the squared layer-wise quantization error under the no-clipping assumption, with the claim that "equality is attained when the target `$\mathbf{y}$` lies at the corner of the hyper-cuboid, so the bound is tight" (Section 3.2). The expected-case refinement (Section D.2) further predicts that under a uniform prior, the expected error is exactly `$1/3$` of the worst-case bound. Both are derived from Babai's classical analysis and are mathematically correct *given the assumptions*, but neither is ever compared against actual measured quantization errors on real layers.

**The consequence.** Without empirical verification, it is unknown whether the bound is practically tight (whether real weight distributions ever approach the worst-case corner-of-the-hyper-cuboid scenario) or whether it is loose by orders of magnitude (making it a theoretically reassuring but practically uninformative guarantee). The expected-error factor of `$1/3$` depends on a uniform prior over the hyper-cuboid that has no justification from the weight statistics of trained neural networks — weights are known to have heavy-tailed, non-uniform distributions (Li et al., 2025). If the bound is loose in practice, it provides a *legally safe* guarantee (the error will never exceed it) but no *operationally useful* guidance (it cannot predict whether 4-bit quantization will be accurate enough for a specific use case). Conversely, if it is tight for some layers, those layers become identifiable candidates for targeted mitigation (e.g., higher precision or basis reduction).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports no measurements of actual layer-wise quantization error `$\|XQ - XW\|_F^2$` for any quantized model, nor any comparison between observed errors and the theoretical bound. The bound appears in Theorem 5 as a derived result, is discussed qualitatively in Sections 4.4 and 4.5, and is used to motivate the min-pivot order heuristic, but it is never plotted against empirical errors. The `$\operatorname{tr}(D)$` values in Table 2 are reported for comparing order heuristics, but these are the *bounds themselves* (proportional to the right-hand side of Theorem 5), not a comparison of bound versus actual error. The expected-error claim (Section D.2) lists no experiment.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the bound as a theoretical contribution whose value lies in connecting GPTQ to the CVP literature and in providing a computable quantity (`$\operatorname{tr}(D)$`) for optimizing quantization order, not as an empirically validated predictive tool. The authors do not flag this as a limitation or suggest verifying the bound as future work — it is left as an unvalidated theoretical result.

---

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for and Potentially Dominant

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's no-clipping methods require additional computation at quantization time that is not accounted for in the headline comparisons. SSQR's scale-adjustment mechanism (Section 5, Algorithm 9) uses a binary search over per-output-channel scale multipliers: `$s_{\min}, s_{\max} \leftarrow 0^r, 2^r$`, running the inner GPTQ-without-clipping procedure (Algorithm 10) at each iteration until the scale vector `$\mathbf{s}$` converges. HPTQ's entropy-guided scale selection (Section 5, Algorithm 11) similarly runs a binary search: `$s_{\min} \leftarrow 0$`, `$s_{\max} \leftarrow \|W\|_\infty$`, quantizing the full model without clipping at each iteration until the Huffman coding cost matches the target bitwidth. The authors state that the loop runs "while `$s$` not converge" for SSQR and "while `$s$` not converge" for HPTQ, but never report the number of iterations, the wall-clock time, or the FLOPs cost of this search relative to standard GPTQ.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt SSQR or HPTQ cannot estimate the quantization-time cost from the paper. If, for example, the binary search requires 15 iterations per layer and each iteration runs a full GPTQ pass (with Cholesky decomposition, error propagation over all output channels, and outlier detection), the quantization time could be 15× that of standard GPTQ. For a 70B-parameter model where GPTQ quantizes in minutes to hours, a 15× multiplier could push the process to days — a prohibitive cost that would eliminate the practical advantage of "one-shot" post-training quantization. The paper's framing as "GPTQ as Babai's algorithm" suggests computational simplicity (Algorithm 4 is structurally similar to GPTQ), but the scale-selection overhead is specific to the no-clipping constraint and is external to the Babai equivalence itself. Without quantifying this cost, the paper provides an incomplete picture of the deployment pipeline.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper reports no iteration counts, no timing measurements, and no FLOPs accounting for the SSQR or HPTQ binary searches. The only computational cost discussed is the asymptotic complexity of the underlying GPTQ/Babai iteration (`$\mathcal{O}(c^3)$` per layer, shared across output channels), not the multiplicative overhead of scale search. The experimental setup (Section E.2) describes calibration data construction and evaluation protocols but omits any mention of quantization-time measurement.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The binary search algorithms are presented as practical procedures (Algorithms 9, 11) without any discussion of their computational overhead, convergence rate, or whether a more efficient method (e.g., a direct optimization instead of binary search, or a warm-start from MSE scales that reduces iterations) could serve the same purpose. This is a significant omission for a paper that positions its methods as practically deployable.

---

### HPTQ's Huffman Overhead and Effective Bitwidth Calculation Are Not Fully Specified

**The assumption or constraint.** HPTQ represents weights using a single global scale `$s$` and integer weights `$Z \in \mathbb{Z}^{c \times r}$`, compressed via Huffman encoding to achieve a target average bitwidth `$h$` (Algorithm 11). The effective bitwidth reported in Tables 3–4 and Figures 4a–b must account not only for the variable-length encoded integers but also for the Huffman codebook itself (the mapping from each distinct integer value to its variable-length bit sequence) and the single floating-point scale `$s$` (typically 16 bits). However, the paper does not specify how the codebook overhead is computed or amortized, nor does it report the distribution of distinct integer values that determines codebook size.

**The consequence.** In the no-clipping setting with a single global scale, the integer weights `$Z$` can span a very wide range — outlier weights produce large integer magnitudes that occur rarely but still require entries in the Huffman codebook. If the integer distribution has a long tail (as is typical for LLM weights; Li et al., 2025), the codebook may contain hundreds or thousands of entries. At, say, 1000 distinct values with 16-bit codes per entry, the codebook overhead is `$1000 \times 16 = 16,000$` bits per weight matrix. For a `$4096 \times 4096$` matrix with `$\approx 16.8$` million weights, amortized over all weights this is `$\approx 0.001$` bits per weight — negligible. But if the codebook is stored once per *layer* (as is standard for Huffman coding applied independently to each matrix) and there are hundreds of layers, the total overhead could accumulate to a non-trivial fraction of the model size in small models. More critically, if the codebook is stored per *block* or per *output channel* for finer granularity, the overhead scales accordingly. Without specifying the codebook granularity and size, the reported average bitwidths cannot be independently verified or reproduced.

Additionally, the binary search in HPTQ (Algorithm 11) adjusts `$s$` until the Huffman encoding bitwidth `$h'$` meets the target `$h$`. The convergence criterion (`"while `$s$` not converge"`) is underspecified — does it mean `$|h' - h| < \epsilon$` for some tolerance `$\epsilon$`? How is `$h'$` computed (empirical entropy over the entire integer matrix, or actual Huffman code length)? The paper does not say, making the algorithm description incomplete for reproduction.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None beyond the algorithmic pseudocode. The average bitwidths in Tables 3–4 are reported to one decimal place (e.g., 4.125, 3.125, 2.125) with no breakdown of how much is attributed to encoded integers versus overhead. The paper does not report codebook sizes, distinct integer counts, or scale storage costs for any configuration. The Huffman encoding baseline HRTN (Algorithm 12) shares this underspecification, so the GPTQ–HRTN–HPTQ comparison in Table 3 is between methods whose bitwidth accounting is equally opaque.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper provides no analysis of codebook storage, no sensitivity study of how effective bitwidth varies with codebook granularity, and no acknowledgment that this is a missing detail in the experimental methodology. For a method whose primary claimed advantage is better accuracy at matched bitwidths, the incomplete bitwidth accounting undermines confidence that the comparison against GPTQ (which has a simple, verifiable bitwidth: `$\lceil \log_2 |\mathbb{Z}_\dagger| \rceil$` plus group scale overhead) is genuinely at matched storage budgets.

### The No-Clipping Guarantee Requires an Infinite Lattice, Limiting Direct Applicability to Standard Fixed-Width Formats

**The assumption or constraint.** Theorem 5 and the Babai equivalence (Theorem 4) both require the no-clipping assumption: `$\mathbb{Z}_\dagger = \mathbb{Z}$` (the infinite integer lattice). The paper explicitly acknowledges that standard fixed-bitwidth quantization grids (e.g., INT4 with `$\{-8, \ldots, 7\}$`) violate this assumption: "GPTQ algorithm clips the overflowed integers at the rounding step, introducing large errors that violate the error bound in Theorem 5" (Section 5). The practical methods (SSQR, HPTQ) work around this by abandoning the fixed-bitwidth-per-weight paradigm — SSQR stores overflowed weights as sparse FP16 values, and HPTQ uses variable-length Huffman codes so that every integer is representable.

**The consequence.** Neither SSQR nor HPTQ produces a representation that is directly compatible with standard quantized inference frameworks that expect a uniform bitwidth per weight (e.g., INT4 or INT8 matrix multiplication kernels). SSQR requires a custom kernel that handles sparse FP16 outliers alongside dense low-bitwidth inliers — the paper provides one (Section 5, Appendix E.6), but it is a single implementation for a single GPU architecture (NVIDIA Ampere, tested on RTX A6000). HPTQ requires Huffman decoding before or during matrix multiplication, which is not supported by any standard inference framework. A practitioner who wants to deploy these methods must either use the authors' custom CUDA kernel (SSQR) or implement their own Huffman-accelerated matmul (HPTQ), neither of which integrates with existing serving stacks like vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, or llama.cpp. The paper's suggestion that "state-of-the-art 4-bit floating-point formats (e.g., MXFP4 and NVFP4) are essentially no-clipping" (Section 6) points toward a future where this limitation is resolved by industry adoption of no-clipping formats, but this is speculative — MXFP4 and NVFP4 are emerging standards, not widely deployed at the time of writing.

The paper also does not explore whether the Babai equivalence *could* be extended to handle clipping. The lattice framework naturally handles bounded integer domains via "CVPs with box constraints," which correspond to a CVP where the solution must lie within an axis-aligned hyper-rectangle in the integer lattice. Such problems have their own algorithmic literature (e.g., Schnorr-Euchner enumeration with bounds), but the paper does not discuss whether GPTQ's clipping behavior corresponds to a known bounded-CVP algorithm or whether the error bound can be adapted for the clipped case. This leaves open the question of whether the Babai insight can improve standard fixed-width quantization without abandoning the fixed-width format.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates that SSQR's CUDA kernel achieves ~2× speedup over PyTorch BF16 (Figure 4c) and provides layer-wise speedup breakdowns (Figure 5), showing that the dual-path architecture (tensor cores for inliers, SIMT cores for outliers) is functional and performant. However, the speedup is relative to PyTorch BF16, not to an optimized INT4 kernel (e.g., bitsandbytes, Marlin, or Cutlass-based implementations), so it is unclear whether the SSQR kernel is competitive with production-grade quantized inference. For HPTQ, no inference kernel is provided at all — the method is evaluated only on model quality (perplexity, zero-shot accuracy), not inference speed. This omission is significant because Huffman decoding during inference could introduce serialization overhead (variable-length codes require sequential decoding, breaking SIMD parallelism) that negates the storage savings.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed for SSQR via the custom CUDA kernel (Section 5, Appendix E.6), but not for HPTQ. The paper acknowledges in the closing remarks that extending the lattice view to "clipped grids" is immediate future work (Section 6), but makes no progress toward it in the current paper. The suggestion that MXFP4/NVFP4 make the no-clipping analysis "directly apply to actual practice" is forward-looking but does not constitute a mitigation for current deployability.

### Single Calibration Dataset with No Sensitivity Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** All quantization experiments use a single calibration dataset — FineWeb-Edu (subset `sample-10BT`), with 256 sequences of length 2048 — to estimate the input Hessian `$X^\top X$` that governs both GPTQ's error propagation and the Babai error bound (Theorem 5). The Hessian is the central data-dependent object in the entire framework: it defines the lattice geometry, determines the coefficients for error propagation, and (through its LDL decomposition diagonal `$D$`) provides the error guarantee. If the calibration data distribution differs from the deployment distribution, the Hessian — and consequently the quantization decisions — are optimized for the wrong distribution, potentially leading to larger errors than the bound predicts (since the bound is computed on the calibration Hessian, not the deployment Hessian).

**The consequence.** Post-training quantization is known to be sensitive to calibration set quality, but standard GPTQ can partially mitigate this by using diverse, large calibration sets that cover the expected input distribution. The paper's calibration set (FineWeb-Edu, a web-text educational subset) may not be representative of all deployment scenarios — a model serving coding queries, scientific papers, or non-English text may see very different activation statistics. Because Theorem 5's bound is computed from the calibration Hessian and does not account for distribution shift, a user might be misled into believing the quantization is safe (based on the bound) when the actual deployment error is substantially larger. This is particularly concerning for the proposed methods, which are marketed as "error-guaranteed" — the guarantee is conditional on the calibration Hessian matching deployment, a condition that is untested and unstated in the theorem.

The paper does not evaluate quantization quality when the evaluation data distribution differs from the calibration distribution. The evaluation datasets (WikiText-2, C4) are both general web-text corpora, likely similar in distribution to FineWeb-Edu. A more stringent test would involve calibration on web text but evaluation on, say, code (HumanEval), mathematics (GSM8K), or domain-specific text (PubMed abstracts) to reveal whether the Babai-derived quantization is robust to distribution shift.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper uses a single calibration dataset throughout and reports no experiments varying the calibration source, size, or domain. There is no discussion of how the Hessian estimate's quality (finite-sample approximation from 256 sequences) affects the bound's reliability, nor any comparison of the LDL diagonal `$D$` estimated from different calibration subsets to assess stability.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge calibration set sensitivity as a limitation, does not suggest cross-validation of the Hessian estimate, and does not propose techniques for robust Hessian estimation (e.g., regularization beyond the diagonal damping factor `$\lambda$`, or ensembling over multiple calibration sets). For a method whose primary theoretical contribution is an error guarantee, the unexamined dependence of that guarantee on calibration data fidelity is a significant gap.

### The Babai Equivalence Depends on Dimensional Order That May Not Matter in Practice

**The assumption or constraint.** Theorem 4 states that GPTQ and Babai's algorithm are equivalent "if we align the dimensional order of these two algorithms, e.g., running GPTQ from the last to the first dimension." The paper emphasizes that the front-to-back GPTQ of the original algorithm (Frantar et al., 2023) is the *opposite* order from Babai's default back-to-front iteration, and that reversing the order reveals the geometric identity. However, all practical experiments in Section 5 use **act-order** — a data-dependent permutation `$P$` (or `$T$`) that reorders dimensions before quantization. Act-order is a permutation, not a directional reversal: when you permute the dimensions, "front-to-back" and "back-to-front" are no longer well-defined concepts — they become front-to-back and back-to-front *in the permuted basis*, which are different sequences of original dimensions. If act-order is symmetric (or if the reverse of act-order produces the same `$\operatorname{tr}(D)$`), then the directional reversal that the theory identifies as crucial may have *no effect whatsoever* on the quantized result for the configurations actually tested.

**The consequence.** The paper's narrative — that GPTQ's identity as Babai's algorithm was "hidden" by the directional mismatch, and that revealing it provides theoretical grounding — is compelling, but the experiments never isolate whether the directional reversal *itself* matters for the accuracy improvements reported. All methods in Section 5 (HPTQ, SSQR, GPTQ, RTN) use act-order. If act-order is symmetric under reversal — meaning processing the permuted dimensions front-to-back produces the same integer solution as processing them back-to-front — then the Babai equivalence is a theoretical insight without practical consequence for the reported results: the standard GPTQ (front-to-front with act-order) is *already* Babai's algorithm, just in disguise. The dramatic accuracy differences between HPTQ and standard GPTQ in Table 3 would then be entirely attributable to the no-clipping representation (Huffman encoding or sparse outlier storage), not to the Babai structure, which both methods share.

The paper provides one datapoint relevant to this concern: Table 2 compares `$\operatorname{tr}(D)$` for front-to-back and back-to-front orders on Qwen3-8B block 18. The values are nearly identical (e.g., 1.169e8 vs. 1.161e8 for Q·K·V, a 0.7% difference; 1.181e8 vs. 1.202e8 for Gate·Up, a 1.7% difference). This suggests that for these Hessians, reversing the order has minimal effect on the error bound, which implies the two directions produce very similar Gram-Schmidt orthogonalizations. If this near-symmetry holds broadly, then the directional equivalence — while mathematically elegant — is not the primary driver of practical improvements. The paper does not test whether running GPTQ back-to-front without clipping (a pure Babai implementation without Huffman encoding or sparse outlier storage) differs in accuracy from standard GPTQ front-to-back without clipping, which would directly measure the directional effect.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The `$\operatorname{tr}(D)$` comparison in Table 2 indirectly addresses this, showing near-symmetry for front-to-back vs. back-to-front. But there is no end-to-end accuracy comparison between front-to-back and back-to-front GPTQ without clipping. The ablation gap between HRTN and HPTQ (Table 3) measures the contribution of error propagation (GPTQ/Babai) vs. just RTN, but both HPTQ and the original GPTQ baseline use act-order, so they share the same dimensional sequence (up to act-order's specific permutation) — only the clipping behavior and Huffman encoding differ. The directional reversal is present in the theory but absent from the experiments.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss whether act-order's permutation makes the front-to-back/back-to-front distinction moot, and does not report results for any method without act-order (the natural order results in Table 2 are only for `$\operatorname{tr}(D)$`, not for perplexity or accuracy). A simple ablation — comparing HPTQ with front-to-back vs. back-to-front iteration under the same act-order permutation — would clarify whether the directional equivalence has practical bite, but it is not performed.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not propose a new quantization algorithm so much as it **reveals the algorithm that was already there**. That distinction carries a specific kind of intellectual weight: it transforms GPTQ from a clever engineering heuristic — "pick weights in a fixed order, round them, propagate the error forward, and hope it works" — into an instance of a classical algorithm with four decades of analysis behind it. The shift is from *what works* to *why it works*, and that shift has cascading consequences for how the field designs, analyzes, and debugs quantization methods.

**The magnitude: a reframing, not a paradigm shift.** The paper does not overturn existing practice — GPTQ remains GPTQ, and the back-to-front variant that matches Babai produces identical results to the front-to-back variant under the act-order permutation that everyone already uses (the near-symmetry in Table 2 shows the directional reversal barely moves `tr(D)`). What changes is the **interpretive lens**. Before this paper, a practitioner debugging a GPTQ failure had only empirical knobs to turn: try a different group size, adjust the damping factor, collect more calibration data. After this paper, the same practitioner can ask geometric questions: Is the Hessian ill-conditioned, producing a lattice with highly skewed Gram-Schmidt vectors that inflate the Babai error bound? Is the clipping threshold truncating the hyperplane geometry? Are the scales `si` misaligned with the LDL diagonal `D` so that large scale entries amplify large Gram-Schmidt norms? These are diagnosable, quantifiable questions — not trial-and-error hunches.

This reframing is most consequential for **two communities that previously had little overlap**:

**For the quantization/compression community:** The paper imports a vocabulary and a toolkit. "Hessian-guided error propagation" becomes "nearest-hyperplane projection on the Gram-Schmidt-orthogonalized basis." The empirically observed phenomenon that act-order helps (Frantar et al., 2023, Section 3.2) gains a geometric justification: act-order approximately sorts dimensions so that the shortest Gram-Schmidt residual is processed first, minimizing the LDL diagonal trace and tightening the Babai bound. The min-pivot heuristic (Algorithm 3) formalizes this connection and provides a principled alternative when act-order is insufficient. The gap between heuristic and theory closes — and with it, the space for principled improvement opens.

**For the lattice algorithms community:** The paper presents CVP instances at an unprecedented scale and with empirically rich structure. Classical lattice reduction (LLL, BKZ) targets worst-case or random bases; the Hessian matrices of trained transformers are anything but random — they encode the correlation structure of natural language data, with block-diagonal patterns reflecting attention heads, low-rank structure, and heavy-tailed eigenvalue spectra. These properties may admit specialized reduction algorithms that outperform generic LLL, or they may reveal that the classical worst-case analysis (CVP is NP-hard; Babai's approximation factor can be exponential in `c`) is overly pessimistic for the structured bases that arise in practice. The paper opens a channel for lattice theorists to study a practically important source of CVP instances, and for systems builders to import lattice algorithms beyond Babai — enumeration, Schnorr-Euchner search, BKZ reduction with early termination — that have been refined over decades in the cryptography and coding theory communities.

**Reconciling contradictions.** The paper provides a unified explanation for a tension that has lingered in the quantization literature: why does GPTQ work robustly at 4 bits but collapse at 2-3 bits on some models and benchmarks? The Babai perspective answers: the error bound scales with the LDL diagonal `D` and the scale vector `si`. As bitwidth decreases, scales must increase to cover the representable range, and larger scales amplify the `(T^{-1}si)^\top D (T^{-1}si)` quadratic form. At 4 bits, the bound remains manageable for typical Hessians; at 2 bits, for ill-conditioned layers (large `D[j,j]` entries), the bound explodes. When clipping is added on top, the explosion is accelerated because clipped weights correspond to points far from the nearest hyperplane — the assumption driving Babai's `±1/2` per-coordinate error tolerance is violated, and errors compound across steps in ways the bound cannot contain. This explains why GPTQ's accuracy on knowledge-intensive tasks (SciQ dropping from 95.7 to 75.46 at 3.125 bits in Table 5) degrades catastrophically: clipping in a few critical layers produces large activation-space displacements that disproportionately damage factual recall. The framework predicts *which* layers are at risk (those with large `tr(D)`), turning an opaque failure mode into a diagnosable condition.

The paper also reconciles a methodological contradiction: prior work using lattice codebooks (QuIP#, QTIP) achieved strong results by treating the quantization *grid* as a lattice, while this paper achieves comparable results (Table 16) by treating the quantization *problem* as a lattice problem with a scalar grid. Both strategies work, but through different mechanisms — the codebook approach reduces representational error by improving the grid geometry, while the Babai approach reduces optimization error by improving the search procedure. The paper implicitly argues that, at current scales, optimization error dominates representational error (since HPTQ's scalar grid matches E8 lattice codebooks at 2 bits), suggesting that future effort should tilt toward better CVP algorithms rather than more exotic codebooks. This is a specific, falsifiable claim that can guide resource allocation in the compression community.

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Basis reduction for quantization-aware Hessians.** The paper explicitly identifies that LLL basis reduction is incompatible with batched quantization because it is scale-sensitive — different output channels `i` with different scale vectors `si` require different reduction transformations. But the paper only considers *full* LLL reduction applied to the raw basis `X diag(si)`. A natural relaxation is *partial* or *approximate* basis reduction that works in the shared Hessian space: apply a single unimodular transformation `T` across all output channels to improve the conditioning of the shared Hessian `X^\top X`, accepting that it may not be optimal for any individual channel but improving the aggregate bound `(T^{-1}si)^\top D (T^{-1}si)` on average. This is an open algorithmic problem — finding a single unimodular matrix that approximately minimizes the sum of per-channel Babai bounds — that is newly tractable because the paper provides the objective function.

- **Scale optimization guided by the error bound.** Currently, GPTQ (and SSQR, HPTQ) compute scales `si` via MSE minimization or binary search, independently of the Babai bound. The bound `1/4 (T^{-1}si)^\top D (T^{-1}si)` provides a differentiable objective in `si`: given the Hessian LDL diagonal `D` and the order `T`, one can optimize scales to directly minimize the worst-case per-channel error rather than the heuristic MSE. This could be formulated as a constrained optimization (minimize the bound subject to a bitwidth constraint via the integer range) and solved per-channel analytically or via convex optimization, replacing the expensive binary search in Algorithms 9 and 11.

- **Layer-wise precision allocation.** The `tr(D^{(i)})` values reported in Table 2 (ranging from `5.990e7` to `1.323e9` across layer types) show that the Babai error bound varies by orders of magnitude across different linear projections within the same transformer block. The Down projection, with the largest `tr(D)` (1.323e9), is the bottleneck for quantization quality — its error bound is ~22× larger than the Gate·Up projection's. This suggests that **uniform bitwidth across all layers is suboptimal**: allocating an extra bit to the Down projection while reducing precision on the Q·K·V projection could reduce the aggregate error bound at fixed total storage. This is a mixed-precision quantization problem with a principled objective function (minimize the maximum or sum of per-layer Babai bounds) that was not previously available.

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Ad-hoc search over quantization hyperparameters without geometric justification.** Before this paper, a researcher might sweep group sizes, damping factors, and order heuristics, measuring perplexity on a validation set to find the best combination. The Babai bound provides a cheap, pre-quantization proxy for quality: compute `tr(D)` for each candidate configuration, and the one with the smallest bound is likely to quantize best. This doesn't eliminate the need for empirical validation, but it dramatically reduces the search space by providing a theoretically motivated filter.

- **Developing ever-more-complex rounding schemes that ignore the Hessian geometry.** The paper proves a compositional ineffectiveness result (Section C.4): once Babai's projection has been executed, any additional GPTQ-style error propagation vanishes. This means that post-hoc "polishing" steps applied after the core quantization are algebraically redundant — they cannot improve the solution within the greedy hyperplane-projection framework. The only way to improve beyond Babai is to change the search strategy itself (e.g., enumeration with backtracking, which corresponds to Schnorr-Euchner search in CVP), not to layer additional greedy corrections on top.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Empirically verify the Babai error bound on real quantized layers.** The paper derives a tight worst-case bound in Theorem 5 but never measures actual layer-wise quantization error `\|XQ - XW\|_F^2` against it. A critical follow-up would: (1) for a range of models (Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.1-8B, and at least one model known to be quantization-resistant), compute the per-layer Babai bound from calibration data; (2) quantize each layer using back-to-front GPTQ without clipping at multiple bitwidths (2, 3, 4 bits with per-group scales); (3) measure the actual activation-space error and compute the ratio `bound / actual_error`. If the ratio is consistently large (e.g., 10–100×), the bound is theoretically valid but practically loose, and the expected-error refinement under uniform prior (factor 1/3, Section D.2) should be tested as an alternative predictor. If the ratio is close to 1 for some layers, those layers are "at the bound" and represent the quantization bottleneck, validating the tightness claim and identifying targets for targeted precision allocation. The experiment requires only a calibration set and a GPTQ implementation that logs intermediate errors — no new algorithms.

**Quantify the calibration-distribution-sensitivity of the Babai bound.** The Hessian `X^\top X` is estimated from a finite calibration sample (256 sequences of length 2048 from FineWeb-Edu). How much does the LDL diagonal `D` — and hence the error bound — vary when the calibration distribution changes? A stress-test would: sample calibration data from multiple domains (web text, code, scientific articles, multilingual text), compute `tr(D)` for each, and measure the variance. Then quantize a model using one domain's Hessian and evaluate perplexity on all domains, checking whether degradation correlates with the bound's increase relative to the in-domain calibration. If the bound is robust (low variance across domains), it is a reliable deployment-time guarantee; if it is brittle, the "error-guaranteed" claim needs qualification. This experiment can be done without retraining any models, using only calibration-set resampling and existing evaluation pipelines.

**Design a scale-aware basis reduction that works with batched quantization.** The paper identifies the central obstacle to importing LLL into GPTQ: LLL reduction is scale-sensitive, producing different transformations for different output channels `i` with different scale vectors `si`, which prevents the computational sharing that makes GPTQ efficient. A concrete algorithmic challenge: find a single unimodular matrix `T \in \mathbb{Z}^{c \times c}` (independent of `i`, so the Hessian decomposition can be shared) that approximately minimizes `\sum_{i=1}^r (T^{-1} \mathbf{s}_i)^\top D^{(T)} (T^{-1} \mathbf{s}_i)`, where `D^{(T)}` is the LDL diagonal of `T^\top X^\top X T`. This is a combinatorial optimization over the set of unimodular matrices. A reasonable starting point: relax the unimodular constraint to allow orthogonal or nearly-orthogonal transformations (e.g., Hadamard matrices, as used in QuIP# for incoherence processing), which preserve the lattice geometry while improving the conditioning of the Hessian and the resulting `tr(D)`. Compare the quantized accuracy of LLL-reduced (per-channel, impractically expensive), Hadamard-transformed (shared across channels, computationally cheap), and unreduced (standard act-order) GPTQ without clipping at 2–3 bits — the gap between LLL and Hadamard quantifies how much reduction potential remains on the table.

**Develop and evaluate a mixed-precision allocator driven by the Babai bound.** The `tr(D)` values in Table 2 vary by ~22× across layer types in the same transformer block. This is a direct signal for non-uniform bitwidth allocation: layers with larger `tr(D)` need more bits to achieve the same worst-case error. A follow-up would implement a greedy allocator that, given a total bit budget for the model, assigns bitwidths to layers by: (1) compute the Babai bound for each layer at a candidate bitwidth (e.g., 2, 3, 4 bits), using the corresponding scale magnitude (approximately inversely proportional to the grid range at that bitwidth); (2) start with the minimum bitwidth everywhere, then iteratively add bits to the layer with the largest bound reduction per extra bit, until the budget is exhausted. Compare this allocation against uniform-bitwidth GPTQ and HPTQ on perplexity and zero-shot accuracy at matched total model size. The comparison would test whether the theoretical bound translates to practical allocation decisions — if the allocator consistently outperforms uniform allocation at the same storage budget, it validates the bound as a decision-making tool rather than just a retrospective guarantee.

**Apply Schnorr-Euchner enumeration as a post-Babai refinement on the hardest layers.** Babai's nearest plane algorithm is not optimal — it greedily rounds each coefficient and never backtracks. The CVP literature provides enumeration algorithms (e.g., Schnorr-Euchner) that search a bounded-radius hypersphere around the Babai solution, potentially finding a lattice point closer to the target at the cost of exponential time in the search radius. For standard transformer layers with `c \approx 4096`, full enumeration is infeasible, but the paper's error bound identifies *which* layers have the largest Babai error — exactly those where enumeration might help most. A targeted experiment: for the single layer with the largest `tr(D)` in Qwen3-8B (likely a Down projection), run Schnorr-Euchner enumeration with a radius proportional to the Babai bound, searching for a lattice point with smaller error. Measure whether the improved solution reduces end-to-end perplexity when that layer's quantized weights are substituted into the full model. If even a small improvement in the bottleneck layer yields a measurable perplexity gain, it motivates developing approximate enumeration heuristics (beam search in the integer lattice, limited backtracking) that scale to full models. If enumeration finds no improvement — meaning Babai is already near-optimal for these Hessians — it confirms that the Hessian structure makes CVP easier than worst-case theory predicts, a finding of independent interest to the lattice community.

**Benchmark HPTQ's Huffman decoding overhead in a realistic inference pipeline.** HPTQ achieves strong accuracy but its inference practicality is unproven — there is no kernel for Huffman-decoded matrix multiplication. A follow-up would implement a minimal viable kernel (likely on CPU first, where variable-length decoding is more natural than on GPU SIMT cores) and measure: (1) decoding throughput in isolation (can Huffman decoding keep up with the matmul?); (2) end-to-end token generation latency for a small model (e.g., Qwen3-0.6B quantized with HPTQ at 3.125 bits) compared to the same model quantized with standard INT4 GPTQ and served via llama.cpp; (3) memory bandwidth consumption during decoding (does the variable-length format cause irregular memory access patterns that destroy cache performance?). If the decoding overhead is prohibitive, it motivates alternative entropy-coding schemes (ANS, arithmetic coding) that may have better hardware-compatibility properties while preserving the no-clipping lattice structure. If decoding is manageable, HPTQ becomes a deployable method rather than just an accuracy benchmark.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device deployment of 8B-class models with sustained accuracy.** The paper's headline result for practitioners is Figure 4b and Table 4: HPTQ at 3.125 bits preserves near-baseline accuracy on Qwen3-8B while shrinking the model from roughly 16 GB (BF16) to roughly 3.1 GB — small enough to fit comfortably on a consumer laptop GPU (8 GB) or even a high-end phone SoC with a dedicated neural engine. The specific numbers: WikiText-2 perplexity degrades from 9.73 to 10.34 (+6.3%), and zero-shot MMLU drops from 73.02% to 70.96% (−2.06 percentage points). For many applications (summarization, chat, code completion), this degradation is imperceptible to users but the memory savings are transformative — a model that previously required cloud offloading now runs entirely on-device, eliminating latency, privacy, and connectivity concerns. The SSQR kernel's ~2× speedup over PyTorch BF16 (Figure 4c) provides a reference point for the inference throughput that is achievable, though with the caveat that the kernel currently supports only the SSQR format (sparse outliers + dense inliers), not HPTQ.

**Cost-efficient batch inference for dataset processing and synthetic data generation.** Organizations that use LLMs to process large corpora — generating training data, scoring candidate responses, extracting structured information from documents — pay per-token inference costs that scale with model size. The paper's finding that HPTQ at 3.125 bits roughly matches 4-bit GPTQ at 4.125 bits in accuracy (Table 3: 10.34 vs. 10.10 WikiText-2 perplexity) at ~30% less storage means a straightforward cost reduction for batch pipelines: use HPTQ instead of GPTQ, shrink the model by an additional gigabyte, and either fit it on a cheaper GPU or increase the batch size within the same memory budget. This is a drop-in change — no modification to the inference code is needed if using the authors' provided quantization scripts, though inference requires either the CPU path for Huffman decoding or conversion to a fixed-bitwidth format. The Pareto optimality of 3.125 bits (Figure 4b) gives a concrete recommendation: for Qwen3 models from 4B to 14B, 3.125-bit HPTQ is the sweet spot, providing the best accuracy-per-byte tradeoff.

**Quantization-aware verifier and difficulty estimation for self-improvement pipelines.** The paper's identification of `tr(D)` — the LDL diagonal trace — as the key quantity governing quantization error has a direct application in data selection for self-improvement loops. When an LLM generates training data for itself (e.g., via STaR or ReST^EM), some generated samples are higher quality than others. Before this paper, filtering was based on surface-level signals (output perplexity, reward model scores, self-consistency). Post-Babai, one can additionally filter based on the *activation-space geometry* of the generation: inputs that produce Hessians with large `tr(D)` are intrinsically harder to quantize, meaning the model's output on those inputs is more sensitive to weight perturbations introduced by the self-improvement fine-tuning. These inputs may be less reliable as training targets. A concrete pipeline: during data generation, compute a fast approximation of `tr(D)` from the intermediate activations (the Hessian diagonal alone requires only computing `X[:,j]^\top X[:,j]` for each input dimension, which is `O(nc)` per layer), and use it as a per-sample quality weight — downweight or discard samples with anomalously high `tr(D)` in critical layers. This connects the lattice geometry to data quality in a way that was not previously possible, because the geometric interpretation of the Hessian as a lattice basis was absent.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper articulates a clear tradeoff between no-clipping representations (SSQR, HPTQ) and standard clipped quantization (GPTQ, RTN). The decision rule emerges from the empirical results in Tables 3–5 and the theoretical framework:

- **Prefer HPTQ when** the target bitwidth is 3 bits or below, and the inference deployment can accommodate variable-length decoding (or when accuracy evaluation is the goal and inference latency is not critical). The accuracy cliff that GPTQ experiences between 3.125 and 2.125 bits — SciQ dropping from 95.7 to 57.83 on Qwen3-8B (Table 5), WikiText-2 exploding from 12.77 to 57.51 (Table 3) — is avoided by HPTQ, which degrades gracefully (SciQ 95.4 at 3.125 bits, 86.8 at 2.125 bits). The 20+ point gap on knowledge-intensive benchmarks at matched bitwidths is large enough that for applications requiring factual reliability, HPTQ is the only viable option among the tested methods below 4 bits.

- **Prefer SSQR when** a custom CUDA kernel is acceptable and outlier rates of 3–5% are tolerable. SSQR with 5% outliers at 4.725 bits achieves 9.80 WikiText-2 perplexity (Table 3), essentially matching BF16, making it suitable for applications where accuracy preservation is paramount and the extra storage (~19% more than 4-bit HPTQ) is acceptable. The ~2× speedup over PyTorch BF16 (Figure 4c) makes SSQR attractive for latency-sensitive on-device deployment when the outlier rate is kept low.

- **Prefer standard GPTQ when** the deployment stack requires fixed-bitwidth inference kernels (e.g., INT4 support in TensorRT-LLM, vLLM, or llama.cpp) and the target bitwidth is 4 bits or higher. At 4.125 bits, GPTQ and HPTQ are nearly indistinguishable in accuracy (Table 3: 10.10 vs. 9.81 WikiText-2, within 3%), but GPTQ integrates with mature, production-hardened inference frameworks that HPTQ and SSQR currently lack. The simplicity and ecosystem compatibility of standard GPTQ outweigh the small accuracy gap at 4 bits for most production deployments.

- **Prefer none of these methods — consider vector quantization (AQLM, QuIP#, QTIP) when** compression below 2 bits is required and accuracy must be maintained. Table 16 shows that at ~2 bits, HPTQ (65.82 WinoGrande, 73.56 PiQA) essentially matches QuIP# (64.90, 75.10) and QTIP (64.70, 75.90) on Llama-2-7B, but these vector methods have been validated at even lower bitwidths (1.5–2 bits) where scalar methods have not been tested. The Babai framework is complementary to these methods — it optimizes the search, while vector quantization optimizes the grid — and combining them (Babai search over an E8 lattice codebook) is unexplored territory.
