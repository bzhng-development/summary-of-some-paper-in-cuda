# All Roads Lead to Rome: Incentivizing Divergent Thinking in Vision-Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2604.00479](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00479)

## 🎯 Pitch

Vision-language models trained with reinforcement learning solve problems more accurately on a single try, but surprisingly become *worse* than their untrained base models when given multiple attempts—because RL training collapses their reasoning diversity. The authors trace this to Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) prematurely discarding alternative solution strategies, and fix it with a simple multi-group policy objective that rewards models for thinking in fundamentally different ways.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes a fundamental behavioral divergence between reinforcement learning (RL)-trained vision-language models and their base counterparts, finding that RL models—despite higher accuracy on single attempts—engage in deeper yet narrower reasoning while base models exhibit broader, more diverse thinking patterns that enable them to solve a wider range of problems when given multiple attempts. Through an examination of training dynamics, the authors identify that Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) suffers from **diversity collapse**, where models prematurely converge to a limited subset of reasoning strategies and discard the majority of potential alternatives, leading to local optima and poor test-time scaling capabilities. To address this, the paper introduces **Multi-Group Policy Optimization (MUPO)**, a drop-in replacement for GRPO that partitions responses into multiple groups with localized advantage estimation and a diversity reward promoting inter-group separation (operationalized as cosine distance between reasoning embeddings), incentivizing divergent thinking across distinct solution strategies. MUPO-Thinker-7B achieves average gains of 2–7% over strong baselines on established benchmarks, including a 6.0% improvement in acc@4 on mathematical benchmarks (58.8% vs. 52.8% for prior best) and a 6.2% gain on general-purpose benchmarks (72.4% vs. 66.2%), establishing that preserving reasoning diversity during RL training enables models to discover globally superior solutions while maintaining sophisticated reasoning along individual paths—though the benefits are task-dependent, with mathematical problems favoring more groups and general problems preferring more uniform strategies.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: RL-Trained VLMs Sacrifice Reasoning Diversity for Depth

The paper addresses a specific and counterintuitive gap in our understanding of how reinforcement learning affects vision-language models. While recent work has demonstrated that RL—particularly Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)—substantially improves VLM performance on reasoning tasks, the paper identifies that this improvement comes with a hidden cost: **RL models lose the divergent thinking capabilities present in their untrained base counterparts**. This is not merely an observation about accuracy—it's a fundamental shift in *how* models approach problems, with direct consequences for their ability to handle novel or challenging cases.

The problem manifests as follows (Section 3.1): When evaluated on a single attempt (acc@1), RL-trained models convincingly outperform their base models. But when multiple attempts are allowed (acc@4), the advantage flips—base models solve substantially more problems, while RL models show only marginal gains from additional sampling. Figure 2 (A) and (B) quantify this: at the 7B scale, the gap between RL and base models narrows dramatically as k increases from 1 to 4, with base models demonstrating far greater capacity to discover correct solutions through diverse approaches. This pattern holds across both the 3B and 7B scales, across multiple RL-trained models (VLA-Thinker, LMM-R1, Vision-R1, R1-OneVision), and across reasoning-centric benchmarks spanning mathematical reasoning (MathVerse, WeMath), logical reasoning (LogicVista), and visual hallucination detection (HallusionBench).

### Why This Matters: The Practical and Theoretical Stakes

**Practical impact: test-time scaling is expensive and increasingly central.** As training costs soar, allocating extra computation at inference time—through parallel sampling, self-consistency, or verifier-based selection—has become a cost-effective strategy to boost performance (Section 2, "Test-Time Scaling"). This is the parallel scaling paradigm described in the paper: generate multiple candidates and aggregate. But if RL-trained models produce highly similar outputs across multiple samples, this entire paradigm is undermined. Every additional sample costs compute but yields diminishing returns because the model is re-exploring the same narrow region of solution space. The paper demonstrates this concretely: RL models show a "notable shift" where "the gains of RL models remain marginal" as k increases (Section 3.1), while base models with higher reasoning diversity show acc@4 improving substantially. In practical terms, organizations deploying RL-trained VLMs for high-stakes reasoning tasks may be paying for test-time compute that delivers far less than expected because the underlying model lacks the diversity to make parallel sampling effective.

**Theoretical significance: a missing axis in RL training dynamics.** The GRPO objective optimizes the model to produce high-quality responses by contrasting multiple candidate outputs and rewarding superior ones while penalizing less effective alternatives (Equation 1). But this objective is fundamentally about *relative quality* within a batch—it rewards responses that are better than the batch average. The paper's key insight is that this mechanism creates an inherent pressure toward mode collapse: early in training, when the policy is still diverse, one strategy randomly achieves slightly higher reward, gets reinforced, and the positive feedback loop rapidly extinguishes alternatives. This is not a bug in GRPO's implementation but a structural property of its advantage-computation mechanism (Equation 2), where advantages are computed globally across all responses in a batch. The paper provides what is, to our knowledge, the first explicit characterization of this diversity collapse phenomenon in VLM training, with Figure 3 (A) showing diversity dropping "sharply during the early training stage, i.e., first 20 steps, where the model has been exposed to only limited training data."

**Connection to human problem-solving.** The paper explicitly frames divergent thinking as more closely aligned with human reasoning: "individuals, when given multiple attempts, often approach the same problem from varied perspectives, increasing their chances of success, especially on challenging tasks where common strategies may fail or be error-prone" (Section 1). This is not merely an analogy—it motivates why diversity matters for hard problems. When the dominant strategy fails (as in the geometry example in Figure 1, where equation-solving leads to cumulative errors), alternative strategies (like the validation-based approach the base model discovers) become essential. The convergent RL model has no fallback; the divergent base model does.

### Prior Approaches and Where They Fall Short

**GRPO and its variants optimize depth, not breadth.** The standard GRPO formulation (Section 4.1) computes token-level advantages by broadcasting normalized sequence-level rewards across all positions. This mechanism naturally favors exploitation: responses that score highly relative to the batch mean get reinforced, and as training progresses, the policy distribution sharpens around high-reward modes. The paper's analysis of training dynamics (Section 3.2) reveals that this concentration happens remarkably early—diversity "drops sharply during the early training stage, i.e., first 20 steps"—meaning the model commits to a narrow strategy set before it has seen most of the training data. The remaining training steps then optimize within this confined space, resulting in "exploitation over exploration" and vulnerability to local optima.

Prior work has attempted to address limitations of GRPO along several axes, but none target diversity preservation directly:

- **Sampling efficiency approaches** (DAPO, GVPO; cited in Section 2) prioritize informative trajectories but operate within the same global-advantage framework. They improve which responses are emphasized but do not prevent mode collapse—they just collapse to a better-chosen mode.

- **Perception-aware methods** (Look Again, Think Slowly; cited in Section 2) focus on fine-grained visual reward design to promote perceptually grounded reasoning. They improve the quality of the dominant strategy without encouraging exploration of alternatives.

- **Entropy-based exploration** (RE, RSRL; cited in Section 2) incorporate uncertainty mechanisms but do so at the token or trajectory level, which the paper argues "fail to foster reasoning across divergent strategies" (Section 2). Adding entropy bonuses encourages the model to be less confident in its predictions, not to develop distinct strategic approaches.

- **Dataset construction and reward shaping** work (MM-Eureka, Perception-Aware Policy Optimization, VisualPRM, Look-Back; cited in Section 2) improves the quality of training signals but does not alter the structural tendency of global advantage estimation to concentrate probability mass.

In all these cases, the model is still optimizing a single policy distribution toward a dominant mode. The paper's contribution is to identify that this *unimodal optimization* is the root problem, not poor reward design or inefficient sampling.

**Test-time scaling research has focused on sequential depth, not parallel breadth.** The paper observes that existing RL algorithms "predominantly focus on sequential thinking, i.e., refining along a single reasoning path, while neglecting alternative branches, which limits their scaling potentials" (Section 2, "Test-Time Scaling"). Sequential scaling—step-by-step generation, self-reflection, verification—improves the quality of individual trajectories but does not increase the diversity of strategies the model can deploy across multiple attempts. Parallel scaling—sampling multiple candidates and aggregating via self-consistency or verifiers—depends on diversity for its effectiveness, but RL training that collapses diversity undermines this mechanism at its foundation.

**The specific failure mode: local optima and limited scalability.** The paper identifies two consequences of diversity collapse (Section 3.2). First, **exploitation over exploration**: by prematurely committing to a single strategy, the model may converge to a local optimum—a strategy that works well on average but fails on problems where different approaches are needed. The geometry problem in Figure 1 illustrates this: the RL model's equation-solving strategy works for many problems but fails on cases requiring spatial reasoning, while the base model can pivot to a validation strategy. Second, **poor scalability**: because the model's reasoning is concentrated in a narrow region of solution space (as visualized in Figure 4's t-SNE projections), it cannot cover the full spectrum of problem types. The embedding analysis in Figure 4 is particularly revealing—in failure cases, "RL reasoning embeddings are more densely clustered" and "their narrow embedding region fails to cover any correct trajectory," while base models, "benefiting from a wider and more diverse reasoning space, are more likely to retrieve correct solutions from alternative regions."

### How This Paper Positions Itself

**A diagnostic contribution first, then a solution.** Unlike prior work that proposes new RL variants without analyzing *why* existing methods have specific limitations, this paper follows a diagnosis-then-treatment structure. Sections 3.1 and 3.2 are entirely diagnostic: they establish that RL models lose diversity, that this diversity matters for performance, and that GRPO's training dynamics cause premature convergence. Only after this foundation does Section 4 introduce MUPO as a targeted intervention designed specifically to address the identified mechanism of diversity collapse.

**MUPO as a structural modification, not a reward engineering approach.** The paper's solution is architectural rather than incentive-based. Rather than adding entropy bonuses or exploration rewards to the existing GRPO objective—which prior work has attempted with limited success—MUPO fundamentally restructures how advantages are computed. By partitioning responses into groups and performing localized advantage estimation within each group (Equation 3), the objective creates separate optimization processes for each reasoning mode. The diversity reward (Equations 5–6) then provides an inter-group signal to maintain separation, but the primary mechanism for preserving diversity is the group structure itself, not the reward. This is a key design choice: the paper is not trying to tweak GRPO's incentives to encourage exploration within a single distribution, but rather to maintain multiple distributions simultaneously.

**Connecting to the exploration-exploitation problem in RL.** The paper implicitly frames GRPO's deficiency as a premature commitment to exploitation before adequate exploration has occurred. The diversity reward annealing schedule (Equation 7)—where λ starts at 0.4 and decays to 0.1 following a cosine schedule—explicitly encodes an explore-then-exploit curriculum. Early training emphasizes inter-group separation (exploration of diverse strategies); later training reduces diversity pressure and allows the model to refine the most promising strategies (exploitation). This is a principled approach to the exploration-exploitation tradeoff that has been studied extensively in classical RL but, the paper argues, has been underexamined in the context of VLM fine-tuning with GRPO.

**Bridging sequential and parallel thinking in a single training process.** The paper explicitly positions MUPO as integrating "parallel thinking into RL" (Section 2, "Test-Time Scaling"), addressing the gap between sequential scaling (which RL models do well) and parallel scaling (which base models do well but RL models lose). By training the model to maintain multiple diverse strategies simultaneously, MUPO aims to produce a model that can both reason deeply along individual paths (sequential capability, inherited from RL optimization within each group) and deploy varied strategies across multiple attempts (parallel capability, enabled by inter-group diversity). This integrated capability is what yields the substantial acc@4 improvements observed in Tables 1–3: the model is not just more accurate on single attempts but can leverage test-time compute more effectively through diverse sampling.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper proposes a training algorithm—Multi-Group Policy Optimization (MUPO)—that replaces the standard GRPO objective to train vision-language models that can reason both deeply along individual solution paths *and* broadly across diverse strategic approaches. The core problem it solves is that GRPO-trained VLMs prematurely converge to a narrow set of reasoning strategies during RL fine-tuning, losing the divergent thinking capabilities present in their base models; MUPO intervenes structurally by partitioning sampled responses into groups, computing advantages locally within each group to preserve multiple reasoning modes, and adding an inter-group diversity reward to maintain separation between those modes, producing a model that explores diverse strategies early in training and gradually exploits the most promising ones.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The MUPO training pipeline has five interacting components:

1. **Policy Model (πθ):** A VLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B) that generates reasoning trajectories given a visual input and a question. This is the model being trained—it is initialized from a base VLM and progressively updated through the MUPO objective.

2. **Response Rollout Generator:** At each training step, the current policy samples `$N$` complete reasoning-to-answer trajectories per training example (with `$N = 15$` in the paper's configuration), using a temperature of 1.0 to maintain stochasticity. These `$N$` responses form the raw material for both group formation and advantage estimation.

3. **Group Partitioning Module:** The `$N$` responses are embedded (using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B on the reasoning segments), and constrained clustering partitions them into `$K$` groups (`$K = 3$` in the default configuration, with a minimum group size `$G_{\text{min}} = 3$`), where each group captures responses with similar reasoning trajectories. This is the structural mechanism that prevents diversity collapse—each group becomes a distinct reasoning mode.

4. **Multi-Group Advantage Estimator:** Within each group, localized advantages are computed by normalizing rewards relative to the group mean rather than the global batch mean (as GRPO does). A diversity reward is added that measures the cosine distance between a trajectory's embedding and embeddings from all other groups, scaled by an annealing weight λ that starts at 0.4 and decays to 0.1. The final per-group objective is weighted by a load-balance scaler `$w_k$` to prevent larger groups from dominating optimization.

5. **Policy Optimizer:** The MUPO objective (Equation 3) is a composition of `$K$` GRPO-like sub-objectives, each operating on one group and computing clipped importance-sampled policy gradients. The optimizer updates the policy model parameters θ to maximize this composite objective, with the KL constraint optionally applied toward a reference policy.

**Information flow in one training step:** (1) The policy model samples `$N$` responses per training example → (2) The embedding model encodes reasoning segments and constrained clustering assigns each response to one of `$K$` groups → (3) A verifier computes accuracy and format rewards for each response → (4) The diversity module computes per-trajectory diversity rewards as average cosine distances to responses in other groups → (5) Per-group advantages are computed as normalized rewards (accuracy + format + diversity) relative to each group's mean → (6) The MUPO loss is computed as a weighted sum of per-group GRPO losses, backpropagated, and used to update the policy parameters.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the standard GRPO objective (Equation 1–2)—what it optimizes, how advantages are computed, and why this mechanism structurally predisposes the model to diversity collapse—since MUPO is defined as a modification of this foundation.

- **Second**, the group partitioning mechanism—how responses are embedded, clustered, and assigned to groups, including the constrained clustering constraint—because this is the architectural intervention that creates separate optimization spaces for different reasoning strategies.

- **Third**, the MUPO objective (Equation 3)—how the multi-group loss is constructed as a composition of per-group GRPO sub-objectives with load-balance weighting, how localized advantage estimation (Equation 4) prevents inter-group interference, and why the `$w_k$` scaler (with exponent β) is necessary.

- **Fourth**, the diversity reward (Equations 5–6)—its computation as average inter-group cosine distance, the accuracy-gating condition that prevents reward hacking, and the cosine annealing schedule (Equation 7) that controls the exploration-to-exploitation transition—since this is the incentive mechanism that actively promotes strategic divergence.

- **Fifth**, the training configuration and implementation details that make the system work, including dataset (ViRL39K), hyperparameters (`$N = 15, K = 3, G_{\text{min}} = 3, β = 1, λ_{\text{max}} = 0.4, λ_{\text{min}} = 0.1$`), and the overall optimization loop.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that GRPO's global advantage estimation causes premature mode collapse during VLM reasoning training, and that splitting the batch into separately-optimized groups with an inter-group diversity incentive preserves the divergent thinking that enables test-time scaling.

#### The GRPO Baseline: Why Global Advantage Estimation Causes Collapse

Before explaining MUPO, we must understand the mechanism it replaces. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is a proximal policy optimization variant designed for language model fine-tuning where the reward signal is sparse and easily verifiable (e.g., final-answer correctness, format compliance). The objective is:

$$J_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,y,I) \sim \mathcal{D}, \{o_i\}_{i=1}^{|G|} \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|q,I)} \left[ \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{i=1}^{|G|} \min\left( r_i(\theta) \hat{A}_i, \operatorname{clip}(r_i(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \hat{A}_i \right) \right]$$

with the importance sampling ratio defined as:

$$r_i(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_i \mid q, I)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_i \mid q, I)}$$

where `$\mathcal{D}$` is the training dataset (each example containing question `$q$`, gold answer `$y$`, and visual input `$I$`), `$G$` is the set of `$|G|$` sampled trajectories from the old policy `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$`, `$\epsilon$` controls the trust-region clipping (preventing the new policy from deviating too far from the old one), and `$r_i(\theta)$` is the per-trajectory importance weight that corrects for the distribution shift between the old and current policies.

**What the GRPO objective computes:** For each training example, the current policy's behavior is compared to the old policy's behavior through the ratio `$r_i(\theta)$`. When `$\hat{A}_i > 0$` (the trajectory is better than average), the objective increases the probability of that trajectory, but the clipping at `$1 + \epsilon$` prevents the ratio from exceeding this bound, ensuring the update is conservative. When `$\hat{A}_i < 0$` (the trajectory is worse than average), the objective decreases its probability, with clipping at `$1 - \epsilon$` preventing excessive suppression. The `$\min$` operation ensures that when the ratio and advantage have opposite signs (e.g., a bad trajectory becomes more probable under the new policy), the objective uses the clipped version, preventing destructive updates. The result is a scalar loss that, when minimized (or the objective maximized), moves the policy toward generating responses that score higher than the batch average.

**Why this form:** The clipping mechanism is inherited from PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) and serves as an implicit trust region—it prevents the new policy from diverging too rapidly from the old policy, which is essential for stable on-policy training with limited samples. Without clipping, a single high-advantage trajectory could cause an unbounded policy update, potentially collapsing the model onto that trajectory. With clipping, even very high advantages produce bounded gradients. This is standard practice in RL fine-tuning of language models.

The token-level advantage `$\hat{A}_i$` is derived from sequence-level rewards by broadcasting:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{R_i - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{R})}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf{R})}, \quad i = 1, \dots, |G|$$

where `$\mathbf{R} = \{R_1, R_2, \dots, R_{|G|}\}$` is the vector of scalar rewards for all trajectories in the batch, `$\operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{R})$` is the batch-averaged reward, and `$\operatorname{std}(\mathbf{R})$` is the batch standard deviation.

**What it computes:** For each trajectory `$i$`, its reward `$R_i$` is centered by subtracting the batch mean and scaled by the batch standard deviation, yielding a z-score normalized advantage. If `$R_i$` is above the batch mean, `$\hat{A}_i$` is positive; if below, negative. This normalized advantage is then assigned to every token position in trajectory `$i$`, meaning all tokens in a good response get positive advantage (reinforced) and all tokens in a bad response get negative advantage (penalized). The result is a per-token scalar that drives the policy gradient.

**Why this form causes diversity collapse:** The normalization is global—it compares each trajectory against the entire batch. This creates a winner-take-all dynamic. In the early training steps, when the policy still generates diverse outputs, one strategy will (by random chance or slight initialization bias) achieve a slightly higher average reward. This strategy gets positive advantages; others get negative advantages. The policy update shifts probability mass toward the winning strategy and away from alternatives. In the next step, the winning strategy is even more likely to be sampled, its advantage grows, and alternatives are further suppressed. This positive feedback loop—inherent to any global relative advantage scheme—produces the rapid diversity collapse documented in Figure 3 (A), where diversity drops "sharply during the early training stage, i.e., first 20 steps." Standard GRPO has no mechanism to resist this collapse because it treats all responses as competing for the same probability mass in a single policy distribution.

A common reward design for reasoning tasks uses verifiable rewards:

$$R_i = R_{\text{acc}} + R_{\text{fmt}}$$

where `$R_{\text{acc}}$` is a binary accuracy reward (1 if the final answer matches the gold answer, 0 otherwise) and `$R_{\text{fmt}}$` is a format consistency reward (e.g., 1 if the response follows the expected reasoning structure, 0 otherwise). The KL constraint toward a reference policy is optional but commonly used to prevent the model from drifting too far from its pretrained behavior.

#### Group Partitioning: Creating Separate Optimization Spaces

The key architectural innovation of MUPO is that it does not compute a single GRPO objective over all `$N$` responses—it partitions them into `$K$` groups and computes independent GRPO objectives per group. The partitioning mechanism determines which responses belong to which group and therefore which strategies are optimized independently versus competing with each other.

**Embedding and clustering procedure.** For each of the `$N$` sampled responses, the reasoning segment is extracted (the portion of the trajectory between the think-start and think-end markers or equivalent delimiters) and encoded using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, producing a dense vector representation. The paper uses constrained k-means clustering (Bradley et al., 2000) to assign these embeddings to `$K$` clusters subject to a minimum cluster size constraint `$G_{\text{min}}$`. The constraint ensures that each group contains at least `$G_{\text{min}} = 3$` responses, which is necessary for reliable advantage estimation within the group—with fewer than 3 responses, the mean and standard deviation in Equation 4 become unstable.

**What the clustering achieves:** Responses with similar reasoning trajectories (similar embeddings) land in the same group; responses with different strategic approaches land in different groups. In the paper's qualitative examples, one group might contain responses that use equation-solving, another responses that use validation-based approaches, and a third that uses counting-by-exclusion. The clustering is purely based on the embedding space—no ground-truth labels about strategy type are used—so it automatically discovers the natural strategic modes present in the batch of sampled responses.

**Why constrained k-means rather than unconstrained clustering:** Without the `$G_{\text{min}}$` constraint, standard k-means could produce degenerate groups with 0 or 1 response, rendering within-group advantage estimation meaningless. The constraint ensures all groups are viable optimization units. Additionally, constrained clustering prevents load-balance failures where most responses collapse into one cluster and the remaining clusters get only outliers, which would defeat the purpose of maintaining multiple modes.

**Why clustering rather than random assignment:** Random group assignment would mix strategies within each group, meaning each group would contain a representative sample of all strategies rather than a distinct mode. The localized advantage estimation (Equation 4) would then be computed over a heterogeneous set where no single strategy dominates, and the group-level optimization would not preserve any particular mode. Clustering by embedding similarity is essential because it makes each group a *coherent strategic unit*—all responses in a group are variations on the same theme, so optimizing within the group refines that theme rather than blending disparate approaches.

#### The MUPO Objective: Composition of Independent GRPO Sub-Objectives

Given the partitioned groups `$\{G_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$`, MUPO defines its overall objective as:

$$J_{\text{MUPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(q,y,I) \sim \mathcal{D}, \{o_i\}_{i=1}^{N} \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|q,I)} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{w_k}{|G_k|} \sum_{i=1}^{|G_k|} \min\left( r_i(\theta) \hat{A}^k_i, \operatorname{clip}(r_i(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \hat{A}^k_i \right) \right]$$

with the per-group importance ratio and load-balance weight defined as:

$$r_i(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(o_i^k \mid q, I)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_i^k \mid q, I)}, \quad w_k = \left( \frac{N}{K |G_k|} \right)^\beta$$

where `$o_i^k$` is the `$i$`-th response in group `$k$`, `$|G_k|$` is the size of group `$k$`, `$N$` is the total number of responses, `$K$` is the number of groups, and `$\beta$` is the load-balance sensitivity exponent (set to `$\beta = 1$` in the default configuration).

**What the MUPO objective computes:** The objective is a sum of `$K$` independent GRPO sub-objectives, each operating on a single group's responses. For each group, the inner term is identical to the standard GRPO clipped surrogate loss (Equation 1), but using group-local advantages `$\hat{A}^k_i$` rather than global advantages. The per-group contribution is weighted by `$w_k$`, which adjusts for group size. The total objective is the expectation over training examples and rollout batches, meaning the optimizer tries to simultaneously improve performance within all groups (each corresponding to a different reasoning strategy) rather than collapsing all probability mass into a single mode.

**Why this form—the structural defense against collapse:** In GRPO, a single strategy that outperforms others gets positive advantage and all others get negative advantage, creating a strong gradient to suppress alternatives. In MUPO, because advantages are computed within each group separately (detailed below), a response only competes with other responses *in the same group* that use the same strategy. Responses in different groups do not directly compete—a good equation-solving response in group 1 and a good validation response in group 2 can both receive positive within-group advantages and be simultaneously reinforced. The group structure decouples the optimization of different strategies, allowing the model to maintain a multimodal policy distribution where each mode corresponds to a distinct reasoning approach.

**The load-balance weight `$w_k$`:** If groups vary substantially in size (e.g., one group contains 8 responses and another contains 2), the larger group's objective would naturally dominate the gradient by sheer volume of samples, even with localized advantages. The weight `$w_k = (N / (K |G_k|))^\beta$` counteracts this: when `$|G_k|$` is large (the group is over-represented), `$w_k$` is small, down-weighting its contribution; when `$|G_k|$` is small (under-represented), `$w_k$` is large, up-weighting its contribution. With `$\beta = 1$`, the weighting is inversely proportional to group size, achieving exact load balancing. With `$\beta > 1$`, smaller groups are over-weighted; with `$\beta < 1$`, the balancing is more conservative. The paper uses `$\beta = 1$` as the default.

**Why load balancing matters:** Without it, the optimization process would create a self-reinforcing cycle: larger groups get stronger gradients → the policy shifts toward those strategies → those strategies are sampled more frequently → groups become even larger. This would reproduce the concentration problem MUPO is designed to prevent, just at the group level rather than the strategy level. Load balancing ensures that all discovered strategic modes receive comparable optimization pressure, maintaining the multimodality of the policy.

---

#### Localized Advantage Estimation: Independent Mode Refinement

The per-group advantage `$\hat{A}^k_i$` is computed as:

$$\hat{A}^k_i = \frac{R^k_i - \operatorname{mean}(\mathbf{R})}{\operatorname{std}(\mathbf{R})}, \quad i = 1, \dots, |G_k|$$

where `$R^k_i$` is the total reward for the `$i$`-th response in group `$k$`, and `$\mathbf{R}$` is the vector of rewards for all `$N$` responses across all groups (not just group `$k$`).

**What it computes:** This appears similar to GRPO's advantage formula, but there is a critical difference in interpretation. While the mean and standard deviation are computed over the full batch `$\mathbf{R}$` (all `$N$` responses), the result `$\hat{A}^k_i$` is used only for responses within group `$k$`. A response in group `$k$` is compared to the *global* average, but the gradient only updates the policy for responses in that group. This means that if group `$k$` contains consistently lower-reward strategies than the global average, its responses will have negative advantages—but the negative gradient only affects the probability of those specific strategies, not all strategies simultaneously.

**Why this form—the localized advantage property:** In practice, using the global mean as the baseline is important because it preserves a signal about *which groups are better than others overall*. If a particular strategy (group) consistently underperforms, its responses will have negative advantages on average, creating a gentle pressure to reduce that strategy's probability over time while still maintaining it as a distinct mode (since within the group, the best responses still get relatively higher advantages). If the mean were computed per-group (purely localized), all groups would have zero-mean advantages and there would be no signal about which strategies are globally superior—the model would maintain all strategies with equal probability regardless of quality. Using the global mean with per-group gradient application strikes a balance: within-group competition refines each strategy, while cross-group quality signals gradually shift probability mass toward better-performing groups.

**Comparison to standard GRPO:** In GRPO, the global mean and per-response gradient application are coupled: a response below the global mean gets suppressed, regardless of whether it represents a viable alternative strategy. In MUPO, the global mean still provides the baseline, but the suppression gradient is applied only to responses within the same strategic group. This means a suboptimal strategy is refined rather than eliminated—the policy learns to execute that strategy better rather than abandoning it completely.

#### Diversity Reward: Incentivizing Strategic Separation

The structural mechanism (grouping + localized advantages) prevents groups from collapsing into each other, but it does not actively encourage them to be *different*. Without an explicit diversity incentive, groups could drift toward similar strategies over training, especially if those strategies yield similar rewards. The diversity reward addresses this.

**Computation per trajectory.** For a specific response `$o_i^k$` in group `$G_k$`, its diversity reward is:

$$R_{\text{div}} = \frac{1}{N - |G_k|} \sum_{\substack{m=1 \\ m \neq k}}^{K} \sum_{j=1}^{|G_m|} d(o_i^k, o_j^m)$$

where `$d(o_i^k, o_j^m)$` is the cosine distance between the reasoning embeddings of response `$o_i^k$` and response `$o_j^m$` from a different group `$G_m$`, `$N$` is the total number of responses, and `$|G_k|$` is the size of the current group.

**What it computes:** For each response, compute its average cosine distance to all responses in *all other groups*. Cosine distance is defined as `$1 - \text{cosine similarity}$`, so it ranges from 0 (identical embeddings) to 2 (maximally different embeddings). The average is taken over `$N - |G_k|$` response pairs (all responses not in group `$k$`). The result is a scalar per trajectory. If the trajectory's reasoning is similar to reasoning in other groups, the diversity reward is low; if it is very different, the diversity reward is high.

**Why cosine distance rather than Euclidean:** Cosine distance measures angular separation rather than magnitude difference, which is appropriate for reasoning embeddings where the "direction" of the reasoning matters more than its "length" (which may reflect verbosity or confidence rather than strategic content). Additionally, common embedding models are typically trained with cosine-similarity-based objectives, making cosine distance the natural metric in their induced space.

**Final per-response reward.** The diversity reward is combined with accuracy and format rewards through an accuracy-gated formula:

$$R_i^k = R_{\text{acc}} + R_{\text{fmt}} + \lambda \cdot \mathbb{1}[R_{\text{acc}} = 1] \cdot R_{\text{div}}$$

where `$R_{\text{acc}} \in \{0, 1\}$` is the binary accuracy reward, `$R_{\text{fmt}}$` is the format reward, `$\lambda$` is the diversity reward weight, and `$\mathbb{1}[R_{\text{acc}} = 1]$` is an indicator that gates the diversity reward—it is only added when the response is already correct.

**Why the accuracy gate:** Without this gate, the model could "reward hack" by generating diverse but incorrect responses. Since `$R_{\text{div}}$` is always non-negative, the model would have an incentive to maximize inter-group distance irrespective of correctness, potentially producing wild and wrong strategies just to score diversity points. The gating term `$\mathbb{1}[R_{\text{acc}} = 1]$` ensures that diversity is only rewarded when the response is already correct, creating a compound incentive: the model must both be correct *and* be strategically distinct from other groups. This aligns the diversity objective with the primary accuracy objective—the model explores diverse *correct* strategies, not diverse *any* strategies.

**Why additive rather than multiplicative:** An alternative design would multiply accuracy and diversity: `$R = R_{\text{acc}} \cdot (1 + \lambda R_{\text{div}})$`. The additive design with gating separates the concerns: accuracy provides a binary threshold (you must be correct to get any diversity bonus), and diversity provides a continuous bonus on top. The additive form makes the diversity bonus directly interpretable as an additional reward component whose magnitude is controlled by λ, which is important for the annealing schedule described next.

---

#### Diversity Reward Annealing: From Exploration to Exploitation

The diversity weight `$\lambda$` is not constant throughout training—it follows a cosine annealing schedule:

$$\lambda = \lambda_{\min} + \frac{\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}}{2} \left( 1 + \cos\left( \frac{\pi \cdot t_{\text{cur}}}{t_{\max}} \right) \right)$$

where `$t_{\text{cur}}$` is the current training step, `$t_{\max}$` is the total number of training steps, `$\lambda_{\max} = 0.4$` is the initial diversity weight, and `$\lambda_{\min} = 0.1$` is the final diversity weight.

**What it computes:** The schedule smoothly interpolates between `$\lambda_{\max}$` at `$t_{\text{cur}} = 0$` and `$\lambda_{\min}$` at `$t_{\text{cur}} = t_{\max}$`, following a cosine curve. At the start of training, `$\lambda$` is at its maximum (0.4), meaning diversity contributes strongly to the reward. By the end of training, `$\lambda$` has decayed to 0.1, meaning diversity contributes less and accuracy dominates. The cosine shape ensures the decay is smooth (no abrupt drops) and the rate of decay is slowest at the beginning and end, with the fastest decay in the middle.

**Why cosine annealing rather than linear decay:** Cosine annealing is standard in learning rate scheduling because it provides a gentle start (preserving the initial exploration-heavy regime for longer) and a gentle end (allowing fine-grained convergence in the final exploitation phase). A linear decay would rush through the middle of training, potentially switching from exploration to exploitation too abruptly. The cosine shape spends proportionally more time in the high-λ and low-λ regimes, matching the intuition that early exploration and late exploitation should both have extended phases.

**Why this particular range (0.4 to 0.1):** The ablation study in Figure 9 validates these choices. When `$\lambda_{\max}$` is too high (>0.4), the diversity reward dominates at the expense of accuracy—the model becomes too exploratory and fails to converge to high-quality strategies. When `$\lambda_{\max}$` is too low (<0.3), exploration is insufficient, increasing the risk of convergence to local optima (similar to standard GRPO). Similarly, `$\lambda_{\min}$` too high (>0.1) means diversity pressure never sufficiently relaxes in late training, preventing the model from fully committing to the best strategies; `$\lambda_{\min}$` too low (near 0) means diversity is entirely abandoned in late training, risking mode collapse in the final stages.

**The exploration-exploitation curriculum:** This annealing schedule implements an explicit explore-then-exploit curriculum. In early training (steps 0–~70 in Figure 8), high λ encourages the model to spread its responses across diverse groups, actively searching the space of possible reasoning strategies. In middle training (~70–210 steps), decaying λ gradually shifts emphasis toward accuracy, causing the model to start preferring strategies that more consistently produce correct answers. In late training (~210–350 steps), low λ allows the model to fully exploit the most promising strategies it has discovered, refining them to high precision. Figure 8 confirms this dynamic: the diversity reward "exhibits a distinct rise-fall-plateau trend"—rising as groups separate (exploration), falling as λ decays (transition), and plateauing as strategies stabilize (exploitation).

---

#### Training Configuration and Implementation

**Dataset and base models.** Training uses ViRL39K, described as "a high-quality and comprehensive dataset," for 2 epochs. The base models are Qwen2.5-VL at 3B and 7B parameter scales. The learning rate is `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`. For ablation studies, a random subset of ViRL39K is used for computational efficiency.

**Rollout configuration.** At each training step, `$N = 15$` responses are sampled per training example with a sampling temperature of 1.0. The temperature of 1.0 is important because lower temperatures would reduce diversity in the raw samples, making group separation harder and potentially undermining the clustering mechanism. The relatively high number of samples (`$N = 15$`) provides enough data for reliable clustering into `$K = 3$` groups with a minimum size of `$G_{\text{min}} = 3$`.

**MUPO-specific hyperparameters.** The number of groups `$K = 3$` (with sensitivity analysis in Table 4 showing this is optimal on average, though mathematical benchmarks prefer larger `$K$` and general benchmarks prefer smaller `$K$`). The minimum group size `$G_{\text{min}} = 3$` ensures each group has at least 3 responses for stable mean and standard deviation computation in the localized advantage formula. The load-balance exponent `$\beta = 1$` provides inverse-proportional weighting. The diversity reward bounds are `$\lambda_{\max} = 0.4$` and `$\lambda_{\min} = 0.1$`.

**Optimization loop.** At each step: (1) Sample 15 responses per example from the current policy at temperature 1.0; (2) Compute accuracy and format rewards via the verifier; (3) Embed reasoning segments using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B; (4) Apply constrained k-means to partition into 3 groups with minimum size 3; (5) Compute per-response diversity rewards as average inter-group cosine distances; (6) Assemble final rewards via Equation 6 with current λ; (7) Compute per-group advantages via Equation 4; (8) Compute the MUPO objective via Equation 3; (9) Update policy parameters via gradient ascent on the objective (optionally with KL constraint). The diversity weight λ is updated each step via Equation 7, following the cosine schedule from 0.4 to 0.1 over the full training duration.

**Why 2 epochs on ViRL39K:** The paper reports that GRPO causes diversity collapse within "the first 20 steps, where the model has been exposed to only limited training data." Two epochs provides sufficient exposure for the model to both explore diverse strategies (early training with high λ) and refine the best ones (late training with low λ). The relatively short training (compared to pretraining scale) reflects the efficiency of RL fine-tuning—substantial behavioral changes occur with modest data exposure, but this also means the diversity collapse problem manifests quickly and requires architectural intervention from the start.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing GRPO's Success as a Trade-off—Depth for Breadth—Rather Than a Universal Improvement

The paper's most fundamental intellectual contribution is not the observation that GRPO-trained models perform well on single attempts—that was already known from the proliferation of reasoning VLMs achieving state-of-the-art acc@1 scores. Rather, it is the **reframing of that success as a behavioral trade-off**: GRPO improves single-trajectory quality (depth) at the cost of strategic diversity (breadth), and this trade-off has concrete, measurable consequences for how models perform under test-time scaling. This reframing matters because it converts a unidimensional "better/worse" narrative into a multidimensional characterization where the same training procedure is simultaneously beneficial (for greedy decoding) and harmful (for parallel sampling).

**What the field assumed before this work:** The rapid adoption of GRPO for VLM reasoning—evidenced by the proliferation of models cited in Section 2 (Vision-R1, R1-OneVision, VLAA-Thinker, LMM-R1, VLM-R1)—implicitly treated GRPO's improvements as uniformly positive. If a model achieves higher acc@1, it is understood as a better reasoner. The paper challenges this directly: "Do RL models truly outperform their base counterparts?" (Section 1). Prior work, when it reported acc@1 improvements, did not systematically investigate whether those gains came at the expense of parallel scaling capability.

**The evidence that forces the reframing:** Figure 2 (A) and (B) show a pattern that cannot be explained by a unidimensional quality improvement. At the 7B scale, the gap between RL and base models is large at acc@1 but dramatically narrows at acc@4. If RL models were uniformly better, the gap should persist or grow with more samples—better models should benefit more from additional attempts. Instead, the gap shrinks because "base models succeed in solving substantially more problems, while the gains of RL models remain marginal" (Section 3.1). This is an interaction effect: the benefit of RL training *depends on* whether you're measuring single-attempt quality or multiple-attempt coverage. A model that is "better" in one regime can be "worse" in another.

**Why this is more than an empirical observation:** The reframing introduces a new axis of evaluation—strategic coverage—that was previously invisible in standard benchmarks reporting only acc@1. It implies that the VLM reasoning field has been systematically underestimating base models and overestimating the net benefit of RL by using a metric (single-sample accuracy) that captures only half the picture. This is a **diagnostic contribution**: it identifies a blind spot in evaluation methodology, not just a limitation of a particular algorithm. The implication extends beyond GRPO to any training procedure that might trade diversity for quality, making the paper's framework applicable to evaluating future RL variants and fine-tuning approaches.

**The conceptual parallel:** The paper's framing echoes the exploration-exploitation trade-off studied extensively in classical reinforcement learning but largely neglected in the VLM fine-tuning literature, where the community has focused on reward design, data quality, and training stability. By recasting GRPO's limitation as premature exploitation (Section 3.2, "Exploitation over exploration"), the paper connects a practical problem in VLM training to a foundational concept, suggesting that solutions from the broader RL literature—such as diversity-preserving objectives—may be applicable.

### Innovation 2: Diagnosing Diversity Collapse as a Structural Property of Global Advantage Estimation, Not a Reward Design Issue

The paper's second key contribution is identifying **why** GRPO loses diversity—and the answer is more fundamental than prior work assumed. The diagnosis (Section 3.2, Figure 3) is that diversity collapse arises from the *structure* of GRPO's advantage computation (global normalization across all responses), not from deficiencies in reward design, data composition, or hyperparameter choices. This is a **mechanism-level diagnostic** that redirects improvement efforts away from reward engineering and toward architectural modifications of the optimization objective.

**What the field assumed before:** Prior work on improving GRPO—DAPO's sampling prioritization, GVPO's variance-based optimization, entropy-based exploration bonuses, perception-aware reward design (all cited in Section 2)—implicitly assumed that GRPO's limitations could be addressed by improving the quality of the reward signal or the efficiency of sample utilization. These approaches treat the GRPO objective as fundamentally sound and focus on feeding it better information. The paper's training dynamics analysis shows this assumption is incorrect: the collapse happens so early ("first 20 steps") that the model hasn't seen enough data for reward quality to matter—the structural pressure toward mode collapse operates faster than reward-based corrections can counteract.

**The evidence for structural causation:** Figure 3 (A) is the key piece of evidence. Diversity drops "sharply during the early training stage, i.e., first 20 steps, where the model has been exposed to only limited training data." This timing is diagnostic: if the collapse were caused by the training data favoring certain strategies, it would happen gradually as the model encounters more examples. If it were caused by reward misspecification, it would depend on the specific reward function. Instead, the near-immediate collapse suggests that *any* global relative advantage scheme will produce this effect regardless of data or reward quality, because comparing all responses against a shared baseline creates an inherent winner-take-all dynamic that extinguishes minority strategies before they can demonstrate their value.

**Why this diagnosis changes what solutions are plausible:** If diversity collapse is structural, then reward-level interventions (entropy bonuses, exploration rewards, better verifiers) are treating a symptom while leaving the underlying mechanism intact. The model might explore slightly more within the dominant strategy but will still collapse to a single mode. This explains why prior entropy-based approaches were insufficient—they "fail to foster reasoning across divergent strategies" (Section 2) because they encourage the model to be less confident, not to develop distinct strategic approaches. The diagnosis implies that the solution must be **architectural**: the advantage computation itself must be restructured to prevent inter-strategy competition. MUPO's group partitioning is the logical consequence of this diagnosis—it doesn't try to make global advantage estimation work better; it replaces it with localized estimation that structurally prevents collapse.

**The negative-result contribution:** The paper's identification of what *doesn't* work is as valuable as its proposal of what does. By showing that diversity collapses within 20 steps regardless of other design choices, the paper establishes a **boundary condition** for GRPO variants: any method that retains global advantage normalization will face this collapse pressure, and the only question is how severe it will be, not whether it will occur. This is a falsifiable claim that can guide future research—if someone proposes a new GRPO variant, the test is whether diversity still collapses in the first 20 steps, not whether the final accuracy is higher.

### Innovation 3: Group Partitioning as a Diversity-Preserving Mechanism—The Idea That Strategy Types Can Be Discovered and Maintained Through Embedding-Based Clustering During Training

The third conceptual contribution is the idea that **reasoning strategies can be automatically identified and maintained as distinct optimization targets during RL training using embedding-based clustering**, without any explicit strategy labels, curriculum design, or architectural changes to the model. This is a fundamentally different approach to encouraging diversity than prior work, which either added exploration incentives to a single policy or used separate models/heads for different strategies.

**What the field did before:** Prior approaches to encouraging diversity in language model fine-tuning fall into three categories, none of which achieve what MUPO does. (1) Entropy regularization adds a bonus proportional to the policy's entropy, but this encourages uniform token-level uncertainty, not distinct high-level strategies—a model can have high entropy by being randomly noisy while still following the same overall approach. (2) Separate reward model ensembles or multi-head architectures require pre-specifying the number of strategies or training separate components, adding complexity and requiring prior knowledge about strategy types. (3) Prompting-based diversity (e.g., "generate solutions using different approaches") shifts the burden to the inference-time prompt and doesn't train the model to internalize diverse strategies. MUPO's innovation is that strategy discovery and maintenance happen **automatically during training**: the clustering discovers what strategies exist in the current batch, the grouped optimization refines each strategy independently, and the diversity reward prevents them from merging—all without human annotation of strategy types.

**Why the clustering mechanism is conceptually novel:** Standard RL treats the batch of sampled trajectories as exchangeable—each trajectory is an independent draw from the same policy, and the objective is to shift the policy's mean toward higher-reward regions. MUPO introduces a **batch-internal structure** that was previously invisible: trajectories are not exchangeable because they represent different strategic approaches, and treating them as such (as GRPO does) destroys valuable information about strategic diversity. The clustering makes this structure explicit and uses it to create separate optimization channels. This is a form of **automatic curriculum discovery**: the model discovers, through its own sampling, what different strategies look like, and then learns to execute all of them well rather than committing to one.

**The significance beyond VLM reasoning:** The idea that embedding-based clustering can discover and maintain behavioral modes during RL training is a general contribution that could apply to any domain where problems admit multiple valid solution strategies—code generation (different algorithmic approaches), mathematical reasoning (different proof techniques), planning (different sequences of actions), or even dialogue (different conversational strategies). The paper provides a concrete instantiation for VLM reasoning, but the conceptual framework—partition sampled trajectories by embedding similarity, optimize each partition independently, incentivize inter-partition separation—is domain-agnostic.

**Evidence that the mechanism works as intended:** Figure 6 provides the crucial validation. The t-SNE projections show that MUPO produces "a broad, multimodal structure, with each mode corresponding to a distinct solution strategy," in contrast to GRPO's narrow, concentrated distribution. More importantly, in the failure case on the right side of Figure 6, "GRPO fails to handle the problem, while MUPO successfully learns the correct reasoning from alternative modes." This demonstrates that the clustering mechanism doesn't just preserve diversity as an end in itself—it preserves *useful* diversity that translates into solving problems the convergent model cannot solve.

### Innovation 4: The Accuracy-Gated Diversity Reward as a Principled Solution to the Reward Hacking Problem in Diversity-Incentivized RL

The fourth conceptual contribution is a specific design pattern for combining accuracy and diversity incentives that addresses a fundamental tension: **diversity without accuracy is useless, but accuracy incentives alone cause mode collapse**. The paper's accuracy-gated diversity reward (Equation 6) is a simple but non-obvious solution to this tension that avoids both the reward hacking of unconditional diversity bonuses and the mode collapse of pure accuracy optimization.

**The problem:** Any diversity incentive faces a reward hacking risk—if the model gets rewarded for being different, it will learn to be different in ways that maximize the reward signal without necessarily producing useful outputs. Unconditional diversity rewards (e.g., adding `$R_{\text{div}}$` to all trajectories regardless of correctness) would create a perverse incentive: the model could produce wildly varying but incorrect responses and get high diversity scores. This is the standard "specification gaming" problem documented throughout the RL literature, and it's particularly acute here because diversity and correctness can be inversely correlated—the most strategically diverse responses might be the ones that abandon sensible reasoning entirely.

**What prior work attempted:** Entropy-based exploration methods avoid this problem by not having an explicit diversity reward at all—they add exploration pressure at the token level, which doesn't create a direct incentive for strategic diversity and doesn't risk reward hacking but also "fail to foster reasoning across divergent strategies" (Section 2). Multi-agent or population-based approaches avoid the problem by having separate models explore different strategies, but this introduces training complexity and doesn't give a single model diverse capabilities.

**Why the accuracy gate solves the problem:** By gating the diversity reward on correctness (`$\mathbb{1}[R_{\text{acc}} = 1]$`), MUPO creates a **lexicographic incentive structure**: correctness is a prerequisite for diversity, not a competing objective. The model cannot sacrifice accuracy to get diversity points because incorrect responses receive zero diversity reward regardless of how different they are. This means the diversity incentive only operates in the region of strategy space that already produces correct answers, encouraging the model to find *multiple distinct ways to be correct* rather than *any way to be different*. The compound incentive is: be correct AND be strategically distinct from other correct responses in the batch.

**The significance—a reusable design pattern:** This accuracy-gated diversity structure is not specific to MUPO's group partitioning or VLM reasoning. It is a general pattern for any RL setting where you want to incentivize behavioral diversity while maintaining a quality threshold: gate the diversity bonus on a minimum quality criterion, and let the diversity incentive operate only within the set of acceptably good behaviors. This is conceptually analogous to constrained optimization approaches but implemented through a simple reward modification rather than a separate constraint solver. The pattern could be applied to code generation (reward diverse correct programs), mathematical proof generation (reward different valid proof strategies), or any domain with a binary or thresholded quality signal.

**Why the annealing adds a second layer of sophistication:** The gating prevents reward hacking, but without annealing, the model would maintain equal pressure for diversity throughout training, potentially preventing convergence to high-quality strategies. The cosine schedule on λ (Equation 7) adds a temporal dimension: early training says "explore diverse correct strategies strongly," while late training says "diversity is less important—focus on the best correct strategies." This prevents the model from maintaining strategic diversity at the expense of refinement quality in the final stages of training. The combination of gating (spatial—diversity only in the correct region) and annealing (temporal—diversity pressure decreases over time) creates a two-dimensional control over the exploration-exploitation curriculum. Figure 8 provides evidence that this works as intended: the diversity reward follows the intended rise-fall-plateau trajectory while accuracy steadily improves, indicating that the model transitions from exploration to exploitation without either phase dominating destructively.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on nine reasoning benchmarks spanning mathematical and general-purpose tasks. Mathematical benchmarks: MathVerse [54], MathVista [20], MathVision [39], LogicVista [44], WeMath [25], and Geometry3K [19]. General-purpose benchmarks: MMStar [5], HallusionBench [9], and MMVet [51]. The exact test split sizes are not specified, but these are standard public benchmarks with established evaluation protocols. Training is conducted on ViRL39K [38], a high-quality curated reasoning dataset, for 2 epochs.

- **Base model(s).** Experiments use Qwen2.5-VL [1] at two scales: 3B and 7B parameters. The 7B model serves as the primary evaluation scale, with 3B results demonstrating the method's effectiveness at a smaller parameter count. The paper also reports scores from proprietary models (GPT-5-Thinking [30], Gemini-2.5-Pro [8]) for reference only, not as training backbones.

- **Metrics.** Two primary metrics are used. acc@1: greedy decoding accuracy—the model generates a single most confident response per question, and accuracy is the fraction of questions answered correctly. acc@4: the fraction of questions for which at least one of four sampled responses (temperature 1.0) produces the correct answer—a measure of test-time scaling capability. For both metrics, correctness is determined by exact answer matching against gold answers using standard benchmark evaluation scripts. Additionally, **reasoning diversity** is quantified by encoding the reasoning segments of generated responses using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B [55], computing cosine distances between all pairs, and averaging to produce a scalar diversity score.

- **Baselines.** For the 7B scale, the paper compares against InternVL2.5-8B [6], R1-OneVision-7B [47], VLAA-Thinker-7B [4], Vision-R1-7B [13], VLM-R1-4B [29], and LMM-R1-4B [24], as well as the base Qwen2.5-VL-7B [1]. For the 3B scale, baselines include Qwen2.5-VL-3B [1], InternVL2.5-2B [6], VLM-R1-4B [29], VLAA-Thinker-3B [4], and LMM-R1-4B [24]. Proprietary models GPT-5-Thinking [30] and Gemini-2.5-Pro [8] are reported for reference. Baseline results are primarily cited from the corresponding papers, secondarily from the OpenCompass leaderboard, and reproduced when neither source is available (exact provenance per-benchmark is not specified).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not report a strict FLOPs budget. Instead, it measures test-time compute in terms of the number of sampled responses (k in acc@k). For the motivational experiments in Section 3.1, k = 1, 2, 4. For the main results, acc@1 uses greedy decoding (1 generation) and acc@4 uses 4 generations with temperature 1.0. Training compute: each GRPO or MUPO training step samples N = 15 responses per example from the current policy. The paper does not compare training compute costs between methods—MUPO's training overhead relative to GRPO (from embedding, clustering, and diversity reward computation) is not quantified.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** None is reported. The paper does not describe cross-validation, multiple random seeds, or statistical significance testing. Results in Tables 1–3 appear to be single-run evaluations. The ablation studies (Table 4, Figure 9) similarly report point estimates without error bars or confidence intervals. The absence of statistical rigor is a notable gap in the experimental design.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Motivational Experiments: RL vs. Base Model Behavior (Section 3.1)

The paper first establishes the behavioral divergence that motivates MUPO. These experiments are diagnostic, not evaluations of the proposed method.

**acc@k analysis (Figure 2, A and B).** At both 3B and 7B scales, RL-trained models (VLAA-Thinker, LMM-R1 for 3B; Vision-R1, R1-OneVision for 7B) outperform their base models on acc@1 by substantial margins. However, the performance gap narrows dramatically as k increases:

- At the 7B scale, the acc@1 gap between the best RL model (Vision-R1) and the base model (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) is roughly 9 percentage points when averaged across benchmarks (approximately 49% vs. 40%, estimated from Figure 2B's bar heights). At acc@4, the gap shrinks to roughly 2–3 percentage points. The paper quantifies this as base models "succeeding in solving substantially more problems, while the gains of RL models remain marginal" as k increases (Section 3.1).

- At the 3B scale, the pattern is similar. The acc@1 gap between RL and base models narrows at acc@4, though exact values are not tabulated in the main text. The paper states that "as k increases, a notable shift occurs: base models succeed in solving substantially more problems."

This interaction effect—where RL's advantage depends on k—is the paper's primary evidence that RL training trades breadth for depth.

**Diversity-accuracy correlation (Figure 2, C and D).** The paper plots reasoning diversity (pairwise average cosine distance among 4 sampled responses) against acc@4 for base models only. A regression line is fitted, and the paper reports "a strong positive correlation: as the reasoning diversity increases, acc@4 improves substantially." No correlation coefficient (Pearson's r or Spearman's ρ) is reported—the claim rests on visual inspection of the scatter plot. Each point represents a set of 4 responses to a single question; the x-axis is their average pairwise diversity, and the y-axis is whether the set contains at least one correct answer (binary). The regression line fitted to this binary outcome is presumably a logistic regression or a linear fit to binned data, but the methodology is not specified in the main text.

**Embedding space visualization (Figure 4).** The t-SNE projections of reasoning embeddings for base and RL models reveal structural differences. For a successful case (where both RL and base models produce correct answers), RL embeddings are "more densely clustered" while base embeddings are "more sparsely distributed." For a failure case (where RL models fail despite multiple attempts), "their narrow embedding region fails to cover any correct trajectory. In contrast, base models, benefiting from a wider and more diverse reasoning space, are more likely to retrieve correct solutions from alternative regions."

This visualization provides qualitative evidence for the mechanism but does not quantify the relationship between embedding spread and correctness probability.

#### Diversity Collapse During GRPO Training (Section 3.2)

**Training dynamics (Figure 3, A).** The paper trains Qwen2.5-VL-3B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B with standard GRPO on ViRL39K and records reasoning diversity on a validation set throughout training. The key finding: "reasoning diversity drops sharply during the early training stage, i.e., first 20 steps, where the model has been exposed to only limited training data." After this initial collapse, diversity remains at low levels (roughly 0.00 to 0.05 on the y-axis, based on Figure 3A's scale) for the remainder of training. The figure also shows MUPO's diversity curve, which "shows a much more gradual decline in contrast to [the] sharp collapse observed in GRPO." Exact diversity values at specific training steps are not tabulated.

**The significance of timing.** The paper emphasizes that 20 steps represents minimal data exposure ("the model has been exposed to only limited training data"), arguing this rules out data-driven explanations for collapse (e.g., certain strategies being genuinely superior on the training distribution). The collapse is attributed to the structural properties of GRPO's global advantage normalization rather than reward design or data composition.

**7B-scale mathematical benchmarks (Table 1).** MUPO-Thinker-7B achieves a 2.5 percentage point average acc@1 improvement over the previous best result (51.6% vs. 49.1%, where 49.1% is Vision-R1-7B's average across the six mathematical benchmarks). This is reported as "average gains of 2∼7%" in the abstract; the "2∼7%" range presumably reflects variation across individual benchmarks.

Benchmark-level breakdown from Table 1:
- MathVerse: 53.9% (MUPO) vs. 52.4% (Vision-R1), a 1.5 percentage point gain in acc@1
- MathVista: 77.9% vs. 73.5%, a 4.4 point gain
- MathVision: 31.3% vs. 28.2%, a 3.1 point gain
- LogicVista: 50.6% vs. 49.7%, a 0.9 point gain
- WeMath: 44.1% vs. 41.6%, a 2.5 point gain
- Geometry3K: 52.1% vs. 49.0%, a 3.1 point gain

The acc@4 improvements are substantially larger: a 6.0 percentage point average gain (58.8% vs. 52.8% for Vision-R1). Per-benchmark:
- MathVerse: 61.7% vs. 56.1%, a 5.6 point gain
- MathVista: 82.4% vs. 75.6%, a 6.8 point gain

The acc@4 gains are uniformly larger than the acc@1 gains, consistent with the paper's claim that MUPO "significantly [raises] the upper bound of their capabilities" by enabling effective test-time scaling.

**7B-scale general-purpose benchmarks (Table 2).** MUPO-Thinker-7B achieves a 2.3 percentage point average acc@1 improvement (65.6% vs. 63.3% for VLAA-Thinker-7B, the previous best). The acc@4 gain is 6.2 percentage points (72.4% vs. 66.2% for VLAA-Thinker-7B). Per-benchmark:

The acc@4 improvements are 5.9 and 5.9 percentage points, respectively. The paper notes that these gains "enable MUPO-Thinker-3B to attain performance comparable to several strong 7B baselines," though no specific cross-scale comparison table is provided—this claim presumably compares the 3B MUPO model's scores against 7B baseline scores in Tables 1 and 2.

**Comparison to base models at acc@4.** MUPO-Thinker-7B's acc@4 average of 58.8% on mathematical benchmarks substantially exceeds the base Qwen2.5-VL-7B's acc@4 of 56.5% (Table 1). This is important because it shows MUPO not only prevents diversity loss but actually improves upon the base model's parallel sampling capability while simultaneously adding RL-style depth (acc@1 of 51.6% vs. base model's 40.1%). On general-purpose benchmarks, MUPO's acc@4 of 72.4% exceeds the base model's 71.5% by a smaller margin of 0.9 points.

#### MUPO Training Dynamics (Figure 8)

**Accuracy and diversity reward curves.** The accuracy reward (presumably EMA-smoothed) shows steady improvement throughout training. The diversity reward exhibits a "rise-fall-plateau trend":
- Initial rise: diversity between groups increases as the model explores distinct strategies
- Decline: as λ decays via cosine annealing, diversity pressure reduces, causing some inter-group separation to decrease
- Plateau: diversity stabilizes, indicating the model has converged around a set of effective diverse strategies

The paper interprets this as evidence that MUPO achieves "a balanced trade-off between exploration and exploitation," distinguishing it from GRPO's monotonic diversity collapse. Exact reward values at specific training steps are not tabulated, but the curves span 350 training steps.

**Validation set diversity (Figure 3A).** The MUPO diversity curve in Figure 3A shows "a much more gradual decline" compared to GRPO's sharp collapse. MUPO diversity starts higher and remains above GRPO diversity throughout training, though it does decline over time (consistent with the annealing schedule reducing diversity pressure). The paper does not report final diversity values for either method.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Number of groups K (Table 4):** The paper sweeps K = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5} on a subset of benchmarks (MathVerse, MathVista, MathVision for mathematical; MMStar, HallusionBench for general). Averages across all five benchmarks:
- K = 1 (equivalent to GRPO): 51.9%
- K = 2: 54.3%
- K = 3: 55.4% (best)
- K = 4: 55.3%
- K = 5: 55.0%

The paper reports that K = 3 is optimal on average. An interesting interaction emerges: "for mathematical benchmarks, larger K yields better performance, whereas smaller K is more suitable for general problems." This suggests task-dependent optimal diversity levels—math problems benefit from more strategic flexibility, while general reasoning tasks prefer more focused strategies. The paper attributes this to "intrinsic task nature: the former desires more flexible and diverse strategies, while the latter favors more uniform and structured reasoning." However, with K = 2 through 5 all within roughly 0.4 percentage points of each other, the practical sensitivity to this hyperparameter appears modest.

**Diversity reward weight λ_max (Figure 9, left):** With λ_min fixed at 0.1, λ_max is varied from 0.1 to 0.6. Accuracy peaks at λ_max = 0.4. Below 0.3, insufficient exploration increases the risk of local optima (accuracy drops). Above 0.5, diversity reward dominates at the expense of accuracy (further drops). The optimal value of 0.4 aligns with the default.

**Diversity reward weight λ_min (Figure 9, right):** With λ_max fixed at 0.4, λ_min is varied from 0.0 to 0.4. Accuracy peaks at λ_min = 0.1. At λ_min = 0.0 (diversity reward completely removed in late training), accuracy drops—the paper interprets this as the model losing the diversity needed to maintain multiple modes, potentially leading to late-stage collapse. At λ_min ≥ 0.2, accuracy also drops—diversity pressure remains too strong, preventing convergence to the best strategies.

**Load-balance exponent β:** The paper mentions that β is "set to 1 in the default configuration" (Section 4.2) but does not report an ablation sweep over β values. This is a missing experiment—β controls how aggressively group sizes are balanced, and its sensitivity is unknown.

**Embedding model choice:** The paper exclusively uses Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B for all diversity computations. No comparison to alternative embedding models (e.g., text-embedding-3, all-MiniLM, or the policy model's own hidden states) is reported. The robustness of results to embedding quality is unexamined.

**Temperature for sampling:** All training uses temperature 1.0. No sweep over sampling temperature is reported. It is unclear whether lower temperatures (which reduce raw sample diversity) would make clustering harder, or whether higher temperatures (which increase noise) would produce spurious group separations.

**Qualitative verification of group coherence (Figure 7):** The example in Figure 7 shows MUPO discovering a reference-object-based height estimation strategy that GRPO's layer-by-layer counting misses. This is a single illustrative example—no quantitative analysis of how often MUPO's alternative strategies are genuinely different (vs. superficially different wordings of the same approach) is provided.

### Critical Assessment

**Does the paper demonstrate that RL models sacrifice diversity?** Yes, this claim is well-supported. Figure 2 (A, B) shows a narrowing acc@1 vs. acc@4 gap between RL and base models at both scales. Figure 3 (A) documents rapid diversity collapse during GRPO training. Figure 4's t-SNE projections visually confirm narrower embedding distributions for RL models. However, the diversity metric (pairwise cosine distance of embedding vectors) is assumed to capture strategic diversity rather than superficial differences in wording, length, or style. No experiment validates this assumption—for example, by having human annotators judge whether two responses use genuinely different strategies and correlating those judgments with embedding distances. The embedding-based diversity metric could conflate lexical variation with strategic variation.

**Does the paper demonstrate that diversity causes better acc@4?** The correlation plot in Figure 2 (C, D) shows a positive relationship, but correlation is not causation. An alternative explanation: easier questions naturally admit more diverse correct solutions AND have higher acc@4, creating a spurious correlation. The paper does not control for question difficulty in this analysis. Additionally, the regression line is fitted to binary outcome data (0/1 for acc@4) without specifying the regression type, and no correlation coefficient is reported. This weakens the causal claim that "divergent thinking increases the odds of success."

**Does MUPO genuinely outperform strong baselines?** The acc@1 improvements of 2–7% across benchmarks (Tables 1–3) are meaningful. The acc@4 improvements of ~6% are substantial and consistent with the method's design goal. However, several caveats apply:

1. **Single-run evaluations.** No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multiple-seed results are reported. With test sets of unknown size (the standard MathVerse test split, for instance, is 3,940 questions for the testmini version, but the paper does not specify which split is used), sampling error could be non-trivial, especially for smaller benchmarks.

2. **Baseline provenance inconsistency.** Baseline results are "primarily cited from the corresponding papers, secondarily from the OpenCompass leaderboard, and reproduced when neither source is available." This introduces systematic error: scores from different sources may use different evaluation protocols, prompt formats, or answer extraction methods. The paper does not specify which baselines came from which source, making it impossible to assess whether comparisons are apples-to-apples.

3. **No GRPO-trained baseline at identical compute.** The paper compares MUPO against published models, but does not report results for a GRPO model trained by the authors on the same data (ViRL39K), with the same base model, for the same number of steps, at the same batch size. The K = 1 ablation in Table 4 provides some evidence (K = 1 is equivalent to GRPO, scoring 51.9% vs. 55.4% for K = 3), but this is on a "subset of benchmarks" and may not be directly comparable to the full-scale results in Table 1.

4. **MUPO's training overhead is unaccounted for.** MUPO adds embedding computation, constrained k-means clustering, and inter-group distance computation at each training step. The paper does not report wall-clock time, GPU memory overhead, or total FLOPs for MUPO vs. GRPO training. If MUPO is 2× more expensive per step, the fair comparison would give GRPO 2× more steps or 2× larger batch size.

**Does MUPO actually preserve diverse reasoning strategies?** The evidence is mixed. Figure 6 shows t-SNE projections with multimodal structure for MUPO and unimodal structure for GRPO. Figure 8 shows higher diversity reward for MUPO than the near-zero diversity of GRPO (Figure 3A). The qualitative example in Figure 7 shows MUPO using a reference-object strategy that GRPO misses. However, several critical validations are missing:

1. **No quantitative measure of how many distinct strategies MUPO maintains.** The paper shows t-SNE plots for single examples but never reports, for instance, the number of clusters found in MUPO vs. GRPO model outputs across a test set, or the distribution of inter-response distances.

2. **No demonstration that groups persist across training examples.** Does MUPO learn to consistently deploy different strategies, or does it merely produce outputs that happen to cluster differently within each batch? The group partitioning is per-batch, meaning group 1 in one training step has no necessary relationship to group 1 in the next step.

3. **The accuracy gate on the diversity reward means only correct responses contribute to diversity incentives.** This creates a potential confound: if MUPO is more accurate than GRPO (which it is), it automatically gets more diversity signal because more responses are correct and thus eligible for diversity rewards. The improvement in diversity could be partially an artifact of improved accuracy rather than a causal mechanism from diversity to accuracy.

**Does the accuracy-gated diversity reward actually prevent reward hacking?** The paper argues that gating prevents the model from pursuing diversity at the expense of correctness, but no direct test of this claim is reported. An experiment that would test this: remove the accuracy gate and observe whether accuracy drops (reward hacking) or remains stable (gate is unnecessary). This experiment is absent.

**What would have strengthened the paper:**

1. **Multiple random seeds** with standard deviations on all benchmark results to establish statistical reliability.

2. **A controlled GRPO baseline** trained by the authors under identical conditions to MUPO (same data, steps, batch size, hardware), rather than relying on published scores from different training pipelines.

3. **Human evaluation of strategy diversity** to validate that embedding-based cosine distance actually captures strategic differences rather than surface-level variation.

4. **Ablation removing the accuracy gate** from the diversity reward to demonstrate its necessity.

5. **Training compute comparison** (step time, memory, total FLOPs) between MUPO and GRPO.

6. **Analysis of whether MUPO groups are semantically meaningful**—for example, by showing that responses within the same group use the same problem-solving approach (identified by human annotation or keyword analysis).

7. **Results on additional model families** beyond Qwen2.5-VL to establish generality.

**Where the claims hold and where they are conditional:**

- The claim that "RL models sacrifice diversity for depth" is well-supported for GRPO-trained Qwen2.5-VL models on reasoning benchmarks. Generalization to other RL algorithms (PPO, DPO), other model families, or other task types is untested.

- The claim that "MUPO outperforms baselines" is supported for the specific benchmarks and models tested but is sensitive to the provenance of baseline scores and lacks statistical error bars.

- The claim that "MUPO preserves divergent thinking" is supported by qualitative visualizations and diversity metric curves but lacks quantitative validation of strategic diversity as distinct from embedding diversity. The causal link from diversity preservation to improved acc@4 is plausible but not rigorously isolated from the confounding effect of overall improved accuracy.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Training Overhead of MUPO Is Unaccounted For in Comparisons

**The assumption or constraint.** MUPO adds non-trivial computation at every training step that standard GRPO does not require: embedding all N=15 sampled responses using Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, running constrained k-means clustering over those embeddings, computing pairwise inter-group cosine distances for the diversity reward, and maintaining the annealing schedule. The paper does not report training wall-clock time, GPU memory consumption, or total FLOPs for MUPO relative to GRPO. Section 5's "Implementation Details" specifies the training configuration but omits any compute-cost comparison.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt MUPO cannot assess the cost-benefit tradeoff. If MUPO training is, hypothetically, 2× slower per step than GRPO, then a fair comparison would give GRPO 2× more training steps or 2× larger batch sizes within the same compute budget. The headline gains of 2–7% over prior published baselines (Tables 1–3) might shrink or vanish if those baselines were retrained at equivalent compute. This is consequential because RL fine-tuning of VLMs is already expensive (sampling 15 responses per example per step, forward and backward passes through a 7B-parameter model), and adding embedding and clustering overhead could make MUPO infeasible under tight compute budgets, especially for larger models or datasets.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not report wall-clock time per step, peak GPU memory, total training FLOPs, or any other compute-cost metric for MUPO versus GRPO. The K=1 ablation in Table 4 (where K=1 "degrades to GRPO") reports accuracy differences but not training cost differences. Section 8's limitations discussion is absent (the paper has no explicit limitations section). The authors acknowledge that compute cost matters—they flag the difficulty estimation cost in related test-time scaling work—but do not apply the same scrutiny to their own method.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not measure, discuss, or attempt to reduce MUPO's training overhead. The embedding model (Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B) is relatively lightweight at 0.6B parameters, but the pairwise distance computation scales as O(N²) across all N responses, and constrained k-means adds further overhead. Whether these costs are negligible relative to the VLM's forward passes (which generate the N responses in the first place) is unknown. A simple mitigation—using the policy model's own hidden states for embedding rather than a separate embedding model—is not explored.

---

### 6.2 Single Benchmark Domain and Model Family—Generalization Is Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use Qwen2.5-VL (3B and 7B) as the base model, trained on ViRL39K, and evaluated exclusively on visual reasoning benchmarks (mathematical and general-purpose VQA). The paper does not test MUPO on other model families (e.g., LLaVA, InternVL as a training backbone, not just a baseline), other RL algorithms affected by mode collapse (e.g., PPO, DPO), other task domains (e.g., pure text reasoning, code generation, embodied planning), or other dataset types (e.g., larger-scale reasoning corpora, multi-turn dialogue). The authors do not claim broader applicability, but the paper's title and abstract frame the contribution as addressing diversity collapse in "vision-language models" generally, not "Qwen2.5-VL trained with GRPO on ViRL39K."

**The consequence.** The findings may not transfer. Qwen2.5-VL has specific architectural properties (vision encoder, cross-attention or MLP-based multimodal fusion, specific pretraining data mixture) that could interact with MUPO's clustering mechanism in unknown ways—for example, whether Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B produces meaningful strategic embeddings for other model families' outputs is untested. GRPO's diversity collapse dynamics might differ for models with different pretraining diversity or for different RL algorithms. The ViRL39K dataset, described as "high-quality and comprehensive," may have properties (e.g., problem difficulty distribution, answer format consistency) that affect whether strategy-level clustering is feasible or beneficial. A practitioner using a different base model, different RL algorithm, or different reasoning domain cannot assess from the paper's evidence whether MUPO will help, do nothing, or hurt.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper evaluates across 9 benchmarks spanning mathematical and general-purpose reasoning (Tables 1–3), which provides some within-domain breadth—the method works on geometry as well as object counting, on logical reasoning as well as math. However, this is breadth within visual reasoning, not across fundamentally different domains. The paper uses two model scales (3B and 7B) within the same family, which shows the method scales but not that it transfers. The explicit test of whether MUPO's benefits persist under different conditions is absent: no experiments with alternative base models, no experiments with alternative RL algorithms, no text-only benchmarks.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss generalization as a limitation or propose follow-up experiments. The "Future Directions" section that would typically address this is absent. The choice of a single model family is pragmatic—Qwen2.5-VL is a strong open-source VLM and a natural choice—but the paper does not calibrate reader expectations about scope.

---

### 6.3 MUPO's Additional Hyperparameters Require Tuning Without Clear Transfer

**The assumption or constraint.** MUPO introduces several hyperparameters beyond standard GRPO: the number of groups K, the minimum group size G_min, the load-balance exponent β, the diversity reward weights λ_max and λ_min, the annealing schedule type (cosine), and the choice of embedding model for clustering. The paper tunes these on ViRL39K with Qwen2.5-VL-7B and reports the optimal settings (K=3, G_min=3, β=1, λ_max=0.4, λ_min=0.1, cosine schedule, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B). However, Figure 9 shows that performance is sensitive to λ_max and λ_min—deviating by 0.1 from the optimum can cause measurable accuracy drops (approximately 0.5–1.5 percentage points based on the y-axis range). Table 4 shows a similar sensitivity to K, with performance varying by ~3.5 percentage points between K=1 and the optimum.

**The consequence.** A practitioner applying MUPO to a new model, dataset, or domain would likely need to re-tune these hyperparameters, which requires running multiple full training runs (each expensive) or conducting ablation studies on subsets. The paper provides no guidance on how these hyperparameters should transfer: does K=3 remain optimal for a 13B model? Does the λ_max=0.4 to λ_min=0.1 range work for code generation? Is G_min=3 sufficient when N is larger than 15? The sensitivity is not catastrophic—all reasonable settings outperform GRPO (K=1 in Table 4)—but the gap between a well-tuned MUPO and a poorly-tuned one could be substantial. This makes adoption more expensive than the paper's "simple yet effective" framing suggests.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 shows K sensitivity on a "subset of benchmarks" with a range of 51.9% (K=1) to 55.4% (K=3). Figure 9 shows λ_max sensitivity from ~52% to ~54% (estimated from y-axis) and λ_min sensitivity from ~53.5% to ~54.5%. The paper does not report a sweep over G_min, β, or the embedding model choice. No experiment tests whether hyperparameters optimized at 7B transfer to 3B (or vice versa).

**Mitigation status.** Partial. The paper does conduct ablation studies on K, λ_max, and λ_min, which provides some evidence that the default settings are reasonable and that performance is not pathologically sensitive to these choices. However, the paper does not provide a hyperparameter selection heuristic, a sensitivity analysis across model scales, or a recommendation for how practitioners should set these values in new settings without full sweeps. The claim that MUPO is a "drop-in replacement" (Section 4.2) understates the tuning burden.

### 6.4 No Causal Evidence That Strategic Diversity (Rather Than General Quality Improvement) Drives acc@4 Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central causal claim is that preserving diverse reasoning strategies during RL training enables better test-time scaling (higher acc@4). The mechanism: MUPO groups responses by strategic similarity, optimizes each group independently, and incentivizes inter-group separation → the model learns multiple distinct reasoning approaches → when sampled 4 times, it covers more of solution space → acc@4 improves. This causal chain is assumed but not directly tested. The paper demonstrates correlation (diversity correlates with acc@4 in Figure 2C,D) and qualitative visualization (t-SNE in Figure 6 shows multimodal structure for MUPO), but these do not isolate the causal role of diversity from the general quality improvement that MUPO also provides (acc@1 also improves).

**The consequence.** The acc@4 gains could arise primarily from MUPO being a better optimizer overall—producing higher-quality individual trajectories—rather than from producing strategically diverse trajectories. A model that produces better single answers would naturally have higher acc@4 because each of the 4 samples is individually more likely to be correct. The paper's own data support this alternative: MUPO improves acc@1 by 2–3 percentage points on average (Tables 1–2), and part of the acc@4 gain is mechanically attributable to this baseline improvement. Without a controlled experiment that varies strategic diversity while holding single-trajectory quality constant (or vice versa), the paper cannot quantify how much of the acc@4 gain comes from diversity versus sheer quality improvement. This matters for diagnosing *why* MUPO works: if the benefit is mostly from better optimization, then simpler improvements to GRPO (better learning rates, longer training, improved reward shaping) might achieve similar gains without the complexity of group partitioning and diversity rewards.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The correlation plot in Figure 2(C,D) shows a positive relationship between diversity and acc@4 for base models, but this does not establish that MUPO's diversity *causes* its acc@4 gains—base model diversity arises from pretraining, while MUPO diversity arises from a training intervention. The t-SNE visualizations (Figures 4, 6) are qualitative and based on single examples. The paper reports both acc@1 and acc@4, and the acc@4 gains are consistently larger (e.g., 6.0 vs. 2.5 percentage points average improvement on mathematical benchmarks), which is consistent with a diversity mechanism but also consistent with a model that simply benefits more from test-time sampling because its outputs are better. No experiment measures the *marginal contribution of diversity* to acc@4 after controlling for single-sample quality—for instance, by comparing MUPO against a hypothetical model matched on acc@1 but with lower diversity.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats the correlation evidence as sufficient and does not design an experiment to isolate the causal role of diversity. The accuracy gate on the diversity reward (Equation 6) actually introduces a confound: because MUPO is more accurate overall, more of its responses are eligible for diversity rewards, creating more diversity signal. This means diversity and accuracy improvements are intertwined in MUPO's training dynamics in a way that makes it impossible to separate their contributions from the reported outcomes alone.

### 6.5 No Statistical Rigor—Single-Run Evaluations Without Error Bars

**The assumption or constraint.** All quantitative results in Tables 1–3, Table 4, and Figures 2, 8, and 9 are reported as point estimates without any measure of uncertainty: no standard deviations, no confidence intervals, no results from multiple random seeds or multiple training runs, and no statistical significance tests comparing MUPO against baselines. The paper does not specify the exact test split sizes for each benchmark, but standard versions of these benchmarks have test sets ranging from a few hundred (MathVista testmini: 1,000 questions; MMStar: 1,500 questions) to several thousand (MathVerse testmini: 3,940 questions). On a 1,000-question benchmark, a 2 percentage point difference (the magnitude of several reported gains) corresponds to 20 questions—small enough that run-to-run variance from random weight initialization, data ordering, or sampling noise could plausibly produce or erase such differences.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot assess whether MUPO's reported improvements are reliable or within the noise floor of VLM training. The field has well-documented instability in RL fine-tuning—small changes in random seed, data order, or hyperparameters can produce substantially different outcomes—and without multiple-run statistics, the paper's specific numerical claims (e.g., 51.6% vs. 49.1% average on mathematical benchmarks) may not replicate. This is particularly important for the baseline comparisons, where published scores from different papers are compared against the authors' own MUPO runs—any systematic difference in evaluation protocol, answer extraction, or random variation could be misattributed to method superiority.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper never reports standard deviations, confidence intervals, or seed counts. The experimental setup in Section 5 states the training configuration (ViRL39K, 2 epochs, lr=1e-6) but does not mention multiple runs or seed averaging. The ablation studies (Table 4, Figure 9) report single values per configuration. The main results (Tables 1–3) compare single MUPO runs against published baselines that may themselves be single runs.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. This is a significant methodological gap that limits the strength of the paper's comparative claims. At a minimum, reporting standard deviation across 3–5 training runs for MUPO on each benchmark would allow readers to assess whether the 2–7% gains exceed run-to-run variance. The absence is particularly notable given that the paper's central contribution is a training algorithm, where training variance is directly relevant to adoption decisions.

---

### 6.6 Clustering Quality Depends on Embedding Model Choice, Which Is Unexamined

**The assumption or constraint.** MUPO's group partitioning mechanism—the structural defense against diversity collapse—relies entirely on the quality of the embedding space produced by Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B. If this embedding model fails to distinguish strategically different reasoning approaches (e.g., mapping equation-solving and validation-based approaches to nearby points because they share vocabulary or length characteristics), then the constrained k-means clustering will produce groups that mix strategies rather than separating them. In this case, the localized advantage estimation would operate over heterogeneous response sets, defeating the purpose of group-wise optimization. Conversely, if the embedding model produces clusters based on surface features irrelevant to strategy (e.g., response length, formatting style, presence of certain keywords), the groups would be strategically meaningless while appearing separated in embedding space.

**The consequence.** MUPO's effectiveness could be tied to properties of Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B that do not generalize. A different base model producing different reasoning styles might require a different embedding model. A different domain (e.g., code generation) might need an embedding model trained on code rather than general text. The paper provides no evidence that Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B successfully captures *strategic* similarity as opposed to surface-level similarity, and no experiment validates the clustering quality beyond showing that MUPO with K=3 groups works better than K=1 (which could be for reasons unrelated to clustering quality—for instance, any random partitioning into independent sub-objectives might regularize training).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper qualitatively shows in Figure 7 that MUPO discovers a reference-object strategy that GRPO misses, but this does not validate that the clustering mechanism correctly grouped reference-object responses together during training. The t-SNE visualizations in Figure 6 show multimodal structure, but t-SNE is applied to the policy model's outputs, not to the embedding model's representations—this shows the *outcome* of diverse training, not the *quality* of the clustering that enabled it. The ablation in Table 4 varies K but not the embedding model. No experiment compares Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B against alternative embedding models (e.g., text-embedding-3-large, the policy model's own hidden states, or a domain-specific embedding model) to assess robustness. No experiment validates clustering quality against human annotations of strategy similarity.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The choice of embedding model is treated as a fixed implementation detail rather than a design dimension requiring validation. The paper mentions that the embedding model encodes "reasoning segments" but does not analyze whether the resulting clusters correspond to human-identifiable strategies. This is a practical concern because a practitioner adopting MUPO for a new domain must choose an embedding model without guidance on what properties it needs. The method's core mechanism—diversity preservation through embedding-based grouping—is only as reliable as the embeddings it depends on, and this reliability is untested.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

**This paper introduces a new diagnostic axis—strategic diversity—into VLM reasoning research, and provides the first mechanistic account of why it collapses under GRPO.** Prior to this work, the field evaluated reasoning VLMs primarily on single-sample accuracy (acc@1), implicitly treating improvements as uniformly positive. The paper's central reframing—that GRPO's success on acc@1 comes at the cost of parallel scaling capability—changes what it means for a reasoning model to be "good." A model that achieves 49% acc@1 but plateaus at 53% acc@4 (Vision-R1-7B on mathematical benchmarks, Table 1) is fundamentally different from one that achieves 52% acc@1 and 59% acc@4 (MUPO-Thinker-7B)—the latter can leverage test-time compute, the former cannot. This is not an incremental improvement; it introduces a new evaluation criterion (parallel scaling efficiency) that was previously invisible and that future reasoning VLM papers will need to report.

**The diversity collapse diagnosis reinterprets GRPO's limitations as a structural property rather than a reward-design problem.** The field has invested substantial effort in improving GRPO through better rewards, uncertainty mechanisms, and sampling strategies (Section 2). The paper's training-dynamics evidence—diversity dropping "sharply during the early training stage, i.e., first 20 steps" (Figure 3A)—implies that these reward-level interventions were targeting symptoms of a deeper mechanism. Global advantage normalization creates a winner-take-all dynamic that extinguishes minority strategies before reward quality can even matter. This diagnosis redirects research attention from *improving GRPO's incentives* to *restructuring how advantages are computed*, a fundamentally different solution space. The implication is that entropy bonuses, perception-aware rewards, and trajectory prioritization—the main directions of prior work—are unlikely to solve the diversity problem because they operate within an objective that structurally favors mode collapse. This makes that entire line of work less attractive as a path to diverse reasoning.

**The paper reconciles the contradiction between RL models' superior single-sample performance and their puzzling inability to benefit from parallel sampling.** Prior to this work, the observation that base models sometimes outperform RL models under test-time scaling (noted by [17, 43], cited in Section 1) was an unexplained anomaly. The paper provides a mechanism-level explanation: RL models concentrate probability mass in a narrow region of solution space (Figure 4's t-SNE visualization), so additional samples re-explore the same strategies, while base models' wider distribution samples distinct approaches. This converts an empirical puzzle into a understood trade-off—depth versus breadth—and provides a framework for evaluating future methods. A proposed RL variant can now be assessed on two axes: does it improve single-trajectory quality, and does it preserve or degrade parallel-scaling capability?

**The accuracy-gated diversity reward establishes a reusable design pattern for combining quality and diversity objectives in RL.** The specific mechanism—gate a diversity bonus on a binary correctness threshold, and anneal the diversity weight to transition from exploration to exploitation—is not specific to VLM reasoning or GRPO. It addresses a general tension in diversity-incentivized RL: unconditional diversity rewards invite reward hacking, while pure accuracy optimization causes mode collapse. The gating-plus-annealing pattern (Equations 6–7) provides a principled template that can be applied to any domain with verifiable correctness signals: code generation (gate on passing unit tests), mathematical proof (gate on proof-checker acceptance), or planning (gate on goal achievement). The ablation in Figure 9 provides initial evidence that the pattern is not trivially insensitive to hyperparameters—both too much diversity (λ_max > 0.4) and too little (λ_max < 0.3) degrade performance—but the optimal range is wide enough to be practically tunable. This is a methodological contribution that extends beyond the paper's immediate domain.

**However, the shift is diagnostic and methodological, not a paradigm change in VLM training.** The paper does not demonstrate that MUPO-trained models surpass the best available reasoning VLMs across all metrics—proprietary models like GPT-5-Thinking (76.1% average acc@1 on mathematical benchmarks) remain far ahead of MUPO-Thinker-7B (51.6%). The contribution is understanding *why* current open-source RL training underperforms relative to its potential under parallel scaling, and providing a targeted intervention. Whether MUPO scales to larger models (13B, 70B), other model families (LLaVA, InternVL), or state-of-the-art proprietary training pipelines is untested. The paper's impact is therefore primarily on the research community's understanding and methodology, not on deployed system performance.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Validating whether embedding-based clusters correspond to human-identifiable reasoning strategies.** The paper's group partitioning mechanism assumes that cosine distance in Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B space captures strategic similarity—that two responses with similar embeddings use the same *type* of reasoning, not just similar wording. This assumption is central to MUPO's design but entirely unvalidated. A follow-up study would sample paired responses from MUPO training, ask human annotators (or domain experts for math problems) to judge whether the two responses use genuinely different strategies or are surface variations of the same approach, and compute the correlation between human strategic-difference judgments and embedding cosine distances. A strong positive correlation (e.g., Spearman's ρ > 0.7) would validate the core mechanism; a weak correlation would suggest MUPO works for reasons other than strategic grouping—perhaps any partitioned optimization regularizes training regardless of semantic coherence. This experiment is straightforward (crowd-sourced annotation on a few hundred response pairs) and would determine whether MUPO's theoretical motivation matches its empirical mechanism.

**Isolating the causal contribution of diversity to acc@4 gains by controlling for single-sample quality.** The paper shows that MUPO improves both acc@1 and acc@4, but cannot attribute the acc@4 gains specifically to diversity rather than to generally better optimization. A controlled experiment would train multiple MUPO variants with identical architecture but systematically varied diversity pressure (e.g., by fixing λ at different constant values rather than annealing), measure both acc@1 and acc@4 for each variant, and compute the *residual* acc@4 after regressing out acc@1. If diversity contributes causally, variants with higher diversity should show positive residuals—better acc@4 than predicted by their acc@1 alone. This experiment would also determine whether the accuracy gate on the diversity reward (Equation 6) creates a confound (more accurate models automatically receive more diversity signal) by comparing variants with and without the gate, controlling for overall accuracy. This is critical for assessing whether MUPO's claimed mechanism—diverse strategies → better parallel scaling—is real or whether the method simply improves general optimization quality through a partitioned loss.

**Testing MUPO on text-only reasoning with standard LLM training pipelines.** The paper evaluates exclusively on visual reasoning benchmarks, but the diversity collapse mechanism (global advantage normalization → mode collapse) should apply identically to text-only LLM reasoning tasks (GSM8K, MATH, ARC, etc.) where GRPO is widely used. A natural extension would train LLaMA-3-8B or Qwen2.5-7B (text-only) with GRPO and MUPO on a standard math reasoning dataset (e.g., MetaMathQA or NuminaMath), evaluate on MATH and GSM8K, and measure both acc@1 and pass@k for k = 4, 8, 16. If MUPO reproduces the pattern of similar acc@1 but substantially higher pass@k, it would establish domain generality and open a much larger application space (text-based reasoning is more widely deployed than visual reasoning). If the pattern does not reproduce, it would suggest that visual reasoning has specific properties—perhaps greater strategic ambiguity or multimodality-induced diversity—that make diversity preservation more impactful, refining our understanding of when the method applies.

**Developing a lightweight, amortized diversity mechanism that avoids per-step clustering.** MUPO's training overhead—embedding all N responses per step, running constrained k-means, computing O(N²) pairwise distances—is unmeasured but potentially significant. A follow-up could replace the explicit per-step clustering with an *amortized* diversity mechanism: train a lightweight classifier head on top of the policy model's hidden states to predict group assignments, using the embedding-based clusters as pseudo-labels for a few initial training steps, then freeze or slowly update the classifier. This would reduce per-step overhead to a single forward pass through a small MLP rather than a separate embedding model plus clustering. The experiment would compare wall-clock time, GPU memory, and final accuracy between standard MUPO and the amortized variant, determining whether the diversity benefit can be preserved at substantially lower cost. Success would make MUPO practical for larger-scale training; failure would indicate that explicit, per-batch reclustering is essential to the method's effectiveness.

**Stress-testing MUPO under distribution shift and adversarial problem sets.** The paper evaluates on standard benchmarks where the training distribution (ViRL39K) and test distribution are presumably similar. A stress-test would construct test sets that specifically require diverse strategies—for example, by selecting problems where the most common reasoning approach (e.g., equation-solving for geometry) systematically fails and alternative approaches (e.g., coordinate geometry, trigonometric solutions, validation-based elimination) are required. If MUPO-trained models genuinely maintain multiple strategies, they should substantially outperform GRPO-trained models on such adversarially constructed sets, even at acc@1 (since the dominant strategy fails and alternatives are needed). If the performance gap is small or absent, it would suggest that MUPO's diversity is real but shallow—the model maintains surface-level variation rather than genuinely distinct strategic capabilities. This experiment would also test the paper's qualitative claim (Figure 7) that MUPO discovers "smarter solutions when faced with various problem types" beyond the single illustrative example provided.

**Investigating whether MUPO's per-batch grouping can be replaced with persistent strategy vectors.** The current design partitions responses into groups independently at each training step, meaning "group 1" has no persistent identity across steps—the model cannot learn that group 1 is "the equation-solving strategy" and group 2 is "the validation strategy." An alternative would maintain K learnable strategy embeddings that are prepended to the input or used to condition the policy, with the clustering used to assign each response to the nearest strategy embedding. This would allow the model to learn *explicit, controllable strategies*—at inference time, a user could request "solve this using the validation approach" by selecting the corresponding embedding. The experiment would compare: (a) standard MUPO, (b) MUPO with persistent learnable strategy tokens, and (c) a variant where strategy tokens are trained but the diversity reward is removed, measuring controllability (can the model follow a specified strategy?) and overall accuracy. Success would produce a more interpretable and controllable reasoning system; failure would suggest that per-batch flexibility (adapting groups to the specific batch composition) is essential.

**Testing MUPO with alternative RL algorithms beyond GRPO.** The paper's diversity collapse diagnosis is specific to GRPO's global advantage normalization, but other RL algorithms for language model fine-tuning—PPO with a learned value function, DPO with pairwise preferences, REINFORCE with a baseline—may exhibit different collapse dynamics. A systematic study would apply MUPO's group partitioning and diversity reward to each of these algorithms, measuring diversity collapse curves (analogous to Figure 3A) and final acc@1/acc@4. This would determine whether MUPO's benefit is specific to GRPO's structural flaw or generalizes as a diversity-preserving wrapper for any policy-gradient method. If MUPO helps across algorithms, it becomes a general-purpose tool; if it only helps GRPO, the paper's diagnostic contribution (global normalization causes collapse) is validated and the solution space is narrower.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Cost-efficient test-time scaling for VLM reasoning in production.** Organizations deploying VLMs for high-stakes reasoning tasks—medical image analysis with differential diagnosis reasoning, engineering diagram interpretation, scientific figure comprehension—commonly use best-of-N or self-consistency to boost accuracy, where each additional sample costs inference compute. The paper's results show that a GRPO-trained model like Vision-R1-7B gains approximately 3.7 percentage points going from acc@1 to acc@4 on mathematical benchmarks (49.1% → 52.8%, Table 1), while MUPO-Thinker-7B gains 7.2 points (51.6% → 58.8%). In a deployment setting with a fixed per-query inference budget, switching from GRPO to MUPO training would approximately double the return on test-time compute—half as many samples needed for the same accuracy, or substantially higher accuracy at the same sample count. For high-volume services processing millions of queries, this directly translates to cost savings or quality improvements without changing model size or hardware.

**Data generation for self-improvement and distillation pipelines.** When using VLMs to generate training data for smaller models or for iterative self-improvement (STaR-style or ReST-style loops), the diversity of generated solutions matters: a dataset containing multiple distinct correct reasoning paths for each problem produces a student model that is more robust and generalizes better than one trained on uniform solutions. The paper shows that MUPO-trained models produce multimodal reasoning distributions (Figure 6) while maintaining high per-sample accuracy (Tables 1–3). Using MUPO as the generator in a distillation pipeline would produce training data with both high correctness and high strategic diversity—each training example could include multiple valid solution approaches sampled from different groups. The acc@4 results provide a rough estimate: MUPO-Thinker-7B produces correct answers for 58.8% of math problems within 4 attempts versus 52.8% for Vision-R1-7B, meaning approximately 6% more problems would have at least one correct trajectory in the generated dataset, and the trajectories would span more diverse strategies.

**On-device or edge deployment where test-time compute compensates for model size.** The paper demonstrates at the 3B scale that MUPO-Thinker-3B's acc@4 (56.0% average, Table 3) approaches the acc@4 of some 7B baselines (e.g., VLAA-Thinker-7B at 51.5%, Vision-R1-7B at 52.8%, Table 1). For applications requiring small on-device models—mobile assistants, augmented reality reasoning, privacy-sensitive document analysis—the ability to run a 3B model with 4 parallel samples and achieve accuracy competitive with a 7B model reduces memory footprint and latency while maintaining quality. The specific benefit: a 3B model requires roughly half the memory and can run faster per token than a 7B model, and parallel sampling is embarrassingly parallelizable across available device cores. MUPO makes this small-model-plus-parallel-sampling strategy viable because it trains the model to produce genuinely diverse samples, avoiding the diminishing returns that plague GRPO-trained models under parallel scaling.
