Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (the author of The Peterman Post reflects on finding his own definition of work-life balance during the pandemic):

**The pandemic and the shift to working more**

When shelter-in-place orders began a few years ago, Ryan faced an unprecedented change: for the first time, he couldn't travel or spend time in public spaces. Rather than let that time go to waste, he decided to work more. He found himself working most of his waking hours on weekdays—and yet, surprisingly, he still felt like he had work-life balance. The experience taught him that "work-life balance" is not a fixed formula but "highly personal"—it varies based on your circumstances, and each person needs to find what it means for themselves rather than adopting someone else's definition.

**Intrinsic motivation: excitement as a buffer against burnout**

The first factor Ryan identifies is intrinsic motivation. The more interested you are in your work, the more you can do without feeling burnt out. During the pandemic, he was able to sustain heavy output and still feel balanced because he was genuinely excited about his work and got fulfillment from achieving his career goals. He contrasts this with a hypothetical scenario: if the project had been some boring migration, or if his values didn't align with his career goals, even 40 hours a week could feel like bad work-life balance. The quality of the work itself—not just the quantity of hours—shapes how sustainable and balanced it feels.

**Opportunity cost: when working doesn't feel like missing out**

The second factor is opportunity cost. During shelter-in-place, the opportunity cost of working "dropped lower than it ever was." With little competing for his time—no travel, no public events, diminished social plans—he didn't feel like he was missing out on anything by working more. The same hours in a different season of life could have felt like poor work-life balance if he had felt he was sacrificing exciting life plans like travel or family time. The balance isn't just about work hours versus non-work hours; it's about what those non-work hours would have contained.

**The non-negotiable: never sacrifice health for extended periods**

The third factor is health, which Ryan treats as an absolute boundary. No matter how much he worked, he always made sure to take care of his health. No amount of excitement about a project could make him give up sleep or exercise for an extended period. His rationale: doing so would be "short-sighted." Late nights take time to recover from and lead to worse results—what actually matters is **quality throughput**, not raw hours. He positions this as the one constant across all phases of life: "You should never sacrifice your health for extended periods," even when everything else about your work-life balance equation shifts.

**Work-life balance as a moving target—and finding work that energizes you**

Ryan emphasizes that it takes time to find what work-life balance means for yourself, and the answer changes throughout your life. While he declines to repeat the standard internet advice on improving balance, he offers one unique lever: **find something you're excited to work on**. When the work itself energizes you rather than drains you, the entire balance equation shifts. He acknowledges that finding such work is a deeper topic for another time, but the implication is clear: chasing a one-size-fits-all hour count misses the point when intrinsic motivation and life circumstances can make the same schedule feel either punishing or fulfilling.
