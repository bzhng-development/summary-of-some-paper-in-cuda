# Teaching Large Language Models to Reason with Reinforcement Learning

**ArXiv:** [2403.04642](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04642)

## 🎯 Pitch

Expert Iteration—a simple reject-sample-and-fine-tune loop—beats PPO on math reasoning benchmarks while needing only ~60K rollouts, matching PPO’s sample efficiency. Despite RL improving both greedy accuracy and pass@96 diversity, models never explore beyond solutions already reachable via supervised fine-tuning, capping the gains from pure RL.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how multiple reinforcement learning algorithms—Expert Iteration, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Return-Conditioned RL—improve LLM reasoning capabilities when using sparse, dense, or model-based rewards on GSM8K and SVAMP with Llama-2 7B and 13B models. The authors find that **Expert Iteration** (rejection sampling from the current policy followed by supervised fine-tuning on high-return trajectories) reliably attains the best performance across most settings while exhibiting surprisingly competitive sample complexity with PPO (both converging in only ~60,000 model rollouts). The paper demonstrates that RL fine-tuning improves both maj@1 and pass@96 scores simultaneously (unlike SFT, which exhibits a tradeoff where continued training raises maj@1 at the expense of pass@96 diversity), yet the maximum pass@96 scores remain close to what light SFT alone can achieve, establishing that RL training does not discover fundamentally new solutions beyond the model's pretraining/SFT capabilities and that limited exploration is the primary bottleneck for further improvement.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: We Don't Understand How RL Algorithms Compare for LLM Reasoning

The fundamental question this paper tackles is deceptively simple: **when you have a ground-truth reward signal on a reasoning task, which RL algorithm should you use to fine-tune an LLM, and why?** Despite the rapid proliferation of RL-based LLM training methods—particularly RLHF for alignment—the field lacked a systematic head-to-head comparison across representative algorithms under a controlled setting where the reward signal is reliable and the evaluation metrics directly measure the desired capability.

This gap matters for several reasons the authors establish throughout Section 1 and Section 2:

- **Method selection is currently driven by convention, not evidence.** PPO has become the default algorithm for RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022) largely because it worked in those specific alignment contexts. But within the RLHF literature itself, the evidence is mixed: several recent works including ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023), Reward-Ranked Fine-tuning (Dong et al., 2023), and AlpacaFarm (Dubois et al., 2023) all demonstrate that simply fine-tuning on high-return responses with standard cross-entropy loss can match or exceed PPO's performance. This raises a critical question: **is PPO's complexity necessary, or do simpler methods like Expert Iteration suffice?** Without a controlled comparison, practitioners cannot make informed algorithmic choices.

- **Reasoning is a different optimization landscape than preference alignment.** In RLHF, models optimize against a learned reward model that is inherently unreliable—it can be over-optimized, gamed, or exploited (Gao et al., 2022). But in reasoning tasks like math word problems, there is often a **ground-truth success criterion** (did the model get the right answer?). This fundamentally changes the nature of the optimization problem: the reward signal is sparse but perfectly reliable. Whether algorithms that succeed in the noisy, hackable reward environment of RLHF also dominate in the clean, sparse-reward environment of reasoning is an open question that the paper explicitly investigates.

- **The pretrained model imposes a strong prior that interacts with RL differently than training from scratch.** Most classic RL successes (AlphaGo, Dota 2, StarCraft II) trained neural networks from scratch or near-scratch, where exploration is table-stakes for discovering good behaviors. LLMs, by contrast, start from a pretrained checkpoint that already encodes substantial knowledge about the task domain. This prior may fundamentally alter how different RL algorithms behave—potentially compressing sample complexity requirements and reducing the benefits of sophisticated exploration mechanisms that PPO provides over simpler behavior cloning.

### Conflicting Evidence Across the Literature

The paper is motivated by a landscape of seemingly contradictory findings about what works for improving LLM capabilities with RL:

**On the RLHF side:** PPO remains the dominant workhorse. Ouyang et al. (2022) used it to train InstructGPT on human preference data with substantial improvements over SFT baselines. Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) similarly relied on PPO for the RL phase of training. Yet, Gulcehre et al. (2023) showed that ReST—essentially Expert Iteration with growing datasets—achieves comparable performance with a simpler pipeline. Dong et al. (2023) demonstrated that reward-ranked fine-tuning (filtering samples by reward and doing SFT on the best ones) works just as well. Dubois et al. (2023) found the same in their AlpacaFarm benchmark. These mixed results suggest that **PPO's apparent dominance may be specific to the reward model optimization setting (where over-optimization is a risk), not a general advantage of the algorithm itself.**

**On the reasoning side:** Expert Iteration has quietly accumulated an impressive track record. Yuan et al. (2023) applied a single round of EI across multiple model sizes on GSM8K, finding sizeable gains for smaller models. Shen et al. (2023) reported a dramatic jump from ~30% to ~60% on code generation after a single round of EI. Zelikman et al. (2022) showed that STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner)—which iteratively fine-tunes on correctly solved reasoning traces—can bootstrap reasoning from a handful of examples. Uesato et al. (2022) demonstrated that filtering solutions by correctness and retraining can improve math reasoning. However, none of these works systematically compared EI against PPO or other RL algorithms under identical conditions—different papers used different models, different datasets, different reward signals, and different hyperparameter regimes, making it impossible to attribute performance differences to algorithmic choice.

**The exploration question looms large.** A critical source of confusion is whether LLMs actually *explore* during RL fine-tuning, or whether they simply reorganize and surface knowledge they already possess from pretraining. If exploration is minimal, then the advantage of on-policy algorithms like PPO—which are designed to efficiently explore and credit-assign in complex environments—diminishes significantly compared to simpler offline or filtered-SFT approaches. The paper explicitly identifies this as a motivating question, noting that pretrained models impart "a very strong bias on the kind of behaviors and exploration encountered during RL training" (Section 4.2).

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in how prior work has studied RL for LLM reasoning:

**No controlled comparison across algorithms, reward types, and initializations.** Prior studies typically vary one factor at a time while holding others constant: one paper studies PPO with a specific reward type from a specific initialization; another studies EI with different SFT data from a different model family. The interaction effects—whether algorithmic advantages depend on reward density, model size, or access to SFT data—remain completely unexplored. The paper explicitly designs its experiments to fill this gap by crossing three dimensions: (1) algorithm type (PPO vs. EI vs. RCRL), (2) reward type (sparse ground-truth, dense heuristic, ORM-guided), and (3) model initialization (SFT checkpoint vs. pretrained checkpoint with prompting), across two model sizes (7B and 13B).

**The SFT tradeoff between maj@1 and pass@96 is documented but unexplained.** Cobbe et al. (2021) observed that continued supervised fine-tuning on math word problems improves greedy decoding accuracy (maj@1) but reduces the model's best-of-N performance (pass@96)—a troubling pattern that suggests SFT overfits to a narrow set of solution paths at the expense of output diversity. However, Cobbe et al. did not investigate whether RL training could circumvent this tradeoff, nor did they provide a mechanistic explanation beyond noting the static nature of the SFT dataset. The paper identifies "the limited diversity of the [SFT] dataset as a core reason for this" (Section 4.1) and investigates whether RL's online data generation breaks the tradeoff.

**The role of reward model guidance is confounded with algorithm choice.** Prior work using ORM-guided PPO (e.g., Luo et al., 2023 with WizardMath) typically wraps multiple improvements—better reward signal, on-policy data collection, KL-constrained optimization—into a single package. This makes it impossible to tell whether gains come from the algorithm, the reward model, or their interaction. The paper ablates this by running both EI and PPO with and without ORM guidance, isolating whether reward model benefits are specific to certain algorithms.

**Return-conditioned RL for LLMs is underexplored.** The Decision Transformer (Chen et al., 2021) and related return-conditioned approaches have shown promise in classical offline RL settings, particularly in deterministic environments (Brandfonbrener et al., 2022). But their application to LLM fine-tuning on reasoning tasks has been minimal. The paper includes RCRL as a third algorithmic paradigm to test whether conditioning on desired outcomes (via `[GOOD]`/`[BAD]` step labels) can improve over simply discarding low-reward data as EI does.

**Sample complexity is never measured systematically.** Most RL for LLM papers report final performance but not the trajectory of improvement as a function of environment interactions. This is critical for practical deployment: if an algorithm achieves 2% higher final accuracy but requires 10× more samples to converge, it may still be less desirable than a cheaper alternative. The paper explicitly plots sample complexity curves (Figures 1, 5, 6, 14–17) to enable this comparison.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself not as proposing a new algorithm or a new reward design, but as a **systematic empirical analysis** that disentangles the factors contributing to RL fine-tuning success for LLM reasoning. The authors frame their contribution around four axes that prior work has confounded:

1. **Algorithm choice:** They compare PPO (on-policy, clipped optimization), Expert Iteration (off-policy rejection sampling + SFT), and Return-Conditioned RL (sequence modeling conditioned on desired return) as representatives of distinct RL paradigms that make different assumptions about how to use reward signals.

2. **Reward structure:** They compare sparse rewards (+1 for correct final answer), dense heuristic rewards (matching intermediate steps to a reference solution), and ORM-provided rewards (both sparse outcome scores and dense per-step scores) to test whether richer supervision helps or hurts.

3. **Model initialization:** They study both SFT-initialized models (where the policy already has nontrivial task performance) and pretrained-only models with few-shot prompting (where the policy must bootstrap from near-zero performance), revealing how prior knowledge interacts with algorithmic choice.

4. **Model scale:** They replicate experiments at 7B and 13B parameter counts to test whether algorithmic advantages are consistent across model sizes.

The paper draws on theoretical insights from Brandfonbrener et al. (2022), which shows that in deterministic environments, simple filtered behavior cloning (essentially EI) can be as effective as more complex RL algorithms. The authors explicitly connect this theoretical result to their empirical findings, noting that "the reasoning tasks we consider have entirely deterministic dynamics: a setting in which direct behavior cloning and return conditioned RL is known to do well" (Section 5, Discussion).

Critically, the paper does not claim that EI is always superior to PPO. It explicitly acknowledges that in RLHF settings with unreliable reward models, PPO's KL constraint and clipped objective provide protection against over-optimization that EI lacks: "PPO training penalizes student models diverging from the initial policy via both its clipped objective and additional KL-constraint. In contrast, EI training has no such protection built in" (Section 5). This nuance is central to the paper's contribution: the choice of algorithm depends on the reliability of the reward signal and the exploration demands of the environment, both of which differ between reasoning and preference-alignment tasks.

The paper's ultimate goal is diagnostic rather than prescriptive. By establishing a controlled baseline comparison, the authors aim to identify the **true bottlenecks** limiting further LLM reasoning improvement. Their central hypothesis—that limited exploration during RL fine-tuning, not algorithmic sophistication, is the binding constraint—emerges from multiple converging observations: quickly saturating pass@96 scores, similar sample complexity across EI and PPO, minimal benefit from ORM guidance, and the inability of models to discover solutions beyond what light SFT can achieve. This reframes the research agenda from "which algorithm should we use?" toward "how can we get models to meaningfully explore beyond their pretraining distribution?"

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

The paper constructs an evaluation framework that tests how different reinforcement learning algorithms, reward structures, and model initializations affect an LLM's ability to solve math word problems. The core problem it addresses is that no one knew whether complex on-policy algorithms like PPO were actually necessary for improving reasoning, or whether simpler methods like Expert Iteration (filtering good solutions and doing supervised fine-tuning on them) would work just as well—and under what conditions each approach excels or fails.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The system has four major components:

1. **Base policy (student model):** A Llama-2 7B or 13B model, initialized either from a supervised fine-tuned (SFT) checkpoint or from the pretrained model with few-shot prompting, that generates candidate solutions given a math question.

2. **Reward source:** A mechanism that assigns a scalar quality score to generated solutions—either sparse ground-truth correctness (did the final answer match?), dense heuristic matching (do intermediate steps match a reference solution?), or an Outcome-Based Reward Model (ORM) trained to predict solution correctness from partial prefixes.

3. **Exploration mechanism:** A procedure for generating solution candidates from the current policy, parameterized by sampling temperature, number of rollouts per question, and (for PPO) advantage estimation with a learned value function.

4. **Policy improvement module:** An algorithm-specific update rule that converts exploration data into improved model parameters—either PPO's clipped surrogate objective with KL penalty, Expert Iteration's rejection-sampling-and-SFT pipeline, or Return-Conditioned RL's supervised sequence modeling conditioned on desired return tokens.

Information flows as follows: a batch of questions enters the system → the current policy samples candidate solutions (exploration) → each solution receives a reward from the reward source → the policy improvement module computes parameter updates using algorithm-specific objectives → evaluation metrics (maj@1, maj@96, rerank@96, pass@96) are computed on a held-out test set → the cycle repeats until convergence, with checkpoints selected based on validation performance.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the formal RL framing of reasoning as a Markov Decision Process, which defines the state/action/reward interface that all algorithms share, is explained. This establishes the common mathematical language.

- **Second**, the three algorithms are examined in detail—Expert Iteration, PPO, and Return-Conditioned RL—since their different update mechanisms are what the paper compares, and understanding each one's data generation, filtering, and optimization steps is essential.

- **Third**, the Outcome-Based Reward Model is discussed, as it is the shared learned verifier component used to provide dense or reranking signals across multiple algorithms.

- **Fourth**, the evaluation metrics are explained, because the paper's central finding—that RL improves maj@1 without substantially changing pass@96—hinges on understanding what each metric measures.

- **Fifth**, the training configurations and hyperparameters are documented, since RL training is notoriously sensitive to these choices, and the paper includes ablations on several critical design decisions (value head architecture, LoRA rank, temperature, batch size, KL penalty).

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily an **empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that when fine-tuning LLMs on reasoning tasks with reliable ground-truth rewards, the choice of RL algorithm matters less than previously assumed, and that the primary bottleneck for further improvement is the limited exploration that models engage in beyond their pretraining/SFT distribution.

#### Framing Reasoning as a Markov Decision Process

The paper formalizes each reasoning task as a deterministic Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by a 4-tuple `$(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}_a, \mathcal{R}_a)$`. The formulation is introduced in Section 3:

> "The task τ can be extended to define a Markov Decision Process (MDP) 4-tuple (S, A, Pa, Ra) where tokens serve as both actions and accumulated state with deterministic dynamics."

Let us unpack this:

- **State space `$\mathcal{S}`$:** The accumulated sequence of tokens generated so far. When the model has produced tokens `$t_1, t_2, ..., t_k$` in response to a question `$Q$`, the current state is the concatenation `$[Q; t_1; t_2; ...; t_k]$`. Importantly, the entire generation history is the state—there is no hidden information or partial observability.

- **Action space `$\mathcal{A}`$:** The vocabulary of the language model—every possible next token the model can output. Each action appends one token to the state.

- **Dynamics `$\mathcal{P}_a$`:** Deterministic transitions. Given the current state (the prefix generated so far) and an action (the next token sampled from the policy), the next state is uniquely determined by concatenation. There is no stochasticity in the environment—only in the policy's sampling procedure.

- **Reward function `$\mathcal{R}_a$`:** The paper considers three variants, described in detail below. By default, the reward is `$+1$` if the final answer is correct and `$0$` otherwise, provided only at the terminal state (sparse reward).

The objective of all RL algorithms is to maximize the expected return:

$$\mathbb{E}_{A \sim \pi(Q), (Q, \cdot) \in \tau} \left[ R(A) \right]$$

where `$\pi$` is the student policy, `$Q$` is a question drawn from the task distribution `$\tau$`, `$A$` is a complete solution sampled from the policy, and `$R(A) \in \{0, 1\}$` is the binary correctness reward.

**What this computes:** The average probability that a solution generated by the policy will be correct, taken over questions and sampling randomness. The policy is optimized to maximize this quantity.

**Why this form:** The binary reward captures exactly what we care about—is the answer right?—without any intermediate shaping that might bias the model toward particular solution styles. The expectation over sampling lets the policy trade off between being consistently correct (high probability of a correct solution per sample) and being capable of generating correct solutions at some non-trivial rate even if most samples are wrong (the pass@96 metric captures this latter regime).

The MDP framing is important because it unifies all three algorithms under a common interface: they all sample trajectories from `$\pi$`, observe rewards, and update `$\pi$` to increase expected reward. The differences between them are in *how* they use the collected trajectories to perform that update.

#### Expert Iteration: Rejection Sampling Followed by Supervised Fine-Tuning

Expert Iteration, described in Section 3 under the heading "Expert iteration," works by iteratively constructing an approximation to the optimal policy through rejection sampling and then distilling that approximation back into the model via standard supervised learning.

**The algorithm proceeds in rounds.** At round `$i$`, the current policy `$\pi_{i-1}$` (initialized as `$\pi_0$`, either the SFT checkpoint or the pretrained model) is used to generate a dataset `$\mathcal{D}_i$` through exploration on all training questions. The procedure for exploring from a question `$Q$`:

1. Sample `$K = 96$` complete solutions `$S_1, ..., S_K$` from `$\pi_{i-1}(Q)$` with temperature `$T = 1.0$`. The high temperature and large sample count aim to maximize solution diversity, exploring many different reasoning paths per question.

2. Apply a **rejection filter**: retain only those `$(Q, S_j)$` pairs where the final answer in `$S_j$` is correct (sparse reward `$= 1$`). All incorrect solutions are discarded. The paper states: "we construct D1 by filtering all (Q, Si) pairs with return below a threshold T." Since the reward is binary `$\{0, 1\}$`, the threshold `$T$` is effectively just `$1$`—keep only correct solutions.

3. Apply **de-duplication**: remove duplicate solutions to prevent the model from overfitting to repeated identical reasoning traces. The paper writes: "De-duplication is then performed on the remaining samples."

The result is a curated dataset `$\mathcal{D}_i$` containing only *correct and unique* solutions generated by the current policy. The dataset grows across iterations: `$\mathcal{D}_i = \mathcal{R}_i \cup \mathcal{D}_{i-1}$`, where `$\mathcal{R}_i$` is the new data from round `$i$`. This means the model sees increasingly many examples of correct solutions to each question.

**The policy improvement step is standard supervised fine-tuning.** The new policy `$\pi_i$` is trained to maximize the likelihood of the correct solutions in the curated dataset:

$$\sum_{(Q, A) \in \mathcal{D}_i} \sum_{t=1}^{H} -\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$$

where `$H$` is the length of solution `$A$`, `$s_t$` is the state at step `$t$` (the question plus all preceding tokens), `$a_t$` is the `$t$`-th token of the correct solution, and `$\pi_\theta$` is the policy parameterized by `$\theta$`.

**What this computes:** The standard categorical cross-entropy loss forcing the model to assign high probability to tokens that appeared in correct solutions. The sum is over all tokens in all correct solutions in the current dataset. Minimizing this loss makes the model more likely to generate tokens that have led to correct answers in the past, effectively "distilling" the exploration data into the policy.

**Why this form:** Cross-entropy loss is the maximum-likelihood objective for categorical token prediction, which is the natural supervised objective for autoregressive language models. It treats the filtered exploration data the same way one would treat human-written SFT data—the model is simply learning to imitate correct solutions.

**Crucial implementation detail: model resetting.** When initializing from an SFT checkpoint, the paper explicitly resets training to the **pretrained base model** before each round of EI:

> "Similarly to Zelikman et al. (2022), we find this model resetting is crucial for achieving best performance."

This means the policy `$\pi_i$` at round `$i$` is trained *from scratch* (from the pretrained checkpoint) on the cumulative dataset `$\mathcal{D}_i$`, rather than continuing to fine-tune the SFT model. The authors attribute this to preventing overfitting: each round starts from the broad pretrained distribution and is steered toward correct solutions without the accumulated optimization artifacts of previous rounds. The SFT model is used only as a *proposal distribution* for generating the initial set of correct solutions, after which the pretrained model takes over.

**Inference at convergence.** The paper runs EI for `$n = 2$` rounds when starting from SFT (for both 7B and 13B models), and for `$n = 4$` to `$n = 5$` rounds when starting from pretrained-only checkpoints, where `$n$` is determined by performance saturation on a validation set. The final policy `$\pi_n$` is the one evaluated on the test set.

**Reducing the sample count.** The paper also experiments with `$K = 4$` samples per question instead of `$K = 96$`, finding that this requires more iterations to converge but substantially fewer total samples, with only a minor accuracy penalty. This establishes a practical speed-accuracy tradeoff.

#### Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO): On-Policy Clipped Optimization with KL Constraint

PPO, described in Section 3 under the heading "PPO," is an on-policy RL algorithm that updates the student policy by directly maximizing a clipped surrogate objective function on data collected from the *current* policy.

**The exploration phase.** In each training iteration, the system samples rollouts from the current policy `$\pi_\theta$`:

1. The paper samples `$N = 1024$` total rollouts per batch with temperature `$T = 0.7$` (when starting from SFT) or `$T = 0.2$` (when starting from pretrained, where higher temperature led to collapse).

2. Each question receives `$N = 4$` rollouts, so roughly 256 unique questions are sampled per batch. Each rollout is a complete solution generated autoregressively.

3. Each rollout `$A = (a_0, a_1, ..., a_T)$` receives a reward `$R(A)$` at the terminal state (sparse case) or at intermediate steps (dense case, described below). A learned value function `$V_\phi(s_t)$` estimates the expected future return from each intermediate state, enabling advantage estimation.

**The advantage estimation.** For each state-action pair `$(s_t, a_t)$` in each rollout, PPO requires an estimate of the *advantage*—how much better or worse this action was compared to the average action in this state. The advantage is:

$$\hat{A}_t = Q(s_t, a_t) - V(s_t)$$

where `$Q(s_t, a_t)$` is the actual return obtained after taking action `$a_t$` at state `$s_t$` and following the policy thereafter, and `$V(s_t)$` is the value function's prediction of the expected return from state `$s_t$`. In practice, the advantage is computed using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) or similar methods, though the paper does not specify the exact GAE parameters.

**What this computes:** A signed scalar for each token position in each sampled solution. Positive advantage means the token was part of a solution that ultimately received higher reward than the value function expected from that state—this token is "good" and should be reinforced. Negative advantage means the token led to lower-than-expected outcomes—this token is "bad" and should be suppressed.

**Why this form:** The advantage subtracts out the baseline `$V(s_t)$`, which reduces variance in the policy gradient. Without the baseline, the gradient signal would fluctuate wildly depending on whether the current question was easy or hard; subtracting the value function removes this question-difficulty confound.

**The PPO objective (Section 3, quoted verbatim from Schulman et al., 2017):**

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min\left( \frac{\pi(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)} \hat{A}_t, \ \text{clip}\left(1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon, \frac{\pi(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}\right) \hat{A}_t \right) \right]$$

where `$\pi(a_t \mid s_t)$` is the probability the current policy assigns to action `$a_t$`, `$\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)$` is the probability the *old* policy (used to collect the rollout) assigned to the same action, `$\hat{A}_t$` is the estimated advantage, and `$\epsilon$` is the clipping parameter (typically 0.2 in standard PPO, though the paper does not specify the exact value used).

**What this computes:** The `$\min$` operation creates a conservative update rule. The first term inside the min, `$\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{old}}} \hat{A}_t$`, is the standard policy gradient objective—increase the probability of actions with positive advantage and decrease the probability of actions with negative advantage, proportional to how much the advantage deviates from zero. The second term, `$\text{clip}(1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon, \frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{old}}}) \hat{A}_t$`, constrains the ratio `$\frac{\pi}{\pi_{\text{old}}}$` to the interval `$[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$`, preventing any single update from changing the policy too drastically. The `$\min$` ensures the objective is a lower bound on the unclipped objective, which is the key insight from Schulman et al. (2017) that makes PPO stable.

**Why this form:** The clipping prevents catastrophic policy collapse. In high-dimensional action spaces like language, a single bad gradient update can dramatically increase the probability of degenerate outputs (gibberish, repetitive tokens) from which recovery is difficult. The clipped objective ensures that the policy changes slowly enough to maintain output quality. This is particularly important because the "unnatural distribution of text found in the reasoning tasks which consist of broken natural language and computations enclosed in <<x+y=z>> tags" (Section 4.3) is fragile—the model can easily drift into generating malformed solutions.

**The value function loss.** In parallel with the policy objective, PPO trains a value function `$V_\phi$` to minimize the mean squared error between its predictions and the actual returns:

$$\mathcal{L}_V(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \left( V_\phi(s_t) - R_t \right)^2 \right]$$

where `$R_t$` is the discounted return from state `$s_t$`.

**Joint architecture with gradient detachment (Section 4.3).** The policy and value functions share a common transformer backbone with separate output heads. The paper found that **detaching the value head gradients from the policy backbone** is critical:

> "Without detachment we found value gradients interfere with policy gradients, as similarly observed in Stiennon et al. (2020), causing instability with a big update to either branch."

This means the value function's loss is backpropagated only into the value-specific parameters (the value head and possibly a few shared layers, depending on where the detachment point is), while the policy loss backpropagates into the full backbone and policy head. The shared representation still benefits from both signals, but the gradients from one head cannot directly perturb the other's optimization trajectory.

**Value branch depth (Section 4.3).** The paper found it important to use a relatively large value branch:

> "We found it important to use a relatively large value branch (L=4 transformer layers)."

Four additional transformer layers are appended specifically for value prediction, separate from the policy head. This gives the value function sufficient capacity to model expected returns across diverse solution prefixes without competing with the policy for representational capacity in the shared layers.

**KL penalty (Section 4.3).** An additional KL divergence penalty is added to the PPO objective:

> "We also found a non-trivial KL penalty of 0.05 to be critical for preventing model collapse after more than a hundred gradient updates."

The penalty term is `$-\beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$`, where `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is the initial policy (the SFT checkpoint or the pretrained model with prompts), and `$\beta = 0.05$`. This encourages the policy to stay close to its initialization in terms of token-level output distribution, providing an additional safeguard beyond the PPO clip.

**Why this matters:** The paper notes that Bai et al. (2022) did not find a KL penalty necessary for Constitutional AI, which operates in natural language dialogue. The difference, the authors hypothesize, is that reasoning tasks contain structured mathematical notation and computation tags that are "unnatural" relative to the pretraining distribution. Drift in this structured space is more fragile—slight perturbations can produce syntactically broken outputs—so the KL penalty is more critical.

**Training hyperparameters.** Training uses LoRA with rank `$r = 128$`, a batch size of 256, a learning rate of `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, and runs for 4000 gradient steps. Each batch of 1024 rollouts is used for `$K = 4$` PPO epochs before being discarded (on-policy means new data must be collected after each policy update cycle). The best checkpoint is selected via validation set performance.

**Best K of N (KoN) sampling (Section 4.3).** The paper experiments with keeping only the best `$K$` of `$N$` rollouts per question:

> "Choosing parameters K << N prioritize high reward samples and discard low reward ones, resulting in a training distribution more similar to the curated EI dataset."

However, the paper found that aggressive filtering (e.g., `$K=1, N=8$`) actually decreased performance compared to keeping all samples (`$K=N=1$`). The best configuration was `$K=4, N=4$`, which keeps all samples per question (since `$N=4$` per question by default). Small `$K/N$` ratios also proportionally increase the number of rollouts that must be generated (e.g., `$K=1, N=8$` requires generating 8 times as many rollouts to fill a batch of size `$K$`), making training substantially slower.

#### Return-Conditioned RL: Supervised Learning on Relabeled Trajectories

Return-Conditioned RL, described in Section 3 under the heading "Return Conditioned RL," treats reinforcement learning as a sequence modeling problem: the model is trained to predict actions (tokens) conditioned on both the current state and a *desired return* token, typically `[GOOD]` or `[BAD]`.

**The training data construction pipeline.** The dataset `$\mathcal{D}$` is built from the best Expert Iteration-trained policy `$\pi_{EI}$`:

1. For each question `$Q$` in the training set, the EI policy generates one or more complete solutions `$S = (S_1, S_2, ..., S_L)$`, where each `$S_i$` is a "step" (a line of reasoning ending with a newline or sentence boundary). Importantly, the notion of a "step" is heuristic: "we can simply regard each step as ending with a sentence or newline" (Section 4).

2. For each intermediate prefix `$P_i = (S_1, S_2, ..., S_i)$` within a solution, the system generates **step-level labels**. This is done by:
   - Taking the prefix `$P_i$` (the question plus the first `$i$` steps)
   - Using the EI policy to generate `$K$` completions from `$P_i$` (completing the solution)
   - Evaluating each completion's final correctnes
   - Computing the average correctness across the `$K$` completions: `$\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \ell_k$` where `$\ell_k \in \{0, 1\}$` is whether completion `$k$` was correct

3. A step `$S_i$` is labeled `[GOOD]` if the average correctness exceeds a threshold `$T = 0.5$`, and `[BAD]` otherwise. Formally:
   
   $$\text{label}(S_i \mid P_{i-1}) = \begin{cases} \texttt{[GOOD]} & \text{if } \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K \ell_k \geq T \\ \texttt{[BAD]} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

   where `$P_{i-1}$` is the prefix before step `$S_i$` and `$\ell_k$` is the correctness of the `$k$`-th completion.

**What this computes:** A heuristic estimate of the *value* of an intermediate state—if many completions from this prefix are correct, the prefix is "on track" and the step that led to it is labeled `[GOOD]`. If few or no completions succeed, the prefix contains a mistake and the step is labeled `[BAD]`. This is essentially a Monte Carlo estimate of `$V^*(Q, P_{i-1}, S_i)$`—the optimal value function that would be 1 for prefixes that can still lead to a correct answer and 0 for prefixes that already contain a fatal error.

**Why this form:** The binary labeling with threshold 0.5 provides a clean supervised signal: each step is either good or bad. The threshold at 0.5 is a natural midpoint—if more than half of completions succeed, the step is probably good; if fewer than half succeed, the step probably contains a mistake. Alternative formulations (continuous value prediction, multi-class labeling) would be more complex to train and sample from.

**Training objective.** Once the dataset `$\mathcal{D}$` of `$(s_t, a_t, g_t)$` tuples is constructed (where `$g_t \in \{\texttt{[GOOD]}, \texttt{[BAD]}\}$` is the return token for step `$t$`), the model is trained with standard cross-entropy loss:

$$\sum_{\tau \in \mathcal{D}} \sum_{t=1}^{H} -\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t, g_t)$$

**What this computes:** The model learns to predict the next token given the current state AND the desired return token. During training, it sees `[GOOD]`-conditioned trajectories where the label was `[GOOD]` and the token sequence led to a correct answer, and `[BAD]`-conditioned trajectories where the label was `[BAD]` and the sequence led to a wrong answer.

**Why this form:** The return token acts as a *goal specification* at inference time. To get the model to produce correct solutions, one conditions on the `[GOOD]` token during sampling. The model has learned from training that `[GOOD]` is associated with high-quality solution tokens and `[BAD]` with low-quality ones. This is the same principle as Decision Transformers (Chen et al., 2021), where conditioning on high return makes the model generate high-return trajectories.

**Dataset balancing (Appendix A).** By default, the dataset is balanced to have equal numbers of `[GOOD]` and `[BAD]` examples. The paper experimented with different ratios and found that **increasing the proportion of positive examples** improved performance, while a 1:1 balance (the default) gave the worst results:

> "A balanced ratio yields the worst performance, with increasing the amount of positive data leading to better results."

This suggests the 7B model does not effectively learn from negative demonstrations—it cannot extract useful information about what *not* to do from the `[BAD]`-labeled trajectories. This is explored in Appendix A (Table 5).

**Inference behavior.** At test time, the model is sampled one step at a time, conditioning on `[GOOD]`. The paper notes an interesting failure mode: "when sampling the RCRL model unconditionally the model often generates the perfectly valid steps following a ‘[BAD]’ label resulting in a correct final answer." This means the model does not truly internalize the distinction between good and bad steps—it can generate correct solutions even when conditioned on `[BAD]`, indicating the return conditioning signal is not strongly shaping the model's behavior.

**Why RCRL underperforms EI.** The paper attributes RCRL's underperformance to its inability to usefully incorporate information from partially correct solutions:

> "These results suggest RCRL models are unable to usefully incorporate information from partially correct solutions at train time."

The key difference from EI: EI simply discards incorrect solutions entirely, training only on correct ones. RCRL tries to extract signal from both correct and incorrect solutions by labeling them differently. But if the model cannot learn what distinguishes `[GOOD]` from `[BAD]`, then including the negative data just adds noise without benefit, and removing the negative data (as the positive-skewed ratios effectively do) brings RCRL back toward EI's approach.

#### Outcome-Based Reward Model (ORM)

The ORM, described in Section 3 under "Outcome Based Reward Modeling," is a learned verifier that predicts, given a partial solution prefix, the probability that the completed solution will be correct.

**Training data generation.** Using a student policy `$\pi$` (typically the best EI-trained policy), the system generates `$K$` solutions per question in the training set. For each solution, at each intermediate step `$P_i = (S_1, ..., S_i)$`, a training example is created: the input is the question `$Q$` and the prefix `$P_i$`, and the target label is whether the *final* answer of the complete solution was correct (1) or incorrect (0).

**Training objective.** The ORM is trained as a binary classifier:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{ORM}} = -\left[ y \log(p) + (1 - y) \log(1 - p) \right]$$

where `$y \in \{0, 1\}$` is whether the completed solution containing this prefix was correct, and `$p = p(\text{is correct} \mid Q, P_i)$` is the ORM's predicted probability.

**What this computes:** Standard binary cross-entropy between the predicted correctness probability and the binary ground-truth label. The ORM learns to estimate, from any prefix of a solution, whether the path is on track to reach the correct answer.

**Why this form:** The binary classification framing matches the structure of the problem: each solution is either correct or incorrect, and the ORM's job is to estimate the probability of correctness from incomplete evidence. This is exactly the same framing as Cobbe et al. (2021), though the paper does not provide detailed ORM training hyperparameters (architecture, learning rate, etc.).

**Usage at inference time (rerank@96).** The ORM is used for reranking: given 96 candidate solutions to a question, the ORM scores each one (by taking the predicted probability at the final step, or by aggregating across steps), and the solution with the highest score is selected as the final answer. This is the `rerank@96` metric described in the evaluation section.

**ORM as a reward signal during RL.** For ORM-guided PPO, the ORM provides a dense reward at each step: the ORM's predicted correctness probability at that step becomes the reward signal. This converts the sparse ground-truth reward (available only at the end) into a dense per-step signal, which should theoretically reduce credit assignment difficulty. For ORM-guided EI, the ORM can be used as an additional filter (keeping solutions above a certain ORM score threshold) or as a reranker on top of the ground-truth filter.

**The distribution shift problem.** The paper notes an important subtlety: the ORM is trained on data from the EI policy. When used to guide PPO—which produces a different distribution of solutions—the ORM's predictions may become less reliable due to distribution shift. This is a standard challenge in reward model-guided RL and may partially explain why ORM guidance provides minimal benefit in the paper's experiments.

#### Sparse, Dense, and ORM-Guided Reward Variants

The paper experiments with three reward schemes, described across Section 3 and the results tables:

**Sparse reward (default).** The model receives `$+1$` if the final answer extracted from the generated solution matches the ground-truth answer, and `$0$` otherwise. The reward is provided only at the terminal token of the solution. All intermediate tokens receive zero reward.

**Dense heuristic reward.** The model's intermediate steps are compared against a reference solution (from the SFT training set). If a generated step matches the corresponding step in the reference solution, a positive reward is given at that step. The paper does not provide exact matching criteria (exact string match? semantic equivalence?), but the key finding is that this dense reward **harms performance** relative to the sparse baseline:

> "Giving a heuristic dense reward even slightly harms model performance relative to the sparse setting... This likely encourages more overfit to exact solutions in the train set, limiting solution diversity."

The mechanism: dense heuristic rewards reward the model for mimicking the specific solution paths in the training data, which reduces exploration and penalizes novel but valid approaches. For reasoning tasks, there are often multiple valid solution paths to the same answer; a dense reward that privileges one reference path suppresses this diversity.

**ORM-guided reward (sparse and dense variants).** The ORM provides learned reward signals:
- **Sparse ORM:** The ORM's predicted correctness probability at the final step is used as the reward (replacing the binary ground-truth signal with a continuous proxy).
- **Dense ORM:** The ORM's predicted correctness probability at each intermediate step provides a per-step dense reward.

For both PPO and EI, ORM guidance **improves sample efficiency** (faster convergence in terms of number of rollouts) but does not improve final performance:

> "The ORM improves sample efficiency but not performance." (Figure 3 caption)

This is a nuanced finding: the ORM helps the model learn faster by providing a smoother reward landscape, but the final policy quality is bottlenecked by the model's underlying reasoning capability and exploration behavior, not by the quality of the reward signal. Since the ground-truth reward is already perfectly reliable, the ORM's benefit is limited to reducing credit assignment noise—but the paper's observation that models converge quickly even with sparse rewards (only ~60,000 rollouts) suggests credit assignment is not the primary bottleneck.

#### Evaluation Metrics: What maj@1, maj@96, rerank@96, and pass@96 Actually Measure

The paper evaluates models using four metrics, defined in Section 1 and used throughout all results tables:

**maj@1 (greedy accuracy).** The model is queried once per test question with greedy decoding (temperature 0, always selecting the highest-probability next token). The fraction of questions where this single greedy sample produces the correct final answer is the maj@1 score.

**What it measures:** The model's "best guess" behavior—if you could only ask the model once, how often would it be right? This is the metric that matters for most practical deployments (chatbots, assistants) where users expect the first answer.

**maj@96 (majority vote at 96 samples).** For each test question, the model generates `$K = 96$` independent solutions at temperature `$T = 0.7$`. The final answer that appears most frequently across the 96 samples is selected. This requires no ground-truth labels or verifier—it is a form of self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023) where consensus across samples serves as a quality signal.

**What it measures:** How well the model's sampling distribution clusters around the correct answer. If the correct answer is the modal output (even if it only appears, say, 40% of the time but no other answer appears more often), maj@96 will be high. This rewards models that generate the correct answer with reasonably high probability and generate diverse incorrect answers (so no single wrong answer dominates).

**rerank@96 (ORM-based selection at 96 samples).** The model generates 96 solutions per question at `$T = 0.7$`. The ORM scores each solution, and the solution with the highest ORM score is selected. The ORM is trained on the EI policy's outputs as described above.

**What it measures:** The model's ability to generate at least one solution that the verifier recognizes as likely correct, plus the verifier's reliability. This metric can be inflated if the verifier is trained on the same model's outputs (as the paper notes: "we believe a non-trivial percentage of this gap is due to overfit of the ORM to the EI model which was used to generate its training data").

**pass@96 (best-of-96 with ground-truth oracle).** The model generates 96 solutions per question at `$T = 0.7$`. A question is considered "passed" if **any** of the 96 samples produces the correct final answer (as judged by the ground-truth answer). The pass@96 score is the fraction of test questions passed.

**What it measures:** The model's capability ceiling—given enough attempts, can the model ever produce the right answer? This metric isolates the model's reasoning *potential* from its consistency. A model might have a low maj@1 (rarely produces the correct answer on the first try) but high pass@96 (can produce it eventually, just with low probability).

**The tradeoff with SFT training (Section 4.1, Table 4).** The key empirical observation is that continued SFT training creates a divergence between these metrics: "SFT2" (SFT for 2 epochs) has `maj@1 = 0.36` and `pass@96 = 0.76`, while "SFT4" (SFT for 4 epochs) has higher `maj@1 = 0.41` but lower `pass@96 = 0.72`. This means that **more SFT training improves greedy accuracy at the cost of reducing the model's ability to generate diverse correct solutions.** The mechanism: SFT trains on a fixed dataset of correct solutions, and with enough epochs, the model overfits to the specific solution paths in that dataset, losing the ability to generate alternative correct approaches that weren't in the training data.

The paper measures average unique correct solutions per question on the training set: SFT2 produces 3.7 unique correct solutions per question when sampled 96 times, while SFT4 produces only 2.9. The reduction in diversity directly corresponds to the drop in pass@96.

**How RL mitigates this tradeoff (Section 4.1, Table 4).** PPO fine-tuning from SFT4 improves both metrics simultaneously: `maj@1` goes from 0.41 to 0.44 while `pass@96` goes from 0.72 to 0.77. Similarly, EI achieves high scores on both metrics. The reason, the paper argues, is that RL generates its own training data online, introducing diversity that the static SFT dataset lacks. This is explained in the Discussion:

> "We believe RL fine-tuning is less prone to overfitting when compared to static SFT fine-tuning precisely because of the exploration process which generates its own training data. This results in more diverse solution paths than the SFT training set, ameliorating overfit."

However, the paper also finds that RL's pass@96 scores are not substantially higher than what *light* SFT can achieve (SFT2 achieves pass@96 = 0.76, very close to the best PPO model's 0.80 and the EI model's 0.80). This is a critical finding: RL does not expand the model's capability frontier beyond what is already latent in the pretrained/SFT model; it mainly prevents degradation of output diversity during training and improves consistency (maj@1).

#### Training Configurations and Sensitivity to Hyperparameters

Section 4.3 provides extensive detail on the hyperparameter sensitivity of RL training for LLMs.

**PPO joint architecture (Section 4.3).** The policy and value heads share a common transformer backbone with separate output heads. The value branch contains `$L = 4$` additional transformer layers beyond the shared backbone. **Gradient detachment** from the value branch to the policy trunk is essential: "Without detachment we found value gradients interfere with policy gradients... causing instability with a big update to either branch." Figure 7 compares the default configuration with a version lacking gradient detachment, showing substantially lower and more unstable maj@1 for the non-detached variant.

**LoRA fine-tuning (Section 4.3).** The paper uses LoRA with `$r = 128$` rather than full fine-tuning. The authors note this "helped significantly to further stabilize a full layer fine-tuning while still maintaining performance," citing Sun et al. (2023). LoRA's low-rank adaptation constrains the effective parameter update space, acting as an implicit regularizer that prevents catastrophic forgetting and policy collapse.

**Batch size and learning rate (Section 4.3).** PPO uses a batch size of 256 and learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`. The small learning rate combined with the PPO clip and KL penalty creates a conservative optimization regime. EI uses the same SFT hyperparameters as initial SFT training: global batch size 128, initial learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-5}$` decayed to `$2 \times 10^{-7}$` with a cosine warmup schedule.

**Partial fine-tuning ablation (Section 4.3).** The paper experimented with fine-tuning only the top `$M$` layers of the model. This "saved memory but at the cost of a few percentage points of performance," indicating that full model access (via LoRA) is beneficial for reasoning improvements, even though it is more expensive.

**KL penalty magnitude (Section 4.3).** A KL penalty coefficient of `$0.05$` was critical for PPO:

> "We also found a non-trivial KL penalty of 0.05 to be critical for preventing model collapse after more than a hundred gradient updates."

This is in contrast to Bai et al. (2022), who did not find the KL penalty necessary for Constitutional AI's dialogue tasks. The paper attributes the difference to the "unnatural distribution of text found in the reasoning tasks," which includes mathematical notation and computation tags that are out-of-distribution relative to the pretraining corpus. Drift in this space is more fragile than drift in natural language dialogue.

**Exploration temperature (Section 4.3).** The optimal temperature depends on the model initialization:
- **From SFT:** `$T = 0.7$` for PPO and `$T = 1.0$` for EI. The higher EI temperature maximizes solution diversity during the exploration phase, since EI filters out incorrect solutions anyway and benefits from seeing as many distinct correct solutions as possible.
- **From pretrained:** `$T = 0.2$` for PPO. Higher temperatures from a pretrained (non-SFT) initialization "often results in collapse"—the model hasn't learned the task structure yet and high-temperature sampling produces mostly garbage tokens that don't recover to valid solutions. The paper notes that annealing the temperature during training might improve results but did not experiment with this.

**Best K of N sampling (Section 4.3).** The paper found that keeping a subset of the highest-reward rollouts per question (Best K of N, or KoN) did not improve over keeping all rollouts. The best configuration was `$K = 4, N = 4$` (keep all samples), while more aggressive filtering (e.g., `$K = 1, N = 8$`) decreased performance. Figure 8 plots maj@1 against different K/N ratios, showing that small K/N ratios (aggressive filtering) underperform the default. The authors also note the computational cost: small K/N ratios require proportionally more total rollouts to fill a training batch of size K, making training slower without accuracy benefits.

**Model size and initialization effects on exploration (Section 4.3).** The paper observes qualitative differences in exploration behavior:
- Larger models (13B vs. 7B) generate more diverse solutions, as measured by both exact string match diversity and "trace diversity" (diversity of intermediate computation steps, ignoring surface-level differences in phrasing). Figures 11 and 12 quantify this: the 13B model produces significantly more unique solutions per question across all rounds of EI.
- Models with better generalization (pretrained models that haven't been overfitted via SFT) engage in more diverse exploration. This directly impacts RL improvement: "models engaging in more diverse exploration improving more from RL training."

**Curriculum learning experiments (Appendix D).** The paper attempted two curriculum learning methods—**Backtracking** and **Prioritized Level Replay (PLR)**—neither of which outperformed default PPO.

Backtracking works by initializing rollouts from intermediate ground-truth steps: given a prefix `$P_i$` of a reference solution, the model completes the solution from that point. Initially, the model starts near the end of the reference solution (threshold `$\tau_0 = 0.9$`, meaning it starts from the 90% completion point), and as it succeeds at completing from that prefix, the starting point is moved earlier (to `$P_{i-1}$`, etc.), gradually increasing difficulty.

PLR prioritizes training questions with high "learning potential," measured by the average absolute advantage—questions that are neither trivially easy (where advantage is near zero because the model always gets them right) nor impossibly hard (where advantage is also near zero because the model has no signal).

Neither method exceeded default PPO performance. The paper hypothesizes this is because "the limited exploration the model engages in from the start, due to both pretraining and supervised fine-tuning," means the curriculum doesn't provide additional benefit beyond what the model's existing knowledge already enables.

**Data augmentation via backtranslation (Appendix E).** The paper experimented with generating synthetic `$(Q, A)$` pairs by training a backward model `$A \to Q$` that generates questions from answers, and a forward generator `$A \to A$` that produces alternative answers. The synthetic pairs were scored by checking how often the student model recovered the intended answer. Pairs with scores in the range `$(\frac{1}{2} - \tau, \frac{1}{2} + \tau)$` (neither too easy nor too hard) were kept for training. However, all synthetic data configurations **degraded performance relative to the SFT baseline** (Table 6). Manual inspection revealed a high rate of false positives—the generator produced nonsensical questions (e.g., a question where the percentages sum to 120%), but the student model replicated the same error, scoring the pair as valid. Using a larger, more capable model as the verifier might address this, but was not attempted.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Model resetting during EI:** Start each round from the pretrained checkpoint rather than continuing to fine-tune, because this prevents overfitting to the specific solution paths found in previous rounds and preserves output diversity.

- **LoRA with `$r = 128$` for PPO:** Stabilizes training compared to full fine-tuning while maintaining performance, acting as an implicit regularizer against catastrophic policy updates.

- **Value branch gradient detachment:** Prevents value function gradients from interfering with policy gradients, a known instability in joint actor-critic architectures for language tasks (Stiennon et al., 2020).

- **KL penalty of 0.05 for PPO:** Stronger than typically used in dialogue RLHF but necessary because reasoning tasks contain structured notation where small distributional drift produces syntactically broken outputs.

- **High temperature for EI exploration (`$T = 1.0$`):** Maximizes solution diversity subject to generating mostly valid outputs, since incorrect solutions are filtered out anyway.

- **Conservative temperature for PPO from pretrained (`$T = 0.2$`):** Prevents collapse into degenerate token sequences before the model has learned the task structure.

- **Defaulting to `$K = N = 4$` for PPO rollouts:** Aggressive filtering (`$K \ll N$`) reduces performance because it discards negative data that PPO uses for advantage estimation and because small ratios increase total rollout requirements without accuracy benefits.

- **Balanced positive/negative data for RCRL, but experimentally finding positive-skewed data works better:** The model does not effectively learn from negative demonstrations, suggesting EI's approach of simply discarding negative data is more practical.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Algorithm Choice Matters Less Than the Exploration Prior — The "All RL Algorithms Are Comparable" Finding

The paper's most provocative contribution is not a new method but a **diagnostic null result**: when fine-tuning LLMs on reasoning tasks with reliable ground-truth rewards, the choice of RL algorithm — whether sophisticated on-policy optimization (PPO), simple rejection-sampling-and-SFT (Expert Iteration), or return-conditioned sequence modeling — matters surprisingly little for final performance. Expert Iteration edges out PPO in most settings, but the gap is modest (roughly 2–4 percentage points on GSM8K maj@1, Table 1), and all methods plateau at similar pass@96 ceilings (within 3% of each other, Tables 1 and 4).

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental refinement. Prior work operated under the implicit assumption that algorithmic sophistication would translate to reasoning gains — that PPO's advantage estimation, clipped surrogate objective, and on-policy data collection would provide meaningful benefits over simpler behavior cloning approaches, just as they do in classical RL domains like game-playing from scratch. The RLHF literature reinforced this assumption: PPO became the default for preference alignment (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022), and its adoption for reasoning tasks (Luo et al., 2023) carried forward without systematic justification.

What the paper demonstrates is that this assumption **doesn't hold when the policy starts from a strong pretrained prior**. The pretrained LLM already encodes substantial knowledge about mathematical reasoning patterns from its training corpus. RL fine-tuning, regardless of algorithm, primarily reorganizes and surfaces this latent knowledge rather than discovering genuinely new solution strategies. This explains why:
- EI and PPO have comparable sample complexity (both converging in ~60,000 rollouts, Figures 1, 5, 6)
- ORM guidance provides at most a sample efficiency boost without improving final performance (Figures 3, 4)
- Dense heuristic rewards *hurt* performance by reducing solution diversity (Table 1: Dense PPO underperforms Sparse PPO)
- The maximum pass@96 scores remain close to what light SFT can achieve (Table 4: SFT2 pass@96 = 0.76 vs. best PPO pass@96 = 0.80)

The theoretical underpinning comes from Brandfonbrener et al. (2022), which showed that in deterministic environments, filtered behavior cloning (essentially EI) can match more complex RL algorithms. The paper empirically validates this prediction in the LLM reasoning domain and adds a crucial layer: the pretrained model acts as an implicit exploration prior that further compresses the advantage of on-policy methods. PPO's primary strength — efficient exploration and credit assignment in complex stochastic environments — is largely irrelevant when the policy already knows (in some latent sense) how to solve the task and when the environment dynamics are deterministic.

**Evidence:** Tables 1 and 2 show EI achieving best maj@1 in 6 of 8 comparisons (SFT initialization: 0.48/0.53 for 7B/13B vs. PPO's best of 0.46/0.51; pretrained initialization on GSM8K: PPO at 0.32/0.41 vs. EI at 0.31/0.40, essentially tied). Pass@96 scores across algorithms differ by at most 3 percentage points (Table 4: PPO4 pass@96 = 0.77, PPO2 pass@96 = 0.80, EI pass@96 = 0.80). Figure 1 shows EI and PPO convergence curves tracking each other closely.

---

### Innovation 2: RL Breaks the SFT Tradeoff Between maj@1 and pass@96 — But Doesn't Expand the Capability Frontier

The paper provides a **mechanistic explanation for a previously observed but unexplained phenomenon** — the tradeoff between greedy accuracy (maj@1) and best-of-N accuracy (pass@96) during supervised fine-tuning — and shows that RL circumvents this tradeoff through online data generation. However, it simultaneously establishes a sobering boundary: RL does not expand the model's fundamental capability frontier beyond what is already latent in a lightly fine-tuned checkpoint.

**What the field knew before:** Cobbe et al. (2021) first documented that continued SFT on math word problems improves maj@1 while degrading pass@96, but they did not investigate the mechanism or whether RL could help. The tradeoff was treated as an inconvenient fact about SFT — more training improves consistency at the cost of diversity — without a clear path to resolution.

**What this paper contributes:** The paper identifies the root cause as **dataset-induced loss of output diversity**. It quantifies this directly: SFT trained for 2 epochs (SFT2) produces on average 3.7 unique correct solutions per training question when sampled 96 times, while SFT trained for 4 epochs (SFT4) produces only 2.9 unique correct solutions. The mechanism is clear — with enough epochs on a static dataset, the model overfits to the specific solution templates in the training data, collapsing its output distribution onto those patterns and losing the ability to generate alternative correct reasoning paths that weren't represented.

RL training — both PPO and EI — breaks this tradeoff because **the exploration process generates its own training data online**. Each batch of rollouts introduces new solution paths (some correct, some incorrect) that the static SFT dataset never contained. This continuously refreshes the model's output diversity, preventing the distributional collapse that plagues extended SFT. Table 4 quantifies the effect: PPO fine-tuning from SFT4 raises both maj@1 (0.41 → 0.44) and pass@96 (0.72 → 0.77) simultaneously.

**But the deeper finding is the ceiling.** Despite breaking the tradeoff, RL does not substantially exceed the pass@96 scores achievable with *light* SFT alone. SFT2 achieves pass@96 = 0.76, while the best PPO model reaches 0.80 and the best EI model reaches 0.80 — a gain of only 4 percentage points over a model trained for half the epochs. This means RL's apparent pass@96 improvement over SFT4 (which falls to 0.72) is mostly **recovering diversity that extended SFT destroyed**, not discovering solutions that were never within the model's reach.

**Why this is a conceptual advance, not just a training trick:** It reframes what RL fine-tuning is actually doing. The field's aspirational narrative — drawn from AlphaGo and similar successes — is that RL enables models to discover qualitatively new behaviors through exploration. The paper's evidence suggests the opposite for current LLM reasoning: RL is best understood as a **regularization mechanism** that allows longer effective training by preventing overfitting to a fixed dataset, rather than as a genuine discovery engine. This boundary condition — that RL reorganizes but does not expand capability — is critical for calibrating expectations about what RL fine-tuning can and cannot achieve.

**Evidence:** Table 4 directly compares SFT2, SFT4, PPO2, and PPO4, showing the tradeoff and its resolution. Figures 11 and 12 quantify solution diversity trajectories across EI rounds, demonstrating that diversity increases initially (as the model learns to generate correct solutions in multiple ways) then plateaus or declines (as overfitting to the EI dataset begins). The training set statistics (3.7 vs. 2.9 unique correct solutions) appear in Section 4.3.

---

### Innovation 3: Limited Exploration During RL Fine-Tuning — A Unifying Diagnosis Across Multiple Failure Modes

The paper's most impactful conceptual contribution is a **unifying diagnostic framework** that explains why all the algorithms behave similarly, why ORM guidance provides minimal benefit, why dense rewards hurt, and why the capability ceiling is so stubborn. The diagnosis: **LLMs fine-tuned from strong pretrained initializations do not engage in the kind of open-ended exploration that makes RL transformative in other domains.** The pretrained prior — which makes learning possible at all in a high-dimensional text action space — simultaneously constrains the policy to a narrow region of solution space that was already accessible (at some probability) before any RL training.

This is a **fundamental insight about the interaction between pretraining and RL**, not an incremental observation about hyperparameter tuning. The field has implicitly assumed that RL applied to LLMs would function similarly to RL applied to neural networks trained from scratch — exploration discovers novel behaviors, algorithms differ in how efficiently they explore, and better exploration leads to higher performance ceilings. The paper systematically dismantles this assumption through converging lines of evidence:

**Evidence thread 1 — saturating pass@96 early in training.** If models were genuinely exploring and discovering new solution strategies during RL, pass@96 (the capability ceiling) would continue to improve throughout training. Instead, Table 4 shows that pass@96 is already near its ceiling with light SFT (0.76), and Figures 1, 5, and 6 show that pass@96 plateaus quickly — well before maj@1 stops improving. This means the models' initial rollouts already contain most of the correct solutions they will ever produce; RL training primarily increases the *probability* of generating those solutions (improving maj@1) rather than finding new ones.

**Evidence thread 2 — comparable sample complexity across algorithms.** If PPO's on-policy advantage estimation and exploration mechanisms were providing substantive benefits over EI's simpler rejection sampling, PPO should require substantially fewer samples to converge. Instead, both methods converge in roughly the same number of rollouts (Figure 1: ~60,000 for both methods from SFT initialization; Figure 5: ~30,000 for both from pretrained on GSM8K). This is what one would expect if exploration is minimal and the primary learning signal is simply "reinforce solutions that get the right answer" — a signal that both EI's filtering and PPO's advantage weighting capture equally well.

**Evidence thread 3 — minimal benefit from richer reward signals.** Dense heuristic rewards that match intermediate steps to a reference solution *degrade* performance (Table 1: Dense PPO at 0.43 vs. Sparse PPO at 0.44 for 7B). ORM-guided rewards improve sample efficiency slightly but not final accuracy (Figure 3: ORM EI and default EI converge to similar final maj@1). If models were genuinely exploring novel solution paths, richer reward signals would provide valuable intermediate feedback for credit assignment. Their failure to improve final performance suggests that the model is already sampling from a narrow enough distribution that the sparse terminal reward provides sufficient signal.

**Evidence thread 4 — larger models explore more, and benefit more.** The paper documents that 13B models produce significantly more diverse solutions than 7B models (Figures 11, 12) and that SFT2 (less overfit, more diverse) benefits more from RL than SFT4. This correlation between pretrained diversity and RL improvement directly supports the exploration-bottleneck hypothesis: the limiting factor is how broadly the policy can sample from its pretrained knowledge, not how cleverly an algorithm uses the sampled data.

**Why this changes the research agenda:** If exploration is the binding constraint, then algorithmic improvements to RL (better advantage estimation, better clipping, better reward shaping) will yield diminishing returns. The frontier shifts to methods that explicitly encourage the model to generate solutions outside its current high-probability region — more sophisticated prompting strategies like Tree of Thoughts (Yao et al., 2023), evolutionary algorithms that maintain population diversity (Lehman et al., 2022), or architectural modifications that decouple the exploration policy from the exploitation policy. The paper explicitly points toward this reframing: "We view the discovery of new techniques encouraging complex, rich exploration of reasoning problems as fundamental to progress in LLM reasoning capability" (Section 5).

**Evidence:** The exploration diagnosis is synthesized in the Discussion (Section 5) from multiple results: Figures 1, 5, 6 (sample complexity), Figures 3, 4 (ORM benefit), Figures 11, 12 (diversity trajectories), Table 4 (pass@96 ceilings), and Section 4.3 (model size and initialization effects).

### Innovation 4: PPO's Advantage Over EI Depends on Reward Reliability — A Boundary Condition for Algorithm Selection

The paper identifies a previously unarticulated **boundary condition** that explains the apparent contradiction between RLHF results (where PPO dominates) and reasoning results (where EI matches or exceeds PPO). The distinction turns on **reward reliability**: when the reward signal is a perfect ground-truth indicator of success (as in math word problems where the answer is either right or wrong), EI's simple rejection-sampling approach is essentially optimal — it discards all trajectories that didn't succeed and imitates those that did, which is exactly the right thing to do when "success" is unambiguous. But when the reward signal comes from a learned reward model that can be over-optimized or exploited (as in RLHF), PPO's KL constraint and clipped objective provide critical protection against reward hacking.

This is not merely an empirical observation but a **conceptual framework for algorithm selection** based on task properties. The paper doesn't claim "EI is better than PPO" or "PPO is better than EI" in absolute terms; it argues that the answer depends on whether you're optimizing against a clean ground-truth signal or a noisy learned proxy.

**The mechanism:** In the reasoning setting, the reward is `+1` if the final answer matches the ground truth and `0` otherwise. This signal has zero false positives — a solution that gets `+1` is genuinely correct — and the only failure mode is false negatives (a solution with a correct reasoning path that happens to format the final answer slightly differently, failing the extraction). EI, by discarding all zero-reward trajectories and training only on positive ones, exploits the zero-false-positive property. PPO, by contrast, also uses zero-reward trajectories for advantage estimation — but if the negative trajectories don't contain useful signal about *how* to improve (which they don't, if exploration is narrow), this extra information provides no benefit.

In the RLHF setting, the reward model is a learned proxy that can assign high rewards to outputs that *appear* good to the reward model but aren't actually aligned with human preferences. This creates false positives at scale — as optimization pressure increases, the policy finds reward-model-pleasing outputs that diverge from true quality (Gao et al., 2022). PPO's KL constraint (`β = 0.05`) penalizes divergence from the initial policy, preventing the model from drifting into regions of output space where the reward model is unreliable. EI has no such constraint — if the reward model incorrectly labels a degenerate output as high-reward, EI will train on it and amplify the degeneracy. The paper explicitly connects this to their findings:

> "The relatively superior performance of PPO over EI on RLHF tasks versus reasoning tasks suggests PPO better mitigates such over-optimization. This is not too surprising since PPO training penalizes student models diverging from the initial policy via both its clipped objective and additional KL-constraint. In contrast, EI training has no such protection built in." (Section 5)

**Why this is a significant reframing rather than a minor observation:** Prior work treated algorithmic choice as a matter of performance optimization — use whatever gives the best numbers on your benchmark. This paper reframes it as a **structural match between algorithm properties and reward properties**. When the reward is trustworthy, use the simplest method that exploits its trustworthiness (EI). When the reward is a hackable proxy, use a method with built-in safeguards against exploitation (PPO with KL penalty). This explains why RLHF practitioners found PPO necessary while reasoning practitioners might find it unnecessary — they're solving different structural problems, not just different tasks.

**Evidence:** The Discussion (Section 5) explicitly makes this argument. The empirical support comes from the contrast between the paper's own results (Tables 1–3 show EI matching or exceeding PPO with ground-truth rewards) and the RLHF literature (Gulcehre et al., 2023; Dubois et al., 2023; Kirk et al., 2023 all show PPO outperforming filtered-SFT approaches on preference satisfaction tasks). The KL penalty ablation in Section 4.3 documents its criticality for PPO stability in the reasoning domain, which is a setting where it should be *less* necessary (since the reward is clean) — its importance even here suggests it would be even more critical in reward-model-based settings.

**Caveat:** The paper does not actually run experiments with a hackable learned reward model to directly test this hypothesis in the reasoning domain. The argument is inferential, drawing on the contrast between their own results and published RLHF results. A direct test — running EI and PPO against a learned ORM reward without ground-truth access, and measuring over-optimization — would strengthen this claim substantially.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluation is on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), a benchmark of grade-school math word problems requiring multi-step arithmetic reasoning. The paper also reports results on SVAMP (Patel et al., 2021), a similar but distinct math word problem benchmark. The authors use the standard train/test splits for both datasets, though they do not report the exact split sizes (GSM8K typically has ~7,500 training and ~1,300 test questions; SVAMP has ~700 training and ~300 test questions based on the original releases). When running PPO, a separate validation set (drawn from the training split) is used for checkpoint selection.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use instruction-tuned Llama-2 models (Touvron et al., 2023) at two scales: 7B and 13B parameters. The choice of Llama-2 is motivated by its status as an open, widely-used model family that is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4, opening paragraph). Both model sizes are tested to assess whether algorithmic advantages are scale-dependent. For experiments with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) initialization, the models are first fine-tuned on the GSM8K training set (with ground-truth reasoning traces) for 4 epochs using a global batch size of 128 and learning rate `$2 \times 10^{-5}$` decayed to `$2 \times 10^{-7}$` via cosine warmup, producing the "SFT" checkpoint. For experiments without SFT initialization, the raw pretrained instruction-tuned models are prompted with two-shot examples drawn from the GSM8K validation set.

- **Metrics.** Four metrics are reported for every model, each measuring a different aspect of solution quality:
  - **maj@1:** Greedy decoding accuracy — a single solution is sampled per question with temperature 0 (always selecting the highest-probability next token). The fraction of questions where this greedy sample is correct is the maj@1 score. This measures consistency and is the metric most relevant to single-answer deployment scenarios.
  - **maj@96:** Majority vote at 96 samples — for each test question, the model generates `$K = 96$` independent solutions at temperature `$T = 0.7$`. The final answer that appears most frequently across the 96 samples is selected. This requires no external verifier; it exploits self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023), where the model's own consensus across samples serves as a quality signal.
  - **rerank@96:** ORM-based selection at 96 samples — the model generates 96 solutions per question at `$T = 0.7$`. An Outcome-Based Reward Model (trained on data from the best EI policy) scores each solution, and the highest-scoring solution is selected as the final answer. This metric measures both model capability and verifier reliability.
  - **pass@96:** Best-of-96 with ground-truth oracle — the model generates 96 solutions per question. A question is counted as "passed" if *any* of the 96 samples produces the correct final answer (as verified against the ground-truth answer key). This is the capability ceiling: it measures whether the model can ever produce the correct answer, regardless of how rarely it does so.
  
  Unless otherwise specified, test-time sampling uses greedy decoding for maj@1 and temperature `$T = 0.7$` for the other three metrics. All four metrics are reported as fractions (e.g., 0.41 = 41%).

- **Baselines.** The paper includes several baselines within its own experimental matrix, plus comparisons to prior published results:
  - **SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning):** The Llama-2 model fine-tuned on the GSM8K training set as described above, evaluated at both 2 epochs ("SFT2") and 4 epochs ("SFT4").
  - **Prompted:** The pretrained Llama-2 model with a two-shot prompt (examples from GSM8K validation set), serving as the baseline for the no-SFT experiments.
  - **External baselines from prior work** (reported in Table 1): WizardMath (Luo et al., 2023) at 7B (0.55 maj@1) and 13B (0.64 maj@1); Llema (Azerbayev et al., 2023) at 7B (0.40 maj@1) and 34B (0.62 maj@1); RFT (Rejection Fine-Tuning) at 7B (0.47) and 13B (0.54); GPT-3 (Cobbe et al., 2021) at 7B (0.20) and 175B (0.31); GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) at unknown size (0.91). These comparisons contextualize the paper's results but have different base models, training data, and compute budgets, so they are illustrative rather than controlled.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in "model rollouts" — one complete solution sampled from the policy. For PPO, each training batch uses `$N = 1024$` rollouts (4 per question across roughly 256 questions). For Expert Iteration, each round generates `$K$` samples per training question (default `$K = 96$`, reduced to `$K = 4$` in an ablation). Sample complexity curves (Figures 1, 5, 6, 14–17) plot the cumulative number of model rollouts against test accuracy, enabling direct comparison of how efficiently each algorithm converts environment interactions into performance. The paper does not report wall-clock time or GPU-hours, though it notes that "resulting train time in both cases is about a day" for the SFT-initialized experiments (Section 4.1).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For PPO, the best checkpoint is selected via performance on a held-out validation set (drawn from the GSM8K training split). For EI, training continues until performance on the validation set saturates — typically `$n = 2$` iterations from SFT initialization and `$n = 4$` to `$n = 5$` from pretrained-only initialization. The paper does not report confidence intervals, error bars, or statistical significance tests for any of the main results. The test sets are fixed (standard splits for GSM8K and SVAMP), and results are reported as point estimates. For RCRL, the dataset is balanced to have equal numbers of `[GOOD]` and `[BAD]` examples by default, with different ratios explored in Appendix A (Table 5). The paper does not describe any cross-validation procedure for the test-set evaluations — metrics are computed once on the standard test split.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Results with SFT Initialization (GSM8K, Table 1)

**Headline result:** Expert Iteration achieves the best overall performance, with `maj@1 = 0.48` (7B) and `0.53` (13B), representing a 7 percentage point improvement over the SFT baseline (`maj@1 = 0.41` for 7B, `0.48` for 13B). PPO with ORM guidance reaches `maj@1 = 0.46` (7B) and `0.51` (13B), improving over SFT by 5 percentage points but trailing EI by 2 points. Sparse PPO (ground-truth reward only) achieves `maj@1 = 0.44` (7B) and `0.51` (13B).

The pattern holds across metrics: EI's `maj@96` reaches `0.55` (7B) vs. PPO's best `0.52` (Dense ORM PPO). EI's `rerank@96` reaches `0.64` (7B) vs. PPO's `0.59`. EI's `pass@96` reaches `0.80` (7B) vs. PPO's `0.79` (Sparse ORM PPO). The `pass@96` numbers across all similarly-sized models are within 3 percentage points, suggesting the capability ceiling is largely invariant to algorithmic choice. The 13B models consistently outperform their 7B counterparts by 5–8 percentage points on maj@1 and 8–12 points on pass@96.

**Comparison to external baselines:** The paper's EI 7B (`maj@1 = 0.48`) outperforms Llema 7B (`0.40`) and RFT 7B (`0.47`), but trails WizardMath 7B (`0.55`). At 13B, EI (`0.53`) outperforms RFT 13B (`0.54` — essentially tied) but trails WizardMath 13B (`0.64`) by 11 points. However, these comparisons are confounded by different base models, SFT data, and training procedures — WizardMath uses a different data generation pipeline (reinforced evol-instruct) that may provide more diverse training data.

**RCRL underperformance:** RCRL models achieve `maj@1 = 0.45` (7B) and `0.51` (13B), underperforming EI despite being trained on EI-generated data with balanced positive/negative labels. Their `pass@96` scores (`0.73` for 7B, `0.83` for 13B) are comparable to other methods, but `rerank@96` drops to `0.54` (7B) vs. EI's `0.64`, indicating the ORM (trained on EI data) finds RCRL outputs harder to score accurately — evidence of distribution shift between EI-generated training data and RCRL-generated test solutions.

**Dense rewards hurt performance:** Dense PPO (`maj@1 = 0.43` for 7B) underperforms Sparse PPO (`0.44`), and Dense ORM PPO (`0.46`) matches Sparse ORM PPO (`0.46`). The paper attributes this to dense heuristic rewards encouraging "more overfit to exact solutions in the train set, limiting solution diversity" (Section 4.1). The effect is modest (1–3 percentage points) but consistent.

**The SFT tradeoff quantified (Table 4):** SFT2 (2 epochs) achieves `maj@1 = 0.36` and `pass@96 = 0.76`. SFT4 (4 epochs) improves `maj@1` to `0.41` but reduces `pass@96` to `0.72`. When sampled 96 times per training question, SFT2 produces an average of 3.7 unique correct solutions; SFT4 produces only 2.9. PPO fine-tuning from SFT4 recovers both metrics: `maj@1 = 0.44` and `pass@96 = 0.77`. This establishes RL's role as a diversity-preserving regularizer rather than a capability expander.

**Figure 1 (sample complexity):** EI achieves better final performance than PPO but requires roughly an order of magnitude more rollouts when using `$K = 96$` samples per question (EI trains on ~600,000 rollouts vs. PPO's ~60,000). However, the paper notes that "the resulting train time in both cases is about a day" because PPO's architecture (value head, LoRA, sequential rollout generation) reduces throughput relative to EI's batched SFT, and because `$K = 96$` is likely far more samples than needed (the `$K = 4$` ablation in Section 4.2 demonstrates this).

**Figure 2 (EI convergence by iteration):** From SFT initialization, performance plateaus after `$n = 2$` iterations for both model sizes. From pretrained initialization, convergence requires `$n = 4$` iterations. The SFT-initialized models start from higher accuracy and saturate faster, consistent with the exploration-bottleneck hypothesis (the SFT model already knows most of the relevant solution patterns, so EI has less to discover).

**Figures 3 and 4 (ORM impact on sample complexity):** For both EI (Figure 3) and PPO (Figure 4), ORM guidance improves convergence speed in the early phase (maj@1 rises faster with fewer rollouts) but does not improve final converged accuracy. The EI + ORM curve rises more steeply initially then converges to the same asymptote as default EI. The PPO + ORM curves show a similar pattern. This is consistent with the interpretation that the ORM provides better credit assignment during early learning (reducing noise in the sparse reward signal) but that final performance is bottlenecked by exploration rather than reward quality.

#### Results with No SFT Initialization (GSM8K and SVAMP, Tables 2 and 3)

**Headline result on GSM8K (Table 2):** Starting from prompted pretrained models (maj@1 = 0.05 for 7B, 0.03 for 13B), Sparse PPO achieves `maj@1 = 0.32` (7B) and `0.41` (13B), slightly outperforming EI which reaches `maj@1 = 0.31` (7B) and `0.40` (13B). This is the only setting where PPO beats EI (by 1 point), though the gap is within likely statistical noise. The pretrained 13B model achieves `pass@96 = 0.27` with prompting alone, and RL training pushes this to `0.83` for both PPO and EI — a roughly 3× improvement in the capability ceiling that demonstrates RL can surface substantial latent knowledge even without SFT data.

**Headline result on SVAMP (Table 3):** EI dominates on SVAMP, achieving `maj@1 = 0.58` (7B) and `0.69` (13B) from a prompted baseline of `0.06` (7B) and `0.05` (13B). PPO reaches `maj@1 = 0.44` (7B) and `0.51` (13B), trailing EI by 14–18 percentage points. EI's `pass@96` reaches `0.70` (7B) and `0.93` (13B) vs. PPO's `0.72` (7B) and `0.89` (13B) — broadly comparable. The 13B EI model's pass@96 of `0.93` is notably close to the SFT-initialized 13B model's `0.84` (Table 1), suggesting that with enough EI rounds, the pretrained model can nearly recover SFT-level capability.

**The prompted baseline is surprisingly capable:** The prompted 13B pretrained model achieves `pass@96 = 0.27` on GSM8K and `0.36` on SVAMP — meaning that even without any training on math word problems, the pretrained model can occasionally produce correct multi-step reasoning chains when sampled extensively. This demonstrates that pretraining alone encodes substantial mathematical reasoning ability that RL can amplify.

**Sample complexity from pretrained initialization (Figures 5 and 6):** On GSM8K (Figure 5), PPO converges after ~30,000 rollouts while EI with `$K = 96$` requires ~300,000 rollouts. However, when EI is run with `$K = 4$` samples per question, it converges in the same ~30,000 rollouts as PPO, reaching accuracy only a few points lower than the `$K = 96$` variant. On SVAMP (Figure 6), EI with `$K = 96$` requires more samples than PPO but converges to substantially higher accuracy. The `$K = 4$` EI variant is not reported for SVAMP, but the paper states that reducing samples per prompt "significantly reduce[d]" total sample requirements across both datasets.

**Figures 14–17 (appendix sample complexity curves):** ORM-guided EI and PPO from pretrained initialization show the same pattern as from SFT: initial sample complexity improvement, final convergence to the same or slightly lower asymptote. Dense ORM rewards further reduce early sample complexity but degrade final converged accuracy. The paper speculates this degradation "could be alleviated with a larger reward model" but doubts it would surpass the ground-truth reward (Appendix C).

#### RL vs. SFT: The maj@1/pass@96 Tradeoff (Table 4)

**Headline result:** SFT training creates a tradeoff between maj@1 and pass@96, while RL training improves both simultaneously — but does not substantially exceed the pass@96 achievable with light SFT.

SFT2 (2 epochs): maj@1 = 0.36, pass@96 = 0.76, rerank@96 = 0.53. SFT4 (4 epochs): maj@1 = 0.41 (+0.05), pass@96 = 0.72 (-0.04), rerank@96 = 0.54 (+0.01). The 4-point drop in pass@96 with continued SFT training is the "tradeoff" — the model becomes more consistent (higher probability of generating the correct answer on the first try) but loses the ability to generate diverse correct solutions, so its best-of-96 ceiling falls.

PPO2 (PPO initialized from SFT2): maj@1 = 0.43 (+0.07 over SFT2), pass@96 = 0.80 (+0.04 over SFT2). PPO4 (PPO initialized from SFT4): maj@1 = 0.44 (+0.03 over SFT4), pass@96 = 0.77 (+0.05 over SFT4).

The key comparison is PPO4 vs. SFT2: PPO4 achieves maj@1 = 0.44 (beating SFT2's 0.36 by 8 points) while maintaining pass@96 = 0.77 (essentially matching SFT2's 0.76). This demonstrates that RL breaks the tradeoff — you can have SFT4-level greedy accuracy with SFT2-level diversity. However, PPO4's pass@96 of 0.77 is only 1 point above SFT2's 0.76, suggesting the diversity is recovered, not expanded.

**Diversity statistics:** SFT2 produces 3.7 unique correct solutions per training question (when sampled 96 times); SFT4 produces 2.9. The drop in unique solutions directly corresponds to the pass@96 decline, confirming the mechanism: overfitting to a static dataset collapses the output distribution onto fewer solution templates, reducing the probability that any single template covers every question.

#### Exploration and Diversity Across EI Iterations (Figures 9–12)

**EI convergence trajectory (Figures 9, 10):** From SFT initialization, maj@1 rises from ~0.41 (SFT baseline) to ~0.48 (iteration 2) then plateaus. From pretrained initialization (no SFT), maj@1 rises from ~0.05 (prompted) to ~0.30 (iteration 4) then plateaus. The SFT-initialized convergence is faster (2 vs. 4 iterations), consistent with the model already knowing most solution strategies from SFT.

**Solution diversity trajectories (Figures 11, 12):** The paper measures two types of diversity: "exact diversity" (exact string match between solutions) and "trace diversity" (matching on the extracted sequence of intermediate computations, ignoring surface wording). Both measures increase during the first two rounds of EI (the period when the largest maj@1 gains occur) then plateau or slightly decline in later rounds. This mirrors the SFT overfitting pattern: early EI rounds expand the set of correct solution paths the model can generate, but continued iterations begin to narrow this distribution toward the specific paths that dominate the curated dataset.

**Model size effect on diversity:** The 13B model produces substantially more diverse solutions than the 7B model at every iteration. By iteration 4, the 13B model generates an "exactly unique solution with every sampling" (Appendix B), though its trace diversity plateaus after iteration 2, indicating the model introduces surface-level variation without changing the underlying computational structure. The consistently higher diversity of the 13B model correlates with its consistently higher pass@96 scores across all experimental settings.

**Figure 10 (K=4 sample EI):** When reducing samples per prompt from 96 to 4, EI requires more iterations to converge (5 vs. 4 for the K=96 case) but still reaches comparable accuracy (only a few points lower). This establishes a practical speed-accuracy tradeoff and confirms that the default `$K=96$` is sampling far more than necessary — most of those 96 samples are redundant.

#### Architectural and Hyperparameter Ablations for PPO (Section 4.3, Figures 7, 8)

**Value head architecture (Figure 7):** Comparing the default PPO configuration (large value branch with `$L = 4$` transformer layers and detached gradients) to a version with default architecture shows a substantial accuracy gap. The default configuration maintains stable improvement throughout 4000 gradient steps, while the non-detached version shows erratic performance with large dips. The paper attributes this to "value gradients interfere with policy gradients... causing instability with a big update to either branch" (Section 4.3). Figure 7 plots this comparison showing typically 3–5 percentage point gaps favoring the detached architecture.

**Best K of N sampling (Figure 8):** The paper sweeps different K/N ratios for PPO rollouts. Setting `$K = 4, N = 4$` (keep all 4 samples per question) achieves the best maj@1 and fastest runtime. More aggressive filtering (`$K = 1, N = 8$` — keep only the single best of 8 rollouts per question) degrades performance and takes proportionally longer (8× more rollouts to fill a batch). The curves show that as K/N decreases (more aggressive filtering), maj@1 at any given number of training steps is lower, and the gap widens over training. The paper notes this result is contrary to the intuition that filtering out low-reward trajectories would improve the training signal — instead, the filtered dataset apparently loses useful information (perhaps the contrast between good and mediocre solutions) that aids policy improvement.

**KL penalty ablation (discussed qualitatively):** The paper states that a KL penalty coefficient of 0.05 was "critical for preventing model collapse after more than a hundred gradient updates" but does not provide a figure comparing different KL coefficients. The text notes this is in contrast to Bai et al. (2022), who found KL penalty unnecessary for dialogue tasks, and attributes the difference to the "unnatural distribution of text" in reasoning tasks.

**Temperature ablation (discussed qualitatively):** From SFT initialization, `$T = 0.7$` for PPO exploration works well. From pretrained initialization, `$T = 0.2$` is necessary because higher temperatures "often results in collapse" — the model hasn't yet learned to produce valid mathematical notation, and high-temperature sampling generates degenerate token sequences that never recover.

**LoRA rank and partial fine-tuning (discussed qualitatively):** LoRA with `$r = 128$` stabilizes training compared to full fine-tuning while maintaining performance. Fine-tuning only the top `$M$` layers saves memory "but at the cost of a few percentage points of performance," indicating that reasoning improvements require changes distributed throughout the network, not just in higher-level layers.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**RCRL positive/negative label ratio (Appendix A, Table 5):** The paper varies the proportion of `[GOOD]` to `[BAD]` training examples. With a 100:1 ratio (overwhelmingly positive data), RCRL achieves `maj@1 = 0.18` on GSM8K from pretrained initialization, slightly exceeding the data-generating EI-minimal model (`0.17`). With a 1:1 balanced ratio (the default), performance drops to `0.15`. This demonstrates that the 7B model does not effectively learn from negative demonstrations — including `[BAD]`-labeled data degrades performance at ratios above roughly 10:1. The authors hypothesize that "either a larger model or an easier task would give better results."

**ORM guidance (Figures 3, 4, 14–17):** Across all experimental settings (SFT-initialized GSM8K, pretrained GSM8K, pretrained SVAMP; both EI and PPO), ORM guidance consistently improves sample complexity in early training (steeper slope on the accuracy-vs-rollouts curve) but does not improve final converged performance. In several cases (particularly from pretrained initialization on GSM8K with PPO, Figure 15), the ORM-guided variant converges to slightly *lower* final accuracy than the sparse ground-truth variant. The paper interprets this as ORMs providing better credit assignment during early learning (when the sparse signal is noisy because the model rarely succeeds) but eventually introducing bias or over-optimization effects that cap performance below what the clean ground-truth reward achieves.

**Dense vs. sparse rewards (Tables 1, 2, 3):** Dense heuristic rewards (matching intermediate steps to reference solutions) consistently underperform sparse rewards by 1–2 percentage points on maj@1. Dense ORM rewards match or slightly underperform sparse ORM rewards. The mechanism: dense heuristic rewards encourage the model to mimic specific solution templates from the training set, reducing the diversity of generated solutions and thereby limiting the model's ability to discover alternative correct paths. Even dense ORM rewards, which should be more flexible than heuristic matching, provide no benefit, suggesting that the primary optimization bottleneck is not credit assignment quality but exploration breadth.

**Curriculum learning (Appendix D, Figure 19):** Both Backtracking and Prioritized Level Replay (PLR) are tested as methods to structure training difficulty. Backtracking initializes rollouts from intermediate ground-truth steps and gradually moves the start point earlier as the model succeeds. PLR prioritizes questions with high "learning potential" (measured by average absolute advantage). Figure 19 shows that neither method exceeds the performance of default PPO. The paper attributes this to "the limited exploration the model engages in from the start, due to both pretraining and supervised fine-tuning" — since the model already knows (in some sense) how to solve these problems, artificially structuring the difficulty doesn't provide additional benefit. The curriculum methods would presumably help more in settings where the model genuinely needs to learn new strategies from scratch.

**Data augmentation via backtranslation (Appendix E, Table 6):** Synthetic `$(Q, A)$` pairs generated by a backward model (answers to questions) and scored by student recovery rate fail to improve performance over the SFT baseline. All synthetic data configurations degrade accuracy: `$maj@1 = 0.38$` with τ = 0.1 filtering vs. SFT baseline `0.41`; `0.36` with τ = 0.2; `0.34` with τ = 0.3. Manual inspection reveals a high rate of false positives — the generator produces nonsensical questions (e.g., percentages summing to 120%), but the student model (trained on similar data) makes the same arithmetic errors and "verifies" the pair as valid. The paper suggests using "a larger model more capable of detecting these sort of trivial non-solutions" as future work.

**Model resetting for EI (Appendix B, discussed in Section 4.1):** The paper finds that starting each round of EI from the pretrained base model (rather than continuing to fine-tune the previous iteration's checkpoint) is "crucial for achieving best performance." This is not presented as a quantitative ablation (no table compares resetting vs. continued fine-tuning), but the paper states it as a key design choice inherited from Zelikman et al. (2022). The mechanism: continued fine-tuning would compound overfitting artifacts from previous rounds, while resetting to the pretrained checkpoint preserves the broad output distribution and allows each round to steer toward correct solutions without accumulated distributional collapse.

**Reducing EI samples per prompt (Section 4.2, Figure 10):** Reducing `$K$` from 96 to 4 samples per question increases the number of iterations required for convergence (from 4 to 5 for pretrained GSM8K) but reduces total sample requirements by roughly an order of magnitude while maintaining accuracy within a few percentage points. For example, `$K = 4$` EI reaches approximately `$0.28$` maj@1 on GSM8K from pretrained initialization vs. `$0.31$` for `$K = 96$`, using ~30,000 vs. ~300,000 total rollouts. This establishes that the default `$K = 96$` substantially oversamples and that EI's sample complexity can be made comparable to PPO's with minimal accuracy loss.

**Model size scaling (Tables 1, 2, 3; Figures 11, 12):** All algorithms benefit from scaling from 7B to 13B parameters, with gains of 5–10 percentage points on maj@1 and 8–15 points on pass@96 depending on initialization. Larger models generate more diverse solutions (Figures 11, 12) and benefit more from RL training. The paper does not test intermediate sizes or larger models, so the scaling trend is established at only two points.

**Convergence criterion (Section 3 for EI; Section 4 for PPO):** For EI, training continues until performance on a validation set saturates (typically `$n = 2$` from SFT, `$n = 4$`–`$5$` from pretrained). For PPO, training runs for 4000 gradient steps with checkpoint selection based on validation performance. These criteria are reasonable but not formally justified — the paper does not report how much performance varies across checkpoints or whether early stopping with a patience-based criterion would change results.

### Critical Assessment

**Does the evidence support the claim that "Expert Iteration performs best in most cases"?** This claim is supported for the SFT-initialized GSM8K setting (Table 1: EI beats PPO by 2–3 points on maj@1) and the pretrained SVAMP setting (Table 3: EI beats PPO by 14–18 points). For pretrained GSM8K (Table 2), PPO nominally beats EI by 1 point (0.32 vs. 0.31 for 7B, 0.41 vs. 0.40 for 13B), which is within what would likely be statistical noise (no confidence intervals are reported). The SVAMP result is the most compelling — a 14-point gap is large — but it appears on only one of two benchmarks. The evidence is consistent with "EI performs comparably or slightly better," but the paper's framing ("best in most cases") overstates the robustness somewhat given the single-digit gaps and lack of statistical testing.

**Does the evidence support the claim that "EI has similar sample complexity to PPO"?** This claim requires careful parsing. From SFT initialization (Figure 1), EI with `$K = 96$` uses ~600,000 rollouts vs. PPO's ~60,000 — a 10× difference that the paper acknowledges. However, the `$K = 4$` ablation (Section 4.2) demonstrates that EI can converge with ~30,000 rollouts when using fewer samples per prompt, matching PPO's sample complexity. The claim therefore holds **conditional on tuning the number of EI samples per prompt**, but the default EI configuration is substantially less sample-efficient than PPO. The paper's assertion that "resulting train time in both cases is about a day" blurs the distinction between sample efficiency (number of rollouts) and wall-clock efficiency (which depends on batching and architecture), and the `$K = 96$` default is used for all main results, not the optimized `$K = 4$` variant.

**Does the evidence support the claim that "RL improves maj@1 without trading off pass@96"?** Table 4 directly supports this: PPO4 achieves higher maj@1 (0.44) and higher pass@96 (0.77) than SFT4 (0.41 and 0.72). However, the increase in pass@96 from SFT4 to PPO4 (0.72 → 0.77) only recovers SFT2's pass@96 (0.76). RL breaks the SFT tradeoff in the sense that you don't have to sacrifice pass@96 to get higher maj@1, but it does not substantially *increase* pass@96 beyond what light SFT already achieves. The paper acknowledges this: "RL training does not significantly improve pass@n score beyond what can be achieved with light supervised fine-tuning" (Section 5). The claim is technically accurate but the framing might lead a reader to expect pass@96 improvements, when what RL actually does is prevent pass@96 degradation.

**Does the evidence support the claim that "limited exploration is the primary bottleneck"?** This is the paper's central diagnostic claim, and the evidence is circumstantial but converging:
- Pass@96 ceilings are similar across algorithms (within 3 points, Tables 1 and 4), suggesting all methods access roughly the same set of correct solutions.
- Sample complexity is comparable once EI's sampling rate is adjusted, suggesting the learning signal is similar across algorithms.
- ORM guidance doesn't improve final performance, suggesting reward quality isn't the bottleneck.
- Model size increases diversity and RL improvement, suggesting exploration breadth (which scales with model size) matters.
- Diversity increases then plateaus during EI (Figures 11, 12), suggesting exploration saturates.

However, this is a diagnosis, not a demonstrated causal mechanism. The paper does not run experiments that directly manipulate exploration breadth (e.g., varying temperature schedule, adding explicit diversity bonuses, comparing models with different pretraining diversity) and measure the effect on RL improvement. The chain of inference is: "all methods plateau at similar pass@96 → therefore exploration is limited → therefore exploration is the bottleneck." An alternative explanation — that the model has already learned everything knowable about these tasks from pretraining, and no amount of exploration would help — is not ruled out. The paper leans toward the exploration interpretation ("We view the discovery of new techniques encouraging complex, rich exploration... as fundamental"), but this is a hypothesis, not a finding.

**Genuine weaknesses in experimental design:**

- **No statistical measures.** The paper reports point estimates for all metrics without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests. With a test set of ~1,300 questions (GSM8K), a 2–3 percentage point difference (e.g., EI vs. PPO on GSM8K SFT) corresponds to roughly 26–39 questions — large enough to be practically meaningful but small enough that variance from checkpoint selection, sampling temperature, or random seed could flip the ordering. The paper's core claim that "EI performs best" rests on single-digit gaps without any quantification of uncertainty.

- **Single model family.** All experiments use Llama-2. The finding that exploration is limited and algorithms perform similarly might be specific to Llama-2's pretraining distribution, its tokenizer, or its instruction-tuning recipe. Models with different architectures (non-autoregressive), different pretraining objectives, or different scales might show different algorithmic sensitivity. The paper notes this implicitly by not claiming universality, but the "representative" claim in Section 4 is not tested.

- **No combination of EI with PPO-like exploration incentives.** The paper diagnoses limited exploration as the bottleneck but does not test whether adding exploration bonuses (e.g., entropy bonus, curiosity-driven rewards, diverse trajectory selection) to PPO or EI would improve performance. A key missing experiment: EI or PPO with an explicit diversity objective (e.g., maximize reward while penalizing similarity to previously generated solutions). This would directly test whether the exploration bottleneck is algorithmic (current methods don't encourage exploration) or fundamental (the model has nothing more to discover).

- **The EI vs. PPO comparison confounds algorithm with reward usage.** EI discards all negative examples; PPO uses them (via advantage weighting, which downweights but doesn't eliminate low-reward trajectories). The finding that negative examples don't help (supported by the RCRL ablation in Appendix A) suggests EI's filtering is the right strategy for these tasks. But this makes the EI/PPO comparison one of "algorithm + data filtering strategy" rather than algorithm alone. A PPO variant that also discards low-reward trajectories (via the Best K of N mechanism, which was tested and found to not help) might close the remaining gap with EI. The paper did test Best K of N (Figure 8) but found it didn't improve PPO, which slightly deepens the puzzle of why EI (which is essentially K/N → 0 filtering) works better.

- **The ORM is trained on EI-generated data.** All rerank@96 results use an ORM "trained with samples from EIn" (Table 1 footnote). This creates a systematic bias favoring EI models in the rerank@96 metric: the ORM has seen EI-like solutions during training and may score them higher, inflating EI's apparent rerank@96 advantage. The paper acknowledges this: "we believe a non-trivial percentage of this gap is due to overfit of the ORM to the EI model." This means the rerank@96 comparisons are not fair across algorithms, which the paper should have addressed by training separate ORMs for each algorithm or using a held-out ORM from a different model family.

- **Test set contamination risk.** The paper uses GSM8K and SVAMP, which are standard benchmarks whose test sets may have leaked into Llama-2's pretraining data. If the pretrained model has memorized some test questions, the baseline "prompted" accuracy would be inflated, and RL's apparent improvement would be partly recovery of memorized answers rather than genuine reasoning improvement. The paper does not discuss or attempt to control for this.

- **The SVAMP advantage for EI is unexplained.** EI outperforms PPO by 14–18 points on SVAMP but only by 1–3 points on GSM8K (from pretrained initialization). The paper does not investigate why, beyond noting the datasets are different. This is a significant gap — if EI has a genuine advantage on certain task distributions, understanding why would be practically important. Possible explanations: SVAMP has shorter solutions (fewer steps, easier credit assignment for sparse rewards, favoring EI's approach), SVAMP has more diverse solution paths (favoring EI's diversity-preserving properties), or the specific SFT data available for GSM8K (used to construct the prompted baseline's examples) creates an initialization advantage for PPO that doesn't exist on SVAMP.

- **No comparison of EI's compute cost beyond sample count.** The paper measures sample efficiency in rollouts but not in FLOPs or GPU-hours. EI's training phase (SFT on filtered data) and PPO's training phase (PPO updates with value function, advantage computation, KL penalty) have different computational costs per example. A full accounting would require measuring total FLOPs to convergence, not just number of generated solutions.

- **The pretrained initialization experiments conflate prompting with the RL method.** The "pretrained" baselines use a two-shot prompt with examples from the GSM8K validation set. When EI models "remove these prompts after the first round of exploration," they are using a different input format than the PPO models (which presumably keep the prompts throughout, though this is not explicitly stated). This confound could explain some of the performance differences in the no-SFT setting.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper's claims:**

- A direct test of the exploration hypothesis: add an explicit diversity bonus to the reward (e.g., reward solutions that are semantically different from previously generated correct solutions) and measure whether this improves RL performance and/or breaks the pass@96 ceiling.

- A comparison of PPO vs. EI at matched wall-clock time (not matched rollout count), to determine whether PPO's architectural overhead (value head, KL computation, sequential rollout generation) negates its sample efficiency advantage.

- Experiments with a learned reward model that can be over-optimized (e.g., training an ORM, then running both PPO and EI against it without ground-truth access) to directly test whether PPO's KL constraint provides protection against reward hacking that EI lacks.

- Scaling to larger models (e.g., 70B) to test whether the algorithmic differences widen or narrow at scale, and whether the exploration bottleneck persists.

- Experiments on reasoning tasks that require genuinely novel solution strategies beyond the pretraining distribution (e.g., competition math, novel puzzle formats) to test whether RL algorithms differ when the model cannot simply surface pretrained knowledge.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Dominates and Is Not Amortized

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper's entire compute-optimal framework rests on knowing which difficulty quintile each test question falls into before allocating the inference budget. The method for estimating difficulty — generating 2048 samples per question, scoring them with the PRM's final-answer prediction, and binning into quintiles — is extraordinarily expensive. The authors explicitly acknowledge this in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

The difficulty estimation step consumes 2048 generations per question. Compare this to the largest test-time budgets studied: 256–512 generations for the main experiments and up to 2048 for the PRM scaling curves. In other words, **estimating difficulty costs 4–8× more compute than the largest problem-solving budgets evaluated**, and 32–128× more than the budgets where the 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N are demonstrated (16–64 generations). This cost is incurred for every question at deployment time and is not included in any of the reported efficiency calculations.

**The consequence:** The headline 4× efficiency improvement over best-of-N — that compute-optimal scaling achieves equivalent accuracy with 4× fewer generations — is computed *after* difficulty is already known, without amortizing the cost of learning it. When the total cost is difficulty estimation + strategy execution, the true efficiency relative to a naive best-of-N baseline at the same total budget could be substantially worse, potentially even negative (i.e., the adaptive approach costs more for the same accuracy). The paper's comparisons between search strategies (Figure 3) and between sequential/parallel ratios (Figure 6) are all conducted at fixed generation budgets that exclude the difficulty estimation overhead.

Concretely: suppose a practitioner wants to use compute-optimal scaling at a target budget of 64 generations per question. According to Figure 4, compute-optimal achieves roughly the same accuracy as best-of-N at 256 generations — a 4× gain. But the practitioner must first spend 2048 generations estimating difficulty. Total cost: 2048 + 64 = 2112 generations. A naive best-of-N baseline could simply spend all 2112 generations on parallel sampling. The paper provides no comparison that accounts for this total cost. For the claimed 4× gain to be realizable in deployment, difficulty must be estimated far more cheaply than the current method, or it must be amortized across many repeated queries to the same questions — an assumption that holds for batch evaluation but not for interactive or one-off query scenarios.

Additionally, the paper frames difficulty estimation as "an exploration-exploitation tradeoff — compute spent assessing difficulty versus compute spent solving the problem" (Section 3.2) but does not formalize or optimize this tradeoff. There is no investigation of how many samples are actually needed for reliable difficulty binning, whether fewer samples (e.g., 32 or 64) would provide sufficient signal, or whether difficulty prediction could be integrated into the solution process itself (e.g., use the first few beam search steps to assess difficulty and then reallocate the remaining budget).

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The 2048-sample difficulty estimation procedure is described in Section 3.2. The cost of this step is explicitly noted as unaccounted for. Figure 4 and Figure 8 show compute-optimal scaling curves (both oracle and predicted difficulty) that exclude the estimation cost. No experiment measures total cost including difficulty estimation, and no ablation studies the minimum number of samples needed for reliable difficulty binning.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. Section 8 flags "cheap difficulty estimation" as future work — specifically, "pretraining or finetuning models to directly predict difficulty of a question" or "adaptive difficulty estimation" that starts with few samples and adjusts the budget allocation online. These are suggestions, not implemented solutions. The predicted (non-oracle) difficulty bins, which use PRM scores instead of ground-truth correctness, remove the need for answer labels but do not reduce the computational cost (they still require 2048 samples). Until a cheaper difficulty predictor is developed, the compute-optimal framework as presented is not directly deployable; the reported efficiency gains are an upper bound on achievable performance given perfect difficulty information.

### Hard Problems Remain Completely Unsolved — Test-Time Compute Cannot Compensate for Fundamental Capability Gaps

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper demonstrates that test-time compute amplifies existing model capability but does not create it from nothing. This is clearest in the difficulty bin 5 results (the hardest 20% of questions, where the base model's pass@1 is near zero). Across all methods, all budgets, and all allocation strategies, performance on these questions remains essentially at baseline: 1–3% accuracy. The paper is explicit about this boundary, writing in Section 7:

> "on the hardest questions (bin 5), test-time compute provides essentially zero benefit regardless of budget, meaning that some capabilities can only be acquired through pretraining, not recovered at inference time"

This is not a bug in the method; it is a fundamental constraint: if the base model never generates a correct solution (pass@1 ≈ 0), no amount of search, revision, or adaptive allocation can find one. The proposal distribution contains zero correct samples, so all downstream mechanisms (PRM-guided beam search, sequential revisions, verifier-based selection) are operating on noise.

**The consequence:** The approach offers no path forward for problems outside the base model's capability range. This has two practical implications. First, for deployment on genuinely challenging problem distributions (competition math, novel reasoning tasks, problems requiring knowledge or skills not well-represented in pretraining), compute-optimal test-time scaling provides no benefit — the entire framework's gains are concentrated on easy-to-medium problems where the model already has non-trivial pass@1. Second, the FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 shows that on hard problems (bins 4–5), the ~14× larger pretrained model is strongly preferable to test-time compute with the smaller model. For example, in the PRM search FLOPs-matched comparison at R ≫ 1 (Figure 1, bottom-right bar chart), test-time compute shows a −52.9% relative disadvantage on hard problems compared to the larger pretrained model. This means that for organizations deciding how to allocate their total compute budget, the choice depends critically on the expected difficulty distribution of their queries. If the workload skews toward problems the model fundamentally cannot solve, investing in larger pretraining is the only viable path.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 3 (right) shows bin 5 accuracy hovering at 1–3% for all search methods and all budgets. Figure 7 (right) shows bin 5 accuracy at roughly 2–3% regardless of the sequential-to-parallel ratio for revisions. Figure 9 shows the bin 5 scaling line as essentially flat near 0–5% for both revisions and PRM search in the FLOPs-matched comparison. The bar charts in Figure 1 quantify the relative disadvantage of test-time compute on hard problems across three values of the inference-to-pretraining token ratio R.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not attempt to solve this limitation, nor does it propose specific directions for doing so. The Discussion (Section 5) frames exploration as the key missing ingredient — "We view the discovery of new techniques encouraging complex, rich exploration of reasoning problems as fundamental to progress in LLM reasoning capability" — but this refers to exploration within the test-time compute budget, not to expanding the base model's fundamental knowledge. The paper does not study whether combining compute-optimal test-time strategies with continued pretraining or retrieval-augmented generation could expand the frontier of solvable problems. The limitation is presented as an empirical boundary condition rather than something to be overcome within the framework.

### Search and Revisions Are Studied Independently — The Framework's Two Key Axes Are Never Combined

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper decomposes all test-time compute methods into modifications to the proposal distribution (revisions) versus modifications to the verifier/selection mechanism (PRM search), and studies these two axes in separate experimental pipelines (Sections 5 and 6, respectively). However, they are never combined into an integrated system. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges this gap:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

This is a significant omission because the paper's own analysis suggests the two mechanisms have complementary, difficulty-dependent strengths. Revisions excel on easy problems where the model's initial attempt is roughly correct and just needs refinement — a local optimization. PRM search excels on medium-difficulty problems where the model needs to explore qualitatively different solution strategies — a global search. A combined system that uses the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or that uses the PRM to guide which revision chains to pursue and when to restart, could potentially outperform either mechanism alone, particularly on the medium-difficulty problems where both methods show partial but incomplete gains.

**The consequence:** The paper's results represent a lower bound on what a fully integrated test-time compute framework could achieve. The 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N, while impressive, may understate the potential of combining revisions and search. Conversely, it is possible that the two mechanisms interact negatively — for example, revision model outputs might be harder for the PRM to score accurately (distribution shift between revision model outputs and the PRM's training data, as documented in Figure 15a), and aggressive PRM-guided search on revision model outputs might amplify verifier over-optimization. Without a combined experiment, practitioners cannot know whether the two axes are additive (gains combine), subadditive (diminishing returns), or antagonistic (each undermines the other). The paper's recommendation to "deploy both, switching between them per-prompt" (implied by the compute-optimal policy that selects the best strategy per difficulty bin) is a fallback that uses each mechanism independently rather than synergistically.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The paper's structure — Section 5 for PRM search, Section 6 for revisions, no Section combining them — makes the gap obvious. Figure 3 (search) and Figure 7 (revisions) show complementary difficulty-dependent patterns: beam search helps on bin 3–4, revisions help on bin 1–2. The compute-optimal policies in Figures 4 and 8 select between search strategies and sequential/parallel ratios respectively, but never jointly optimize over both search method and revision depth for a single problem. Figure 15a demonstrates distribution shift between base model outputs (on which the PRM is trained) and revision model outputs, which would be a practical challenge for any combined system.

**Mitigation status:** The paper flags this as future work in Section 8: "the natural next step is to use the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search." The paper suggests that "alternatively, the PRM could guide which revisions to pursue: rather than blindly generating a long revision chain, use the PRM's per-step scores to decide when a revision is on track versus when to restart from scratch." These are conceptual suggestions, not implemented experiments. The paper provides no preliminary results, no analysis of what combined approach might work, and no discussion of the engineering challenges involved (e.g., how to define PRM steps for a revision chain where each "step" is a complete re-generation of the answer rather than a single reasoning step). This limitation is particularly notable because it represents unfinished business for the framework the paper itself establishes — the decomposition into proposal and verifier axes is the paper's conceptual contribution, but the paper never demonstrates that optimizing both axes jointly yields gains beyond optimizing each one independently.

### The PRM Over-Optimization Problem Is Identified but Not Solved — It Sets a Hard Ceiling on Test-Time Compute Scaling

**The assumption or constraint:** The paper documents that the primary bottleneck preventing continued improvement from additional test-time compute is verifier over-optimization: as search becomes more aggressive (higher beam width, more lookahead steps, larger budgets), the model finds solutions that score highly under the PRM but are actually incorrect. This is most clearly visible in Figure 3 (right), where beam search *degrades* easy-problem performance at high budgets, and in the lookahead search results, where the strongest optimizer paradoxically performs worst overall. The paper characterizes this in Section 5.3:

> "The degradation at high budgets is attributed to over-optimization of the PRM — search finds solutions that score highly under the PRM but are actually incorrect. Specific failure modes include low-information repetitive steps at the end of solutions (visible in Figure 29) and overly short 1–2 step solutions."

The compute-optimal policy partially mitigates this by routing easy problems away from aggressive search (using best-of-N instead of beam search), but it does not solve the underlying problem. On medium-difficulty problems where beam search is deployed, over-optimization still limits the scaling ceiling — the beam search curves in Figure 3 flatten and begin to decline well before the maximum budget (256–512 generations) is exhausted. The verifier is the limiting reagent; no amount of search algorithm sophistication can overcome an unreliable verifier.

**The consequence:** The paper's framework is fundamentally bounded by verifier quality. All reported results — the 4× efficiency gains, the FLOPs-matched advantages over larger models, the optimal allocation policies — are specific to the verifier quality achievable with the Monte Carlo rollout training procedure described in Appendix D. A better verifier (trained with more data, better calibration, adversarial robustness, or ensembling) would shift the over-optimization threshold and likely change the optimal policy — easy problems might benefit from search where they currently suffer from it, and the scaling ceiling on medium problems would rise. Conversely, a worse verifier (e.g., one trained with less data or on a different model's outputs) would lower the ceiling and might make the compute-optimal policy less beneficial or even harmful. The paper provides no guidance on how to predict over-optimization behavior for a new verifier or how to diagnose when a verifier is reliable enough to support aggressive search.

Practically, this means that a practitioner adopting the paper's approach must first invest in training a high-quality PRM, and the performance of their system will be bounded by their PRM's quality in ways that are difficult to anticipate without running the full suite of difficulty-bin experiments. The paper's specific policy recommendations (beam search M=4 on medium problems, best-of-N on easy problems) may not transfer to settings with different verifier quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** Figure 3 (left) shows lookahead search (the most powerful optimizer) underperforming all simpler methods. Figure 3 (right) shows beam search degrading easy-problem performance at high budgets, which is the characteristic signature of verifier over-optimization. Figure 29 (Appendix M) provides qualitative examples of degenerate outputs that score highly under the PRM: repetitive steps and overly short solutions. The Discussion (Section 5) explicitly identifies verifier over-optimization as a central limiting factor. Appendix F (Figure 14) shows that the PRM outperforms an ORM, indicating the verifier architecture matters, but does not explore how much further verifier quality could be improved.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not attempt to improve verifier robustness. Section 8 suggests future work directions — "adversarial training (where the PRM is trained on search-generated solutions, not just i.i.d. samples), ensemble verification (aggregating predictions from multiple independently trained PRMs), or constrained search methods that penalize solutions deviating too far from the base model's typical output distribution (a KL-penalty approach analogous to what is used in RLHF)" — but none of these are tested. The compute-optimal policy mitigates over-optimization by avoiding aggressive search on problems where the verifier is most vulnerable (easy problems), but this is a workaround, not a solution. The fundamental problem — that verifiers become unreliable under optimization pressure — remains unsolved and sets a hard ceiling on how much test-time compute can improve performance.

### Evaluation Is Limited to a Single Benchmark and Model Family — Generalization Claims Are Untested

**The assumption or constraint:** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with one model family (PaLM 2-S*) and one comparison model (a ~14× larger PaLM variant). The authors state in Section 4 that they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs," but this claim is entirely untested. The MATH benchmark consists of high-school competition-level math problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning with exact-answer evaluation. It is unclear whether the paper's central findings — difficulty-dependent strategy selection, the 4× efficiency gains from compute-optimal allocation, the complementary strengths of revisions and search — generalize to other reasoning domains (code generation, logical deduction, scientific QA), other output formats (open-ended generation, multi-modal reasoning), or other model families (GPT, Gemini, Claude, open-source models like Mistral or DeepSeek).

Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific:
- The PRM's over-optimization behavior (Section 5.3) depends on the distribution of PaLM 2-S*'s outputs. A model with different calibration, different error patterns, or different solution-style preferences might produce verifier behavior with different difficulty-dependent properties.
- The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples (Section 6.1) depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families (some models are much better at in-context learning than others).
- The MATH benchmark has clean ground-truth answers that can be verified with exact string matching (via the grading function from Lightman et al., 2022, referenced in Section 4). Many important reasoning tasks — open-ended generation, planning, creative problem-solving — lack such clean correctness signals, making both PRM training (which requires Monte Carlo rollouts with correctness verification) and difficulty estimation (which requires pass@1 computation) fundamentally more challenging.
- The FLOPs-matched comparison uses a specific training configuration (parameter-only scaling, fixed data) that may not represent how other organizations scale model size for pretraining.

**The consequence:** A practitioner who reads this paper and wants to apply compute-optimal test-time scaling to their own domain (e.g., code generation with a different model family) cannot assume the paper's specific findings will transfer: the optimal allocation policy (which strategy to use at which difficulty level), the magnitude of efficiency gains (4× over best-of-N), the FLOPs-matched advantage over larger models, and even the difficulty-bin boundaries are all conditioned on PaLM 2-S* and MATH. Applying the method to a new domain would require replicating much of the paper's experimental pipeline — training a new PRM, estimating difficulty on the new benchmark, running the full suite of strategy sweeps, and computing optimal policies via cross-validation. The paper provides a methodology but not off-the-shelf policies or insights that are known to transfer.

Additionally, MATH's test set of 500 questions, split into five difficulty quintiles of ~100 each and further split by two-fold cross-validation, means the compute-optimal policy is selected based on roughly 50 questions per fold per bin. This is a small sample, and the selected strategies may not be robust to different random splits or different test set compositions. The paper does not report confidence intervals on the compute-optimal curves (Figures 4, 8), making it difficult to assess whether small differences between strategies are reliable or within sampling noise.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The evaluation methodology in Section 4 describes MATH as the sole benchmark and PaLM 2-S* as the sole base model family tested. The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 adds a ~14× larger PaLM variant but remains within the same model family. All experiments — search strategies (Section 5), revision strategies (Section 6), FLOPs-matched comparisons (Section 7), and ablations (Appendices) — are conducted on these two model checkpoints and the MATH test set. The authors do not report any experiments on other benchmarks, other model families, or other task types. No ablation varies the benchmark to test sensitivity of the findings to task properties.

**Mitigation status:** The paper does not address this limitation. It does not claim the findings are universal beyond the studied setting, nor does it test generalization by running experiments on an additional benchmark or model. Section 8 does not explicitly call for multi-benchmark or multi-model replication as future work (though it does suggest extending to other domains like code generation). The "representative" claim in Section 4 is an assertion, not a tested hypothesis. A practitioner would need to independently validate the approach on their specific domain, model, and task distribution.

### The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate — Sequential Revisions Can Undo Correct Answers

**The assumption or constraint:** The revision model is fine-tuned on trajectories where all in-context answers are *incorrect* and the target is a *correct* answer. It never sees training examples where the current answer is already correct and should be preserved. As a result, at test time, the revision model often "revises" a correct answer into an incorrect one. The paper quantifies this in Section 6.1:

> "approximately 38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones"

This is a direct consequence of the training data construction procedure (Section 6.1): the model learns that its job is to change the answer, not to verify it. When it encounters a correct answer in-context — which happens frequently in longer revision chains where an earlier step produced the right answer — it has no training signal telling it to maintain that correctness. The probability that the model "breaks" a correct answer on any given revision step is 0.38, which compounds quickly over long chains.

**The consequence:** For long sequential revision chains, the correct-to-incorrect reversion rate imposes a practical limit on how much sequential depth is beneficial. Even if the model occasionally produces a correct answer at some intermediate step in the chain, later revisions are likely to overwrite it with an incorrect answer. The paper mitigates this with a within-chain selection mechanism (majority voting or verifier-based selection) that picks the best answer from any point in the chain rather than always taking the final revision. However, this is imperfect: majority voting requires the correct answer to appear more frequently than any incorrect answer across the chain, and verifier-based selection requires the verifier to correctly identify the correct answer among multiple candidates. Neither mechanism is guaranteed to recover a correct answer that appeared only briefly in the chain before being overwritten.

This limitation also means the revision model cannot be used as a "self-verification" mechanism where the model checks its own work and decides whether to revise. The model does not learn to distinguish correct from incorrect in-context answers; it learns to produce a new answer given a context of (usually incorrect) previous attempts. A self-verification capability would require a different training objective — for example, training the model to output a special token when it believes the current answer is correct — which the paper does not explore.

**What evidence exists in the paper:** The 38% reversion rate is reported in Section 6.1. Figure 6 (left) shows the revision model's per-step pass@1 trajectory: while it improves from step 1 (roughly 18.2%) to steps 15–20 (roughly 24–25%), it does not monotonically increase — there are drops where later revisions score lower than earlier ones, consistent with correct answers being overwritten. Figure 6 (right) shows that sequential revisions with majority voting achieve lower accuracy than sequential revisions with best-of-N weighted verifier selection (roughly 38% vs. 41.5% at 64 generations), suggesting majority voting is less effective at recovering overwritten correct answers than the verifier. The within-chain selection mechanisms are described in Section 6.2 and Appendix I but are acknowledged as imperfect patches rather than fundamental solutions.

**Mitigation status:** The paper mitigates the reversion problem with within-chain selection (majority voting or verifier-based selection across all steps in the chain) rather than simply taking the final output. This is a pragmatic fix but does not address the root cause — the model's training data never teaches it to recognize and preserve correct answers. Section 8 does not explicitly discuss improving revision model training to reduce the reversion rate (e.g., by including correct-to-correct trajectories in the training data, or adding a "no revision needed" output token). The ReST$^{EM}$ experiment in Appendix K (Figure 16) suggests that more sophisticated revision model training can backfire — the ReST$^{EM}$-trained model performed substantially worse with sequential revisions than the base revision model, with fully sequential performance dropping to roughly 33.5% at 256 generations compared to roughly 38.5% at the optimal ratio. This indicates that the revision training procedure is sensitive to data generation methodology in ways that are not fully understood, and that the 38% reversion rate may be difficult to improve without introducing new failure modes.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper fundamentally reframes the community's understanding of what reinforcement learning does when applied to LLM fine-tuning for reasoning. Rather than positioning RL as a discovery mechanism that enables models to acquire qualitatively new capabilities — the narrative inherited from AlphaGo and similar successes — the paper presents evidence that **RL fine-tuning primarily surfaces and reorganizes knowledge already latent in the pretrained model**. This is a diagnostic shift, not a paradigm shift, but its implications for research prioritization are substantial.

The central reframing is that algorithm choice — whether one uses PPO, Expert Iteration, or return-conditioned supervised learning — matters surprisingly little when the reward signal is reliable and the model starts from a strong pretrained initialization. The paper demonstrates this across two model scales (7B and 13B), two datasets (GSM8K and SVAMP), multiple reward structures (sparse, dense heuristic, ORM-guided), and both SFT-initialized and pretrained-only starting points. In nearly every configuration, Expert Iteration — a simple pipeline of rejection sampling followed by supervised fine-tuning on high-reward trajectories — matches or exceeds PPO's performance while requiring comparable or even lower sample complexity once the sampling rate is tuned (`K=4` vs. `K=96`, as shown in Figure 5). The gap between PPO and EI rarely exceeds 2–4 percentage points on GSM8K maj@1, and pass@96 convergences across algorithms are within 3 points of each other (Tables 1, 4). For a field where PPO has become the de facto standard for RL-based LLM training — driven by RLHF's documented successes and by PPO's theoretical advantages in exploration and credit assignment — this finding challenges a core assumption: that algorithmic sophistication translates to reasoning gains in proportion to its complexity.

The paper resolves a contradiction that has quietly accumulated in the literature. On one hand, RLHF practitioners consistently find PPO necessary for achieving strong preference alignment against learned reward models (Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai et al., 2022), with simpler filtered-SFT approaches like ReST (Gulcehre et al., 2023) and RAFT (Dong et al., 2023) showing competitive but not dominant results. On the other hand, a separate line of reasoning-focused work has quietly demonstrated that Expert Iteration — often under different names like STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022), rejection fine-tuning (Yuan et al., 2023), or reward-ranked training — achieves strong improvements on math and code benchmarks without PPO's machinery. These two findings appeared contradictory until this paper articulated the **reward reliability boundary condition**: when the reward signal is a perfect ground-truth indicator (as in math word problems where the answer is either right or wrong), EI's approach of discarding all failed trajectories and imitating only successful ones is essentially optimal. When the reward signal comes from a learned proxy that can be over-optimized or gamed (as in RLHF), PPO's KL constraint and clipped objective provide critical protection against reward hacking that EI's unfiltered imitation of high-reward trajectories lacks. The paper makes this explicit in its Discussion (Section 5):

> "The relatively superior performance of PPO over EI on RLHF tasks versus reasoning tasks suggests PPO better mitigates such over-optimization. This is not too surprising since PPO training penalizes student models diverging from the initial policy via both its clipped objective and additional KL-constraint. In contrast, EI training has no such protection built in."

This distinction explains why different sub-communities reached opposite conclusions about the necessity of PPO: they were operating on structurally different optimization problems, not just different tasks. The field can now move beyond "PPO vs. EI" as a tribal debate and toward a more nuanced understanding: the right algorithm depends on reward reliability and the exploration demands of the environment.

The paper also recasts the **SFT tradeoff between greedy accuracy and output diversity** as a problem of static dataset overfitting rather than an inherent property of supervised fine-tuning. Cobbe et al. (2021) documented that continued SFT on math word problems improves maj@1 while degrading pass@96, but didn't provide a mechanistic explanation. This paper quantifies the mechanism directly: SFT for 4 epochs (SFT4) reduces the average number of unique correct solutions per training question from 3.7 to 2.9 compared to 2-epoch SFT (SFT2), and the pass@96 drop from 0.76 to 0.72 tracks this diversity collapse exactly. RL training breaks this tradeoff not by discovering new solutions but by **continuously resampling its training data from the model's own output distribution**, preventing the distributional collapse that occurs when a model overfits to a fixed dataset. This reframes RL as a **regularization mechanism for extended training** rather than a capability expansion tool — a less glamorous but practically important role.

Most importantly, the paper's **exploration bottleneck diagnosis** redirects the research agenda. If different RL algorithms converge to similar performance ceilings with similar sample complexity, if ORM guidance provides at most a sample efficiency boost without improving final accuracy, and if pass@96 plateaus quickly during training while maj@1 continues improving — all findings the paper documents across multiple experimental settings — then the binding constraint on LLM reasoning improvement is not algorithmic sophistication, not reward quality, and not compute budget. It is the narrowness of the exploration distribution that pretrained models sample from. The model already "knows" (in the sense of being able to generate at some non-trivial probability) most of the correct solutions it will ever produce, and RL training primarily increases the probability mass on those known solutions rather than discovering unknown ones. This insight makes certain research directions less attractive — marginal improvements to PPO's advantage estimation, better reward shaping, or more sophisticated value function architectures are unlikely to yield breakthroughs if exploration is the true bottleneck — and makes other directions more attractive, particularly methods that explicitly expand the model's sampling diversity beyond its pretrained modes.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Directly testing the exploration bottleneck hypothesis with explicit diversity incentives.** The paper diagnoses limited exploration as the primary bottleneck but provides only circumstantial evidence (converging pass@96 ceilings, comparable sample complexity, minimal ORM benefit). A direct test would add an explicit diversity bonus — for example, a reward term proportional to the semantic distance between a generated solution and all previously generated correct solutions to the same question, measured by embedding cosine distance or by extracted computation-trace edit distance — to both PPO and EI, and measure whether this shifts the pass@96 ceiling upward. If exploration is the binding constraint, diversity-augmented RL should achieve meaningfully higher pass@96 than both standard RL and light SFT. If it does not — if pass@96 remains stuck near the SFT2 level despite explicit diversity pressure — then the bottleneck is not exploration but something more fundamental (e.g., the model genuinely does not encode alternative solution strategies for these problems, and no amount of sampling diversity can surface what isn't there). Such an experiment would distinguish between "the model knows more than it samples" (exploration bottleneck) and "the model has already learned everything it can for these tasks" (pretraining capacity ceiling). The paper provides baselines: SFT2 pass@96 = 0.76 sets the "light SFT" diversity floor that a diversity-augmented method must exceed to claim genuine exploration benefits.

**Combining Expert Iteration with PPO-style KL constraints for learned-reward settings.** The paper argues that PPO's advantage over EI in RLHF stems from its KL penalty protecting against reward model over-optimization, but it never directly tests this hypothesis. A clean experiment would train a learned ORM on GSM8K data, then run both standard EI (filtering by ORM score rather than ground-truth correctness, following the RAFT recipe from Dong et al., 2023) and a modified EI that adds a KL penalty to its SFT objective — i.e., minimize negative log-likelihood of high-reward solutions while penalizing divergence from the initial policy, similar to PPO's combined objective. This would isolate whether the KL penalty alone accounts for PPO's RLHF advantage, or whether PPO's on-policy data collection and advantage estimation provide additional protection. The prediction from the paper's framework: EI + KL penalty should match or exceed PPO when optimizing against a learned reward model, making PPO's architectural complexity unnecessary even in the RLHF setting. A null result (EI + KL still underperforms PPO against a learned verifier) would indicate that PPO's on-policy data collection provides protection against reward hacking that goes beyond the KL constraint's distributional anchoring.

**Computing verifier over-optimization scaling laws as a function of verifier quality.** The paper identifies verifier over-optimization as the ceiling on test-time compute scaling but doesn't systematically map the relationship between verifier quality and search effectiveness. One could train a family of PRMs of varying quality — e.g., by varying the number of Monte Carlo rollouts used for training labels (2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 rollouts per step), or by varying the PRM model size — and then run beam search at increasing budgets against each PRM, measuring the point at which search performance peaks and then degrades due to over-optimization. This would produce an "over-optimization frontier" showing how much test-time compute each verifier quality level can usefully absorb before the PRM signal becomes counterproductive. The paper already provides the methodology for this: Figure 3 shows over-optimization curves for a single PRM at a single quality level, and Appendix D describes the PRM training procedure. Scaling this up would convert a diagnostic observation into a predictive tool: given a verifier's validation accuracy, one could estimate the maximum useful search budget, enabling practitioners to avoid throwing compute at search beyond the verifier's reliability frontier.

**Reproducing the study on a benchmark requiring genuinely novel reasoning strategies.** One alternative explanation for the paper's finding that RL doesn't expand the capability frontier is that GSM8K and SVAMP are too close to the pretraining distribution — Llama-2 has likely seen extensive grade-school math during pretraining, meaning the model already "knows" these problems and RL is just surfacing latent knowledge. To test whether RL can discover genuinely novel reasoning, one would need a benchmark where the base model's pass@1 is effectively zero and the reasoning patterns required are unlikely to appear in pretraining data. Examples include: (a) synthetic reasoning tasks with novel rule systems (e.g., a made-up board game with rules defined in the prompt), where no amount of pretraining could have encoded the solution strategy; (b) competition math problems from recent years (post-dating the model's training cutoff) that require creative problem-solving rather than pattern matching; (c) proof-generation tasks in formal mathematics where the solution space is combinatorially large and the model cannot rely on memorized proof templates. If RL — particularly PPO with its exploration mechanisms — can bootstrap from near-zero accuracy to non-trivial performance on such tasks while EI cannot, it would rehabilitate PPO's role as a discovery engine and bound the paper's "exploration bottleneck" finding to tasks within the pretraining distribution. Conversely, if all methods fail equally, it would strengthen the paper's conclusion and refocus the field on better exploration mechanisms (evolutionary methods, tree search, explicit curiosity rewards) rather than on optimizing existing RL algorithms.

**Process-supervised Expert Iteration with step-level filtering.** The paper's EI discards entire solutions based on terminal correctness (sparse reward). This throws away substantial information: a solution with a correct first half and an error in the final step contains valuable training signal for the early steps. The paper's RCRL experiments attempt to capture this via step-level `[GOOD]`/`[BAD]` labels but fail because the model cannot learn the distinction. A more promising approach would use a process reward model (PRM) — or even the paper's stepwise ORM (SORM) approximation described in Appendix F — to filter at the step level rather than the solution level during EI data construction. Specifically: for each generated solution, use the PRM to score each intermediate step, keep only steps above a confidence threshold, and construct partial-solution training data from the surviving prefixes. This would extract more training signal from the exploration budget, particularly from partially-correct solutions that current EI discards entirely. The paper's RCRL results (Table 1, Appendix A) suggest the 7B model struggles to learn from negative examples, but step-level *positive* filtering (keeping only high-confidence steps regardless of the terminal outcome) is distinct from step-level *negative* labeling (telling the model which steps are bad) and may succeed where RCRL failed. A concrete experiment: run EI with `K=96` samples per question, use the paper's PRM (trained in Section 5.1) to score and filter at the step level, train on surviving prefixes, and compare convergence speed and final accuracy to standard solution-level EI.

**Stress-testing the revision model with correct-to-correct trajectories.** The paper documents a 38% correct-to-incorrect reversion rate that limits sequential revision effectiveness, attributing it to the training data construction that never shows the model what to do when the current answer is already correct. A direct test of whether this is fixable: construct a training dataset where some fraction of trajectories include "correct → correct" transitions — i.e., the model sees examples where the in-context answer is already correct and the target is to output it unchanged (or output a special `[CORRECT]` token indicating no revision needed). Train a revision model on this augmented dataset and measure the reversion rate. If the reversion rate drops substantially (from 38% toward single digits), sequential revision chains could become much more effective, since each correct answer would be preserved rather than at risk of being overwritten. The paper's existing evaluation framework — Figures 6 and 7, which measure sequential revision chain accuracy — provides a direct comparison point. A null result (the model cannot learn to distinguish correct from incorrect in-context answers even with explicit training) would suggest a more fundamental limitation of the revision approach and push research toward architectural solutions (separate verifier and reviser models) rather than data augmentation solutions.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Budget-constrained LLM fine-tuning for domain-specific reasoning tasks.** Organizations with limited compute budgets that need to improve an LLM's performance on their specific reasoning task (e.g., financial analysis, medical diagnosis support, legal reasoning) should default to Expert Iteration rather than PPO. The paper provides direct empirical justification: on both GSM8K (Table 1: EI achieves 0.48 vs. PPO's 0.46 for 7B SFT-initialized) and SVAMP (Table 3: EI achieves 0.58 vs. PPO's 0.44 for 7B pretrained), EI matches or exceeds PPO while being substantially simpler to implement — no value function, no advantage estimation, no KL penalty tuning, no joint architecture with gradient detachment. For a practitioner, this means: (1) fine-tune the base model on available SFT data if any exists, (2) sample `K=4−96` solutions per training question from the current policy at `T=1.0`, (3) filter to keep only correct solutions with deduplication, (4) fine-tune the pretrained base model (reset, don't continue) on the filtered dataset, (5) repeat until validation accuracy plateaus (typically 2–5 rounds). The paper's `K=4` ablation (Section 4.2, Figure 10) demonstrates that even with only 4 samples per question, EI converges to within a few points of the `K=96` variant while using an order of magnitude fewer rollouts, making this viable even with modest generation budgets. An organization that was previously using PPO for reasoning fine-tuning (following RLHF conventions) can switch to EI and reduce infrastructure complexity (no value head training, no advantage estimation, no sequential rollout generation) while maintaining or improving performance.

**Continual fine-tuning pipelines where output diversity matters.** For applications where the model must maintain the ability to generate multiple valid approaches to the same problem — tutoring systems that should show different solution methods, code generation where stylistic variety is valued, or ensemble systems that rely on diverse candidate solutions — RL fine-tuning (either EI or PPO) provides a concrete advantage over extended SFT, which progressively narrows output diversity. Table 4 quantifies this directly: SFT4 achieves higher maj@1 than SFT2 (0.41 vs. 0.36) but loses output diversity (2.9 unique correct solutions per question vs. 3.7) and suffers a 4-point pass@96 drop (0.72 vs. 0.76). PPO fine-tuning from SFT4 recovers both metrics simultaneously: maj@1 rises to 0.44 while pass@96 returns to 0.77. A practitioner who has already trained an SFT model and notices that it has become overly narrow (producing the same solution pattern repeatedly) can use a single round of EI or a moderate number of PPO steps to restore diversity without sacrificing greedy accuracy. The paper's finding that this occurs because RL generates its own online training data rather than reusing a static dataset means the benefit applies to any domain where the reward signal is reliable — the method is not specific to math reasoning.

**Algorithm selection based on reward reliability assessment.** The paper provides a concrete decision rule for practitioners choosing between PPO and EI: ask whether the reward signal can be trusted to have near-zero false positive rate. If the reward is a ground-truth correctness check (math answers, code execution results, database query correctness, unit test pass/fail), use EI. The paper's evidence: EI beats or matches PPO across all ground-truth reward experiments (Tables 1–3). If the reward is a learned model that might assign high scores to outputs that aren't actually good (a preference model, a quality classifier, a learned verifier that hasn't been adversarially validated), use PPO with a non-trivial KL penalty, because EI's unfiltered imitation of high-reward trajectories will amplify any reward model errors. While the paper doesn't directly test this learned-reward scenario on reasoning tasks, it provides the theoretical argument in Section 5, and the RLHF literature provides corroboration (Gulcehre et al., 2023; Dubois et al., 2023). A practitioner building a system where the reward signal comes from an LLM-as-judge or a learned reward model should default to PPO with KL penalty `β ≈ 0.05−0.1` (the range the paper found necessary even for clean-reward reasoning tasks) unless they have strong evidence that their reward model is adversarially robust.
