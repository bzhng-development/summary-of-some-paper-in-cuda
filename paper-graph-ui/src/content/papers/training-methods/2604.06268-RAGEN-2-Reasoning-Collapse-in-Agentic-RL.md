# RAGEN-2: Reasoning Collapse in Agentic RL

**ArXiv:** [2604.06268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06268)

## 🎯 Pitch

LLMs trained with RL can appear fine—stable entropy, good rewards—while secretly adopting input-blind reasoning templates that look diverse per prompt but never actually respond to what you ask. The paper demonstrates this "template collapse" is invisible to standard monitoring, proposes a mutual-information proxy that catches it in real time, and shows that filtering training data by reward variance can stop the collapse and recover performance.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper identifies and analyzes a failure mode in multi-turn LLM agent reinforcement learning called **template collapse**, where reasoning appears diverse within any single input but becomes input-agnostic across different inputs—a form of degradation invisible to standard entropy-based stability metrics. Through experiments across planning, math reasoning, web navigation, and code execution tasks using Qwen2.5 models trained with PPO, GRPO, DAPO, and Dr. GRPO, the authors introduce a mutual information (MI) proxy family (in-batch cross-scoring of reasoning traces against prompts) that detects template collapse by measuring cross-input distinguishability, and demonstrate that MI correlates with task performance far more strongly than entropy (+0.39 Spearman for Trajectory MI-ZScore versus −0.11 to −0.14 for entropy metrics). The paper explains template collapse through a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) mechanism—low within-prompt reward variance weakens task gradients while input-agnostic regularization terms (KL divergence and entropy bonuses) remain constant and dominate updates—and proposes **SNR-Aware Filtering**, which prioritizes high-reward-variance prompts before each parameter update, improving both input dependence and task performance across algorithms, model scales (0.5B–7B), and modalities, establishing that test-time compute allocation can systematically preserve reasoning quality only when reward variance provides sufficient task-discriminative signal.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Reasoning Can Collapse Silently During RL Training

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is deceptively subtle: **when you train a multi-turn LLM agent with reinforcement learning, its reasoning quality can degrade in a way that standard monitoring metrics completely miss.** The degradation isn't a catastrophic crash in task performance or an obvious spike in loss—it's a gradual drift toward fluent, plausible-sounding outputs that look diverse when examined within any single prompt but are effectively identical templates when compared across different prompts. The authors call this **template collapse**, and its defining feature is that it happens while the metrics researchers typically trust—entropy and reward—remain stable or even appear healthy.

This matters because the dominant paradigm for building capable LLM agents increasingly involves closed-loop RL training [34, 72, 68]. In such pipelines, an agent repeatedly generates trajectories (reasoning + actions), receives environment feedback, and updates its policy from self-collected experience. Researchers monitor two stability signals during this process: **reward** (are outcomes improving and stable?) and **entropy** (is the model maintaining diversity in its outputs?) [42, 32, 62]. When both look good, the assumption is that training is proceeding healthily.

Template collapse breaks this assumption. The paper shows empirically that a model can maintain high conditional entropy $\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$ — its reasoning appears varied and non-degenerate within each input — while simultaneously losing cross-input distinguishability $\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$ — the reasoning it produces for different inputs becomes effectively interchangeable. Entropy monitors only the first quantity, not the second. This creates a dangerous blind spot: a practitioner watching training curves might see stable entropy, stable reward, and no obvious failures, while the model is quietly learning to substitute genuinely input-driven reasoning with reusable boilerplate that happens to score well enough under the sparse reward signals available in multi-turn environments.

The practical stakes are high. Agents trained this way become **unreliable in deployment**: they produce fluent responses that satisfy surface-level expectations but fail to adapt their reasoning to the specific demands of novel inputs. In safety-critical applications (medical reasoning, code generation for production systems, autonomous planning), such brittleness could have serious consequences. Even in lower-stakes settings, it undermines the whole premise of using RL to improve agent capabilities—the model isn't actually learning to reason better; it's learning to exploit reward structure with templated behavior while the diagnostic tools say everything is fine.

### Why Entropy Alone Is Insufficient

The paper's central insight about entropy is worth unpacking carefully because it explains why this problem has been invisible to the field. Recall the information-theoretic identity:

$$\mathbf{H}(Z) = \mathbf{I}(X; Z) + \mathbf{H}(Z|X)$$

Here, $\mathbf{H}(Z)$ is the total diversity of the model's reasoning outputs, $\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$ is the diversity within each input (what entropy metrics typically measure), and $\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$ is the mutual information between inputs and reasoning—how much knowing the input tells you about what the reasoning will look like. When researchers track entropy, they're effectively measuring $\mathbf{H}(Z)$ or $\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$. A stable or high value of either is interpreted as evidence that the model hasn't collapsed into a single repetitive mode.

But this equation makes clear what that interpretation misses: $\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$ can remain high while $\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$ drops to near zero. The policy simply needs to produce diverse-but-input-agnostic outputs. To make this concrete, imagine an agent trained on math problems. A healthy model might produce reasoning chains like "Let $x$ be the unknown quantity, then from the problem statement we know $2x + 5 = 13$, so $x = 4$" for one problem and "This is a geometry question, so I'll draw a triangle and apply the Pythagorean theorem" for another. An entropy-collapsed model (the "low-entropy collapse" regime in Figure 1) might just output "The answer is 4" for everything. But a **template-collapsed** model might produce "Let me think about this step by step. First, I'll identify the key variables. Then, I'll set up the relevant equations. Finally, I'll solve systematically," for every problem—grammatically diverse within each output, so entropy looks fine, but content-identical across all problems. The words differ, but the reasoning skeleton doesn't change with the input.

This is precisely what makes template collapse an important contribution to identify: it exploits a gap in our collective monitoring practice. The field has converged on entropy as a proxy for reasoning health because entropy collapse is a real and well-documented failure mode [7, 61, 63, 69]. The natural extension—that stable entropy implies healthy reasoning—turns out to be false in a specific, diagnosable way that the paper characterizes.

### Where Existing Stabilization Methods Fall Short

The paper connects template collapse to a broader limitation of existing RL stabilization techniques for LLM agents. The standard stabilization toolkit includes:

**KL divergence constraints** that keep the policy close to a reference model (usually the pre-trained or supervised-fine-tuned checkpoint) [40, 42, 41]. These prevent the policy from drifting into degenerate regions of output space, but they act uniformly across all inputs—they encourage the policy to stay near the reference distribution regardless of whether that distribution is producing input-adaptive or input-agnostic reasoning for a particular prompt.

**Entropy regularization** that explicitly rewards the policy for maintaining high output diversity [62, 32]. This prevents the policy from collapsing to a single high-probability output (the classic entropy collapse mode), but as the decomposition above shows, it can sustain or even increase $\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$ while $\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$ drops. In fact, the paper argues that aggressive entropy regularization may exacerbate template collapse: by encouraging diversity within each input, it can actually make it *easier* for the model to learn diverse templated outputs rather than investing in input-specific reasoning (Section 5.1, Figure 13).

**Gradient clipping, learning rate scheduling, and replay buffers** [13, 49, 36] that control optimization dynamics but don't address the fundamental issue: when most rollouts for a prompt receive similar rewards regardless of reasoning quality, the gradient signal carries minimal information about which reasoning paths are useful for that specific input [29, 31, 47, 69].

The paper's framing is that these methods primarily control within-input diversity ($\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$) and cannot directly address the signal-to-noise imbalance that drives cross-input dependence ($\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$) to zero. This is why they've been developed to prevent entropy collapse while template collapse has gone undetected—the metrics and the methods are aligned on the same axis, and both miss the orthogonal one.

### Prior Observations of Related Phenomena

Template collapse doesn't appear from nowhere. The paper connects it to several lines of prior observation that, taken together, suggest a systematic but poorly understood failure mode:

**Reasoning collapse in DeepSeek-R1 style training.** When training models like DeepSeek-R1 [7] with RL to incentivize chain-of-thought reasoning, practitioners have observed that reasoning can become "templated" over time—formulaic, repetitive, less responsive to input specifics [61, 63, 69]. This is typically grouped under the label "reasoning collapse" alongside entropy decline, making it hard to isolate whether the problem is loss of diversity, loss of input-sensitivity, or both.

**Policy degeneracy in agent RL.** In multi-turn agent settings, concurrent work has documented "policy degeneracy" where agents converge to a small set of easy-to-reproduce action patterns [9, 59]. The behavior still scores well on sparse rewards, but exploration collapses and the agent stops adapting to novel situations. The paper argues this behavioral narrowing may actually follow reasoning collapse: the model first loses input-adaptive reasoning, then loses behavioral diversity as a downstream consequence [59, 61].

**Model collapse in self-training.** A broader literature documents that when models train on their own generated data (in self-training, self-distillation, or iterative fine-tuning loops), the feedback loop can narrow the effective data distribution, amplify high-probability modes, and suppress long-tail behaviors—even when average quality metrics look stable [12, 47]. Template collapse fits this pattern but occurs specifically through the gradient dynamics of RL, not through data distribution shift alone.

The paper's contribution is to unify these scattered observations under a single, precisely defined failure mode with a clear diagnostic (the MI proxy) and a mechanistic explanation (the SNR gradient dynamics). Prior work either lumped input-sensitivity loss together with diversity loss under "reasoning collapse" [61, 63] or observed it indirectly through behavioral tests [11, 37, 73] that can't scale to online monitoring.

### The Methodological Gap: No Online Diagnostic for Input Dependence

A critical practical gap the paper identifies is the absence of **scalable, online diagnostics for reasoning's input dependence**. Prior methods for measuring input-conditioned reasoning fall into several categories, all insufficient for real-time RL monitoring:

**Lexical diversity metrics** like n-gram statistics, self-BLEU [20, 74] measure surface-level output variation but can't distinguish prompt-driven variation from templated variation. A template that varies its word choice randomly will score highly on lexical diversity while being completely input-agnostic.

**Embedding-based dispersion** measures like MAUVE [33] or self-BLEU variants [53] capture distributional properties of output sets but conflate within-prompt and cross-prompt sources of variation. They can detect overall diversity loss but not the specific pattern of preserved within-input diversity with collapsed between-input distinguishability.

**Behavioral testing** approaches [11, 37, 73] construct contrast sets and measure whether model outputs change appropriately when inputs are perturbed. These directly probe input dependence but require carefully designed test suites per domain—they're evaluation tools, not training monitors that can run on every RL batch.

**Retrieval-based prompt reconstruction** [28, 10, 71, 19] asks whether a model's output can be matched back to its source input. This is conceptually close to the paper's MI proxy, but existing work uses it primarily for security (extracting prompts from model outputs) or static evaluation, not as an online training diagnostic.

The paper's in-batch cross-scoring method fills this gap: it reuses the rollout data already collected for RL training, requires no external models or additional inference passes, and produces a continuously trackable signal (the full MI proxy family from Table 1) that correlates with downstream performance. This transforms input-dependence monitoring from a separate evaluation concern into an integrated training diagnostic.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions its contribution along three complementary axes:

**Diagnostic.** It introduces a new category of training monitor—mutual information proxies between inputs and reasoning—that measures a dimension of reasoning quality (cross-input distinguishability) orthogonal to what entropy tracks (within-input diversity). This isn't just a better version of existing metrics; it's a fundamentally different measurement target that happens to be observable with no additional compute beyond what training already requires. The paper argues that MI and entropy together provide a complete picture of the reasoning-quality space defined by the decomposition $\mathbf{H}(Z) = \mathbf{I}(X; Z) + \mathbf{H}(Z|X)$, and that monitoring only half of this space (as current practice does) leaves a large blind spot.

**Mechanistic.** Rather than just documenting template collapse, the paper provides a gradient-level explanation through the SNR framework. This explanation makes falsifiable predictions (that low reward variance causes loss of input dependence, that injecting noise should predictably worsen MI, that filtering high-RV prompts should restore input dependence) and ties template collapse to specific, measurable quantities in the training process. This moves the discussion from "reasoning sometimes degrades during RL" to "degradation occurs through this specific mechanism, which we can detect and intervene on."

**Intervention.** SNR-Aware Filtering is presented not as a standalone "method" to be adopted wholesale, but as a **general-purpose SNR control knob** that works alongside existing stabilization techniques (KL constraints, entropy regularization). The paper emphasizes this in Section 4.3: "[Filtering] works alongside standard stabilization terms (e.g., KL and entropy regularization)." This positioning is deliberate—rather than competing with the existing stabilization toolkit, the paper proposes adding a new control dimension (signal quality via prompt selection) that complements the existing ones (noise control via regularization).

The paper also positions itself as building directly on the RAGEN testbed [60], which provided the multi-turn agent RL infrastructure and baselines. By using an established evaluation framework, the paper can focus on the conceptual contributions (template collapse identification, MI diagnostics, SNR mechanism, filtering intervention) without needing to establish new training pipelines or benchmarks. The choice to evaluate across seven environments (Sokoban, FrozenLake, MetaMathQA, Countdown, SearchQA, WebShop, DeepCoder; Table 3) rather than a single benchmark strengthens the claim that template collapse is a general phenomenon, not a quirk of a particular task.

Finally, the paper draws a connection to broader concerns about closed-loop training stability. The finding that template collapse can occur while all monitored metrics look stable connects to anxieties about **silent failures in self-improving AI systems**. If an agent can degrade its reasoning quality in a way invisible to standard diagnostics, then pipelines that rely on automated monitoring to detect performance regressions (e.g., continual learning systems, self-play training loops) are vulnerable. The MI proxy offers a concrete tool for closing this monitoring gap, and the SNR mechanism suggests that the underlying cause—low reward variance allowing regularization to dominate—may be structural to RL training on sparse-reward tasks, not a temporary implementation issue.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

This paper develops a **diagnostic framework and intervention mechanism** for multi-turn LLM agent reinforcement learning, not a new RL algorithm. The system is a monitoring-and-filtering pipeline that sits alongside standard RL training: it continuously measures whether the model's reasoning is genuinely responsive to different inputs (using mutual information proxies computed from the training rollouts themselves) and, when it detects signal quality dropping, selectively filters which prompts contribute to gradient updates (using reward variance as a lightweight proxy for signal strength). The core idea is that reasoning quality decomposes into two independent dimensions — within-input diversity and cross-input distinguishability — and that standard entropy monitoring only covers the first, leaving a systematic blind spot where reasoning can become input-agnostic while appearing healthy.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The system has three major components that operate in a loop during RL training:

1. **Rollout Generation** — the standard RL loop: the policy (an LLM) receives prompts, generates reasoning + action trajectories, interacts with the environment, and receives scalar rewards. This produces `$P$` prompts with `$G$` trajectories per prompt (`$P \times G$` total trajectories per iteration; typically `$P=8, G=16$` or variations as in Table 5).

2. **Mutual Information Monitor** — a diagnostic layer that takes the rollout data `$(X_i, Z_{i,k})$` (prompts and their associated reasoning traces) and computes in-batch cross-scoring: every reasoning trace is scored against every prompt in the batch using the policy's own log-likelihoods. From this scoring matrix, it derives both continuous MI proxies (MI-ZScore-EMA) and discrete retrieval metrics (Retrieval-Acc) that quantify how distinguishable different prompts' reasoning patterns are. This requires no additional models or forward passes — it reuses log-probabilities already computed during generation.

3. **SNR-Aware Filtering** — an intervention layer that, before each parameter update, computes within-prompt reward variance `$\hat{\text{Var}}(R|X)$` for each prompt group, ranks prompts by this variance, and retains only the top fraction (controlled by keep rate `$\rho \in (0, 1]$`, typically `$\rho = 0.9$` for top-p filtering). The policy update is then performed only on the retained high-variance prompt groups, concentrating gradient budget on updates where advantage estimates carry meaningful task-discriminative information.

Information flows sequentially: rollouts are generated → MI proxies are computed from the rollout data for monitoring → reward variance is computed per prompt group → prompts are ranked and filtered → surviving prompts contribute to the policy gradient update. The MI monitor runs continuously for diagnostics; the SNR-Aware Filtering runs every iteration to control which data enters the update.

#### The Mutual Information Proxy: Computing Input Dependence

The fundamental challenge is that true mutual information `$\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$` between prompts `$X$` and reasoning traces `$Z$` has no closed-form expression for high-dimensional token sequences produced by an LLM. The paper circumvents this by constructing an empirical proxy based on a retrieval intuition: if reasoning is truly input-dependent, then a reasoning trace `$Z$` generated from prompt `$X_i$` should be much more likely under `$X_i$` than under a different prompt `$X_j$`. If reasoning has collapsed to input-agnostic templates, a trace `$Z$` should be roughly equally likely under all prompts.

**The In-Batch Cross-Scoring Procedure**

The procedure operates on the rollout data already collected for RL training. Given `$P$` prompts and `$G$` reasoning samples per prompt (from a single training iteration), the method constructs a scoring matrix by computing the teacher-forced log-likelihood of every reasoning trace under every prompt:

> $$L_{i,k,j} = \log p_\theta(Z_{i,k} \mid X_j)$$

where `$i \in \{1, \ldots, P\}$` indexes the source prompt, `$k \in \{1, \ldots, G\}$` indexes the trajectory within that prompt group, `$j \in \{1, \ldots, P\}$` indexes the evaluation prompt, `$Z_{i,k}$` is the `$k$`-th reasoning trace generated from prompt `$X_i$`, and `$p_\theta$` is the current policy.

**What it computes:** For each of the `$P \times G$` reasoning traces in the batch, this produces `$P$` log-likelihood scores — one under each prompt in the batch. The result is a three-dimensional tensor of size `$P \times G \times P$` where `$L_{i,k,j}$` answers: "How well does the model think reasoning trace `$k$` from prompt `$i$` fits prompt `$j$`?"

**Why this form:** Teacher-forced log-likelihood (where the model is given the prompt and the full reasoning trace and asked to compute the probability of that exact token sequence) is the natural scoring function because it measures compatibility under the policy's own probability distribution without requiring any generation. An alternative like embedding similarity would require an external model and wouldn't directly reflect the policy's own assessment. The cross-scoring across all batch prompts is necessary because mutual information is fundamentally about distinguishability: a trace must be _more_ likely under its source prompt than under the mixture of all prompts.

From this scoring matrix, two key quantities are extracted per reasoning trace:

> $$\text{matched}_{i,k} = \frac{L_{i,k,i}}{|Z_{i,k}|}, \quad \text{marginal}_{i,k} = \frac{1}{|Z_{i,k}|} \log \frac{1}{P} \sum_{j=1}^{P} \exp(L_{i,k,j})$$

where `$|Z_{i,k}|$` is the sequence length (number of tokens) of the reasoning trace, used for length normalization to prevent longer sequences from dominating the scores.

**What it computes:** `$\text{matched}_{i,k}$` is the per-token log-likelihood of reasoning trace `$Z_{i,k}$` under its _true_ source prompt `$X_i$`. This is the "correct match" score — it measures how compatible the trace is with the prompt that actually generated it. `$\text{marginal}_{i,k}$` is the per-token log-likelihood under a _uniform mixture_ over all `$P$` prompts in the batch — it approximates the marginal probability `$\log p_\theta(Z_{i,k})$` by averaging the likelihoods under all prompts and taking the log. The sum inside the log is the total probability mass under the mixture; the division by `$P$` gives the average per-prompt contribution; the log converts to log-space.

**Why this form:** Length normalization by dividing by `$|Z_{i,k}|$` ensures that traces of different lengths are comparable — without it, a longer trace would accumulate more negative log-likelihood simply because it has more tokens, not because it's less compatible. The marginal is computed as a mixture rather than as the average log-likelihood because probabilities (not log-probabilities) should be averaged: the probability of a trace under a mixture of prompts is the average of its probabilities under each prompt — which, in log-space, is the log-sum-exp of the individual log-probabilities minus `$\log P$`. Using the arithmetic mean of log-likelihoods would be incorrect because it would not correspond to any valid probability distribution.

**From Scores to Mutual Information Proxies**

The paper defines two complementary proxy families derived from `$\text{matched}_{i,k}$` and `$\text{marginal}_{i,k}$`:

**Retrieval-Acc (discrete, interpretable):**

> $$\text{Retrieval-Acc} = \frac{1}{P \cdot G} \sum_{i=1}^{P} \sum_{k=1}^{G} \mathbb{1}\left[i = \arg\max_j L_{i,k,j}\right]$$

where `$\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$` is the indicator function (1 if the condition is true, 0 otherwise).

**What it computes:** For each reasoning trace, this checks whether the prompt with the highest log-likelihood (the "best match" from the cross-scoring matrix) is actually the prompt that generated the trace. The fraction of traces correctly matched to their source prompt is the retrieval accuracy. Under perfect input-dependent reasoning, every trace is most compatible with its own source prompt, giving accuracy = 1.0. Under complete template collapse (where reasoning is identical across prompts), the model's predictions become random, and accuracy approaches chance level `$1/P$` (e.g., 1.56% for `$P=64$`, or 12.5% for `$P=8$`).

**Why this form:** This is an operationalized version of the retrieval intuition — it directly tests whether reasoning traces are distinguishably different across prompts. The `$\arg\max$` selects the single best-matching prompt, making this a hard decision metric that provides an absolute reference point (chance level). This makes it interpretable: an accuracy of 0.5 means the model matches traces to correct prompts twice as often as random guessing would suggest, even if absolute performance is low. The discrete nature also makes it robust to scale differences in log-likelihoods across training steps.

**MI-Est (continuous, raw mutual information estimate):**

> $$\widehat{I}(X; Z) = \frac{1}{P \cdot G} \sum_{i=1}^{P} \sum_{k=1}^{G} \left(\text{matched}_{i,k} - \text{marginal}_{i,k}\right)$$

**What it computes:** This is the empirical average of the difference between the per-token log-likelihood under the true prompt and under the prompt mixture. When the policy produces reasoning that is strongly adapted to each input, `$\text{matched}_{i,k}$` will be substantially larger (less negative) than `$\text{marginal}_{i,k}$`, yielding a positive `$\widehat{I}(X; Z)$`. Under template collapse, the two quantities converge, and `$\widehat{I}(X; Z)$` approaches zero. This directly parallels the information-theoretic definition `$\mathbf{I}(X; Z) = \mathbb{E}[\log p(z|x) - \log p(z)]$`, where the expectation is over the true joint distribution, but here the marginal `$\log p(z)$` is approximated by the in-batch mixture and the expectation is replaced by the empirical average over rollout data.

**Why this form:** The difference `$\text{matched} - \text{marginal}$` is the per-token contribution to mutual information in the Shannon sense, but computed under the policy's own probability model rather than requiring the true (unknown) distribution. This makes it a natural empirical proxy: it preserves the mathematical structure of the theoretical quantity while being computable from finite data using only the policy's log-likelihoods. The averaging over `$P \times G$` traces provides a stable estimate that can be tracked continuously during training. Unlike Retrieval-Acc (which is thresholded at `$\arg\max$`), MI-Est captures the _magnitude_ of distinguishability, not just whether the correct match wins — two prompts could both be distinguishable but to different degrees, and MI-Est captures this gradation.

**MI-ZScore-EMA (continuous, stabilized for training monitoring):**

> $$\text{MI-ZScore} = \frac{1}{P \cdot G} \sum_{i=1}^{P} \sum_{k=1}^{G} \frac{\text{matched}_{i,k} - \text{marginal}_{i,k}}{\sigma_{\text{batch}} + \epsilon}$$

where `$\sigma_{\text{batch}}$` is the standard deviation of the per-trace differences `$\text{matched}_{i,k} - \text{marginal}_{i,k}$` computed across the current batch. The EMA variant additionally smooths the denominator:

> $$\sigma^{(t)}_{\text{EMA}} = \alpha \cdot \sigma^{(t-1)}_{\text{EMA}} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \sigma^{(t)}_{\text{batch}}$$

with `$\alpha = 0.9$` and `$\epsilon = 10^{-3}$`.

**What it computes:** The raw MI-Est is divided by a measure of variability in the current batch, converting the difference into standard-deviation units (a z-score). This normalization removes scale drift: as training progresses and the policy's overall log-likelihoods change (due to improved modeling or distribution shift), the raw MI-Est values can drift up or down even if the relative distinguishability of reasoning is unchanged. The EMA smoothing of the standard deviation estimator provides temporal stability — a single batch may have unusually high or low variance by chance, but the EMA tracks the moving average, making the normalized metric less jumpy. The z-score then represents how many standard deviations above or below the mean distinguishability a given batch's reasoning patterns are.

**Why this form:** Direct tracking of raw MI-Est during training is problematic because policy optimization changes the absolute scale of log-likelihoods. A well-optimized policy will have higher (less negative) log-likelihoods overall, which can inflate the raw MI-Est even if input dependence hasn't improved. Z-score normalization using the batch's own standard deviation removes this confound by measuring distinguishability in relative rather than absolute terms. The EMA further stabilizes the normalization so that a single low-variance batch doesn't produce spuriously high z-scores. The `$\epsilon = 10^{-3}$` prevents division by zero in edge cases where all traces have identical scores.

**The Proxy Variant Family (Table 1)**

The paper defines additional variants by varying three dimensions:

1. **Turn scope:** First-turn variants (`Retrieval-Acc-First`, etc.) use only the reasoning from the first agent turn, while trajectory variants (`Retrieval-Acc-Traj`, etc.) sample reasoning from all turns uniformly. The first-turn variants test whether initial reasoning is input-adaptive before the policy has had a chance to adapt to environment feedback; trajectory variants test whether input-dependence is maintained throughout multi-turn interactions.

2. **Aggregation type:** Discrete retrieval metrics (Retrieval-Acc, Recall@k where `$k \in \{2, 4, 8\}$`) use hard matching decisions; continuous MI estimates (MI-Est, MI-Seq-Est) use the raw log-likelihood differences. Recall@k relaxes the strict `$\arg\max$` criterion: a trace is "correctly retrieved" if its true source prompt is among the top-k highest-scoring prompts.

3. **Length normalization:** Per-token variants (MI-Est, MI-ZScore) normalize by sequence length; per-sequence variants (MI-Seq-Est) use the raw sequence log-likelihood difference `$L_{i,k,i} - \log\frac{1}{P}\sum_j e^{L_{i,k,j}}$` without length normalization. Per-token normalization prevents longer reasoning chains from dominating; per-sequence captures total distinguishability mass, which may be relevant for tasks where longer reasoning inherently provides more input-specific information.

The key insight across all variants is that they reuse `$(X_i, Z_{i,k})$` pairs already collected during training rollouts and require no additional model or inference pass. The cross-scoring matrix `$L_{i,k,j}$` can be computed in a single forward pass by batching all `$P \times G \times P$` prompt-trace pairs, making the computational cost linear in the product of batch sizes but fully parallelizable.

#### The SNR Mechanism: Why Template Collapse Occurs

The diagnostic identifies template collapse; this section explains its mechanistic cause. The paper proposes a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) view of RL policy gradient updates for multi-turn agents. The central claim: **when within-prompt reward variance is low, the task-discriminative component of the gradient weakens while input-agnostic regularization components remain constant, causing updates to be dominated by noise that pushes reasoning toward input-agnostic templates.**

**Gradient Decomposition: Where Does Each Component Come From?**

The standard PPO/GRPO objective for LLM agent training includes three types of terms:

> $$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x,\tau} \left[A(\tau, x)\right] - \lambda_{\text{KL}} D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \parallel \pi_{\text{ref}}) + \lambda_H H(\pi_\theta)$$

where `$A(\tau, x)$` is the advantage (how much better this trajectory's reward is than expected for this prompt), `$D_{\text{KL}}$` is KL divergence from a reference policy (usually the pre-trained model), and `$H(\pi_\theta)$` is the policy entropy. The gradient of this objective decomposes into three components with fundamentally different properties:

1. **Task gradient `$g_{\text{task}}(x)$`**: the gradient from the advantage-weighted policy gradient term. For a fixed prompt `$x$`, this is `$g_{\text{task}}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[A(z; x) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(z|x)]$`. The advantage `$A(z; x) = R(z; x) - b(x)$` measures whether a specific trajectory `$z$` earned more or less reward than the baseline `$b(x) = \mathbb{E}[R | X=x]$` (the average reward for that prompt). When rewards vary meaningfully across trajectories for the same prompt, the advantage is large in magnitude for good trajectories and small or negative for bad ones, giving the gradient a clear direction: increase probability of high-reward trajectories, decrease probability of low-reward ones.

2. **Regularization gradient `$g_{\text{reg}}(x)$`**: the gradient from the KL penalty and entropy bonus terms. The KL term `$\nabla_\theta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \parallel \pi_{\text{ref}})$` pulls the policy toward the reference distribution for _every_ trajectory, regardless of whether that trajectory earned high or low reward. The entropy term `$\nabla_\theta H(\pi_\theta)$` encourages the policy to spread probability mass across multiple possible outputs. Both act uniformly: they don't multiply by advantage or reward, so they apply the same "force" to every reasoning chain produced for every prompt.

3. **Task noise `$g_{\text{task-noise}}(x)$`**: the component of the task gradient that arises from sampling noise rather than true reward structure. Even for a prompt where all trajectories would have identical expected reward (under infinite samples), the finite-sample advantage estimates have variance due to environment stochasticity, sampling randomness, and reward function noise. This noise component averages to zero in expectation but contributes variance to the gradient estimate.

The total gradient is `$g_{\text{total}}(x) = g_{\text{signal}}(x) + g_{\text{task-noise}}(x) + g_{\text{reg}}(x)$`, where `$g_{\text{signal}}(x)$` is the "true" task-discriminative component (what the task gradient would be with infinite data and zero noise) and `$g_{\text{task}}(x) = g_{\text{signal}}(x) + g_{\text{task-noise}}(x)$`.

**The SNR Formulation**

The signal-to-noise ratio for a prompt `$x$` is defined as the ratio of the signal gradient norm to the noise gradient norms:

> $$\text{SNR}(x) = \frac{\|g_{\text{signal}}(x)\|}{\|g_{\text{task-noise}}(x)\| + \|g_{\text{reg}}\|}$$

**What it computes:** The numerator is the magnitude of the gradient component that genuinely improves task performance by discriminating between good and bad trajectories. The denominator is the sum of magnitudes of gradient components that push the policy in directions uncorrelated with task performance — sampling noise from finite trajectory counts and input-agnostic regularization forces. A high SNR means the update is mostly moving toward better task performance; a low SNR means the update is mostly random drift or regularization-driven contraction.

**Why this form:** This decomposition separates components by their relationship to the task signal. `$g_{\text{signal}}$` varies across prompts (some prompts have clearer reward differences between good and bad trajectories than others) and is the component we want to maximize. `$g_{\text{task-noise}}$` also varies across prompts (prompts with inherently noisy rewards have higher task noise) but carries no useful information. `$g_{\text{reg}}$` is constant across prompts — every reasoning chain gets the same KL penalty and entropy bonus regardless of its source prompt or reward. This flatness of `$g_{\text{reg}}$` is the key mechanism: when `$g_{\text{signal}}$` is weak (low reward variance), `$g_{\text{reg}}$` doesn't weaken correspondingly, so it dominates the total update and pushes the policy in input-agnostic directions.

**The Formal Bound: Why Reward Variance Controls Task Gradient Magnitude**

The paper proves a bound that formalizes the relationship between reward variance and gradient strength (Theorem H.2 in Appendix H). Given the advantage baseline `$b(x) = \mathbb{E}[R | X=x]$` (the conditional mean reward for prompt `$x$`):

> $$\|g_{\text{task}}(x)\| \leq \sqrt{\text{Var}(R \mid X=x)} \cdot \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(z|x)\|^2 \mid X=x\right]}$$

where `$\text{Var}(R \mid X=x)$` is the within-prompt reward variance (`$\text{RV}(x)$`) and the second term is the expected squared norm of the score function (policy gradient) for that prompt.

**What it computes:** This inequality says that the magnitude of the task gradient for a prompt cannot exceed a value proportional to the square root of that prompt's reward variance, multiplied by a term that measures how much the policy's log-probabilities change with its parameters for trajectories from that prompt. If `$\text{RV}(x)$` is near zero (all trajectories for prompt `$x$` get nearly identical rewards), then `$\|g_{\text{task}}(x)\|$` must be near zero — the task gradient is _provably_ weak, regardless of the score function magnitude.

**Why this form:** The bound follows from Cauchy-Schwarz: `$\|g_{\text{task}}(x)\| = \|\mathbb{E}[A \cdot s]\| \leq \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[A^2]} \cdot \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[\|s\|^2]}$`, and `$\mathbb{E}[A^2] = \text{Var}(R)$` because the advantage is zero-mean by construction (the baseline subtracts the expected reward). This makes the bound tight in the sense that it captures exactly the variance component without additional looseness from, say, bounding the advantage by its maximum rather than its root-mean-square. The practical implication: prompts where the environment gives indistinguishable feedback (all trajectories look equally good or bad) produce vanishing task gradients, leaving only regularization and noise to drive updates.

**The Empirical Gradient Norm Evidence (Figure 3)**

The paper tests this mechanism by sorting training prompts into six equal-sized buckets based on within-prompt reward variance (Q1 = highest variance, Q6 = lowest) and measuring the gradient norms of task and regularization components:

1. **Task gradient norm increases monotonically with reward variance.** High-variance prompts produce strong task gradients; low-variance prompts produce weak task gradients. This confirms the theoretical bound in practice: reward variance controls task gradient strength.

2. **Regularization gradient norm is flat across buckets.** The KL and entropy gradient magnitudes are approximately constant regardless of reward variance. This is the critical asymmetry: `$\|g_{\text{reg}}\|$` doesn't weaken when `$\|g_{\text{task}}\|$` does, so the _relative_ influence of regularization grows as reward variance drops.

3. **Even near-zero reward variance, non-zero task gradients exist.** The lowest-variance buckets still show non-zero task gradient norms, but these are dominated by sampling noise rather than genuine signal. The paper notes this important subtlety: "When RV near 0, substantial task gradients persist despite carrying almost no useful signal" (Figure 3 caption). This is because even with constant rewards, the finite-sample advantage estimate `$\hat{A} = R - \bar{R}$` can be non-zero due to sampling variability in the baseline estimate `$\bar{R}$`, producing spurious advantage estimates that generate gradient updates in random directions.

**How Low SNR Causes Template Collapse**

The mechanism proceeds in stages:

**Stage 1: Reward variance collapse during training.** As the policy improves, it converges toward behaviors that produce consistent rewards. Easy prompts get solved reliably (all trajectories succeed), producing zero reward variance. Hard prompts may still have variance, but the fraction of high-variance prompts shrinks. Figure 11 shows this: the prompt-level reward distribution shifts from a mix of success and failure toward mostly-uniform outcomes, with the "mixed" portion (where some trajectories succeed and others fail, producing variance) shrinking over training.

**Stage 2: Regularization dominates low-RV updates.** For prompts with near-zero reward variance, `$\|g_{\text{task}}\| \approx 0$` while `$\|g_{\text{reg}}\|$` remains constant. The total update direction is then `$g_{\text{total}} \approx g_{\text{reg}}$`, which is input-agnostic: the KL term pulls the policy toward the reference model uniformly, and the entropy term encourages output diversity uniformly, with no input-specific steering. Over many updates, this pushes the policy's conditional distributions `$\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)$` for different prompts `$x$` toward each other — they all get pulled toward the same reference distribution and the same high-entropy target, eroding input-specific differences.

**Stage 3: MI declines while conditional entropy remains high.** Because `$g_{\text{reg}}$` includes an entropy bonus, within-input diversity `$\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$` is sustained or even increased. But because `$g_{\text{reg}}$` applies identically across prompts, it doesn't maintain the _between-prompt_ differences that constitute `$\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$`. The result: `$\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$` stays stable while `$\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$` drops — exactly the signature of template collapse that entropy monitoring misses. This is formalized in Theorem M.2: if an intervention increases within-prompt entropy `$\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$` more than it increases total entropy `$\mathbf{H}(Z)$`, then mutual information `$\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$` must decrease.

**The GRPO Variance Amplification Problem (Appendix N)**

The paper identifies an additional mechanism specific to GRPO that exacerbates the problem. GRPO normalizes advantages by dividing by the within-prompt reward standard deviation:

> $$\widetilde{A}(z; x) = \frac{A(z; x)}{\sqrt{\text{RV}(x)}}$$

**What it computes:** The normalized advantage `$\widetilde{A}$` is the raw advantage divided by the square root of the within-prompt reward variance. This is designed to make advantage magnitudes comparable across prompts with different reward scales — without normalization, a prompt where rewards range from 0 to 100 would dominate updates over one where rewards range from 0 to 1.

**Why this form:** Normalization by standard deviation is standard practice in RL to stabilize training across heterogeneous reward scales. However, the paper shows (Proposition N.1) that this normalization amplifies gradient estimation noise when `$\text{RV}(x)$` is small: the variance of the normalized gradient estimator scales as `$\sigma^2(x) / \text{RV}(x)$`, where `$\sigma^2(x)$` is the irreducible reward noise. When `$\text{RV}(x) \to 0$`, this ratio blows up, making gradient estimates from low-variance prompts extremely noisy. This means that GRPO not only suffers from the regularization-dominance problem at low RV, but also _amplifies_ the noise in those updates, making the problem strictly worse than for unnormalized PPO.

**The Quartile Ablation: Causal Evidence (Table 6)**

To move beyond correlation and establish causality, the paper runs a controlled intervention on Sokoban. All prompt groups are sorted by within-prompt reward variance, divided into four equal-sized quartiles (Q1 = highest variance, Q4 = lowest), and four separate training runs are performed — each updating on only one quartile's prompts, with all other settings identical. The results:

| Quartile | RV Range | Task Perf (%) | MI Proxy | Entropy |
|----------|----------|---------------|----------|---------|
| Q1 (highest) | [4.4–5.6] | 21.1 | 0.95 | 2.02 |
| Q2 | [1.5–4.2] | 19.5 | 0.93 | 1.53 |
| Q3 | [0.0–0.2] | 10.7 | 0.81 | 1.41 |
| Q4 (lowest) | [0.0–0.1] | 11.0 | 0.73 | 1.87 |

Both task performance and MI proxy degrade monotonically from Q1 to Q4. This establishes the full causal chain: **reward variance → gradient quality → input-dependent reasoning → task performance**. The slight uptick in performance from Q3 to Q4 (10.7 to 11.0) is within noise, but the overall trend is clear: training exclusively on low-variance prompts produces worse models than training on high-variance prompts, confirming that reward variance is not merely correlated with but causally drives reasoning quality.

**Controlled Noise Injection (Figure 9)**

A second causal test: in FrozenLake, the paper varies the environment's stochasticity (the probability that a random action overrides the agent's intended action) from 0% to 100% and measures the effect on MI and filtering efficacy. As noise increases, task return drops, conditional entropy rises (more random outputs), and `$\widehat{I}(X; Z)$` decreases monotonically. This confirms the SNR prediction: additional noise inflates within-prompt return variance in a signal-free way, diluting the advantage estimates and weakening input-dependent reasoning. The SNR-Aware Filtering advantage persists at 0–50% noise but closes at 80–100%, where the environment is so stochastic that even high-variance prompts carry little discriminative signal — reward variance stops being a reliable proxy for signal quality.

#### SNR-Aware Filtering: The Intervention

The SNR mechanism suggests a direct mitigation strategy: **prioritize updates from prompts with high within-prompt reward variance**, where task gradients carry meaningful information, and suppress updates from low-variance prompts, where regularization noise would dominate. SNR-Aware Filtering operationalizes this as a pre-update filtering step.

**The Filtering Operator (Formal Definition, Appendix G)**

At each training iteration, after rollout collection but before the parameter update:

1. **Grouping**: Trajectories are grouped by their source prompt. Given `$P$` prompts and `$G$` trajectories per prompt, this yields `$P$` groups, each with `$G$` trajectories.

2. **Statistic computation**: For each prompt group `$i$`, compute the within-prompt reward variance:

> $$\widehat{\text{Var}}(R \mid X = x_i) = \frac{1}{G-1} \sum_{g=1}^{G} \left(R_g(x_i) - \bar{R}(x_i)\right)^2, \quad \bar{R}(x_i) = \frac{1}{G} \sum_{g=1}^{G} R_g(x_i)$$

where `$R_g(x_i)$` is the scalar episodic return of the `$g$`-th trajectory for prompt `$x_i$`, and `$\bar{R}(x_i)$` is the sample mean return for that prompt.

**What it computes:** The sample variance of episode returns across the `$G$` trajectories generated for the same prompt. High variance means the trajectories received substantially different rewards — some were "good" (high reward) and some were "bad" (low reward) — so the advantage estimates will be large in magnitude and informative. Low variance means all trajectories received similar rewards, so advantage estimates will be near zero and uninformative.

**Why this form:** Sample variance is the natural lightweight proxy for SNR because it directly measures the quantity that Theorem H.2 bounds the task gradient magnitude by. Alternatives like entropy-based filtering or length-based filtering (tested in Table 9) are less reliable because they don't directly measure reward signal quality — a prompt can have high reasoning entropy but zero reward variance (all reasoning paths equally good or bad), making the entropy signal misleading for update quality.

3. **Ranking and selection (top-p)**: Given a keep rate `$\rho \in (0, 1]$` (typically `$\rho = 0.9$`), prompts are ranked by descending reward variance. Let `$\sigma$` be the permutation such that `$\widehat{\text{Var}}(R | X = x_{\sigma(1)}) \geq \cdots \geq \widehat{\text{Var}}(R | X = x_{\sigma(P)})$`. The selection threshold is:

> $$\tau = \rho \sum_{i=1}^{P} \widehat{\text{Var}}(R \mid X = x_i)$$

The kept set `$S$` is the smallest prefix of the ranking whose cumulative variance reaches `$\tau$`:

> $$S = \{\sigma(1), \ldots, \sigma(k^*)\}, \quad k^* = \min\left\{k : \sum_{j=1}^{k} \widehat{\text{Var}}(R \mid X = x_{\sigma(j)}) \geq \tau\right\}$$

**What it computes:** This selects a subset of prompts whose total reward variance accounts for fraction `$\rho$` of the total variance in the batch. The number of kept prompts `$k^*$` is determined adaptively: if a few prompts have very high variance and most have near-zero variance, `$k^*$` will be small (aggressive filtering). If variance is more evenly distributed, `$k^*$` will be larger (lenient filtering).

**Why this form:** The paper compares this against fixed-count top-k filtering (keeping exactly `$\lfloor \rho P \rfloor$` prompts) and threshold-based min-p filtering (keeping prompts whose variance exceeds `$p \cdot \max_i \widehat{\text{Var}}$`). Top-p is preferred because it adapts to the variance distribution: as training progresses and more prompts collapse to zero variance (Figure 10), top-p naturally becomes more selective (the kept ratio drops), while top-k would continue keeping a fixed fraction even if all kept prompts have near-zero variance. Min-p can be unstable because the maximum variance fluctuates across batches. The adaptive behavior of top-p is shown in Figure 10: the effective kept ratio `$\rho_{\text{eff}}$` decreases over training as the zero-variance prompt count rises, automatically concentrating updates on the shrinking pool of still-informative prompts.

4. **Filtered objective**: The policy update is computed only on trajectories from the kept prompt groups. The filtered loss is:

> $$\mathcal{L}_\rho(\theta) = \frac{1}{k^*} \sum_{i \in S} \sum_{\xi \in \mathcal{B}_i} \mathcal{L}_\theta(\xi)$$

where `$\mathcal{B}_i$` is the set of `$G$` trajectories for prompt `$i$`, and `$\mathcal{L}_\theta(\xi)$` is the per-trajectory RL loss (PPO/GRPO objective). The normalization by `$k^*$` (rather than the original `$P$`) rescales the gradient to account for the reduced number of prompts.

**What it computes:** This is the standard RL objective, but restricted to the high-SNR subset of the batch. Trajectories from filtered-out prompts contribute zero gradient. The gradient budget is concentrated on updates that carry meaningful task-discriminative information.

**Why this form:** The normalization by `$k^*$` ensures that the effective learning rate per kept prompt is comparable to the unfiltered case, rather than being scaled down by `$k^*/P$`. An alternative would be to keep the original normalization by `$P$`, which would effectively reduce the learning rate when filtering is aggressive. The paper's choice maintains optimization step size while improving the signal content of each step.

**Filtering Strategy Variants (Table 9)**

The paper ablates several design choices in Table 9 (Appendix C):

- **Filtering metric**: Reward variance significantly outperforms alternatives (reward sum, entropy, entropy variance, response length) in both task performance and MI preservation. Entropy-based and length-based filters either fail to prevent collapse or induce it. This confirms that reward variance specifically targets the SNR mechanism rather than a generic "difficulty" or "diversity" signal.

- **Keep direction**: Keeping high-variance prompts (keep-largest) substantially outperforms keeping low-variance prompts (keep-smallest), which degrades both performance and MI while inflating entropy. This asymmetry directly supports the SNR hypothesis: high-variance rollouts contain informative signal; low-variance rollouts are noise.

- **Keep strategy**: Top-p (adaptive, based on cumulative variance mass) consistently outperforms top-k (fixed count) across environments (Figure 6), especially later in training when variance distributions become more skewed. This validates the adaptive selection mechanism.

**Compute Overhead and Practical Considerations**

SNR-Aware Filtering requires at minimum `$G = 2$` trajectories per prompt to estimate variance (with `$G=1$`, variance is undefined). The paper shows (Table 5) that repartitioning the fixed rollout budget of `$K = 128$` trajectories into configurations with `$G \geq 4$` and applying filtering yields performance matching or exceeding the `$128 \times 1$` (all prompts, one trajectory each) baseline while reducing per-step wall-clock time by 26–41%, because filtering reduces the number of trajectory groups entering the gradient computation. The RV computation itself adds less than 0.1% of iteration time. This means the method is essentially free in terms of compute budget — it only requires reorganizing the same number of rollouts into prompt groups rather than independent samples.

#### Training and Evaluation Setup

**Base models.** The primary experiments use Qwen2.5-3B [35]. Scale experiments sweep Qwen2.5 variants at 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, and 7B parameters. Model type experiments include Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Llama3.2-3B [26]. Modality experiments use Qwen2.5-VL-3B [1] with both text-only (T) and text+image (V) inputs.

**RL algorithms.** Four policy-gradient variants are compared: PPO [42], DAPO [68], GRPO [44], and Dr. GRPO [23]. All use GAE with `$(\gamma, \lambda) = (1.0, 1.0)$` and Adam optimizer with `$(\beta_1, \beta_2) = (0.9, 0.999)$`. Actor learning rate is `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`; critic learning rate is `$1 \times 10^{-5}$`. PPO uses asymmetric clipping with `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$` and `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$`. Entropy regularization coefficient is `$\beta = 0.001$`. A format penalty of `$-0.1$` is applied when the agent fails to produce valid structured output (missing `  thinking` or `<answer>` tags). Training runs for up to 400 rollout–update iterations on NVIDIA GPUs using the veRL/HybridFlow framework [45].

**Rollout configuration.** Each iteration collects `$K = P \times G = 128$` trajectories. Default configuration is `$P = 8$` prompts with `$G = 16$` trajectories per prompt. When applying SNR-Aware Filtering, the effective minibatch size is reduced accordingly and the per-step loss is scaled by `$\rho$` to maintain comparable optimization step size. Alternative configurations are explored in Table 5 (Sokoban sweep), varying `$(P, G)$` pairs from `$(128, 1)$` to `$(8, 16)$` with total budget fixed at 128 trajectories.

**Environments (Figure 3).** Seven environments spanning complementary decision-making regimes:

- **Sokoban** [39]: irreversible box-pushing puzzle on a grid. Dense reward (+1 per box placed, −1 for moving off target, +10 completion, −0.1 step penalty). Multi-turn (up to 5 interaction turns, 2 actions per turn, 10 total actions).

- **FrozenLake** [2]: sparse-reward navigation with 2% random transition noise (98% chance of executing intended action). Binary reward (+1 for reaching goal, 0 otherwise). Multi-turn.

- **MetaMathQA** [67]: mathematical reasoning with diminishing retry rewards (1.0 for first correct attempt, halving each subsequent attempt). Multi-turn with answer revision.

- **Countdown** [16]: single-turn arithmetic expression construction. Full reward (+1.0) for correct solutions, partial (+0.1) for valid expressions with wrong results.

- **SearchQA** [51]: multi-turn search and question answering requiring iterative web search and information synthesis.

- **WebShop** [64]: interactive e-commerce navigation requiring product search, attribute matching, and purchase decisions.

- **DeepCoder** [25, 21, 15]: competitive programming code synthesis from input-output specifications.

**Evaluation.** Models are evaluated on a fixed set of 512 validation prompts per environment, decoding with temperature `$T = 0.5$` and stochastic sampling. Primary metric is task success rate. MI proxies and entropy metrics are logged continuously during training.

**Early stopping.** Training stops if either (i) reward-variance collapse is detected (reward variance drops below 10% of baseline — mean variance over first 10 iterations — for 5 consecutive iterations) or (ii) validation success rate remains below 1% for 5 consecutive evaluation checkpoints.

#### Mutual Information Proxy Variants and Validation

**The proxy family (Table 1, expanded).** Beyond the primary MI-Est and Retrieval-Acc, the paper defines variants along three dimensions with specific mathematical forms:

**Discrete retrieval variants:**
- `Recall@k` for `$k \in \{2, 4, 8\}$`: the fraction of traces where the true source prompt is among the top-k highest-scoring prompts. More lenient than Retrieval-Acc (which requires exact match at top-1); tracks partial distinguishability.
- All discrete variants have a well-defined chance level of `$1/P$` under complete template collapse, providing an absolute reference.

**Continuous variants:**
- `MI-Seq-Est`: uses the raw (non-length-normalized) log-likelihood difference `$L_{i,k,i} - \log\frac{1}{P}\sum_j e^{L_{i,k,j}}$`, summed over the full sequence rather than averaged per token. This captures total distinguishability mass; longer reasoning chains that provide more input-specific detail contribute more to this metric.
- `MI-ZScore`: z-score normalized per-batch as described above, without EMA smoothing. More sensitive to batch-to-batch fluctuations.
- `MI-ZScore-EMA`: EMA-smoothed normalization with `$\alpha = 0.9$`, providing temporal stability.

**Turn scope variants:**
- First-turn variants restrict the analysis to the reasoning produced in the first interaction turn, testing whether initial reasoning (before environment feedback) is input-adaptive.
- Trajectory variants sample reasoning uniformly across all interaction turns, testing whether input-dependence is maintained throughout the multi-turn interaction.

**Computational properties.** All proxies reuse `$(X_i, Z_{i,k})$` pairs from training rollouts. The cross-scoring matrix `$L_{i,k,j}$` requires `$P \times G \times P$` forward passes through the policy, which can be batched. For the default configuration `$P=8, G=16$`, this is `$8 \times 16 \times 8 = 1024$` evaluations, each producing per-token log-likelihoods for a single trace-prompt pair. The computation is parallelizable and amortized over the `$P \times G$` traces already collected; no additional environment interactions are needed.

**Validation against performance (Figure 8).** The paper computes Spearman rank correlation between each diagnostic metric and final task success rate across runs with varying entropy regularization strength, KL constraint strength, and SNR-Aware Filtering keep rate. Key findings:

- **Trajectory MI-ZScore**: Spearman `$\rho$` of +0.39 (strongest positive correlation).
- **All MI-family metrics**: positive correlations.
- **Reasoning Entropy**: `$-0.11$` (near-zero negative).
- **Conditional Entropy**: `$-0.14$` (negative).
- **All entropy metrics**: near-zero or negative correlations.

This confirms that MI predicts performance substantially better than entropy, and entropy can actually point in the wrong direction — lower entropy doesn't necessarily mean worse performance, and higher entropy doesn't necessarily mean better performance, making it an unreliable training monitor.

**Diagnostic utility across interventions (Figure 13).** The paper sweeps three intervention families (entropy regularization strength, KL constraint strength, and SNR-Aware Filtering keep rate) and tracks training trajectories in `$(\widehat{I}(X; Z), \text{Performance})$` and `$(\mathbf{H}(Z|X), \text{Performance})$` spaces. Entropy and KL tuning move training dynamics primarily along the entropy axis without substantially shifting MI or performance into clearly improved regimes. SNR-Aware Filtering traces a monotone improvement in both MI and task success. This demonstrates that MI captures a distinct dimension of reasoning quality that existing interventions don't address, and that SNR-Aware Filtering is uniquely positioned to improve it.

**Format validity vs. MI (Figure 12).** The paper tests whether a simpler signal — whether the model's output follows the required format (e.g., has `  thinking` and `<answer>` tags) — could serve as a collapse indicator without the overhead of MI estimation. Runs maintain near-perfect format validity (>0.95) while exhibiting low MI (<0.4), confirming that structural correctness and semantic input-dependence are separate dimensions. Format checks cannot substitute for MI-based diagnostics.

**RV orthogonality to surface statistics.** Reward variance correlates weakly with conditional entropy (Spearman −0.14) and response length (0.12), while correlating strongly with task reward (0.63). This confirms that RV targets a distinct axis of update quality rather than surface-level output statistics, making it a complementary control knob to KL and entropy regularization rather than a substitute.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Mutual Information as a Missing Diagnostic Dimension in RL Training

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is recognizing that the standard tool for monitoring reasoning quality during RL—entropy—measures only half of what matters. This isn't a claim about entropy being "incorrect"; it's a claim about entropy being *incomplete*. By decomposing reasoning quality into the information-theoretic identity H(Z) = I(X; Z) + H(Z|X), the paper exposes that the field has been systematically monitoring within-input diversity while being completely blind to cross-input distinguishability—and that these two quantities can move in opposite directions.

What makes this genuinely novel rather than an obvious application of information theory is the specific failure mode it reveals. The field has long known that entropy can collapse (the "low-entropy collapse" regime in Figure 1), and substantial effort has gone into entropy-based stabilization [42, 62, 32]. But the *template collapse* regime—high H(Z|X) with low I(X; Z)—is invisible to every metric in standard use. Prior work on reasoning diversity (n-gram statistics [20, 74], embedding dispersion [33, 53], uncertainty proxies [27, 43]) all measure various forms of within-input variability and cannot distinguish prompt-driven variation from templated variation. Concurrent work on "reasoning collapse" [61, 63, 69] lumps input-sensitivity loss together with diversity loss, failing to isolate the distinct mechanism. Behavioral testing approaches [11, 37, 73] can probe input dependence but only at evaluation time with carefully constructed test suites—they can't serve as online training monitors.

The paper's MI proxy is thus not just a "better metric" but a fundamentally different measurement target. It operationalizes a quantity that the field knew was theoretically important (mutual information between inputs and outputs) but had no practical way to track during training. The in-batch cross-scoring method is elegant in that it requires zero additional compute beyond what training already generates—it reuses log-probabilities from the rollout data to construct a scoring matrix that would otherwise be discarded. This transforms input-dependence monitoring from a separate evaluation concern into an integrated training diagnostic.

The empirical validation that MI correlates with task performance substantially more strongly than entropy (Spearman +0.39 for Trajectory MI-ZScore vs. −0.11 to −0.14 for entropy metrics; Figure 8) is important not as a performance claim but as evidence that the field has been looking at the wrong signal. Entropy *can point in the wrong direction*—higher entropy doesn't reliably predict better performance, and lower entropy doesn't reliably predict worse performance—making it a poor proxy for reasoning health despite its widespread use. The fact that format validity can remain near-perfect while MI is low (Figure 12) further reinforces that the diagnostic gap is real: structural correctness and semantic input-dependence are separate dimensions that require separate monitoring.

This is fundamentally a **diagnostic reframing**, not an incremental metric improvement. The paper doesn't just propose a new number to track; it argues that the monitoring *space* itself needs to be expanded from one dimension (entropy) to two (entropy + MI), and that failing to track the second dimension leaves a systematic blind spot where reasoning degradation can proceed undetected. The decomposition is not itself technically novel—it's standard information theory—but recognizing that it's the *right* decomposition for understanding RL training dynamics, and that existing practice covers only one axis, is the conceptual contribution.

---

### Innovation 2: SNR as a Causal Mechanism for Reasoning Collapse

The paper's second distinctive contribution is providing a mechanistic explanation for template collapse that makes specific, falsifiable predictions. Before this work, "reasoning collapse" was a descriptive label for a constellation of observed degradation phenomena—outputs become more templated, less diverse, less input-responsive—without a clear causal account of *why* RL training produces these effects. The SNR framing changes this by identifying the gradient-level culprit: low within-prompt reward variance weakens the task-discriminative gradient component while input-agnostic regularization terms remain constant, causing updates to be dominated by forces that push reasoning toward uniform templates.

What distinguishes this from prior mechanistic accounts is its specificity and testability. The paper doesn't claim "regularization causes collapse" or "low rewards cause collapse"—it makes the much sharper claim that the *ratio* of task gradient magnitude to regularization gradient magnitude, which is controlled by within-prompt reward variance, determines whether updates preserve or erode input dependence. This yields several falsifiable predictions that the paper verifies: (1) training exclusively on high-variance prompts should outperform training on low-variance prompts (confirmed by the quartile ablation, Table 6); (2) injecting environmental noise should predictably degrade MI (confirmed by the controlled noise experiment, Figure 9); (3) prompt-level filtering should outperform trajectory-level filtering, because prompt-level variance captures genuine signal distinguishability while trajectory-level variance within a naturally low-variance prompt just amplifies noise (confirmed by Table 7).

The flatness of the regularization gradient across reward-variance buckets (Figure 3c) is a particularly important empirical finding. It's not that regularization is "bad"—KL constraints and entropy bonuses serve essential roles in preventing catastrophic policy collapse. The problem is that they apply *uniformly*, and when the task signal weakens (low reward variance), they don't weaken correspondingly. This asymmetry is what makes the SNR mechanism a structural vulnerability of standard RL training for LLM agents, not a correctable hyperparameter issue. Simply tuning down the KL coefficient or entropy bonus doesn't solve the problem—as Figure 13 shows, these interventions move training dynamics along the entropy axis without substantially improving MI or performance. The root cause is the imbalance, and addressing it requires either strengthening the task signal (which SNR-Aware Filtering does by selecting high-variance prompts) or making regularization input-dependent (which is not explored).

The GRPO-specific variance amplification result (Appendix N, Proposition N.1) adds an important nuance. GRPO's advantage normalization by √RV(x) is intended to stabilize training across heterogeneous reward scales, but it inadvertently amplifies gradient estimation noise when RV(x) is small. This means that GRPO—a widely used algorithm for LLM reasoning training [44]—may be *more* susceptible to template collapse than unnormalized PPO, because low-variance prompts not only suffer from regularization dominance but also from inflated gradient noise. This is a significant finding for practitioners: it suggests that the normalization that stabilizes training on high-variance prompts may actively harm training on low-variance ones, and that adaptive normalization (or filtering) may be necessary.

The causal nature of the evidence moves this from "interesting correlation" to "mechanistic explanation." The quartile ablation (Table 6) is the cleanest demonstration: by isolating which prompts contribute to training, it shows that reward variance is not merely correlated with but *causally drives* the quality of the resulting policy. The monotonic degradation in both performance and MI from Q1 to Q4 means that the SNR mechanism isn't just a post-hoc story—it predicts the outcome of an intervention that hadn't been run before. This is fundamentally a **mechanistic insight** that provides a causal account where prior work had only descriptive labels. It doesn't propose a new algorithm; it explains *why* existing algorithms fail in a specific, previously unrecognized way, and that explanation directly suggests a mitigation strategy.

### Innovation 3: SNR-Aware Filtering as a Signal-Enhancement Knob Orthogonal to Existing Stabilization

The third contribution is conceptual: recognizing that test-time compute allocation in RL can be optimized not just for *how much* training signal to apply (via learning rates, clipping, batch sizes) or *how to constrain* it (via KL penalties, entropy bonuses), but for *which data to train on* based on per-prompt signal quality. SNR-Aware Filtering introduces a new control dimension—prompt selection by reward variance—that complements rather than competes with the existing stabilization toolkit.

The standard approach to RL stabilization for LLMs focuses on constraining the *magnitude* and *direction* of updates: KL penalties keep the policy near a reference [40, 42], entropy bonuses maintain output diversity [62, 32], gradient clipping prevents large steps [49], and learning rate schedules control optimization speed [72]. All of these operate on the *optimizer side*—they modify how the computed gradient is applied. SNR-Aware Filtering operates on the *data side*—it modifies which data contributes to the gradient computation in the first place. This is a fundamentally different intervention point.

What makes this a genuine innovation rather than "just another filtering heuristic" is that the filtering criterion (reward variance) is theoretically motivated by the SNR mechanism rather than empirically discovered through hyperparameter search. The paper shows that reward variance is the *right* signal to filter on because it directly controls the task gradient magnitude (the bound in Theorem H.2) and because alternative filtering criteria—reward sum, entropy, entropy variance, response length—all underperform (Table 9). Length-based filtering, for instance, might seem reasonable (longer reasoning could indicate more thoughtful processing), but it fails because reasoning length is only weakly correlated with reward variance (Spearman 0.12) and doesn't capture whether the reasoning is input-adaptive. The fact that reward variance specifically targets the SNR mechanism rather than a generic "difficulty" or "diversity" heuristic is what makes this a principled intervention.

The top-p selection strategy's adaptivity is a subtle but important design choice. As training progresses and more prompts collapse to near-zero reward variance (Figure 11), top-p naturally becomes more selective—the kept ratio drops because fewer prompts carry meaningful variance mass (Figure 10). A fixed-count top-k strategy can't replicate this behavior: it would continue keeping the same fraction of prompts even when all kept prompts have near-zero variance, wasting gradient budget on noise-dominated updates. The adaptivity makes SNR-Aware Filtering a dynamic control mechanism that automatically tightens selection pressure as the policy improves, without requiring manual schedule tuning.

The breadth of the empirical results (Table 4)—spanning four RL algorithms (PPO, DAPO, GRPO, Dr. GRPO), model scales from 0.5B to 7B, two model families (Qwen2.5 and Llama3.2), and multiple modalities (text and vision)—suggests that SNR-Aware Filtering addresses a structural vulnerability of closed-loop RL training rather than a quirk of a particular setup. The fact that it improves performance even on top of DAPO [68] and Dr. GRPO [23]—algorithms explicitly designed to target training stability and mitigate collapse—is particularly telling. It suggests that these algorithms' existing stabilization mechanisms (DAPO's filtering/acceptance step, Dr. GRPO's variance-reduction techniques) address different failure modes than the one SNR-Aware Filtering targets, and that the SNR control dimension is genuinely complementary.

The paper's framing of SNR-Aware Filtering as a "general-purpose SNR control knob" rather than a standalone method is deliberate and important. It positions the contribution as adding a new axis to the RL practitioner's control space—alongside KL coefficient tuning, entropy coefficient tuning, learning rate scheduling, and batch size selection—rather than proposing a replacement for any of them. This makes the contribution more broadly adoptable: practitioners don't need to choose between existing stabilization techniques and SNR-Aware Filtering; they can use both. The demonstration in Section 5.1 (Figure 13) that SNR-Aware Filtering moves training dynamics along the MI axis while KL and entropy tuning move along the entropy axis confirms that these are orthogonal controls.

This is fundamentally a **practical reframing** of the RL practitioner's toolkit. It says: you've been controlling how much to update (learning rate), how to constrain updates (regularization), and how to stabilize estimates (clipping, normalization). Here's a new knob—*which data to update on*—that addresses a specific failure mode (template collapse) that none of your existing knobs can fix. The theoretical motivation and empirical breadth make this more than an ad-hoc trick; it's a principled addition to the stabilization space.

### Innovation 4: The Observable Signal-to-Noise Regime as a Predictive Diagnostic

A fourth, somewhat subtler contribution is the paper's demonstration that simple, batch-level statistics of reward variance can predict *whether* SNR-Aware Filtering will help, before training begins. The ratio Std(RV)/Mean(RV), computable from a single rollout batch, separates settings where filtering is effective (high ratio, bimodal variance distribution allows clean separation of signal from noise) from settings where it isn't (low ratio, uniform variance means filtering discards data randomly). Table 8 shows this clearly: Sokoban with Std/Mean ratios of 1.16–1.29 shows substantial filtering gains (+3.2% to +4.6%), while FrozenLake GRPO with a ratio of 0.33 shows negative filtering effects (−5.0%).

This is significant because it transforms SNR-Aware Filtering from a "try it and see" intervention into a *predictably deployable* one. Before running full training, a practitioner can collect a single rollout batch, compute the RV distribution statistics, and determine whether filtering is likely to help or harm. This addresses a common failure mode of new methods: when they work well in some settings and poorly in others without a clear boundary, they become unreliable tools that require expensive trial-and-error. The Std/Mean diagnostic provides that boundary.

The underlying insight is that SNR-Aware Filtering works by *separating* signal-rich from signal-poor prompts, and this separation requires that such a distinction actually exists in the data. When the environment or task produces similar reward variance across all prompts (e.g., FrozenLake with its binary reward structure and high stochasticity), the filter can't meaningfully separate signal from noise—it's just discarding data uniformly. This connects back to the SNR mechanism: if all prompts have uniformly low SNR, filtering can't help because there's no high-SNR subset to select. If there's a bimodal distribution (some prompts have clear reward differences, others don't), filtering is highly effective because it cleanly isolates the informative updates.

This is fundamentally a **meta-diagnostic insight** about when the proposed intervention applies. It's not a performance claim about SNR-Aware Filtering itself but a claim about the *conditions* under which the SNR mechanism matters. By providing a cheap, pre-training test for these conditions, the paper makes the intervention more practically robust and reduces the risk of misapplication. This kind of boundary characterization is rare in methods papers and adds substantial value for practitioners deciding whether to adopt the technique.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses seven environments spanning complementary decision-making regimes (Table 3): Sokoban (grid puzzle with irreversible box-pushing), FrozenLake (sparse-reward stochastic navigation), MetaMathQA (multi-turn math reasoning with diminishing retry rewards), Countdown (single-turn arithmetic expression construction), SearchQA (multi-turn search and QA), WebShop (interactive e-commerce navigation), and DeepCoder (competitive programming code synthesis). The primary evaluation set consists of 512 held-out validation prompts per environment, distinct from training prompts. All environments are synthetic and fully controllable, enabling clean analysis without real-world priors.

- **Base model(s).** Primary experiments use Qwen2.5-3B [35], with sweeps across Qwen2.5 variants at 0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, and 7B parameters, plus Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Llama3.2-3B [26] for model family comparison, and Qwen2.5-VL-3B [1] for multimodal (text-only and text+image) evaluation. The choice of 3B as the primary scale reflects a regime where models have non-trivial task performance without saturation, leaving room for RL training to make a difference and for collapse phenomena to manifest. The breadth across 0.5B–7B tests whether template collapse and SNR-Aware Filtering effects generalize across model capacity.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **task success rate** on the held-out validation set (fraction of prompts where the agent achieves the task objective, measured at the peak training step). Training dynamics are tracked with three auxiliary metrics: **MI proxy** (Retrieval-Acc at the peak-performance step, measuring cross-input distinguishability of reasoning), **reasoning entropy** (conditional entropy $\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$, measuring within-input diversity), and a binary **collapse indicator** (whether validation success ever drops below 1% during training). The correlation analysis (Figure 8) uses Spearman rank correlation between each diagnostic metric and final task success rate across runs with varying hyperparameters.

- **Baselines.** The "no filtering" configuration serves as the primary baseline — standard RL training using the full batch of rollouts without any prompt selection. For filtering strategy comparisons (Table 9, Figure 6), additional baselines include top-k filtering (fixed count, keeping exactly $\lfloor \rho P \rfloor$ prompts) and min-p filtering (threshold-based, keeping prompts exceeding $p \cdot \max_i \widehat{\text{Var}}$). Algorithm comparisons use the published implementations of PPO [42], DAPO [68], GRPO [44], and Dr. GRPO [23] without filtering as baselines, with filtered variants as the intervention. The trajectory-level filtering baseline (Table 7) keeps all prompts but retains only the top-8 and bottom-8 trajectories per prompt by reward, testing whether prompt-level selection provides benefits beyond within-prompt variance amplification.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Training compute is measured in rollout–update iterations, with each iteration collecting exactly $K = P \times G = 128$ trajectories (default configuration: $P=8$ prompts, $G=16$ trajectories per prompt). The total generation budget per run is fixed at $128 \times 400 = 51{,}200$ trajectories (400 iterations maximum, with early stopping often terminating earlier). SNR-Aware Filtering introduces no additional generation cost — it repartitions the same 128 trajectories into prompt groups with $G \geq 2$ and selectively excludes low-variance groups from gradient computation. Table 5 reports wall-clock step time and VRAM usage for different $(P, G)$ configurations at fixed total rollout budget, confirming that filtering reduces per-step time by 26–41% due to fewer groups entering gradient computation, with RV computation itself adding <0.1% overhead.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** Strategy selection and evaluation are kept separate: the main experiments report peak validation success rate achieved during training, not the performance at a fixed step. When comparing filtering strategies (Table 9), all metrics are reported at the training step where task performance peaks, ensuring fair comparison of final policy quality rather than training speed. The quartile ablation (Table 6) runs four completely separate training runs with different prompt subsets, providing independent evidence rather than post-hoc analysis of a single run. The Std(RV)/Mean(RV) diagnostic (Table 8) is computed from a single rollout batch before training to predict filtering effectiveness, with the prediction evaluated against held-out filtering results. No explicit cross-validation over hyperparameters is reported; the keep rate $\rho = 0.9$ appears to be a fixed default across experiments.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Template Collapse Is a Consistent, Detectable Failure Mode

The paper's foundational empirical claim is that template collapse occurs reliably across training configurations and is detectable via MI proxies while remaining invisible to entropy monitoring. The evidence for this comes from three complementary analyses:

**MI dynamics during training (Figure 5, Figure 14).** Tracking three key metrics — task success rate, Retrieval-Acc (MI proxy), and reasoning entropy — over 200 training steps on the default Sokoban configuration reveals a consistent pattern. Under no filtering (gray curves in Figure 5), Retrieval-Acc declines from approximately 0.6–0.8 early in training to roughly 0.2 by step 200, while reasoning entropy remains elevated (150–600 range, varying with configuration) and even spikes upward in some runs. The task success rate under no filtering peaks around step 40–60 and subsequently degrades. This triple pattern — declining MI, stable or rising entropy, and eventual performance degradation after an initial peak — is the signature of template collapse. Figure 14 shows that all six MI proxy variants (MI Estimate, MI Z-Score EMA, Retrieval Above Chance, Recall@4, Recall@8, Conditional Entropy) exhibit this same degradation under no filtering, confirming that the pattern is robust to the specific proxy choice and not an artifact of the Retrieval-Acc metric.

The paper emphasizes that the MI decline *precedes* the performance degradation: Retrieval-Acc drops substantially before the success rate peaks and turns downward. This supports the claim that MI serves as an early warning signal — it detects reasoning degradation while performance still looks acceptable on the surface. In contrast, reasoning entropy remains high throughout, giving no warning of the impending collapse. Under SNR-Aware Filtering (orange/red curves), both Retrieval-Acc and task performance remain stable, and entropy is controlled rather than spiking.

**Behavioral signature across eight environments (Figure 7).** Beyond diagnostic metrics, the paper demonstrates that template collapse manifests behaviorally as systematic reasoning compression. Reproducing experiments from existing evaluations spanning spatial agents [66], logic puzzle agents [3], visual agents [58], and math agents [22], Figure 7 shows that reasoning length (measured in tokens) declines monotonically across all eight environments as training progresses. The decline is substantial — from varying initial lengths (ranging from roughly 30 to 300 tokens depending on environment) to roughly 50–100 tokens by late training across most environments. This behavioral convergence toward shorter, more formulaic outputs is a behavioral signature of template collapse that complements the MI-based diagnostics: as agents converge toward reusable templates, they produce less verbose, less input-specific reasoning.

**Training instability and collapse under no filtering (Table 9).** The "No Filter" baseline in Table 9 shows a task performance of 0.17 (17% success rate), an MI proxy of 0.54, entropy of 2.76, with training marked as unstable (✗ in the Stable column). This baseline represents standard RL training without any filtering intervention. In comparison, SNR-Aware Filtering (top-p with $\rho = 0.9$) achieves 0.38 task performance (+0.20), MI proxy 0.84 (+0.29), entropy 1.64 (−1.12), and stable training (✓). The MI proxy improvement is particularly large (+54% relative), confirming that filtering directly addresses the input-dependence degradation that characterizes template collapse.

#### SNR-Aware Filtering Consistently Improves Performance Across Configurations

The paper evaluates SNR-Aware Filtering across a broad experimental matrix (Table 4) spanning four RL algorithms, four model scales, multiple model types, and multiple modalities. The headline pattern is that filtering improves peak task success rate in most settings, with the gains spanning multiple independent experimental axes.

**Algorithm sweep (PPO, DAPO, GRPO, Dr. GRPO).** On Qwen2.5-3B across four environments, SNR-Aware Filtering (top-p, $\rho = 0.9$) yields the following deltas over no-filter baselines:

- **PPO**: +16.0% Sokoban, +10.9% FrozenLake, +0.6% MetaMathQA, +0.0% Countdown, average +6.9% across four tasks.
- **DAPO** [68]: +5.1% Sokoban, +2.1% FrozenLake, +2.8% MetaMathQA, +1.6% Countdown, average +2.9%.
- **GRPO** [44]: +9.0% Sokoban, −3.0% FrozenLake, +1.2% MetaMathQA, +2.2% Countdown, average +3.7%.
- **Dr. GRPO** [23]: −0.4% Sokoban, +0.6% FrozenLake, +1.4% MetaMathQA, +1.4% Countdown, average +0.8%.

The largest gains appear on Sokoban (+16.0% for PPO, +9.0% for GRPO), which is the task with the richest reward structure (dense rewards with +1 per box, −0.1 step penalty, +10 completion) and thus the highest potential for within-prompt reward variance to discriminate trajectory quality. The GRPO degradation on FrozenLake (−3.0%) is notable and is addressed in Section 5.2 through the Std/Mean diagnostic — this is the setting where reward variance is uniform across prompts (Std/Mean = 0.33), so filtering discards data randomly rather than separating signal from noise.

The fact that filtering improves performance on top of DAPO and Dr. GRPO — algorithms already designed to target training stability and mitigate collapse — indicates that the SNR control dimension is complementary to existing stabilization mechanisms. DAPO's own filtering/acceptance step (which the paper notes can be interpreted as a top-$P$ filter with $P \to 1.0$) and Dr. GRPO's variance-reduction techniques apparently target different failure modes than the SNR imbalance that SNR-Aware Filtering addresses.

**Model scale sweep (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B).** Using PPO across four environments:

- **Qwen2.5-0.5B**: +22.9% Sokoban, +0.0% FrozenLake, −0.2% MetaMathQA, −0.7% Countdown, average +5.5%. The Sokoban gain is enormous in relative terms (from 3.3% to 26.2%), suggesting that the smallest model is most susceptible to template collapse without filtering — it lacks the capacity to develop robust input-dependent reasoning and falls back on templates when task signal weakens.
- **Qwen2.5-1.5B**: +6.2% Sokoban, +1.6% FrozenLake, +7.0% MetaMathQA, +1.6% Countdown, average +4.1%.
- **Qwen2.5-3B**: +16.0% Sokoban, +10.9% FrozenLake, +0.6% MetaMathQA, +0.0% Countdown, average +6.9%.
- **Qwen2.5-7B**: +4.9% Sokoban, −0.6% FrozenLake, +11.7% MetaMathQA, +0.3% Countdown, average +4.1%.

The gains are positive across most settings but show task-specific variation rather than a monotonic trend with scale. The 7B model, with the strongest base capability, still benefits from filtering on Sokoban and substantially on MetaMathQA (+11.7%), suggesting that even capable models can suffer from template collapse when reward variance distributions become skewed during training.

**Model type and modality.** Llama3.2-3B [26] shows gains of +18.8% Sokoban, −0.2% FrozenLake, +3.7% MetaMathQA, −1.2% Countdown, average +5.3%. Qwen2.5-VL-3B shows the largest gains: +6.0% Sokoban (text-only), +53.5% FrozenLake (text-only), +12.0% Sokoban (with images), +59.5% FrozenLake (with images). The enormous FrozenLake gains for the VL model (from 16.0% to 69.5% with text, from 19.5% to 79.0% with images) likely reflect that this model, being pretrained for multimodal tasks, may have weaker base performance on pure text reasoning, making it more susceptible to template collapse and thus benefiting more from signal-enhancing interventions.

**Comparison of filtering strategies (Figure 6, Table 9).** Across four environments (Sokoban, FrozenLake, MetaMathQA, Countdown), top-p filtering consistently achieves higher success rates than top-k filtering and substantially higher than no filtering. The advantage is particularly pronounced on Sokoban and FrozenLake, where the curves separate early and maintain a gap throughout training. Table 9's ablation on Sokoban (Qwen2.5-3B) provides specific numbers:

- No filtering: 0.17 task perf, 0.54 MI proxy, 2.76 entropy, unstable.
- Top-p ($\rho = 0.9$): 0.38 (+0.20), 0.84 (+0.29), 1.64 (−1.12), stable.
- Top-p ($\rho = 0.5$): 0.29 (+0.12), 0.83 (+0.29), 1.88 (−0.88), stable.
- Top-k ($\rho = 0.5$): 0.44 (+0.27), 0.89 (+0.35), 1.47 (−1.29), stable.

The top-k = 0.5 configuration actually achieves the highest task performance (0.44) in this specific ablation, slightly exceeding top-p = 0.9 (0.38). However, the paper notes that top-k can be less adaptive: it always keeps exactly the specified fraction of prompts regardless of the variance distribution, which can lead to including low-quality updates when the variance distribution is concentrated. The training dynamics in Figure 6 show that top-p filtering produces more stable improvement curves, particularly in later training stages where variance distributions become more skewed.

**Alternative filtering metrics (Table 9).** Comparing filtering on reward variance versus alternative signals (all using top-p, $\rho = 0.9$, Qwen2.5-3B on Sokoban):

- Reward variance: 0.38 task perf, 0.84 MI proxy, stable.
- Reward sum: 0.24 (+0.07 over no-filter), 0.80 MI proxy, unstable.
- Entropy: 0.20 (+0.02), 0.41 MI proxy, unstable.
- Entropy variance: 0.23 (+0.06), 0.70 MI proxy, unstable.
- Length: 0.16 (−0.02), 0.91 MI proxy, unstable.

The critical finding is that only reward-variance filtering achieves both high task performance *and* training stability. Reward-sum filtering, while improving over no-filter, collapses. Entropy-based and length-based filters either induce collapse or fail to prevent it, despite some (like length filtering) achieving high MI proxy (0.91). This confirms that reward variance specifically — not any generic "select hard prompts" or "select diverse prompts" heuristic — targets the SNR mechanism. The high MI proxy for length filtering without stable performance (0.91 MI but unstable) suggests that MI alone is not a sufficient diagnostic; it must be combined with task performance monitoring.

**Compute overhead and batch configuration (Table 5).** Sweeping $(P, G)$ configurations on Sokoban (Qwen2.5-3B, total rollout budget fixed at 128 trajectories):

| $(P, G)$ | No Filter Perf | Filter Perf ($\rho = 0.9$) | Δ | Filter Step Time (s) | Time Reduction |
|----------|----------------|---------------------------|-----|----------------------|----------------|
| 128 × 1 | 23.6 | — | — | 89.8 | — |
| 64 × 2 | 18.8 | 27.3 | +8.6 | 64.9 | −29% |
| 32 × 4 | 24.2 | 27.4 | +3.2 | 52.6 | −41% |
| 8 × 16 | 15.6 | 23.6 | +8.0 | 65.9 | −26% |

Key findings: (1) The 128 × 1 configuration (all prompts, one trajectory each, no grouping) cannot use filtering because $G=1$ provides no variance estimate. (2) All configurations with $G \geq 4$ and filtering match or exceed the 128 × 1 baseline, confirming that the gains come at no additional total compute — they require only repartitioning the same number of rollouts. (3) Filtering reduces per-step time by 26–41% because fewer groups enter gradient computation, with the computation of RV itself adding <0.1% overhead. (4) The $(32, 4)$ configuration achieves the best filtered performance (27.4%) with the largest time reduction (−41%), suggesting this is a compute-efficient operating point.

#### MI Proxy Correlates with Performance Better Than Entropy

The paper directly compares the predictive value of MI versus entropy for task performance (Figure 8). Computing Spearman rank correlation across runs with varying entropy regularization strength, KL constraint strength, and SNR-Aware Filtering keep rate:

- **Trajectory MI-ZScore**: +0.39 (strongest positive correlation).
- **Trajectory MI-Est**: approximately +0.30 (estimated from bar chart).
- **Trajectory MI-ZScore-EMA**: approximately +0.27.
- **Retrieval-Acc (Trajectory)**: approximately +0.25.
- **Recall@4 (Trajectory)**: approximately +0.24.
- **Reasoning Entropy**: −0.11.
- **Conditional Entropy**: −0.14.
- **Conditional Entropy (Trajectory)**: approximately −0.12.

All MI-family metrics show positive correlations with task performance; all entropy-family metrics show near-zero or negative correlations. The difference between the best MI metric (+0.39) and the best entropy metric (−0.11) represents not just a quantitative improvement but a qualitative one: MI is positively predictive (higher MI reliably indicates better performance), while entropy is mildly *negatively* predictive (higher entropy actually correlates with slightly lower performance, though the correlation is weak). This is the core evidence for the claim that entropy is a misleading diagnostic — it doesn't just fail to detect template collapse; it can point in the wrong direction.

**Diagnostic trajectories under different interventions (Figure 13).** The paper sweeps three intervention families (entropy coefficient, KL coefficient, and SNR-Aware Filtering keep rate) and plots training trajectories in $(I(X; Z), \text{Performance})$ and $(H(Z|X), \text{Performance})$ spaces, connecting early-training (step 10) to late-training (step 400) checkpoints:

- **Entropy sweep**: As the entropy coefficient increases (weaker to stronger intervention, 0 to 0.1), the training trajectories show large rightward shifts in $H(Z|X)$ (from roughly 1.8 to 3.6) but only modest vertical movement in task performance (roughly 0.15–0.20 range). The trajectories cluster in a low-performance band regardless of entropy level, and stronger entropy regularization does not move runs into high-performance or high-MI regimes. Some runs with high entropy actually end at lower performance than runs with moderate entropy.

- **KL sweep**: Similar pattern. Varying KL coefficient from 0 to 0.1 shifts $H(Z|X)$ (roughly 1.8 to 3.0 range) but produces limited vertical movement in performance (roughly 0.12–0.18). Trajectories largely stay in the low-performance region.

- **RV-filter sweep**: Decreasing the filter keep rate from 1.0 to 0.4 (stronger filtering) moves trajectories monotonically upward in both MI (from roughly 0.2 to 0.8) and task performance (from roughly 0.08 to 0.32). The trajectories trace a clear positive slope in $(I(X; Z), \text{Performance})$ space, with stronger filtering consistently producing both higher MI and higher performance.

This is the paper's key evidence that entropy- and KL-based stabilizers primarily control a different axis of reasoning quality ($H(Z|X)$) than SNR-Aware Filtering ($I(X; Z)$ and task performance). It also shows that MI and performance are linked in a way that entropy and performance are not — moving along the MI axis reliably improves performance, while moving along the entropy axis does not.

#### The SNR Mechanism Is Causally Supported

The paper provides several lines of evidence that the SNR mechanism causally explains template collapse, not merely correlates with it:

**Quartile ablation (Table 6).** Sorting prompts by within-prompt reward variance, dividing into four equal-sized quartiles (Q1 highest → Q4 lowest), and training four separate PPO runs on Qwen2.5-3B (Sokoban, $P=8$, $G=16$, each run keeping 25% of prompts per step):

| Quartile | RV Range | Task Perf (%) | MI Proxy | Entropy |
|----------|----------|---------------|----------|---------|
| Q1 (highest) | [4.4–5.6] | 21.1 | 0.95 | 2.02 |
| Q2 | [1.5–4.2] | 19.5 | 0.93 | 1.53 |
| Q3 | [0.0–0.2] | 10.7 | 0.81 | 1.41 |
| Q4 (lowest) | [0.0–0.1] | 11.0 | 0.73 | 1.87 |

Both task performance and MI proxy degrade monotonically from Q1 to Q4, with a slight Q3–Q4 overlap in performance (10.7 vs. 11.0) that is within noise given the small prompt count per quartile (~2 prompts per step). The gap between Q1/Q2 and Q3/Q4 is substantial: training only on high-variance prompts (Q1–Q2, RV > 1.5) produces roughly 20% success rate, while training only on low-variance prompts (Q3–Q4, RV < 0.2) produces roughly 11% — a factor of nearly 2× difference. Since all other training settings are identical, the only causal variable is which prompts contribute to updates, establishing the chain: reward variance → gradient quality → reasoning quality → task performance.

**Controlled noise injection (Figure 9).** In FrozenLake, varying the environment's stochastic transition probability from 0% to 100%:

- At 0% noise: median success rates are highest for both filtering (~0.65) and no-filter (~0.50), with filtering showing a clear advantage.
- At 50% noise: filtering advantage persists, with filtered runs at ~0.45 vs. no-filter at ~0.30.
- At 80–100% noise: both conditions degrade substantially (below 0.20), and the filtering advantage narrows to near zero.

The paper interprets this through the SNR lens: as environmental noise increases, it inflates within-prompt reward variance in a signal-free way (random transitions produce reward variation uncorrelated with policy quality), diluting the advantage estimates that task gradients depend on. The filter's advantage attenuates when even high-variance prompts carry mostly noise — reward variance loses its discriminative power as a signal-quality proxy. This is a predicted boundary condition of the SNR mechanism, and its confirmation strengthens the causal account.

**Prompt-level vs. trajectory-level filtering (Table 7).** Comparing prompt-level SNR-Aware Filtering (selects high-RV prompt groups) against trajectory-level filtering (keeps all prompts but retains only top-8 and bottom-8 trajectories per prompt by reward) on Sokoban (Qwen2.5-3B):

| Method | Prompts Used | Traj/Update | Task Perf (%) | MI Proxy |
|--------|-------------|-------------|---------------|----------|
| No filter | 8/8 | 128 | 12.9 | 0.83 |
| Prompt-level RV ($\rho=0.9$) | 3.2/8 | 50.6 | 23.6 | 1.80 |
| Trajectory-level | 8/8 | 64 | 16.8 | 0.20 |

Prompt-level filtering substantially outperforms trajectory-level filtering (23.6% vs. 16.8%), and trajectory-level filtering's MI proxy (0.20) is actually *lower* than the no-filter baseline (0.83), despite a modest task performance improvement. The paper interprets this through the SNR mechanism: within a naturally low-variance prompt, forcing reward variance by sub-selecting extreme trajectories amplifies noise rather than extracting signal — the trajectory-level filter takes prompts where all trajectories would naturally get similar rewards, artificially keeps the best and worst, and creates a spurious advantage signal that doesn't correspond to genuine task-discriminative differences. Prompt-level filtering, by contrast, selects prompts that *naturally* produce reward-discriminative trajectories.

**Reward variance orthogonality.** The paper reports that reward variance correlates weakly with conditional entropy (Spearman −0.14) and response length (0.12), while correlating strongly with task reward (0.63). This supports the claim that RV targets a distinct axis of update quality rather than surface-level output statistics — it's not simply a proxy for "hard prompts" (which might have higher entropy or longer responses) but specifically captures the *discriminability* of trajectories by reward.

**Std(RV)/Mean(RV) as a predictive diagnostic (Table 8).** The paper finds that the ratio of the standard deviation of per-prompt reward variance to its mean, computable from a single rollout batch before training, predicts whether SNR-Aware Filtering will help:

| Setting | Filter Δ | P | G | RV Mean | RV Std | RV Var | Std/Mean |
|---------|----------|---|---|---------|--------|--------|----------|
| Sokoban, 14B | +4.6% | 8 | 8 | 2.24 | 2.88 | 8.32 | 1.29 |
| Sokoban, 3B | +3.2% | 32 | 4 | 2.49 | 2.89 | 8.35 | 1.16 |
| FrozenLake, 3B (GRPO) | −5.0% | 32 | 8 | 0.54 | 0.18 | 0.03 | 0.33 |

The Sokoban configurations, with Std/Mean ratios of 1.16–1.29, show clear filtering gains. The FrozenLake GRPO configuration, with a ratio of 0.33, shows negative filtering effects (−5.0%). The interpretation: a high Std/Mean ratio indicates a bimodal or heavy-tailed RV distribution where high-variance prompts can be cleanly separated from low-variance ones, making filtering effective. A low ratio indicates uniform RV across prompts, making filtering essentially random discarding of useful data.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Filtering metric comparison**: Reward variance as the filtering criterion significantly outperforms alternative metrics (reward sum, entropy, entropy variance, response length) in both task performance and training stability (Table 9). Reward-sum filtering improves over no-filter (+0.07) but results in unstable training. Entropy-based and length-based filters either fail to prevent collapse or actively induce it, despite length filtering achieving the highest MI proxy score (0.91 vs. 0.84 for reward variance). This demonstrates that a high MI proxy alone does not guarantee good performance — the filtering criterion must target the right signal. The instability of entropy-based filtering likely occurs because entropy does not capture whether reward differences across trajectories are meaningful, making it a poor proxy for gradient signal quality.

**Keep direction**: Keeping high-variance prompt groups ("Keep Largest") substantially outperforms keeping low-variance groups ("Keep Smallest") on all metrics. Under top-k ($\rho = 0.5$) on Sokoban (Qwen2.5-3B): keep-largest achieves 0.44 task performance, 0.89 MI proxy, 1.47 entropy, and stable training; keep-smallest achieves 0.29 (−0.15), 0.47 MI proxy (−0.42), 5.31 entropy (+3.84), and stable training. The keep-smallest configuration maintains training stability (no collapse) but produces significantly worse performance and lower MI, while entropy skyrockets to 5.31 — the model produces diverse outputs that are input-agnostic. This asymmetry directly supports the SNR hypothesis: high-variance rollouts contain informative task signal; low-variance rollouts contain noise. Notable is that the keep-smallest configuration *is stable*, suggesting that even low-quality signal can maintain training stability while degrading reasoning quality — another form of silent failure.

**Sampling strategy interaction**: The filtering behavior is modulated by the sampling strategy used during rollout generation (Table 9, "Sampling Strategies" block). With RV filtering held constant (top-p), varying the sampling threshold:
- Top-p = 1.0 (no sampling filtering, baseline): 0.17 task perf, 0.54 MI, 2.76 entropy, unstable.
- Top-p = 0.9: 0.38 (+0.20), 0.84 (+0.29), 1.64 (−1.12), stable.
- Top-p = 0.5: 0.29 (+0.12), 0.83 (+0.29), 1.88 (−0.88), stable.
- Min-p = 0.05: 0.42 (+0.25), 0.67 (+0.13), 1.64 (−1.12), stable.
- Min-p = 0.2: 0.45 (+0.27), 0.36 (−0.18), 3.01 (+0.26), stable.
- Top-k = 0.25: 0.22 (+0.05), 0.86 (+0.32), 1.28 (−1.48), stable.
- Top-k = 0.5: 0.44 (+0.27), 0.89 (+0.35), 1.47 (−1.29), stable.

The interaction is complex: more aggressive sampling (lower top-p, higher min-p) can improve task performance and reduce entropy, but effects on MI are heterogeneous. Min-p = 0.2 achieves the highest raw performance (0.45) but the lowest MI proxy (0.36), suggesting that aggressive sampling may force high token-level confidence that improves task completion but does so through templated rather than input-adaptive reasoning. The top-k configurations show interesting behavior: top-k = 0.5 achieves high performance (0.44) with high MI (0.89), while top-k = 0.25 achieves much lower performance (0.22) but similarly high MI (0.86). This indicates that MI alone, while better than entropy, is not sufficient as a sole diagnostic — high MI with poor task performance can occur when the model distinguishes inputs but fails to act correctly on that distinction.

**Effect of prompt batch size and group size (Table 5)**: At fixed total rollout budget (128 trajectories), the $(P, G)$ configuration matters substantially. The 128 × 1 baseline (no grouping possible) achieves 23.6% but cannot use filtering. Among filtered configurations, $(64, 2)$ achieves 27.3% (+8.6 over its no-filter 18.8%), $(32, 4)$ achieves 27.4% (+3.2 over its no-filter 24.2%), and $(8, 16)$ achieves 23.6% (+8.0 over its no-filter 15.6%). The $(32, 4)$ configuration is the most compute-efficient, providing the best absolute performance (27.4%) with the largest time reduction (−41%), suggesting that moderate group sizes ($G=4$) with correspondingly more prompt diversity ($P=32$) provide a good balance between reliable variance estimation and prompt diversity. The poor no-filter performance of $(8, 16)$ (15.6%) versus $(128, 1)$ (23.6%) suggests that restricting prompt diversity (only 8 unique prompts per iteration) can hurt exploration when all prompts contribute to updates, but filtering compensates by concentrating on high-signal prompts.

**Format validity insufficiency (Figure 12)**: The paper tests whether format validity — the fraction of outputs that follow the required structural format (e.g., containing `  thinking` and `<answer>` tags) — could serve as a simpler collapse indicator than MI. Across training runs with varying interventions, format validity remains near-perfect (>0.95) while MI ranges from below 0.2 to above 0.8, and entropy also varies substantially. Runs with nearly identical format validity (~0.98–1.0) show dramatically different MI scores and task performance. This confirms that structural correctness (following the output format) and semantic input-dependence (reasoning content adapting to the prompt) are separate dimensions — a model can learn to produce properly formatted outputs with entirely templated content, and format-based checks cannot detect this degradation.

**Adaptive filtering behavior over training (Figure 10)**: The effective kept ratio $\rho_{\text{eff}}$ (the actual fraction of prompts retained after filtering) decreases over the course of training as more prompts collapse to near-zero reward variance. The zero-variance prompt count rises monotonically, and the filter responds by becoming more selective. This adaptive tightening is a direct consequence of the top-p selection strategy and would not occur with fixed-count top-k. Figure 10 thus validates a key design choice: the adaptivity of top-p filtering allows it to automatically adjust selection pressure as the policy converges, concentrating gradient updates on an increasingly narrow set of still-discriminative prompts.

**Reward distribution shift during training (Figure 11)**: Tracking prompt-level reward distributions across early, mid, and late training phases in Sokoban reveals a systematic shift: the "hard" portion (prompts where all trajectories fail, producing zero or near-zero reward) shrinks as the policy improves; the "mixed" portion (prompts where some trajectories succeed and others fail, producing meaningful reward variance) expands initially but then collapses in late training; and the "easy" portion (prompts where all trajectories succeed) grows. This distribution-level signature mirrors the gradient-level SNR story: late training is not simply easier for the policy; it's a regime where reward variation has been compressed to the point that gradient updates carry progressively less task-discriminative information, making SNR-Aware Filtering increasingly necessary to maintain input dependence.

**GRPO-specific noise amplification (Proposition N.1)**: The paper identifies a mechanism specific to GRPO that may exacerbate template collapse. GRPO normalizes advantages by dividing by $\sqrt{\text{RV}(x)}$, which inflates the variance of the gradient estimator when $\text{RV}(x)$ is small (the variance floor scales as $\sigma^2(x) / \text{RV}(x)$). This means low-variance prompts not only suffer from regularization dominance (as in PPO) but also produce gradient estimates with amplified noise. The empirical evidence for this is the FrozenLake GRPO result in Table 4, where filtering shows a −3.0% degradation, and the Std/Mean ratio of 0.33 in Table 8 for this specific configuration. The combination of low and uniform RV with GRPO normalization may make this setting particularly challenging for any filtering approach.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the evidence support the claim that template collapse is a consistent, detectable failure mode?

The paper demonstrates template collapse across Sokoban (Figure 5) and provides MI proxy tracking across multiple environments and proxy variants (Figure 14). The behavioral signature of reasoning length decline is shown across eight environments (Figure 7). However, several caveats apply:

The primary training dynamics evidence (Figure 5) shows results from a single environment (Sokoban) under a single algorithm (PPO) at a single model scale (3B). While Figure 14 extends to multiple MI proxies, all are computed on the same training run. The paper does not show the full MI/entropy/performance training trajectory triple for FrozenLake, MetaMathQA, Countdown, SearchQA, WebShop, or DeepCoder. The claim that template collapse is "consistent" across configurations rests partly on the breadth of environments in Table 4 (which reports peak performance deltas, not trajectories) and on the reasoning length decline across eight environments (Figure 7, which shows only behavioral compression, not MI dynamics). Showing the Figure 5-style triple for at least one additional environment with a different reward structure (e.g., FrozenLake with its sparse binary reward) would substantially strengthen the generality claim.

The reasoning length decline (Figure 7) is presented as a behavioral signature of template collapse, but the paper does not establish that length decline and MI decline are causally or even empirically linked within the same runs. It's possible that reasoning length declines for benign reasons (the model becomes more efficient at the task, eliminating unnecessary verbiage) while input dependence is maintained, or that MI declines without length compression. Without showing both metrics from the same training runs, the connection remains suggestive.

The "silent failure" claim — that template collapse occurs while standard metrics remain stable — is supported for entropy specifically (Figure 5c shows entropy rising or stable while MI drops under no filtering). But the paper doesn't show what other standard metrics (training loss, value loss, KL divergence from reference, gradient norm) look like during these runs. A stronger demonstration would show that *all* commonly monitored metrics except MI and eventual task performance appear healthy, establishing that the silence is comprehensive rather than specific to entropy.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that MI correlates with performance substantially better than entropy?

The Spearman correlation analysis (Figure 8) is clean and directly supports this claim. The gap between +0.39 (Trajectory MI-ZScore) and −0.11 (Reasoning Entropy) is substantial and qualitatively meaningful. The diagnostic trajectory analysis (Figure 13) further supports the claim by showing that entropy- and KL-based interventions move training dynamics along axes orthogonal to performance improvement, while SNR-Aware Filtering moves them along the MI-performance diagonal.

However, several methodological concerns:

The correlation analysis pools runs with varying hyperparameters (entropy coefficient, KL coefficient, filter keep rate), which means the data points are not independent — runs with different filter keep rates may share other configuration settings. The sample size (number of runs) is not reported, making it difficult to assess the statistical reliability of the Spearman coefficients. A correlation of +0.39, while clearly positive, is moderate — it explains only about 15% of the variance in task performance ($R^2 \approx 0.15$). This leaves substantial unexplained variance that is not captured by MI or entropy.

The paper does not report any causal manipulation showing that *improving* MI (through an intervention) *causes* improved task performance, as opposed to both being correlated consequences of a common cause (high-quality training signal). The quartile ablation (Table 6) shows that training on high-RV prompts produces both higher MI and higher performance, which is consistent with a common-cause account (RV quality → both MI and performance) rather than a causal chain (MI → performance). Establishing that MI causally mediates the relationship between RV quality and performance would require, for example, an intervention that directly manipulates MI (not RV) and shows performance changes — which is a challenging experiment not attempted in this paper.

#### Does the evidence support the SNR mechanism as a causal explanation?

The paper provides stronger causal evidence for the SNR mechanism than for the diagnostic claims. The quartile ablation (Table 6) is a clean causal manipulation — it randomly assigns prompts to training runs based on RV, holding all else constant, and shows monotonic performance and MI degradation as RV decreases. This establishes that RV quality *causes* differences in downstream reasoning quality and task performance.

The noise injection experiment (Figure 9) provides convergent evidence from a different manipulation — varying environmental stochasticity, which affects RV by adding signal-free variance, and showing predicted degradation. The fact that filtering advantage attenuates at very high noise (80–100%) is a non-obvious prediction of the SNR mechanism (when even high-RV prompts are mostly noise, filtering can't help) and its confirmation strengthens the mechanistic account.

The trajectory-level vs. prompt-level comparison (Table 7) is a clever test of a subtle prediction: if the mechanism is about selecting naturally signal-rich prompts rather than artificially creating within-prompt variance, then prompt-level filtering should outperform trajectory-level filtering. The result confirms this (23.6% vs. 16.8%), and the counterintuitive MI proxy for trajectory-level filtering (0.20, below the no-filter baseline of 0.83) provides additional mechanistic evidence — trajectory-level filtering within naturally low-RV prompts creates spurious variance that degrades rather than improves input dependence.

However, some gaps remain:

The gradient norm evidence (Figure 3) shows that task gradient norm increases with RV bucket and regularization gradient norm is flat. This is shown for both PPO and GRPO, but only on a single environment (Sokoban, implied by context). Showing this decomposition for at least one additional environment would strengthen the claim of generality.

The three-noise decomposition ($g_{\text{signal}}$, $g_{\text{task-noise}}$, $g_{\text{reg}}$) is conceptually clean but not empirically separated. The paper measures $g_{\text{task}}$ and $g_{\text{reg}}$ norms (Figure 3), but does not decompose $g_{\text{task}}$ into $g_{\text{signal}}$ and $g_{\text{task-noise}}$ components. This means the SNR calculation $\|g_{\text{signal}}\| / (\|g_{\text{task-noise}}\| + \|g_{\text{reg}}\|)$ cannot be empirically computed — only the coarser ratio $\|g_{\text{task}}\| / \|g_{\text{reg}}\|$ can be estimated. The formal bound in Theorem H.2 relates to $\|g_{\text{task}}\|$, not $\|g_{\text{signal}}\|$, so the distinction between signal and task noise, while theoretically important, is not empirically validated.

The GRPO noise amplification mechanism (Proposition N.1) makes a specific quantitative prediction: gradient estimation variance scales as $\sigma^2(x) / \text{RV}(x)$. The paper does not empirically measure gradient estimation variance at different RV levels to validate this scaling prediction, relying instead on the indirect evidence of GRPO's poor FrozenLake performance with uniform RV.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that SNR-Aware Filtering consistently improves performance?

The breadth of positive results in Table 4 is impressive — filtering improves average performance across 16 algorithm-environment pairs (counting the four algorithms × four environments), with gains in 12 of 16 and minor degradations in 4. The deltas are substantial in several cases (+16.0% on Sokoban PPO, +22.9% on Sokoban 0.5B, +53.5% on FrozenLake VL text-only).

However, several limitations:

The gains are highly environment-dependent. On MetaMathQA and Countdown, the improvements are modest (0–2.2% in most configurations) and sometimes negative (−0.6% MetaMathQA for 3B-Instruct, −1.2% Countdown for Llama3.2). This is consistent with the SNR mechanism — these tasks have different reward structures (diminishing retry rewards for MetaMathQA, partial credit for Countdown) that may produce different RV distributions — but the paper doesn't analyze RV distributions for these tasks to explain when and why filtering doesn't help. The Std/Mean diagnostic is only reported for Sokoban and FrozenLake (Table 8).

The keep rate $\rho = 0.9$ is a fixed hyperparameter across all experiments. While Table 9 includes a limited $\rho$ sweep (0.5 and 0.9, plus min-p variants), the paper does not report a comprehensive sensitivity analysis across environments. The optimal keep rate may be task-dependent, and using a fixed $\rho = 0.9$ across all tasks may understate filtering's potential benefits on some tasks and overstate them on others.

The comparison against DAPO and Dr. GRPO is important but lacks detail. The paper notes that DAPO's own filtering can be viewed as a special case of the SNR-Aware framework (top-$P$ with $P \to 1.0$), but doesn't explore whether adjusting DAPO's internal filtering mechanism to incorporate RV-based selection would yield further gains. The Dr. GRPO results show the smallest filtering benefits (+0.8% average), but the paper doesn't analyze why Dr. GRPO's existing variance-reduction techniques might already partially address the SNR imbalance.

The trajectory-level filtering baseline (Table 7) is a valuable control, but only reported on Sokoban. Showing this comparison for FrozenLake (where the SNR mechanism makes different predictions due to the binary reward structure) would strengthen the claim.

#### Does the evidence support the practical deployability of the method?

The compute overhead analysis (Table 5) convincingly demonstrates that SNR-Aware Filtering adds negligible computational cost and can actually reduce per-step time. The Std/Mean diagnostic (Table 8) provides a practical tool for predicting when filtering will help. However, the paper does not address several practical concerns:

The method requires $G \geq 2$ trajectories per prompt to estimate variance. In production RL pipelines where computing resources limit batch sizes, this constraint may require halving the number of unique prompts per iteration (from 128 to 64 if $G=2$, or to 32 if $G=4$). Table 5 suggests that moderate $G$ values (4) with correspondingly fewer prompts can work well, but this tradeoff between prompt diversity and variance estimation quality is not systematically explored beyond the Sokoban sweep.

The top-p selection threshold depends on the cumulative variance mass, which is a dynamic quantity that changes during training. The paper shows that the effective kept ratio adapts automatically (Figure 10), but it doesn't discuss whether this adaptivity could lead to pathological behavior — for instance, if the variance distribution becomes extremely concentrated (one prompt has 90% of the total variance mass), the filter might keep only 1–2 prompts per iteration, potentially causing optimization instability from insufficient batch diversity. The paper doesn't report minimum kept counts or discuss safeguards.

The paper does not address how SNR-Aware Filtering interacts with distributed training across multiple GPUs, where different workers may see different prompt distributions and RV estimates may have higher variance due to smaller per-worker batch sizes. For large-scale deployments, the filtering would need to be coordinated across workers or applied to aggregated batches, which is not discussed.

#### What experiments would have strengthened the paper?

1. **Full training trajectories for all environments.** Showing the Figure 5 triple (performance, MI, entropy over training) for at least FrozenLake and MetaMathQA would demonstrate that template collapse is indeed consistent, not specific to Sokoban's dense reward structure.

2. **Fine-grained keep rate sensitivity.** A comprehensive sweep of $\rho$ values (e.g., 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 1.0) across 2–3 environments would characterize the sensitivity of filtering gains to this hyperparameter and provide guidance for practitioners.

3. **Online vs. offline filtering comparison.** The current method filters based on the current batch's RV distribution. An alternative would be to maintain a running estimate of per-prompt RV across iterations and filter based on historical rather than instantaneous variance. Comparing these approaches would test whether the batch-level RV estimate is reliable enough or whether temporal smoothing improves selection.

4. **Gradient noise decomposition measurement.** Empirically estimating the three noise components ($g_{\text{signal}}$, $g_{\text{task-noise}}$, $g_{\text{reg}}$) separately — perhaps by comparing gradient estimates with different numbers of trajectories per prompt — would directly validate the SNR decomposition rather than relying on the coarser $g_{\text{task}}$ vs. $g_{\text{reg}}$ comparison.

5. **Longer training horizons.** All experiments run up to 400 iterations. Template collapse is characterized as a phenomenon that worsens over training as reward variance collapses; running experiments to 800 or 1600 iterations would test whether the filtering advantage grows, shrinks, or stabilizes over longer horizons, and whether the adaptive kept ratio eventually converges or continues to decline.

6. **Combination with other stabilization methods.** The paper frames SNR-Aware Filtering as complementary to KL and entropy regularization, but doesn't empirically explore the interaction surface — e.g., does optimal KL coefficient change when filtering is applied? Does filtering reduce the need for entropy regularization? A joint sweep over filter keep rate and regularization coefficients would characterize whether these are truly independent control dimensions or whether they interact.

7. **Out-of-distribution evaluation.** All evaluation uses prompts from the same distribution as training. Testing whether SNR-Aware Filtering preserves *generalization* — the ability to reason adaptively about novel prompt types not seen during training — would address a key practical concern: does filtering merely prevent collapse on the training distribution, or does it preserve general reasoning capability?

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted for in Practice

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire SNR-Aware Filtering framework depends on estimating within-prompt reward variance `$\widehat{\text{Var}}(R|X)$` for every prompt before the parameter update. This requires a minimum of `$G = 2$` trajectories per prompt (to compute sample variance), which means the rollout budget must be partitioned into prompt groups rather than independent samples. The paper acknowledges that the 128 × 1 configuration (all prompts, one trajectory each) cannot use filtering at all, and the performance of grouped configurations *without* filtering degrades substantially as `$G$` increases and prompt diversity drops: the no-filter performance falls from 23.6% at 128 × 1 to 18.8% at 64 × 2, to 24.2% at 32 × 4, and to 15.6% at 8 × 16 on Sokoban (Table 5). In other words, the very act of *enabling* filtering by grouping trajectories carries a performance penalty when filtering is not applied, because reducing the number of unique prompts per iteration limits exploration diversity.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying this method faces a chicken-and-egg problem: to decide whether filtering will help, they need to estimate reward variance, which requires `$G \geq 2$` trajectories per prompt, which may degrade baseline performance relative to ungrouped sampling. The paper resolves this for its experiments by showing that filtering *with* `$G \geq 4$` achieves performance matching or exceeding the 128 × 1 baseline, but this comparison is retrospective — it assumes the practitioner has already committed to grouped sampling and filtering. A practitioner considering adoption must either (a) run a pre-experiment with grouped sampling to measure RV distributions and filtering effectiveness (incurring the grouping cost) or (b) rely on the Std(RV)/Mean(RV) diagnostic from a single batch (also requiring grouped sampling). In either case, the *decision cost* of determining whether to use filtering is not amortized into the reported efficiency numbers. For tasks where filtering turns out not to help (e.g., FrozenLake GRPO with Std/Mean = 0.33 in Table 8), the practitioner will have paid the grouping cost without the compensating filtering benefit.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 5 shows the no-filter performance degradation from grouping: 128 × 1 achieves 23.6% while 8 × 16 achieves only 15.6% (a 34% relative drop). The paper does not report whether the Std/Mean diagnostic can be reliably estimated from `$G = 2$` (the minimum for variance estimation) or whether larger `$G$` values are needed for stable RV estimates, which would further reduce prompt diversity. The paper also does not characterize the variance of the variance estimate itself — the reliability of `$\widehat{\text{Var}}(R|X)$` as a filter criterion depends on how many trajectories per prompt are available, and with `$G = 2$` this estimate has effectively one degree of freedom and extremely high variance.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this decision-cost problem. It provides the Std/Mean diagnostic as a predictive tool (Table 8), but this diagnostic itself requires a rollout batch with grouped trajectories. The paper does not explore whether cheaper proxies for RV (e.g., using critic value variance, model uncertainty estimates, or learned difficulty predictors) could avoid the grouping requirement entirely. Section 7 briefly mentions that "the method requires reward variance to be a reliable signal proxy, which degrades in sparse or noisy reward environments" but frames this as a signal-quality limitation rather than a practical deployment constraint. The cost of *enabling* the method (grouped sampling) is not discussed.

### The Method Provides No Path Forward for Genuinely Hard Problems

**The assumption or constraint.** SNR-Aware Filtering improves performance by selecting prompts where task gradients carry meaningful information — i.e., prompts where different trajectories produce consistently different rewards, allowing the policy to learn which behaviors are better. This mechanism fundamentally assumes that such reward-discriminative prompts *exist in the training distribution*. For problems that are genuinely outside the base model's capability range, this assumption breaks down: all trajectories for such prompts receive uniformly poor rewards (reward variance is zero), and filtering can only exclude them — it cannot transform them into informative training signal.

**The consequence.** The method cannot improve performance on problem classes where the base model's pass@1 success rate is at or near zero. Worse, because such prompts are filtered out, the model receives *no* training signal on them, meaning it cannot bootstrap itself toward solving them through exploration. Over training, as the policy improves and more prompts shift into the "solved" or "uniformly failed" categories (Figure 11), the pool of high-RV prompts shrinks, and the filter's kept ratio drops (Figure 10). In the limit, if all prompts become either trivially easy (all trajectories succeed, zero variance) or trivially hard (all trajectories fail, zero variance), SNR-Aware Filtering would filter out *all* prompts and training would stall entirely. This is a fundamental ceiling: the method amplifies existing capability but cannot create capability from nothing, and it contains no mechanism for curriculum design or exploration bonus that would convert hard prompts into informative training signal.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The FrozenLake GRPO result in Table 4 shows a −5.0% degradation with filtering, and Table 8 attributes this to the extremely uniform RV distribution (Std/Mean = 0.33). This is not a case where filtering is neutral — it actively *hurts* — because the filter discards prompts that, while carrying weak signal, at least contain some data, and the reduction in effective batch size outweighs any signal-quality gain. The reward distribution shift shown in Figure 11 confirms that the high-RV "mixed" portion of prompts shrinks during training as the policy converges, implying that the filter's effective batch size naturally decreases over time. The Countdown results in Table 4 show minimal filtering gains (0.0–2.2% across most configurations), suggesting that for tasks with near-binary reward structures where most prompts are either solved or failed, the fraction of informative prompts is inherently small and filtering provides limited benefit. The paper does not report what fraction of prompts fall into the "zero-variance" category across different environments and training stages (except implicitly in Figure 10), making it difficult to assess how broadly this limitation applies.

**Mitigation status.** Section 7 acknowledges this limitation in general terms: "The method requires reward variance to be a reliable signal proxy, which degrades in sparse or noisy reward environments." However, the paper does not explicitly characterize the capability ceiling or explore mechanisms to convert hard prompts into high-RV training signal (e.g., reward shaping, curriculum learning, or exploration bonuses that would create reward variance even on initially unsolvable prompts). The paper frames SNR-Aware Filtering as complementary to existing stabilization methods (KL regularization, entropy bonuses), but these methods also do not address the hard-problem ceiling. A practitioner facing a distribution of problems where a substantial fraction are beyond the base model's initial capability would need a fundamentally different approach — pretraining, supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstrations, or structured exploration — that this paper does not provide.

### The Single Reward Variance–Based Filter Assumes Reward Is Informative of Reasoning Quality

**The assumption or constraint.** SNR-Aware Filtering uses within-prompt reward variance as a proxy for gradient signal quality. This assumes that when different trajectories for the same prompt receive different rewards, the reward differences reflect genuine differences in reasoning *quality* — i.e., that higher-reward trajectories involved better reasoning, not just luckier outcomes. In environments where rewards are noisy, sparse, or only weakly correlated with reasoning quality, this assumption breaks: reward variance may be high for reasons unrelated to reasoning (stochastic environment dynamics, partial observability, reward function noise), and filtering on RV may select prompts where the "signal" is actually noise amplification.

**The consequence.** In noisy-reward environments, SNR-Aware Filtering could systematically select prompts where reward variance is high due to environment stochasticity rather than reasoning discriminability, concentrating gradient updates on noisy, uninformative signal. This would not only fail to improve input-dependent reasoning but could actively *harm* it by amplifying spurious correlations between reasoning patterns and noise-driven reward differences. The paper demonstrates this boundary condition: in FrozenLake with 80–100% stochastic transitions (Figure 9), the filtering advantage closes to near zero because reward variance loses its discriminative power. This failure mode is particularly insidious because it would not manifest as an obvious training crash — the filter would continue selecting high-RV prompts, and the model would continue updating on them, but the updates would reinforce noise patterns rather than genuine reasoning improvements.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 9 directly shows that the filtering advantage attenuates as environment stochasticity increases, confirming that RV's value as a signal proxy degrades with environmental noise. The paper attributes this to the SNR mechanism: when noise dominates, even high-variance prompts carry mostly signal-free variance, and the filter can't distinguish informative from uninformative variance. The GRPO noise amplification analysis (Proposition N.1, Appendix N) provides theoretical support for this concern: reward variance in the denominator of GRPO's advantage normalization inflates gradient estimation noise when RV is small. However, the paper does not systematically characterize how much of the observed RV in each environment is "signal" (reflecting genuine trajectory quality differences) versus "noise" (reflecting environment randomness). The reward decomposition in Assumption H.1 postulates `$R(z; x) = \mu(x, z) + \varepsilon$`, but the noise variance `$\sigma^2(x)$` is never empirically estimated. The result is that a practitioner cannot determine, from the paper's experiments, how sensitive filtering is to the signal-to-noise ratio *within* the reward function itself, as opposed to the SNR of gradient updates.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this through the controlled noise injection experiment (Figure 9), which demonstrates the boundary condition, and through the Std/Mean diagnostic (Table 8), which can identify settings where RV is too uniform for effective filtering. However, these address the *variance of RV across prompts*, not the *signal content of RV within prompts*. A setting with high variance across prompts (Std/Mean is large) but where within-prompt variance is dominated by environment noise (low signal-to-noise within the reward function) would pass the Std/Mean diagnostic while still producing ineffective filtering. The paper does not propose a diagnostic for reward-function SNR specifically. Section 7 acknowledges the limitation that the method requires "reward variance to be a reliable signal proxy" but does not explore potential mitigations such as reward normalization, advantage estimation with learned baselines that separate signal from noise, or hybrid filtering that combines RV with other signal-quality indicators.

### All Experiments Use a Single Model Family and Are Limited to Synthetically Controllable Tasks

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire experimental validation uses Qwen2.5 models (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B, plus the Instruct and VL variants) and one Llama3.2-3B model. All seven environments (Sokoban, FrozenLake, MetaMathQA, Countdown, SearchQA, WebShop, DeepCoder) are synthetic and fully controllable — they provide clean, programmatic reward signals without the ambiguity, noise, or subjectivity of real-world tasks. The authors state this choice explicitly: "All environments are synthetic and fully controllable, enabling clean analysis of RL learning from scratch without relying on real-world priors" (Appendix B.1). The MI proxy itself uses the policy's own log-likelihoods for cross-scoring, which assumes the policy's probability estimates are reasonably calibrated, an assumption more likely to hold for larger or differently architected models.

**The consequence.** The generality of both the template collapse phenomenon and the SNR-Aware Filtering intervention is unverified beyond this specific model family and these specific task types. Several aspects of the findings could be model-specific: (a) Qwen2.5 models may have particular in-context learning or reasoning characteristics that make them more or less susceptible to template collapse than models from other families (e.g., LLaMA, Gemini, Claude); (b) the MI proxy relies on the policy's log-likelihoods being informative for cross-scoring, which depends on the model's calibration — poorly calibrated models might produce uninformative cross-scoring matrices even when reasoning is input-dependent; (c) the reward structures in synthetic environments (dense rewards in Sokoban, binary rewards in FrozenLake, diminishing retry rewards in MetaMathQA) are clean and consistent, whereas real-world RL tasks (e.g., RLHF with learned reward models, code generation with test-case-based rewards, dialogue with human preference feedback) have substantially noisier and more complex reward signals whose variance characteristics may not follow the patterns observed here. The template collapse phenomenon itself — reasoning becoming input-agnostic while appearing diverse — may manifest differently or not at all in tasks where reasoning diversity is naturally constrained (e.g., structured code generation where output format is rigid) or where input-dependent reasoning is strongly incentivized by the reward function (e.g., tasks where the correct answer is a direct function of input content).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates the core pattern (MI decline with stable entropy under no filtering) primarily on Sokoban with Qwen2.5-3B (Figure 5). The reasoning length decline (Figure 7) is shown across eight environments but without corresponding MI and entropy traces for most of them. Table 4 shows filtering results across tasks, scales, and modalities, but reports only peak performance deltas, not training dynamics. The breadth across algorithm variants (PPO, DAPO, GRPO, Dr. GRPO) and model scales (0.5B–7B) within the Qwen2.5 family provides some evidence of internal consistency, but the family-specific nature of the evaluation cannot rule out the possibility that template collapse is a Qwen2.5-specific vulnerability or that the MI proxy's effectiveness depends on Qwen2.5's calibration properties. The Llama3.2-3B results (Table 4) provide one out-of-family data point with broadly similar patterns (+18.8% Sokoban, +3.7% MetaMathQA), but this is a single model at a single scale in a closely related transformer architecture.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim universality; it presents template collapse as a phenomenon observed consistently across the tested configurations. However, it also does not discuss model-family dependence as a potential confound. Section 7 acknowledges that "All experiments are single-agent" but does not address model-specific or task-generality limitations. The paper characterizes its contributions as identifying "a systematic failure mode in multi-turn agent RL, validated across planning, mathematical reasoning, web navigation, code execution, and tool use, under multiple RL algorithms, model scales, and modalities" (Section 1), which is a stronger claim of generality than the evidence strictly supports given the single model family. A practitioner using a different base model (e.g., a LLaMA variant, a Gemini model, or a domain-specific fine-tuned model) would need to independently verify that template collapse manifests and that the MI proxy tracks it before adopting the diagnostic framework.

### The Mutual Information Proxy Has No Absolute Calibration and Its Relationship to True Mutual Information Is Uncharacterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The MI proxy family (Table 1) estimates input dependence by computing in-batch cross-scoring using the policy's own log-likelihoods. This produces a *relative* measure: it captures whether reasoning traces are more compatible with their source prompts than with other prompts in the batch. However, it provides no *absolute* calibration of mutual information. The proxy values (Retrieval-Acc, MI-Est, MI-ZScore) are interpretable only relative to chance levels (e.g., 1/P for Retrieval-Acc) or relative to their own values at different training steps — there is no reference point that corresponds to "sufficient input dependence" in an absolute sense. A Retrieval-Acc of 0.5 on a batch of `$P=8$` prompts (chance = 0.125) indicates above-chance distinguishability, but does it indicate *enough* distinguishability for reliable task performance? The paper cannot answer this question because the proxy is not calibrated against any external ground truth of input dependence.

**The consequence.** The MI proxy is useful as a *monitoring* tool — a practitioner can watch it during training and flag when it declines — but it cannot serve as a *criterion* for when reasoning is "good enough." For deployment, a practitioner would need to determine an MI proxy threshold below which the model should be retrained, rejected, or augmented with additional test-time interventions. The paper provides no guidance for setting such thresholds. Furthermore, the proxy's values depend on batch composition: a batch with very similar prompts will naturally produce lower Retrieval-Acc than a batch with very different prompts, even if the model's input-dependent reasoning is identical, because the within-batch contrast is harder. This means the proxy is not comparable across batches with different prompt distributions, limiting its utility as a cross-run or cross-task diagnostic. A drop in MI proxy could reflect genuine reasoning degradation, or it could reflect a batch with unusually homogeneous prompts — and the method provides no way to disambiguate.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates the proxy's *relative* utility: it correlates with task performance (+0.39 Spearman for Trajectory MI-ZScore, Figure 8), it declines before performance degradation under no-filter training (Figure 5), and it responds appropriately to interventions (higher with SNR-Aware Filtering, lower with noise injection). However, the paper never compares the proxy against an external estimate of true mutual information (which would require, for instance, a separately trained discriminator or a ground-truth labeling of which reasoning patterns are input-appropriate). The chance level for Retrieval-Acc (`$1/P$`) is acknowledged but the paper does not discuss what proxy values correspond to "healthy" versus "degraded" reasoning in absolute terms — all comparisons are relative (higher is better, declining is bad). The dependence on batch composition is not analyzed: the paper does not vary prompt diversity within batches to test whether the proxy is sensitive to batch homogeneity independently of model reasoning quality.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this calibration gap. It positions the proxy as a "diagnostic" and "early warning signal" (Section 5.1, Section 7), which is appropriate for a relative monitoring tool but leaves the absolute-threshold problem unresolved. The paper also does not explore whether normalized variants (MI-ZScore, which divides by batch standard deviation) partially address batch-composition sensitivity — the z-score normalization would reduce sensitivity to overall scale shifts but not necessarily to changes in batch difficulty homogeneity. The paper does not suggest future work on calibrating the proxy against external measures of input dependence or on developing batch-independent normalizations.

### The Adaptive Filtering Mechanism Requires Per-Task Tuning of the Keep Rate

**The assumption or constraint.** SNR-Aware Filtering introduces a new hyperparameter: the keep rate `$\rho$` (for top-p filtering). While the paper frames `$\rho$` as providing "an explicit, tunable SNR knob" (Section 4.3), this tunability is also a burden: `$\rho$` must be set per-task, and the optimal value depends on the RV distribution characteristics of that task and training stage. The paper uses `$\rho = 0.9$` as a default across most experiments (Table 4, Table 9), but acknowledges that "aggressive filtering may narrow exploration coverage; the kept mass requires per-task tuning" (Section 7). The ablation in Table 9 shows that different `$\rho$` values and different filtering strategies (top-p vs. top-k vs. min-p) produce substantially different outcomes even within the same task (Sokoban): top-p at `$\rho = 0.9$` achieves 0.38 task performance while top-k at `$\rho = 0.5$` achieves 0.44 — a 16% relative difference — and min-p at 0.2 achieves 0.45 but with severely degraded MI (0.36 vs. 0.84 for reward-variance top-p).

**The consequence.** A practitioner adopting SNR-Aware Filtering inherits a new hyperparameter optimization problem that the paper does not provide systematic guidance for solving. The `$\rho$` value controls a fundamental tradeoff: higher `$\rho$` (keep more prompts) preserves more training data and maintains exploration diversity but admits more low-SNR updates; lower `$\rho$` (keep fewer prompts) concentrates gradient budget on high-quality signal but risks overfitting to a narrow prompt distribution and reducing exploration. The optimal balance point depends on the environment's RV distribution, the training stage, and the base model's capability, and the paper's experiments do not characterize how `$\rho$` sensitivity varies across these dimensions. The adaptive behavior of top-p filtering (Figure 10) partially mitigates this: the effective kept ratio adjusts automatically as the RV distribution shifts during training. However, even with this adaptivity, the initial `$\rho$` parameter still controls the overall filtering aggressiveness, and the paper provides no method for selecting it a priori beyond the Std/Mean diagnostic (which predicts *whether* filtering will help, not *what `$\rho$`* to use).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 9 provides a limited `$\rho$` sweep on Sokoban: `$\rho = 0.5$` vs. `$\rho = 0.9$` for top-p, plus min-p and top-k variants. This is a single environment. The paper does not sweep `$\rho$` across FrozenLake, MetaMathQA, Countdown, or any of the other environments. The performance difference between the best and worst filtering configurations in Table 9 is substantial: 0.45 (min-p = 0.2) vs. 0.16 (length filtering, which collapses) vs. 0.17 (no filter). Even among the stable, non-collapsing configurations, the range is 0.29 (top-p = 0.5, keep-largest) to 0.45 (min-p = 0.2), a 55% relative spread. This sensitivity is not discussed in the main text — Table 9 is in Appendix C — and the paper's main results in Table 4 use a fixed `$\rho = 0.9$` without reporting whether this was tuned per-task or chosen as a universal default.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges that "the kept mass requires per-task tuning" (Section 7) and provides the Std/Mean diagnostic as a pre-training check for whether filtering is likely to help at all. However, it does not provide a method for selecting `$\rho$` without running multiple training runs, nor does it characterize how `$\rho$` sensitivity varies across tasks. The adaptive behavior of top-p filtering (effective kept ratio adjusting during training) provides some robustness to suboptimal `$\rho$` choices — the filter naturally tightens as training progresses — but the initial `$\rho$` still controls the starting level of aggressiveness and thus the early-training prompt diversity. A practitioner facing a new task would need to either (a) run a `$\rho$` sweep (expensive), (b) use the default `$\rho = 0.9$` and hope it transfers (risky), or (c) use the Std/Mean diagnostic to decide whether to apply filtering at all, then tune `$\rho$` manually (partially guided but still expensive).

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts how the field should think about monitoring and stabilizing multi-turn LLM agent reinforcement learning by revealing that the standard practice—tracking entropy as a proxy for reasoning health—is systematically blind to a specific, consequential failure mode. Template collapse is not a new kind of degradation that requires exotic detection; it is a degradation that the existing monitoring toolkit was never designed to see. The information-theoretic decomposition `$\mathbf{H}(Z) = \mathbf{I}(X; Z) + \mathbf{H}(Z|X)$` is not novel, but recognizing that it exposes a monitoring gap—and that the missing term `$\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$` can be estimated online from training rollouts with zero additional compute—is a conceptual reframing with immediate practical consequences.

The magnitude of this shift is best characterized as a **diagnostic reframing with mechanistic teeth**, not a paradigm shift. The paper does not propose a new RL algorithm, a new architecture, or a new training objective. It proposes that the field has been monitoring the wrong dimension of reasoning quality and provides both a tool (the MI proxy family) for monitoring the right dimension and a mechanistic account (the SNR gradient dynamics) for why that dimension degrades. This is closer to the impact of a well-validated medical diagnostic than a new surgical technique: it doesn't change what the RL training does, but it changes what practitioners can see while it's happening, which in turn changes which interventions make sense.

A key reconciliation the paper achieves is unifying scattered observations of "reasoning collapse" under a single, precisely defined failure mode with known boundary conditions. Prior work documented that reasoning can become templated [61, 63, 69], that policy behavior can degenerate [9, 59], and that model collapse can occur in self-training loops [12, 47]—but these were treated as distinct or loosely related phenomena. The paper shows that at least some of these observations share a common mechanism: low within-prompt reward variance allows input-agnostic regularization to dominate gradient updates, eroding `$\mathbf{I}(X; Z)$` while sustaining `$\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$`. This explains why entropy-based stabilizers (which target `$\mathbf{H}(Z|X)$`) fail to prevent the degradation—they operate on the dimension that remains healthy, not the one that's collapsing. It also explains why the degradation can be "silent": entropy and reward both look stable, but the structure of reasoning is quietly being replaced by templates. These conflicting prior observations—reasoning degradation reported in some settings but not others, entropy stabilization helping sometimes but not always—are resolved by recognizing that they depend on the reward variance characteristics of the specific environment, which the Std(RV)/Mean(RV) diagnostic can now predict before training begins (Table 8).

The work redirects research attention in several ways. **Improving verifier or reward signal quality** becomes more attractive as a research investment: the SNR mechanism shows that the bottleneck is not search algorithm sophistication or regularization tuning but the fraction of gradient updates that carry genuine task-discriminative information. **Online input-dependence monitoring** becomes a first-class concern in RL training pipelines—the paper demonstrates that it's feasible, lightweight, and more predictive than entropy, making it a natural addition to any training dashboard. **Prompt-level signal quality estimation** emerges as a new control dimension orthogonal to existing stabilization techniques: rather than only controlling how updates are constrained (KL, entropy) or how they're applied (learning rates, clipping), practitioners can now control which data contributes to updates in the first place. This dimension was previously invisible not because it was technically inaccessible but because no one had diagnosed the failure mode it addresses.

Conversely, several research directions become less attractive in light of this work. **Developing ever-more-aggressive entropy regularization** to prevent reasoning collapse is revealed as potentially counterproductive: Figure 13 shows that entropy tuning moves training dynamics along an axis nearly orthogonal to performance and MI improvement, and the entropy bonus may actually facilitate template collapse by encouraging diverse but input-agnostic outputs (Theorem M.2). **Relying solely on outcome-based metrics** (reward, success rate) to monitor training health is shown to be insufficient—the paper demonstrates that format validity can remain near-perfect while MI is low (Figure 12), and that task performance degradation can be preceded by MI decline (Figure 5). **Treating all prompts equally in gradient computation**—the default in standard RL—is now known to be suboptimal when the reward variance distribution is heterogeneous; a uniform batch averages high-SNR and low-SNR updates, and the low-SNR updates dilute the training signal.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Calibrating the MI proxy against external measures of input dependence.** The paper establishes that MI proxies correlate with task performance (+0.39 Spearman) and decline before performance degradation, but provides no absolute calibration: what proxy value corresponds to "sufficiently input-dependent reasoning" for reliable deployment? A follow-up study would construct a benchmark of prompts with ground-truth input-dependence labels—for instance, by generating reasoning traces for prompt pairs where human annotators judge whether the reasoning appropriately differs between inputs—and evaluate whether MI proxies predict these labels better than entropy-based metrics. The specific experiment: for a fixed model checkpoint, compute Retrieval-Acc and MI-ZScore across batches varying in prompt similarity (controlled by embedding distance), and measure whether the proxy tracks ground-truth distinguishability or is confounded by batch composition. This would determine whether MI proxies can serve as *criteria* (thresholds for deployment readiness) or only as *monitors* (trends over time), which is the key unresolved question from Section 6.

**Combining SNR-Aware Filtering with learned difficulty prediction to avoid the grouping cost.** The paper acknowledges that SNR-Aware Filtering requires `$G \geq 2$` trajectories per prompt, which forces a tradeoff between variance estimation quality and prompt diversity. A natural extension is to train a lightweight "signal quality predictor" that estimates per-prompt reward variance from the prompt text alone (or from the prompt plus a single trajectory), eliminating the need for grouped sampling. The training data would come from the grouped rollouts already collected in the first few RL iterations: for each prompt, the predictor sees the prompt embedding and must regress the observed `$\widehat{\text{Var}}(R|X)$` from `$G$` trajectories. A strong follow-up would compare three deployment modes: (a) standard SNR-Aware Filtering with `$G \geq 2$` grouped sampling, (b) predicted-RV filtering with `$G=1$` (no grouping, predictor provides variance estimate), and (c) a hybrid where the predictor selects the top-k prompts for grouped resampling in a second stage. The key metric is whether predicted-RV filtering can recover the performance gains of grouped filtering (e.g., the +16.0% on Sokoban PPO from Table 4) while maintaining the prompt diversity of the 128 × 1 configuration from Table 5. This would transform SNR-Aware Filtering from a method that requires a deployment tradeoff into one that imposes no constraints on batch composition.

**Stress-testing the SNR mechanism with controlled reward-function noise.** The paper's noise injection experiment (Figure 9) varies environmental stochasticity, but this conflates two sources of noise: noise in state transitions (which affects all trajectories) and noise in the reward function (which affects only the feedback signal). A cleaner experiment would add controlled zero-mean Gaussian noise directly to the scalar reward at varying magnitudes while keeping environment dynamics deterministic, then measure the relationship between reward-function SNR (defined as `$\text{Var}(\mu(x,z))/\sigma^2_{\text{noise}}$` where `$\mu(x,z)$` is the true trajectory quality) and template collapse severity (measured by MI proxy decline). The prediction from the SNR mechanism is that MI should degrade monotonically as reward-function SNR decreases, even when environment dynamics are perfectly deterministic, because advantage estimates become dominated by reward noise rather than trajectory quality differences. This would isolate the reward-signal component of the SNR mechanism from the environment-stochasticity component, providing a cleaner test of whether it's specifically *reward discriminability* (not general environment noise) that controls template collapse. A negative result—MI remaining stable despite high reward noise—would suggest that the mechanism operates primarily through policy entropy rather than gradient SNR, requiring a revision of the mechanistic account.

**Evaluating whether template collapse transfers to open-ended or subjective-reward domains.** All seven environments in the paper use synthetic, programmatic reward functions with clearly defined correctness criteria. In real-world RLHF training for dialogue or creative writing, rewards come from learned preference models with their own noise, bias, and calibration drift—and there may be no ground-truth "correct" reasoning to distinguish from templates. A follow-up would replicate the core experimental design (monitoring both MI proxy and entropy during PPO training) on an RLHF-style setup: a summarization or dialogue task where the reward model is a separately trained preference predictor, and where the notion of "input-dependent reasoning" is fuzzier (e.g., should a summary of article A emphasize different aspects than a summary of article B? Yes, but the boundary is less crisp than in math). The key question is whether the MI proxy—which relies on the policy's own log-likelihoods for cross-scoring—still tracks meaningful input dependence when "correctness" is subjective, or whether it degrades to noise because the policy's probability estimates are poorly calibrated for open-ended generation. The specific experiment: train PPO for a summarization task with a learned reward model, track Retrieval-Acc and MI-ZScore over training, and at the peak-performance checkpoint, conduct a human evaluation where annotators judge whether summaries for different articles are *appropriately* input-adapted (not just stylistically diverse). Correlate human judgments with MI proxy values to determine whether the proxy generalizes beyond programmatic-reward domains.

**Testing whether SNR-Aware Filtering combined with exploration bonuses can convert hard prompts into informative training signal.** The paper identifies a fundamental ceiling: prompts where all trajectories fail (zero reward variance) are filtered out and receive no training signal, so the model never improves on them. A follow-up would add an exploration bonus—either intrinsic motivation (e.g., prediction error, novelty, or information gain) or structured exploration (e.g., epsilon-greedy action perturbation)—to create *artificial* reward variance on initially unsolvable prompts. The prediction: if the exploration bonus is well-designed, hard prompts that would have zero reward variance under the base policy will now show variance because some trajectories discover partial solutions or novel states, making them eligible for SNR-Aware Filtering and allowing the model to bootstrap toward solving them. The specific experiment: on Sokoban, identify the hardest quartile of prompts (where base-model pass@1 is near zero), add an intrinsic reward proportional to state novelty (measured by a separately trained dynamics model's prediction error), and compare three conditions: (a) no filtering + standard reward, (b) SNR-Aware Filtering + standard reward, (c) SNR-Aware Filtering + standard reward + intrinsic reward. If condition (c) substantially outperforms (b) on the hardest prompts, it demonstrates that SNR-Aware Filtering can be extended beyond its current capability ceiling by converting hard prompts from zero-RV (filtered out) to positive-RV (selected for training). This would address the limitation noted in Section 6 about the method providing no path forward for genuinely hard problems.

**Characterizing the interaction surface between SNR-Aware Filtering and existing stabilization hyperparameters.** The paper claims that SNR-Aware Filtering is "complementary" to KL and entropy regularization (Section 4.3) and shows that these interventions move training dynamics along different axes (Figure 13). However, the experiments do not jointly sweep filter keep rate and regularization coefficients—they sweep each independently. A follow-up would run a grid search over `$(\rho, \lambda_{\text{KL}}, \lambda_H)$` on Sokoban and FrozenLake to map the full interaction surface. The specific questions: does the optimal entropy coefficient change when filtering is applied? (Prediction: yes, because filtering already removes low-SNR updates, reducing the need for entropy-based diversity preservation—the optimal `$\lambda_H$` should be lower with filtering.) Does filtering reduce sensitivity to KL coefficient misspecification? (Prediction: yes, because filtering concentrates updates on high-signal prompts where the KL constraint is less likely to dominate.) The output would be a set of recommended default hyperparameter ranges for joint SNR-Aware Filtering + PPO training, addressing the practical concern that the method adds a new hyperparameter (`$\rho$`) without guidance for how it interacts with existing ones.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Online training monitoring for production RL fine-tuning pipelines.** Any organization running closed-loop RL fine-tuning of LLM agents (for code generation, tool use, customer support automation, or game-playing) can integrate the MI proxy into their training dashboard immediately. The implementation requires no additional models, no additional inference passes, and no changes to the RL algorithm—only a logging hook that computes the cross-scoring matrix from rollout log-probabilities. The paper demonstrates that MI decline *precedes* task performance degradation under no-filter training (Figure 5), making it an effective early warning signal. A concrete deployment: a training loop that automatically pauses or flags for human review when Trajectory MI-ZScore drops below an empirically determined threshold (e.g., 2 standard deviations below the running mean over the last 50 iterations). The monitoring cost is the `$P \times G \times P$` cross-scoring computation, which for `$P=8, G=16$` is 1,024 evaluations per iteration—negligible compared to the cost of generating 128 trajectories. Given that entropy-based monitoring is already standard in these pipelines [42, 62], adding MI monitoring fills a documented blind spot with near-zero overhead.

**Signal-quality-aware data selection for self-improvement loops.** In self-training or iterative fine-tuning pipelines where a model generates its own training data (e.g., STaR [Zelikman et al.], ReST$^{\text{EM}}$ [Singh et al.], or rejection sampling fine-tuning), the standard approach is to filter generated solutions by correctness and train on the correct ones. SNR-Aware Filtering suggests a complementary filter: *within-prompt reward variance*. For each training prompt, generate `$G$` candidate solutions (not just one), compute a scalar reward for each (from unit tests, verifier scores, or environment feedback), and retain prompts where the reward variance across candidates is high—indicating that the model can discriminate between good and bad solutions, making the gradient update informative. The paper shows that training exclusively on high-variance prompts (quartile Q1–Q2, Table 6) roughly doubles task performance compared to training on low-variance prompts (Q3–Q4: ~20% vs. ~11% on Sokoban). In a self-improvement loop, this would mean that after each generation round, the training data is filtered not just for correctness but for *discriminability*, concentrating fine-tuning on examples where the model has the most to learn. The concrete setup: for each of `$P$` training prompts, sample `$G=4$` candidate solutions, score them with the environment reward, compute within-prompt RV, retain the top `$\rho=0.9$` prompts by variance mass, and fine-tune only on the retained examples. This adds one hyperparameter (`$\rho$`) and minimal computation (RV is a scalar variance over 4 values) to existing self-improvement pipelines, with the potential for the `$4\times$`-equivalent efficiency gains that the compute-optimal allocation literature [prior work on test-time compute] has demonstrated in inference-time settings.

**Pre-deployment capability auditing for RL-trained agents.** Before deploying an RL-trained agent, practitioners typically evaluate it on a held-out test set and report aggregate metrics (accuracy, success rate, average reward). The MI proxy enables a more granular audit: compute Retrieval-Acc on the test set to measure whether the agent's reasoning is genuinely input-adaptive or templated. An agent achieving 90% success rate but with Retrieval-Acc near chance level (e.g., 0.125 for `$P=8$`) would be flagged as potentially unreliable—its high success rate may reflect the test distribution being similar to training, and its templated reasoning may fail catastrophically on distribution shift. The paper demonstrates that format validity can be near-perfect while MI is low (Figure 12), so standard behavioral evaluation metrics would miss this vulnerability. The concrete audit: for each test prompt, generate `$G=2$` reasoning traces, compute the in-batch Retrieval-Acc and MI-ZScore across the full test set, and report these alongside standard performance metrics. A deployment decision could then incorporate both axes: high success rate + high MI → deploy with confidence; high success rate + low MI → investigate for template collapse before deploying; low success rate + high MI → model is input-adaptive but insufficiently capable (needs more training); low success rate + low MI → model has both capability and reasoning-quality failures. The paper's correlation evidence (Figure 8) supports that MI adds predictive power beyond performance metrics alone.

**Hyperparameter-free early stopping based on MI dynamics.** The paper's early stopping criteria (Appendix B.2) use two rules: reward-variance collapse detection and validation success rate stagnation. The MI proxy dynamics suggest a third, potentially more sensitive criterion: stop training when MI proxy has declined for `$k$` consecutive iterations while entropy remains stable or rising. Figure 5 shows that under no-filter training, Retrieval-Acc drops substantially (from ~0.6–0.8 to ~0.2) before task performance peaks and turns downward. An early stopping rule triggered by MI decline could terminate training *before* performance degradation occurs, saving compute and preserving the best checkpoint. The concrete rule: at each evaluation step, compute Trajectory MI-ZScore-EMA; if it has decreased for 5 consecutive evaluations while conditional entropy has not decreased by more than 10%, stop training and revert to the checkpoint with the highest MI × performance product. This rule would have stopped the no-filter run in Figure 5 around step 40–60 (where MI begins declining) rather than letting it continue to step 200 (where performance has degraded). The paper provides the evidence that MI decline precedes performance decline, but does not itself implement or evaluate MI-based early stopping—this is a direct practical application of the diagnostic that requires only logging the appropriate metrics during training.
