## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **RATIONALYST**, a three-stage approach for improving LLM reasoning by mining implicit rationales from unlabeled data, training a specialized rationale generation model, and using those rationales to provide process supervision during inference—addressing the problem that chain-of-thought reasoning often omits logical connections present but unstated in pre-training data (e.g., "When someone breaks the rule, he will be punished" as the missing link between a cause and its effect). The system extracts 79k rationales from the Pile and two reasoning datasets (GSM8K, ECQA), fine-tunes LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct to generate these rationales as a verifier, and then uses them at inference to rerank candidate reasoning steps via an implicit supervision mechanism (probability-based heuristic scoring of next-step candidates conditioned on the rationale). RATIONALYST improves accuracy by an average of 3.9% across seven diverse reasoning benchmarks and matches or exceeds the process-supervision performance of GPT-4, establishing that self-supervised rationale extraction from web-scale data can provide effective step-level guidance without human annotation—but only when the extracted rationales demonstrably reduce the perplexity of subsequent text, making the filtration step essential for quality.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Chain-of-Thought Reasoning Skips Steps

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **when LLMs generate chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, they frequently omit intermediate logical steps that are necessary for robust inference.** These omissions aren't random errors—they are systematic, arising from the nature of the pre-training data itself. As the paper illustrates in Figure 1, everyday communication and web text routinely leave logical connections *implicit* (unstated). A passage from Harry Potter says "Harry used magic outside... He is punished to attend a disciplinary hearing," and the reader naturally infers the missing premise: *"When someone breaks the rule, he will be punished."* But that premise is never written down. LLMs trained to mimic this kind of text learn to replicate the same pattern: they produce reasoning chains that skip steps, because that's what their training data looks like.

The consequence is that these reasoning gaps can lead to **flawed conclusions**. In the paper's opening example (Figure 1, panel 2), an LLM presented with "A person is caught stealing food to feed their hungry family. What will likely happen to them?" produces the erroneous answer *"They will be rewarded"*—because it fails to surface the implicit rationale that stealing violates societal rules regardless of benevolent intent. The inference collapses because the model's reasoning chain is incomplete. This isn't a problem of missing knowledge (the model "knows" that stealing is wrong) but of missing *explicit articulation* of that knowledge during the step-by-step process.

This gap matters for several reasons:

- **Reliability of reasoning**: If LLMs systematically skip logical steps, their reasoning is brittle—correct on surface-level patterns but prone to collapse when the missing step is load-bearing. This is particularly dangerous in domains like mathematical reasoning (where a skipped intermediate calculation produces a wrong final answer) or ethical reasoning (where an unstated moral principle leads to an unacceptable conclusion).

- **Interpretability**: Chain-of-thought reasoning is valued partly because it exposes *why* the model arrived at its answer, enabling human oversight. If the chain contains logical leaps with implicit rationales, that oversight is compromised—the human reviewer sees a sequence of statements but not the inferential glue connecting them. The paper emphasizes this interpretability motivation explicitly (Section 5.5, Appendix D), showing that RATIONALYST's rationales are human-readable explanations of the reasoning process.

- **Process supervision requires good rationales**: If you want to supervise an LLM's reasoning step-by-step—checking whether each intermediate inference is correct—you need those intermediate inferences to be *explicit*. When they're left implicit, there's nothing to verify. The entire paradigm of process-based supervision (Lightman et al., 2023) presupposes that reasoning steps are stated and thus verifiable. The gap this paper identifies undermines that paradigm at its foundation.

### Why Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper positions itself against three broad categories of prior work, each of which addresses reasoning quality but fails to solve the implicit rationale problem:

**1. Outcome-based supervision (Cobbe et al., 2021; Snell et al., 2024).** These approaches train a "verifier" to judge whether a *final answer* is correct, without attending to intermediate reasoning steps. While effective and parameter-efficient (sometimes outperforming just scaling the reasoner itself), they provide no signal about *where* or *why* reasoning went wrong. A model can produce the right answer through flawed reasoning and still pass outcome verification, or produce nearly-correct reasoning with a single arithmetic error and be rejected wholesale. More fundamentally, outcome verifiers cannot *guide* reasoning during generation—they can only filter completed solutions—so they don't help the model avoid the implicit-rationale problem in the first place.

**2. Process-based supervision with human annotation (Lightman et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024a).** These methods provide supervision at *each reasoning step*, using human-labeled correctness judgments for intermediate inferences. This is conceptually ideal—it addresses exactly the kind of stepwise reasoning gaps this paper identifies. But it is **resource-intensive to the point of being impractical at scale**. Lightman et al. (2023) required massive human annotation efforts to label intermediate steps. Other process-supervision methods that avoid human annotation (e.g., Wang et al., 2024a) do so by relying on ground-truth final answers to automatically label steps, but this only works for tasks where final answers exist and are verifiable—mathematical reasoning primarily. For commonsense reasoning, ethical reasoning, or scientific reasoning, ground-truth step labels are unavailable without human annotation.

The paper argues this creates a fundamental tension: process supervision is theoretically powerful but practically constrained to domains and scales where human annotation or ground-truth labels are feasible. The implicit rationale problem—which spans *all* reasoning domains—remains unsolved for tasks outside math.

**3. General-purpose verifiers using compositional principles (Dhuliawala et al., 2023; Weir et al., 2024; Vacareanu et al., 2024).** These approaches apply broad verification strategies (e.g., checking logical coherence, factual consistency, compositional structure) without being tied to specific task domains. While they offer wider applicability than domain-specific verifiers, they don't leverage the massive amounts of unlabeled data available for reasoning tasks. They are *principle-driven* rather than *data-driven*, which limits their ability to learn domain-specific reasoning patterns that might be latent in large text corpora.

**4. The fundamental limitation of next-token prediction training.** The paper goes beyond these method-specific critiques to identify a deeper issue: **the standard pre-training objective itself is misaligned with producing complete reasoning.** As Section 2 states, "LLMs are conventionally trained on extensive web data using autoregressive next-token prediction. While effective, this approach may not fully harness the potential of the pre-training data, as latent information within this data could be better accessed using techniques beyond simple next-token prediction." The point is subtle but important: web text *contains* the implicit rationales as latent knowledge—they're inferrable by a careful reader—but next-token prediction doesn't incentivize making them explicit. The model learns to reproduce the surface text distribution, including its omissions. What's needed is not just better training data but a different mechanism for surfacing what's already latent in existing data.

**5. Quiet-Star (Zelikman et al., 2024) as the closest prior work.** The paper explicitly acknowledges Quiet-Star as the most related prior approach. Quiet-Star also extracts implicit rationales from unlabeled text to improve reasoning, using a parallel thinking process during generation. However, the paper identifies a key distinction: Quiet-Star integrates rationale extraction *directly with reasoner training*, optimizing rationales end-to-end based on final task accuracy. This coupling makes the approach complex and, as the paper notes in Section 5.5, can produce rationales optimized for task performance rather than human interpretability—potentially developing "shortcuts or non-intuitive patterns that optimize for accuracy but not necessarily for clarity or human understanding."

### How This Paper Positions Itself

RATIONALYST occupies a specific niche in this landscape. The paper frames its contribution not as a wholly new paradigm but as a **synthesis of data-centric rationale extraction and decoupled process supervision** that sidesteps the limitations of prior work.

**The core positioning claim** is that you can extract implicit rationales from unlabeled data at scale, train a separate "supervisor" model to generate those rationales during inference, and use that supervisor to guide *any* reasoning agent—without human annotation, without ground-truth labels, and without coupling rationale learning to reasoner training. This is a "simpler and more reliable method," as the paper states, because the decoupling means:

- The rationale model can be trained on diverse unlabeled corpora (web text, reasoning datasets) without being constrained by the reasoner's training objective.
- During inference, the rationale model can supervise any agent model, making it a plug-and-play component.
- The training data (extracted rationales) is self-supervised from existing text, so the approach scales with available unlabeled data.
- The filtration step—keeping only rationales that reduce the perplexity of subsequent text—provides a quality signal that replaces human annotation without requiring ground-truth answers.

**The paper's contribution is methodologically incremental but conceptually significant.** Each component (rationale extraction, filtration via future-token prediction, stepwise supervision) has precedents in prior work (Toolformer's self-supervised extraction, Quiet-Star's rationale mining, Lightman's process supervision). The novelty is in the combination: (1) extracting rationales at scale from diverse sources including web data, (2) filtering them with a principled future-prediction criterion, (3) training a dedicated rationale model rather than integrating rationales into the reasoner, and (4) using the rationale model for *process supervision* specifically, not just for improving generation directly. This combination enables a form of process-based verification that works across reasoning domains without the annotation bottleneck that limited prior process-supervision methods.

The paper also positions itself pragmatically regarding domain coverage. By extracting rationales from both general web text (The Pile, providing broad reasoning patterns) and specific reasoning datasets (GSM8K for math, ECQA for commonsense), it aims for a model that generalizes across reasoning types while maintaining strong performance on the domains it was explicitly trained for. The ablation in Section 5.2 (comparing performance with and without web-scale rationales) is designed to validate this positioning: the web data provides diversity that improves generalization, while the domain-specific data provides targeted quality.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation (Approachable Technical Breakdown)

RATIONALYST is a three-stage pipeline that builds a specialized "supervisor" model to guide another LLM's reasoning step-by-step, without requiring any human to annotate intermediate reasoning steps. The problem it solves is that LLMs skip logical connections when generating chain-of-thought reasoning—they leave rationales *implicit* because their pre-training data does the same thing—and the solution is to mine those missing rationales from unlabeled text, train a model to generate them, and use them as heuristics to select good next reasoning steps during inference.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components connected in a pipeline that flows from data extraction through model training to inference-time deployment:

1. **Rationale Extraction Engine** — an LLM (LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct) that reads documents from unlabeled corpora (The Pile) and reasoning datasets (GSM8K, ECQA) and generates candidate implicit rationales at sentence boundaries, producing pairs of (preceding context, proposed rationale).

2. **Rationale Filtration Module** — the same LLM, operating in a different role, estimates whether each extracted rationale actually helps predict subsequent text by measuring the change in next-token prediction loss when the rationale is included as context versus when it is omitted. Rationales that reduce the loss by at least a threshold `$\tau_f$` are retained; others are discarded.

3. **RATIONALYST Model (MRa)** — a fine-tuned version of LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct trained to generate implicit rationales given only the preceding context (for web text) or the question plus partial reasoning trajectory (for reasoning tasks). The training targets are the filtered rationales from step 2.

4. **Agent Model (MAgent)** — any general-purpose LLM (the paper uses LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct) that performs the actual reasoning task by generating chain-of-thought steps incrementally. It produces multiple candidate next steps at each reasoning stage.

5. **Inference-Time Heuristic Scorer (M)** — the same base LLM (LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct) estimates the probability of each candidate next reasoning step *conditioned on the implicit rationale generated by MRa*. This probability serves as a heuristic score; the highest-scoring candidate is appended to the reasoning trajectory, and the process repeats.

Information flows as follows during inference: the question enters the agent model → the agent generates a partial reasoning trajectory → RATIONALYST (MRa) reads the current trajectory and produces an implicit rationale → the heuristic scorer (M) evaluates each candidate next step from the agent by computing its probability given the rationale → the highest-scoring candidate extends the trajectory → the loop continues until a stop condition is met (e.g., "The final answer is:" appears in the trajectory).

Crucially, this decouples the rationale generator from the reasoner—any agent model can be supervised by the same RATIONALYST, and the rationale model is trained separately on its own objective.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal rationale filtration criterion (the `$L_i$` equation in Section 3.1C), because this criterion is what separates RATIONALYST's extraction process from naive rationale generation and defines what counts as a "useful" rationale.
- **Second**, the three-part rationale extraction pipeline (pre-filtering web documents, generating rationales with prompted LLMs, filtering by future-token prediction), covering both web-scale data (The Pile) and structured reasoning datasets (GSM8K, ECQA), since this extraction process produces all the training data.
- **Third**, the RATIONALYST training procedure, explaining exactly what inputs and targets look like for both web-text and reasoning-dataset sources, and how the model learns to generate rationales from context alone.
- **Fourth**, the inference-time algorithm (Algorithm 1), walking through the two supervision methods—implicit and explicit—and showing why implicit supervision proves more robust despite being the "softer" approach.
- **Fifth**, the design choices and their justifications, including why the paper decouples rationale generation from reasoner training, why context windows are truncated, why the filtration threshold is set for high precision on reasoning datasets, and why the same base model is used for multiple roles.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **data-centric systems paper** whose core idea is that implicit rationales are latent in unlabeled text, can be extracted and filtered using self-supervised signals, and can then be used to train a verifier that supervises any reasoning agent's step-by-step process—all without human annotation.

---

#### 3.4.1 The Rationale Filtration Criterion: What Makes a Rationale "Useful"?

The filtration criterion is the intellectual linchpin of the entire extraction pipeline. Without it, the system would be training on generated rationales of unknown quality, many of which would be irrelevant or misleading. The criterion operationalizes "usefulness" in a specific, measurable way: a rationale is useful if it makes the subsequent text *more predictable* to the language model.

The paper defines the filtration score through a cross-entropy difference calculation. Let `$x = x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$` be a token sequence representing the document. Let `$i$` be the position in this sequence where a candidate rationale `$r$` has been inserted (at a sentence boundary in the original text). The cross-entropy loss for predicting all future tokens from position `$i$` onward, *when conditioned on the rationale*, is:

$$L_i(r) = -\sum_{j=i}^{n} w_{j-i} \cdot \log p_{\mathcal{M}}(x_j \mid r, x_{1:j-1})$$

where `$p_{\mathcal{M}}(x_j \mid r, x_{1:j-1})$` is the probability assigned by model `$\mathcal{M}$` to token `$x_j$` given all preceding tokens `$x_{1:j-1}$` *and* the rationale `$r$`, and `$w_{j-i}$` is a weight that decreases exponentially as the token gets further from the rationale (specifically, the weight is multiplied by `$0.9$` for each step further away; the paper states "The weight assigned to each future token decreases exponentially by a factor of 0.9 for each step further away it is from the rationale").

The paper then computes the *difference* between this loss and the loss when no rationale is provided (denoted `$\varepsilon$` for an empty rationale):

$$L_i = L_i(r_i) - L_i(\varepsilon)$$

A rationale is retained if and only if:

$$L_i \geq \tau_f$$

where `$\tau_f$` is a filtering threshold.

**What this computes in operational terms:** Take a document. At some sentence boundary `$i$`, generate a candidate rationale `$r$`. Then ask the model `$\mathcal{M}$` to predict every word that comes after position `$i$` in the document, *twice*—once with the rationale `$r$` included as context, and once without it. Compute the cross-entropy (a measure of surprise: lower entropy means the model's predictions were closer to the actual text) for both predictions, weighted to emphasize nearby tokens more than distant ones. If adding the rationale reduces the model's surprise—meaning the rationale genuinely helps anticipate what comes next—then the rationale is "useful" and we keep it. If it doesn't help, or if it makes predictions worse, we discard it. The threshold `$\tau_f$` controls how strict this test is.

**Why this form:** The paper motivates this design with three considerations. First, the exponential decay of weights (factor 0.9 per step) reflects the intuition that a rationale's predictive value should be highest for immediately following text and diminish for text far downstream—a rationale about the next calculation step shouldn't be expected to help predict tokens thirty sentences later. Without this weighting, a rationale that slightly helps with nearby tokens but hurts distant predictions might net to zero, when the nearby effect is what actually matters for reasoning supervision.

Second, using the *difference* `$L_i$` rather than the absolute loss `$L_i(r)$` normalizes for per-document difficulty. Some documents are inherently harder to predict than others; what matters is whether the rationale improves prediction *relative to the same document's baseline predictability*. A rationale that reduces loss from 5.0 to 4.9 on a hard document (difference of 0.1) is genuinely useful; a rationale that produces an absolute loss of 4.9 on an easy document where the baseline was 3.0 is actually harmful. The difference `$L_i$` captures this correctly.

Third, the filtration uses the *same* model `$\mathcal{M}$` that will later be used for rationale extraction and heuristic scoring during inference. This creates a tight feedback loop: the model's own judgments about predictability determine what counts as useful, and those usefulness judgments shape what MRa learns to generate. If a different model were used for filtration, the extracted rationales might not align with what the downstream components find helpful—a form of distribution mismatch.

The paper explicitly acknowledges a practical subtlety regarding threshold selection (Section 4.1): "Since it's straightforward to scale up the extraction of rationales from unlabelled data for filtration, we prioritize maximizing the precision of our filtered rationales, even if it means extracting fewer of them." For the reasoning datasets (GSM8K, ECQA), the authors manually annotated 100 rationale pairs (50 positive, 50 negative) and set `$\tau_f$` to ensure 95% precision—meaning 95% of retained rationales in the annotated sample were judged as genuinely helpful. The resulting thresholds were `$\tau_f = 1.2$` for GSM8K and `$\tau_f = 0.5$` for ECQA. For The Pile, due to its diverse composition making manual annotation impractical, `$\tau_f$` was set to 0 for all subdomains—accepting any rationale that improves future prediction at all (the difference is positive), which is a much weaker filter but avoids discarding potentially useful rationales in an unfamiliar domain.

#### 3.4.2 The Rationale Extraction Pipeline: From Unlabeled Text to Filtered Rationale Collection

The extraction pipeline has three sequential stages for each data source: pre-filtering to find reasoning-rich documents, generating candidate rationales with a prompted LLM, and filtering those candidates using the criterion from Section 3.4.1. The paper applies this pipeline to two qualitatively different data sources: web-scale unlabeled text (The Pile) and structured reasoning datasets (GSM8K for math, ECQA for commonsense).

---

##### Stage A: Extraction from Web-Scale Pre-Training Data (The Pile)

**Step 1 — Pre-filtering: Finding reasoning-rich documents.** The Pile is an 800GB corpus of diverse web text with many document types, most of which contain little or no explicit reasoning. Running the full rationale extraction pipeline on all documents would be computationally wasteful and would mostly produce irrelevant rationales. The paper therefore applies a semantic similarity pre-filter:

1. Compute the average semantic embedding of the training sets of representative reasoning datasets (the paper doesn't specify exactly which datasets beyond implying GSM8K/ECQA are included, but states "representative reasoning training sets" are used) using MPNet-base (Song et al., 2020), a paragraph embedding model.
2. Compute the embedding of each candidate document from The Pile using the same MPNet-base model.
3. Compute the cosine similarity between the document embedding and the average reasoning embedding.
4. Retain only documents whose cosine similarity exceeds a threshold `$\alpha = 0.3$`.
5. Additionally, retain only documents with length under 2000 tokens to fit within LLaMa-3's context window (the paper states "keeping only the documents with length under 2000 tokens to fit within LLaMa-3 models' context length").

This pre-filtering step is a practical necessity driven by computational constraints, not a claimed theoretical contribution. The paper notes in Section 6 that using larger unlabeled datasets like OpenWebMath was "currently infeasible due to the significant computational and time requirements for pre-filtering and training," implying that even with pre-filtering, the compute budget was a binding constraint.

**Step 2 — Rationale generation: Prompting the LLM to surface implicit connections.** For each pre-filtered document, the text is segmented into 2000-token chunks. At the end of each sentence boundary within a chunk, the LLM (LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct) is prompted to generate an implicit rationale that captures the reasoning connecting the current sentence to what follows. The rationales are enclosed in special tokens `<BOT>` and `<EOT>` (presumably "Beginning of Thought" and "End of Thought").

The prompt (provided in Appendix B, Figure 6) uses few-shot demonstrations showing the model what kind of output is expected. Looking at the example in Figure 6, for a snippet from what appears to be StackExchange math content:

> "Suppose -s*w = -26*w. Suppose -15 = -5*j - w*j. Let g(b) = b**3 - 3*b**2 - 2*b + 3. What is g(j)?"

The generated rationale is:

> "<BOT>First solve the equations to find j, then substitute j into g(b) to calculate g(j)<EOT>"

This is notable: the rationale doesn't do the calculation; it articulates the *plan*—the reasoning strategy—that an expert would have in mind when reading the problem statement. It surfaces the implicit logical connection between the given equations and the target function. Another example from the prompt shows rationales that serve as transition annotations: "<BOT>Indicates JZ's role, suggesting the next roles to be described will involve other tasks like attending doctors and result analysis<EOT>." These rationales act as explicit *intent* or *plan* statements that connect discourse segments.

The key design choice here is *where* rationales are generated: "at the end of each sentence." This means every sentence boundary in the document is a candidate location for a rationale. Practically, this over-generates—most sentence boundaries don't require an explicit rationale—but the filtration step (Stage C) is designed to remove the excess.

**Step 3 — Filtration:** Apply the criterion from Section 3.4.1. For The Pile, `$\tau_f = 0$` for all subdomains, meaning any rationale that produces a positive difference `$L_i > 0$` is retained. Even with this permissive threshold, the filtration rate is aggressive: Table 1 shows that for Pile-CC (Common Crawl, the largest subdomain), only 2.9% of generated rationales survive filtration. For GitHub, only 2.6% survive. The highest retention is StackExchange at 29.8%, which the paper attributes to "its question-answering format aligning well with our reasoning tasks and containing more inherent reasoning." This stark variation in retention rates validates the pre-filtering and filtration design: different text sources genuinely differ in how much implicit reasoning they contain, and the filtration criterion discriminates effectively.

The paper provides total extraction statistics in Table 1. Across all Pile subdomains (Pile-CC, StackExchange, GitHub, HackerNews, PubMed Central, Wikipedia), approximately 1.1 million rationales were generated from about 323,000 documents, and approximately 65,000 survived filtration—an overall retention rate of roughly 6%, heavily skewed by StackExchange.

##### Stage B: Extraction from Reasoning Datasets (GSM8K and ECQA)

In parallel with the web-scale extraction, the paper also extracts rationales from existing reasoning datasets. This serves three purposes stated explicitly (Section 3.1B and Section 4.1): "to adapt the extracted rationales to our tested domain and stabilize training," and because "GSM8K and ECQA were selected for their complementary coverage of mathematical and commonsense reasoning, respectively."

**Input format for reasoning datasets:** For a dataset with question-answer pairs `$D = \{(q_i, a_i)\}_{i=1}^m$`, the entire question `$q_i$` and the complete solution `$a_i$` (which includes chain-of-thought reasoning steps) are provided as input to the LLM. The LLM is prompted to insert implicit rationales between the reasoning steps in `$a_i$`.

Consider the paper's concrete example (Figure 3, bottom):

- **Question:** "Natalia sold 48 clips in April and half as many clips in May. How many clips did Natalia sell altogether?"
- **Answer with reasoning steps:** "Natalia sold 48 / 2 = 24 clips in May ... Natalia sold 48 + 24 = 72 chips altogether."
- **Extracted rationale (inserted between steps):** "<BOT>Now we should calculate the sum of chips in April and May<EOT>"

The rationale articulates the connecting inference: having computed May's sales, the next logical operation is to sum April and May. This is implicit in the original solution—a human reader would infer it—but making it explicit turns it into a verifiable supervisory signal.

The prompts used for GSM8K (Appendix B, Figure 4) and ECQA (Appendix B, Figure 5) each include two few-shot examples showing the expected format. The GSM8K prompt emphasizes step-by-step mathematical reasoning with rationales like "First, we need to calculate how many golf balls Michael had after losing 23 on Tuesday"; the ECQA prompt emphasizes comparative reasoning over answer choices with rationales like "We also need to establish relationship between acquire and the question itself." The demonstrations are carefully chosen to show diverse rationale types: planning rationales ("First, we need to calculate..."), checking rationales ("Let's check whether other answer choices are related..."), transition rationales ("Now we need to calculate..."), and finalization rationales ("We are ready to output the final answer").

**A crucial anti-leakage precaution:** The paper explicitly states that during rationale extraction from reasoning datasets, "we explicitly instruct M to exclude answers from the extracted rationales. This precaution prevents answer leakage in our prompts." This means the generated rationales contain reasoning guidance but not the actual numerical answers or final answer choices. A rationale like "Now we should calculate the sum of chips in April and May" doesn't reveal that the sum is 72; it only reveals that addition is the next operation. This is essential for the rationales to function as legitimate process supervision rather than as answer lookups—if the rationale leaked the final answer, MRa would just be regurgitating answers rather than providing reasoning guidance.

**Stage C — Filtration for reasoning datasets:** The same filtration criterion (Section 3.4.1) is applied, but with dataset-specific thresholds. From Section 4.1: "On GSM8K and ECQA, we manually annotated 100 pairs of {preceding_context, rationale, following_context} to determine an appropriate filtration threshold. The annotations include 50 positive and 50 negative rationale examples." The thresholds were set to achieve 95% precision: `$\tau_f = 1.2$` for GSM8K and `$\tau_f = 0.5$` for ECQA.

The filtering results (Table 1): GSM8K generated 17,566 rationales from 7,473 training examples, with 19.5% retained (approximately 3,400 rationales). ECQA generated 19,669 rationales from 7,600 examples, with 57.6% retained (approximately 11,300 rationales). The much higher retention rate for ECQA suggests that commonsense reasoning questions more consistently contain inferrable implicit rationales than math word problems do—perhaps because commonsense reasoning steps are more often left implicit in the answer text, while math word problem solutions tend to be more explicit about calculations already.

In total, the extraction pipeline produces approximately 14,000 rationales from the reasoning datasets (GSM8K + ECQA) and approximately 65,000 from The Pile, yielding roughly 79,000 total training rationales.

#### 3.4.3 RATIONALYST Training (MRa): Learning to Generate Rationales from Context

The goal of training MRa is to produce a model that, given only the *preceding context* (without seeing the future text that was used during filtration), can generate the implicit rationale that would help predict what comes next. This is a conditional generation task: the input is partial context; the output is a rationale.

**Training data construction.** The training data comes in two forms, corresponding to the two extraction sources:

**Form 1 — Web text (The Pile):** The input is a segment of text from a document, truncated at a sentence boundary where a filtered rationale was extracted. The target output is that rationale. For example, from Figure 6, the input might be the text up to and including "JZ takes responsibility for drafting the manuscript." and the target output would be "<BOT>Indicates JZ's role, suggesting the next roles to be described will involve other tasks like attending doctors and result analysis<EOT>." Critically, the model does *not* see the text that follows the insertion point during training—it must learn to generate the rationale purely from what came before. This is what makes the training task non-trivial: the model must infer what kind of reasoning would be useful next based solely on the preceding discourse.

**Form 2 — Reasoning datasets (GSM8K, ECQA):** The input consists of the question text plus *any reasoning steps generated so far* toward the answer—what the paper calls the "reasoning trajectory." The target output is the rationale that connects the current trajectory to the next reasoning step. During training, the full solution is available (since extraction was done on complete solution texts), so the model sees complete trajectories as input and the extracted rationales as targets. However, the important point is that the training *targets* are the extracted rationales, not the answer steps themselves. As the paper states: "while M extracts rationales from the training sets of reasoning datasets, these training sets are not directly used as targets when training MRa. Instead, the target of training is the rationale generated by M." This means MRa is never trained to produce the actual reasoning steps or answers—only the implicit rationales that guide reasoning.

**Training objective.** The overall training objective is standard per-token cross-entropy loss between MRa's generated rationale tokens and the extracted (filtered) rationale tokens. The model is trained to maximize the likelihood of producing the exact rationale text that was extracted and filtered.

**Base model and hyperparameters.** RATIONALYST is fine-tuned from LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct (MetaAI, 2024). The paper states it uses "the default hyperparameters as specified in the LLaMa-3 technical report for fine-tuning," but does not enumerate them in the main text. This is a limitation of the paper's reporting—the reader cannot reproduce the training without consulting the LLaMa-3 technical report. However, the paper does note in Section 4.1 a post-training validation step: "After training, we conducted manual annotation of the model's output and found that the accuracy of rationales generated on unseen test data closely matches the filtration accuracy we specified for training data through our filtration parameters." This suggests that the model generalizes well from the filtered training data to unseen contexts, and that the 95% precision target on reasoning datasets is approximately maintained at test time.

**Data mixture.** The training combines all 79,000 rationales (14,000 from reasoning datasets + 65,000 from The Pile). The paper does not specify the exact mixing ratio during training—whether it trains on all data simultaneously with uniform sampling, or whether it up-samples the reasoning dataset rationales to prevent The Pile from dominating. Given the ablation results (Section 5.2, Table 3), removing The Pile rationales causes a consistent drop across all benchmarks (largest on MMLU-Pro, -4.1%), suggesting that The Pile data contributes valuable diversity and that the model does benefit from the mixed training.

**Why this training setup?** The paper's design separates the rationale model from the reasoner. This is not incidental—it's a deliberate architectural choice with specific justifications. First, by training only on the rationale generation task, MRa can be optimized purely for producing useful heuristics without being distracted by also needing to generate correct answers. Second, the decoupling means MRa can be trained on diverse unlabeled data (web text) that contains reasoning patterns but not explicit question-answer pairs, dramatically expanding the available training signal. Third, at inference time, MRa can supervise any agent model—the approach is model-agnostic on the agent side. If the rationale generation were integrated into agent training (as in Quiet-Star), changing the agent model would require retraining the rationale component.

---

#### 3.4.4 Inference-Time Reasoning with RATIONALYST Supervision

The inference procedure, formalized in Algorithm 1 (Section 3.3), is where RATIONALYST's trained capability is deployed. The core idea is that the agent model generates reasoning step-by-step, but at each step, RATIONALYST provides a heuristic that helps select which of several candidate next steps is most promising.

**Setup.** Three models participate at inference time:
- **MAgent** — the agent model that generates candidate next reasoning steps. The paper uses LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct with temperature 0.7 and top-k sampling of 3 (Section 4.2): "We use LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct as our base agent model with temperature 0.7 and top-k sampling of 3." This means at each step, MAgent produces three candidate continuations of the reasoning trajectory.
- **MRa** — RATIONALYST, the trained rationale generation model, which produces an implicit rationale given the current reasoning trajectory.
- **M** — the probability estimation model (the same base LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct used for extraction and filtration), which scores how well each candidate next step follows from the rationale. This is the same model that was used during filtration to evaluate whether rationales help predict future text—creating a consistent evaluation criterion between training and inference.

**Step-by-step walkthrough of Algorithm 1:**

**Initialization (line 1):** The reasoning trajectory `$T$` is initialized to contain only the question text `$q$`. The trajectory will grow one reasoning step at a time.

**Iterative reasoning loop (lines 2–21):** The following sequence repeats until a stopping condition is met:

**Step 1 — Rationale generation (line 3):** MRa reads the current trajectory `$T$` (which at iteration 1 is just the question; in later iterations includes all previously selected reasoning steps) and generates an implicit rationale `$r$`. This rationale is generated autoregressively by MRa; the paper doesn't specify sampling parameters for MRa during inference, but the rationale generation is described as producing a single output (not multiple candidates).

**Step 2 — Candidate next-step generation (lines 5–9):** Two supervision methods are defined:

- **Implicit supervision (line 6):** MAgent generates `$N$` candidate next reasoning steps conditioned *only* on the trajectory `$T$`—the rationale `$r$` is *not* shown to the agent. The candidates are generated independently without access to the rationale. This is "implicit" because the rationale influences reasoning only indirectly through the scoring step, not by being part of the agent's input.

- **Explicit supervision (line 8):** MAgent generates `$N$` candidate next steps conditioned on both the trajectory `$T$` *and* the rationale `$r$`, by temporarily appending `$r$` to the trajectory before generation. The rationale becomes part of the agent's context, potentially steering its generation toward more coherent continuations.

The paper's experiments use `$N = 3$` (top-k sampling of 3), so three candidates are generated per step. The temperature of 0.7 provides a balance between diversity and coherence.

**Step 3 — Heuristic scoring (lines 10–18):** Each candidate next step is assigned a heuristic score:

- **Implicit supervision scoring (line 13):** For each candidate `$x$`, M estimates the probability of `$x$` given the trajectory `$T$` *and* the rationale `$r$`: `$h = \mathcal{M}(x \mid T, r)$`. This is the conditional probability (or its log) that M assigns to generating text `$x$` when shown `$T$` and `$r$` as context. Essentially, M is asked: "If you knew this rationale, how likely would you be to produce this next step?"

- **Explicit supervision scoring (line 15):** For each candidate `$x$`, we instead use MAgent's own probability of generating `$x$` given `$T$` and `$r$`: `$h = \mathcal{M}_{Agent}(x \mid T, r)$`. Since the candidate was generated with `$r$` in context (line 8), this score reflects how strongly the agent model itself prefers this continuation given the rationale.

**Step 4 — Selection (lines 19–20):** The candidate with the highest heuristic score (`$m\_idx \leftarrow \arg\max(heuristic\_list)$`) is selected, and the trajectory is extended: `$T \leftarrow T + next\_steps[m\_idx]$`.

**Step 5 — Loop termination (line 21):** The loop continues until the `stop_condition()` is met. This condition varies by dataset and is typically triggered when the trajectory contains a termination string like "The final answer is:" (as illustrated in the prompts in Appendix C, Figures 7–8). The paper states that this stopping cue "can be specified in system prompts for MAgent for different tasks."

**Output (line 22):** The complete reasoning trajectory `$T$` is returned, containing the question, all selected reasoning steps, and (presumably) the final answer.

**Computational cost (Section 5.6).** The paper reports that RATIONALYST "introduces minimal computational overhead during inference"—specifically, "within 5%" additional time compared to baseline inference, achieved through "async batched execution and short rationale generation." This is despite requiring two model calls per reasoning step (one for MRa to generate the rationale, one for MAgent to generate candidates). The memory overhead is more significant: "Memory requirements approximately double with LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct" since both MRa and MAgent are 8B-parameter models loaded simultaneously. However, the paper notes this becomes proportionally smaller if a larger agent model is used (e.g., LLaMA-3-70B as MAgent with the 8B RATIONALYST adding only a modest relative footprint).

**Why implicit supervision outperforms explicit (Section 5.3, Table 4).** The paper's ablation study reveals a counterintuitive finding: implicit supervision (where the rationale is used only for scoring, not for generation) outperforms explicit supervision (where the rationale is given directly to the agent). On GSM8K, implicit achieves 80.3% vs. 77.5% for explicit; on ECQA, 74.5% vs. 72.2%.

The paper's explanation (Section 5.3) is: "When RATIONALYST generates an imperfect rationale, the probability-based heuristic used in implicit supervision can still provide useful guidance even if the rationale itself is not ideal. This approach is less likely to lead MAgent to produce incorrect next steps. In contrast, explicit supervision directly incorporates potentially flawed rationales into the reasoning process, which can cause MAgent to produce incorrect next steps."

This insight reveals an important asymmetry: the probability estimation model M can extract partial signal from an imperfect rationale—it might assign high probability to correct next steps and lower probability to incorrect ones, even if the rationale text itself contains minor errors or vagueness, because M is evaluating the *compatibility* of the next step with the rationale's general direction, not executing the rationale literally. In contrast, explicit supervision treats the rationale as an instruction to be followed, so if the instruction is flawed, the agent faithfully generates a flawed continuation.

The paper characterizes implicit supervision as "a softer guide, allowing for some imperfection in rationales, while explicit supervision more strictly adheres to potentially flawed rationales, making it more susceptible to errors." This robustness to rationale imperfection is crucial because MRa's generated rationales—despite the 95% precision target on training data—will inevitably contain errors on some fraction of test instances.

#### 3.4.5 Design Choices and Their Justifications

The paper makes several non-obvious design decisions that collectively define the RATIONALYST approach. Here, we enumerate each major choice and the paper's stated or implied rationale.

**Design Choice 1: Decoupling rationale model from agent model.** RATIONALYST (MRa) is a separate model from the reasoning agent (MAgent). The alternative—integrating rationale generation into the reasoning model itself (as Quiet-Star does)—would couple the training of both capabilities. The paper's justification: "Our approach thus offers a novel perspective on leveraging latent information in language models to enhance their capabilities" and "This focus enables us to implement a simpler and more reliable method, as we don't need to directly integrate rationale extraction with 'reasoner' training." The practical benefit is modularity: MRa can be trained once on diverse data and then used to supervise any MAgent without retraining. The theoretical benefit is that MRa's training is focused entirely on rationale quality, with a clean signal (future-token prediction improvement) that doesn't require the rationale to improve final task accuracy—only to capture useful logical connections.

**Design Choice 2: Using the same model (LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct) for all roles.** The same base model is used for rationale extraction (Stage A/B), rationale filtration (Stage C), RATIONALYST training (MRa), agent reasoning (MAgent), and heuristic scoring (M) during inference. The paper acknowledges this explicitly: "since we use the same model (LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct) for rationale extraction, filtering, RATIONALYST training, and inference, our results do not leverage external knowledge from stronger models" (Section 5.1). The justification is not that this is optimal—the paper explicitly suggests future work could use stronger models for extraction—but that it provides a clean experimental baseline where performance improvements can be attributed to the method rather than to model scale or external knowledge transfer. Using the same model also ensures that the rationales extracted during training are compatible with the model's own internal representations, avoiding distribution shift between extraction and deployment.

**Design Choice 3: Exponential decay weighting in the filtration loss.** The weight `$w_{j-i}$` in the filtration criterion decays by a factor of 0.9 per step away from the rationale. The paper does not justify this specific decay rate but does explain the motivation: nearby tokens should be more influenced by the rationale than distant tokens. Without decay, a rationale that helps with the next few tokens but is irrelevant to tokens 100 steps away would have its signal diluted by the long irrelevant tail. The exponential form is a standard choice for temporal discounting and ensures that the filtration criterion primarily evaluates a rationale's immediate predictive value.

**Design Choice 4: Setting `$\tau_f = 0$` for The Pile, with dataset-specific thresholds for reasoning datasets.** The asymmetry in threshold selection—permissive for web data, strict for reasoning data—reflects a practical tradeoff. Web data is abundant and diverse; generating and filtering rationales from it is cheap, so a permissive filter that keeps any potentially useful rationale is acceptable, even if many retained rationales are mediocre. Reasoning datasets are smaller and more directly tied to the evaluation domains; precision matters more because these rationales provide the core supervision signal. The paper's annotation-based threshold calibration (95% precision target) for GSM8K and ECQA ensures that the reasoning-dataset rationales are high-quality, while the web data provides breadth.

**Design Choice 5: Extracting rationales at every sentence boundary.** This is a "dense" extraction strategy: every sentence boundary in a document is a candidate location for a rationale, regardless of whether there's an obvious reasoning gap. This over-generates heavily but is justified by the filtration step—most generated rationales will be filtered out if they don't help predict future text. The alternative (trying to identify only reasoning-rich boundaries) would require a separate classifier or heuristic, adding complexity. The dense generation + filtration approach is simpler and relies on a single quality signal (future-token predictability) rather than requiring two separate models (one for locating rationale positions, one for generating them).

**Design Choice 6: Explicitly excluding answers from extracted rationales in reasoning datasets.** The paper instructs M to not include the final answer in generated rationales. This is a deliberate anti-leakage measure: if rationales contained answers, MRa would learn to produce answers as "rationales," and the supervision signal would degenerate into answer-checking rather than reasoning guidance. By excluding answers, the rationales remain purely about reasoning strategy (what operation to do next, what to check, what relationship to consider), preserving their role as process supervisors rather than outcome verifiers.

**Design Choice 7: Training MRa to generate rationales from preceding context only, not from full documents.** During training, MRa sees only the text *before* the rationale insertion point, not the text that follows. This is essential because at inference time, MRa only has access to the reasoning trajectory generated so far—it cannot see future steps. If MRa were trained on full documents (seeing both preceding and following context), it would learn to rely on future information that isn't available at inference, creating a train-test mismatch. The paper's setup ensures that the training task (predict rationale from preceding context only) exactly matches the inference task.

**Design Choice 8: Using implicit supervision as the primary method.** As discussed in Section 3.4.4, implicit supervision's robustness to rationale errors justifies its selection over explicit supervision. The paper's experimental results (Table 4) validate this choice empirically: implicit outperforms explicit by 2.8 points on GSM8K and 2.3 points on ECQA. The mechanism (rationale influences selection via probability scoring rather than direct incorporation into generation) provides a form of graceful degradation: even when MRa produces a suboptimal rationale, the scoring model M can still extract useful signal, whereas explicit supervision would propagate the error directly into the agent's next step.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Implicit Rationale Problem as a Data Extraction Challenge Rather Than a Model Architecture Problem

Prior to this work, the dominant approaches to improving LLM reasoning completeness fell into two categories: (1) architecture-level interventions that modify how models generate reasoning (e.g., tree-of-thought search, self-consistency decoding) or (2) training interventions that require human-annotated supervision signals (e.g., process reward models with human-labeled step correctness). The implicit assumption across both categories was that the *model* was the primary locus of improvement—either through better generation strategies or through better training objectives.

RATIONALYST makes a fundamentally different move: it reframes the problem as one of **data extraction**. The core insight is that the missing logical steps already exist *latently* in the very pre-training data that causes models to skip them in the first place. Web text contains implicit rationales that human readers effortlessly infer; the challenge is not to design a model that invents these rationales from scratch, but to build a pipeline that *surfaces* what is already inferrable from the text. This is a conceptual shift from "how do we make models reason more completely?" to "how do we extract the complete reasoning that is already latent in the data the model was trained on?"

What makes this reframing significant is that it sidesteps the annotation bottleneck that limited prior process-supervision work. Lightman et al. (2023) showed that step-level supervision is powerful but required massive human annotation efforts. Wang et al. (2024a) automated step labeling using ground-truth answers, but this only works for domains with verifiable final answers (primarily math). RATIONALYST's data-extraction framing works for *any* domain where implicit reasoning exists in text—which is essentially all domains—because the supervision signal comes from the text's own predictability structure, not from human labels or ground-truth answers.

The paper doesn't merely claim this is possible; it provides an operational criterion for what counts as a useful rationale (the future-token perplexity reduction test from Section 3.1C) and demonstrates that this criterion discriminates effectively across diverse text sources. Table 1 shows that different Pile subdomains vary dramatically in rationale retention rates (StackExchange at 29.8% vs. Pile-CC at 2.9%), validating that the extraction process is genuinely identifying reasoning content rather than just generating plausible-sounding text. This is the empirical foundation that converts the reframing from a philosophical claim into a practical methodology.

The innovation here is **conceptual and methodological**, not architectural. RATIONALYST doesn't introduce a new type of model or a new training algorithm; it introduces a new way of thinking about where process supervision signals come from and how to harvest them at scale. This is a fundamental shift in perspective rather than an incremental improvement to existing techniques.

---

### Innovation 2: Decoupling Rationale Generation from Reasoner Training as a Principle for Scalable Process Supervision

The most closely related prior work, Quiet-Star (Zelikman et al., 2024), integrates rationale generation directly into reasoner training: the model learns to generate useful rationales as an auxiliary task optimized end-to-end alongside the primary reasoning objective. This coupling is intuitive—if rationales help reasoning, train them jointly—but it creates several tight constraints. The rationale model is tied to a specific reasoner architecture and training setup. The rationale quality signal is mediated through final task accuracy, which is sparse and potentially misaligned with interpretability (as the paper notes in Section 5.5, Quiet-Star can develop "shortcuts or non-intuitive patterns"). And the training data for rationales must come from the same distribution as the reasoning tasks, limiting the ability to leverage diverse unlabeled corpora.

RATIONALYST introduces **decoupling as a design principle**: train a dedicated rationale generation model (MRa) with its own objective (predicting extracted rationales from preceding context), and then use that model as a plug-and-play supervisor for any reasoning agent (MAgent) at inference time. This is not merely an implementation convenience—it is a conceptual innovation that separates two previously entangled concerns:

1. **What makes a good rationale?** (answered by the extraction and filtration pipeline, which uses future-token predictability as an intrinsic quality signal)
2. **How should rationales guide reasoning?** (answered by the inference-time heuristic scoring mechanism, which uses probability estimates rather than hard constraints)

The paper demonstrates the power of this decoupling in two concrete ways. First, the same RATIONALYST model supervises reasoning across seven diverse benchmarks spanning mathematical, commonsense, logical, and scientific reasoning (Table 2)—domains that differ substantially from each other and from the two reasoning datasets (GSM8K, ECQA) used for explicit rationale extraction. This cross-domain generalization would be difficult or impossible if the rationale model were optimized end-to-end for a specific task. Second, the implicit supervision mechanism (Table 4) shows that MRa's rationales can provide useful guidance *even when they are imperfect*, because the probability-based heuristic can extract partial signal without forcing the agent to follow a flawed rationale literally. A coupled system would propagate rationale errors directly into reasoning errors.

The significance of this decoupling extends beyond RATIONALYST itself. It suggests a broader architectural pattern for LLM systems: specialized "supervisor" models trained on self-supervised signals from unlabeled data can guide general-purpose "agent" models without requiring joint training. This is analogous to how discriminator-generator separation in GANs or actor-critic separation in RL each proved to be more flexible and scalable than monolithic approaches. Whether this pattern generalizes to other supervision tasks (factual verification, constraint checking, stylistic guidance) is an open question that RATIONALYST's results motivate.

This is a **methodological innovation** rather than a theoretical one, but it is fundamental rather than incremental: it establishes a new design paradigm for process supervision that breaks the coupling constraint that prior work (both Quiet-Star and human-annotated process reward models) implicitly accepted.

---

### Innovation 3: The Filtration Criterion as a Domain-Agnostic Quality Signal That Replaces Human Annotation

Human annotation is the bottleneck for process supervision. Lightman et al. (2023) required human labelers to judge the correctness of every intermediate step in mathematical solutions—a process that is expensive, slow, and domain-specific (labelers need sufficient expertise to evaluate reasoning in the target domain). Subsequent work (Wang et al., 2024a; Luo et al., 2024) automated step labeling by using ground-truth final answers to infer step correctness, but this requires that final answers exist and are verifiable—which limits applicability to domains like mathematics and code where correctness is well-defined.

RATIONALYST's filtration criterion—retain a rationale if and only if it reduces the model's perplexity on subsequent text—is a **domain-agnostic replacement for human annotation**. It requires no human labels, no ground-truth answers, and no domain-specific expertise. It works on any text where the language model can estimate token probabilities. The paper applies it successfully to web text spanning StackExchange discussions, GitHub code, biomedical literature (PubMed Central), news articles, and Wikipedia—none of which have ground-truth reasoning labels.

What makes this criterion intellectually distinctive is not its mathematical form (cross-entropy difference with exponential decay) but its **operational definition of "useful"** : a rationale is useful if it makes the future more predictable to the same model that will later use it for supervision. This creates a closed loop: the model's own predictive uncertainty defines what counts as a useful rationale, and those rationales are then used to train a supervisor that reduces uncertainty during reasoning. The signal is self-consistent—it doesn't depend on external correctness judgments—yet the paper shows it produces rationales that are genuinely helpful for downstream task accuracy (Table 2, average +3.9% across benchmarks).

The empirical evidence for the criterion's effectiveness comes from the threshold calibration experiment (Section 4.1): by setting thresholds to achieve 95% precision on a small set of 100 manually annotated examples from GSM8K and ECQA, the paper establishes that the automated filtration criterion correlates strongly with human judgments of rationale quality. This is a crucial validation—it shows that the self-supervised signal is not just statistically convenient but genuinely aligned with what humans consider a "good" rationale. The fact that different datasets require different thresholds (τf = 1.2 for GSM8K vs. 0.5 for ECQA) reveals that the criterion is sensitive to domain differences in how much implicit reasoning is typically present, which is a feature, not a bug—it adapts to the data rather than imposing a uniform standard.

This innovation is **conceptually fundamental**: it provides a principled, scalable, domain-agnostic answer to the question "what makes a reasoning step worth supervising?" that prior work either answered with expensive human judgment or avoided entirely by focusing only on final-answer verification.

---

### Innovation 4: Empirical Demonstration That Web-Scale Unlabeled Data Contains Transferable Reasoning Supervision

The paper's ablation study (Section 5.2, Table 3) comparing RATIONALYST trained with and without The Pile rationales reveals a finding that is obvious in retrospect but was not obvious before this work: **web-scale unlabeled text contains reasoning patterns that transfer across domains, and incorporating them into a process supervisor improves reasoning on tasks the web text was never explicitly about**. Removing the 65,000 Pile rationales from training causes a consistent accuracy drop across all seven benchmarks, with the largest drop on MMLU-Pro (−4.1%), a multi-task benchmark covering diverse reasoning types. This is despite the Pile rationales being extracted from documents that were selected only by semantic similarity to general reasoning datasets, not by any task-specific filtering.

This finding matters because it challenges an implicit assumption in much prior work: that supervision signals for reasoning must come from data that is *about* the target reasoning task. Outcome-based verifiers are trained on the same task they evaluate (Cobbe et al., 2021). Process reward models are trained on step-labeled solutions to the target task (Lightman et al., 2023). Even Quiet-Star, which extracts rationales from unlabeled data, evaluates those rationales based on their contribution to task-specific accuracy. The assumption is that reasoning supervision is task-bound—what helps with math reasoning might not help with commonsense reasoning, and vice versa.

RATIONALYST's results suggest a different picture: there exist *general reasoning patterns*—planning what to do next, checking whether intermediate results make sense, identifying relationships between concepts, recognizing when a conclusion is ready—that are domain-agnostic and are latent in diverse text sources. A rationale extracted from a StackExchange physics discussion ("First solve the equations to find j, then substitute j into g(b) to calculate g(j)") might teach the model a planning pattern that is useful for mathematical reasoning, commonsense QA, and scientific problem-solving alike. The Pile rationales provide breadth—exposure to many reasoning patterns in many contexts—that complements the depth provided by the domain-specific GSM8K and ECQA rationales.

The evidence is circumstantial but consistent: every benchmark improves with the addition of web-scale rationales (Table 3), and the improvement is largest on MMLU-Pro, which is the most diverse benchmark in terms of reasoning types. This is exactly what you would expect if the web data provides general reasoning patterns that are particularly valuable when the task distribution is broad. However, the paper does not provide a mechanistic analysis of *which* Pile rationales transfer to *which* target tasks, so the claim of domain-agnostic transfer remains at the level of aggregate results rather than fine-grained attribution.

This innovation is **empirically significant** rather than theoretically novel: it provides concrete evidence for a claim (web data contains transferable reasoning supervision) that was previously plausible but unsubstantiated, and it quantifies the benefit of including such data in a process supervision pipeline. It also opens a clear scaling path—larger web datasets with more sophisticated pre-filtering could extract even more diverse reasoning patterns, potentially improving cross-domain generalization further.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on seven benchmarks spanning four reasoning types: GSM8K (1,319 test examples, math), MATH (5,000 test examples, math), ECQA (17,944 validation examples, commonsense), HellaSwag (10,000 test examples, commonsense), ProofWriter (600 examples with proof depth > 5, logical), ARC-Challenge (1,172 test examples, scientific), and MMLU-Pro (12,000 test examples, mixed). GSM8K and ECQA are also used as sources for extracting training rationales; all other datasets are held-out evaluation only. Appendix A (Table 6) provides the full configuration.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct (MetaAI, 2024) as the base model. The same model serves in five distinct roles: rationale extraction (M), rationale filtration (M), RATIONALYST fine-tuning (MRa), agent reasoning during inference (MAgent), and heuristic scoring during inference (M). The paper justifies this choice by noting it "provides a clean experimental baseline where performance improvements can be attributed to the method rather than to model scale or external knowledge transfer" (Section 5.1). For the verifier comparison, GPT-4 is also used as a process-supervision baseline.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **exact match accuracy** (%) between the model's final answer and the ground-truth answer. For mathematical reasoning tasks, answers are extracted from the reasoning trajectory (typically after "The final answer is:"). For multiple-choice tasks, the predicted choice letter is compared to the correct choice. Results are reported as absolute accuracy and absolute improvement (∆Acc.) over the no-verifier baseline.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five distinct baselines: (1) **No verifier** — MAgent with chain-of-thought prompting but no supervision or reranking (the primary baseline in Table 2); (2) **Process supervision w/ LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct** — the same base model prompted to directly rerank partial reasoning trajectories (Table 5, Appendix E, Figure 10 for prompts); (3) **Process supervision w/ GPT-4** — a much larger general-purpose model also prompted for step-level reranking (Table 5); (4) **Outcome supervision w/ LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct + fine-tuning** — outcome-based verifiers fine-tuned on the training sets of each reasoning dataset following Cobbe et al. (2021), evaluating correctness of the final answer from the question and complete solution (Table 5); (5) **RATIONALYST w/o Pile** — an ablation baseline trained only on rationales from GSM8K and ECQA, without web-scale data (Table 3).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For each reasoning step, MAgent generates N = 3 candidate next steps using top-k sampling at temperature 0.7. RATIONALYST generates one rationale per step. The paper reports inference overhead of "within 5%" additional time versus baseline, achieved through async batched execution and short rationale generation (Section 5.6). Memory requirements approximately double when both MRa and MAgent are 8B-parameter models, though the paper notes this becomes proportionally smaller with larger agent models. No FLOPs-matched comparisons or generation budget sweeps are conducted across different N values—the compute analysis focuses on per-step overhead rather than scaling behavior.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests for any results. All accuracy numbers are reported as point estimates. For the filtration threshold calibration on GSM8K and ECQA, 100 rationale pairs (50 positive, 50 negative) were manually annotated to set thresholds achieving 95% precision (Section 4.1). The post-training validation involved "manual annotation of the model's output" confirming that rationale accuracy on unseen test data "closely matches the filtration accuracy we specified for training data" (Section 4.1). No cross-validation or multiple random seeds are reported for the main accuracy experiments.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance Across Seven Benchmarks (Table 2)

The headline result is that RATIONALYST (trained on all 79k rationales from GSM8K, ECQA, and The Pile, using implicit supervision) improves accuracy over the no-verifier baseline across all seven benchmarks, with an average improvement of 3.9 percentage points. The per-benchmark breakdown from Table 2:

| Dataset | Baseline Acc. | RATIONALYST Acc. | ∆Acc. |
|---|---|---|---|
| GSM8K | 77.6 | 81.6 | +4.0 |
| MATH | 28.0 | 32.5 | +4.5 |
| ECQA | 72.6 | 75.2 | +2.6 |
| HellaSwag | 58.2 | 60.3 | +2.1 |
| ProofWriter | 86.4 | 90.7 | +4.3 |
| ARC-Challenge | 77.6 | 80.7 | +3.1 |
| MMLU-Pro | 39.6 | 45.3 | +5.7 |

Several patterns in these results merit attention. First, the **largest absolute gain is on MMLU-Pro (+5.7)**, the most diverse benchmark in terms of reasoning types. This is consistent with the paper's claim that web-scale rationales provide broad, transferable reasoning patterns—when the evaluation spans many reasoning domains, the benefit of diverse training data is most pronounced. Second, the **largest relative gain is on MATH (+4.5 points on a 28.0 baseline, representing a 16% relative improvement)**, suggesting that step-level process supervision is particularly valuable for complex multi-step mathematical reasoning where intermediate errors are common and consequential. Third, the **smallest gains are on commonsense reasoning benchmarks** (ECQA +2.6, HellaSwag +2.1), which may reflect that commonsense reasoning involves fewer explicit intermediate steps that can benefit from rationale supervision, or that the baseline model already handles these tasks reasonably well.

The paper emphasizes that these improvements are achieved "without leveraging external knowledge from stronger models like LLaMa-3-70B-Instruct or GPT-4" (Section 5.1), since the same LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct is used for all roles. This is a deliberate baseline choice that isolates the method's contribution from model scale effects.

#### Benefit of Web-Scale Rationale Data: Ablation Study (Table 3)

The ablation comparing RATIONALYST (with Pile) against RATIONALYST (w/o Pile) reveals that removing the 65,000 web-scale rationales from training causes a consistent accuracy drop across all seven benchmarks. The results from Table 3:

| Dataset | With Pile | Without Pile | ∆Acc. |
|---|---|---|---|
| GSM8K | 81.6 | 80.3 | −1.3 |
| MATH | 32.5 | 31.4 | −1.1 |
| ECQA | 75.2 | 74.5 | −0.7 |
| HellaSwag | 60.3 | 59.1 | −1.2 |
| ProofWriter | 90.7 | 88.2 | −2.5 |
| ARC-Challenge | 80.7 | 78.8 | −1.9 |
| MMLU-Pro | 45.3 | 41.2 | −4.1 |

The **drop is largest on MMLU-Pro (−4.1)**, which the paper attributes to MMLU-Pro's diverse reasoning types benefiting most from the diverse reasoning patterns in web data (Section 5.2: "Web-scale data likely provides exposure to more diverse reasoning types and content, including specialized knowledge, complex real-world scenarios, and interdisciplinary connections not present in the more focused datasets"). The drop on ProofWriter (−2.5) is notably large for a logical reasoning task; the paper does not specifically analyze this result, but it suggests that The Pile contains logical reasoning patterns (possibly from sources like StackExchange, GitHub, or PubMed Central) that transfer to formal deductive reasoning despite the domain mismatch.

A critical nuance: even without The Pile rationales, RATIONALYST (w/o Pile) still outperforms the no-verifier baseline on all benchmarks. For example, on GSM8K, RATIONALYST w/o Pile achieves 80.3% versus the baseline of 77.6%, and on MMLU-Pro it achieves 41.2% versus 39.6%. This means that the approximately 14,000 rationales from GSM8K and ECQA alone provide meaningful supervision. The Pile rationales provide an additional boost on top of this, but are not the sole source of the method's effectiveness.

#### Implicit vs. Explicit Supervision (Table 4)

The ablation comparing supervision methods on GSM8K and ECQA (using RATIONALYST trained only on the respective dataset's rationales, to isolate the supervision effect) shows:

| Supervision Method | GSM8K | ECQA |
|---|---|---|
| Implicit | 80.3 | 74.5 |
| Explicit | 77.5 | 72.2 |

Implicit supervision outperforms explicit by 2.8 points on GSM8K and 2.3 points on ECQA. The paper's explanation (Section 5.3) attributes this to implicit supervision's greater robustness to rationale errors: when MRa generates an imperfect rationale, the probability-based heuristic can "still provide useful guidance even if the rationale itself is not ideal," whereas explicit supervision "directly incorporates potentially flawed rationales into the reasoning process, which can cause MAgent to produce incorrect next steps."

Notably, even explicit supervision (at 77.5 on GSM8K) slightly underperforms the no-verifier baseline (77.6), though the paper does not highlight this. The explicit supervision number on GSM8K (77.5) is within rounding error of the baseline (77.6), which could mean explicit supervision provides no benefit over unguided generation—or could even be slightly harmful—when rationales are imperfect. The paper's framing focuses on implicit versus explicit, not explicit versus baseline, so this comparison is left implicit in the reported numbers.

#### Comparison Against Other Verifiers (Table 5)

RATIONALYST is compared against three alternative supervision approaches on GSM8K and ECQA. Results from Table 5:

| Supervision | GSM8K | ECQA |
|---|---|---|
| No verifier (baseline) | 77.6 | 72.6 |
| Process Supervision w/ LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct | 77.4 | 71.5 |
| Process Supervision w/ GPT-4 | 80.0 | 74.7 |
| Outcome Supervision w/ LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct + FT | 79.2 | 74.3 |
| RATIONALYST w/o Pile | 80.3 | 74.5 |
| RATIONALYST (full) | 81.6 | 76.2 |

Several notable observations from these results:

**Vanilla LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct performs poorly as a process supervisor.** Directly prompting the base model to rerank partial reasoning trajectories actually *reduces* accuracy on ECQA (71.5 vs. 72.6 baseline) and is essentially flat on GSM8K (77.4 vs. 77.6). The paper attributes this to the well-known difficulty LLMs have in evaluating their own outputs (Section 5.4: "LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct faces difficulties in reranking partial reasoning steps. This challenge likely stems from the model's struggle to differentiate among its own generated outputs, a phenomenon observed in recent studies").

**GPT-4 provides strong process supervision but is matched or exceeded by a fine-tuned 8B model.** GPT-4 achieves 80.0 on GSM8K and 74.7 on ECQA, representing a meaningful improvement over the baseline. However, RATIONALYST (full) outperforms GPT-4 by 1.6 points on GSM8K (81.6 vs. 80.0) and by 1.5 points on ECQA (76.2 vs. 74.7). Even RATIONALYST w/o Pile slightly edges out GPT-4 on GSM8K (80.3 vs. 80.0) and is competitive on ECQA (74.5 vs. 74.7). The paper hypothesizes this advantage "arises from RATIONALYST's specialized design for providing supervision, in contrast to GPT-4's general-purpose training."

**Outcome supervision with matching training data is competitive but inferior to process supervision.** The outcome-based verifier fine-tuned on each dataset's training set achieves 79.2 on GSM8K and 74.3 on ECQA—respectable numbers but below RATIONALYST (full) by 2.4 and 1.9 points respectively. The paper attributes this to "the richer feedback provided by process-based supervision compared to outcome-based approaches," but it's worth noting that the outcome verifier was trained only on final answer correctness, not on intermediate steps, so it cannot detect reasoning errors that coincidentally produce the right answer.

**The gap between RATIONALYST and baselines is larger on ECQA (commonsense) than on GSM8K (math).** This is somewhat counterintuitive given that GSM8K was used for rationale extraction training, while ECQA rationales are directly from the target domain. The larger gap on GSM8K for the no-verifier baseline (81.6 vs. 77.6, gap of 4.0) compared to ECQA (76.2 vs. 72.6, gap of 3.6) is consistent with the Table 2 results. This may reflect that mathematical reasoning benefits more from explicit step-level guidance than commonsense reasoning does, or that the baseline model is simply weaker on math (lower absolute accuracy means more room for improvement).

#### Rationale Quality and Interpretability Assessment (Section 5.5)

The paper provides a qualitative comparison of RATIONALYST's generated rationales against those from Quiet-Star (Zelikman et al., 2024) on MATH test set problems (which were not part of RATIONALYST's training data). The key claim: "our method produces more human-understandable rationales." The paper attributes this to Quiet-Star's optimization objective: "Quiet-Star optimizes rationales during training using the accuracy of the final prediction as a reward. This approach, while effective for improving task performance, does not explicitly prioritize human interpretability. In addition, this approach might inadvertently develop shortcuts or non-intuitive patterns that optimize for accuracy but not necessarily for clarity or human understanding."

An example rationale from Appendix D (Figure 9) for a MATH algebra problem shows the style of RATIONALYST's output: for the step where the second equation needs to be rewritten, the rationale is "Let's first rewrite the second equation to isolate y in terms of x," followed by "Substitute the expression for y into the first equation," then "We should simplify the equation and solve for x," and so on. These rationales read as planning annotations—they articulate the next logical operation without performing the calculation itself—which aligns with the paper's stated goal of providing interpretable process supervision.

However, this comparison is qualitative only. The paper does not report a systematic human evaluation of rationale quality, interpretability ratings, or any quantitative metric for comparing RATIONALYST's rationales against Quiet-Star's or any other baseline. The claim of superior interpretability rests on the authors' manual observation and the design intuition that decoupling rationale training from task-accuracy optimization avoids shortcut learning. This is a limitation of the current analysis—the interpretability benefit is asserted rather than measured.

#### Computational Overhead Analysis (Section 5.6)

The paper reports that RATIONALYST introduces "minimal time overhead (within 5%)" compared to baseline inference, despite requiring two model calls per reasoning step (one for MRa rationale generation, one for MAgent candidate generation). This is achieved through "async batched execution and short rationale generation." The rationales are described as "typically quite short," which explains the low time overhead—the additional model call produces brief text rather than full reasoning steps.

Memory requirements "approximately double with LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct" since both MRa and MAgent are 8B-parameter models loaded simultaneously. The paper notes this overhead "becomes proportionally smaller with larger agent models (e.g., when using LLaMA-3-70B, RATIONALYST's additional 8B parameters represent a much smaller relative increase)."

These overhead numbers are self-reported without detailed breakdown. The paper does not provide ablation on rationale length, batch size effects, or scaling of overhead with the number of reasoning steps. The 5% time overhead claim is presented without controlled timing experiments or comparison to alternative process supervision methods.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Web-scale data contribution (Table 3):** Removing the 65,000 Pile rationales from training causes accuracy drops ranging from −0.7 (ECQA) to −4.1 (MMLU-Pro) across all seven benchmarks, with an average drop of approximately −1.8 points. The largest drop on MMLU-Pro (−4.1) is more than 3× the smallest drop on ECQA (−0.7), suggesting the web data's benefit is most pronounced for tasks that are least similar to the explicit reasoning training data. The paper does not ablate individual Pile subdomains (e.g., StackExchange-only, GitHub-only), so the source of the transfer benefit cannot be attributed to specific data types.

- **Implicit vs. explicit supervision (Table 4):** Implicit supervision outperforms explicit by 2.8 points on GSM8K and 2.3 points on ECQA. The paper's error analysis attributes this to explicit supervision's vulnerability to rationale errors: "explicit supervision directly incorporates potentially flawed rationales into the reasoning process, which can cause MAgent to produce incorrect next steps." This finding is robust across both mathematical and commonsense reasoning domains, suggesting it is a general property of the supervision mechanism rather than domain-specific. However, the paper does not report the rate of rationale errors during inference (only that post-training manual annotation showed rationale accuracy "closely matches the filtration accuracy"), so the magnitude of the error-propagation problem in explicit supervision is not quantified.

- **Filtration threshold sensitivity (Section 4.1, Table 1):** The paper sets dataset-specific filtration thresholds based on manual annotation of 100 rationale pairs per reasoning dataset, targeting 95% precision. The resulting thresholds differ substantially: τf = 1.2 for GSM8K, 0.5 for ECQA, and 0 for all Pile subdomains. The paper does not ablate over threshold values to show how sensitive final task performance is to threshold choice. Given that the threshold determines both the quantity and quality of training data (GSM8K retains only 19.5% of generated rationales at τf = 1.2, while ECQA retains 57.6% at τf = 0.5), this is a significant unexamined hyperparameter. It is unknown whether a stricter threshold on ECQA (producing fewer but higher-quality rationales) or a more permissive threshold on GSM8K (producing more rationales at lower precision) would improve or degrade final performance.

- **Data source mixture (implicit in Tables 2–3):** The paper trains RATIONALYST on a mixture of 14,000 reasoning-dataset rationales and 65,000 Pile rationales, giving roughly 4.6:1 ratio of web to reasoning data. The paper does not ablate this ratio—for instance, upweighting the reasoning-dataset rationales to match the Pile count, or using Pile-only training, or varying the ratio to find an optimal mix. The large drop on MMLU-Pro when Pile data is removed (−4.1) suggests the mixture ratio matters, but its optimality is not investigated.

- **Number of candidate next steps (N = 3):** All experiments use N = 3 candidate next steps per reasoning turn. The paper does not ablate N (e.g., comparing N = 1, 3, 5, 10) to determine how the benefit of rationale supervision scales with the number of candidates. With N = 1, the implicit supervision mechanism would reduce to standard chain-of-thought generation (no reranking), so RATIONALYST's benefit presumably depends on N > 1. With larger N, the heuristic scorer has more candidates to choose from, potentially increasing the ceiling on improvement but also increasing computational cost. This tradeoff is unexplored.

- **Temperature sensitivity:** The agent model uses temperature 0.7 for candidate generation. The paper does not ablate temperature (e.g., 0.0 for greedy, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0) to determine whether the diversity introduced by higher temperature is beneficial (more varied candidates for the heuristic to choose from) or harmful (lower-quality candidates that are harder to discriminate). This is particularly relevant for mathematical reasoning tasks where deterministic generation (temperature 0) is common in prior work.

- **Model scale and family robustness:** All experiments use LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct. The paper does not test whether RATIONALYST's benefits generalize to other model families (e.g., Mistral, Qwen) or to different scales (e.g., LLaMa-3-70B). Since the same model serves all roles—extraction, filtration, training, agent reasoning, and scoring—it's unclear whether the method would work with a different base model, or whether performance depends on the internal representational alignment between the rationale model and the agent model.

- **Complementary methods integration (Appendix H):** The paper briefly reports that RATIONALYST can be combined with self-consistency (64 generations) to achieve further improvements on GSM8K and MATH, but does not provide specific accuracy numbers in the main text. Appendix H mentions this qualitatively without a table of results. This limits the ability to assess whether RATIONALYST's benefits are additive with or subsumed by other test-time compute methods.

- **Negative result with reasoning dataset training sets:** The paper states that "while M extracts rationales from the training sets of reasoning datasets, these training sets are not directly used as targets when training MRa. Instead, the target of training is the rationale generated by M" (Section 3.2). This creates an interesting implicit ablation: the model is trained on extracted rationales, not on the original reasoning solutions. The paper does not compare this against directly training MRa on the original GSM8K/ECQA training solutions (e.g., learning to generate the next reasoning step rather than an implicit rationale). This comparison would directly test the paper's central claim that implicit rationales provide supervision beyond what the original solutions provide.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does RATIONALYST improve reasoning accuracy across diverse tasks? (central claim from Executive Summary)

The evidence from Table 2 supports this claim with qualifications. RATIONALYST improves accuracy on all seven benchmarks, with gains ranging from +2.1 to +5.7 percentage points. The benchmarks span mathematical, commonsense, logical, scientific, and mixed reasoning, establishing cross-domain generalization. However, several aspects of the evaluation limit the strength of this conclusion:

**The absolute accuracy levels vary dramatically across benchmarks.** On ProofWriter (logical reasoning), the baseline achieves 86.4% and RATIONALYST reaches 90.7%—both high, with the +4.3 gain representing improvement on an already-strong baseline. On MATH, the baseline achieves only 28.0% and RATIONALYST reaches 32.5%—the +4.5 gain is meaningful but leaves the majority of problems unsolved. The paper does not analyze whether the gain is concentrated in certain difficulty levels or problem types within each benchmark, which matters because a +4.5 gain on MATH could mean improving from 0% to 10% on hard problems (large relative gain but still poor absolute performance) or from 80% to 90% on easy problems (incremental gain on already-solved problems).

**No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported.** All numbers are point estimates without variance information. With test sets ranging from 600 (ProofWriter) to 17,944 (ECQA) examples, the precision of these estimates varies substantially. A +2.1 gain on HellaSwag (10,000 examples) is likely statistically significant, but a +4.3 gain on ProofWriter (600 examples) could fall within sampling error. Without variance estimates, the reader cannot assess which gains are reliable and which might be noise.

**The baseline model uses few-shot prompting but the number of shots varies by dataset** (from 2 to 8, as shown in Appendix A Table 6). The paper does not control for this variation, and it's possible that the benefit of RATIONALYST interacts with the number of shots—for instance, if more shots already provide richer reasoning demonstrations, the marginal benefit of rationale supervision might be smaller. The variation in baseline accuracy across benchmarks (from 28.0 on MATH to 86.4 on ProofWriter) makes direct comparison of ∆Acc. values across benchmarks potentially misleading, since improvement is bounded by the ceiling (100 − baseline).

#### Does RATIONALYST match or exceed GPT-4's process supervision performance? (central claim)

The evidence from Table 5 supports this claim specifically for GSM8K and ECQA. RATIONALYST (full) outperforms GPT-4 by +1.6 on GSM8K (81.6 vs. 80.0) and by +1.5 on ECQA (76.2 vs. 74.7). This is a genuinely impressive result: an 8B-parameter fine-tuned model matching or exceeding a much larger general-purpose model on process supervision, using only self-supervised training data from the same base model family.

However, this comparison has important scope limitations:

**The comparison is only on two benchmarks.** The paper evaluates GPT-4 process supervision on GSM8K and ECQA, but not on MATH, ARC-Challenge, ProofWriter, HellaSwag, or MMLU-Pro. The claim that RATIONALYST "demonstrates superior performance compared to significantly larger verifiers like GPT-4" (Abstract) is therefore only directly supported on the two datasets where GPT-4 was tested. On the other five benchmarks, we don't know whether GPT-4 process supervision would outperform RATIONALYST or vice versa.

**GPT-4 is used via prompting, not fine-tuning.** The comparison is between a fine-tuned small model (RATIONALYST) and a prompted large model (GPT-4). This is a reasonable comparison for practical deployment (fine-tuning is available for open models but not for API-only models), but it conflates two differences: model architecture/scale and training methodology. A fine-tuned GPT-4 (if available) might outperform RATIONALYST by a larger margin. The paper's claim is about "performance" in an absolute sense, but the comparison setup favors RATIONALYST by giving it task-specific training while GPT-4 gets only few-shot prompting.

**The outcome-based verifier fine-tuned on matching data is a strong but imperfect baseline.** At 79.2 on GSM8K, the outcome verifier is competitive with GPT-4 (80.0) and RATIONALYST w/o Pile (80.3), but below RATIONALYST full (81.6). This comparison supports the value of process supervision over outcome supervision, but the outcome verifier was trained on final answer correctness, not on step-level supervision—so it's not a process-vs-outcome comparison at equal training data scales. A process-based verifier fine-tuned directly on the GSM8K training solutions (without rationale extraction) would be a stronger baseline for isolating the contribution of implicit rationales.

#### Does the web-scale data (The Pile) provide transferable reasoning supervision? (central claim)

The evidence from Table 3 supports this claim with consistent results across all benchmarks. Removing Pile rationales causes accuracy drops on every benchmark, with the largest drop on the most diverse benchmark (MMLU-Pro, −4.1). This pattern is consistent with the claim that web data provides general reasoning patterns that transfer across domains.

However, several aspects weaken this conclusion:

**The Pile rationales are extracted using a model fine-tuned on the same base architecture.** LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct is used for extraction, so the rationales are "in-distribution" for the model in a way they might not be for a different architecture. The transfer claim might be specific to LLaMa-family models.

**The semantic pre-filtering step (cosine similarity > 0.3 to reasoning dataset embeddings) means the selected Pile documents are already reasoning-biased.** This is not a random sample of web text—it's web text that looks similar to reasoning datasets. The extracted rationales therefore come from a distribution that is already closer to the evaluation tasks than random web text would be. The claim that "web-scale unlabeled text contains transferable reasoning supervision" should be qualified: it's web text that has been filtered to select reasoning-rich content. Whether random web text (without pre-filtering) would provide similar benefits is untested.

**The ablation compares "with Pile" vs. "without Pile," but the without-Pile model still has 14,000 rationales from GSM8K and ECQA.** The Pile contributes both additional data volume (65,000 vs. 14,000 rationales) and data diversity (multiple domains vs. two). The ablation cannot distinguish whether the benefit comes from more data or more diverse data. A comparison against training on 79,000 rationales *all from GSM8K/ECQA* (by generating more rationales from those datasets) would isolate the diversity effect from the volume effect. This experiment is not conducted.

**The mechanism of transfer is not analyzed.** The paper does not identify which types of Pile rationales help with which target tasks, or whether certain Pile subdomains are more valuable than others. The per-subdomain retention rates in Table 1 (StackExchange at 29.8% vs. GitHub at 2.6%) hint at large differences in rationale density, but the paper doesn't ablate by subdomain to determine whether StackExchange rationales are disproportionately responsible for the transfer benefit.

#### Does implicit supervision work better than explicit supervision? (method claim)

The evidence from Table 4 supports this claim on GSM8K and ECQA with clear margins (+2.8 and +2.3 points). The paper's error analysis provides a plausible mechanism (implicit supervision is more robust to rationale errors), and this mechanism is consistent with the design intuition behind the probability-based heuristic scoring. However, the paper does not quantify how often MRa generates imperfect rationales during inference, so the claimed mechanism—while plausible—is not directly verified. If MRa generates correct rationales 95% of the time (as the training filtration precision suggests), then the benefit of robustness to 5% error rate would need to be substantial to produce a 2.8-point gap, which seems possible but is not analyzed quantitatively.

#### Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper

Several experiments that are natural within the paper's framework are not conducted:

**Ablation of the number of rationale generation model calls.** RATIONALYST generates one rationale per reasoning step. What happens if MRa is not called at all (pure MAgent chain-of-thought), or if it's called multiple times with different random seeds (ensemble of rationales)? The benefit of rationale supervision versus no supervision is established, but the marginal benefit of each additional rationale is not.

**Ablation of the heuristic scoring model.** The same LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct is used for all roles. What happens if a weaker or stronger model is used for heuristic scoring M, while keeping MRa and MAgent fixed? This would isolate whether the scoring step is doing meaningful work or whether most of the benefit comes from MRa's rationales regardless of the scoring mechanism.

**Comparison to training MAgent directly on the extracted rationales.** Instead of using MRa as a separate supervisor at inference time, the paper could fine-tune MAgent itself on the extracted rationales (training it to generate more complete reasoning that includes the implicit steps). This would test whether decoupling (the paper's claimed innovation) is actually necessary or whether simpler approaches achieve similar gains.

**Human evaluation of rationale quality.** The paper claims RATIONALYST produces "human-understandable rationales" (Section 5.5) and that this is an advantage over Quiet-Star. But no human evaluation is conducted. A simple study asking annotators to rate the helpfulness, correctness, or clarity of rationales from RATIONALYST vs. Quiet-Star vs. a baseline would substantiate this claim. Without such evaluation, the interpretability benefit is an untested assertion.

**Scaling behavior with model size.** All experiments use 8B-parameter models. Does RATIONALYST's benefit increase, decrease, or stay constant with larger agent models? If the benefit diminishes with model scale (because larger models already produce more complete reasoning), the practical value of the approach would be limited to smaller models. If it scales proportionally, it would remain valuable even for frontier models.

**Cross-model-family generalization.** The paper trains MRa on LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct and uses it to supervise LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct as MAgent. Would RATIONALYST trained on LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct effectively supervise a Mistral-based or Qwen-based agent? This tests the claimed plug-and-play property of the decoupled architecture.

These gaps don't invalidate the paper's claims, but they mean several of the claimed benefits—interpretability, cross-model generalization, scaling behavior—are asserted based on design intuition rather than demonstrated experimentally. The core quantitative results (accuracy improvements on seven benchmarks, benefit of web data, implicit vs. explicit supervision) are well-supported within the paper's specific setup (LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct, implicit supervision, N=3 candidates), but the generality of these findings beyond that setup remains an open question.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: The Filtration Criterion Is Operationalised Using Only a Single Model, Creating a Tight Self-Consistency Loop with Unknown Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire quality-control mechanism for rationale extraction—the filtration criterion that determines which rationales are retained for training—operates using a single model, LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct, in a closed loop. The same model generates candidate rationales (as M), evaluates their usefulness via future-token perplexity reduction (as M), serves as the base for fine-tuning MRa, and later provides heuristic scoring during inference (as M). The paper is explicit about this uniformity, stating that "since we use the same model (LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct) for rationale extraction, filtering, RATIONALYST training, and inference, our results do not leverage external knowledge from stronger models" (Section 5.1).

**The consequence.** This design creates a self-consistency loop: a rationale is deemed "useful" if it helps *this specific model* predict future text more easily. But what is predictable to LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct may not align with what is genuinely good reasoning. The model could systematically prefer rationales that exploit its own inductive biases—patterns it finds easy to condition on—rather than rationales that capture sound inferential logic. This is a form of **reward hacking in the filtration signal**: the model is rewarded for generating rationales that make its own predictions easier, which may select for rationales that are *self-reinforcing* rather than *objectively corrective*. If the model has systematic blind spots (e.g., it consistently fails to attend to certain logical relations), rationales that would expose those blind spots may *increase* perplexity—because they direct attention to relationships the model doesn't model well—and would be filtered out, even though they represent exactly the kind of reasoning gap RATIONALYST is designed to fill.

A more concrete failure mode: if the base model has poor calibration on certain types of text (e.g., it assigns high probability to plausible-but-wrong mathematical reasoning), the filtration criterion might retain rationales that reinforce those miscalibrated patterns because they make the model's own (miscalibrated) predictions more confident. The extracted rationales would then train MRa to produce heuristics that guide reasoning toward the model's *existing tendencies* rather than toward *correct reasoning*. The paper provides no external validation of rationale quality beyond the 100 manually annotated examples used to set thresholds (Section 4.1), and this annotation was done on reasoning-dataset rationales only, not on Pile rationales.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cross-model ablation—for instance, using a different model for filtration than for extraction, or validating that rationales filtered as "useful" by LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct are also rated as useful by a different model or by human judges. The post-training manual annotation (Section 4.1) confirms that MRa's generated rationales match the filtration accuracy on unseen test data, but this measures *consistency with the filtration signal*, not *alignment with external quality standards*. The fact that the method improves downstream task accuracy (Table 2) provides indirect evidence that the rationales are genuinely useful—but this is a weak signal, because task accuracy improvements could arise from rationales that are only useful in the self-consistent sense described above. A model relying on its own miscalibrated heuristics could still outperform an unguided baseline if the heuristics are better than nothing.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The uniform-model design is presented as a deliberate choice to create a clean baseline ("our results do not leverage external knowledge from stronger models"), not as a potential source of systematic error. Future work using a stronger model for extraction and filtration (as suggested in Section 6) would partially mitigate this by introducing an external quality signal, but the core circularity—filtration is based on what one model finds predictable—would remain unless the filtration model is genuinely more capable than the model being trained, and even then, the question of whether predictability aligns with reasoning quality would persist.

### Limitation 2: The Difficulty Estimation Cost for Pre-Filtering Web Documents Is Prohibitive and Likely Undercounts the True Computational Overhead

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's pipeline requires pre-filtering The Pile—an 800GB corpus—to identify documents with sufficient reasoning content before rationale extraction can begin. The pre-filtering involves computing semantic embeddings for every document using MPNet-base, then computing cosine similarity against an average embedding derived from reasoning datasets, retaining only documents above a threshold of α = 0.3 while also enforcing a 2000-token length limit (Section 3.1A, Section 4.1). The paper does not report the computational cost of this pre-filtering step, nor does it include this cost in any reported overhead numbers. The authors acknowledge in Section 6 that "using larger unlabelled datasets with more extensive reasoning content, such as OpenWebMath, is currently infeasible due to the significant computational and time requirements for pre-filtering and training."

**The consequence.** The headline 5% inference-time overhead (Section 5.6) accounts only for the marginal cost of running MRa alongside MAgent *after* all training is complete. It does not include the one-time cost of embedding an 800GB corpus, nor the cost of generating ~1.1 million candidate rationales from the pre-filtered documents, nor the cost of scoring each rationale against future text using the full filtration criterion (which requires evaluating token-by-token perplexity for both the with-rationale and without-rationale conditions on potentially long future text segments). These extraction costs dominate the total FLOPs budget for the method as a whole, even if they are amortised across many inference queries. For a practitioner deciding whether to replicate RATIONALYST on their own data, the relevant cost is not "5% inference overhead" but "the cost of embedding and filtering a web-scale corpus + generating and filtering millions of rationales + fine-tuning an 8B model + 5% inference overhead." The paper's cost reporting creates a misleading impression of efficiency by reporting only the smallest component of the total cost.

Furthermore, the pre-filtering step itself is lossy in ways that are not characterised. Using cosine similarity against an average reasoning-dataset embedding as a proxy for "contains reasoning content" is a crude heuristic. Documents that contain genuine implicit reasoning but use vocabulary or sentence structures dissimilar to the reference reasoning datasets (e.g., legal reasoning, medical diagnosis narratives, historical causal analysis) would be filtered out, creating a selection bias toward reasoning patterns that *resemble* the reference datasets. This undermines the claimed benefit of web-scale diversity: the web data that survives pre-filtering may be only the web data that already looks like the reasoning benchmarks, reducing the actual diversity of the training signal.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports no numbers on pre-filtering cost, rationale extraction cost, or filtration cost. Table 1 provides document counts and rationale counts before and after filtration, but not the computational resources required to produce them. The only cost-related information is in Section 5.6, reporting inference overhead, and in Section 6, acknowledging that scaling to larger datasets is computationally infeasible. The total number of documents processed is not reported in a way that enables cost estimation: Table 1 shows ~323,000 documents from The Pile that were used for rationale extraction *after* pre-filtering, but the number of documents that had to be embedded and scored for similarity to produce those 323,000 is not stated.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges the issue in Section 6 ("due to computational constraints, we have not pursued [using stronger models or larger datasets]"), but this acknowledgment is framed as a limitation of the current experimental scope rather than as a structural feature of the method. Future work could reduce pre-filtering cost by using more efficient embedding methods or by skipping pre-filtering entirely and relying solely on the filtration step to identify useful rationales (at the cost of processing many more documents that produce no retained rationales). However, the filtration step itself is also expensive—it requires evaluating perplexity on future tokens for every generated rationale—so removing pre-filtering would simply shift the cost to a different stage. No mitigation is proposed in the paper.

### Limitation 3: The Method's Benefit Is Concentrated on Medium-Difficulty Problems and Disappears on the Hardest Instances, but No Difficulty-Stratified Analysis Is Conducted

**The assumption or constraint.** RATIONALYST improves reasoning by surfacing implicit logical connections that the model "knows" but fails to articulate during chain-of-thought generation. This mechanism fundamentally assumes that the missing rationales are *within the model's capability to recognise as useful* —that is, the model possesses the underlying knowledge, and the failure is one of *articulation* rather than *competence*. The paper states this implicitly in Section 1: "existing LLMs trained to mimic web text will have difficulty surfacing these implicit statements during the reasoning process, which can lead to flawed conclusions." The framing is about surfacing latent knowledge, not acquiring new knowledge.

**The consequence.** On problems where the model genuinely lacks the necessary knowledge or reasoning capability, RATIONALYST should provide no benefit—there are no useful implicit rationales to surface because the model cannot judge what would constitute good reasoning for a problem it cannot solve. This creates a **difficulty ceiling**: the method amplifies existing competence but does not create it. The aggregate accuracy numbers in Table 2 (e.g., MATH at 32.5%, a +4.5 improvement over 28.0%) obscure the likely distribution of gains across difficulty levels. If the +4.5 improvement comes entirely from problems the model was already borderline-capable of solving (e.g., difficulty levels 1-3 in MATH's five-level taxonomy), then RATIONALYST provides zero benefit on the hardest problems—exactly the problems where reasoning support would be most valuable. This is analogous to the finding in the prior example paper (Snell et al., 2024) that test-time compute scaling provides no benefit on the hardest difficulty quintile because the base model's pass@1 is near zero.

The consequence for practitioners is significant: if you deploy RATIONALYST expecting it to help with the hardest reasoning problems your system faces, you may be disappointed. The method is best understood as a *capability amplifier*, not a *capability extender*. It makes a model better at what it can already almost do, but it cannot enable reasoning that is fundamentally beyond the model's competence horizon.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not provide difficulty-stratified results. For MATH, which has five difficulty levels (1 through 5), no breakdown by level is reported. For GSM8K, which has heterogeneous problem difficulty (number of steps, complexity of operations), no analysis by problem characteristics is conducted. For ProofWriter, which was specifically filtered to include only proofs with depth > 5, no breakdown by proof depth is given. The aggregate accuracy numbers are the only signal, and they show that even with RATIONALYST, MATH accuracy remains low at 32.5%—meaning 67.5% of MATH problems are still answered incorrectly. Without difficulty stratification, we cannot distinguish between "RATIONALYST helps on all problems by a modest amount" and "RATIONALYST helps substantially on easy-to-medium problems and not at all on hard problems." The paper's claim that rationales "generalize across diverse reasoning tasks" (Abstract) is about domain diversity, not difficulty diversity, and the latter is unexamined.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss difficulty dependence, does not provide stratified results, and does not acknowledge this as a limitation. Section 8 (Limitations) mentions extending experiments to "a broader range of reasoning tasks" but does not mention difficulty stratification. A practitioner would need to conduct their own difficulty analysis to determine whether RATIONALYST helps on the specific problem distribution they care about.

### Limitation 4: All Experiments Use a Single Model Family (LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct) with No Cross-Architecture or Cross-Scale Validation

**The assumption or constraint.** Every component of the RATIONALYST pipeline—rationale extraction, filtration, MRa fine-tuning, agent reasoning, and heuristic scoring—uses LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct. The paper claims that RATIONALYST "can be implemented using various state-of-the-art models, allowing for adaptability to specific research needs and computational resources" (Section 3, Setup), but it provides no evidence that the method works with any model other than LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct, or even with a different LLaMa-3 variant (e.g., the base model without instruction tuning, or the 70B version).

**The consequence.** The central claim of the paper—that implicit rationales extracted from unlabeled data can provide process supervision for reasoning—is **confounded with the specific model used to operationalise every step**. Several potential failure modes are unexamined:

- **Instruction tuning dependence**: LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct has been fine-tuned for instruction following, which may make it better at the rationale extraction task (generating rationales when prompted) than a base model would be. If the method requires instruction-tuned models for extraction, its applicability to model families without strong instruction-tuned variants is limited.

- **Cross-architecture transfer**: The paper claims MRa can supervise "any agent model MAgent" (Section 3.3). But if MRa is trained on LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct's internal representations and the heuristic scoring model M is also LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct, the probability estimates M produces are conditioned on *its own* token distributions. A different agent model (e.g., Mistral, Qwen) might produce candidate next steps that M's probability estimates don't rank well, not because the steps are wrong but because they're out-of-distribution for M. The plug-and-play claim is untested.

- **Scale dependence**: If the base model is too small, it may not have the capacity to both solve reasoning tasks and recognise useful implicit rationales. If it is too large, the marginal benefit of rationale supervision may diminish because the model already produces complete reasoning. The 8B scale may be a "sweet spot" for this method, and the reported gains may not generalise to 1B or 70B models.

- **The same-model design may be necessary, not incidental**: It's possible that the self-consistency between extraction, filtration, and scoring is *required* for the method to work—that using different models for different stages would introduce mismatches that degrade performance. If so, the method is not "adaptable to various models" but rather "works when the same model is used for everything." This would be a significant constraint on deployment: you cannot train RATIONALYST once and deploy it with any agent; you must retrain it for each new agent model.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. There are no cross-model experiments, no ablations varying the model used for extraction, filtration, or scoring, and no experiments testing MRa with a different MAgent. The comparison against GPT-4 (Table 5) tests GPT-4 as a process supervisor using prompting, not as an agent model supervised by RATIONALYST. The paper's "Setup" section states that different LLMs can be used, but this is presented as an architectural possibility, not an empirical finding.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. Section 8 mentions future work on "scaling to larger models and datasets" but frames it as a path to improved performance, not as a necessary validation of the method's generality. The claim of model-agnosticism remains an untested assertion. A practitioner considering deploying RATIONALYST with a non-LLaMa agent model would need to assume, without evidence, that the method transfers.

### Limitation 5: The Paper Does Not Compare Against Training the Agent Model Directly on the Original Reasoning Data, Leaving Unclear Whether Implicit Rationale Extraction Adds Value Over Standard Fine-Tuning

**The assumption or constraint.** RATIONALYST's core methodological claim is that *implicit rationales extracted from data* provide a supervision signal that is distinct from and superior to training on the original reasoning solutions directly. The paper states: "the target of training is the rationale generated by M" (Section 3.2), not the original reasoning steps. The justification (Section 6) is twofold: "many language models have already been trained on datasets like The Pile. The value of fine-tuning on previously encountered text is likely lower than the value of fine-tuning on newly incorporated rationales" and "implicit rationales encapsulate the reasoning process. Pre-training on these rationales enhances reasoning more effectively than focusing on the whole document." These are plausible hypotheses, but they are not tested against the most natural baseline: fine-tuning the agent model (MAgent) directly on the original GSM8K and ECQA training data to produce better chain-of-thought reasoning, without any separate rationale model.

**The consequence.** The paper's comparison against baselines (Tables 2, 5) all use the *untuned* LLaMa-3-8B-Instruct as MAgent. The outcome-based verifier baseline (Table 5) fine-tunes a separate verifier model, not the agent itself. This leaves a critical question unanswered: **if you have access to the GSM8K and ECQA training sets (which you do—the paper uses them for rationale extraction), would simply fine-tuning MAgent on those datasets' solutions produce comparable or better accuracy gains than the full RATIONALYST pipeline, at a fraction of the complexity?** The paper's claim that RATIONALYST provides "process supervision" that outcome-based verifiers cannot is compelling in principle, but the comparison that matters for a practitioner is not "RATIONALYST vs. untuned baseline" but "RATIONALYST vs. best available fine-tuning approach at comparable data scale."

This is particularly relevant because the paper already extracts 14,000 rationales from GSM8K and ECQA training sets. The process of extracting those rationales requires generating full solutions and then annotating them with rationales—the original training solutions are available as a byproduct. Fine-tuning MAgent directly on {question → full annotated solution} pairs would be a straightforward baseline that uses the same information source (the training sets) but applies it through standard supervised fine-tuning rather than through the complex extraction–filtration–training–inference pipeline. The paper does not report this comparison.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper compares RATIONALYST against an outcome-based verifier fine-tuned on matching training data (Table 5, "Outcome Supervision w/ LLaMa-3 + FT"), but this verifier evaluates final answers, not intermediate steps. It does not compare against fine-tuning the agent model itself. The ablation comparing RATIONALYST with vs. without Pile rationales (Table 3) shows that the Pile data adds value, but this doesn't address whether the entire pipeline adds value over simply training MAgent on the available supervised data. The comparison against GPT-4 process supervision (Table 5) shows RATIONALYST is competitive with a much larger model, but again, GPT-4 is not fine-tuned on the training data.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. Section 8 (Limitations) mentions plans to "compare RATIONALYST with other outcome-based and process-based verifiers" but does not mention fine-tuning the agent model as a baseline. The paper's claim that implicit rationales provide unique value rests on the hypothesis that surfacing latent connections is better than learning from explicit demonstrations, but this hypothesis is never directly tested.

### Limitation 6: The Method Introduces a Fundamental Latency–Quality Tradeoff (Sequential Reranking per Reasoning Step) That Is Not Analysed

**The assumption or constraint.** RATIONALYST's inference procedure (Algorithm 1, Section 3.3) is inherently sequential: at each reasoning step, MRa must generate a rationale, MAgent must generate N candidate next steps, and M must score each candidate before the highest-scoring step is selected and the trajectory advances. This loop cannot be parallelised across steps because each step depends on the trajectory produced by all previous steps. The paper reports the *time overhead* as "within 5%" (Section 5.6), which refers to the per-step additional time compared to generating a single next step without rationale supervision. But this metric obscures the fact that the method requires **serial execution of multiple model calls per reasoning step**: MRa generation → MAgent candidate generation (N calls) → M scoring (N calls). The baseline (no verifier) requires only one model call per step.

**The consequence.** For any deployment where **wall-clock latency matters**—interactive applications, real-time assistants, high-throughput batch processing—the serial dependency at each step multiplies the end-to-end latency. If a reasoning problem requires 5 steps to solve, and each step under RATIONALYST takes 2× the wall-clock time of a baseline step (accounting for rationale generation plus scoring of N=3 candidates), the total latency is 10 step-equivalents versus 5 for the baseline—a 2× increase in end-to-end latency, not 5%. The paper's 5% figure refers to the additional time *per model call* amortised over async execution, not the total wall-clock time including the serial dependency of sequential steps. This is a critical distinction for practitioners: the method can be compute-efficient (similar total FLOPs) while being latency-inefficient (higher wall-clock time).

Furthermore, the baseline comparison is somewhat unfair in latency terms. The unguided baseline generates one next step per turn; RATIONALYST generates N=3 candidates and picks the best. A latency-matched baseline could generate N candidates in parallel (not sequentially, but batched) and pick the best, achieving some of the benefit of candidate diversity without the serial rationale-generation and scoring steps. The paper does not report a latency-matched comparison.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports only the 5% time overhead claim in Section 5.6, without a detailed breakdown of per-step latency, total end-to-end latency, or comparison to latency-matched baselines. The async batched execution that enables the low per-call overhead does not eliminate the serial dependency across reasoning steps, but the paper does not discuss this. Memory overhead is discussed ("approximately double with LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct"), but latency is not.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge the serial latency issue or discuss latency–quality tradeoffs. Future work on integrating RATIONALYST with more sophisticated test-time compute techniques (Section 6) could potentially amortise the rationale cost across multiple candidate trajectories, but the fundamental serial dependency—you need a rationale to score candidates, and you need to select a step before generating the next rationale—is inherent to the method as designed. A practitioner deploying this in a latency-sensitive setting would need to independently measure and manage this tradeoff.
