# Scaling Smart: Accelerating Large Language Model Pre-training with Small Model Initialization

**ArXiv:** [2409.12903](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12903)

## 🎯 Pitch

You can cut large language model pre-training time by up to 4× while improving final accuracy—simply by copying a small pre-trained model’s knowledge into a bigger network before training begins. The paper’s HyperCloning method expands a small model’s weights so the larger model produces identical outputs at initialization, then lets training naturally break the symmetry. Across three model families, this gave the same accuracy in 250B tokens that random initialization needed 2.4T tokens to reach.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **HyperCloning**, a method that initializes the parameters of a large language model by expanding the weights of a smaller pre-trained model, ensuring the larger model functionally preserves the smaller model's output logits before training begins. Evaluated on OPT-1.3B, Pythia-1.4B, and OLMO-2.9B architectures initialized from smaller counterparts across 10 downstream tasks, HyperCloning improves both training speed and final accuracy relative to standard random initialization, achieving convergence speedups of 2.2× to 4× (matching the random-initialization baseline's final accuracy in substantially fewer tokens, such as 250B rather than 2.4T for OLMO). Ablation studies further reveal that symmetric weight duplication breaks naturally during training due to stochastic operations like dropout and that more accurate source models yield better target initialization, establishing that the method's benefits are robust across model families and training budgets only when the source model possesses reasonable predictive capability.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Pre-training Costs Dominate the LLM Lifecycle

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that **training large language models from scratch is prohibitively expensive**, yet scaling up model size remains the most reliable way to improve performance. This creates a tension that affects virtually every organization working with LLMs: larger models deliver better results, but the pre-training cost grows super-linearly with model scale, making experimentation, iteration, and even single-shot training runs financially burdensome.

The paper opens by grounding this problem in concrete numbers. Training a 12-billion-parameter model requires approximately **72,000 GPU hours** (citing Biderman et al., 2023), and the total cost from scratch using public cloud compute is described as "staggering" (Section 1). These aren't abstract complaints — they reflect a structural bottleneck in the field's research and deployment pipeline. When a single training run costs hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars:

- **Hypothesis testing becomes prohibitively slow.** Researchers cannot easily ablate architecture choices, training recipes, or data mixtures at the scale that matters, because each experiment requires a full pre-training run. This slows the pace of empirical science around LLMs.
- **Failure is catastrophically expensive.** Training runs can fail for reasons including improper learning rate tuning, hardware failures, or loss divergence (the authors cite Narayanan et al., 2021 and Dubey et al., 2024). When a run fails after consuming tens of thousands of GPU hours, the investment is lost — there's no partial credit or salvageable artifact.
- **Smaller players are locked out.** Organizations without access to massive compute clusters are effectively excluded from pushing the frontier of model scale, concentrating progress in a handful of well-resourced labs.

The paper frames small language models as the obvious alternative: they are "less costly to train and impose lower financial and environmental burdens during research and development" (Section 1). But the dilemma is that they "often lack the desired level of accuracy." This is the central tradeoff the paper confronts — a tradeoff that, prior to this work, had no principled resolution beyond "just train the bigger model."

### The Gap: No Practical Mechanism to Transfer Small-Model Knowledge Into Large-Model Initialization

The gap the paper identifies is more specific than "training large models is expensive." It's that **the pre-training phase almost always begins from scratch** — randomly initialized parameters — even when a smaller model trained on similar or identical data already exists. This is wasteful in an information-theoretic sense: the smaller model has already learned features, representations, and statistical regularities from the data distribution. A randomly initialized large model possesses none of this knowledge. The large model must rediscover from scratch what the small model already knows, consuming enormous compute simply to reach the starting point that the small model already occupies.

The paper articulates this gap as an open question right in the abstract and introduction:

> "Can we develop a method to initialize large language models using smaller pre-trained models? Will such initialization bring any benefits in terms of training time and final accuracy?"

Notice what makes this question non-trivial. It's not simply "can we copy weights between models?" — that's architecturally impossible when the hidden dimensions differ. The question is whether there exists a **function-preserving transformation** that maps the parameters of a smaller model onto the higher-dimensional parameter space of a larger model in a way that (a) the larger model initially computes exactly the same function as the smaller model, and (b) this initialization provides a meaningful head start for subsequent training.

Prior to this work, the field lacked such a transformation for modern decoder-only transformer architectures at the scales relevant to contemporary LLM pre-training. The gap is methodological: *how* to do the expansion, not *whether* expanding could be useful.

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper situates itself within the broader literature of **model growth** — techniques that start with a smaller model and expand it to a larger one, either during or before training. The authors structure their critique of prior work along several axes.

#### Depth Growth (Stacking Blocks) Is Well-Studied But Incomplete

The most active area of prior work on model growth focuses on **increasing depth** — taking a pre-trained transformer and adding more layers, often by duplicating existing layer weights. The paper cites a substantial body of work in this direction: Gong et al. (2019), Samragh et al. (2023), Yang et al. (2020), Karp et al. (2024), Li et al. (2023), and Wang et al. (2023a). The typical approach involves initializing new layers as copies of existing ones, exploiting the residual structure of transformers to ensure that the expanded model initially computes something close to the original function.

The paper acknowledges the value of this work but identifies a key limitation: **depth scaling alone is not a complete solution**. The authors explicitly state that their width-expansion method is designed as "a complementary technique that can be accompanied by any of these methods to provide a full recipe for model scaling" (Section 2). In other words, depth growth and width growth address different architectural dimensions, and a comprehensive model-scaling strategy would want both. The gap is that width growth — particularly function-preserving width growth for decoder transformers — was under-explored compared to depth growth.

A 2024 study by Du et al. is cited as a comprehensive survey of model growth strategies, including both depth and width expansion. Their findings provide important context: they "concluded that depth growth achieves the best accuracies, while non-function-preserving width growth results in poorer performance" (Section 4). This is a critical data point — it suggests that naive width expansion (e.g., randomly initializing new neurons) actively *hurts* performance relative to starting from scratch, which explains why the field gravitated toward depth expansion. But it also sets up the paper's central challenge: **can function-preserving width expansion reverse this negative result?**

#### Width Growth Exists But Has Critical Limitations

Width expansion is not a new idea. The paper traces its lineage to Net2Net (Chen et al., 2015), which introduced function-preserving width expansion for convolutional neural networks. That work showed that you could expand the width of a trained CNN's layers while preserving its input-output mapping, enabling the larger model to start training from the smaller model's converged solution rather than from scratch.

This was later extended to BERT-style encoder transformers by Chen et al. (2021), but the paper argues this extension was incomplete for the modern LLM landscape. The paper explicitly states that it "builds on these foundations by generalizing width expansion techniques to **decoder-style transformers**, which are increasingly utilized in modern large language models" (Section 4, emphasis added). The specific gaps it identifies:

- **Attention layers were not addressed.** Prior width expansion techniques for transformers didn't provide a recipe for expanding the query/key/value dimensions within multi-head attention while preserving the attention computation.
- **Position embeddings lacked a cloning procedure.** Decoder-only transformers use position embeddings that must be expanded to match the wider hidden dimensions, and prior work hadn't specified how to do this functionally.
- **Validation at scale was missing.** Prior width expansion experiments were conducted on smaller models and datasets, leaving open the question of whether the approach transfers to the billion-parameter, trillion-token regime.

Another prior approach comes from Shen et al. (2022), who proposed a staged training method where width is expanded by initializing new weights with **zeros in the off-diagonal blocks** of expanded weight matrices. The paper's ablation studies (Section 3.4) directly address this: "diagonal initialization can lead to slower convergence compared to our symmetric initialization method." In essence, zero-initializing the cross-connections between original and new neurons creates a bottleneck — the model must learn these connections from scratch, losing some of the benefit of pre-training.

#### Knowledge Distillation Is Powerful But Invasive

The paper also briefly addresses knowledge distillation as an alternative knowledge transfer mechanism (Xu et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2023). Distillation can transfer knowledge from a smaller teacher to a larger student, but the authors note a practical drawback: distillation "usually require[s] changing the training setup" (Section 2). Specifically, distillation modifies the training objective — adding a KL-divergence term to match teacher logits — which means the standard pre-training loop must be altered. One of HyperCloning's explicit design goals is an **"Unchanged Training Loop"**: "For ease of deployment, the training loop should remain unchanged. The only modification should be in the network initialization" (Section 2). This constraint is motivated by practical engineering considerations — modifying distributed training pipelines, which are heavily optimized and tuned for specific hardware configurations, is itself a significant undertaking.

#### The Symmetry Problem and Its Resolution in Prior Work

A notable thread in prior width-expansion literature concerns **weight symmetry**. When you initialize new neurons as duplicates of existing ones (as function-preserving expansions typically do), the duplicated neurons receive identical gradients during training, at least initially. This raises the concern that they will remain coupled — learning the same features rather than diversifying — effectively wasting the additional parameters. Wang et al. (2023b) explicitly raised this concern: "the symmetry of neurons in an expanded network suggests these neurons may not contribute independently to the model's learning" (as quoted in Section 4).

The paper takes this concern seriously, dedicating an entire analysis section (Section 3.2) to tracking weight symmetry during training. The resolution — that symmetry "naturally breaks during training, potentially due to random operations such as dropout" — is one of the paper's empirical contributions, providing evidence that the theoretical concern does not manifest as a practical problem in the LLM pre-training regime.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions HyperCloning as filling a specific methodological gap: **function-preserving width expansion for modern decoder-only LLMs at scale**. Its design goals (Section 2) make the positioning explicit:

1. **Expansion Dimension**: The method targets width (hidden dimension), not depth. This is positioned as complementary to the depth-growth literature, not competitive with it — the two can be combined.
2. **Function Preservation**: The expanded model must compute exactly the same logits as the source model at initialization. This is what differentiates HyperCloning from non-function-preserving width expansion approaches that Du et al. (2024) found perform poorly.
3. **Low Compute Overhead**: The expansion itself should be a straightforward weight-copying operation, not requiring iterative optimization, distillation training, or heavy computation. This makes the method practical — the expansion step is essentially free compared to pre-training.
4. **Unchanged Training Loop**: This is a deployment constraint motivated by practical engineering. The method should be a drop-in initialization change that leaves the entire training infrastructure untouched.

Critically, the paper doesn't claim to have invented width expansion or function preservation. It explicitly cites Net2Net (2015) and BERT2BERT (2021) as foundations. What it claims is the **adaptation, generalization, and scaled validation** of these ideas for the decoder-only transformer architectures that dominate modern LLM development. The contributions are:

- Specifying the cloning procedure for each architectural component in a decoder transformer: linear layers (three cases depending on which dimensions expand), attention layers (handling both head-dimension expansion and head-count expansion while preserving attention scores), layer normalization, and position embeddings.
- Validating that the approach actually delivers speedups and accuracy improvements at the billion-parameter scale across three different model families (OPT, Pythia, OLMO) trained on trillion-token-scale datasets.
- Providing empirical analysis that addresses the key concerns from prior literature: demonstrating that weight symmetry breaks during training (addressing Wang et al., 2023b's concern), showing that the expanded model achieves full-rank weight matrices after training (Section 3.3), and benchmarking against alternative expansion strategies including diagonal initialization (addressing Shen et al., 2022).

The paper's framing is deliberately practical rather than theoretical. It doesn't propose new architectural innovations or training algorithms. It offers a **recipe**: take your pre-trained small model, apply these weight-duplication rules, and start training your large model from a much better initialization than random. The motivation is economic and scientific — reducing the cost of scaling experiments and enabling faster iteration — and the positioning is that this recipe fills a gap that, despite prior work on model growth, remained unfilled for the specific architectures and scales that matter in contemporary LLM development.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

HyperCloning is a weight-initialization algorithm that takes a fully trained small language model and a target architecture (identical in depth but wider in hidden dimensions) and produces a set of initial weights for the larger model such that the larger model computes exactly the same output logits as the smaller model on every input. The system solves the problem of "how do you warm-start a large model with knowledge from a small one when the hidden dimensions don't match?" — and it does so via a set of weight-duplication rules, one per architectural component, that collectively constitute a function-preserving map from the small model's parameter space into the larger model's parameter space.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has two explicit components and one implicit constraint that governs all design decisions:

1. **Source Model** — a fully pre-trained decoder-only transformer LM with some hidden dimension `d_model` and some number of layers `L`. This model already achieves non-trivial accuracy and carries all the knowledge the paper wants to transfer.

2. **Target Model** — an architecture with the **same number of layers `L`** but with hidden dimensions expanded by a factor of `n` (where `n` is an integer, typically 2 or 4). All linear projections, attention heads, and normalisation layers must be wider, but depth and the overall computation graph topology are preserved.

3. **Function-Preservation Constraint** — the binding requirement that makes the whole scheme work. Every weight matrix and bias vector in the target model must be set such that the cloned hidden representations (vectors formed by stacking the source model's representations `n` times) propagate through each layer identically to the source model, producing cloned output logits. This constraint is what guarantees the target model starts training from the source model's accuracy rather than from random-guess performance.

The flow is: source model weights → per-layer cloning rules (Section 3.4, Appendix A) → target model weights → standard LM training loop (unchanged). The cloning step is a one-time, non-iterative operation with negligible compute cost relative to pre-training.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the core cloning primitive (vector cloning and the three linear-layer cases):** This is the atomic operation that underpins every other component. Understanding how a weight matrix maps cloned inputs to cloned outputs — in all three configurations of which dimensions expand — is prerequisite to everything that follows.
- **Second, linear layer cloning with the three cases illustrated:** The paper identifies three distinct scenarios (only input expands, only output expands, both expand) and provides the weight-initialisation formula for each along with the proof that function preservation holds.
- **Third, attention-layer cloning:** This is the most architecturally complex component because expanding width in the attention mechanism can be done two ways — expanding per-head dimension or expanding head count — and each requires modifications to the query/key/value projections plus attention-score rescaling.
- **Fourth, normalisation layers and positional embeddings:** These are architectural bookkeeping — they must be expanded to match the wider hidden dimensions, but the cloning logic is relatively straightforward once the vector-cloning primitive is understood.
- **Fifth, the noise-augmented symmetric expansion (and why symmetry breaks):** The paper's symmetric duplication creates an initial state where groups of `n` neurons have identical weights — a potential capacity concern. The paper addresses this with noise injection and an empirical analysis showing symmetry decays naturally during training.
- **Sixth, the rank and singular-value analysis:** This provides the mathematical intuition for why the expanded model doesn't remain low-rank despite starting with duplicated weights — the randomness from dropout and stochastic gradient descent acts as a symmetry-breaking mechanism that allows the model to utilise its full parameter capacity.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that a set of precise weight-duplication rules — one for each layer type in a decoder-only transformer — can map a small pre-trained model onto a larger architecture while preserving the input-output function exactly, and that this functionally-preserved initialisation serves as a dramatically better starting point for pre-training than random weights.

#### Vector Cloning: The Atomic Operation

The entire method rests on a single primitive repeatedly applied: **vector cloning**. The paper defines it in Section 2 with notation that carries through every subsequent derivation.

> Let `$x_S \in \mathbb{R}^d$` be a hidden representation in the source (small) network. The `$n$`-fold cloned version of `$x_S$` is:

> $$x_D = [x_S, ..., x_S]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n d}$$

where `$d$` is the original hidden dimension, `$n$` is the integer expansion factor, and `$[\cdot, ..., \cdot]^\top$` denotes vertical concatenation into a column vector.

**What it produces:** a vector `$n$` times longer than the original, formed by stacking `$n$` identical copies. Every block of `$d$` consecutive elements in `$x_D$` is exactly `$x_S$`. 

**Why this operation:** the goal is to make the larger model's internal representations structurally identical to the source model's — just replicated `$n$` times. If the target model can be initialised such that its hidden representations are always cloned versions of the source model's representations at every layer, then the target model's final output will also be a cloned version of the source model's output, guaranteeing function preservation. The vector clone is the **invariant that the method maintains**: every activation tensor in the target model should be a cloned version of the corresponding activation in the source model, before any training occurs.

This primitive appears everywhere: the embedding layer must output a cloned token embedding, every transformer block must accept a cloned input and produce a cloned output, the final unembedding layer must accept a cloned hidden state and produce cloned logits (which, when summed or averaged appropriately, collapse to the original logits).

#### Overview of Weight Expansion

The core challenge is: given a source linear layer with weight matrix `$W_S$` and bias `$b_S$` that maps input `$x_S$` to output `$y_S$`, how do you construct `$W_D$` and `$b_D$` such that when the target input is a cloned version of the source input, the target output is a cloned version of the source output? The answer depends on **which dimensions have been expanded** — input dimension only, output dimension only, or both. The paper identifies three mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive cases (Figure 3).

---

#### Linear Layer Cloning: Case 1 — Only the Input Is Expanded

**Where this occurs:** the final unembedding layer. The hidden dimension feeding into it has been expanded (so its input `$x_D$` is `$n$`-fold cloned), but its output dimension is fixed — it must produce logits over the same vocabulary as the source model, so `$y_D$` must equal `$y_S$`.

**The weight construction.** The paper specifies (Appendix A, Case 1):

> $$W_D = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{W_S}{n} + \eta_1 & \frac{W_S}{n} - \eta_1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b_D = b_S$$

where `$W_S$` is the source weight matrix (output_dim × d), `$n$` is the expansion factor, and `$\eta_1$` is a random noise tensor of the same shape as `$W_S / n$` (output_dim × d). In the 2-fold case shown in Figure 3 (left panel), the expanded weight is formed by concatenating two blocks horizontally: the left block is `$W_S/n + \eta_1$` and the right block is `$W_S/n - \eta_1$`. The bias is left unchanged.

**Verification of function preservation:**

$$y_D = W_D x_D + b_D = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{W_S}{n} + \eta_1 & \frac{W_S}{n} - \eta_1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_S \\ \vdots \\ x_S \end{bmatrix} + b_S$$

The product splits into `$n$` terms, each of the form `$(\frac{W_S}{n} \pm \eta_i) x_S$`. Summing across all `$n$` blocks, the `$\pm \eta_i$` contributions cancel pairwise when `$n$` is even and the noise terms are constructed in `$+/-$` pairs. The `$W_S/n$` terms sum to `$n \cdot (W_S x_S / n) = W_S x_S$`. Therefore:

$$y_D = W_S x_S + b_S = y_S$$

**Why the `$1/n$` scaling:** if you simply stacked the source weight `$n$` times horizontally without scaling, the matrix-vector product with the cloned input would be `$n$` times larger than the source output (`$n \cdot W_S x_S$` instead of `$W_S x_S$`). The `$1/n$` factor compensates for the fact that the input has been replicated `$n$` times, keeping the output magnitude identical. This is essential — without it, the logits would be scaled by `$n$` after the expansion step, changing the model's predictions and breaking function preservation.

**Why the noise terms:** the symmetric construction `$[W_S/n, W_S/n]$` would make the two halves of the expanded weight matrix identical. Adding paired noise (`$+\eta_1, -\eta_1$`) breaks this symmetry while still cancelling out in the forward pass, so function preservation is maintained. The noise gives the duplicated input pathways slightly different starting points, which the paper argues helps the symmetry break during training. The signal-to-noise ratio is a tunable hyperparameter, though the paper finds in Section 3.4 that the noise-free version works equally well in practice.

---

#### Linear Layer Cloning: Case 2 — Only the Output Is Expanded

**Where this occurs:** the embedding layer. The input dimension is fixed (token indices), but the output dimension must be expanded to match the wider hidden dimension of the target model. The input `$x_D = x_S$` (same token index), and the output `$y_D$` should be an `$n$`-fold clone of `$y_S$`.

**The weight construction (Appendix A, Case 2):**

> $$W_D = \begin{bmatrix} W_S \\ W_S \end{bmatrix}, \quad b_D = \begin{bmatrix} b_S \\ b_S \end{bmatrix}$$

In the 2-fold case (Figure 3, middle panel), the expanded weight is formed by vertically stacking two copies of `$W_S$`. The bias is similarly duplicated by vertical stacking.

**Verification:**

$$y_D = W_D x_D + b_D = \begin{bmatrix} W_S \\ W_S \end{bmatrix} x_S + \begin{bmatrix} b_S \\ b_S \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} W_S x_S + b_S \\ W_S x_S + b_S \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} y_S \\ y_S \end{bmatrix}$$

**What's notable here:** there is no `$1/n$` scaling in this case. The input is not cloned (it's just a single token index), so each copy of `$W_S$` independently produces the full output `$y_S$`. The output is cloned by construction — each vertical block of the weight matrix produces its own copy of the source output, which are then stacked.

**Why this is simpler than Case 1:** the expansion happens in the output dimension, so the weight matrix simply needs to produce multiple identical copies of the source output. Vertical stacking achieves this trivially without scaling factors or noise terms.

---

#### Linear Layer Cloning: Case 3 — Both Input and Output Are Expanded

**Where this occurs:** the vast majority of layers in the transformer — all hidden linear projections in attention and feed-forward networks. Both the input hidden dimension and the output hidden dimension have been expanded by `$n$`, so the weight matrix must map an `$n$`-fold cloned input to an `$n$`-fold cloned output.

**The weight construction (Appendix A, Case 3):**

> $$W_D = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{W_S}{n} + \eta_1 & \frac{W_S}{n} - \eta_1 \\ \frac{W_S}{n} + \eta_2 & \frac{W_S}{n} - \eta_2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b_D = \begin{bmatrix} b_S \\ b_S \end{bmatrix}$$

In the 2-fold case (Figure 3, right panel), `$W_D$` is a 2×2 block matrix. Each block has the same shape as `$W_S$`. The top row contains `$W_S/n + \eta_1$` and `$W_S/n - \eta_1$`; the bottom row contains `$W_S/n + \eta_2$` and `$W_S/n - \eta_2$`. The bias is vertically duplicated.

**Verification:** multiplying this block matrix by the cloned input `$x_D = [x_S; x_S]$`:

The top output block receives contributions from both input blocks: `$(W_S/n + \eta_1)x_S + (W_S/n - \eta_1)x_S = 2W_S x_S / n = W_S x_S$` (when `$n=2$`). The noise cancels. The bottom output block similarly receives `$(W_S/n + \eta_2)x_S + (W_S/n - \eta_2)x_S = W_S x_S$`. Adding the duplicated bias gives `$[W_S x_S + b_S; W_S x_S + b_S] = [y_S; y_S]$`.

For general `$n$`-fold expansion, the pattern generalises: `$W_D$` is an `$n \times n$` block matrix where each row contains `$n$` blocks. The blocks in row `$i$` are constructed as `$W_S/n$` plus noise terms `$\eta_{i,1}, \eta_{i,2}, ..., \eta_{i,n}$` that sum to zero within that row. Each row independently produces `$y_S$` as its output block, and stacking all rows yields the cloned output.

**Why this block-structured form:** the block matrix is a direct realisation of the vector-cloning invariant. Each row of blocks corresponds to one copy of the output, and each column of blocks corresponds to one copy of the input. The `$1/n$` scaling in every block ensures that when the row sums over all input copies, it recovers exactly one copy of the source output. The noise terms within each row sum to zero to maintain function preservation while providing per-row diversity.

**Alternative considered:** the paper explores a diagonal variant in Section 3.4:

> $$W_D = \begin{bmatrix} W_S & 0 \\ 0 & W_S \end{bmatrix}$$

This also preserves function: `$[W_S, 0][x_S; x_S] = W_S x_S$` and `$[0, W_S][x_S; x_S] = W_S x_S$`. However, the zero blocks mean that half the parameters in the expanded matrix receive zero gradient during backpropagation from half the input dimensions — each output block only sees one copy of the input. The symmetric (full-block) construction gives every output block gradient signal from every input copy, which the paper finds accelerates convergence.

#### Attention Layer Cloning: Two Expansion Strategies

The attention mechanism presents a design choice: when expanding the model's hidden dimension, should you **(a) expand the per-head dimension** (keeping the number of heads fixed but making each head wider) or **(b) expand the number of attention heads** (keeping per-head dimension fixed but adding more heads)? The paper addresses both.

**Strategy 1: Expanding per-head dimension.** In a standard multi-head attention layer, the input hidden state is projected through query, key, and value weight matrices (`$W_Q$`, `$W_K$`, `$W_V$`) to produce per-head query, key, and value tensors. If the hidden dimension doubles (`$n=2$`) and the number of heads stays constant, each head's query/key/value dimension must double. The query/key/value weight matrices are treated as Case 3 linear layers (both input and output expand) and cloned accordingly.

However, there is a subtlety with the attention score computation. The paper identifies it (Appendix A, "Cloning Attention Layers"):

> Let `$q_S$` and `$k_S$` represent the query and key values in the small network. Then the corresponding values in the expanded network would be `$q_D = [q_S; q_S]$` and `$k_D = [k_S; k_S]$`.

The attention score in the source model is:

$$a_S = \frac{q_S k_S^\top}{\sqrt{d}}$$

In the target model, using cloned queries and keys:

$$a_D = \frac{q_D k_D^\top}{\sqrt{2d}} = \frac{2 q_S k_S^\top}{\sqrt{2d}} = \sqrt{2} a_S$$

**The problem:** the dot product doubles (because each cloned query vector has twice as many elements, each pair contributing `$q_S \cdot k_S$`), but the scaling denominator only increases by `$\sqrt{2}$`. The result is that attention scores are scaled by `$\sqrt{2}$`, which changes the softmax distribution and breaks function preservation.

**The fix:** the paper prescribes scaling the query (or key) weights by an additional factor:

> "To make `$a_D$` equal to `$a_S$`, we should scale the query value by `$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$`. More generally, the expanded query weights should be scaled by `$\sqrt{\frac{d_S}{d_D}}$`, where `$d_S$` and `$d_D$` are the head dimensions in the original and extended layers."

**Why this matters:** attention scores are the backbone of the transformer's information-routing mechanism. If they are scaled differently in the expanded model, the softmax over attention scores produces different attention weights, which changes which tokens the model attends to — even at initialisation. This would violate function preservation at a fundamental level.

**Strategy 2: Expanding the number of attention heads.** This is the approach taken for the OPT and Pythia experiments in the paper (Table 1: OPT goes from 16 heads to 32; Pythia goes from 16 to 32). Here, the per-head dimension stays constant (`$d_{head}$` unchanged), but the number of heads doubles. The paper states (Appendix A): "This case is straightforward. We can simply duplicate the attention heads."

Concretely, the `$W_Q$`, `$W_K$`, and `$W_V$` weight matrices (which concatenate all heads' projections) are treated as Case 3 linear layers. Since the per-head dimension is unchanged, no rescaling of attention scores is needed — each duplicated head computes identical attention to its source counterpart. The output projection `$W_O$` (which combines all heads back to the hidden dimension) is also treated as a Case 3 linear layer.

The paper does note one additional requirement: "the fully connected layer that follows the attention layer will also be expanded to increase the hidden representation's dimensionality." This is the standard Case 3 expansion applied to the output projection.

#### Cloning Normalisation Layers

The paper addresses Layer Normalisation specifically (Appendix A, "Cloning Layer Norm"), and notes that the argument extends to RMSNorm, BatchNorm, and GroupNorm.

Let `$x_S$` be a hidden representation vector. Layer Normalisation computes:

$$l(x_S) = \frac{x_S - \mathbb{E}[x_S]}{\sqrt{\text{var}(x_S) + \epsilon}} \cdot \gamma_S + \beta_S$$

where `$\mathbb{E}[x_S]$` is the scalar mean, `$\text{var}(x_S)$` is the scalar variance, `$\epsilon$` is a small constant for numerical stability, and `$\gamma_S, \beta_S$` are learnable affine parameters (each a vector of length `$d$`).

**The cloning procedure.** The paper specifies:

> $$\beta_D = \begin{bmatrix} \beta_S \\ \beta_S \end{bmatrix}, \quad \gamma_D = \begin{bmatrix} \gamma_S \\ \gamma_S \end{bmatrix}$$

The affine parameters are vertically duplicated. No scaling factor is needed.

**Verification.** When the input is a cloned vector `$x_D = [x_S; x_S]$`:

- The mean `$\mathbb{E}[x_D]$` equals `$\mathbb{E}[x_S]$` because all `$2d$` elements are drawn from two copies of the same `$d$` values — the average is identical.
- The variance `$\text{var}(x_D)$` equals `$\text{var}(x_S)$` for the same reason — the empirical distribution is just two copies of the source distribution.
- Therefore, `$\frac{x_D - \mathbb{E}[x_D]}{\sqrt{\text{var}(x_D) + \epsilon}} = [\frac{x_S - \mathbb{E}[x_S]}{\sqrt{\text{var}(x_S) + \epsilon}}; \frac{x_S - \mathbb{E}[x_S]}{\sqrt{\text{var}(x_S) + \epsilon}}]$` — the normalised output is itself a cloned vector.
- Multiplying by duplicated `$\gamma$` and adding duplicated `$\beta$` preserves the cloning structure.

**Why this works with no modification beyond duplication:** normalisation is an element-wise operation after the mean/variance statistics are computed. Since the statistics are invariant under duplication of the dataset, the normalised values are duplicated too. The affine parameters are per-dimension scaling factors, so duplicating them applies the same scaling to each copy.

---

#### Cloning Positional Embeddings

Decoder-only transformers use position embeddings — either learned or sinusoidal — to encode token position information. When the hidden dimension expands from `$d_S$` to `$n \cdot d_S$`, the position embeddings must also expand.

The paper defines the cloned positional embedding (Appendix A):

> Let `$P_S(x_S, i) \in \mathbb{R}^d$` denote the positional embedding of a pretrained small network. The `$n$`-times cloned positional embedding is defined as:

> $$P_D(X_D, i) = \begin{bmatrix} P_S(x_S, i) \\ \vdots \\ P_S(x_S, i) \end{bmatrix}$$

**What this produces:** for each position index `$i$`, the expanded positional embedding is the vertical stack of `$n$` copies of the source model's position embedding for that position. If the source model used learned position embeddings (a matrix of shape `[max_seq_len, d_S]`), the target model's position embedding matrix becomes `[max_seq_len, n * d_S]` by horizontally replicating each row `$n$` times.

**Why this is necessary:** position embeddings are added to token embeddings before the first transformer block. If the token embedding has been expanded via Case 2 cloning (producing cloned vectors), and we add a non-cloned positional embedding, the sum would not be a cloned vector — it would be a cloned token vector plus a non-cloned position vector, breaking the invariant before the first layer even runs. Cloning the positional embedding ensures `$e_{\text{token}} + e_{\text{position}}$` remains cloned.

---

#### The Noise-Augmented Symmetric Expansion

The base weight-duplication formulas create symmetries: in Case 3, each row of blocks in `$W_D$` contains identically structured sub-blocks. This means that at initialisation, groups of `$n$` output neurons (corresponding to different copies of the same source neuron) have identical incoming weights and identical outgoing weights. The concern, raised in prior work (Wang et al., 2023b), is that these duplicated neurons receive identical gradients and fail to diversify, effectively wasting the extra capacity.

**The noise mechanism.** The paper introduces paired noise terms in the Case 1 and Case 3 formulas: in each row of blocks, the noise terms sum to zero (e.g., `$+\eta_1$` and `$-\eta_1$` in the 2-fold case). The paper states (Appendix A) that "`$\eta_1$` and `$\eta_2$` are random tensors with reasonable magnitudes." The experiments in Section 3.4 use a **signal-to-noise ratio of 10 dB**, meaning the noise variance is set such that the ratio of signal power (from `$W_S/n$`) to noise power is 10:1.

The noise serves two purposes:
- **Symmetry breaking at initialisation:** Even before training starts, the duplicated neurons in each group have slightly different weights. This means their gradients will differ from the very first backward pass, encouraging them to specialise.
- **Function preservation maintained:** Because the noise terms sum to zero within each row, they cancel exactly in the forward pass. The initial forward computation is completely unaffected — the model still produces exactly the source model's logits.

**Why symmetric + noise rather than fully random expansion:** A fully random expansion (randomly initialising the new parameters while keeping original weights for existing dimensions) would break function preservation — the output logits would change. The paper's approach preserves the source model's accuracy exactly. The noise is a perturbation that doesn't affect forward computation but gives the optimiser a non-symmetric starting landscape.

---

#### Principal Component and Rank Analysis: Why the Model Doesn't Stay Low-Rank

A deeper mathematical concern is that the cloned weight matrices are **rank-deficient** at initialisation. Consider Case 3 with the symmetric construction (no noise, for clarity):

$$W_D = \begin{bmatrix} W_S/n & W_S/n \\ W_S/n & W_S/n \end{bmatrix}$$

The rank of this matrix is at most the rank of `$W_S$`, because every 2×2 block column is a scalar multiple of `$[W_S; W_S]$`. If `$W_S$` is full-rank with rank `$r = \min(d_{\text{out}}, d_{\text{in}})$`, then `$W_D$` in the 2-fold case has dimensions `$(2d_{\text{out}} \times 2d_{\text{in}})$` and rank at most `$r$` — whereas a randomly initialised matrix of that shape would have expected rank `$\min(2d_{\text{out}}, 2d_{\text{in}})$`. In the OLMO-2.9B example, **half the singular values are zero** at initialisation (Figure 6, "Before Training" panels for HyperCloning).

**The concern is that the model might remain in this low-rank regime after training**, failing to utilise its full parameter capacity. The paper provides empirical evidence that this does NOT happen.

**The evidence (Section 3.3, Figure 6):** the paper plots singular values of selected weight matrices in OLMO-2.9B before and after training, for both random initialisation and HyperCloning. The key observations:

- **Before training, HyperCloning matrices show half their singular values at zero** (the three leftmost panels of Figure 6, blue curves). This is exactly the rank-deficiency described above.
- **Before training, randomly initialised matrices show a smooth distribution of non-zero singular values** (orange curves), as expected from random matrices.
- **After training, the HyperCloning matrices show singular-value distributions that closely resemble the randomly initialised trained matrices** (the three rightmost panels of Figure 6). The zero singular values have been "filled in" — the matrices have achieved full rank.

**Why this happens:** the paper attributes the symmetry breaking to "random operations such as dropout" (Section 3.2, Section 4). During training:

1. Dropout randomly zeros out different subsets of neurons in each forward pass. Two neurons that would receive identical gradients (because their weights were initially identical) see different effective inputs and therefore accumulate different gradient contributions over time.
2. Stochastic gradient descent (via mini-batch sampling and data ordering) introduces additional randomness — even without dropout, different data samples provide different gradient signals to different neurons.
3. The noise initialisation (where used) provides different starting points, accelerating the divergence.

**Why this matters:** it demonstrates that the low-rank initialisation is not a permanent constraint — it's a **transient state** that the optimiser naturally escapes. The model starts with compressed (low-rank) knowledge from the source model and expands into higher-rank representations during training, using the full parameter budget. This is the key counter-argument to the concern that HyperCloning wastes parameters through duplication.

---

#### Expanding the Number of Attention Heads vs. Per-Head Dimension

The paper's experiments use different expansion strategies for different model families (Table 1):

- **OPT (350M → 1.3B):** heads expand from 16 to 32; `d_model` from 1024 to 2048; `d_head` remains at 64. This is **head-count expansion** (Strategy 2).
- **Pythia (460M → 1.4B):** heads expand from 16 to 32; `d_model` from 1024 to 2048. Also **head-count expansion**.
- **OLMO (1B → 2.9B):** heads stay at 16; `d_model` expands from 2048 to 4096; `d_head` expands from 128 to 256 (since `d_model` / `n_heads` = 4096/16 = 256). This is **per-head-dimension expansion** (Strategy 1).

**The architectural implication:** when `d_head` expands (OLMO case), the query/key rescaling factor `$\sqrt{d_S / d_D}$` must be applied. For OLMO, `$d_S = 128$`, `$d_D = 256$`, so the query weights are scaled by `$\sqrt{128/256} = \sqrt{1/2} = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$`. The paper implements this as a post-hoc scaling of the cloned query weight matrix.

When head count expands (OPT/Pythia case), no rescaling is needed because the attention computation per head is unchanged — each new head is an exact duplicate computing identical attention scores to its source counterpart.

**Design choice:** the paper doesn't systematically compare these two strategies for the same model. The choice in each experiment is dictated by the target architectures (OPT-1.3B, Pythia-1.4B, OLMO-2.9B), which have fixed hyperparameters. The paper's contribution is providing the cloning formulas for both strategies, not advocating one over the other.

---

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Symmetric block-duplication with 1/n scaling** over diagonal (zero-filled) expansion: the symmetric construction gives every output neuron gradient signal from every input copy, while the diagonal construction creates zero-gradient pathways that slow convergence (Section 3.4, Figure 7). The 1/n factor exactly compensates for the n-fold input replication.

- **Function preservation over approximate initialisation:** unlike staged training approaches (Shen et al., 2022) or random expansion (Du et al., 2024), HyperCloning guarantees the target model initially computes identically to the source. This means there is zero "recovery period" where the model must re-learn what the source already knew — training starts from a floor of source-model accuracy.

- **Width expansion over depth expansion:** explicitly positioned as complementary, not competitive. The paper acknowledges depth expansion is well-studied and effective, but argues width expansion is an under-explored dimension that can be combined with depth growth.

- **Noise as optional symmetry-breaker:** the paper includes noise mechanisms in the formulas but finds (Section 3.4) that symmetric expansion without noise performs equivalently well, likely because dropout provides sufficient symmetry breaking during training. The paper opts for the noise-free version "to avoid having to tune an extra hyper-parameter, the signal-to-noise ratio."

- **Unchanged training loop (design goal 4):** all complexity is confined to the one-time initialisation step. The training code, hyperparameters, distributed setup, and data pipeline are identical to standard pre-training. This is a practical engineering consideration — modifying the training loop in a large-scale distributed setting introduces risk and overhead that the paper deliberately avoids.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Model Growth as Function-Preserving Hidden-Dimension Expansion

The dominant intellectual framework for model growth prior to this paper was **depth expansion**: take a pre-trained transformer, duplicate layer blocks, and train the deeper model, exploiting residual connections so the expanded network initially approximates the source function. This approach was validated extensively — Gong et al. (2019), Samragh et al. (2023), Yang et al. (2020), and others demonstrated it works — and a comprehensive 2024 survey by Du et al. explicitly concluded that "depth growth achieves the best accuracies, while non-function-preserving width growth results in poorer performance."

HyperCloning inverts this conventional wisdom. The innovation is not the width-expansion mechanism per se (Net2Net introduced function-preserving width expansion for CNNs in 2015), but **the demonstration that function-preserving width expansion can reverse the negative verdict on width growth** that Du et al. arrived at. The key conceptual move is recognising that prior width-expansion approaches failed not because width is intrinsically less valuable to scale than depth, but because they abandoned function preservation — randomly initialising new parameters or setting them to zero, which forced the model to re-learn from scratch what the source model already knew, erasing the benefit of pre-training.

HyperCloning's function-preservation constraint — that the target model's logits exactly match the source model's at initialisation — is what makes width expansion competitive. Figure 2 shows that HyperCloning-initialised models reach the random-initialisation baseline's final accuracy 2.2× to 4× faster, and Figure 4 shows they exceed it at convergence. This is not an incremental improvement over random initialisation; it represents a qualitative shift in what width expansion can deliver. The paper's framing — that width and depth expansion are "complementary" rather than competing — is a conceptual contribution that opens the door to full-dimensional model growth (wider and deeper simultaneously) rather than forcing practitioners to choose one axis.

The distinction is fundamental rather than incremental: Du et al. (2024) closed the book on width expansion with a negative conclusion; HyperCloning reopens it with a positive result by changing the constraint (function preservation) rather than the operation (weight copying). This is a conceptual reframing, not a new mechanism.

### Innovation 2: Empirical Resolution of the Weight-Symmetry Concern Through Training Dynamics Analysis

A persistent theoretical objection to function-preserving expansion — raised explicitly by Wang et al. (2023b) and acknowledged as a legitimate concern in Section 4 — is that duplicating neurons creates weight symmetries that prevent the expanded model from utilising its full parameter capacity. The reasoning is straightforward: if two neurons have identical incoming and outgoing weights, they receive identical gradients and remain locked in synchrony, effectively wasting the additional parameters.

The paper's contribution here is not a theoretical argument for why symmetry should break — that argument existed (dropout, SGD noise). Rather, it is the **empirical demonstration, with diagnostic evidence, that the symmetry actually does break during training at the billion-parameter scale**. Section 3.2 (Figure 5) tracks cosine similarity between duplicated weight vectors over the course of training and shows that the initial similarity of 1.0 (perfect symmetry) decays substantially across most layers in all three model families. Section 3.3 (Figure 6) provides a complementary perspective via singular value spectra: HyperCloning-initialised matrices start with half their singular values at zero (rank-deficient by construction), but after training, the singular value distributions closely match those of randomly initialised and trained matrices — the model has achieved full rank.

This is significant beyond performance validation because it resolves a theoretical roadblock that had discouraged adoption of symmetric expansion methods. The concern was not that symmetry might slow convergence slightly, but that it might impose a permanent capacity ceiling — that the model would never effectively use the duplicated parameters. The paper's evidence shows this ceiling does not exist in practice for LLM pre-training with standard dropout. The fact that the noise-free symmetric variant performs equivalently to the noise-augmented variant (Section 3.4, Figure 7) further reinforces that training dynamics, not careful initialisation tricks, are sufficient to break symmetry.

This is a diagnostic contribution — it identifies a phenomenon (natural symmetry breaking under stochastic training) that was hypothesised but not previously demonstrated at scale, and it provides the measurement tools (cosine similarity trajectories, singular value comparisons) that future work can use to analyse similar methods. The finding that this happens "likely due to random operations such as dropout" (Section 3.2) is not a proof but a well-supported empirical observation with clear visual evidence.

### Innovation 3: The Training-Speed-vs-Initial-Accuracy Tradeoff Is Navigable Without Changing the Training Loop

A less obvious but practically significant conceptual move is the paper's insistence that the method must impose **zero changes to the training loop** (Section 2, design goal 4). This distinguishes HyperCloning from knowledge-distillation approaches (Xu et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2023), which modify the training objective and therefore require changes to the distributed training infrastructure. The paper recognises that in large-scale industrial training, the training loop is heavily optimised — tuned for specific hardware topologies, communication patterns, and memory constraints — and that modifying it introduces engineering risk and overhead disproportionate to the algorithmic change.

HyperCloning confines all complexity to a one-time, offline initialisation step that is computationally negligible (simple weight copying and concatenation). The training loop — optimiser, learning rate schedule, data pipeline, distributed communication, gradient accumulation — is untouched. This is an **architectural design insight** rather than an algorithmic one: the observation that knowledge transfer between models of different scales can be achieved purely through weight initialisation, without runtime penalties or objective-function modifications, if the initialisation satisfies function preservation.

The significance is practical but non-trivial. It means HyperCloning can be adopted by any team with access to a pre-trained small model and a target architecture, with no changes to their existing training infrastructure. This lowers the barrier to adoption compared to distillation-based methods, which require implementing and tuning a multi-objective loss (standard LM loss plus distillation loss) in a distributed setting. The paper frames this as an engineering concern, but it reflects a deeper insight: **initialisation and training are separable concerns**, and solving the knowledge-transfer problem entirely within the initialisation phase is both simpler and more broadly deployable than interleaving it with training.

This innovation is incremental in concept — many initialisation methods aim to improve training without modifying the loop — but fundamental in its application to the cross-scale transfer problem, where prior solutions (distillation, staged training) universally required training-loop modifications.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the **DOLMA** dataset provided by Groeneveld et al. (2024), an open-source corpus totalling approximately **2.4 trillion tokens**. The authors note that their training jobs "do not process this many tokens due to the extensive cost" (Appendix B). To ensure fair representation across sub-datasets, the data shards are shuffled with a fixed random seed held constant across all experiments, eliminating data-ordering as a confounding variable.

- **Base model(s).** The paper studies **three model families**: OPT (Zhang et al., 2023), Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023), and OLMO (Groeneveld et al., 2024). The base pre-trained models are **OPT-350M** (24 layers, 16 heads, d_model=1024), **Pythia-410M** (24 layers, 16 heads, d_model=1024), and **OLMO-1B** (16 layers, 16 heads, d_model=2048). The authors train their own OPT-350M base model on 30B tokens rather than using the HuggingFace version, because the public checkpoint contains extra linear layers that the target OPT-1.3B architecture lacks (Appendix B). For Pythia, the base model was pre-trained on the Pile dataset (~250B tokens), while for OLMO the base model was trained on the full DOLMA corpus (2.4T tokens). These three base models were chosen to represent different scenarios: same dataset with limited base training (OPT), different dataset with moderate base training (Pythia), and same dataset with extensive base training (OLMO).

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **average accuracy across 10 downstream tasks**, computed using the **LM Evaluation Harness** framework (Gao et al., 2023), an open-source, widely-used LLM evaluation tool. The 10 specific tasks are not enumerated in the paper, but the Harness framework typically includes question-answering, commonsense reasoning, and reading comprehension benchmarks. Accuracy is measured at multiple checkpoints during training to produce convergence curves (Figures 2, 4). For the singular-value and cosine-similarity analyses (Sections 3.2–3.3), the metrics are **layer-wise cosine similarity** between duplicated weight vectors and **singular value spectra** of selected weight matrices.

- **Baselines.** The single baseline used throughout is **standard random initialisation**, which represents the default pre-training workflow for language models. All other hyperparameters — learning rate, optimiser type, number of GPU nodes, batch size, context size, and training data order — are held identical between the random-initialisation and HyperCloning conditions (Section 3.1.1). The paper also compares alternative expansion strategies (Section 3.4) against each other and against random initialisation: **symmetric**, **diagonal**, **noisy symmetric**, and **noisy diagonal** cloning, where the diagonal variant corresponds to the method of Shen et al. (2022) in which off-diagonal blocks of expanded weight matrices are initialised to zero.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** Compute is measured in **training tokens processed**, not GPU hours or FLOPs. The paper reports convergence in terms of how many tokens are required to reach a given accuracy level (e.g., "250B tokens rather than 2.4T" for OLMO, Section 3.1.1). Speedup is defined as the ratio of tokens required by random initialisation to reach a specific accuracy versus tokens required by HyperCloning to reach the same accuracy (Figure 2). All models are trained on **64 GPUs** with varying batch sizes, context sizes, and learning rates summarised in Table 2: OPT-1.3B and Pythia-1.4B use batch size 2, context size 1024, max learning rate 1.5E-4, processing ~65K tokens per iteration; OLMO-2.9B uses batch size 2, context size 2048, max learning rate 3E-4, processing ~82K tokens per iteration. The optimiser is AdamW with weight decay 0.05, β1=0.9, β2=0.999, gradient accumulation over 16 steps, and ZeRO-2 gradient sharding (Rajbhandari et al., 2020).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not describe a formal cross-validation procedure. All experiments appear to use single training runs with fixed data ordering (controlled via a constant shuffle seed). The paper does not report confidence intervals, error bars, or statistical significance tests on accuracy measurements. The singular-value and cosine-similarity analyses (Figures 5, 6) are computed at the end of training for single runs.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Convergence Speedup: HyperCloning vs. Random Initialisation

The central quantitative claim is that HyperCloning provides a **2.2× to 4× speedup** in training convergence compared to random initialisation, while also improving final accuracy. These results are presented in Figure 2 (average accuracy over 10 tasks vs. training tokens) and Figure 4 (per-benchmark breakdown at convergence).

**OPT-1.3B (Figure 2a, Figure 4a).** HyperCloning starts at a substantially higher accuracy than random initialisation — approximately 50% average accuracy at token 0 (inherited from the OPT-350M base model) vs. ~25% for random initialisation. HyperCloning reaches the random baseline's final accuracy (~53–54%) in roughly **30–40B tokens**, compared to ~140B tokens for random — a speedup exceeding **3×**. At the end of the reported training run (~140B tokens), HyperCloning achieves approximately **57% average accuracy** vs. ~54% for random initialisation, a gain of approximately **3 percentage points** (Figure 2a). The per-benchmark breakdown in Figure 4a shows HyperCloning outperforming random initialisation on 8 of 10 tasks, with particularly large gains on tasks like ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, and PIQA.

**Pythia-1.4B (Figure 2b, Figure 4b).** The speedup is the largest of the three models. HyperCloning starts at approximately 48% accuracy vs. ~26% for random. It matches random initialisation's final accuracy (~50%) in roughly **30B tokens** vs. ~120B tokens for random — approximately **4× speedup**. At the end of training (~140B tokens), HyperCloning reaches approximately **53%** vs. ~50% for random initialisation (Figure 2b). Figure 4b confirms HyperCloning outperforms random on 9 of 10 benchmarks. Notably, this is despite the base model (Pythia-410M) being trained on a different dataset (the Pile) than the target model (trained on DOLMA), demonstrating that the transferred knowledge is not merely dataset-specific.

**OLMO-2.9B (Figure 2c, Figure 4c).** This is the largest-scale experiment and shows the most dramatic initial advantage. HyperCloning starts at approximately **59% average accuracy** — essentially at the OLMO-1B base model's final accuracy after 2.4T tokens of training — while random initialisation starts near zero. However, Figure 2c reveals a significant complication: HyperCloning exhibits **catastrophic forgetting at the beginning of training**, with accuracy dropping from ~59% to ~52% in the first ~50B tokens before recovering. Despite this dip, HyperCloning matches random initialisation's final accuracy (~55%) in roughly **120B tokens** vs. ~500B tokens for random — approximately **4× speedup** if measured from the start of training, or roughly **2.2×** if measured from the post-forgetting trough. At the end of training (~500B tokens), HyperCloning reaches approximately **57%** vs. ~55% for random (Figure 2c). Figure 4c shows HyperCloning outperforming random on 7 of 10 benchmarks.

The paper explicitly flags this forgetting behaviour: "models initialized with HyperCloning tend to exhibit catastrophic forgetting at the beginning of training. This is evident in the training curve for the OLMO benchmark" (Section 3.1.1). The authors note that "with sufficient training, this forgetting can be compensated for" and that "understanding the underlying causes of catastrophic forgetting, identifying strategies to mitigate it, and exploring why HyperCloning continues to outperform random initialization despite its occurrence are valuable avenues for future research."

**Headline speedup summary:** The paper claims speedups ranging from 2.2× to 4× (Section 5), with the specific figures being approximately **3× for OPT**, **4× for Pythia**, and **2.2–4× for OLMO** depending on how the forgetting dip is accounted for (Section 1, Figure 2). The final accuracy improvements at convergence are approximately **3 percentage points** for OPT, **3 percentage points** for Pythia, and **2 percentage points** for OLMO (Figure 2).

#### Alternative Expansion Strategy Comparison

Section 3.4 compares four function-preserving expansion strategies plus random initialisation, evaluated on Pythia-1.4B (Figure 7). The key findings:

- **All function-preserving methods outperform random initialisation** in both convergence speed and final accuracy. By the end of training (~140B tokens), all four cloning variants achieve approximately 52–53% average accuracy vs. ~50% for random (Figure 7a, rightmost cluster of bars).
- **Symmetric and noisy symmetric are the best performers**, achieving nearly identical convergence trajectories and final accuracy (Figure 7a). The noise addition provides no meaningful benefit in the symmetric case, leading the authors to select the noise-free version as their default.
- **Diagonal initialisation performs the worst among cloning methods**, converging more slowly and reaching slightly lower final accuracy (~52% vs. ~53% for symmetric). This gap is visible throughout training in Figure 7a. The noisy diagonal variant slightly outperforms the noise-free diagonal, particularly in early training, but still underperforms symmetric.
- **Per-benchmark breakdown** (Figure 7b) shows the symmetric variant outperforming diagonal on 8 of 10 tasks, with the largest gaps on ARC-Challenge and OpenBookQA. The diagonal variant does match or slightly exceed symmetric on a few tasks (e.g., Winogrande).

The paper attributes the diagonal variant's underperformance to "the presence of zero values in the expanded weight matrices" (Section 3.4), which create pathways where half the input dimensions contribute zero gradient to half the output neurons, effectively halving the learning signal per parameter in the early stages of training.

#### Effect of Source Model Accuracy

Section 3.5 examines how the source model's quality affects downstream target model performance, using OPT-350M checkpoints trained for 16B, 32B, and 64B tokens as source models for initialising OPT-1.3B (Figure 8). The key results:

- **More accurate source models yield better target initialisation.** At the start of training, the OPT-1.3B model initialised from the 64B-token checkpoint achieves approximately 53% average accuracy, vs. ~50% for the 32B-token checkpoint, ~47% for the 16B-token checkpoint, and ~25% for random initialisation (Figure 8a). This ordering reflects the source models' own accuracies.
- **The advantage narrows during training but does not disappear.** By ~140B tokens, all three HyperCloning variants converge to approximately the same accuracy (~56–57%), with the 64B-token source model retaining a slight edge (~0.5 percentage points). All three substantially outperform random initialisation (~54%).
- **Per-benchmark breakdown** (Figure 8b) confirms the same pattern: all three source checkpoints outperform random initialisation on 9 of 10 tasks. The differences among source checkpoints are small at convergence, typically within 1 percentage point.

This demonstrates that HyperCloning benefits from source model quality but is **not critically dependent on it** — even a relatively weak source model (16B tokens, roughly 3× less training than the 64B-token checkpoint) provides a substantial and durable advantage over random initialisation.

#### Effect of Source Model Size

Section 3.6 tests whether a larger, more capable source model provides better initialisation for a target model, using OPT-5.3B (a 4× width expansion of OPT-1.3B) initialised from either OPT-350M (via 4-fold cloning) or OPT-1.3B (via 2-fold cloning). The results (Figure 9):

- **OPT-1.3B as source outperforms OPT-350M as source**, achieving approximately 52% average accuracy at the start of training vs. ~44% for OPT-350M (Figure 9a). This is expected because OPT-1.3B is a more capable model.
- **Both source models substantially outperform random initialisation**, which starts at ~25% accuracy and remains below the HyperCloning curves throughout the entire training trajectory shown (~70B tokens).
- **The gap between the two source models narrows during training.** After ~70B tokens, OPT-1.3B-sourced initialisation reaches approximately 55% vs. ~53% for OPT-350M-sourced, a gap of ~2 percentage points (vs. ~8 points at initialisation). Random initialisation reaches only ~48% at this point.
- **Per-benchmark breakdown** (Figure 9b): OPT-1.3B-sourced outperforms OPT-350M-sourced on 7 of 10 tasks, with both substantially outperforming random.
- An important **4-fold cloning detail**: initialising OPT-5.3B from OPT-350M requires 4-fold cloning (since d_model goes from 1024 to 4096), demonstrating that the method extends beyond simple 2-fold expansion and works at higher expansion ratios.

#### Weight Symmetry Analysis

Section 3.2 tracks whether the duplicated weight structure in HyperCloning persists during training, using the average cosine similarity between duplicated weight vectors within selected layers (Figure 5). The analysis covers "up-project feed forward layers and the final unembedding layer" across all three model families.

**The key quantitative pattern** (Figure 5): At initialisation, all tracked layers show a cosine similarity of exactly 1.0 — perfect symmetry, as expected from the duplication procedure. As training progresses:

- In most layers, cosine similarity **decays substantially**, dropping to 0.2–0.8 after approximately 20B–30B tokens of training and stabilising thereafter.
- The rate and extent of symmetry breaking varies across layers within each model. In OPT (Figure 5a), the unembedding layer (green curve) retains higher symmetry (~0.7) than the FFN up-project layers (~0.3–0.5). In Pythia (Figure 5b), all tracked layers decay to 0.3–0.6. In OLMO (Figure 5c), the dynamics are more varied, with some layers decaying rapidly and others more gradually.
- Critically, **symmetry does not remain at 1.0 in any layer** — every tracked weight matrix shows measurable reduction from perfect duplication.

This evidence directly addresses the concern raised by Wang et al. (2023b) that "the symmetry of neurons in an expanded network suggests these neurons may not contribute independently to the model's learning." The paper's interpretation is that "this issue does not occur in our implementation, likely due to the randomness introduced by techniques such as dropout" (Section 3.2). The data in Figure 5 supports this: symmetry breaks naturally and substantially across all models and layers.

#### Principal Component Analysis of Weight Matrices

Section 3.3 provides a complementary perspective through singular value decomposition of trained weights, comparing HyperCloning-initialised and randomly initialised OLMO-2.9B models before and after training (Figure 6). The analysis examines three representative layers: block 0 up-project weights, block 3 QKV weights, and block 12 down-project weights.

**Before training:**
- HyperCloning-initialised matrices (blue curves in left three panels) show exactly **half their singular values at zero** — the expected rank deficiency from the n=2 symmetric duplication. The non-zero singular values mirror the distribution of the source model's weights (since each non-zero singular value in the source matrix produces exactly one non-zero singular value in the cloned matrix, with a smaller magnitude due to the 1/n scaling).
- Randomly initialised matrices (orange curves) show a smooth, continuous distribution with no zero singular values — the expected full-rank property of random matrices.

**After training:**
- The HyperCloning-initialised matrices (blue curves in right three panels) show singular value distributions that **closely resemble the randomly initialised, trained matrices** (orange curves). The zero singular values have been "filled in" — the matrices achieve full rank.
- In block 0 up-project weights (rightmost top panel), the HyperCloning singular value curve is essentially indistinguishable from the random initialisation curve. In block 3 QKV weights (rightmost middle panel), the distributions are similar but not identical, with HyperCloning showing slightly more mass in the larger singular values. In block 12 down-project weights (rightmost bottom panel), the distributions are comparable in shape.
- **No singular values remain at zero** in any of the post-training HyperCloning matrices.

This analysis provides the mathematical evidence that HyperCloning's rank deficiency is a **transient initialisation artefact**, not a permanent structural constraint. The model does "effectively utilize its parameter space during learning, comparable to a network trained from scratch" (Section 3.3).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Symmetric vs. diagonal expansion (Section 3.4, Figure 7):** The diagonal variant — which initialises off-diagonal blocks to zero, following the approach of Shen et al. (2022) — converges more slowly than the symmetric variant and achieves slightly lower final accuracy (~52% vs. ~53% on Pythia-1.4B after ~140B tokens). The performance gap is attributable to the zero-initialised cross-connections, which receive no gradient signal from one copy of the input in the early stages of training. The noisy diagonal variant partially recovers this gap but remains below symmetric performance.

- **Noise vs. no-noise symmetric expansion (Section 3.4, Figure 7):** Adding 10 dB signal-to-noise ratio noise to the duplicated weight blocks produces nearly identical convergence trajectories and final accuracy as the noise-free symmetric variant. The paper concludes that noise addition is unnecessary — dropout provides sufficient symmetry breaking — and opts for the noise-free version as the default to avoid tuning the extra signal-to-noise hyperparameter.

- **Function preservation under noise (Section 3.4):** All four expansion strategies tested (symmetric, diagonal, noisy symmetric, noisy diagonal) are described as "function-preserving" — the noise terms cancel in the forward pass because they are added in +/− pairs. The paper does not explicitly verify this preservation empirically (e.g., by measuring KL divergence between source and target logits at initialisation), but the formulas in Appendix A guarantee it mathematically.

- **Cross-dataset transfer (Pythia experiment, Figure 2b and 4b):** The Pythia base model was trained on the Pile dataset, while the target Pythia-1.4B was trained on DOLMA — a different corpus. Despite this distribution mismatch, HyperCloning still achieves the largest speedup among the three model families (~4×). This demonstrates robustness to dataset shift: the transferred knowledge generalises across training corpora, suggesting the method captures architectural and representational priors, not merely dataset-specific statistics.

- **Catastrophic forgetting in OLMO (Figure 2c):** The OLMO-2.9B model initialised via HyperCloning experiences a ~7 percentage point accuracy drop in the first ~50B tokens of training (from ~59% to ~52%) before recovering. This is a significant negative result that the paper does not fully explain, flagging it as future work. It demonstrates that function preservation at initialisation does not guarantee monotonic accuracy improvement during early training — the optimiser can and does move the model away from the preserved function before finding a better solution.

- **4-fold cloning (Section 3.6, Figure 9):** Expanding OPT-350M (d_model=1024) to OPT-5.3B (d_model=4096) requires 4-fold cloning, demonstrating that the method generalises beyond the 2-fold case. The results show that 4-fold cloning from a smaller source (OPT-350M) underperforms 2-fold cloning from a larger source (OPT-1.3B) but still substantially outperforms random initialisation, confirming that the cloning procedure is valid at higher expansion ratios.

- **Source model training duration (Section 3.5, Figure 8):** Cloning from a model trained for 16B tokens (OPT-350M checkpoint) vs. 64B tokens produces a measurable gap of ~6 percentage points at initialisation and ~0.5 percentage points at convergence. This shows that source model quality matters but its effect diminishes with extended target model training — a form of robustness to source model strength.

- **Layer-wise symmetry breaking (Section 3.2, Figure 5):** The rate and extent of symmetry breaking varies significantly across layers. In OPT, the unembedding layer retains substantially higher cosine similarity (~0.7 after training) than FFN up-project layers (~0.3–0.5). The paper does not investigate why some layers break symmetry more readily than others, but the observation suggests that not all duplicated parameters are used equally — some layers may benefit more from the duplicated capacity than others.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the evidence support the claim that HyperCloning provides 2.2×–4× training speedup?

The paper's speedup claims are based on comparing the number of training tokens required for HyperCloning-initialised models to reach the final accuracy achieved by randomly initialised models (Figure 2). The methodology for these claims has several strengths: the comparison is done at matched hyperparameters, identical data ordering, and across three different model families.

However, the speedup numbers reported are **qualitative estimates from visual inspection of convergence curves** rather than precisely computed metrics. The paper states "a speedup ranging from 2.2× to 4×" (Section 3.1.1) without specifying the exact accuracy threshold used for the comparison, how interpolation was performed between evaluation checkpoints, or whether the comparison uses average accuracy across all 10 tasks or a subset. In Figure 2a (OPT), the vertical dashed line indicating where HyperCloning reaches random's final accuracy is positioned visually; the paper does not report the precise token count at that intersection. The speedup claims should be understood as approximate indicators of the method's benefit rather than precisely reproducible metrics.

A more fundamental concern: the comparison measures speedup relative to **random initialisation's final accuracy at the end of the reported training run**, not at convergence. If random initialisation were trained longer (beyond the ~140B tokens shown for OPT and Pythia, or ~500B tokens for OLMO), it might continue improving. The reported speedups could therefore be **inflated if random initialisation was stopped before full convergence**. The paper does not demonstrate that the random initialisation baselines have plateaued — the curves in Figure 2 for OPT and Pythia appear to still be rising slowly at the right edge of the plots. This means the speedup numbers may reflect a combination of (a) genuine faster convergence and (b) simply stopping the baseline before it finishes training.

Additionally, the speedup metric is **measured only in tokens, not wall-clock time or GPU hours**. Since HyperCloning-initialised models and randomly initialised models have the same architecture (only the initial weights differ), the per-token training cost is identical. Token-based speedup should therefore translate directly to cost savings, but this equivalence is not explicitly verified (e.g., by reporting actual GPU hours consumed).

The OLMO speedup is further complicated by the **catastrophic forgetting dip**. If speedup is measured from the post-forgetting trough (~52% at ~50B tokens) rather than from the initial HyperCloning accuracy (~59%), the speedup drops from approximately 4× to approximately 2.2×. The paper cites both numbers without clarifying which represents the "true" benefit for a practitioner who would experience the dip.

**Verdict:** The speedup claims are directionally correct — HyperCloning clearly trains faster — but the specific multipliers (2.2×–4×) should be treated as approximate, potentially overestimated if the random baselines were undertrained, and complicated by the forgetting phenomenon in the OLMO case.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that HyperCloning improves final accuracy?

The paper claims HyperCloning "enhances both training speed and final accuracy" (Section 1) and the conclusion states it achieves "better final accuracy."

The evidence for improved final accuracy is **modest in absolute terms** but consistent across experiments. At the end of the reported training runs: OPT shows ~57% vs. ~54% (+3 percentage points, Figure 2a), Pythia shows ~53% vs. ~50% (+3 points, Figure 2b), and OLMO shows ~57% vs. ~55% (+2 points, Figure 2c). These improvements are small — within the range that could be affected by run-to-run variance — and the paper does not report multiple training runs or confidence intervals to establish statistical significance.

A critical limitation is that **all experiments use single training runs with no replication**. The paper does not report error bars, standard deviations, or significance tests on any accuracy measurement. Given that LLM training is known to exhibit substantial variance across random seeds (even with fixed data ordering, different initialisations interact differently with the optimiser trajectory), a single run per condition cannot distinguish a genuine improvement from random variation. The 2–3 percentage point final accuracy gains could potentially be explained by seed variance alone.

Furthermore, the "final" accuracy is measured at the end of the reported training budget, not at true convergence. If HyperCloning converges faster but both methods asymptote to the same accuracy with sufficient training, then the "better final accuracy" claim is an artefact of stopping the baseline early. The paper does not demonstrate that the accuracy gap persists if both models are trained to convergence.

**Verdict:** The evidence suggests HyperCloning may provide small final accuracy improvements, but the magnitude (2–3 percentage points) is modest, the statistical significance is unknown (single runs, no error bars), and the possibility that the gap would close with longer training is not ruled out.

#### Does the evidence support the claim that the method works across model families?

Yes — with the qualification that all three model families share the same fundamental architecture (decoder-only transformers) and the paper does not test on encoder-decoder, encoder-only, or mixture-of-experts architectures. The method is validated on OPT, Pythia, and OLMO, which represent different design choices (head dimension vs. head count expansion, different normalisation schemes, different training data regimes). The consistent speedup across all three families is the paper's strongest evidence for generality.

However, **all experiments are at the 1–5 billion parameter scale**. The paper's largest target model is OLMO-2.9B. The introduction motivates the method with the cost of training 12-billion-parameter models and larger, but no experiments are conducted at that scale. Whether the method's benefits scale to 10B+, 70B+, or 100B+ models — where pre-training costs are most prohibitive and where the method would be most valuable — is untested. The forgetting phenomenon observed in OLMO-2.9B might become more severe at larger scales, and the speedup might diminish if the relative benefit of a warm start decreases as total compute budgets grow.

#### What experiments are missing?

Several experiments would have substantially strengthened the paper:

1. **Multiple training runs per condition with error bars.** Given the small final accuracy differences claimed, replication is essential to establish that the improvements are not due to seed variance.

2. **Training the random baselines to convergence.** Running the randomly initialised models for 2×–3× longer would determine whether the final accuracy gap persists or closes, and would provide a cleaner speedup measurement (time to convergence rather than time to match an arbitrary endpoint).

3. **Direct comparison to depth-based model growth.** The paper positions HyperCloning as "complementary" to depth expansion methods (Gong et al., 2019; Samragh et al., 2023) but never tests this combination. A head-to-head comparison of width expansion alone vs. depth expansion alone, at matched parameter counts, would clarify the relative value of each axis. A combined width+depth expansion experiment would test the paper's "complementary" claim.

4. **Larger-scale experiments.** Testing at the 7B–13B parameter scale would address whether the method's benefits persist where they matter most practically (the regime where pre-training costs are truly prohibitive).

5. **Ablation of the attention-score rescaling factor.** In the OLMO experiment (per-head-dimension expansion), the query weights are rescaled by `sqrt(d_S / d_D)`. The paper does not ablate whether this rescaling is necessary — an experiment without the rescaling would quantify its importance for function preservation and downstream training dynamics.

6. **Measurement of function preservation fidelity.** The paper claims mathematical function preservation but never empirically verifies it — e.g., by measuring KL divergence between source and target model output distributions at initialisation, or by reporting the target model's initial accuracy on the 10 benchmark tasks to confirm it matches the source model's accuracy. This would catch implementation errors and validate the mathematical derivation in practice.

7. **Comparaison of head-count vs. per-head-dimension expansion for the same model.** The paper uses different strategies for different model families but never compares them on the same architecture, leaving unclear which strategy (if either) is preferable when both are architecturally feasible.

#### Catastrophic forgetting: an under-explored negative result

The OLMO forgetting phenomenon — a ~7 percentage point accuracy drop in the first ~50B tokens — is the most intriguing and under-explained result in the paper. The paper identifies it clearly (Figure 2c) and flags it as future work, but several observations are worth noting:

- The forgetting does **not** occur in the OPT or Pythia experiments (Figures 2a and 2b), where HyperCloning accuracy is flat or slightly increasing in the first ~10B tokens. This suggests the phenomenon is not inherent to HyperCloning but depends on specific training conditions. Possible factors include: the OLMO source model was trained for far longer (2.4T tokens vs. 30B for OPT or ~250B for Pythia), the learning rate for OLMO-2.9B is higher (3E-4 vs. 1.5E-4), and the per-head-dimension expansion (OLMO) vs. head-count expansion (OPT/Pythia) might interact differently with the optimiser.
- The paper does not analyse **what** is being forgotten — e.g., by measuring per-task accuracy trajectories to see if specific capabilities are lost while others are retained. This would provide mechanistic insight into the phenomenon.
- The fact that the model eventually recovers and surpasses random initialisation is encouraging but doesn't eliminate the concern: if the forgetting dip were deeper or the recovery slower in larger models, the practical benefit of HyperCloning could be substantially reduced. A method that avoids the forgetting dip entirely (e.g., by freezing cloned weights for the first N steps, or by using a lower initial learning rate) would be valuable but is not explored.

The forgetting result is a **genuine weakness** that the paper is transparent about but does not resolve. It complicates the simple narrative of "HyperCloning gives you a 4× speedup" by showing that the speedup depends on navigating an initial accuracy loss that varies across settings.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Forgetting Phenomenon: Function Preservation Does Not Prevent Early-Training Accuracy Loss

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire HyperCloning framework is built around function preservation — the guarantee that at initialisation, the target model produces exactly the same logits as the source model on every input. The implicit promise is that this warm start translates into a monotonic or near-monotonic advantage: training begins from the source model's accuracy and improves from there. The OLMO experiment violates this assumption dramatically. The paper states explicitly (Section 3.1.1):

> "models initialized with HyperCloning tend to exhibit catastrophic forgetting at the beginning of training. This is evident in the training curve for the OLMO benchmark."

The OLMO-2.9B model, initialised from the OLMO-1B checkpoint trained on 2.4T tokens, loses approximately **7 percentage points of average accuracy** in the first ~50B tokens of training (Figure 2c), dropping from ~59% to ~52% before recovering. The paper acknowledges that "understanding the underlying causes of catastrophic forgetting, identifying strategies to mitigate it, and exploring why HyperCloning continues to outperform random initialization despite its occurrence are valuable avenues for future research."

**The consequence.** The practical consequence is serious: a practitioner who trains a HyperCloning-initialised model and evaluates it during the forgetting trough would observe substantially *worse* performance than the source model they started with — and potentially worse than a randomly initialised model trained for the same number of tokens. This undermines the core value proposition. The method is supposed to accelerate training and reduce cost, but if a large fraction of the training budget is spent simply recovering lost ground (re-learning capabilities the source model already possessed), the effective speedup is substantially reduced. In the OLMO case, if measured from the post-forgetting accuracy trough rather than from initialisation, the speedup drops from ~4× to ~2.2×. The paper also provides no guarantee that the forgetting will reverse — it does in this training run, but the mechanism is unexplained, and there is no evidence that a practitioner would reliably observe recovery.

More subtly, the forgetting calls into question the paper's design philosophy. Function preservation was motivated as a way to avoid exactly this scenario — the target model should never perform worse than the source. The fact that standard optimiser dynamics can undo function preservation so quickly (within the first 1–2% of the training budget) suggests that **function preservation at initialisation is a weaker guarantee than the paper implies**. The optimiser does not respect the preserved function — it can and does move the model away from it before finding a better basin.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The forgetting is directly visible in Figure 2c (OLMO average accuracy curve, blue line dipping from ~59% to ~52% in the first ~50B tokens). It is also indirectly visible — though much less severe — in the OPT and Pythia curves (Figures 2a and 2b), where HyperCloning accuracy stays flat or increases very slightly in early training, rather than improving immediately. The phenomenon is most pronounced in the OLMO case, which is also the largest-scale experiment (2.9B parameters), the one with the most extensively trained source model (2.4T tokens vs. 30B for OPT and ~250B for Pythia), and the one using per-head-dimension expansion rather than head-count expansion. The paper does not ablate which of these factors causes the forgetting, nor does it measure *what* capabilities are being forgotten (e.g., by tracking per-task accuracy trajectories rather than only the average).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate the forgetting. It identifies it as a phenomenon, flags it for future work, and notes that recovery does occur with sufficient training. No strategies are proposed: no learning rate warmup or cooldown specific to the cloned weights, no freezing of certain layers, no gradual unfreezing schedule. The paper's default approach — drop HyperCloning weights into the target model and train with the standard hyperparameters — is shown to be vulnerable to this failure mode. The paper provides no diagnostic for predicting *when* forgetting will occur (e.g., based on source model training duration, expansion ratio, or learning rate), leaving practitioners to discover it empirically in their own settings.

### Single Training Runs With No Statistical Replication Undermine Final Accuracy Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper conducts exactly **one training run per condition** for every experiment. There are no replicate runs with different random seeds for any model, initialisation strategy, or baseline. The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, error bars, or statistical significance tests on any accuracy measurement. This is the default throughout the entire experimental section — Figures 2, 4, 7, 8, and 9 all present single-run results.

**The consequence.** The paper's claim that HyperCloning improves final accuracy (Section 1, Section 5) rests on differences of **2–3 percentage points** at the end of training (Figure 2): OPT ~57% vs. ~54%, Pythia ~53% vs. ~50%, OLMO ~57% vs. ~55%. These are small absolute improvements. In large-scale language model training, run-to-run variance from different random initialisations — even with identical data ordering — can easily produce differences of this magnitude. The paper itself uses random initialisation as a baseline and implicitly acknowledges that different random seeds produce different weights, yet does not control for this when comparing HyperCloning to random initialisation. A single random-initialisation run could be "unlucky" (a poor random seed that converges slowly or to a lower local minimum), making HyperCloning look better than it would against the average random-initialisation outcome. Similarly, a single HyperCloning run could be "lucky."

The consequence is that **the final accuracy claims are not statistically supported**. A practitioner reading the paper cannot determine whether the reported 2–3 percentage point gains are genuine method benefits or within the range of seed-dependent noise. For the speedup claims, the lack of replication is somewhat less problematic because the convergence gap is large and visible early (50–100% relative improvement at 30B tokens for OPT and Pythia) — but the final accuracy differences at convergence are small enough that replication is necessary to establish them as real.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All Figures (2, 4, 7, 8, 9) show single curves with no error bars or shaded regions. The paper does not mention random seed control or replication in the experimental setup (Section 3.1.1, Appendix B). The cross-dataset Pythia experiment (Figure 2b) shows the largest final accuracy gap (~3 points), which could be a genuine effect of distribution shift making random initialisation particularly slow, or could be seed noise. The OLMO experiment (Figure 2c) shows the smallest final accuracy gap (~2 points) and the noisy forgetting dynamics — both of which make seed variance a particularly salient concern.

**Mitigation status.** There is no mitigation. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not report variance estimates, and does not discuss the statistical power of its experimental design. The closest the paper comes to addressing variance is fixing the data-shuffling seed across experiments "to eliminate the impact of data ordering on our conclusions" (Appendix B) — but this controls only data-order variance, not initialisation-seed variance, which is likely the larger source of run-to-run differences.

### No Experiments Beyond the ~3B Parameter Scale Leave the Core Use Case Untested

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper motivates HyperCloning primarily by the prohibitive cost of training large models. The introduction cites the cost of training a 12-billion-parameter model (~72,000 GPU hours) and describes the financial burden as "staggering." The clear implication is that HyperCloning is most valuable at scale — for the models that are so expensive to train from scratch that any speedup translates into massive absolute savings. Yet the largest target model trained in the paper is **OLMO-2.9B**, roughly one-quarter the size of the 12B model cited in the motivation. The OPT-1.3B and Pythia-1.4B experiments are smaller still. No experiments are conducted at 7B, 13B, or larger scales.

**The consequence.** The paper cannot speak to whether HyperCloning's benefits persist, diminish, or change qualitatively at the scales where the method would be most impactful. Several failure modes become plausible at larger scales that are invisible at 1–3B parameters:

- **The forgetting phenomenon might scale with model size.** The OLMO-2.9B experiment showed the most severe forgetting of the three experiments, and it was also the largest model. If forgetting severity increases with scale (e.g., because larger models have more parameters to perturb during early training, or because the optimiser's effective step size relative to the loss landscape curvature changes), the speedup could degrade at 7B, 13B, or 70B parameters. A 10+ percentage point accuracy drop in a 70B model would be catastrophic.
- **The relative value of a warm start might diminish.** As total training budgets increase, the fraction of training spent "catching up" to where the source model already was becomes smaller. If a 1.3B model requires ~140B tokens to converge, HyperCloning saves ~100B tokens of that (Figure 2a). If a 70B model requires 2T tokens to converge, does HyperCloning still save ~70% of training time, or does the relative benefit shrink? The paper provides no scaling trend to extrapolate from.
- **The expansion ratio's effect is unexplored at scale.** The largest expansion tested is 4-fold (OPT-350M to OPT-5.3B, Section 3.6), but this experiment trains for only ~70B tokens and does not reach convergence. Higher expansion ratios (8×, 16×) — which would be needed to initialise very large models from small ones — might encounter qualitatively different dynamics (rank deficiency becoming harder to escape, symmetry breaking becoming slower relative to total training duration).

The consequence is that **the paper's primary motivation and its experimental evidence are mismatched in scale**. A practitioner with a 12B, 70B, or 100B+ model to train — exactly the person the introduction is written for — cannot confidently extrapolate from the paper's 1–3B results to their setting.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's own architecture table (Table 1) shows the largest target model is OLMO-2.9B. The largest model for which convergence is shown is also OLMO-2.9B (~500B tokens, Figure 2c). The OPT-5.3B experiment (Section 3.6, Figure 9) trains for only ~70B tokens and is clearly not converged (the learning curves are still rising). The paper provides no scaling trend analysis (e.g., plotting speedup as a function of target model size) that could support extrapolation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss this scale limitation. The abstract and introduction do not qualify the claims with model-size constraints. The only hint is in the model architecture descriptions (Section 3, Table 1), which show the maximum scale tested. A reader focused on the headline claims might not notice that the experiments are at a smaller scale than the motivation implies.

### The Diagonal Expansion Ablation Reveals a Design Choice That Matters More Than the Paper Acknowledges

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper presents HyperCloning as a specific method — symmetric block duplication with 1/n scaling — and validates it against random initialisation. In Section 3.4 (Figure 7), the paper compares several function-preserving expansion strategies and finds that the symmetric variant outperforms the diagonal variant (where off-diagonal blocks are zero, following Shen et al., 2022). The paper attributes this to "the presence of zero values in the expanded weight matrices" and selects the symmetric variant as the default.

**The consequence.** The diagonal variant is more than just an underperforming ablation — it represents a fundamentally different inductive bias. In the diagonal construction, each copy of the input connects to exactly one copy of the output; there are no cross-connections. This means that at initialisation, the expanded model is not just a wider version of the source — it is a model where the additional parameters are **completely disconnected from half the input dimensions**. The symmetric variant avoids this by giving every output neuron gradient signal from every input copy.

The paper's treatment of this difference is thin. It reports the final accuracy gap (~1 percentage point on Pythia-1.4B, Figure 7a) and moves on. But this misses the deeper tradeoff: the diagonal variant's poor performance suggests that **how you initialise the cross-connections matters a great deal** — and the symmetric variant, by making all cross-connections equal, imposes its own inductive bias (initially, all copies of the input are treated identically). The paper never explores whether the symmetric construction is optimal among function-preserving expansions, or whether the uniform-connection pattern it creates is actually beneficial for learning. It also does not explore whether the gap between symmetric and diagonal grows with expansion ratio (in 4-fold cloning, the zero blocks would be even more prevalent) or with model size.

Furthermore, the noise-augmented variants (noisy symmetric, noisy diagonal) are tested at only a single signal-to-noise ratio (10 dB). The paper does not sweep this hyperparameter, nor does it test whether the optimal ratio depends on model size, expansion factor, or training duration. The conclusion that noise is unnecessary — "the benefits of noise addition are minimal" — is based on one experiment at one SNR value and one model scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 7 provides the only comparison of symmetric vs. diagonal vs. noisy variants, on Pythia-1.4B with 2-fold expansion. The gap is visible but not dramatic (symmetric ~53%, diagonal ~52% at the end of training). The paper does not ablate across expansion ratios, model scales, or noise levels.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges that "further studies are necessary to fully understand the benefits of depth versus width scaling and function-preserving versus non-function-preserving transformations" (Section 4), but this is about the broader model-growth landscape, not specifically about the symmetric-vs-diagonal design choice. The paper opts for the symmetric noise-free version and does not recommend exploring the design space further. A practitioner who wants to push HyperCloning to larger scales or higher expansion ratios is left without guidance on whether the symmetric construction remains optimal or whether design choices that barely matter at 1.4B parameters become critical at 70B.

### The Method Transfers Architecture-Dependent Knowledge But Does Not Guarantee Transfer of Capabilities

**The assumption or constraint.** HyperCloning transfers **weights** — the numerical parameters of the source model — into the target architecture via a set of mathematically prescribed duplication rules. The guarantee is that the target model initially computes the same function as the source: identical logits for every input. The paper treats this as equivalent to transferring the source model's "knowledge" (Section 1: "the larger model already inherits the predictive power and accuracy of the smaller model before the training starts").

**The consequence.** Weight transfer and capability transfer are not the same thing, and the paper's experiments reveal a subtle but important gap between them. The Pythia experiment demonstrates this most clearly: the source model (Pythia-410M) was trained on the Pile dataset, while the target model (Pythia-1.4B) is trained on DOLMA — a different corpus. HyperCloning successfully transfers the weights (the target model starts at the source model's accuracy despite the dataset shift), and training on DOLMA improves from there (Figure 2b). This demonstrates that the transferred *architecture* provides a useful inductive bias even across datasets.

But this also reveals the limitation: HyperCloning transfers the **representational structure** learned by the source model, not the **specific capabilities** that depend on the source model's training data. If the source model learned dataset-specific facts or patterns that are absent from the target dataset, those capabilities are embodied in the initial weights but may be overwritten during target training — the forgetting phenomenon is one manifestation of this. Conversely, if the target dataset requires capabilities the source model never developed (because the source was smaller or differently trained), HyperCloning provides no mechanism for acquiring them except standard training.

More subtly, HyperCloning assumes that the **optimal representational structure for a small model is also a good starting point for a larger model**. This is an untested assumption. It is possible that the inductive biases that serve a 350M-parameter model well (e.g., certain attention patterns, certain feature detectors) are suboptimal for a 1.3B-parameter model — that the larger model would be better off learning a different kind of representation from scratch. The paper provides no evidence against this; the fact that HyperCloning-initialised models converge faster is consistent with both "good transfer" and "the initialisation is in a better-connected region of the loss landscape even if the specific features are suboptimal."

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The Pythia cross-dataset experiment (Figures 2b, 4b) is the primary evidence for the method's robustness. The catastrophic forgetting in OLMO (Figure 2c) is evidence that weight transfer does not prevent capability loss. The paper does not analyse which specific capabilities transfer and which are lost — it reports only average accuracy across 10 tasks, not per-task trajectories that would reveal differential transfer. The singular value analysis (Figure 6) shows that weight structure changes dramatically during training (the rank deficiency is filled in), but this does not reveal whether the pre-training structure was useful or merely not harmful.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It treats weight transfer as equivalent to knowledge transfer throughout, and does not discuss the possibility that source model representations might be suboptimal initialisation for a larger target. The paper's experiments with different source model accuracies (Section 3.5) and sizes (Section 3.6) show that "better source → better target initialisation," which is consistent with the weight-transfer-as-knowledge-transfer assumption — but does not rule out the possibility that a very different initialisation strategy (non-function-preserving, or preserving different properties) could be even better.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

HyperCloning does not introduce a new training algorithm, a new architecture, or a new objective function. What it changes is the **starting point**. The paper's central methodological contribution is a precise, mathematically-verified recipe for mapping the weights of a small pre-trained decoder-only transformer onto a wider architecture such that the larger model initially computes exactly the same function. This is not a paradigm shift — the core idea of function-preserving width expansion dates to Net2Net in 2015 — but it is a **reframing with practical consequences** that the field had not fully internalised.

The reframing is this: **pre-training a large model and pre-training a small model are not independent events**. The conventional workflow treats them as such — you pick a target scale, randomly initialise, and train. If you later want a larger model, you start over from scratch. HyperCloning makes the case, with empirical evidence at the billion-parameter scale, that the small model's training compute is not wasted when you scale up — it can be directly transferred into the larger model's initialisation, giving the large model a head start proportional to the small model's quality. This changes the economics of the scaling pipeline: instead of a single expensive training run at the target scale, organisations can invest in progressively larger models, each building on the previous one's weights.

The magnitude of this shift is **incremental but under-appreciated**. The paper is not claiming to halve the total cost of training a 70B model. The speedups demonstrated — roughly 2× to 4× in tokens to reach a given accuracy — are meaningful but not transformative in absolute terms. The more significant conceptual change is in how researchers and engineers should think about **model development as a cumulative process**. Prior to this work, the dominant view (reinforced by Du et al., 2024's conclusion that non-function-preserving width growth performs poorly) was that scaling model width was best done from scratch or via depth expansion. HyperCloning demonstrates that function-preserving width expansion reverses this negative verdict, opening an axis of model growth that the field had prematurely dismissed.

The paper also provides a **reconciliation of conflicting signals in the model-growth literature**. Du et al. (2024) concluded that depth growth beats width growth. Wang et al. (2023b) raised theoretical concerns about neuron symmetry under duplication. HyperCloning's empirical results — particularly the cosine similarity decay in Figure 5 and the singular value recovery in Figure 6 — show that these concerns, while theoretically valid, do not manifest as practical barriers under standard LLM training conditions with dropout. This does not invalidate prior work but clarifies its boundary conditions: width expansion fails when it is non-function-preserving, and symmetry concerns are mitigated by stochastic training regularisation.

One direction this work makes **less attractive** is the default assumption that pre-training must begin from random initialisation. The paper does not prove HyperCloning is universally better — the forgetting phenomenon in OLMO (Figure 2c) is a concrete counterexample to unqualified enthusiasm — but it shifts the burden of proof. The question is no longer "can we avoid random initialisation?" but "when should we, and what are the failure modes?" The paper provides partial answers and a diagnostic toolkit (cosine similarity tracking, singular value spectra) for answering the rest.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterising and mitigating the catastrophic forgetting observed in OLMO-2.9B.** The OLMO experiment (Figure 2c) shows a ~7 percentage point accuracy drop in the first ~50B tokens of training, which the paper flags as unexplained and unmitigated. A direct follow-up would systematically ablate the factors that might cause this: (a) source model training duration (OLMO-1B was trained for 2.4T tokens, far longer than OPT-350M at 30B), (b) learning rate (3E-4 for OLMO vs. 1.5E-4 for OPT/Pythia), (c) expansion strategy (per-head-dimension vs. head-count), and (d) model scale (does forgetting severity increase with target model size?). A strong follow-up would train several OLMO-2.9B variants from the same OLMO-1B checkpoint while varying one factor at a time, measuring per-task accuracy trajectories (not just the average) to identify *which* capabilities are lost during the forgetting dip. The mitigation experiments could test: freezing the cloned weights for the first N thousand steps, using a reduced learning rate for the first N steps, or applying learning rate warmup specifically to the cloned layers. A negative result — finding that forgetting is inherent to the method and not mitigatable — would be equally valuable in bounding HyperCloning's applicability.

**Combining HyperCloning with depth-expansion methods for full-dimensional model growth.** The paper explicitly positions width expansion as "complementary" to depth expansion methods like those of Gong et al. (2019), Samragh et al. (2023), and Yang et al. (2020) but never tests this combination. A natural experiment would start with a small pre-trained model, apply HyperCloning to double its width, then apply a depth-expansion method (e.g., block duplication) to increase its depth by 50–100%, producing a model that is both wider and deeper than the source. The key question is whether the benefits compound — does a width-and-depth expanded model converge faster than either alone, or do the two expansion axes interfere? The experiment should be conducted at matched total parameter counts: compare (a) width-only expansion, (b) depth-only expansion, and (c) width+depth expansion, all targeting the same final model size, with all three compared to random initialisation. The paper's OLMO architecture (16 layers, 16 heads) would be a natural starting point, targeting a 32-layer, 32-head model via combined expansion.

**Scaling HyperCloning to 7B–13B parameter models to test benefit persistence.** The paper's largest target model is OLMO-2.9B, yet the introduction motivates the method with the cost of training 12B-parameter models. The most urgent scaling question is whether HyperCloning's benefits persist at the scales where pre-training costs become truly prohibitive. A follow-up would replicate the core experiment at the 7B or 13B scale using available open-source base models — for example, HyperCloning from a publicly available 1.5B–3B model to a 7B–8B target architecture. The key measurements: (a) does the speedup factor (2×–4× in tokens) hold at larger scale, or does the relative benefit shrink as total training budgets grow? (b) does the forgetting phenomenon become more severe with scale, as the OLMO result hints? (c) does the final accuracy gap over random initialisation persist when both models are trained to convergence? This experiment would also need to address the compute cost pragmatically — possibly by measuring convergence speedup in the early-to-mid training regime rather than full convergence — but would provide the first evidence for or against the method's relevance at the scales the paper's motivation targets.

**Training a difficulty predictor or adaptive expansion schedule to reduce source model requirements.** The paper shows in Sections 3.5 and 3.6 that better source models yield better target initialisation, but also that the benefit of source model quality diminishes during training (the 16B, 32B, and 64B OPT-350M checkpoints converge to nearly the same accuracy after ~140B tokens, Figure 8). This suggests a potential inefficiency: the full cost of training a highly-accurate source model may not be necessary to reap most of HyperCloning's benefits. A follow-up would systematically vary source model training duration (e.g., 10B, 20B, 40B, 80B, 160B tokens) and measure the downstream target model's convergence speed and final accuracy at each source quality level. The goal is to identify the **point of diminishing returns** — the source training budget beyond which additional source tokens produce negligible downstream benefit. Equivalently, one could train a proxy model to predict, from early training curves of the source model, when the source is "good enough" for effective cloning. This would transform HyperCloning from a post-hoc use of whatever small model happens to be available into an optimisable decision in the overall training budget.

**Testing HyperCloning on non-decoder architectures and non-language domains.** The paper's cloning formulas are derived specifically for decoder-only transformers (Section 2, Appendix A), but the underlying principle — function-preserving width expansion via weight duplication with 1/n scaling — is architecturally general. The most valuable extension would test whether the method transfers to encoder-decoder models (T5-style) and encoder-only models (BERT-style), where attention patterns and cross-attention mechanisms introduce additional cloning considerations. A second axis is domain transfer: the paper evaluates on language modelling (MATH benchmark-style accuracy tasks), but the method should be agnostic to modality. Training a small vision transformer on ImageNet-21k and cloning it to a larger ViT for further training, or cloning a small code-generation model to a larger one for continued pre-training on code, would test whether the speedup generalises beyond natural language. A negative result in either architecture or domain — e.g., finding that encoder-decoder attention mechanisms break function preservation under naive cloning, or that vision models do not benefit from the transferred representations — would clarify the method's boundaries and motivate architecture-specific adaptations.

**Systematic analysis of which layers benefit most from cloned initialisation and which resist symmetry breaking.** Figure 5 shows significant variation in how quickly and completely weight symmetry decays across layers. In OPT, the unembedding layer retains ~0.7 cosine similarity after training while FFN up-project layers drop to ~0.3–0.5. This variation is not analysed: the paper reports it as an observation but does not investigate causes or consequences. A follow-up would instrument all linear layers in a HyperCloning-initialised model during training, tracking per-layer cosine similarity, gradient norm, and effective rank, then correlate these metrics with the layer's contribution to downstream task performance. The hypothesis is that layers which retain higher symmetry are those where the additional capacity is less useful — the model effectively "ignores" the duplicated neurons in those layers because the source model's original capacity was already sufficient. If true, this would suggest a more efficient variant of HyperCloning where only *some* layers are expanded (those where the model actually uses the extra capacity), reducing the parameter count of the target model without sacrificing accuracy. The experiment could be conducted as a post-hoc analysis of the already-trained OPT-1.3B, Pythia-1.4B, and OLMO-2.9B models.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Progressive model scaling in industrial R&D pipelines.** In an industrial setting where a team is developing a language model and plans to release multiple sizes (e.g., 1B, 3B, 7B), the standard approach is to train each from scratch — three independent, full-cost training runs. HyperCloning enables a **sequential pipeline**: train the 1B model first (lowest cost), clone it to initialise the 3B model (negligible cost), train the 3B model (benefiting from the 1B model's knowledge), clone the 3B to initialise the 7B, and train the 7B. Based on the paper's results, each successive model could require 2×–4× fewer tokens to reach a target accuracy compared to training from scratch. The cumulative savings across three model scales could be substantial — instead of paying for 1B + 3B + 7B training from scratch, the team pays for 1B training + (3B training / speedup) + (7B training / speedup). The Pythia experiment (Figure 2b, ~4× speedup) and the OPT-5.3B experiment with 4-fold cloning (Figure 9) provide direct evidence that this pipeline works even when source and target models differ substantially in scale. The key practical requirement is that the smaller models be trained first or already available — a constraint that fits naturally into the iterative model development workflows common in industry.

**Cost-efficient hyperparameter and architecture search at scale.** A major obstacle to systematic hyperparameter optimisation for large language models is that each trial requires a full pre-training run. HyperCloning offers a cheaper proxy: rather than training each candidate hyperparameter configuration on a randomly-initialised large model, train them on HyperCloning-initialised models and use early-training performance (e.g., validation loss after 10–20% of the full training budget) to rank configurations. The paper's convergence curves (Figure 2) show that HyperCloning-initialised models maintain a stable accuracy ordering with random-initialised models — the HyperCloning curves are consistently above the random curves, but their relative ranking across different configurations (had different hyperparameters been tested) would likely be preserved. A team could test 5–10 hyperparameter configurations on a HyperCloning-initialised model, select the best performer, and then train the final model from that configuration either from the cloned initialisation or, if maximum final accuracy is critical, from random initialisation with the now-validated hyperparameters. The key assumption — that the optimal hyperparameters for a HyperCloning-initialised model are similar to those for a randomly-initialised model — requires validation, but the paper's use of identical hyperparameters across conditions and the consistent speedup suggests the loss landscapes are sufficiently aligned.

**Rapid prototyping of larger architectures when a small pre-trained model is already available.** For research labs exploring new architectural variants (e.g., modified attention mechanisms, novel normalisation schemes, alternative position embeddings), testing at scale is often prohibitively expensive, so experiments are confined to small models where the results may not generalise. HyperCloning provides a middle path: take a pre-trained small model with a standard architecture, clone it into the experimental larger architecture (applying the cloning rules to the components that are shared, and randomly initialising or adapting the novel components), and train for a reduced budget. The shared components start from a strong initialisation, while the novel components learn from scratch. This allows the researcher to evaluate the experimental architecture at a larger scale than would be feasible with full random-initialisation training, while still being cheaper than a full from-scratch run. The OPT and Pythia experiments (Figures 2a, 2b) — where the base models were trained on different data or for limited durations — demonstrate that even imperfect source models provide meaningful acceleration, so the approach is robust to the source model not being perfectly matched to the target task or architecture.
