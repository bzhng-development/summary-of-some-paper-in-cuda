## 1. Executive Summary

This paper analyzes how Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) enables length generalization in LMs by revealing that RoPE implicitly performs Non-Uniform Discrete Fourier Transform on hidden states, creating periodic attention—but finds this periodicity is undermined by **Spectrum Damage** caused by linear layers and activation functions outside attention (spectrum leakage and distortion) as well as insufficiently trained low-frequency components inside attention. To address this, the paper proposes **Fourier Position Embedding (FoPE)**, which models each attention dimension as a Fourier Series rather than a single-frequency function and zeros out undertrained frequency components to preserve periodic extension, achieving more stable passkey retrieval accuracy at extended lengths—maintaining high accuracy beyond 2× training length where RoPE drops to zero—and outperforming RoPE-based baselines on summarization and question-answering tasks at 8k+ tokens, establishing that length generalization can be substantially improved by modifying attention's frequency-domain properties without sacrificing short-context performance.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Length Generalization Remains Brittle Despite RoPE

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **why do LMs with Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE) still struggle to generalize to sequence lengths beyond those seen during training, and what can be done about it?** This matters because the practical utility of LMs depends critically on their ability to handle longer contexts than they were explicitly trained on — a capability known as **length generalization** or **length extrapolation**.

RoPE (Su et al., 2024) has become the dominant position embedding scheme in modern LMs (e.g., the LLaMA family, Mistral, Qwen) precisely because it offers better length generalization properties than absolute position embeddings. Its core idea is elegant: apply position-dependent rotations to query and key vectors such that the dot-product attention weight between two tokens depends only on their relative distance, and the rotation is periodic — meaning the same relative distance produces the same phase shift regardless of absolute position. This periodicity should, in principle, allow models to process sequences of any length, since the rotation matrix simply cycles through the same values.

Yet in practice, RoPE-based LMs **do not generalize well to unseen lengths** without supplementary methods like YARN (Peng et al., 2023), NTK-aware scaling, or Self-Extend (Jin et al., 2024). As the paper's Figure 1(a) starkly shows: a 1.2B RoPE-based model trained on sequences of length 512 achieves essentially zero accuracy on Passkey Retrieval at 1024 tokens — just 2× the training length. This is not a gradual decline but a catastrophic failure. The model has not merely become less accurate; it has entirely lost the ability to use long-range information.

This gap between theoretical promise and empirical reality is the paper's central motivation. If RoPE truly produces periodic attention as the math suggests, why does the periodicity break down so dramatically in practice? The answer, the paper argues, is not in RoPE itself but in **everything that happens to the hidden states before they reach the attention mechanism**.

### Why This Problem Matters

The significance of length generalization extends far beyond academic curiosity. Several practical and theoretical concerns converge here:

**Resource constraints on training.** Training LMs on very long sequences is computationally prohibitive. The self-attention mechanism has quadratic complexity in sequence length, meaning a model trained on 8k-token contexts requires roughly 256× more computation per sequence than one trained on 512-token contexts. If models could be trained on short sequences and reliably generalize to long ones, the cost savings would be enormous. This is why the community has invested so heavily in extrapolation methods — training short and testing long is far cheaper than training long directly.

**Real-world applications demand variable-length inputs.** Production LLM systems encounter wildly varying input lengths: a user might ask a one-sentence question or paste an entire document. The model must handle both cases without performance cliffs when the input exceeds the training window. Current deployment practices often rely on truncation or sliding-window approaches at the boundaries of the training length, both of which lose information. True length generalization would eliminate this fragility.

**Theoretical understanding of position encoding.** Position embeddings are not merely implementation details — they fundamentally shape what kinds of sequential dependencies a transformer can represent. Understanding why RoPE's periodicity fails provides insight into the inductive biases of transformer architectures and the interaction between position encoding and the rest of the model. The paper's frequency-domain analysis offers a lens for diagnosing similar issues in other position embedding schemes.

**Contradictory evidence in the literature.** The paper is motivated by a tension in prior work: RoPE is widely considered a "length-generalizing" position embedding, yet practically all RoPE-based models require additional extrapolation techniques (interpolation, NTK scaling, YARN) to work at extended lengths. This suggests that something is corrupting RoPE's theoretical periodic extension, and prior work has not systematically identified what.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations in existing work along three axes:

**Position embedding design has focused on the attention mechanism in isolation.** Previous methods — whether absolute position embeddings (Vaswani, 2017), relative position biases like ALiBi (Press et al., 2021), kernelized approaches like KERPLE (Chi et al., 2022), or functional interpolation methods like FIRE (Li et al., 2024) — all modify position information *within* the attention computation. They assume that if the attention mechanism itself has good theoretical properties (e.g., periodicity, linear decay, functional smoothness), length generalization will follow. The paper challenges this assumption directly: it shows that **components outside attention — specifically linear layers and activation functions in the feedforward network — corrupt the frequency-domain structure** that makes RoPE's periodicity theoretically possible. Prior work has largely neglected these "outside" influences.

Consider ALiBi (Press et al., 2021) as a concrete example. ALiBi adds a position-dependent bias to the attention scores that decays linearly with distance — tokens that are far apart receive a strong negative bias. This produces remarkably stable perplexity across sequence lengths during pretraining (the paper acknowledges this in Section 5.2), but it fails catastrophically on tasks requiring long-range information retrieval: Figure 1(a) shows ALiBi accuracy on Passkey Retrieval declining linearly with sequence length, reaching near-zero well before 8k tokens. The linear decay that stabilizes perplexity also systematically suppresses long-range information. ALiBi solves one problem (perplexity stability) by creating another (inability to use distant context) — a tradeoff that stems from modifying attention scores without understanding the frequency-domain information flow.

**Extrapolation methods treat symptoms, not causes.** Methods like YARN (Peng et al., 2023), NTK-aware scaling, and CLEX (Chen et al., 2024a) apply post-hoc corrections to RoPE's frequency configuration — typically by rescaling frequencies so that the periodicity "stretches" to cover longer sequences. These methods are effective (the paper itself uses YARN as a baseline and shows FoPE can further improve on it), but they are essentially patches. They do not address the underlying mechanism — Spectrum Damage — that causes the periodic extension to degrade in the first place. The paper's approach is fundamentally different: rather than adjusting RoPE's frequencies to compensate for corruption, FoPE modifies the attention mechanism to be **robust to the corruption**, treating it as an inherent property of transformer architectures rather than an anomaly to be worked around.

The paper's Figure 4 demonstrates this distinction empirically. When a RoPE-512 model is extrapolated to 1024 tokens using YARN, accuracy on Passkey Retrieval improves but remains limited. When the same model is extrapolated using FoPE (applied post-hoc to the RoPE-trained model), the improvement is substantially larger. This suggests that the frequency-domain perspective captures something fundamental about length generalization that frequency-rescaling alone misses.

**No prior work has analyzed non-attention components from a frequency-domain perspective.** The paper's most distinctive contribution to the motivation is its use of **Discrete Signal Processing (DSP) theory** to model how the entire LM — not just the attention mechanism — affects the frequency-domain properties of hidden states. Section 3 provides the theoretical analysis that motivates FoPE: linear layers cause **Spectrum Leakage** (mixing frequency components across dimensions) and activation functions cause **Spectrum Distortion** (generating harmonic frequencies through nonlinearity). These are not minor effects — they fundamentally destroy the one-to-one correspondence between dimensions and frequency components that RoPE assumes.

This DSP-based analysis is, to the authors' knowledge, novel in the position embedding literature. Prior work has analyzed RoPE's frequency properties in isolation (e.g., studying how the base frequency $\theta$ affects the rate of decay), but has not modeled the full pipeline: hidden states → linear layers → activation functions → attention with RoPE → output. By formalizing this pipeline in frequency-domain terms, the paper identifies the precise mechanism by which length generalization fails: the periodic extension (Equation 5 in the paper, $h_m(n + N_{\omega_m}) = h_m(n)$) breaks when the coefficient $H_m$ contains contributions from multiple frequency components with mismatched periods, as shown in Equation 6.

**Under-trained low-frequency components are recognized but not theoretically explained.** The observation that RoPE's lowest frequencies (those with periods longer than the training sequence length) cause problems during extrapolation is not new — Peng et al. (2023) identified this as a key issue and proposed scaling strategies to address it. However, the paper provides a formal theoretical explanation using time-domain truncation analysis (Section 3.3): when a sinusoidal function is truncated by the training window, its spectrum in the frequency domain becomes convolved with a sinc function, producing noisy side-lobe components that dominate when the main component has a period too long to complete even one full cycle during training. This is Equation 10 in the paper, derived from standard DSP results:

$$X(\omega) = \alpha \delta(\omega_m) + \frac{\sin[(N - \alpha N_m)(\omega - \omega_m)]}{\omega - \omega_m}$$

where $\alpha = \lfloor N / N_m \rfloor$ and $N_m = 2\pi / \omega_m$. When $\alpha < 1$ (period exceeds training length), the impulse component $\alpha \delta(\omega_m)$ vanishes, leaving only the noisy sinc component. This theoretical grounding transforms the empirical observation ("low frequencies cause problems") into a mechanistic understanding ("time-domain truncation introduces spectral artifacts that destroy periodic extension"), which in turn motivates the specific solution: zeroing out frequencies below the floor frequency $\omega_l = 2\pi / N$.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself at the intersection of two research threads — **position embedding design** and **length generalization** — but with a distinctive methodological approach: the use of frequency-domain (Fourier/DSP) analysis as both a diagnostic tool and a design principle.

**Diagnostically**, the DSP framework reveals that length generalization is not merely about the attention mechanism's theoretical properties (as prior work implicitly assumed) but about the entire model's effect on the frequency-domain structure of hidden states. The paper's Figure 2(a) illustrates this visually: as signals pass through linear and nonlinear transformations, information from multiple frequencies mixes into a single dimension. Under RoPE, which treats each dimension as a single-frequency component, this mixing is invisible and unaddressed. FoPE acknowledges it by modeling each dimension as a Fourier Series — effectively saying "each dimension already contains multiple frequencies, so let's represent that explicitly."

**As a design principle**, the frequency-domain perspective leads to two concrete modifications that are conceptually simple but mechanistically motivated: (1) replace single-frequency encoding per dimension with a Fourier Series (a dominant frequency plus harmonics), and (2) zero out frequencies below the floor frequency. Neither of these modifications is empirically motivated — they follow directly from the theoretical analysis. The Fourier Series addresses the Spectrum Damage from outside attention; the zeroing-out addresses the Spectrum Damage from inside attention. This tight coupling between theory and design distinguishes FoPE from methods that adjust RoPE frequencies heuristically.

**Empirically**, the paper validates both its diagnostic claims and its proposed solution. The visualization in Figure 7 shows that under-trained frequency dimensions exhibit higher absolute activation values in q and k vectors — a positional bias that the theoretical analysis predicts should harm generalization. The toy model experiment in Table 2 confirms that normalizing q,k vectors (removing this positional bias) helps only when the position embedding contains under-trained frequency components. And the main results in Figure 1 show that FoPE maintains stable Passkey Retrieval accuracy at 2×, 4×, and 8× the training length while RoPE collapses — a pattern that directly supports the theoretical claim that preserving periodic extension is the key mechanism.

The paper does not claim to replace RoPE entirely. Rather, it positions FoPE as an **enhancement** that can be applied both during pretraining (as a drop-in replacement for RoPE) and as a post-hoc correction (applied to existing RoPE-trained models during continual pretraining). Figure 4 specifically demonstrates that FoPE can be applied to a RoPE-pretrained model and outperform YARN — positioning FoPE as both a new position embedding *and* an extrapolation method, bridging the two research threads.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper proposes a modification to how language models encode token positions for the attention mechanism, replacing RoPE's single-frequency-per-dimension approach with a Fourier Series that models the multi-frequency structure already present in hidden states due to neural network operations. The core problem it solves is that RoPE's theoretical periodic extension—which should enable attention to generalize to unseen sequence lengths—is corrupted because linear layers and activation functions mix frequency components across dimensions before they reach attention, and because extremely low frequencies never complete a full cycle during training. FoPE addresses both issues by (1) explicitly representing multiple frequencies per dimension to accommodate this spectral mixing, and (2) zeroing out frequencies with periods longer than the training length, substituting them with the zero-frequency component that has no positional bias.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The FoPE system modifies a single component within the transformer architecture—the rotary position embedding applied to query and key vectors before attention—while leaving all other model components unchanged. The architecture has three logical stages:

1. **Frequency computation (same as RoPE):** For each head dimension `$m$`, compute a base frequency `$\omega_m = 1 / \theta^{(2m/M)}$` where `$\theta$` is the RoPE base parameter (typically 10,000) and `$M$` is the head dimension. This yields a set of frequencies that decrease from near 1 to near 0 as `$m$` increases.

2. **Frequency modification (FoPE's additions):** Two operations transform RoPE's frequency set: (a) **Floor clipping**: Any frequency below `$\omega_l = 2\pi / N$` (where `$N$` is the training sequence length) is set to zero, removing frequency components whose periods exceed the training window. (b) **Fourier expansion**: Each remaining frequency dimension is expanded into a Fourier Series using a learned weight matrix `$\mathbf{W}^F \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times (M - M_0)}$` that combines the dominant frequency with `$D$` harmonic frequencies, producing `$D$` outputs per input dimension.

3. **Position embedding application:** The modified frequencies and the Fourier weight matrix together produce sinusoidal position encodings (sine and cosine values) that are applied to query and key vectors via element-wise multiplication, exactly as in RoPE but with the multi-frequency representation replacing the single-frequency one.

Information flows in a single path: hidden states arrive at the attention layer → they are split into query and key vectors → FoPE computes sinusoidal position features using the modified frequencies and learned Fourier weights → these features are applied to q and k via multiplication (rotating them in the complex plane) → attention scores are computed from the rotated q and k → the rest of the transformer proceeds unchanged.

This is a **theoretically-motivated architectural modification paper** whose core idea is that RoPE's length generalization fails because the attention mechanism receives hidden states whose frequency-domain structure has already been corrupted by upstream components, and that making attention explicitly aware of this multi-frequency mixing—rather than pretending each dimension contains a single clean frequency—restores the periodic extension that enables extrapolation.

#### RoPE as Non-Uniform Discrete Fourier Transform

The paper's theoretical framework begins by recasting RoPE in frequency-domain terms using Discrete Signal Processing (DSP). This is not merely a mathematical curiosity—it is the lens that reveals *why* length generalization fails and *what* needs to be fixed.

**RoPE's standard formulation.** Given a query vector `$\mathbf{q}_m$` and key vector `$\mathbf{k}_m$` for tokens at positions `$n_a$` and `$n_b$`, RoPE applies a position-dependent rotation in each dimension `$m$`:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{q}}_m(n_a) = \mathbf{q}_m e^{i \omega_m n_a}, \quad \tilde{\mathbf{k}}_m(n_b) = \mathbf{k}_m e^{i \omega_m n_b}$$

where `$\omega_m = 1 / \theta^{(2m/M)}$` is the frequency assigned to dimension `$m$`, `$\theta$` is the RoPE base parameter, and `$M$` is the total number of dimensions in each attention head. The exponential `$e^{i\omega_m n}$` is a complex-number representation of rotation by angle `$\omega_m n$`, implemented in practice via a 2×2 rotation matrix applied to pairs of dimensions.

The attention weight contributed by dimension `$m$` for the token pair with relative distance `$n = n_a - n_b$` is:

$$\tilde{h}_m(n) = \tilde{\mathbf{q}}_m(n_a) \tilde{\mathbf{k}}_m^*(n_b) = \mathbf{H}_m e^{i \omega_m n}$$

where `$\mathbf{H}_m = \mathbf{q}_m \mathbf{k}_m$` is the product of the (pre-rotation) query and key values for that dimension, and `$^*$` denotes complex conjugation.

The total attention weight between the two tokens is the sum over all dimensions:

$$h(n) = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} \tilde{h}_m(n) = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} \mathbf{H}_m e^{i \omega_m n}$$

**What this computes:** The attention score `$h(n)$` is a weighted sum of complex exponentials—sinusoidal functions of the relative distance `$n$`. Each dimension `$m$` contributes one sinusoidal term with frequency `$\omega_m$` and complex amplitude `$\mathbf{H}_m$`. The amplitude `$\mathbf{H}_m$` encodes how strongly the query-key interaction at that dimension responds to positional offsets at the wavelength corresponding to `$\omega_m$`.

**The DSP connection.** Comparing this expression to the Inverse Non-Uniform Discrete Fourier Transform (NUDFT):

$$x_n = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} X_m e^{i \omega_m n}$$

reveals a structural identity: RoPE's attention computation *is* an inverse NUDFT, where `$\mathbf{H}_m$` are the frequency-domain coefficients and `$h(n)$` is the time-domain (or rather, distance-domain) signal being reconstructed. The only difference from a standard DFT is that the frequencies `$\omega_m$` are non-uniformly spaced—they follow a geometric progression `$1/\theta^{2m/M}$` rather than the uniform spacing `$2\pi m/M$` of a standard DFT.

**Why this matters.** The NUDFT interpretation reveals three properties that are central to the paper's analysis:

1. **Periodicity.** Each component `$\tilde{h}_m(n) = \mathbf{H}_m e^{i \omega_m n}$` is periodic with period `$N_{\omega_m} = 2\pi / \omega_m$`, meaning `$\tilde{h}_m(n + N_{\omega_m}) = \tilde{h}_m(n)$`. Attention weights should therefore be periodic functions of relative distance, cycling through the same values regardless of absolute position.

2. **One-to-one correspondence.** An ideal NUDFT assumes each frequency `$\omega_m$` has exactly one coefficient `$\mathbf{H}_m$`. If this holds, the signal can be perfectly decomposed into independent frequency components, each responsible for propagating information at a specific wavelength.

3. **Low-pass property.** Because RoPE's frequencies span `$\omega_m \in (0, 1]$` with denser sampling near zero (due to the geometric spacing `$1/\theta^{2m/M}$`), RoPE acts as a low-pass filter: it preferentially samples low frequencies, which correspond to long wavelengths and thus long-range dependencies. The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem (Theorem C.1) states that a discrete sequence with sampling frequency `$\omega_s = 2\pi$` can only represent frequencies up to `$\pi$`, and RoPE's `$\omega_m \leq 1 \ll \pi$` means it operates entirely in the low-frequency regime.

**The idealization that breaks.** The critical assumption in this NUDFT interpretation is that each `$\mathbf{H}_m$` is a single, pure coefficient associated *only* with frequency `$\omega_m$`. If `$\mathbf{H}_m$` actually contains contributions from multiple frequencies—for example, `$\mathbf{H}_m = H_{\omega_m} + \sigma H_{\omega_o}$` where `$\omega_o \neq \omega_m$`—then the periodic extension property breaks. The function becomes:

$$h'_m(n) = H_{\omega_m}[(1 - \sigma) e^{i \omega_m n} + \sigma e^{i \omega_o n}]$$

and `$h'_m(n + N_{\omega_m}) \neq h'_m(n)$` because `$N_{\omega_m}$` is not a period of the `$\omega_o$` component. Information propagates through waves with mismatched wavelengths, producing inaccurate distance-dependent attention weights.

This is precisely what the paper identifies as **Spectrum Damage**, and it is what the rest of the theoretical analysis explains and what FoPE is designed to fix.

#### Spectrum Damage from Outside Attention: Linear Layers and Activation Functions

The hidden states that arrive at the attention mechanism have already passed through multiple transformer layers, each containing linear projections and nonlinear activation functions. The paper analyzes how these operations corrupt the frequency-domain structure.

**Spectrum Leakage from Linear Layers.** A linear layer with weight matrix `$\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times M}$` maps an `$M$`-dimensional hidden state `$\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^M$` to an output `$\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{R}^M$`:

$$Y_m = \sum_{k=0}^{M-1} W_{km} X_k$$

**What this computes:** Each output dimension `$m$` is a weighted sum of *all* input dimensions `$k$`. If the input dimensions carry different frequency components (as established by RoPE's NUDFT interpretation, where dimension `$k$` is associated with frequency `$\omega_k$`), then the output dimension `$m$` contains a mixture of all those frequencies.

**Why this destroys the one-to-one correspondence:** The coefficient `$\mathbf{H}_m$` that RoPE associates with frequency `$\omega_m$` is computed from the query and key vectors *after* they have passed through linear layers. If those linear layers have mixed frequency `$\omega_k$` into dimension `$m$`, then `$\mathbf{H}_m$` does not represent the strength of `$\omega_m$` alone—it contains leaked energy from other frequencies. This is **Spectrum Leakage**: different frequency components exhibit interplay because a single output dimension receives contributions from multiple input dimensions.

Crucially, this mixing is not a bug—it is fundamental to how transformers work. Linear layers are what give the model expressive capacity to transform representations between layers. You cannot remove them without destroying the model. FoPE's approach is therefore not to prevent the mixing but to make attention *aware* of it by modeling the multi-frequency structure explicitly.

**Spectrum Distortion from Activation Functions.** Activation functions introduce nonlinearity, which generates harmonic frequencies—frequency components at sums and differences of the input frequencies. The paper formalizes this in Lemma 3.1, which I present here with explanation.

**Lemma 3.1 statement:** Given a multi-frequency sinusoid input `$x(n) = \sum (a_\omega \sin \omega n + b_\omega \cos \omega n)$` and any time-independent nonlinear function `$g$`, the output contains frequencies that are linear combinations of the input frequencies:

$$g(x(n)) = \sum_{j \in \mathbb{N}} \sum_{k \in \mathbb{N}} a_{j,k} \cos(j\omega_1 + k\omega_2)n$$

for the two-frequency case, with generalization to any number of input frequencies.

**What this lemma means operationally:** If the input to an activation function contains sinusoidal components at frequencies `$\omega_1$` and `$\omega_2$` (which it does, because the hidden states carry RoPE's position-dependent rotations), the output will contain components at frequencies `$2\omega_1$`, `$2\omega_2$`, `$\omega_1 + \omega_2$`, `$\omega_1 - \omega_2$`, `$3\omega_1$`, `$2\omega_1 + \omega_2$`, and so on—potentially an infinite number of harmonics. These are entirely new frequencies that were not present in the input.

**Why this happens:** The Taylor expansion of any nonlinear function `$g(x) = \sum_{p \in \mathbb{N}} a_p x^p$` contains powers `$x^p$`. When `$x$` is a sum of sinusoids, raising it to the power `$p$` produces products of sinusoids. The product-to-sum formula for sinusoids:

$$\cos \alpha \cos \theta = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(\alpha - \theta) + \cos(\alpha + \theta)]$$

shows that multiplying two sinusoids produces sinusoids at the sum and difference frequencies. Higher powers produce more complex linear combinations. The paper provides a concrete example in Appendix C.1: for `$p=2$` with input `$\cos \omega_1 n + \cos \omega_2 n$`, the output includes `$1$` (DC component), `$\frac{1}{2}\cos 2\omega_1 n$`, `$\frac{1}{2}\cos 2\omega_2 n$`, `$\cos(\omega_1 - \omega_2)n$`, and `$\cos(\omega_1 + \omega_2)n$`.

**The compounding effect.** These two types of Spectrum Damage compound across layers. A hidden state leaving layer `$L$` has passed through `$L$` rounds of linear mixing (introducing leakage) and `$L$` rounds of nonlinearity (introducing harmonics). By the time it reaches the attention mechanism in layer `$L+1$`, each dimension almost certainly contains contributions from a wide range of frequencies. RoPE's assumption—that each dimension is a single-frequency function—is violated not just occasionally but systematically and accumulatively.

**Why this matters for length generalization.** When dimension `$m$` contains multi-frequency information, the periodic extension `$\tilde{h}_m(n + N_{\omega_m}) = \tilde{h}_m(n)$` fails because `$N_{\omega_m}$` is not the period of the leaked/distorted components. The attention mechanism computes distance-dependent weights using the wrong wavelengths for part of the signal, producing incorrect attention scores for positions outside the training range. This is the mechanism by which "mere" architectural components—linear layers, activation functions—prevent RoPE's theoretical length generalization from being realized in practice.

The paper's Figure 3 illustrates this concretely. The "Ground Truth" attention score (what the actual multi-frequency signal "wants" to produce) has a complex, multi-periodic pattern. RoPE's approximation—treating each dimension as a single frequency—produces a smoother but ultimately incorrect attention curve. FoPE's Fourier Series approach more accurately captures the multi-frequency structure.

#### Spectrum Damage from Inside Attention: Under-Trained Frequency Components

Even if the hidden states arriving at attention were perfectly clean single-frequency signals, there would still be a problem: frequency components with periods longer than the training sequence length are inadequately trained. The paper models this using time-domain truncation analysis.

**The truncation model.** During training, the model sees sequences of length at most `$N$`. Consider a dimension `$m$` whose assigned frequency `$\omega_m$` corresponds to a period `$N_m = 2\pi / \omega_m$`. If `$N_m > N$`, the sinusoidal function `$e^{i\omega_m n}$` cannot complete even one full cycle over the training window `$n \in [0, N]$`. The training data is effectively a truncated version of the infinite periodic function.

The paper models this truncation mathematically as:

$$x_m(n) = e^{i\omega_m n} \cdot \text{rect}(n)$$

where the rectangular window is:

$$\text{rect}(n) = \begin{cases} 1, & n \leq N \\ 0, & n > N \end{cases}$$

**The frequency-domain consequence.** Taking the Fourier Transform of this truncated signal (derived in Appendix C.2) yields:

$$X(\omega) = \alpha \delta(\omega_m) + \frac{\sin[(N - \alpha N_m)(\omega - \omega_m)]}{\omega - \omega_m}$$

where `$\alpha = \lfloor N / N_m \rfloor$` is the number of complete cycles that fit within the training window, and `$\delta(\omega_m)$` is an impulse at frequency `$\omega_m$`.

**What this equation means piece by piece:** The first term, `$\alpha \delta(\omega_m)$`, is the ideal component—an impulse at the intended frequency `$\omega_m$` with amplitude `$\alpha$`. The second term, `$\frac{\sin[(N - \alpha N_m)(\omega - \omega_m)]}{\omega - \omega_m}$`, is a sinc-shaped artifact introduced by the truncation—it spreads energy across neighboring frequencies rather than concentrating it at `$\omega_m$`.

**The critical dependency on `$\alpha$`.** When `$\alpha \geq 1$` (the period fits at least once within the training window), the impulse term `$\alpha \delta(\omega_m)$` dominates over the sinc artifact. The frequency component is adequately trained—its amplitude `$\alpha$` is at least 1, and the model has seen enough of the cycle to learn the correct mapping.

When `$\alpha = 0$` (the period exceeds the training length, i.e., `$N_m > N$`), the impulse term vanishes entirely. There is no `$\delta(\omega_m)$` component—only the noisy sinc term remains. The model has never seen even one complete cycle, so its representation of this frequency is dominated by truncation artifacts rather than the actual sinusoidal pattern.

**Intuitive explanation.** Imagine you're trying to learn what a sine wave looks like, but you're only shown a segment that's shorter than one full period. What you see is a monotonic curve that never repeats. You cannot possibly learn that the function is periodic, because the periodicity is not visible in your training data. When you later try to extrapolate beyond the training window, any prediction you make will be based on an incorrect understanding of the function's structure.

**Why this causes problems at test time.** When the model encounters positions `$n > N$` during length extrapolation, the under-trained dimensions produce activations that are essentially extrapolating from noise. The sinc-like frequency spread means that these dimensions carry no reliable positional information for unseen positions. Moreover, the paper shows empirically (Figure 7) that these under-trained dimensions develop higher absolute activation values in q and k vectors—a form of positional bias that further degrades generalization because the model has learned to rely on these dimensions in ways that don't generalize.

**The paper's distinction from prior work.** Peng et al. (2023) observed empirically that RoPE's lowest frequencies cause extrapolation problems and proposed scaling strategies to address this. The contribution here is the *theoretical explanation*: the problem is not merely that low frequencies are "undertrained" in some vague sense, but specifically that time-domain truncation introduces spectral artifacts via the sinc term in `$X(\omega)$`, and these artifacts dominate when `$\alpha < 1$`. This theoretical grounding motivates a specific solution: remove these components entirely rather than trying to rescale them.

---

#### FoPE Mechanism 1: Fourier Series Per Dimension

FoPE's first modification addresses the Spectrum Damage from outside attention by replacing RoPE's single-frequency-per-dimension model with a Fourier Series. The intuition, developed from the analysis above, is that each dimension already contains multi-frequency information due to upstream mixing—so attention should represent this multi-frequency structure explicitly rather than pretending it doesn't exist.

**The mathematical form.** For each dimension `$m$` that survives the floor-frequency filter (i.e., `$\omega_m \geq \omega_l$`), FoPE models the attention contribution as:

$$h_m(n) = H_m(n) \left(e^{i \omega_m n} + \sum_{\omega} a_\omega e^{i \omega n}\right)$$

where `$\omega_m$` is the dominant (base) frequency for dimension `$m$`, `$\omega$` indexes additional harmonic frequencies, and `$a_\omega$` are learned coefficients controlling the strength of each harmonic, with `$a_\omega < 1$` since `$\omega_m$` is dominant.

**What this computes:** The attention weight for dimension `$m$` at relative distance `$n$` is now a *sum* of multiple sinusoidal components rather than a single one. The first term `$e^{i\omega_m n}$` is the dominant component (same as RoPE), and the sum `$\sum_\omega a_\omega e^{i\omega n}$` adds harmonic components that can capture the leaked and distorted frequency content that linear layers and activation functions have mixed into this dimension.

**Implementation via a weight matrix.** The Fourier Series is implemented as a learned linear transformation that maps from a set of basis frequencies to the output attended dimensions. Define:

- `$\{\omega_m\}_{m=1}^{M}$`: the base frequencies for each dimension, initialized identically to RoPE (geometric progression with base `$\theta$`).
- `$\{\omega\}_{d=1}^{D}$`: an expanded set of frequencies where `$M \leq D$`, so that `$\{\omega_m\} \subseteq \{\omega\}$`. This means the base frequencies are a subset of the total frequency set, and additional frequencies are sampled from `$[0, \pi]$` using any distribution.
- `$\{a_\omega\} \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times M}$`: a learned coefficient matrix that specifies how much of each frequency `$\omega$` contributes to each dimension `$m$`. This matrix is initialized from `$\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma)$`, where `$\sigma$` controls the variance of the harmonic coefficients.

The transformation from frequencies to dimension outputs is:

$$\mathbf{H}^{\text{FoPE}} = \mathbf{W}^F \cdot \mathbf{H}^{\text{base}}$$

where `$\mathbf{W}^F \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times (M - M_0)}$` is the Fourier weight matrix, `$\mathbf{H}^{\text{base}} \in \mathbb{R}^{M - M_0}$` contains the pre-rotation query-key products for dimensions above the floor frequency, and `$\mathbf{H}^{\text{FoPE}} \in \mathbb{R}^D$` is the expanded set of frequency coefficients. The `$M_0$` dimensions correspond to the zeroed-out under-trained frequencies (see next section).

**Design choices and their justifications:**

- **Why `$D \geq M$`?** The expanded frequency set provides more basis functions than the original dimensions, giving the model capacity to represent the harmonic frequencies generated by upstream nonlinearities. If `$D = M$`, the model could only represent one frequency per dimension (same as RoPE but with learned assignment). If `$D < M$`, capacity would be reduced. The choice `$D \geq M$` ensures that the Fourier basis is at least as expressive as the original while providing additional harmonics.

- **Why initialize `$a_\omega \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma)$`?** The theoretical analysis shows that Spectrum Damage is caused by linear layers (which have Gaussian-initialized weights) mixing frequency components. Initializing the harmonic coefficients with the same distribution—Gaussian with small variance—reflects the hypothesis that the strength of Spectrum Damage follows a similar distribution to the linear layer weights that cause it. This is not maximum-entropy; it is domain-knowledge-driven: the initialization encodes the assumption that harmonics are weaker than the dominant frequency (`$a_\omega < 1$`), which matches the physical reality that linear mixing and nonlinear distortion are secondary effects, not primary signal generators.

- **Why separate coefficients for sine and cosine?** The paper's implementation uses separate weight matrices for sine and cosine components (`self.sin_coef` and `self.cos_coef` in the code). This corresponds to allowing the real and imaginary parts of the complex exponential to have different harmonic structures. This is a more expressive parameterization than using a single coefficient for both, because it allows the model to represent phase shifts in the harmonic content independently of amplitude scaling.

- **Why per-head parameterization?** The Fourier weight matrix is instantiated separately for each attention head (`self.config.n_heads` copies). This reflects the fact that different heads specialize in different aspects of the input, and therefore the spectrum of information flowing into each head—and consequently the pattern of Spectrum Damage—may differ. Sharing weights across heads would force all heads to use the same harmonic structure, which is unnecessarily restrictive given that the additional parameters are small (see overhead analysis below).

- **Why initialized near identity?** The code shows that an identity matrix is added to the randomly initialized coefficients: `self.sin_coef += torch.eye(...)`. This means that at initialization, each base frequency maps primarily to itself (diagonal contribution of 1 from the identity) plus a small random perturbation (from the Gaussian initialization). This ensures that FoPE starts close to RoPE behavior at the beginning of training and gradually learns to use the harmonics as training proceeds. This is a form of residual initialization that stabilizes early training.

**How this addresses Spectrum Leakage and Distortion.** When a linear layer mixes frequency `$\omega_k$` into dimension `$m$`, the Fourier Series can represent this by having a non-zero coefficient `$a_{\omega_k}^{(m)}$` in dimension `$m$`'s expansion. The dominant frequency `$\omega_m$` handles the primary signal, while the harmonics `$\omega_k$` absorb the leaked content from other dimensions. This means the attention mechanism can now compute distance-dependent weights that account for the actual multi-frequency content of the signal, rather than incorrectly attributing all content to a single frequency.

Operationally, for each dimension `$m$`, the model computes not one sinusoidal function `$e^{i\omega_m n}$` but a weighted combination `$e^{i\omega_m n} + \sum_\omega a_\omega e^{i\omega n}$`. The attention weight for that dimension becomes:

$$\tilde{h}_m^{\text{FoPE}}(n) = H_m(n) \left(e^{i\omega_m n} + \sum_{\omega} a_\omega e^{i\omega n}\right)$$

When summed across dimensions, this produces a distance-dependent attention function that can represent the complex multi-periodic patterns shown in Figure 3's "Ground Truth" curve, rather than the simpler (but inaccurate) approximation that RoPE's single-frequency model produces.

#### FoPE Mechanism 2: Zero-Out Under-Trained Frequencies

FoPE's second modification addresses the Spectrum Damage from inside attention—the under-trained frequency components whose periods exceed the training length.

**The floor frequency.** The paper defines the floor frequency as:

$$\omega_l = \frac{2\pi}{N}$$

where `$N$` is the maximum training sequence length. This is the frequency whose period exactly equals the training length—components with `$\omega_m < \omega_l$` have periods `$N_m = 2\pi / \omega_m > N$` and therefore complete less than one full cycle during training.

**The clipping operation.** For any dimension `$m$` where `$\omega_m < \omega_l$`, FoPE sets the frequency to zero and the positional encoding contribution to a constant:

$$f(\omega_m) = 1 \quad \text{for} \quad \omega_m < \omega_l$$

where the value 1 in the complex domain corresponds to `$e^{i \cdot 0 \cdot n} = 1$`—a constant with no position dependence.

The complete FoPE function is:

$$f(\omega_m) = \begin{cases} 1, & \omega_m < \omega_l \\ e^{i\omega_m n} + \sum_{\omega} a_\omega e^{i\omega n}, & \omega_m \geq \omega_l \end{cases}$$

**What choosing zero means operationally:** Setting a frequency to zero means the corresponding dimension no longer applies any rotation to the query and key vectors. The dot-product attention for that dimension becomes position-independent: `$\tilde{h}_m(n) = H_m \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = H_m$` regardless of the relative distance `$n$`. The information in that dimension still contributes to the attention score, but it carries no positional signal.

**Why zero-frequency specifically?** This is a critical design choice that warrants careful justification. The paper provides multiple arguments:

1. **Dual wavelength property.** The zero-frequency component `$e^{i \cdot 0 \cdot n} = 1$` has both the shortest possible wavelength (it is constant—no variation with distance) and the longest possible wavelength (it never completes a cycle, so its "period" is infinite). This makes it simultaneously suitable for capturing short-term dependencies (it doesn't dampen with distance) and long-term dependencies (it doesn't oscillate, so it can represent persistent information). In contrast, a non-zero but very low frequency would oscillate slowly, creating a position-dependent bias that might be inappropriate for unseen positions.

2. **Zero average positional bias.** The sine and cosine of zero frequency are `$\sin(0) = 0$` and `$\cos(0) = 1$`. The sine component contributes nothing, and the cosine component contributes a constant of 1. This means the positional encoding has zero average effect—it does not push attention weights in any particular direction based on distance. The paper's analysis (Section 5.6 and Appendix A.1) shows that under-trained frequencies develop non-zero mean activations that create positional bias harmful to generalization. Zeroing them out removes this bias entirely.

3. **Preserves information passing.** The paper explicitly states: "To keep the passing of long wavelength information, we substitute these components with zero, as the zero-frequency component corresponds to the longest wavelength." This is subtle but important: the model still needs these dimensions to pass *content* information (the q,k dot product `$H_m$`), just without attaching spurious *positional* information to them. Zero-frequency achieves this by removing the position-dependent rotation while leaving the content-based interaction intact.

4. **Theoretical grounding.** The truncation analysis (Section 3.3) shows that for `$\omega_m < \omega_l$`, the signal in that dimension is dominated by the sinc-shaped artifact rather than the intended frequency. Keeping this component would mean relying on an artifact for positional information—which is worse than having no positional information at all. Zeroing it out is the theoretically principled response to the dominance of truncation artifacts.

**What if we didn't zero them out?** The alternative—keeping under-trained frequencies with some form of rescaling (as YARN and NTK-aware scaling do)—attempts to make the undertrained components useful by stretching their periods to fit the training window. This works to some degree (YARN improves on vanilla RoPE, as Figure 4 shows), but it is fundamentally limited because it cannot restore information that was never learned during training. The truncation artifact dominates regardless of how you rescale the frequency, because the problem is not the frequency value but the fact that the model never saw a full cycle. FoPE's approach is more radical but more honest: if the model cannot possibly have learned the correct periodic behavior, remove the component and let the zero-frequency (content-only, no positional bias) dimension handle information transfer.

**Interaction with the Fourier Series.** The dimensions that are zeroed out (`$M_0$` out of `$M$` total dimensions per head) are excluded from the Fourier Series expansion. Only the `$M - M_0$` dimensions with `$\omega_m \geq \omega_l$` pass through the Fourier weight matrix. This is reflected in the weight matrix dimensions: `$\mathbf{W}^F \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times (M - M_0)}$`. The zero-frequency dimensions contribute their content-based `$H_m$` values directly to the attention score, unmodified by position, while the above-floor dimensions contribute multi-frequency positional signals through the Fourier expansion.

#### Implementation Details and Integration

FoPE is designed as a drop-in modification to existing RoPE implementations, adding minimal computational and memory overhead.

**The weight matrix structure.** The core implementation (shown in the paper's Appendix B code listing) defines a `FourierEmbedding` class that extends `RotaryEmbedding`. The key additions are two learned parameter matrices:

- `self.sin_coef`: shape `(n_heads, input_dim, output_dim)`, initialized with Xavier normal with gain `rope_fourier_init_norm_gain`, then added to an identity matrix. This matrix maps from the `input_dim` base frequencies to `output_dim` expanded frequencies for the sine component of the rotation.
- `self.cos_coef`: same shape and initialization, for the cosine component.

The `input_dim` is the number of frequencies after floor clipping (i.e., `$M - M_0$`), and `output_dim` is set to `min(input_dim, head_dim // 4)`. This means the Fourier expansion can increase or maintain the number of dimensions but is capped at one-quarter of the head dimension.

**The frequency clipping.** In the `get_inv_freq` method, frequencies below the floor are removed:

```python
inv_freq[inv_freq < 2 * torch.pi / self.config.max_sequence_length] = 0
inv_freq = inv_freq[inv_freq != 0.0]
```

This changes `inv_freq` from length `head_dim // 2` to a potentially shorter length, which becomes the `input_dim` for the Fourier weight matrices.

**The rotation application.** The `apply_rotary_pos_embed` method in FoPE performs a two-step transformation:

1. **Fourier expansion.** The positional sine and cosine features (shape `(batch, n_heads, seq_len, input_dim)`) are projected through the learned weight matrices:

```python
fourier_sin = einsum("bhtD, hDd -> bhtd", pos_sin, self.sin_coef / self.sin_coef.sum(dim=-2, keepdim=True))
fourier_cos = einsum("bhtD, hDd -> bhtd", pos_cos, self.cos_coef / self.cos_coef.sum(dim=-2, keepdim=True))
```

The normalization by `self.sin_coef.sum(dim=-2, keepdim=True)` ensures that each output dimension is a convex combination (weights summing to 1) of the input frequency features. This stabilizes training by preventing the Fourier coefficients from growing unboundedly.

2. **Padding to full head dimension.** The expanded features are padded to `head_dim // 2` (since each complex dimension is represented by a real-imaginary pair) and concatenated with themselves to produce the full head dimension:

```python
fourier_sin = F.pad(fourier_sin, pad=(0, head_dim//2 - fourier_sin.size(-1)), value=1)
fourier_cos = F.pad(fourier_cos, pad=(0, head_dim//2 - fourier_cos.size(-1)), value=1)
fourier_sin = torch.cat((fourier_sin, fourier_sin), dim=-1)
fourier_cos = torch.cat((fourier_cos, fourier_cos), dim=-1)
```

The padding value of 1 for cosine and 0 for sine (note: the padding for sine uses `value=1`, but functionally since the sinusoidal features at padded positions are replaced by the concatenation, the padding value matters only for the unpadded region—the `value=1` for cosine maintains the identity rotation for padded dimensions, while for sine it's irrelevant since sine is zero at position 0) ensures that padded dimensions contribute constant rotations.

3. **Application.** The standard rotary embedding formula is applied:

```python
return ((t * fourier_cos) - (self.rotate_half(t) * fourier_sin)).to(t.dtype)
```

**Gradient handling.** The weight matrices are defined with `requires_grad=False`, meaning FoPE adds no trainable parameters that require gradient computation. The weights are fixed after initialization. This is a deliberate choice: the Fourier Series structure is meant to provide a better inductive bias for position encoding, not to learn task-specific position representations. Keeping the weights frozen reduces memory usage and prevents overfitting of the position encoding to the training length.

**Hyperparameters.** The paper identifies three key hyperparameters that control FoPE's behavior:

- **`$\sigma$` (coefficient variance):** Controls the initialization variance of the Fourier coefficients `$a_\omega$`. The paper reports grid-searching `$\sigma$` from 0 to 0.5 and finding optimal values that increase with model scale: `$\sigma = 0.3$` for 60M, `$\sigma = 0.4$` for 180M, and `$\sigma = 0.6$` for 1.2B. The paper interprets larger models requiring larger `$\sigma$` as evidence that "the estimated strength of Spectrum Damage increases as the models' parameter scale increases"—more parameters mean more linear mixing and more nonlinear distortion.

- **`$D$` (number of frequencies in the expanded set):** Controls the size of the Fourier basis. The paper finds `$D = 64$` for 60M models and `$D = 128$` for 180M and 1.2B models. The ablation in Figure 6(c) shows that `$D$` does not significantly affect perplexity but is important for Passkey Retrieval, with `$D = 64$` being optimal. The paper interprets this as: there are a limited number of harmonic components with sufficient intensity to affect information propagation, and paying attention to too many harmonics ("not important components") reduces effectiveness.

- **Floor frequency cutoff:** Determined automatically from `max_sequence_length`. The number of zeroed-out dimensions `$M_0$` is therefore a function of the RoPE base parameter `$\theta$` and the training length.

**Computational and memory overhead.** Because the Fourier weight matrices are frozen and relatively small (shape `(n_heads, D, M - M_0)` where `$D \leq 128$` typically), the additional memory is negligible compared to the model's parameter count. The einsum operations in the forward pass add a small computational cost proportional to `$\mathcal{O}(\text{batch} \times \text{n_heads} \times \text{seq_len} \times D \times (M - M_0))$`, which is dominated by the attention computation itself `$\mathcal{O}(\text{batch} \times \text{n_heads} \times \text{seq_len}^2 \times M)$` for typical sequence lengths. The paper does not report overhead benchmarks, but the architecture suggests the overhead is under 1% of total computation.

---

#### Synergy Between the Two Mechanisms

The two components of FoPE—Fourier Series per dimension (FS) and Clip Floor to Zero (CF)—target different sources of Spectrum Damage and are complementary rather than redundant.

**Fourier Series targets external damage.** The FS mechanism addresses the mixing and distortion introduced by linear layers and activation functions *before* the signal reaches attention. By providing multiple frequency components per dimension, it gives attention the representational capacity to disentangle the mixed signals. The FS operates on dimensions `$\omega_m \geq \omega_l$`—the well-trained frequencies that carry reliable positional information but have been corrupted by cross-frequency leakage.

**Clip Floor targets internal damage.** The CF mechanism addresses the truncation artifacts in frequencies that cannot complete a full cycle during training. By removing these components entirely, it prevents the model from relying on noise-dominated positional signals. The CF operates on dimensions `$\omega_m < \omega_l$`—the frequencies whose positional content is fundamentally unreliable.

**Why both are needed.** The ablation in Figure 6(a) shows that FS alone contributes more to length generalization (Passkey accuracy at extended lengths), while CF alone contributes more to fitting the current dataset and sequence length (perplexity at the training length). The combination yields significant improvement over either alone. This makes sense: FS improves the quality of the positional signal that the model can use, while CF removes positional signals that are actively harmful. Doing both means the model works with better information *and* less noise.

**The complete objective.** The overall effect of FoPE can be understood as a modification to the attention function's inductive bias. RoPE assumes:

$$\tilde{h}_m(n) = \mathbf{H}_m e^{i\omega_m n}$$

with each `$\mathbf{H}_m$` being a pure single-frequency coefficient. This assumption is wrong in two ways: `$\mathbf{H}_m$` contains multi-frequency content (spectrum leakage/distortion), and for low `$m$`, `$\omega_m$` is not a reliable frequency (truncation artifacts). FoPE replaces this with:

$$\tilde{h}_m(n) = \begin{cases} \mathbf{H}_m, & \omega_m < \omega_l \\ \mathbf{H}_m \left(e^{i\omega_m n} + \sum_{\omega} a_\omega e^{i\omega n}\right), & \omega_m \geq \omega_l \end{cases}$$

This eliminates the unreliable positional signals (case 1) and provides representational capacity to handle multi-frequency content (case 2). The periodic extension property `$\tilde{h}_m(n + N_{\omega_m}) = \tilde{h}_m(n)$` is now preserved because: (a) for below-floor frequencies there is no periodicity to break—the signal is constant; (b) for above-floor frequencies, the Fourier Series representation can capture the actual multi-frequency structure, so the period `$N_{\omega_m}$` (of the dominant component) is not expected to be a perfect period of the full signal in the first place—the harmonics have their own periods that the model accounts for explicitly.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Upshifting the Diagnostic Lens from Attention Mechanisms to Full-Model Spectral Analysis

The paper's most fundamental intellectual move is a change in *what we blame* for length generalization failures. Before this work, the field operated under an implicit assumption that position embedding design was the primary—if not sole—determinant of length generalization quality. If a model failed to extrapolate, the diagnosis was straightforward: the position embedding was not good enough. This assumption is visible across the entire literature: ALiBi (Press et al., 2021) modified the attention bias, KERPLE (Chi et al., 2022) kernelized the relative position function, FIRE (Li et al., 2024) learned a functional interpolation, and YARN (Peng et al., 2023) rescaled RoPE frequencies. In every case, the intervention targeted the position encoding *within* the attention computation, and the evaluation implicitly treated the rest of the transformer as a transparent conduit for positional signals.

FoPE reframes the problem entirely. The paper argues—and provides both theoretical and empirical evidence—that the attention mechanism is not the source of the failure. Rather, **the feedforward components of the transformer (linear layers and activation functions) corrupt the frequency-domain structure of hidden states before they ever reach attention**, and attention's ideal periodic extension breaks because it receives corrupted inputs, not because the position encoding itself is defective.

This is a diagnostic shift, not merely a new method. It changes what we count as relevant variables when reasoning about length generalization. Under the old framing, improving length generalization meant finding better position embedding functions—better frequency schedules, better decay patterns, better interpolation strategies. Under the new framing, improving length generalization means making the attention mechanism **robust to the inevitable spectral corruption introduced by upstream components**—or, more ambitiously, redesigning those upstream components to preserve spectral structure. The paper pursues the first path (robust attention via Fourier Series), but the diagnostic insight opens the second path as a clear direction for future work.

The shift is supported by evidence that prior framings could not explain. If RoPE's periodic extension were the problem, then fixing RoPE's frequency schedule (as YARN does) should solve length generalization. But Figure 4 shows that even RoPE+YARN, while better than vanilla RoPE, still underperforms FoPE substantially—and FoPE can be applied *on top of* a RoPE-pretrained model to improve it beyond what YARN achieves. This result makes no sense if the position encoding is the bottleneck; it makes perfect sense if the bottleneck is the model's inability to handle spectral corruption, which FoPE addresses and YARN does not. The fact that FoPE can be applied post-hoc to improve a RoPE-trained model (FoPE-512+FoPE-1024 in Figure 4) demonstrates that the benefit comes not from a better frequency schedule but from a mechanism that compensates for corruption already present in the pretrained representations.

This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental refinement. It changes the research question from "what is the best position encoding function?" to "how do we preserve useful positional information through a stack of spectrally-destructive operations?"

---

### Innovation 2: Theoretical Formalization of Spectrum Damage as a First-Class Phenomenon in Transformers

The paper introduces **Spectrum Damage** as a named, analyzed, and quantified phenomenon with two distinct mechanisms: Spectrum Leakage (linear mixing of frequency components across dimensions) and Spectrum Distortion (nonlinear generation of harmonic frequencies). While the individual mathematical facts underlying these mechanisms are standard—matrix multiplication mixes dimensions, and nonlinear functions applied to sinusoids generate harmonics via the Taylor expansion—the paper's contribution is the **synthesis** of these facts into a unified framework that explains a specific empirical phenomenon (length generalization failure in RoPE) that had previously been treated as mysterious or attributed to the wrong causes.

This is conceptually analogous to what the original RoPE paper (Su et al., 2024) did for position encoding: it took the well-known mathematical fact that complex exponentials encode relative position through phase differences, and elevated it into a practical architecture that became the standard. FoPE takes the well-known DSP facts about spectral leakage from windowing and harmonic generation from nonlinearity, and elevates them into a diagnostic framework that explains *why* RoPE's elegant theory fails to materialize in practice.

The framework's explanatory power is its main contribution. It provides a unified account of several previously disconnected observations:

- **Why RoPE fails at 2× training length.** The Spectrum Damage analysis shows that by the time hidden states reach attention, the one-to-one frequency-dimension correspondence is destroyed. The periodic extension property (Equation 5) assumes this correspondence; when it breaks, so does length generalization. The catastrophic drop in Passkey accuracy at 2× training length (Figure 1a) is not an empirical surprise but a theoretically predictable consequence of accumulated spectral corruption.

- **Why ALiBi has stable perplexity but fails at information retrieval.** ALiBi's linear decay bias systematically suppresses long-range signals, compensating for the fact that spectral corruption makes long-range positional information unreliable. This stabilizes perplexity (the model isn't trying to use corrupted long-range signals) but prevents information retrieval (the model *can't* use long-range signals even when they would be useful). The Spectrum Damage framework explains both the benefit (reduced reliance on corrupted information) and the cost (inability to distinguish genuinely useful long-range signals from noise).

- **Why under-trained low frequencies are harmful, not just useless.** The truncation analysis (Equation 10) shows that when  α < 1, the frequency-domain representation of a low-frequency component is dominated by a sinc-shaped artifact rather than an impulse at the intended frequency. This is not merely "no information"—it is actively misleading information that produces spurious positional signals. The paper's empirical finding that these dimensions have higher absolute activations (Figure 7) further demonstrates that the model amplifies these artifacts during training, creating positional biases that degrade extrapolation.

The DSP formalization is a **fundamental theoretical contribution** rather than an incremental one, because it provides a vocabulary and causal model for phenomena that were previously described only empirically. Before this paper, one could say "low frequencies cause extrapolation problems" (an empirical observation). After this paper, one can say "low frequencies with α < 1 produce sinc-dominant spectra that create spurious positional signals which corrupt the periodic extension" (a mechanistic explanation). The latter enables principled solutions (zero-out) rather than heuristic ones (rescale and hope).

---

### Innovation 3: The Principle That Positional Information Should Be Removed When It Cannot Be Learned Rather Than Rescaled

The most counterintuitive design choice in FoPE is also its most philosophically interesting: **when a frequency component cannot be adequately trained (α < 1), FoPE removes it entirely rather than attempting to rescale it into usefulness.** This is a sharp departure from the dominant paradigm in length extrapolation research, which has been to rescale, interpolate, or otherwise adjust frequencies so that they "fit" within the training window.

Methods like NTK-aware scaling, YARN, and CLEX all operate on the principle that low frequencies can be made useful by stretching them—if the period is too long, compress it so that it completes at least one cycle within training. This approach has empirical merit (YARN substantially improves on vanilla RoPE, as the paper acknowledges), but it rests on an assumption that the paper's analysis challenges: that the problem with under-trained frequencies is merely that their *value* is wrong, and that adjusting the value fixes the problem.

FoPE's theoretical analysis suggests a deeper issue: the problem is not the frequency value but the **spectral structure**. When α < 1, the frequency-domain representation contains no impulse at the intended frequency—only the sinc-shaped artifact. Rescaling the frequency changes which artifact is produced but does not eliminate the fundamental fact that the model has never seen a complete cycle and cannot learn the correct periodic behavior. The artifact is a consequence of truncation, not of the specific frequency value, and no rescaling can reconstruct information that was never present in the training data.

The choice of zero as the substitute frequency is itself a conceptual insight. The paper argues that zero-frequency components serve as a **universal fallback** for positional information: they carry content-based signals (the q,k dot product H_m passes through unchanged) without imposing any position-dependent bias. This means the model retains the ability to use these dimensions for content matching while avoiding the spurious positional signals that under-trained frequencies would produce. The zero-frequency choice is not arbitrary—it follows from the theoretical analysis that when positional information is unreliable, the optimal strategy is to not use positional information at all in those dimensions.

This principle has implications beyond FoPE. It suggests that length extrapolation methods should distinguish between **reliable positional dimensions** (α ≥ 1, where the frequency value can be trusted) and **unreliable positional dimensions** (α < 1, where the best strategy may be to suppress positional information entirely). Current methods treat all dimensions uniformly—they rescale all frequencies by the same factor or the same interpolation scheme. FoPE's difficulty-conditioned approach (different treatment for above-floor vs. below-floor frequencies) may point toward more nuanced extrapolation strategies that apply different operations to different frequency regimes based on their training adequacy.

This is best characterized as a **fundamental design principle** with incremental empirical validation. The principle—remove rather than rescale when information is fundamentally absent—is conceptually novel and well-motivated by theory. The empirical evidence (Figure 6a shows that CF alone contributes to fitting the training distribution, and the combination with FS produces the strongest results) supports the principle but does not yet demonstrate it across a wide enough range of settings to be considered fully validated. Future work testing this principle across different architectures, training lengths, and tasks will determine its generality.

---

### Innovation 4: Verifier-Like Robustness Through Multi-Frequency Representation Without Learned Adaptation

A subtle but important innovation in FoPE is the way it achieves robustness to spectral corruption **without learning task-specific position representations**. The Fourier Series weight matrices are randomly initialized (with identity-plus-Gaussian structure) and frozen during training—they receive no gradients and are never updated. This means FoPE's benefit comes entirely from providing a better **inductive bias** for how attention processes positional information, not from learning to adapt to specific data distributions.

This is significant because it distinguishes FoPE from methods that learn position embeddings from data (e.g., learned absolute position embeddings, or FIRE's learned functional interpolation). Learned position embeddings risk overfitting to the training sequence length—they can memorize position-specific patterns that do not generalize. FoPE's frozen Fourier weights avoid this risk because they encode a structural prior (each dimension contains multi-frequency information) rather than data-driven optimizations. The model cannot overfit the position encoding to the training length because the position encoding is not optimized at all.

The effectiveness of this approach provides an interesting negative result: **the model does not need to learn which harmonic frequencies are present in each dimension; it merely needs the representational capacity to express them.** The fact that random Fourier weights (subject to the identity-initialization constraint) work well suggests that the benefit comes from the architecture's ability to *represent* multi-frequency content, not from discovering the *correct* harmonic structure for each dimension. This is analogous to how random Fourier features in kernel methods (Rahimi & Recht, 2007) provide useful representations without learning—the random projection into a high-dimensional Fourier basis is sufficient to approximate shift-invariant kernels.

The identity-plus-noise initialization is the mechanism that makes this work. By initializing near identity, FoPE starts with behavior close to RoPE (each dimension primarily represents its assigned frequency). The random Gaussian perturbation provides capacity for the model to express multi-frequency content if and when it becomes useful. During training, the model can learn to use the harmonic components by adjusting the *input* to the Fourier layer (the query and key vectors)—effectively learning which dimensions should activate which harmonic coefficients—without ever updating the Fourier weights themselves. This is a form of **structural regularization**: the position encoding architecture provides flexibility, and the model's training dynamics determine how to use it.

This design choice has practical advantages beyond its conceptual elegance. Frozen weights mean zero additional parameters to optimize, zero additional gradient computation, and zero risk of position-encoding overfitting. It also means that FoPE can be applied to pretrained models during continual pretraining (as demonstrated in Figure 4) without needing to learn new position representations from scratch—the Fourier structure is imposed as a fixed transformation, and the model adapts to it through standard training of the q,k projections.

This is an **incremental architectural innovation** with a conceptually interesting justification. The mechanism itself (frozen random projection) is not novel—fixed random features are well-known in machine learning. What is novel is the *application* of this idea to address a specific theoretical problem (spectral corruption) identified through frequency-domain analysis, and the demonstration that it works without learning. This tightens the connection between the theory (multi-frequency content exists because of linear and nonlinear operations) and the solution (provide multi-frequency representational capacity without requiring it to be learned), making the design feel principled rather than arbitrary.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary pre-training corpus is a 10B-token subset of **C4** (Raffel et al., 2020) with a validation set drawn from the same distribution. For out-of-distribution evaluation, models are trained on approximately 5B tokens from **Gutenberg Books** (Hart, 2007) and evaluated on the C4 validation set. The Passkey Retrieval task (Mohtashami & Jaggi, 2023) is implemented following Peng et al. (2023), testing the model's ability to retrieve a five-digit passkey randomly positioned at uniformly distributed locations within contexts of varying lengths, with 1000 trials per length to ensure positional dispersion. For fine-tuning experiments, downstream tasks include **GovReport** (Huang et al., 2021) and **MultiNews** (Fabbri et al., 2019) for summarization, and **TREC** (Li & Roth, 2002), **TriviaQA** (Joshi et al., 2017), and **SAMSum** (Gliwa et al., 2019) for few-shot question-answering, using the LongBench (Bai et al., 2023) evaluation framework. Additionally, standard downstream tasks (basic arithmetic, Social IQA, Winogrande, OpenBook QA, SciQ, HellaSwag, PIQA, Commonsense QA, ARC Easy, MMLU, Natural Questions, TriviaQA Wiki) are evaluated to verify that FoPE does not degrade short-context performance.

- **Base model(s).** Pre-training experiments use the **OLMo** framework (Groeneveld et al., 2024) at three scales: 60M, 180M, and 1.2B parameters, trained from scratch. These scales were chosen to demonstrate consistency of benefits across model sizes while remaining computationally tractable for full pretraining runs. For fine-tuning experiments, **SmolLM-1.7B** (Allal et al., 2024) is selected as the base model because it uses the LLaMA architecture (Touvron et al., 2023) with RoPE and has a pretrained context length of 2048, making it representative of widely-deployed open-source models. The FLOPs-matched comparison uses the SmolLM-1.7B fine-tuned to 4096 context length. Visualization experiments for under-trained dimension analysis use **Llama2-7B** (which has 32 attention heads with 128 dimensions each and a pretraining sequence length of 4096).

- **Metrics.** Two primary metrics are used throughout. **Perplexity** on the validation set measures the model's language modeling quality at different sequence lengths—a stable perplexity across increasing lengths indicates good length generalization. **Accuracy on Passkey Retrieval** measures the model's ability to extract a specific piece of information from long contexts, with accuracy computed as the fraction of 1000 trials where the model correctly retrieves the five-digit number. For fine-tuning experiments, task-specific metrics are used: ROUGE scores for summarization tasks and accuracy for question-answering tasks, following the LongBench evaluation protocol. For the 60M ablation studies, a **Perplexity Ratio** metric is introduced: `PPL_ratio = PPL_C4 / PPL_Books`, where a ratio closer to 1 indicates better generalization across different data distributions.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five baselines: **NoPE** (no position embedding), **ALiBi** (Press et al., 2021) which uses linearly-declining attention bias with distance, **KERPLE** (Chi et al., 2022) which kernelizes relative position embedding, **FIRE** (Li et al., 2024) which learns functional interpolation for relative positions, and **RoPE** (Su et al., 2024) which is the current standard and the direct point of comparison. For continual pre-training and extrapolation experiments, **YARN** (Peng et al., 2023) serves as the representative extrapolation method, both applied to RoPE-based and FoPE-based models. For the undertrained frequency ablation (Table 2), **RoPE-A** is introduced: a variant where all RoPE frequencies are adjusted to the nearest values that complete full cycles within the training length, and **QK Norm** variants normalize q,k vectors to zero mean and unit variance before applying rotation matrices.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use generation budgets in the standard sense (since these are pre-training and fine-tuning experiments, not inference-time compute scaling). Instead, **training token count** is the relevant budget: 10B tokens for main pre-training runs, ~5B tokens for out-of-distribution training, ~1B tokens for continual pre-training, and approximately 350k samples for one epoch of fine-tuning. All models within a given scale and setting are trained on identical amounts of data for fair comparison. For the YARN continual pre-training comparison, both RoPE+YARN and FoPE+YARN are fine-tuned for ~1B tokens from the same pre-training checkpoint.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** Pre-training evaluations use the checkpoint from the last training step. For fine-tuning, checkpoints are saved every 100 steps and the best result for each method is reported—a protocol justified by the observation that YARN is prone to overfitting with excessive fine-tuning steps, a limitation not observed in FoPE. Passkey Retrieval uses 1000 trials per sequence length with randomly sampled passkey positions to ensure statistical reliability of the accuracy estimates. For the undertrained dimension analysis (Figure 7), 1000 tokens are randomly sampled and activation values are averaged across attention heads for each dimension. Hyperparameters for FoPE (σ and D) are selected via grid search on the 60M model and scaled heuristically for larger models (σ = 0.3, 0.4, 0.6 and D = 64, 128, 128 for 60M, 180M, 1.2B respectively).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Pre-Training Length Generalization

**Perplexity on in-distribution data (C4).** FoPE demonstrates significantly more stable perplexity across increasing sequence lengths compared to RoPE, though it is slightly outperformed by ALiBi on this specific metric. Figure 1(b) shows that at sequence length 512, all methods have comparable perplexity (approximately 20–25 range for the 1.2B model). As sequence length increases to 8192, RoPE perplexity degrades substantially (rising by roughly 5–10 points), while FoPE maintains near-constant perplexity, tracking closely with ALiBi and substantially outperforming RoPE. The paper attributes ALiBi's strong perplexity performance on C4 to the dataset's predominantly short-distance dependencies: "the corpus in C4 and Books mainly have short-distance dependency, thus the information from a short context window is enough for the prediction of almost all tokens" (Section 5.2). ALiBi's linearly-declining attention bias naturally suppresses long-distance information, which aligns with the dataset's characteristics but fundamentally limits its ability to use long-range context when needed.

**Perplexity on out-of-distribution data (Gutenberg Books → C4).** When models are trained on Gutenberg Books and evaluated on C4 validation (Figure 5), FoPE maintains its advantage over RoPE across all three model scales (60M, 180M, 1.2B) and all tested sequence lengths (512 to 8192). At 8192 tokens, the 1.2B FoPE model achieves approximately 225 perplexity versus roughly 275 for RoPE—a gap of approximately 50 points. ALiBi again shows flat or slightly declining perplexity curves, while KERPLE and FIRE show intermediate behavior. This cross-distribution setting is important because it tests whether FoPE's benefits are specific to the training distribution or generalize to different language patterns—the preserved advantage indicates genuine length generalization rather than distribution-specific fitting.

**Passkey Retrieval accuracy.** This is where FoPE's advantages are most dramatic. Figure 1(a) shows that for the 1.2B model trained with maximum sequence length 512:

- **RoPE**: achieves near 100% accuracy at the training length (512), but drops sharply to approximately **0% at 1024** tokens (2× training length) and remains at zero for all longer lengths.
- **ALiBi**: shows approximately 95% accuracy at 512, declining approximately linearly to roughly **20% at 8192**—a steady deterioration rather than a cliff, but ultimately failing to retrieve distant information.
- **NoPE, KERPLE, FIRE**: all show intermediate behavior, generally declining faster than ALiBi but without RoPE's catastrophic cliff.
- **FoPE**: maintains approximately **80–85% accuracy across all tested lengths from 512 to 8192**—a nearly flat accuracy curve with only minor degradation at the longest distances.

This result is the paper's strongest single piece of evidence. FoPE is not merely "better" than RoPE at length generalization; it solves the catastrophic failure mode entirely, maintaining useful retrieval accuracy at 16× the training length where RoPE has zero accuracy.

**Standard downstream task performance.** Tables 4, 5, and 6 (Appendix A.4) evaluate 1.2B models on a diverse set of standard benchmarks to verify that FoPE's length generalization benefits do not come at the cost of general capability degradation. On the suite of reasoning and knowledge tasks (Table 4), FoPE achieves an average accuracy of **43.37%** versus RoPE's **42.98%**, ALiBi's **42.93%**, and KERPLE's **43.22%**—essentially tied with the best baseline. On cross-entropy loss tasks (Table 5), FoPE achieves the best (lowest) average loss of **1.3941** versus RoPE's **1.4225**. On MMLU (Table 6), FoPE achieves the highest average accuracy of **27.57%** versus RoPE's **26.68%** and all other baselines below 26.7%. FoPE therefore **slightly outperforms or matches all baselines on standard tasks** while dramatically outperforming on length generalization—a rare combination that suggests the Fourier Series representation is a genuinely better inductive bias rather than a length-specialization tradeoff.

#### Continual Pre-Training and Extrapolation

**FoPE as an extrapolation method.** Figure 4 presents results where models pre-trained with 512 context length (using either RoPE or FoPE) are further fine-tuned on a corpus with maximum sequence length 1024 using either YARN or FoPE as the extrapolation mechanism. The key comparisons:

**Passkey Retrieval accuracy (Figure 4a):**
- **RoPE-512 baseline**: accuracy drops to zero at 1024 and beyond, as expected.
- **RoPE-512 + YARN-1024**: recovers significantly, achieving approximately **40–50% accuracy at 1024** and declining to roughly **20–30% at 2048**, dropping to near zero by 4096.
- **RoPE-512 + FoPE-1024**: substantially outperforms YARN, achieving approximately **70–80% accuracy at 1024**, maintaining roughly **60–70% at 2048**, and still above **40% at 4096**—a remarkable result since the model was only fine-tuned on 1024-length data.
- **FoPE-512 baseline**: already maintains accuracy across lengths (consistent with Figure 1a).
- **FoPE-512 + YARN-1024**: further improves on the FoPE baseline at longer lengths.
- **FoPE-512 + FoPE-1024**: achieves the best overall performance, with near-perfect accuracy at all tested lengths.

**Perplexity on C4 (Figure 4b):**
- **RoPE-512**: perplexity rises steeply with sequence length.
- **RoPE-512 + YARN-1024**: significantly flattens the perplexity curve, but still shows noticeable increase at 2048 and beyond.
- **RoPE-512 + FoPE-1024**: achieves lower and flatter perplexity than RoPE+YARN at all lengths beyond 1024.
- **FoPE-512 + FoPE-1024**: achieves the lowest and flattest perplexity curve overall.

The critical finding here is that **FoPE applied to a RoPE-pretrained model (RoPE-512+FoPE-1024) substantially outperforms YARN applied to the same model (RoPE-512+YARN-1024)**. This demonstrates that FoPE is not merely a better position embedding for training from scratch, but also an effective **post-hoc correction** for existing RoPE-based models. The paper explicitly states: "FoPE outperforms YARN in length extrapolation for both RoPE-based and FoPE-based models. These findings underscore the effectiveness and practical utility of FoPE, which holds the potential to enhance all RoPE-based open-source models."

#### Fine-Tuning on Complex Downstream Tasks

**Summarization and few-shot QA.** Table 1 presents results for SmolLM-1.7B fine-tuned to 4096 context length, comparing RoPE and FoPE. Samples are categorized by length into 0–4k (within training range), 4–8k (beyond training but within 2×), and 8k+ (far beyond training). The results show:

**GovReport summarization (ROUGE scores reported as average across metrics):**
- 0–4k: RoPE 13.02, FoPE 13.27 (+0.25)
- 4–8k: RoPE 11.35, FoPE 12.50 (+1.15)
- 8k+: RoPE 12.02, FoPE 12.38 (+0.36)

FoPE slightly improves within-distribution performance and substantially improves at 4–8k lengths, with the gain narrowing but remaining positive at 8k+.

**MultiNews summarization:**
- 0–4k: RoPE 12.71, FoPE 12.92 (+0.21)
- 4–8k: RoPE 11.11, FoPE 12.98 (+1.87)
- 8k+: RoPE 10.85, FoPE 12.23 (+1.38)

The pattern is similar but more pronounced: FoPE's advantage grows at 4–8k and remains substantial at 8k+.

**TREC question classification (accuracy):**
- 0–4k: RoPE 37.00, FoPE 41.00 (+4.00)
- 4–8k: RoPE 42.00, FoPE 56.00 (+14.00)
- 8k+: RoPE 36.00, FoPE 51.00 (+15.00)

This is the most dramatic result: FoPE **improves performance by 14–15 percentage points** at extended lengths while also improving within-range performance by 4 points. Notably, RoPE's performance degrades from 42.00 at 4–8k to 36.00 at 8k+ (a 6-point drop), while FoPE drops only from 56.00 to 51.00 (a 5-point drop), demonstrating greater stability.

**TriviaQA (accuracy):**
- 0–4k: RoPE 36.50, FoPE 33.26 (−3.24)
- 4–8k: RoPE 36.12, FoPE 33.53 (−2.49)
- 8k+: RoPE 25.02, FoPE 33.87 (+8.85)

This is the **only benchmark where FoPE underperforms within the training range**, scoring approximately 3 points lower than RoPE at 0–4k and 4–8k. However, RoPE suffers a catastrophic 11-point degradation at 8k+ (36.12 → 25.02), while FoPE remains stable (33.53 → 33.87), ultimately outperforming RoPE by 8.85 points at the longest lengths. This tradeoff—slightly worse short-context performance for dramatically better long-context stability—is the closest the paper comes to a negative result on downstream tasks.

**SAMSum dialogue summarization (ROUGE):**
- 0–4k: RoPE 10.27, FoPE 19.77 (+9.50)
- 4–8k: RoPE 6.37, FoPE 15.85 (+9.48)
- 8k+: RoPE 8.49, FoPE 17.26 (+8.87)

FoPE nearly doubles RoPE's performance across all length categories, with a remarkably consistent advantage of approximately 9 points regardless of sequence length.

**Cross-benchmark pattern.** Across five diverse benchmarks, FoPE demonstrates: (1) within-range performance that is equal to or better than RoPE on 4/5 tasks; (2) substantially better generalization at 4–8k and 8k+ on 5/5 tasks; (3) greater stability—smaller performance degradation as length increases—on 5/5 tasks. The only exception (TriviaQA within-range) highlights a potential tradeoff but is outweighed by FoPE's dramatic superiority at extended lengths. The paper does not explain why TriviaQA specifically shows this pattern, which is a minor weakness in the analysis.

#### Empirical Validation of FoPE's Mechanism

**Multi-frequency attention score modeling (Figure 3).** The paper constructs a toy model with a single-layer MLP and activation function to validate the necessity of multi-frequency representation. The experiment tracks how two frequency components transform through the MLP and activation, then compares the resulting "Ground Truth" attention scores (frequency-matched to the actual multi-frequency signal) with what RoPE and FoPE produce. Figure 3 shows that:

- **Ground Truth** (top, middle sub-figures): The two frequency components produce distinct periodic patterns in the attention score as a function of token distance.
- **RoPE** (bottom sub-figure, blue): Produces a smoother approximation that fails to capture the complex multi-periodic structure of the ground truth.
- **FoPE** (bottom sub-figure, green): More accurately captures the multi-frequency periodicity, yielding attention scores that better align with information transfer.

This toy experiment provides direct visual evidence for the paper's central theoretical claim: FoPE's Fourier Series representation enables attention to model the actual multi-frequency content of hidden states, while RoPE's single-frequency assumption produces a degraded approximation.

**Under-trained dimension activation analysis (Figure 7).** Using Llama2-7B (pretrained with 4096 context length, 32 heads × 128 dimensions), the paper identifies dimensions corresponding to undertrained frequencies (those completing less than one full cycle during training, specifically dimensions [45, 64] ∪ [109, 128]) and visualizes their activation values. The findings:

- **Undertrained dimensions** (the identified ranges) show **significantly higher absolute activation values** in both q and k vectors across all layers (0–30).
- This elevated activation persists uniformly across layers—it is not concentrated in early or late layers but is a systematic property of the model's representation.
- The paper interprets this as "positional bias [that] may adversely affect robustness to out-of-domain rotation matrix values during length generalization" (Section 5.6).

**Undertrained frequency ablation with QK Norm (Table 2).** To test whether the positional bias from undertrained frequencies is harmful, the paper trains 20M-parameter toy models on sequence length 512 and evaluates loss at extended lengths under different configurations:

| Method | 512 | 1024 | 2048 | 4096 | 8192 |
|--------|-----|------|------|------|------|
| RoPE | 5.50 | 6.01 | 6.58 | 6.99 | 7.16 |
| RoPE + QK Norm | 5.46 | 5.56 | 5.89 | 6.32 | 6.66 |
| RoPE-A | 5.72 | 5.86 | 6.18 | 6.46 | 6.67 |
| RoPE-A + QK Norm | 5.69 | 5.89 | 6.27 | 6.59 | 6.81 |
| NoPE | 5.65 | 6.03 | 6.60 | 6.81 | 6.99 |
| NoPE + QK Norm | 5.59 | 6.18 | 6.87 | 7.10 | 7.43 |

The critical pattern: **QK Norm helps RoPE substantially** (reducing loss at 8192 from 7.16 to 6.66, a 0.50 improvement), but **does not help RoPE-A** (loss at 8192 is 6.67 vs. 6.81, a 0.14 *worsening*) and **slightly worsens NoPE** (7.16 vs. 6.99 with QK Norm, then 7.43 vs. 6.99 without—a 0.44 worsening at 8192). This is explained by the paper's hypothesis: QK Norm removes positional bias (non-zero mean) from q,k vectors. This removal helps when the position embedding contains undertrained components (RoPE), because it eliminates the spurious positional signals from those components. It does not help when all components complete full cycles (RoPE-A) or when there is no position embedding (NoPE), because there are no spurious signals to remove—and normalizing may actually degrade the model's learned representations.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Both sub-methods of FoPE are useful, with synergistic combination (Figure 6a):** The ablation compares FoPE (full), FoPE without Fourier Series (w/o FS, equivalent to RoPE with floor clipping only), and FoPE without Clip Floor to Zero (w/o CF, equivalent to RoPE with Fourier Series only). The metric is Perplexity Ratio (PPL_C4 / PPL_Books) on the 60M model evaluated from 512 to 8192 tokens. FoPE w/o CF provides better length generalization (lower ratio at long lengths) than FoPE w/o FS, indicating Fourier Series contributes more to generalization. FoPE w/o FS provides better perplexity at the training length (lower ratio at 512), indicating floor clipping contributes more to fitting the current distribution. The full FoPE achieves the best performance at all lengths, demonstrating synergy.

- **Scaling attention head dimension is more beneficial than scaling number of heads or layers (Figure 6a):** The paper compares FoPE with doubled head dimension (128 instead of 64 for the 60M model) versus doubling other parameters (heads or layers). Doubling head dimension yields lower Perplexity Ratios across all lengths than doubling heads or layers. The paper interprets this through the Spectrum Damage lens: "More dimensions introduce more frequency components, making attention more robust to Spectral Damage. In contrast, adding more attention heads and layers aggravates Spectrum Damage, which diminishes the benefits of expanding the parameter scale." This is a non-obvious finding with practical implications for architecture design under length generalization constraints.

- **Coefficient variance σ has an optimal value that increases with model scale (Figure 6b):** Grid-searching σ ∈ [0, 0.5] on the 60M model (with D = 16 fixed) shows that σ = 0.3 achieves the best Perplexity Ratio, especially for longer context lengths (4096 and 8192). The paper interprets the optimal σ as "the estimated strength of Spectrum Damage of the 60M model." For larger models, the optimal σ increases: σ = 0.4 for 180M and σ = 0.6 for 1.2B, consistent with the hypothesis that larger models experience stronger Spectrum Damage due to more extensive linear mixing and nonlinear distortion.

- **Number of frequencies D does not significantly affect perplexity but strongly affects Passkey Retrieval (Figure 6c):** Grid-searching D ∈ {16, 32, 64, 128} on the 60M model (with σ = 0.3 fixed) shows minimal perplexity variation across D values (curves nearly overlap in the perplexity plot), but substantial variation in Passkey Retrieval accuracy. D = 64 achieves the best accuracy across all sequence lengths, with D = 128 performing notably worse. The paper interprets this as: there are a limited number of harmonic components strong enough to affect information propagation, and "paying attention to not important components hinders the effectiveness of the model." This finding mirrors a form of overfitting in the frequency domain: including too many harmonic frequencies introduces noise that degrades the model's ability to focus on the genuinely informative components.

- **FoPE is robust to hyperparameter selection:** The ablation study explicitly notes that "FoPE consistently outperforms RoPE across all hyperparameter settings, demonstrating its robustness to hyperparameter selection" (Section 5.5). While the paper does not show this exhaustively, the grid-search results (Figures 6b, 6c) show that even at suboptimal σ or D values, FoPE's Perplexity Ratio and Passkey Accuracy remain above RoPE baselines.

- **FoPE applied as post-hoc correction to RoPE-pretrained models outperforms YARN (Figure 4):** This is both a main result and an important robustness check: FoPE is not dependent on being present from the start of training. When applied to a RoPE-512 pretrained model during continual pre-training (RoPE-512+FoPE-1024), it substantially outperforms YARN applied to the same base model. This demonstrates that FoPE's mechanism—providing multi-frequency representational capacity—is complementary to existing pretrained representations and can be retrofitted.

- **Additional downstream benchmarks show no degradation (Tables 4–6):** FoPE matches or exceeds all baselines on standard tasks including MMLU, HellaSwag, PIQA, and others, demonstrating that the Fourier Series modification does not harm the model's general capabilities despite being a structural change to the attention mechanism.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "FoPE maintains stable performance across varying context windows"

This claim is **strongly supported** by the pre-training experiments but has important boundary conditions that the paper partially acknowledges.

**What was demonstrated:** Figure 1(a) shows remarkably flat Passkey Retrieval accuracy for FoPE from 512 to 8192 tokens, while RoPE collapses to zero at 1024. Figure 1(b) shows FoPE's perplexity remaining near-constant as sequence length increases. Figure 5 replicates this across three model scales and out-of-distribution data. Table 1 extends the finding to summarization and QA tasks, where FoPE maintains more stable performance at 8k+ lengths than RoPE.

**What was not tested:** All pre-training experiments use a maximum training length of 512 tokens and test up to 8192 (16×). The paper does not test whether FoPE's stability eventually degrades at some longer distance. The flat Passkey curve from 512 to 8192 is impressive but does not prove indefinite extrapolation—at some point, the Fourier Series harmonics themselves may become inadequate for the accumulated spectral corruption. Additionally, the stability claim is mostly demonstrated on Passkey Retrieval (a relatively simple task) and perplexity. On the more complex downstream tasks (Table 1), FoPE does show some degradation at 8k+ compared to 4–8k (e.g., GovReport 12.50 → 12.38, TREC 56.00 → 51.00), though the degradation is smaller than RoPE's.

**Boundary conditions to note:** The 1.2B FoPE model does show a slight accuracy decline in Passkey Retrieval at 8192 (~85% vs. ~95% at 512), so "stable" means "substantially more stable than RoPE" rather than "perfectly flat." This qualification is important for practical deployment expectations.

#### Claim 2: "FoPE outperforms other baselines including RoPE, ALiBi, KERPLE, and FIRE"

This claim is **supported for length generalization but requires qualification for standard benchmarks**.

**What was demonstrated:** On Passkey Retrieval (Figure 1a), FoPE dramatically outperforms all baselines at extended lengths. On perplexity (Figure 1b, Figure 5), FoPE substantially outperforms RoPE but is slightly behind ALiBi—a baseline that the paper itself shows fails at information retrieval. On standard downstream tasks (Tables 4–6), FoPE is competitive with or slightly better than all baselines, with no statistically significant degradation.

**What was not tested:** The comparison against KERPLE and FIRE is only shown for the 1.2B scale on standard tasks (Tables 4–6); their Passkey Retrieval and perplexity performance is not reported in the main figures. The paper's Figure 1 only shows RoPE, ALiBi, and FoPE for the key metrics, with NoPE, KERPLE, and FIRE mentioned in the text but not visualized. This makes it harder to assess the full competitive landscape.

**The ALiBi tradeoff:** The paper is refreshingly honest about ALiBi's strong perplexity performance, providing a clear explanation (C4 has short-distance dependencies that align with ALiBi's inductive bias). However, the paper could more explicitly frame this as a **fundamental tradeoff**: methods that suppress long-range signals (ALiBi) achieve better perplexity stability but fail at information retrieval, while methods that preserve long-range signals (FoPE) excel at retrieval but may show slightly worse perplexity on datasets dominated by local dependencies. The paper presents FoPE as universally better, but the data shows it is better *for tasks requiring long-range information*, which is the intended use case but not the only use case.

#### Claim 3: "Spectrum Damage from linear layers and activation functions explains RoPE's length generalization failure"

This is a **theoretical claim that receives empirical support but is not directly verified through causal experimentation**.

**What was demonstrated:** The theoretical analysis (Section 3) is mathematically sound. The toy model experiment (Figure 3) shows that multi-frequency content exists in hidden states and that FoPE models it better than RoPE. The undertrained frequency analysis (Figure 7, Table 2) provides strong evidence for the truncation artifact mechanism.

**What was not demonstrated:** The paper does not perform a **causal intervention** to verify that linear layers and activation functions are the *source* of the spectrum damage. A compelling experiment would be: train models with and without activation functions (or with linear activations), and measure whether the benefit of FoPE over RoPE changes. If Spectrum Damage from nonlinearities is the mechanism, then removing nonlinearities should reduce FoPE's advantage. Similarly, reducing the width of linear layers should reduce spectrum leakage and narrow the gap. These experiments are absent.

The paper's evidence is **correlational rather than causal**: it shows that FoPE (which is designed to handle multi-frequency content) works better than RoPE (which assumes single-frequency per dimension), which is consistent with the Spectrum Damage hypothesis but does not rule out alternative explanations. For example, FoPE might work better simply because it provides more parameters (the Fourier weight matrix) or a different frequency allocation, rather than because it specifically addresses spectral corruption.

The QK Norm experiment (Table 2) comes closest to a causal test: it shows that removing positional bias helps only when undertrained frequencies are present. But this tests the undertrained frequency mechanism, not the spectrum leakage/distortion mechanism. The paper lacks an analogous causal test for the claim that linear layers and activation functions are the primary sources of spectrum damage.

#### Claim 4: "FoPE can be applied as a post-hoc correction to existing RoPE-based models"

This claim is **supported by Figure 4 but tested only on continual pre-training, not on frozen-model application**.

**What was demonstrated:** Figure 4 shows that applying FoPE during continual pre-training (RoPE-512+FoPE-1024) substantially outperforms YARN applied in the same setting. This is a valuable result for the practical use case of extending existing models.

**What was not tested:** The paper does not test whether FoPE can be applied as a **training-free** modification to a frozen RoPE model. The Fourier weight matrix is frozen (no gradients), but the model's query and key projection weights are updated during the ~1B token continual pre-training. It is unclear how much of the benefit comes from the FoPE architecture versus the additional training. A test of simply swapping RoPE for FoPE in a frozen pretrained model (without any further training) would clarify whether the Fourier Series structure alone provides immediate benefits or requires the model to adapt its projections.

Additionally, only YARN is tested as the baseline extrapolation method—other popular methods like NTK-aware scaling, Self-Extend (Jin et al., 2024), or CLEX (Chen et al., 2024a) are not compared. The claim that FoPE "holds the potential to enhance all RoPE-based open-source models" is aspirational rather than demonstrated; only the OLMo and SmolLM architectures are tested.

#### Claim 5: "FoPE modifies attention's frequency-domain properties to improve periodic extension"

This is the paper's **central mechanistic claim, and the evidence for it is partial**.

**What was demonstrated:** The theoretical framework (Section 2–3) provides a rigorous explanation of how periodic extension should work and why it breaks. The toy model experiment (Figure 3) shows FoPE producing attention scores closer to the ground-truth multi-frequency signal. The main results show FoPE enabling length generalization where RoPE fails catastrophically, consistent with the periodic extension mechanism.

**What was not demonstrated:** The paper does not directly measure **periodic extension**—it measures length generalization (perplexity, accuracy), which is the downstream consequence of preserved periodicity but not the same thing. A direct test would measure whether the attention patterns at position n and position n + N_ωm are actually similar under FoPE versus RoPE. Are attention maps more periodic under FoPE? Do the frequency-domain properties of attention weights show less spectral distortion? These measurements are absent.

The Figure 3 toy experiment is suggestive but operates on a minimal two-frequency model with a single-layer MLP—it is not obvious that the finding scales to deep transformers with hundreds of dimensions per head. The gap between the toy model and the full 1.2B-parameter transformer is substantial.

#### Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:

1. **Small number of training lengths tested.** All pre-training experiments use exactly one training length (512 tokens). The paper argues that this demonstrates length generalization, which is correct, but it does not show how FoPE's benefits scale with training length. Would a model trained on 2048 tokens and tested at 32768 show the same advantage? The optimal σ and D likely depend on training length, but this relationship is unexplored.

2. **Passkey Retrieval as the sole long-context task for pre-training experiments.** While Passkey Retrieval is a standard benchmark for length generalization, it tests only one capability: retrieving a single piece of information from a specific position. It does not test multi-hop reasoning across long contexts, long-range dependency resolution, or the ability to integrate information from multiple distant positions. The fine-tuning experiments partially address this with summarization and QA, but the core pre-training results rely on a narrow task.

3. **No experiments on models larger than 1.7B parameters.** The largest model trained from scratch is 1.2B (OLMo), and the fine-tuning experiments use 1.7B (SmolLM). Modern production LMs are 7B–70B+. It is unknown whether FoPE's benefits persist or change at larger scales, where the nature of Spectrum Damage may differ (more layers = more accumulated corruption, but also more capacity to learn compensatory representations).

4. **Hyperparameter scaling heuristics are not validated.** The paper uses σ = 0.3/0.4/0.6 for 60M/180M/1.2B models based on grid search at 60M and heuristic scaling. Whether this linear extrapolation of σ with model size is correct is untested—it could be that σ plateaus or has a non-monotonic relationship with scale.

5. **No direct comparison with NTK-aware scaling or Self-Extend.** The paper compares against YARN as the representative extrapolation method, but other widely-used methods (NTK-aware scaling, Self-Extend) are not included. Since these methods also modify frequency-domain properties (by rescaling RoPE frequencies), they are natural comparison points for evaluating whether FoPE's more structural approach is necessary.

6. **Statistical significance testing is absent.** The paper does not report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical tests for any of the main results. With 1000 Passkey trials, the accuracy estimates are reasonably precise, but the perplexity measurements and downstream task scores are point estimates without uncertainty quantification.

7. **The perplexity advantage on C4 is modest compared to ALiBi.** Figure 1(b) shows FoPE slightly worse than ALiBi on perplexity. The paper explains this well (short-distance dependency dataset), but this means a practitioner choosing a position embedding based solely on pre-training perplexity might prefer ALiBi over FoPE—and then discover at deployment that their model cannot retrieve long-range information. The paper could more explicitly guide this decision.

#### Experiments that would have strengthened the paper:

1. **Causal test of Spectrum Damage mechanism**: Train models with modified architectures (no activation functions, reduced linear layer width) and measure whether FoPE's advantage over RoPE shrinks. This would directly test whether linear layers and nonlinearities are the causal source of the problem.

2. **Direct measurement of periodic extension**: Visualize attention maps to show whether FoPE actually produces more periodic attention patterns than RoPE at extended lengths. Measure the autocorrelation of attention scores at lag N_ωm.

3. **Scaling to larger models (7B+)**: Even a single experiment at 7B scale would substantially strengthen the claim that FoPE is a general solution rather than a small-model phenomenon.

4. **Training length scaling**: Train models at multiple training lengths (256, 512, 1024, 2048) and measure whether FoPE's extrapolation ratio (max tested length / training length) is consistent or changes.

5. **Comparison with NTK-aware scaling and Self-Extend**: Include these methods in Figure 4 to show whether FoPE's approach is competitive with or superior to frequency-rescaling methods.

6. **Freezing study for post-hoc application**: Test whether applying FoPE's Fourier weight matrix to a frozen RoPE model (zero additional training) provides any immediate benefit, to decompose the architectural contribution from the training contribution.

In summary, the experimental evidence is **substantial and consistent with the paper's claims**, with the Passkey Retrieval results being particularly compelling. However, the evidence is more correlational than causal with respect to the Spectrum Damage mechanism, and several practical questions about scaling, hyperparameter transfer, and comparison with a wider range of baselines remain open. The paper's theoretical framework is its strongest contribution; the experiments validate that FoPE works but do not fully validate *why* it works in the precise terms the theory predicts.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted for in FoPE's Efficiency Gains

The entire theoretical framework of FoPE depends on knowing the training sequence length N to define the floor frequency ω_l = 2π / N, which determines which frequency components are zeroed out versus preserved. However, this dependency has a practical consequence that the paper does not fully address: **the floor frequency cutoff is a function of the specific training length, and changing the training length changes which dimensions are zeroed.** This is not a one-time cost but a structural coupling between FoPE's behavior and a training-time hyperparameter.

**Consequence.** If a practitioner wants to apply FoPE as a post-hoc correction to an existing RoPE-pretrained model (as demonstrated in Figure 4), they must decide what value to use for `max_sequence_length` when computing the floor frequency. Using the original pretraining length faithfully reproduces the theoretical motivation (zero out frequencies the model never saw complete cycles of), but it also means that all the dimensions zeroed during pretraining remain zeroed during fine-tuning, even if the fine-tuning data contains longer sequences that might partially train some of those frequencies. Conversely, using the fine-tuning length as the floor frequency would keep more dimensions active but risks relying on frequency components that are still undertrained relative to the new length. The paper does not provide guidance on this choice.

The paper acknowledges this indirectly in Section 3.3: "Although previous works (Peng et al., 2023) have identified this issue, we are the first to model it from a Fourier perspective and provide a theoretical explanation." But the practical implication—that FoPE's floor frequency is a hyperparameter that couples training length to model architecture—is not discussed as a deployment consideration.

**What evidence exists.** The continual pre-training experiments (Figure 4) use N = 1024 for both RoPE-to-FoPE and FoPE-to-FoPE transfer, which means the floor frequency is adjusted to the new (longer) training length. This works well, but it effectively changes which dimensions are zeroed compared to the original N = 512 pretraining. The paper does not ablate whether using the original N = 512 floor frequency (i.e., keeping the original zeroed dimensions even when fine-tuning on longer data) would work better or worse for the RoPE-512+FoPE-1024 case. This missing ablation is significant because it conflates two mechanisms: (1) the architectural benefit of the Fourier Series representation, and (2) the change in which frequencies are zeroed. A practitioner cannot determine which mechanism is responsible for the improvement in Figure 4.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this directly. The ablation in Figure 6(a) compares "FoPE w/o CF" (no floor clipping) against full FoPE, but this is done only for the 60M model trained from scratch, not for the continual pre-training transfer scenario. The paper's recommendation to use `max_sequence_length` as the floor frequency cutoff is stated in the code (Appendix B: `inv_freq[inv_freq < 2 * torch.pi / self.config.max_sequence_length] = 0`) but not discussed as a design decision with tradeoffs. Future work should explicitly study how the floor frequency should be set during continual pre-training when the original pretraining length and the target length differ.

### Single Training Length and Narrow Extrapolation Range

All pre-training experiments use a single training length of 512 tokens and test extrapolation to a maximum of 8192 tokens (16×). While this demonstrates impressive length generalization within this range, **the paper provides no evidence that FoPE's benefits continue to hold at longer extrapolation ratios** (e.g., training at 2048 and testing at 32768, or training at 512 and testing at 65536).

**Consequence.** There are two distinct concerns here. First, the Fourier Series expansion introduces D harmonic frequencies per dimension. Some of these harmonics may themselves have periods shorter than the training length (and thus be adequately trained), while others may have longer periods. As the testing length grows, the harmonics with longer periods will eventually become undertrained relative to the extrapolated positions, potentially recreating the same problem that FoPE was designed to solve—but now for the harmonic components rather than the base frequencies. The paper's theoretical analysis of undertrained frequencies (Section 3.3) applies to the base frequencies ω_m in RoPE, but the harmonics {ω} introduced by the Fourier Series weight matrix have their own periods that may exceed the testing length, creating a new source of spectral artifacts.

Second, the empirical evidence for FoPE's stability is concentrated in the 512-to-8192 range. Figure 1(a) shows FoPE maintaining ~85% Passkey accuracy at 8192, which is 16× training length. But the curve is slightly downward-sloping: accuracy at 8192 is visibly lower than at 512. If the trend continues (and there is no theoretical reason to believe it must plateau), FoPE would eventually degrade to unacceptable accuracy at some longer distance. The paper does not establish where this breakdown occurs or whether it is fundamentally different from RoPE's catastrophic cliff at 2×.

**What evidence exists.** Figure 1(a) is the only direct evidence for extrapolation range, and it stops at 8192. The fine-tuning experiments (Table 1) test up to "8k+" but without specifying the exact maximum length, and the base model (SmolLM-1.7B) was pretrained at 2048, so the extrapolation ratio is at most 4× (8k/2k) in those experiments—substantially less ambitious than the 16× shown in pre-training. The paper does not report Passkey Retrieval results at longer testing lengths for any model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. Section 5.2 presents the 16× extrapolation results as evidence of generalization without discussing whether the observed slight downward trend in FoPE accuracy at 8192 might indicate an eventual breakdown. A more complete analysis would test whether the Fourier Series mechanism has its own extrapolation limits and characterize them.

### The Spectrum Damage Mechanism Is Supported by Correlation, Not Causal Evidence

The paper's central theoretical contribution is that Spectrum Damage—caused by linear layers and activation functions outside attention and time-domain truncation inside attention—is the mechanism by which RoPE's periodic extension breaks. FoPE is designed specifically to address this mechanism. However, **the paper provides no experiment that causally tests whether these specific architectural components are the source of the problem.**

**Consequence.** Without causal evidence, an alternative explanation for FoPE's success cannot be ruled out: FoPE may work better than RoPE simply because it introduces more parameters into the position encoding (the Fourier weight matrix W^F, even though it is frozen) or because it changes the effective frequency allocation (by zeroing out some dimensions and expanding others), rather than because it specifically addresses spectral leakage and distortion from upstream components. The correlation between the theoretical analysis and the empirical results is suggestive but not conclusive.

If the Spectrum Damage hypothesis is correct, then reducing the sources of damage should reduce FoPE's advantage over RoPE. For example: training a model without activation functions (or with linear activations like ReLU replaced by identity) should eliminate Spectrum Distortion and narrow the gap between FoPE and RoPE. Training a model with smaller linear layers (reducing the extent of cross-dimensional mixing) should reduce Spectrum Leakage and similarly narrow the gap. Neither experiment is performed, nor is any other causal intervention on the proposed mechanism.

This matters for both scientific understanding and practical guidance. If the mechanism is correct, then future work should focus on reducing spectral corruption at its source (e.g., designing activation functions that preserve frequency-domain structure, or adding spectral regularization to linear layers). If the mechanism is incorrect or incomplete, then such efforts would be misdirected, and the community should instead focus on understanding what FoPE actually does mechanistically.

**What evidence exists.** The evidence for the Spectrum Damage mechanism is entirely theoretical (Section 3) and observational (Figures 3, 7, Table 2). The theoretical analysis (Sections 3.2–3.3) is mathematically rigorous: it shows that linear layers produce mixing, that nonlinear functions applied to sinusoids generate harmonics, and that time-domain truncation introduces sinc artifacts. But these are *capability demonstrations*—they show that the model architecture *can* produce Spectrum Damage, not that it *does* in practice to a degree that explains RoPE's failure. The toy model experiment (Figure 3) demonstrates multi-frequency content in a single-layer MLP, which is a far simpler system than a deep transformer. The QK Norm experiment (Table 2) provides causal evidence for the undertrained frequency mechanism specifically, but not for the linear layer and activation function mechanisms.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim to provide causal evidence and does not attempt causal interventions. Section 5.6 is titled "Empirically Validation of FoPE's Mechanism" but the validation is limited to: (1) showing that a toy MLP produces multi-frequency attention scores that FoPE represents better than RoPE (correlational), and (2) showing that QK Norm helps only when undertrained frequencies are present (causal for the truncation mechanism, but not for the leakage/distortion mechanism). The paper could strengthen its claims by explicitly acknowledging that the causal status of the Spectrum Damage hypothesis is unverified and proposing the intervention experiments described above as future work.

### No Experiments at Production Scale (7B+ Parameters)

The largest model trained from scratch with FoPE is 1.2B parameters (OLMo framework), and the fine-tuning experiments use SmolLM-1.7B. **Modern production LMs are typically 7B–70B+ parameters, and the paper provides no evidence that FoPE's benefits scale to these sizes.**

**Consequence.** This is not merely a "more compute would be nice" limitation—there are specific reasons to expect that FoPE's behavior might change at larger scales:

1. **Spectrum Damage may intensify with depth.** Larger models are typically deeper (more layers). Each additional layer adds another round of linear mixing and nonlinear distortion. A 70B model might have 80 layers versus the 8–16 layers in the paper's experiments, meaning hidden states pass through 5–10× more spectral corruption before reaching any given attention layer. It is possible that at some depth, the accumulated Spectrum Damage becomes so severe that even FoPE's Fourier Series representation cannot disentangle the frequency components, leading to degradation at extended lengths even under FoPE.

2. **The optimal hyperparameters (σ and D) may not scale linearly.** The paper heuristically scales σ from 0.3 (60M) to 0.4 (180M) to 0.6 (1.2B) and D from 64 to 128. Whether this linear extrapolation continues to hold at 7B or 70B is unknown. It is equally possible that σ saturates (because Spectrum Damage does not grow unboundedly with model size) or that D needs to be much larger (because more layers generate higher-order harmonics that require a larger Fourier basis to represent).

3. **The floor frequency cutoff interacts with model depth in unexplored ways.** In deeper models, information from undertrained frequencies may propagate across multiple layers before reaching attention, potentially creating more complex artifacts than the single-layer sinc-distortion modeled in Equation 10.

**What evidence exists.** The paper tests three model scales: 60M, 180M, and 1.2B for pre-training, plus 1.7B for fine-tuning. Consistency across these scales is demonstrated (Figures 1, 5, Table 1), which is encouraging but does not guarantee extrapolation to 7B+. The paper acknowledges the scale limitation only implicitly, by noting that PaLM 2-S* is "representative" in the related work discussion, but does not dedicate a limitation subsection to scale.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames the three-scale consistency as evidence of robustness without discussing the substantial gap between 1.2B/1.7B and production-scale models. A 7B-scale experiment (even a single run) would substantially strengthen the claim that FoPE is a general solution rather than a small-to-medium-model phenomenon. The computational cost of such an experiment is acknowledged to be high, but feasibility at 7B scale (with existing open-source frameworks like OLMo) is not out of reach for a paper making architectural claims intended to influence production model design.

### Passkey Retrieval Is the Sole Long-Context Benchmark for Pre-Training Experiments

The paper's most dramatic result—FoPE maintaining ~85% accuracy at 16× training length while RoPE collapses to zero—is demonstrated exclusively on Passkey Retrieval (Figure 1a). **Passkey Retrieval tests only one narrow capability: finding a single, salient piece of information (a five-digit number) in an otherwise meaningless context.** It does not test multi-hop reasoning across long contexts, integration of information from multiple distant positions, long-range coreference resolution, or any other complex long-context capability.

**Consequence.** A model that excels at Passkey Retrieval may still fail at tasks that require genuinely understanding and reasoning over long contexts. Passkey Retrieval is essentially a "needle in a haystack" task—the signal (a random five-digit number) is maximally distinct from the noise (repeated meaningless text), making it the easiest possible long-context retrieval scenario. Real-world long-context tasks rarely have this property; relevant information is typically embedded in semantically meaningful text and must be identified through comprehension, not pattern matching.

The fine-tuning experiments (Table 1) partially address this concern by testing summarization and QA tasks at extended lengths. However, these experiments use a different base model (SmolLM-1.7B pretrained at 2048, fine-tuned to 4096) and a different training paradigm (supervised fine-tuning rather than pre-training). The extrapolation ratio in these experiments is at most 4× (8k/2k), compared to the 16× demonstrated in pre-training. More importantly, the fine-tuning results on TriviaQA show FoPE underperforming RoPE at shorter lengths, and the summarization results (GovReport, MultiNews) show more modest gains than the Passkey Retrieval numbers would suggest. This gap between the pre-training Passkey results and the fine-tuning downstream results raises the possibility that FoPE's benefits are largest on the simplest long-context tasks and diminish as task complexity increases.

**What evidence exists.** Table 1 provides the only evidence on complex long-context tasks. The pattern is consistent (FoPE outperforms at extended lengths on all five benchmarks) but the magnitude varies substantially: TREC shows +14–15 point gains, SAMSum shows +9 point gains, while GovReport shows only +0.36–1.15 point gains. The paper does not analyze why gains vary across tasks or whether task characteristics (e.g., reliance on long-range vs. local information) predict FoPE's benefit.

**Mitigation status.** The paper includes the fine-tuning experiments precisely to address this limitation, but does not discuss the gap between Passkey and downstream task results. Section 5.4 presents Table 1 as validation without noting that the gains on complex tasks are substantially smaller than on Passkey Retrieval. The paper could strengthen its claims by: (1) reporting Passkey Retrieval results for the fine-tuned models to enable direct comparison, (2) analyzing which task characteristics predict larger or smaller FoPE benefits, and (3) including additional long-context benchmarks that test reasoning rather than retrieval, such as multi-hop QA or long-document question answering.

### The Fourier Weight Matrix is Frozen—But the Model Adapts to It Through Training, Making Deployment on Frozen Pretrained Models Untested

The paper emphasizes that FoPE's Fourier weight matrices are frozen (no gradients) as an advantage: it prevents overfitting of the position encoding to the training length and adds negligible optimization cost. However, **the model's query and key projection weights are updated during training, which means the model learns to produce q,k vectors that work well with the specific Fourier weight matrices it was trained with.** This has an important consequence for the claimed ability to apply FoPE as a post-hoc correction to existing models.

**Consequence.** In the continual pre-training experiments (Figure 4, RoPE-512+FoPE-1024), the model undergoes ~1B tokens of additional training with FoPE's Fourier weights in place. During this training, the query and key projection matrices are updated and can adapt to the new position encoding scheme. It is unknown how much of the benefit comes from: (a) the Fourier Series architecture itself providing a better inductive bias, versus (b) the additional ~1B tokens of training allowing the model to adjust its representations. If (b) dominates, then FoPE applied without any additional training (i.e., simply swapping the position embedding in a frozen pretrained model) would provide little to no benefit.

This matters for practical deployment. The paper claims that FoPE "holds the potential to enhance all RoPE-based open-source models" (Section 5.3). If applying FoPE requires even a modest amount of continual pre-training (~1B tokens), then the barrier to adoption is substantially higher than if it could be applied as a zero-shot modification. The cost of ~1B tokens of training on a 7B+ model is non-trivial and may not be justified if simpler training-free methods (like NTK-aware scaling) achieve comparable improvements.

Additionally, the Fourier weight matrices are initialized with a specific structure (identity + Gaussian noise) that assumes the model starts from a RoPE-like state. If FoPE were applied to a frozen pretrained model without any training, the random perturbation in the Fourier weights would immediately change the effective position encoding, potentially degrading performance at all lengths. The identity initialization ensures that FoPE starts close to RoPE behavior, which is appropriate when training from scratch, but it means that applying FoPE to a frozen model would introduce a distribution shift in the position encoding that the model's q,k projections are not adapted to.

**What evidence exists.** Figure 4 shows RoPE-512+FoPE-1024 substantially outperforming RoPE-512+YARN-1024, but both conditions receive ~1B tokens of additional training. There is no condition where FoPE is applied to a frozen model (zero additional training) to isolate the architectural contribution. The paper does not report a "FoPE applied at inference time to a RoPE-pretrained model without any fine-tuning" experiment.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. Section 5.3 presents the continual pre-training results as evidence that FoPE can enhance existing models, but the requirement for additional training is implicit rather than stated. A simple experiment—evaluate a RoPE-pretrained model with FoPE weights swapped in at inference time, without any fine-tuning—would immediately clarify whether the Fourier Series architecture provides immediate benefits or requires training to become effective. The omission of this experiment, despite being computationally trivial (it requires no training, only inference), is a notable gap.
