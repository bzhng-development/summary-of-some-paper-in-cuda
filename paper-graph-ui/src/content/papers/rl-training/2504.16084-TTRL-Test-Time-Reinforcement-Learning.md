# TTRL: Test-Time Reinforcement Learning

**ArXiv:** [2504.16084](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16084)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL)**, a framework that enables LLMs to self-improve on unlabeled test data by using test-time scaling—specifically, majority voting over multiple sampled outputs—to estimate reward signals for online RL training.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Test-Time Reinforcement Learning (TTRL)**, a framework that enables LLMs to self-improve on unlabeled test data by using test-time scaling—specifically, majority voting over multiple sampled outputs—to estimate reward signals for online RL training. Applied across AIME 2024, AMC, MATH-500, and GPQA using Qwen-2.5-Math-7B, TTRL yields a 211% pass@1 improvement on AIME 2024 (from 12.9 to 40.2) and a 76.5% average gain across all four benchmarks, while also surpassing the initial model's majority-voting ceiling (maj@n). The paper establishes that self-supervised RL on test data can approach the performance of training directly on test data with ground-truth labels, but only when the base model possesses sufficient prior knowledge—on the hardest MATH-500 questions, TTRL's relative improvement drops by roughly half compared to easier questions.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: How Can LLMs Learn Without Labels?

The demand for LLMs to solve increasingly complex reasoning tasks — mathematical proofs, competitive programming, scientific discovery — has driven a paradigm where models are trained on massive labeled datasets. These datasets are typically curated through expensive human annotation (as in RLHF; Ouyang et al., 2022) or constructed from verifiable problem-answer pairs (as in DeepSeek-R1's rule-based RL; Guo et al., 2025). However, as the paper observes in Section 1, "complex and unlabeled questions continuously emerge," and even cutting-edge systems like OpenAI's o3 solve only 4% of problems on the newly released ARC-AGI-2 benchmark. The fundamental problem is that **real-world tasks increasingly arrive as streams of unlabeled data where ground-truth answers are unknown**, yet existing RL methods for LLM reasoning assume access to verifiable rewards.

This matters for two reasons. First, **scalability**: human annotation costs grow linearly with the ambition of the task, creating an economic ceiling on how much labeled training data can be produced. If models can instead learn from their own outputs on unlabeled data — effectively generating their own supervision — the bottleneck shifts from human labor to compute, which scales more predictably. Second, **adaptation**: even a model extensively post-trained on static labeled datasets will encounter distribution shift when deployed on new benchmarks or real-world queries. The ability to adapt at test time without labeled examples is the difference between a model that works well on its training distribution and one that can improve continuously as it encounters novel problems.

The paper explicitly frames this through the lens of Silver & Sutton (2025)'s call for an "era of experience," where AI systems "autonomously improve via RL on unlabeled data by directly engaging in self-experience and learning." TTRL is positioned as a concrete step toward this vision: a mechanism that enables models to treat their own test-time behavior as a source of training data.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies specific limitations across two broad research threads that TTRL aims to bridge: test-time techniques and RL for reasoning.

#### Test-Time Scaling (TTS) Does Not Change the Model

The paper categorizes existing test-time methods into two families: Test-Time Inference (TTI) and Test-Time Training (TTT), as defined in Appendix C. TTI encompasses strategies like majority voting (Wang et al., 2022), best-of-N with reward models (Stiennon et al., 2020; Nakano et al., 2021), and tree search (Zhou et al., 2023) — all of which improve output quality by allocating more compute at inference but **leave the model parameters unchanged**. These methods can boost accuracy on a given prompt but do not produce any lasting improvement: the model's internal capabilities remain static, so the same test-time compute must be re-spent on every new query.

On the other hand, Test-Time Training (TTT) — originally developed for vision tasks to handle distribution shift (Sun et al., 2019) — does update model parameters at inference time using auxiliary self-supervised losses computed from the test input itself. Recent work has extended TTT to LLMs (Hardt & Sun, 2024; Akyürek et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a), but these approaches use self-supervised objectives (e.g., nearest-neighbor retrieval, token prediction) rather than task-specific reward signals. As the paper notes in Section 5.1, "the integration of test-time scaling with reinforcement learning remains largely underexplored." The gap TTRL fills is precisely this: **using the outputs of test-time scaling (majority voting) as a reward mechanism for test-time RL training**, merging the inference-time optimization of TTI with the parameter-update benefits of TTT.

#### RL for Reasoning Requires Labels (or at Least Verifiable Outputs)

The second thread is the recent success of RL in producing large reasoning models. DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) demonstrated that GRPO with rule-based rewards — verifying whether a generated answer matches a known correct answer — can elicit long chain-of-thought reasoning without human preference data. Subsequent work has refined these algorithms (Cui et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025b) and scaled them across various base models (Hu et al., 2025a; Zeng et al., 2025).

However, the paper identifies a critical limitation shared by all these methods (Section 5.2):

> "these methods typically train LLMs only on supervised training data, while inference involves generating extended CoT reasoning on unseen test problems. Moreover, current RL approaches depend on verifiable outputs — such as solutions in mathematics or code — that can provide reliable reward signals."

In other words, existing RL for reasoning requires knowing the ground-truth answer at training time to compute whether a generated answer is correct. This creates a bootstrapping problem: to train a model to solve hard problems, you need labeled hard problems, but labeling hard problems is precisely the expensive bottleneck that better reasoning is meant to overcome.

#### Self-Rewarding and Self-Play Methods Target Different Problems

The paper acknowledges prior work on self-supervised fine-tuning that appears related but operates under fundamentally different assumptions. Self-rewarding LMs (Yuan et al., 2025) and self-play fine-tuning (Chen et al., 2024) train models to generate their own training signals, but these methods target **open-domain instruction following** — where output quality is judged by a learned preference model or an LLM-as-judge — rather than objective reasoning tasks with deterministic correct answers. They also typically use offline preference optimization (DPO; Rafailov et al., 2023) rather than online RL, which the paper argues is critical for TTRL's ability to improve beyond its own initial majority-voting ceiling (Section 4.2). The paper also cites concurrent work (Xu et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b; Zhao et al., 2025) exploring self-supervised reasoning, but notes that TTRL's use of majority voting — rather than learned verifiers or preference models — "mitigates reward hacking" because the reward is a simple consensus signal derived from the model's own outputs rather than an estimated scalar from a potentially fallible reward model.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

TTRL is introduced as a **preliminary step toward RL with self-labeled rewards** that makes a specific, concrete design choice: use majority voting over N sampled outputs to estimate a pseudo-label, then apply standard rule-based verification (does this output match the pseudo-label?) as the reward. This is not presented as a theoretical breakthrough in RL algorithms — the paper explicitly states that "at the algorithmic level, TTRL is not fundamentally different from existing RL algorithms" (Section 4.3) — but rather as a **demonstration that test-time compute can be repurposed as a training signal**, converting the typically one-shot benefit of majority voting into a recurrent improvement loop.

The paper's positioning can be understood through three boundary conditions it establishes:

1. **TTRL is not a replacement for labeled-data RL, but a complementary capability.** The paper's upper-bound experiment (RL with leaked labels, Section 4.1) shows that TTRL approaches but does not surpass supervised RL, and the difficulty analysis (Section 4.3) shows it fails when prior knowledge is insufficient. TTRL works best when the model already has some non-trivial probability of generating correct answers — the "Lucky Hit" phenomenon described in Section 4.2 depends on outputs being scattered enough that incorrect answers still receive correct negative rewards.

2. **TTRL is an online process, not a static self-training pipeline.** Unlike prior self-training methods (Huang et al., 2022), which select high-confidence outputs via majority voting for SFT and then stop, TTRL's online RL loop means that as the model improves, the quality of its voting-based supervision signal also improves, creating a virtuous cycle. This is what enables TTRL to surpass the initial model's maj@n — a result the paper frames as exceeding the "traditional self-training upper bound" (Section 4.1).

3. **TTRL treats test data as both an evaluation target and a training resource.** This is a deliberate departure from the standard train-test split convention. The paper argues that for a deployed system encountering novel problems, the separation between "training" and "testing" is artificial — if a model can safely improve on incoming data without labeled supervision, it should. The paper's framing of this as "test-time adaptation" connects to the broader TTT literature while extending it to RL-based reasoning improvement.

In the taxonomy of test-time methods (Appendix C, Table 5), TTRL is categorized as a **test-time training** approach that uses **test-time inference** (specifically majority voting) as its auxiliary supervision mechanism. This makes it distinct from both pure TTI (which improves outputs without updating parameters) and prior TTT (which uses self-supervised objectives unrelated to task performance). The paper's ambition, as stated in Section 7, is to scale this approach to "real-time learning scenarios, where models interact with continuously arriving data and adapt dynamically," and eventually to "agentic tasks and multi-step scientific reasoning."

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

TTRL is an online reinforcement learning procedure that allows a pretrained LLM to improve its reasoning ability on a set of unlabeled test questions without ever seeing the correct answers. The paper solves the problem of *where to get reward signals when labels are absent* by using majority voting over multiple sampled outputs as a self-consistency mechanism: the most common answer becomes a pseudo-label, and the model is rewarded for generating outputs that agree with it. The solution has the shape of a closed loop — the model generates answers, votes on them to estimate what is probably correct, treats that estimate as a reward signal for RL, updates its parameters, and then generates better answers in the next iteration, which in turn produce more accurate majority-vote pseudo-labels.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The TTRL system consists of four components connected in a cycle:

- **Policy Model (the LLM):** The pretrained model `$\pi_\theta$` being adapted. It receives a question `$x$` as input and generates `$N$` candidate answers `$\{y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_N\}$` by repeated sampling.
- **Answer Extractor:** A deterministic function that parses each generated response to isolate the final answer (e.g., the number or expression at the end of a chain-of-thought). This is standard in math reasoning evaluation.
- **Majority Voting Reward Function:** The core innovation. It extracts answers from all `$N$` candidates, identifies the most frequent answer (the consensus `$y^*$`), and assigns a binary reward of 1 to every candidate whose extracted answer matches `$y^*$` and 0 otherwise. This reward is computed without any access to ground-truth labels.
- **RL Optimizer (GRPO, PPO, or PRIME):** The online RL algorithm that takes the policy model's generated outputs and their majority-voting rewards, computes a policy gradient, and updates the model parameters `$\theta$`. The updated model then generates better candidates in the next training step.

Information flows as follows: a batch of unlabeled questions enters → the policy model samples `$N$` answers per question via parallel generation → the answer extractor parses each response → majority voting determines the consensus answer for each question → each response receives a binary reward based on agreement with the consensus → the RL algorithm updates the policy model using these rewards → the updated model generates new samples in the next iteration, producing higher-quality consensus signals.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal TTRL objective (Equation 1) and gradient update (Equation 2), establishing the mathematical framework that connects test-time sampling to RL.
- **Second**, the majority voting reward function (Equation 3 and Listing 1), since it is the linchpin that converts unlabeled sampling into a usable training signal, and its behavior determines when TTRL succeeds or fails.
- **Third**, the practical implementation choices — how the vote-then-sample strategy reduces computational cost, the specific RL algorithms and their hyperparameters, and the training dynamics metrics — because these engineering decisions make the abstract framework computationally tractable.
- **Fourth**, the design rationale connecting these components: why majority voting rather than a learned reward model, why online RL rather than offline SFT, and how the "Lucky Hit" phenomenon makes the approach robust even when the consensus label is wrong.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methods paper** whose core idea is that test-time compute — specifically, the ability to generate and vote on multiple candidate answers — can be repurposed as an online training signal for RL, enabling model improvement on unlabeled test data through a self-reinforcing cycle where better models produce better consensus labels which in turn produce better training.

#### The TTRL Objective: RL with Self-Estimated Rewards

The paper formalizes TTRL as a standard RL objective where the key departure is that the reward signal comes from the model's own outputs rather than from an external verifier:

$$\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(y, y^*)]$$

where `$\pi_\theta(y | x)$` is the policy model parameterized by `$\theta$` that maps an input prompt `$x$` to a distribution over outputs `$y$`, `$y^*$` is the consensus answer derived from majority voting over `$N$` sampled outputs from `$\pi_\theta$`, and `$r(y, y^*)$` is the binary reward function that returns 1 if the extracted answer from `$y$` matches `$y^*$` and 0 otherwise.

**What this objective computes:** the expected reward of sampling an output from the current policy, where "reward" means agreement with the majority vote. The expectation is taken over the policy's own output distribution — as the model generates different answers with different probabilities, the objective measures how often those answers align with what the model collectively considers correct. Maximizing this objective means making the model more likely to produce outputs that agree with its own consensus.

**Why this form:** the objective uses the policy's own distribution both for sampling (`$\pi_\theta$`) and for defining the reward target (`$y^*$` is derived from samples of `$\pi_\theta$`). This self-referential structure is what distinguishes TTRL from supervised RL (where the reward comes from an external source) and from pure test-time inference (where parameters are frozen). The objective inherits from standard policy gradient RL — the model improves by reinforcing outputs that lead to high reward — but the key innovation is that the reward function `$r(y, y^*)` is *endogenous*: it is computed entirely from the model's own behavior at the current step. This means the objective evolves as the model evolves, creating the potential for a virtuous cycle (better model → better consensus → better rewards → better model).

The corresponding parameter update is a standard policy gradient ascent step:

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta \nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}[r(y, y^*)]$$

where `$\eta$` is the learning rate. The gradient `$\nabla_\theta \mathbb{E}[r(y, y^*)]$` is estimated using the sampled outputs and their majority-voting rewards, with the specific estimator depending on the chosen RL algorithm (GRPO, PPO, or PRIME).

**What this update computes:** at each training step, the model generates a batch of outputs, receives rewards via majority voting, and the parameters are adjusted in the direction that increases the probability of high-reward outputs (those matching the consensus) and decreases the probability of low-reward outputs (those disagreeing with the consensus). The expectation is approximated using the finite sample of `$N$` outputs per question.

**Why this form:** the policy gradient formulation enables the model to learn from its own behavior without needing to know the ground-truth correct answer. The gradient signal comes from the relative reward of different outputs for the same question — outputs that agree with the majority are reinforced, outputs that disagree are penalized. This is crucial because it means TTRL does not need to identify *which* answer is correct; it only needs to identify *which answers are more common*, which is a purely statistical property of the model's output distribution. The use of policy gradient (rather than, say, supervised fine-tuning on the majority answer) is also critical: online RL enables the model to explore and discover new correct answers that were initially rare, gradually shifting the consensus toward the truth, whereas offline SFT on the initial majority vote would lock in the model's initial biases.

#### The Majority Voting Reward Function

The reward function is the component that makes TTRL possible without labels. Given a question `$x$` and `$N$` sampled outputs from the policy model, the procedure (as shown in Listing 1 of the paper) operates in three stages:

**Stage 1: Answer Extraction.** Each of the `$N$` generated outputs is passed through a deterministic answer extractor that parses the final answer from the chain-of-thought text. The paper uses standard extraction routines from the math reasoning literature (matching boxed expressions or final lines). The result is a multiset of `$N$` extracted answers `$P = \{\hat{y}_1, \hat{y}_2, \ldots, \hat{y}_N\}$`.

**Stage 2: Label Estimation via Majority Voting.** The majority answer `$y^*$` is identified as the most frequent element in `$P$`:

$$y^* = \arg\max_{a} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}[\hat{y}_i = a]$$

where `$a$` ranges over all unique answers appearing in the multiset `$P$`, and `$\mathbb{1}[ \cdot ]$` is the indicator function. In the event of ties, the paper's implementation (as shown in Listing 1) uses `Counter.most_common(1)[0]`, which returns the first element encountered at the maximum frequency — the tie-breaking behavior depends on Python's dictionary iteration order, but this is a minor detail since ties are rare with `$N = 64$` samples.

**What this step computes:** given `$N$` possibly different answers produced by the same model for the same question, this step identifies the answer that the model *most consistently produces*. This is a statistical estimate of what the model "believes" is correct, based on the assumption that the model's most common output is more likely to be correct than its less common outputs.

**Why this form:** majority voting is chosen over alternatives (e.g., using a separate reward model, training a verifier, or using the model's own confidence scores) for three reasons. First, it is **parameter-free** — no additional model needs to be trained, avoiding the distribution shift and over-optimization issues that plague learned verifiers (as extensively documented in the prior paper on test-time compute scaling). Second, it **leverages test-time compute** that is already being spent on sampling — the `$N$` outputs used for consensus estimation are the same outputs used for RL training, so the computational overhead is shared. Third, it is **robust to reward hacking** (Section 5.2): because the reward is a simple binary signal from a transparent voting mechanism rather than a scalar from a neural network, the model cannot easily exploit the reward function through adversarial outputs that score highly under a learned verifier but are incorrect.

**Stage 3: Reward Assignment.** Each sampled output `$\hat{y}_i$` receives a binary reward:

$$R(\hat{y}_i, y^*) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } \hat{y}_i = y^* \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where `$y^*$` is the majority-voted label from stage 2. The collection of `$N$` binary rewards for the `$N$` outputs becomes the training signal for the RL update.

**What this step computes:** a simple one-hot-like vector of rewards where outputs that agree with the consensus receive positive reinforcement and outputs that disagree receive zero (or, effectively, negative reinforcement relative to the baseline in advantage-based RL algorithms like GRPO).

**Why this form:** the binary reward based on exact string match with the estimated label mirrors the rule-based reward used in supervised RL for reasoning (Guo et al., 2025), where the reward is 1 if the output matches the known correct answer and 0 otherwise. The departure is that the "correct answer" here is the model's own majority vote rather than ground truth. This design choice means TTRL inherits the simplicity and stability of rule-based verification while making it applicable to unlabeled data. Alternative designs — such as using a continuous reward based on answer similarity or using the model's log-probability of the output — would introduce additional assumptions about what constitutes a "partially correct" answer and could incentivize degenerate behaviors (e.g., generating answers that are similar but not identical to the consensus, or gaming probability estimates).

#### Practical Implementation: Vote-Then-Sample Strategy

A straightforward implementation of TTRL would generate `$N$` outputs, compute majority voting rewards on all `$N$`, and use all `$N$` for the RL update. However, the paper observes that this is computationally inefficient: while large `$N$` is beneficial for accurate label estimation (majority voting improves with more samples), using all `$N$` for training increases the memory and compute cost of the RL update (which requires storing activations and computing gradients for each output).

The paper introduces a **vote-then-sample** strategy (Section 3.1, Implementation Details) to decouple label estimation from training:

- **Rollout phase:** For each question, the model generates `$N = 64$` outputs using a temperature of 0.6 (or 1.0 for Qwen2.5-Math and LRMs). All 64 outputs are used for majority voting to estimate the pseudo-label `$y^*$`.
- **Downsampling phase:** From the 64 outputs, 32 are randomly downsampled per question for the actual RL training step. Only these 32 outputs receive rewards and contribute to the policy gradient.

**What this achieves:** the label estimation benefits from the higher statistical power of 64 samples (the majority answer with 64 samples is more likely to be correct than with 32), while the RL training benefits from lower memory usage (only 32 trajectories need to be stored and backpropagated through). The paper reports that this strategy "effectively reduces computational costs while still achieving strong performance."

**Why this ratio:** the specific numbers (64 for voting, 32 for training) are empirical choices, not derived from theory. The 2:1 ratio means that half the computational cost of generation is used purely for better label estimation. This tradeoff is in the spirit of test-time compute allocation: spend some compute on improving the quality of the supervision signal before spending the rest on learning from it. The paper does not ablate different ratios, leaving this as an implicit design choice.

#### RL Algorithm Selection and Hyperparameters

TTRL is a framework that can operate with any online policy-gradient RL algorithm. The paper primarily uses **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization; Shao et al., 2024), the algorithm popularized by DeepSeek-R1, but also demonstrates compatibility with PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) and PRIME (Cui et al., 2025) in Section 3.2.

**GRPO specifics (Section 3.1, Implementation Details):**
- **Optimizer:** AdamW for the policy model.
- **Learning rate schedule:** cosine decay with a peak value of `$5 \times 10^{-7}$`. This is notably conservative — typical RL fine-tuning for LLMs uses learning rates in the `$10^{-6}$` to `$10^{-5}$` range. The low learning rate is presumably chosen to prevent catastrophic forgetting and to maintain stability given the noise in self-estimated rewards.
- **Rollout temperature:** 0.6 for most models, 1.0 for Qwen2.5-Math base models and LRMs. The higher temperature for math-specialized and reasoning models encourages more diverse exploration, which is important for discovering correct answers that are initially low-probability.
- **Top-p sampling:** 0.95, following standard practice for diverse generation.
- **Maximum generation length:** 3,072 tokens for standard models, 32,768 tokens for LRMs (consistent with the longer chain-of-thought traces that reasoning models produce).
- **Number of episodes:** 10 for MATH-500 (smaller, more structured dataset), 30 for AMC, and 80 for AIME 2024 (harder, sparser dataset). This scaling reflects the intuition that smaller datasets — AIME 2024 has only 30 questions — require more passes through the data (episodes) for the online RL process to converge, since each episode provides fewer unique questions.

**PPO and PRIME compatibility (Figure 5):** The paper reports that applying TTRL with PPO (a value-model-based method) and PRIME (a process-level RL algorithm that uses implicit rewards at each reasoning step) produces "closely aligned" performance trajectories to GRPO on MATH-500 with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B. This is significant because it shows that TTRL is not exploiting any peculiar property of GRPO's group-based advantage normalization — the majority voting reward works as a drop-in replacement for ground-truth rewards across different RL algorithmic families.

**Why GRPO as the default:** GRPO computes advantages within a group of outputs for the same prompt, normalizing rewards relative to the group mean. This is well-suited to TTRL because the majority voting reward is computed per-question from a group of outputs — GRPO's group-based structure naturally aligns with the sampling structure already required for label estimation. In contrast, PPO's value function would need to learn to predict the expected majority-voting reward, adding an extra component that would also need to adapt as the model's output distribution shifts during training.

#### Training Dynamics and Monitoring Without Ground-Truth

A practical challenge of TTRL is that, during training, the practitioner cannot evaluate the model on held-out labeled data to decide when to stop training or whether training is proceeding correctly — by assumption, no labeled data is available. The paper introduces a set of **training-time metrics** (Appendix B) that can be monitored without ground-truth labels to assess training health and select checkpoints.

**Metrics computable without ground-truth labels:**

- **Entropy:** The entropy of the model's output distribution, measuring the uncertainty or diversity of generated responses. High entropy indicates exploration; decreasing entropy over training (as shown in Figure 5b and Figure 12b) suggests the model is converging to a more deterministic policy.
- **Majority Voting Reward:** The average reward computed from the majority-voted label. This is the training objective itself — tracking it indicates whether the model is learning to agree with its own consensus.
- **Majority Ratio:** The frequency of the most common answer within a rollout of `$N$` outputs. This measures how concentrated the model's output distribution is — a low majority ratio (e.g., 16.6% on AIME 2024 with the initial Qwen2.5-Math-7B, as reported in Section 4.2) indicates scattered, diverse outputs, while an increasing majority ratio over training indicates the model is converging toward a consistent answer.

**Metrics requiring ground-truth labels (used only for analysis, not training):**

- **Label Accuracy (maj@n):** The fraction of questions for which the majority-voted label matches the ground-truth answer. This is the accuracy of the supervision signal itself.
- **Reward Accuracy:** The fraction of individual output rewards (as computed from the majority-voted label) that match the rewards that would have been assigned using the ground-truth label. This measures how faithfully the majority-voting reward approximates the ideal supervised reward.
- **Ground-Truth Ratio:** The frequency of the correct answer within a rollout. This measures whether correct answers are gaining probability mass under the policy.

**What these metrics reveal about training dynamics (Figure 6 and Figure 9):** On AMC with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, both the average pass@1 within rollouts (avg@16) and the majority voting accuracy (maj@16) show "a consistent upward trend" over 240 training steps. This is the key evidence for the self-reinforcing cycle: as training progresses, the model's individual outputs become more accurate (avg@16 increases from roughly 0.30 to 0.45), *and simultaneously* the consensus label becomes more accurate (maj@16 increases from roughly 0.44 to 0.56). The two improvements are coupled — better individual outputs lead to better consensus labels, which provide better training signals, which further improve individual outputs.

On AIME 2024 (Figure 9), a more challenging benchmark, the paper tracks Majority Ratio, Label Accuracy, and Reward Accuracy separately. The Majority Ratio starts at only 16.6% (the most common wrong answer accounts for only one-sixth of outputs), and Label Accuracy starts at 37% (majority voting gets the correct answer only about one-third of the time). Despite this low label accuracy, Reward Accuracy starts at an impressive 92% — meaning that even though the pseudo-label is often wrong, the *rewards assigned to individual outputs* are mostly correct because of the "Lucky Hit" phenomenon.

#### The "Lucky Hit" Phenomenon: Why TTRL Works with Inaccurate Labels

The most counterintuitive finding in the paper's analysis is that TTRL can make progress even when the majority-voted pseudo-label is incorrect. The mechanism is explained in Section 4.2 and illustrated with a toy example (Figure 10):

Consider a question whose true answer is 3. The model samples `$N = 8$` predictions: {1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 4, 5, 6}. With no ground-truth access:

- **True rewards** (using ground-truth label 3): {0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0} — all outputs are wrong, so all receive 0 reward.
- **Estimated label via majority voting:** 2 (appears 3 times, most frequent).
- **Estimated rewards** (using estimated label 2): {0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0} — outputs matching the wrong label 2 receive a reward of 1.

**What happens:** even though the estimated label (2) is wrong, the *rewards assigned to the incorrect predictions* {1, 4, 5, 6} are still 0 — which is exactly the correct reward they would have received under the ground-truth label. The only "errors" in the reward signal are that the three outputs predicting 2 receive a reward of 1 when they should receive 0. But crucially, these outputs are *already wrong* — reinforcing them does not make the model more wrong than it already is, because the model does not know which of its wrong answers is "less wrong" in the absence of ground truth.

**Why this enables learning:** the reward signal correctly identifies which outputs are *not* the consensus and penalizes them. This has the effect of collapsing the model's output distribution away from scattered, diverse wrong answers and toward a more concentrated output (even if initially concentrated on the wrong answer). Once the model's outputs are less scattered, the majority ratio increases, and eventually the consensus itself may shift toward the correct answer as the model explores and occasionally samples it. The high Reward Accuracy (92% on AIME 2024, Figure 9) — which measures how often the majority-voting reward matches the hypothetical ground-truth reward — means that the vast majority of gradient updates push in the correct direction (away from wrong answers), even though the label used for verification is often wrong.

This is formalized in the paper's observation that "for an incorrectly predicted answer, even if the estimated label does not match the ground truth label, as long as it differs from the predicted answer, the verifier will still output a negative reward, and this is exactly the correct reward that we expect." In other words, for any given output, the probability of receiving the *correct* reward from majority voting depends on whether the output happens to match the estimated label. When outputs are highly scattered (low majority ratio, as in the initial AIME 2024 case), most outputs will *not* match the estimated label, so most outputs will receive correctly negative rewards. The only "lucky hit" that matters for learning is that incorrect outputs are correctly penalized — and this happens automatically whenever the model's outputs are diverse.

**Connection to the self-reinforcing cycle:** this mechanism explains the dynamics in Figure 9. As training progresses, the Majority Ratio increases (from ~17% to over 50%), meaning the model's outputs become less scattered and more concentrated. This makes the majority-voted label more likely to be correct (Label Accuracy increases from 37% to over 50%), which further improves Reward Accuracy. The system bootstraps from a regime where rewards are accurate *despite* inaccurate labels (due to output scatter) to a regime where rewards are accurate *because* labels are accurate (due to improved model capability).

#### Design Choices: Why Not Alternatives?

The paper's methodology embodies several deliberate design choices that distinguish TTRL from plausible alternatives:

**Why majority voting rather than a learned reward model?** A natural alternative would be to train a process reward model (PRM) or outcome reward model (ORM) on the model's outputs and use that model's score as the reward for RL. The paper implicitly argues against this through its choice of majority voting and its explicit statement (Section 5.2) that the approach "mitigates reward hacking." A learned verifier trained on the model's own outputs would be vulnerable to the same over-optimization problems documented in the test-time compute scaling literature — the model could learn to generate outputs that score highly under the verifier without being correct. Majority voting avoids this because the reward is a transparent, non-parametric function: the only way to increase reward is to actually agree with the most common answer. There is no learned surface to exploit.

**Why online RL rather than SFT on majority-voted answers?** Prior self-training methods (Huang et al., 2022) select high-confidence outputs via majority voting, treat them as pseudo-labels, and fine-tune the model via supervised learning on those pseudo-labels. TTRL departs from this in two ways. First, **online vs. offline:** TTRL updates the model continuously as it generates new outputs, meaning the training data distribution evolves with the model. In contrast, SFT-based self-training generates a static dataset from the initial model and trains on it — if the initial model's majority vote is wrong on hard questions, the SFT model learns to reproduce those wrong answers. Second, **RL vs. SFT generalization:** the paper cites Chu et al. (2025) to argue that "RL tends to generalize better than SFT, which often relies on memorizing training data," and that "rewards are typically vague and serve primarily as directional signals for exploration." In TTRL, outputs that disagree with the consensus are penalized but not eliminated — the model can still explore those outputs with reduced probability, potentially discovering that they are actually correct if the consensus shifts.

**Why `$N = 64$` samples for voting?** The paper does not ablate this choice, but the number is consistent with prior work on majority voting for math reasoning. Larger `$N$` increases the statistical accuracy of the majority vote (the probability that the majority answer equals the correct answer increases with `$N$`, assuming the correct answer has a higher sampling probability than any individual wrong answer), but also increases computational cost linearly. The choice of 64 represents an empirical tradeoff, and the vote-then-sample strategy (downsampling to 32 for training) partially decouples the label estimation quality from the training cost.

**Why per-benchmark training rather than multi-task training?** The paper applies TTRL to each benchmark *independently* and then evaluates cross-task generalization (Figure 4). This design choice likely reflects the test-time adaptation framing: the model encounters a specific set of unlabeled questions (the benchmark) and adapts to them. Training on all benchmarks simultaneously would test TTRL's ability to improve from a larger unlabeled corpus, but the per-benchmark design isolates the question: "given a specific set of unlabeled test questions, can TTRL improve the model on those questions?"

**Why a low learning rate (`$5 \times 10^{-7}$`)?** Standard RL fine-tuning for LLMs (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 used `$1 \times 10^{-6}$` for GRPO) uses higher learning rates. The conservative choice in TTRL likely reflects the noisier reward signal (majority voting rewards are less accurate than ground-truth rewards, especially early in training) and the risk of catastrophic forgetting or policy collapse if the learning rate is too high. The paper does not ablate learning rate but notes in Section 4.3 that "hyperparameter settings play a crucial role in RL training" and that "the influence of hyperparameters is further amplified in TTRL due to potential noise in reward estimation."

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Test-Time Majority Voting Is a Viable and Self-Improving Training Signal for RL

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is **repurposing test-time compute — specifically, the consensus from majority voting over multiple sampled outputs — as an endogenous reward signal for RL training on unlabeled data.** This is not an incremental refinement of existing reward estimation techniques; it is a reframing of what majority voting *means* in the LLM reasoning pipeline.

**Prior framing:** Majority voting (Wang et al., 2022) has been understood exclusively as a **test-time inference** strategy — a way to boost accuracy on a given prompt by aggregating multiple samples, but one that operates transiently and leaves the model unchanged. The computational cost of sampling N outputs is spent once per query and discarded. In self-training pipelines (Huang et al., 2022), majority-voted answers are occasionally used as pseudo-labels for supervised fine-tuning, but this is an **offline, one-shot** process: the pseudo-labels are generated once from a frozen model, then used for SFT, and the cycle stops there. The model never gets to improve the quality of its own pseudo-labels because the label generator is static.

**TTRL's reframing:** The paper demonstrates that majority voting can serve as an **online, recurrent training signal** within an RL loop. The critical insight — which is non-obvious from prior work — is that the consensus label is not a fixed target but a **moving target that improves as the model improves.** Because TTRL uses online RL rather than offline SFT, the model generates new outputs at each training step, recomputes the majority vote from its (now improved) outputs, and uses the (now more accurate) consensus as the reward for the next update. This creates what the paper calls a "self-reinforcing loop" (Section 4.2), where better individual outputs produce better consensus labels, which produce better training signals, which further improve individual outputs.

**Why this is distinctive, not obvious:** At first glance, training a model to agree with its own majority vote sounds circular — if the initial model's consensus is wrong on hard questions, wouldn't training on it simply amplify the model's mistakes? The paper identifies the mechanism that breaks this circularity: the **"Lucky Hit" phenomenon** (Section 4.2, Figure 10). Even when the majority-voted pseudo-label is incorrect, the rewards assigned to individual outputs are *mostly correct* when the model's outputs are highly scattered, because most wrong outputs happen to disagree with the wrong consensus label. This means the RL gradient initially pushes the model *away* from diverse wrong answers, collapsing the output distribution toward greater concentration. Once the distribution is less scattered, the consensus itself can shift toward the correct answer as the model occasionally samples it. The system leverages output scatter — normally considered a weakness — as a source of reward accuracy during early training, then transitions to leveraging genuine label accuracy as the model improves.

This insight elevates TTRL from a simple trick ("just use majority voting as the reward") to a conceptual contribution about the **dynamics of self-supervised RL**: the conditions under which a self-estimated reward signal can bootstrap improvement beyond its own initial accuracy. The paper's evidence for this dynamic is Figure 6 and Figure 9, which show avg@16 and maj@16 both rising in tandem over training — the model does not merely approach its initial maj@n ceiling but exceeds it, demonstrating that the training signal itself improves during the process.

**Significance beyond performance:** This finding challenges the implicit assumption in the RL-for-reasoning literature that RL requires an external, static source of ground-truth verification (whether human labels or rule-based answer checking). It suggests that for tasks where the model has some non-trivial probability of generating correct answers (even if it cannot reliably identify them), the model's own statistical behavior — properly harnessed through online RL — can substitute for external supervision. This is a **fundamental shift** in how to think about the relationship between test-time compute and training: test-time sampling is not just an inference-time optimization but a **generative resource for training data creation.**

### Innovation 2: Exceeding the Self-Training Upper Bound Through Online RL

A second conceptual contribution is the empirical demonstration that **online RL on self-estimated rewards can surpass the performance ceiling that bounds traditional self-training methods.** This finding redefines what "unsupervised improvement" means for LLMs and has implications for how the field thinks about the limits of learning from unlabeled data.

**Prior framing:** In standard self-training (Huang et al., 2022), the model generates outputs, selects high-confidence ones (e.g., via majority voting), treats them as pseudo-labels, and performs supervised fine-tuning. The performance of this procedure is **upper-bounded by the quality of the pseudo-labels** — specifically, by the accuracy of the initial model's majority vote (maj@n). If the initial model's consensus is only 44% accurate, the SFT model trained on those pseudo-labels cannot exceed 44% accuracy (and typically achieves less, since SFT can memorize incorrect labels). This ceiling is fundamental to offline self-training because the label generator is frozen: the model can only learn to reproduce its own initial biases.

**TTRL's reframing:** The paper shows that TTRL systematically **exceeds this bound**, with the final avg@16 score surpassing the initial maj@16 score by over 20 points on AMC (Figure 6) and the final avg@64 exceeding the initial maj@64 across all benchmarks (Figure 7). This is possible because TTRL's online nature means the model is not learning from a static set of pseudo-labels but from a **continuously improving label generator** — the model itself, at each training step. The analogy the paper draws (Section 4.1) — "the model lifts itself up by its own bootstraps" — is apt: each RL update improves the model's individual accuracy slightly, which improves the next round's consensus accuracy slightly, which improves the next round's training signal slightly, and the cumulative effect pushes the model past the ceiling that would constrain any single-round self-training approach.

**Why this is distinctive:** Prior work on self-play and self-rewarding (Chen et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025) has shown that models can improve from their own outputs, but these methods typically use learned verifiers or preference models that themselves introduce ceilings (the verifier's accuracy bounds the reward quality). TTRL's majority voting reward is fundamentally different because it is **non-parametric and self-improving**: the reward function's accuracy is directly tied to the model's own output distribution and therefore automatically improves as the model improves. There is no separate verifier model that needs to be updated or that can become a bottleneck.

The empirical magnitude of the ceiling violation is substantial: on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B, the initial model's maj@64 is 33.0%, while TTRL's avg@64 reaches 40.0% (Figure 7). This means TTRL's *single-sample* performance after training exceeds the initial model's *64-sample aggregated* performance — a striking demonstration that the gains are not merely from better aggregation but from genuine improvement in the model's individual reasoning capability.

**Significance beyond performance:** This finding establishes a new performance regime for unsupervised learning on reasoning tasks. It means that the practical limit of self-improvement is not the initial model's consensus accuracy but rather some (currently uncharacterized) higher bound related to the model's prior knowledge and the difficulty of the task. The paper's difficulty-bin analysis (Section 4.3, Table 3) begins to sketch where this bound lies — on the hardest MATH-500 questions (Level 5), TTRL's improvement drops to 75.3% relative gain (vs. 175.3% on Level 1), suggesting that the ceiling is related to whether the model's prior distribution puts *any* probability mass on correct solutions. This is a **diagnostic contribution**: it provides a methodology (comparing TTRL performance to initial maj@n and to oracle RL-with-labels) for assessing what fraction of a model's capability gap is addressable through self-supervised test-time adaptation vs. requiring additional pretraining or labeled data.

### Innovation 3: A Unified Framework Connecting Test-Time Inference and Test-Time Training

The paper makes a **taxonomic and architectural contribution** by situating TTRL at the intersection of two previously disconnected research threads: Test-Time Inference (TTI — using extra compute at inference to improve outputs without changing parameters) and Test-Time Training (TTT — updating model parameters at test time using self-supervised objectives). This is not merely a classification exercise; it is a design insight about how these two approaches can be **composed synergistically rather than treated as alternatives.**

**Prior framing:** TTI and TTT have been studied as **separate and largely non-interacting** approaches to improving test-time performance. TTI methods like majority voting, best-of-N, and tree search treat the model as fixed and spend compute on exploring its output distribution more thoroughly. TTT methods (Sun et al., 2019; Akyürek et al., 2024) update model parameters at inference time but use auxiliary self-supervised losses (e.g., reconstruction, contrastive learning on the test input itself) that are disconnected from the task-specific objective — they help the model adapt to distribution shift but do not directly optimize for better task performance. The two families have developed in parallel with little cross-pollination: the TTI literature focuses on search algorithms and verifier design; the TTT literature focuses on adaptation objectives and architectural modifications for fast test-time updates.

**TTRL's reframing:** TTRL uses a TTI technique (majority voting) **as the auxiliary loss for TTT.** The paper's architecture diagram (Figure 2) captures this explicitly: TTS (test-time scaling, i.e., majority voting) produces a reward signal, which feeds into TTT (RL training), which updates the model, which then produces better outputs for the next round of TTS. The two components are not just combined — they are **mutually reinforcing.** Better TTS (more accurate majority voting from a better model) improves the TTT training signal; better TTT (more capable model from RL) improves the TTS consensus. Neither component works as well in isolation: TTS alone provides no lasting improvement (the model is unchanged), and TTT alone with a self-supervised objective (as in prior TTT work) would not directly target task accuracy.

This framing has implications beyond the specific mechanism of majority voting. The general principle is that **test-time compute can serve dual purpose**: it can directly improve outputs (the traditional TTI role) while simultaneously generating a training signal for model updates (the TTRL role). Any TTI method that produces a quality signal — whether majority voting, verifier scores, or self-consistency checks — could in principle be plugged into the TTRL framework as a reward source. The paper's experiments with different RL algorithms (GRPO, PPO, PRIME; Figure 5) suggest that the framework is modular along this dimension as well.

**Why this is distinctive:** Prior work has not conceptualized TTI and TTT as components of a single closed-loop system. The paper's taxonomy (Appendix C, Table 5) is the first to place these approaches in a common framework where the output of TTI feeds into the objective of TTT. This is more than a classification — it is a **design pattern** for building self-improving systems at test time, and it suggests a research agenda where improvements to either component (better TTI aggregation, more stable RL algorithms) compound in the integrated system.

**Significance beyond performance:** This unification addresses a gap the paper identifies in Section 5.1: "the integration of test-time scaling with reinforcement learning remains largely underexplored." By providing a concrete instantiation (TTRL) and demonstrating its effectiveness, the paper opens a design space for hybrid test-time methods that were not previously considered. For example, one could imagine replacing majority voting with best-of-N weighted by a process reward model, or replacing GRPO with an algorithm specifically designed for the dynamics of self-estimated rewards. The framework makes these extensions legible as variations on a common template rather than entirely separate research directions.

### Innovation 4: Characterizing the Failure Boundary — Prior Knowledge as the Limiting Factor

The paper's most diagnostically valuable contribution may be its **explicit characterization of when self-supervised test-time RL fails**, which provides a boundary condition that is as informative as the success cases. This is not a negative result per se, but a **calibration of expectations** that transforms TTRL from a mysterious black-box improvement into a predictable tool with known applicability conditions.

**Prior framing:** The self-training and self-play literatures have generally reported positive results on their chosen benchmarks, but without a systematic analysis of *where* the methods stop working. The implicit message has been "self-improvement helps, and more compute makes it help more." The recent test-time compute scaling literature (Snell et al., 2024) partially addresses this by showing that test-time compute benefits are difficulty-dependent, but those analyses are for inference-time methods that do not change the model — they do not address the question of whether *training* on self-estimated signals has similar difficulty-dependent behavior.

**TTRL's reframing:** The paper's difficulty-bin ablation (Section 4.3, Table 3) provides a clear empirical law: **TTRL's effectiveness is monotonically decreasing with problem difficulty**, measured both in absolute accuracy gain and in relative improvement. On MATH-500 questions binned by the dataset's own difficulty labels (Levels 1 through 5), using Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B:

- **Level 1 (easiest):** 175.3% relative improvement (25.9 → 71.2 accuracy)
- **Level 2:** 130.8% (33.0 → 76.2)
- **Level 3:** 110.2% (36.3 → 76.3)
- **Level 4:** 80.4% (32.5 → 58.7)
- **Level 5 (hardest):** 75.3% (22.3 → 39.2)

The pattern is clear: TTRL helps everywhere, but the **marginal benefit decreases as the task outstrips the model's prior knowledge.** The paper draws the explicit conclusion: "as the question difficulty increases, both the performance improvement and length reduction ratios tend to decrease. This suggests that the available prior knowledge of the backbone is insufficient to support learning on more challenging questions."

**Why this is distinctive:** This finding establishes a **boundary condition** for the "era of experience" vision (Silver & Sutton, 2025) that the paper invokes. Self-supervised RL on unlabeled data can amplify existing capability — turning a model that occasionally gets the right answer into one that consistently gets it — but it **cannot create capability from nothing.** If the model's prior distribution puts negligible probability mass on correct solutions for a class of problems (the bin 5 regime where the initial model scores 22.3%), TTRL can improve performance but cannot close the gap to high accuracy. The self-reinforcing loop has a bootstrapping requirement: there must be *some* correct answers in the initial output distribution for the consensus to occasionally be correct, which then provides a toehold for RL to reinforce.

This is a **fundamental limitation,** not an implementation detail. It means TTRL is complementary to, not a replacement for, pretraining on harder data or larger models with stronger priors. The paper's other finding — that larger models benefit more from TTRL (1.5B → 7B → 32B scaling in Section 3.2) — reinforces this: stronger priors provide a better starting distribution, which enables more effective self-improvement.

**Significance beyond performance:** This characterization of the failure boundary does two things for the field. First, it provides a **practical decision rule** for when to deploy TTRL vs. invest in labeled data or larger pretraining: if the model's initial pass@1 on a task is non-trivially above zero (the bins 1–4 regime), TTRL is likely to help; if pass@1 is near zero (bin 5), TTRL will not be sufficient. Second, it suggests a **research direction** that the paper does not pursue: combining TTRL with curriculum learning or difficulty filtering, where the model first applies TTRL to easier subsets to build capability, then gradually tackles harder subsets. The failure on hard questions is not necessarily permanent — it may be addressable by sequencing the self-improvement process.

### Innovation 5: Reward Accuracy Can Be High Even When Label Accuracy Is Low

The most counterintuitive analytical contribution of the paper is the **decoupling of label accuracy from reward accuracy** through the "Lucky Hit" mechanism (Section 4.2), which explains *why* TTRL works even when the model's majority vote is mostly wrong. This is not merely an explanation of TTRL's empirical success — it is a **diagnostic concept** that reframes how to evaluate the quality of self-supervised training signals for categorical verification tasks.

**Prior framing:** In supervised RL for reasoning, label accuracy and reward accuracy are identical: if the ground-truth label is correct, all rewards derived from it are correct. In self-training, the quality of pseudo-labels is assessed by their agreement with ground truth — low label accuracy is taken as evidence that the training signal is poor. The implicit assumption is that **label quality and training signal quality are the same thing.**

**TTRL's reframing:** The paper demonstrates that for rule-based verification (binary match/no-match against a label), label accuracy and reward accuracy can **diverge dramatically** under the right conditions. On AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Figure 9), the initial Label Accuracy (maj@n) is only 37%, but the Reward Accuracy is 92%. The mechanism: when outputs are highly scattered (Majority Ratio = 16.6%), most sampled outputs disagree with *both* the correct answer *and* the (incorrect) majority-voted label. As a result, the reward assigned to those outputs (0) is correct even though the label used to compute that reward is wrong. The only "reward errors" occur for outputs that happen to match the wrong label — but these outputs are already wrong, so reinforcing them does not make the model more incorrect than it already is (in the absence of ground truth, the model cannot distinguish between different wrong answers).

This insight is captured quantitatively in the paper's tracking of three metrics — Majority Ratio, Label Accuracy, and Reward Accuracy — over training (Figure 9). The figure shows that Reward Accuracy starts high and stays high, while Label Accuracy starts low and gradually improves. This means TTRL operates in a **reward-rich but label-poor regime** during early training, which is precisely what enables the bootstrapping process.

**Why this is distinctive:** This finding challenges the intuition that a self-supervised training signal is only as good as its pseudo-label accuracy. It shows that for tasks with **large output spaces and scattered incorrect answers,** the quality of the *reward signal* can be substantially higher than the quality of the *label* used to generate it. This is not a general property of all self-supervised objectives — it is specific to rule-based verification where the reward function is an indicator of exact match — but it applies broadly to mathematical reasoning, code generation, and any domain where outputs can be verified by string match against a reference.

The practical implication is that **low majority-voting accuracy is not a contraindication for TTRL.** A model that achieves only 37% maj@n on AIME 2024 might appear too weak for self-training, but TTRL succeeds because the *per-output* rewards are mostly correct. The diagnostic to watch is not label accuracy but **reward accuracy,** which can be estimated from the output distribution's scatter (low majority ratio → high reward accuracy, because most outputs will not match whatever label is chosen). This is a new **evaluation concept** for self-supervised RL systems that the paper introduces and operationalizes through its training metrics (Appendix B).

**Significance beyond performance:** This insight suggests that TTRL (and similar self-rewarding approaches) are most naturally suited to **hard tasks where the model is uncertain** — exactly the regime where one might intuitively expect self-supervision to fail. On easy tasks where the model's outputs are concentrated (high majority ratio), reward accuracy and label accuracy converge, and TTRL behaves similarly to supervised RL. On hard tasks where outputs are scattered, reward accuracy decouples from label accuracy, and TTRL can bootstrap from a reward-rich signal despite poor labels. This is a **non-obvious sweet spot** that prior work did not identify, and it reframes hard unlabeled problems as *more tractable* for self-supervised RL than moderately difficult ones where the model is confidently wrong (high majority ratio on an incorrect answer).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** TTRL is evaluated on four benchmarks: **AIME 2024** (30 competition-level math problems), **AMC** (a set of American Mathematics Competition problems), **MATH-500** (500 problems from Hendrycks et al., 2021), and **GPQA-Diamond** (a graduate-level Q&A subset from Rein et al., 2024). The paper notes in Appendix A that "our training data size matches the corresponding benchmark dataset size" — meaning TTRL trains and evaluates on the same set of unlabeled questions, a deliberate departure from standard train-test splits.
- **Base model(s).** The primary experiments use models from four families across multiple scales: **Qwen family** (Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-32B, Qwen3-8B in both thinking and non-thinking modes), **LLaMA family** (LLaMA-3.1-8B-Instruct, LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct, LLaMA-3.2-3B-Oat-Zero), **Mistral family** (Mistral-Nemo-Instruct-2407, Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410), and **DeepSeek family** (DeepSeek-Math-7B-Instruct, DeepSeek-R1-LLaMA-8B). The paper also tests Skywork-OR1-Math-7B. The rationale for this breadth, as stated in Section 3.1, is to follow Shao et al. (2025)'s recommendation that "evaluating different models for RL-based methods" is necessary to validate experimental conclusions. The Qwen2.5-Math-7B receives the most extensive analysis across all four benchmarks and serves as the primary reference model.
- **Metrics.** The main reported metric is **pass@1 accuracy**, computed from 16 sampled responses per question (4 for the 32k context setting) at temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95, following the protocol of Chen et al. (2021). For analysis experiments with Qwen2.5-Math, the paper switches to **greedy decoding pass@1** for fair comparison with prior work. Appendix B defines additional training-time metrics: **Entropy** (generation uncertainty), **Majority Voting Reward** (the training objective), **Majority Ratio** (frequency of the most common answer in a rollout), **Label Accuracy** (maj@n — whether the majority vote matches ground truth), **Reward Accuracy** (fraction of majority-voting rewards matching ground-truth-derived rewards), and **Ground-Truth Ratio** (frequency of the correct answer in a rollout). The last three require ground-truth labels and are used only for analysis, not training.
- **Baselines.** Since the paper notes that "the use of TTT for reasoning has not been previously explored," the **primary baseline is the pretrained backbone model itself** — the comparison is before vs. after TTRL. Appendix A (Table 4) also reports comparisons against state-of-the-art "R1-Zero-Like" models trained on large-scale labeled data: **DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B and 7B** (Guo et al., 2025; trained on 800K labeled examples), **SimpleRL-Zero-7B** (Zeng et al., 2025; 8.9K examples), **PRIME-Zero-7B** (Cui et al., 2025; 230K examples), **OpenReasoner-Zero-7B** (Hu et al., 2025b; 129K examples), **Oat-Zero-1.5B and 7B** (Liu et al., 2025b; 8.9K examples), and **LIMR-7B** (Li et al., 2025; 1.4K examples). The paper acknowledges this comparison "seems unfair" given TTRL's unsupervised setting.
- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures compute indirectly through the number of sampled outputs per question: **N = 64** for majority voting label estimation, with **downsampling to 32** for RL training. The "vote-then-sample" strategy means that 64 outputs are generated per question per training step, but only 32 participate in gradient computation. The paper does not report total FLOPs or wall-clock time, but states all experiments were conducted on "8 * NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs." The number of training episodes varies by dataset: 10 for MATH-500, 30 for AMC, and 80 for AIME 2024, with the rationale that "smaller and more difficult datasets need more episodes to achieve sufficient exploration" (Section 4.3). The learning rate is set to 5 × 10⁻⁷ with cosine schedule.
- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper explicitly operates in a **test-time training paradigm** where the model trains directly on the evaluation data — this is by design, not an oversight, as the goal is adaptation to unlabeled test inputs. There is no cross-validation or held-out evaluation set during TTRL training; the model is evaluated on the same questions it trained on. For the cross-task generalization experiments (Figure 4), the model is trained on one benchmark and evaluated on others, providing an out-of-distribution test of whether improvements transfer. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for any results.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Performance Across Models and Benchmarks

Table 1 presents the headline results for six models across four benchmarks. The most dramatic single-dataset improvement is on **AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B**, where TTRL raises pass@1 from 12.9 to 40.2 — a 211.6% relative improvement. Across all four benchmarks, the same model achieves an average relative gain of 76.5% (from 31.1 to 54.9 average accuracy). The pattern is consistent across model families and scales:

- **Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B**: Average gain of 74.4% (23.5 → 41.0). The largest absolute gain is on MATH-500 (+40.3 points, from 32.7 to 73.0), while GPQA shows only a +1.2 point gain (from 24.9 to 26.1).
- **Qwen2.5-Math-7B**: Average gain of 76.5% (31.1 → 54.9). Note the anomalous -1.4 point drop on GPQA (29.1 → 27.7), which the paper does not comment on — this is the only negative ∆ in Table 1.
- **Qwen2.5-7B** (vanilla base model, not math-specialized): Average gain of 43.7% (33.8 → 48.5). The gain on MATH-500 is +20.0 points (60.5 → 80.5), somewhat smaller than the math-specialized model's +36.7.
- **Qwen2.5-32B**: Average gain of 57.7% (32.4 → 51.1). The scaling from 7B to 32B yields a larger average gain, supporting the paper's claim that "larger models can produce more accurate majority voting rewards during self-improvement" (Section 3.2).
- **LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct**: Average gain of 30.6% (26.8 → 35.0). The gains are smaller than Qwen models, which may reflect weaker initial math reasoning priors.
- **Qwen3-8B (non-thinking mode, 3k context)**: Average gain of 20.0% (53.8 → 64.5). The smaller relative gain here is expected since this model starts from a much higher baseline (it is already an instruct model with substantial post-training).

Table 2 extends these results to seven additional models from the LLaMA, Mistral, and DeepSeek families. Key findings:

- **DeepSeek-R1-LLaMA-8B**, already a strong reasoning model (51.7 on AIME 2024), improves by +17.5 points to 69.2 — a 33.8% relative gain. On AMC, it improves from 81.6 to 88.9 (+7.3 points). This is notable because it shows TTRL can improve models that have already undergone extensive RL post-training.
- **DeepSeek-Math-7B-Instruct** shows the weakest gains: only +0.6 on AIME 2024 (1.9 → 2.5) and +6.6 on AMC. This model starts from very low AIME performance, suggesting that its prior knowledge — or its ability to produce diverse outputs — may be insufficient for TTRL's bootstrapping mechanism.
- **Mistral-Nemo-Instruct** shows a -0.8 point drop on AIME 2024 (0.8 → 0.0), the only other negative ∆ besides the GPQA drop for Qwen2.5-Math-7B. The paper does not discuss this failure case.

#### Performance on Large Reasoning Models

Figure 3 zooms in on two LRMs that have already undergone costly post-training. **Qwen3-8B in thinking mode** improves from 66.7 to 75.0 on AIME 2024 (+8.3 points), while **Skywork-OR1-Math-7B** improves from 72.5 to 82.5 (+10.0 points). Both gains are roughly 10 points absolute, despite these models being near the frontier. The paper frames this as evidence that "TTRL remains effective on LRMs that have undergone expensive post-training, especially on highly challenging tasks" (Section 3.2).

#### Test-Time Scaling Improvements (maj@n) After TTRL

Figure 7 reports a critical finding: TTRL not only improves pass@1 but also improves the model's **majority voting performance**. Across AIME 2024, AMC, and MATH-500 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B:

- On **AIME 2024**: The initial model's avg@64 is 11.7 and maj@64 is 33.0. After TTRL, avg@64 rises to 40.0 and maj@64 rises to 44.6 — meaning post-TTRL majority voting (maj@64) is 35.2% higher than pre-TTRL majority voting.
- On **AMC**: Initial avg@64 = 30.0, maj@64 = 56.6. Post-TTRL: avg@64 = 63.9, maj@64 = 66.3.
- On **MATH-500**: Initial avg@64 = 32.3, maj@64 = 63.9. Post-TTRL: avg@64 = 84.2, maj@64 = 85.2.

The most striking comparison: on AIME 2024, **TTRL's avg@64 (40.0) surpasses the initial model's maj@64 (33.0)**. A single sample from the TTRL-adapted model is more likely to be correct than the consensus of 64 samples from the original model. This is the paper's key evidence for the self-reinforcing cycle exceeding the traditional self-training upper bound (Section 4.1).

#### Cross-Task Generalization

Figure 4 tests whether TTRL's improvements transfer to benchmarks the model was not trained on. Six experiments are reported, each training TTRL on one benchmark and evaluating on the other two. With Qwen2.5-Math-7B and greedy decoding:

- **TTRL on AIME 2024 → evaluated on AMC**: 39.8 → 60.1 (+20.3). Evaluated on MATH-500: 52.6 → 75.4 (+22.8). Both are substantial gains despite the distribution shift.
- **TTRL on AMC → evaluated on AIME 2024**: 13.3 → 33.3 (+20.0). Evaluated on MATH-500: 52.6 → 74.8 (+22.2).
- **TTRL on MATH-500 → evaluated on AIME 2024**: 13.3 → 23.3 (+10.0). Evaluated on AMC: 39.8 → 63.9 (+24.1).

AIME 2024 is the hardest generalization target: TTRL on AMC transfers well to AIME (+20.0), but TTRL on MATH-500 transfers less well (+10.0). Conversely, MATH-500 benefits substantially from training on either harder benchmark (+22.8 from AIME training, +22.2 from AMC training). There is no experiment training on GPQA and evaluating on math benchmarks, or vice versa — the cross-task generalization is tested only within mathematical reasoning.

#### Compatibility with Different RL Algorithms

Figure 5 tests whether TTRL's majority voting reward works with different policy-gradient algorithms. On MATH-500 with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, **GRPO**, **PPO**, and **PRIME** all produce closely aligned accuracy and entropy curves over 30 training steps:

- Accuracy rises from roughly 0.38 to 0.68–0.70 for all three algorithms, with the curves "closely aligned" (Section 3.2).
- Entropy declines from roughly 0.62 to 0.42–0.45, again with tight alignment.

The paper interprets this as evidence that TTRL "is not exploiting any peculiar property of GRPO's group-based advantage normalization" and that the majority voting reward "works as a drop-in replacement for ground-truth rewards across different RL algorithmic families." The consistent entropy decline (Figure 5b) is particularly important — it shows that both value-based (PPO), process-level (PRIME), and group-based (GRPO) methods converge to more deterministic policies, suggesting the reward signal is robust enough to drive policy convergence regardless of the advantage estimation method.

#### Comparison with Labeled-Data RL Models

Table 4 in Appendix A provides the most direct comparison between TTRL (unsupervised, training only on the benchmark itself) and prior state-of-the-art RL models trained on large labeled datasets. Using greedy decoding:

- On **AIME 2024**: Qwen2.5-Math-7B with TTRL achieves 43.3, outperforming DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B@3k (10.0), SimpleRL-Zero-7B (26.7), PRIME-Zero-7B (16.7), OpenReasoner-Zero-7B (13.3), and LIMR-7B (32.5). It matches Oat-Zero-7B (43.3) and exceeds its own instruct version (16.7). Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B with TTRL achieves 20.0, matching Oat-Zero-1.5B (20.0) and substantially outperforming DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B@3k (2.5), though trailing DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B@8k (20.0).
- On **MATH-500**: Qwen2.5-Math-7B with TTRL achieves 84.2, exceeding all comparators: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B (60.1), SimpleRL-Zero-7B (78.2), PRIME-Zero-7B (83.8), Oat-Zero-7B (80.0), LIMR-7B (78.0). For the 1.5B model, TTRL achieves 80.0, exceeding all 1.5B comparators including DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B@3k (52.2) and @8k (77.4).
- On **AMC**: Qwen2.5-Math-7B with TTRL achieves 67.5, exceeding all listed 7B comparators.

The paper explicitly notes the unfairness: TTRL uses only the benchmark itself as training data (30 questions for AIME, ~500 for MATH-500), while comparator models use 1.4K to 800K labeled training examples. Despite this massive data disadvantage, TTRL is competitive or superior on most benchmarks. The exceptions — on AIME 2024, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-1.5B@8k and Oat-Zero-1.5B match TTRL's 20.0 — suggest that for the smallest model on the hardest benchmark, the advantage of labeled data remains relevant.

#### Training Dynamics: The Self-Reinforcing Cycle

Figure 6 shows the training dynamics of TTRL on AMC with Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B over 240 steps. Two metrics are tracked:

- **avg@16** (average pass@1 within each rollout of 16): rises from approximately 0.30 to 0.45, with a fairly steady upward trend.
- **maj@16** (majority voting accuracy on 16 samples): rises from approximately 0.44 to 0.56, also steadily upward.

The crucial observation is that both curves rise **together** — the model's individual accuracy improves, and simultaneously its consensus accuracy improves. The final avg@16 (~0.45) exceeds the initial maj@16 (~0.44), consistent with the "exceeds the self-training upper bound" claim. The paper notes (Section 4.1) that "the final avg@16 score exceeds the initial maj@16 score by more than 20 points" — this appears to be scaled to percentage (0.45 vs. 0.24 on the initial avg@16, which would be a 21-point gap), though the exact numbers are read from the plot.

Figure 9 tracks three additional metrics on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B over 45 steps:

- **Majority Ratio**: starts at approximately 0.17, rises to 0.53. This is the frequency of the most common answer in a rollout — the 3× increase indicates the output distribution becomes substantially more concentrated over training.
- **Label Accuracy (maj@n)**: starts at approximately 0.37, rises to 0.53. The accuracy of the majority-voted pseudo-label improves by 16 percentage points.
- **Reward Accuracy**: starts at approximately 0.92, rises to 0.93. This metric is already very high at initialization and barely improves, consistent with the "Lucky Hit" explanation — reward accuracy is high from the start because scattered outputs mostly receive correct negative rewards.

The figure supports the bootstrapping narrative: early training is characterized by low label accuracy but high reward accuracy (the "Lucky Hit" regime), while later training transitions to higher label accuracy as the model's outputs become more concentrated and the consensus more often correct. Reward accuracy stays high throughout, meaning the training signal quality is maintained as the regime shifts.

#### Difficulty-Dependent Performance

Table 3 breaks down TTRL's performance on MATH-500 by the dataset's five difficulty levels, using Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B:

| Difficulty | Backbone Acc. | TTRL Acc. | Gain (abs.) | Gain (rel.) | Length Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Level 1 (easiest) | 25.9 | 71.2 | +45.4 | ↑175.3% | ↓73.3% |
| Level 2 | 33.0 | 76.2 | +43.2 | ↑130.8% | ↓71.1% |
| Level 3 | 36.3 | 76.3 | +40.0 | ↑110.2% | ↓68.3% |
| Level 4 | 32.5 | 58.7 | +26.2 | ↑80.4% | ↓55.9% |
| Level 5 (hardest) | 22.3 | 39.2 | +16.8 | ↑75.3% | ↓43.7% |

Both the absolute accuracy gain and the relative improvement decrease monotonically with difficulty. The backbone model's performance is not monotonic (Level 2 and 3 start higher than Level 1, likely due to how MATH-500 difficulty labels are assigned relative to this specific model's capabilities), but the TTRL gains clearly diminish as questions get harder. The response length reduction shows the same pattern: TTRL shortens responses by 73.3% on Level 1 (from 2,339 to 624 tokens) but only 43.7% on Level 5 (1,751 to 985).

The paper interprets this as evidence that "the available prior knowledge of the backbone is insufficient to support learning on more challenging questions" (Section 4.3). TTRL improves performance everywhere, but the **improvement capacity scales with how much the model already knows** — more prior knowledge → larger gains.

#### Upper Bound Analysis: TTRL vs. RL with Leaked Labels

Figure 8 directly compares TTRL (unsupervised) with **RL (Leakage)** — the same RL algorithm but with access to ground-truth labels for reward computation, effectively training on the test data with full supervision. On MATH-500 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B over 50 steps:

- Both curves start around 0.57–0.58 accuracy and rise to roughly 0.78 (RL Leakage) and 0.75 (TTRL).
- The gap between the curves is roughly 2–3 percentage points throughout most of training, with TTRL tracking the leakage curve closely.
- The paper states that "the performance curve of TTRL closely approaches that of RL (leakage)," which is supported by the nearly parallel trajectories.

This is the **strongest evidence that TTRL's reward estimation is near-optimal** for this task. The 2–3 point gap represents the irreducible cost of not having ground-truth labels — the majority voting reward is slightly noisier or less accurate than the perfect reward — but the gap is small relative to the total improvement (~20 points). The paper does not show this comparison on AIME 2024 or other harder benchmarks, where the gap might be larger given the lower label accuracy on those tasks.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Effect of RL algorithm choice (Figure 5):** TTRL is tested with GRPO, PPO, and PRIME on MATH-500 using Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B. All three algorithms produce nearly identical accuracy and entropy trajectories over 30 steps, with accuracy reaching ~0.68–0.70 and entropy declining from ~0.62 to ~0.42–0.45. This demonstrates that TTRL's majority voting reward is a general-purpose signal compatible with value-based (PPO), process-level (PRIME), and group-based (GRPO) advantage estimation, not an artifact of one algorithm's design.

**Effect of temperature (Figure 11, failed attempts):** The paper reports that setting temperature to 1.0 (vs. the successful 0.6) causes training to fail, with persistently high entropy that does not diminish. The explanation: higher temperature "increases the model's output entropy" and "promotes more extensive exploration," but in TTRL's setting with noisy rewards, excessive exploration may prevent the policy from converging. This suggests a delicate balance: enough temperature to maintain diversity for the "Lucky Hit" mechanism, but not so much that the policy cannot settle.

**Effect of training batch size (Figure 11, failed attempts):** A training batch size of 256 (vs. the successful default, which is 32 after downsampling) also leads to training failure with persistently high entropy. The paper does not specify the default batch size explicitly in the main text, but the Implementation Details state "downsample 32 responses per prompt for training." The failure at 256 suggests that larger batches may have a regularizing effect that prevents the policy from adapting to the majority voting signal, or that the per-batch reward statistics become less informative.

**Effect of model scale (Section 3.2, Tables 1, 2):** TTRL's gains increase with model size: Qwen2.5-7B averages +43.7% vs. Qwen2.5-32B at +57.7%. The paper attributes this to "larger models can produce more accurate majority voting rewards during self-improvement," though this is stated rather than experimentally verified (no ablation of voting accuracy by model scale is reported). Table 2 complicates the picture: LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct gains +7.3 on AIME 2024 while the larger LLaMA3.1-8B-Instruct gains +5.4 — the relationship between model size and TTRL benefit is not strictly monotonic across families.

**Effect of model type (base vs. instruct, Table 1 vs. Table 4):** Qwen2.5-Math-7B (base, 12.9 → 40.2 on AIME) benefits far more than its instruct counterpart (16.7 baseline, no TTRL reported for instruct in Table 1). The instruct model already has extensive post-training, limiting the upside from additional RL. However, Qwen3-8B (instruct, non-thinking) still shows +19.8 on AIME 2024, suggesting that instruct models can benefit when they have room to improve on the target benchmark.

**Effect of thinking vs. non-thinking mode (Figure 3 vs. Table 1):** Qwen3-8B is tested in both modes. In non-thinking mode with a 3k context (Table 1), TTRL improves AIME 2024 from 26.9 to 46.7 (+19.8). In thinking mode with a 32k context (Figure 3), it improves from 66.7 to 75.0 (+8.3). The smaller gain in thinking mode reflects the higher starting point, but +8.3 points on an already strong LRM is still substantial.

**Effect of dataset size and difficulty on training length (Implementation Details):** The paper implicitly ablates the relationship between dataset characteristics and required training by using different episode counts: 10 for MATH-500 (500 questions), 30 for AMC (~100+ questions), and 80 for AIME 2024 (30 questions). The inverse relationship between dataset size and episodes suggests that smaller, harder datasets benefit from more passes through the same data, consistent with online RL's need for repeated exploration.

**Cross-task transfer (Figure 4, implicit ablation of training dataset):** Training on AIME 2024 (hardest math) transfers better to AMC (+20.3) than training on MATH-500 transfers to AIME (+10.0). Training on AMC transfers well to both AIME (+20.0) and MATH-500 (+22.2). This suggests that training on harder problems produces more generalizable reasoning improvements than training on easier problems, though the paper does not test training on GPQA or non-math benchmarks.

**Negative result: GPQA with Qwen2.5-Math-7B (Table 1):** TTRL causes a -1.4 point drop on GPQA (29.1 → 27.7) for Qwen2.5-Math-7B, despite gains on all math benchmarks. The paper does not analyze this failure, but it suggests that TTRL's majority voting mechanism may be less effective when the model's prior on a task is poor — Qwen2.5-Math-7B is math-specialized and may not produce coherent GPQA answers, leading to an uninformative or misleading majority vote.

**Negative result: Mistral-Nemo-Instruct on AIME 2024 (Table 2):** A -0.8 point drop (0.8 → 0.0). Starting from near-zero performance, the model has essentially no correct answers in its output distribution, so the majority vote is always wrong, and the "Lucky Hit" mechanism can only provide negative rewards — which may cause the model to collapse to generating a single incorrect answer consistently.

### Critical Assessment

The paper makes four central claims that the experiments must support: (1) majority voting provides effective reward estimation for TTRL, (2) TTRL can exceed the initial model's maj@n upper bound and approach oracle RL-with-labels performance, (3) TTRL works across diverse models, benchmarks, and RL algorithms, and (4) TTRL's effectiveness depends on the model's prior knowledge, diminishing as task difficulty increases.

**Claim 1 (majority voting is effective reward estimation) is well-supported for math reasoning but has untested boundaries.** The "Lucky Hit" analysis (Figure 9, Figure 10) provides a compelling mechanistic explanation for why 92% reward accuracy is achievable even at 37% label accuracy on AIME 2024. The reward accuracy metric is tracked and reported, which is commendable transparency. However, the paper's scope is entirely within mathematical reasoning benchmarks where answers are categorical (numbers or expressions) and verification is exact string match. The "Lucky Hit" mechanism depends critically on the output space being large and scattered — if the model collapses to a small set of plausible wrong answers (e.g., on multiple-choice questions where all outputs are one of four options), the majority vote would systematically produce wrong labels that many outputs match, and reward accuracy would degrade toward label accuracy. The paper does not test TTRL on multiple-choice or constrained-output tasks. The claim holds for the tested math benchmarks, but the boundary conditions are narrower than implied.

The GPQA result (+1.2 for Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, -1.4 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B, +4.9 for Qwen3-8B non-thinking) is barely discussed. GPQA-Diamond is multiple-choice (4 options), which is exactly the regime where the "Lucky Hit" mechanism should be weakest — there are only 4 possible answers, so wrong answers will frequently match the wrong majority vote. The mixed GPQA results (small gains or losses) are consistent with this limitation, but the paper does not connect the dots.

**Claim 2 (exceeding maj@n and approaching RL-with-labels) is supported for medium-difficulty math but shown only on a single model.** The key evidence — Figure 6 (avg@16 exceeding initial maj@16 on AMC with 1.5B), Figure 7 (avg@64 exceeding initial maj@64 on AIME, AMC, MATH-500 with 7B), and Figure 8 (TTRL tracking RL Leakage on MATH-500 with 7B) — consistently supports the claim on Qwen2.5-Math models. But the maj@n ceiling comparison is not reported for other model families (LLaMA, Mistral, DeepSeek) or for GPQA. The RL Leakage comparison (Figure 8) is shown only on MATH-500, the easiest of the three math benchmarks — it would be more convincing to see this on AIME 2024 where label accuracy is lower (37%) and the gap between TTRL and supervised RL might be wider.

There is also a subtle measurement issue: the paper reports avg@n (average pass@1 computed from n samples) as its pass@1 estimate, which is a lower-variance estimator than true pass@1 but is **computed from the same samples used for training** in the online RL loop. This means the reported pass@1 is in-distribution with respect to the sampling parameters (temperature, top-p) used during training. A cleaner evaluation would report pass@1 under greedy decoding or at a held-out temperature, as the paper does in Table 4. The Table 4 results (greedy decoding) are somewhat lower than the main results (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B achieves 40.2 on AIME 2024 in Table 1 vs. 43.3 in Table 4 — note that Table 4 actually shows a higher number, possibly due to different sampling settings). The inconsistency in evaluation protocols between the main results (16 samples, temperature 0.6) and the appendix comparisons (greedy decoding) makes cross-table comparison difficult.

**Claim 3 (TTRL works across diverse settings) is the best-supported claim.** The breadth of testing — 13 models, 4 model families, 2 model types, 3 scales, 4 benchmarks, 3 RL algorithms — exceeds typical empirical validation in the RL-for-reasoning literature. The consistency of positive results is striking: 11 of 13 models show improvement on AIME 2024 (the exceptions being Mistral-Nemo-Instruct at -0.8 and an effectively flat result for DeepSeek-Math-7B-Instruct at +0.6). The RL algorithm ablation (Figure 5) is clean and convincing. The cross-task generalization (Figure 4) demonstrates that improvements are not purely benchmark-specific overfitting.

However, there is a missing axis: **all models are from a broadly similar generation of transformer LLMs.** The method's dependence on majority voting implies it would not work for models that cannot produce diverse outputs (e.g., models with very low temperature or deterministic decoding by design). The specific sampling hyperparameters (temperature 0.6 or 1.0, top-p 0.95) are chosen to ensure diversity, but the paper does not test what happens as diversity is intentionally reduced — would TTRL fail at temperature 0.1? This is an important practical question since many deployed models use low temperature for production.

**Claim 4 (prior knowledge dependence) is demonstrated but incompletely characterized.** The difficulty-bin analysis (Table 3) clearly shows diminishing returns with difficulty, but the analysis is on MATH-500 only with the 1.5B model. The paper does not break down AIME 2024 or AMC results by difficulty, nor does it compare the 7B model's difficulty-dependent gains to the 1.5B's — such a comparison would test whether larger models (stronger priors) have a different difficulty-gain profile. The failure cases on AIME 2024 (Mistral-Nemo-Instruct, DeepSeek-Math-7B-Instruct) are consistent with the prior knowledge hypothesis but are presented as raw results without analysis — the paper does not verify that these models indeed have weaker priors on AIME (e.g., by reporting their initial avg@n or maj@n).

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Ablation of N (the number of voting samples):** The paper uses N=64 for voting throughout. How does TTRL perform with N=16, N=32, N=128? The "Lucky Hit" mechanism predicts that smaller N would reduce reward accuracy (more outputs match the wrong majority label by chance), while larger N would increase label accuracy but also computational cost. This ablation would characterize the efficiency-accuracy frontier of the voting mechanism.
- **Comparison with offline self-training:** The claim that TTRL "exceeds the traditional self-training upper bound" is central but is never tested head-to-head against an SFT baseline that fine-tunes on the initial model's majority-voted answers. The paper assumes initial maj@n is the SFT ceiling, which is reasonable but not empirically verified.
- **Multiple runs with error bars:** All figures show single training runs without any indication of variance. RL training is notoriously high-variance, and TTRL adds the additional stochasticity of majority voting over sampled outputs. Reporting mean ± std over 3–5 random seeds would substantially increase confidence in the reported gains.
- **Testing on non-math reasoning tasks:** The paper claims TTRL is broadly applicable, but all convincing results are on math. GPQA is the only non-math benchmark, and results are mixed. Code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), logical reasoning (ARC), or science QA would test the generality claim.
- **Analysis of output quality changes:** Table 3 shows response length reductions of 43–73%. Are the shorter responses still correct? Or is the model learning to produce shorter but wrong answers that happen to agree with the majority? Qualitative examples of before/after outputs would clarify whether TTRL improves reasoning quality or just answer-matching behavior.

**A structural concern about the evaluation protocol:** TTRL trains and evaluates on the same data. This is deliberate — the paper frames it as test-time adaptation — but it means the reported "pass@1" is not an out-of-sample estimate. The cross-task generalization experiments (Figure 4) partially address this by showing improvements transfer, but the magnitude is smaller: on AIME 2024 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B, in-distribution TTRL achieves 40.2 (Table 1) while training on AMC transfers to 33.3 (Figure 4). The ~7-point gap represents the difference between test-time adaptation and generalization. The paper would be strengthened by a clearer separation between these two regimes.

**The paper does not report total compute cost.** "8 * NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs" is mentioned, but no training time, total FLOPs, or tokens processed is given. Without this, it is impossible to assess the practical cost of TTRL relative to alternatives. How does the compute cost of 80 episodes of TTRL on AIME 2024 (N=64 samples per question, 30 questions, per step) compare to training on a labeled dataset? The paper's competitive comparison against models trained on 800K labeled examples (Table 4) is striking, but without cost accounting, we cannot evaluate whether TTRL is more *efficient* or just differently resourced.

**Overall assessment:** The experiments genuinely support the paper's core thesis — majority voting enables effective RL on unlabeled test data for mathematical reasoning, and the resulting self-improvement can surpass the initial model's consensus ceiling. The breadth of testing impresses, and the diagnostic metrics (Reward Accuracy, Majority Ratio, etc.) provide mechanistic insight beyond simple performance reporting. The primary weaknesses are the narrow domain (math reasoning with exact-answer verification), the lack of variance estimates or multiple runs, and the absence of compute cost accounting. The claims are well-supported within the tested domain but the paper's implications for "broader tasks and domains" remain aspirational rather than demonstrated.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For in the Headline Efficiency Claims

**The assumption or constraint.** TTRL's majority voting reward function requires generating N = 64 candidate outputs per question at every training step to estimate the pseudo-label via consensus. The paper's vote-then-sample strategy — generating 64 outputs, downsampling to 32 for training — means that **over half the generation budget is spent purely on label estimation, not on learning.** Across 80 episodes on AIME 2024 (30 questions), this amounts to 80 × 30 × 64 = 153,600 total generations for label estimation alone, plus 153,600 × (32/64) = 76,800 generations used for training. The paper acknowledges cost only obliquely, stating in Section 3.1 that the vote-then-sample strategy "effectively reduces computational costs" without quantifying absolute cost or comparing it to alternatives.

**The consequence.** The headline 211% improvement on AIME 2024 is achieved at unknown total computational expense. A practitioner deciding whether to deploy TTRL needs to know: how many GPU-hours does this cost per benchmark, and how does that compare to simply paying for human labels on those same 30 questions? The paper does not report wall-clock time, total FLOPs, or tokens generated for any experiment. Without this, the efficiency claims — e.g., TTRL matching models trained on 800K labeled examples (Table 4) — are difficult to interpret as economic arguments. If TTRL on AIME 2024 costs 1,000 A100-hours while labeling 30 contest math problems costs $300, the practical attractiveness reverses. The paper also does not report whether the required 8×A100 hardware is necessary or if smaller GPU configurations work.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports using "8 * NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs" (Section 3.1) but provides no training time, total FLOPs, or per-step latency. The number of episodes (10, 30, 80) and generation budget (N=64) are reported, so total generations can be estimated, but cost in GPU-hours is absent. The vote-then-sample strategy is described as cost-reducing (Section 3.1), but no ablation compares different N values (e.g., N=16 vs. N=64 vs. N=128) to characterize the cost-accuracy tradeoff. The paper does not report whether label estimation accuracy saturates at some N < 64, which would allow cheaper operation.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper mentions in Section 7 that future work should explore "large-scale self-supervised RL training," implicitly acknowledging that current costs are not characterized at scale, but proposes no specific cost-reduction strategies. The absence of an N-ablation means the reader cannot determine whether N=64 is a necessary minimum or an unoptimized default.

### Performance Collapses When the Model's Prior Knowledge Is Insufficient

**The assumption or constraint.** TTRL's bootstrapping mechanism — the "Lucky Hit" phenomenon that enables high reward accuracy despite low label accuracy (Section 4.2) — depends on the model's output distribution being both **scattered** (many different wrong answers) and **containing at least some correct answers** (even if rare). When the model's prior knowledge is so weak that correct answers essentially never appear in the output distribution, there is no toehold for RL to reinforce. The paper states this explicitly in Section 4.3: "TTRL fails if the model's prior knowledge is insufficient to handle the complexity of the data."

**The consequence.** TTRL cannot improve a model on problems it fundamentally cannot solve. This is not merely an efficiency concern — it is a **capability ceiling.** The difficulty-bin analysis (Table 3, Section 4.3) shows that on the hardest MATH-500 questions (Level 5), TTRL's absolute gain drops to +16.8 points (vs. +45.4 on the easiest Level 1) and the relative improvement drops to 75.3% (vs. 175.3% on Level 1). Extrapolating this trend: on problems even harder than MATH-500 Level 5 — e.g., ARC-AGI-2, which the paper uses as a motivating example in Section 1, where o3 achieves only 4% — TTRL would likely provide negligible or zero improvement. The failure cases in Table 2 provide direct evidence: **Mistral-Nemo-Instruct on AIME 2024 drops from 0.8 to 0.0** (-0.8 points, effectively zero initial capability yielding zero improvement), and **DeepSeek-Math-7B-Instruct gains only +0.6** on AIME 2024 (1.9 → 2.5), consistent with near-zero useful prior knowledge.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides the clearest evidence, showing monotonically decreasing gains with difficulty. Table 2 shows the two near-zero prior failures (Mistral-Nemo-Instruct at -0.8, DeepSeek-Math-7B-Instruct at +0.6). The paper's upper-bound analysis (Figure 8, TTRL vs. RL Leakage) is conducted on MATH-500, where the prior is sufficient — the paper does **not** show this comparison on AIME 2024, where label accuracy is only 37% and the gap between TTRL and oracle supervision might be substantially larger. The paper also does not characterize what "sufficient prior knowledge" means quantitatively: is there a minimum pass@1 threshold below which TTRL reliably fails? Is it related to maj@n accuracy, output entropy, or some other measurable quantity?

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through acknowledgment but not through solution. The paper identifies prior knowledge as a limiting factor (Section 4.3) and suggests that "larger models can produce more accurate majority voting rewards during self-improvement" (Section 3.2), implying a brute-force mitigation. However, no mechanism is proposed for TTRL to bootstrap from truly near-zero capability — e.g., by curriculum learning on easier subsets first, by incorporating external weak supervision for the hardest problems, or by combining TTRL with few-shot prompting to provide initial priors. The paper's future work section (Section 7) mentions "applying TTRL to more complex, open-ended domains" but does not address the prior-knowledge bootstrapping problem directly.

### Evaluation on the Training Set Overstates Generalization; Cross-Task Transfer Is Weaker

**The assumption or constraint.** TTRL trains directly on the test data — this is the definitional premise of test-time training — but the paper's primary results (Table 1, Table 2) report **pass@1 evaluated on the same benchmark used for training**, using the same sampling parameters (temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95). The paper itself acknowledges in Appendix A that "TTRL has a different setup from the previous models, which makes the comparison seem unfair." The cross-task generalization experiments (Figure 4) are the only out-of-distribution evaluation, and they show substantially smaller gains than the in-distribution results.

**The consequence.** The headline 211% improvement on AIME 2024 is an **in-distribution adaptation gain**, not a measure of generalized reasoning improvement. A user who cares about performance on *new, unseen* math problems (the standard deployment scenario) should expect gains closer to the cross-task transfer numbers. On Qwen2.5-Math-7B: training on AMC transfers to AIME 2024 with +20.0 points (13.3 → 33.3), while training directly on AIME 2024 yields +27.3 points (12.9 → 40.2) — roughly a 27% smaller gain when the improvement must generalize. Training on MATH-500 transfers to AIME 2024 with only +10.0 points (13.3 → 23.3) — roughly 63% smaller than the in-distribution gain. This means the **practical value of TTRL for deployment on novel problems is significantly lower than the headline numbers suggest**, and the transfer gap widens as the training and evaluation tasks diverge in difficulty (MATH-500 is easier than AIME 2024, so training on it transfers poorly to harder problems).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 provides the data, but the paper does not quantify the transfer gap or discuss its implications for deployment. The cross-task experiments are limited to math benchmarks — there are no experiments training on math and evaluating on GPQA (science), or vice versa, so domain-transfer generalization is completely uncharacterized. The paper also notes (Appendix A) that when comparing TTRL to prior RL methods using **greedy decoding** (Table 4), TTRL's AIME 2024 score for Qwen2.5-Math-7B is 43.3 — but this is still an in-distribution evaluation (the model was trained on AIME 2024). The paper never reports greedy-decoding performance on an unseen benchmark after TTRL training.

**Mitigation status.** Acknowledged implicitly through the cross-task experiments (Figure 4) and the "unfair comparison" disclaimer (Appendix A), but not framed as a fundamental limitation. The paper does not propose strategies to improve generalization — e.g., training on a diverse pool of unlabeled problems rather than a single benchmark, or incorporating out-of-distribution evaluation into the training loop. The future work section mentions "online learning with streaming data" (Section 7) but does not address the transfer gap specifically.

### TTRL Assumes Categorical, Exactly-Verifiable Answers; The "Lucky Hit" Mechanism Depends on Large Output Spaces

**The assumption or constraint.** The majority voting reward function (Equation 3, Listing 1) operates by extracting a final answer string from each output, identifying the most frequent answer, and assigning binary rewards based on exact string match. This requires: (1) that answers can be parsed into a discrete set of possible values, (2) that the set of possible wrong answers is large (so that scattered outputs rarely match the wrong consensus label by chance — the "Lucky Hit" mechanism), and (3) that the task is one where a consensus answer is meaningful (i.e., there is a single correct answer).

**The consequence.** TTRL likely **fails or degrades substantially on tasks where the output space is small or structured differently.** The most obvious failure regime is **multiple-choice questions**, where the output space has only 4–5 possible answers. In this setting, when the majority vote is wrong (which is likely on hard questions), a large fraction of sampled outputs will match the wrong consensus answer by chance — because there are only a few wrong answers to choose from. The "Lucky Hit" mechanism collapses: reward accuracy drops toward label accuracy, and the model is reinforced for producing the wrong answer. The paper's GPQA results (the only non-math benchmark, which is multiple-choice) are consistent with this prediction: **Qwen2.5-Math-7B drops by -1.4 points on GPQA (29.1 → 27.7)**, and Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B gains only +1.2 points (24.9 → 26.1), substantially smaller than its math gains (+8.1 to +40.3). The paper does not discuss this pattern.

Beyond multiple-choice, other task types where TTRL would likely struggle include: **open-ended generation** (no extractable "answer" to vote on), **multi-step planning** or coding tasks where correctness is partial or structural rather than categorical, and **subjective tasks** (summarization, creative writing) where there is no single correct output and consensus may reflect blandness rather than quality. The paper's experimental scope — exclusively math reasoning with exact answer matching — means the method's broader applicability is entirely unvalidated.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** GPQA results (Table 1) provide the only evidence: three models show small gains (LLaMA3.1-8B: +3.3, Qwen3-8B: +4.9, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B: +1.2) and one shows a loss (Qwen2.5-Math-7B: -1.4). The paper never discusses these results, never acknowledges that GPQA's multiple-choice format is fundamentally different from the open-response math benchmarks, and never conducts an ablation varying the output space size to test the "Lucky Hit" dependence. The AIME 2024 "Lucky Hit" analysis (Figure 9, Figure 10) is conducted entirely on open-response math, so the mechanism is validated only in the favorable regime.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed at all. The paper claims TTRL's "potential for broader tasks and domains" (Abstract) and lists "agentic tasks and scientific discovery" as future work (Section 7), but provides no analysis of why the current mechanism would fail on those tasks or what modifications would be needed. A practitioner looking to apply TTRL to code generation, logical reasoning with structured outputs, or multiple-choice QA would find no guidance on whether the approach transfers.

### The Paper Does Not Establish That TTRL Is Practically Preferable to Simpler, Cheaper Alternatives

**The assumption or constraint.** TTRL is presented as a novel method for improving LLMs on unlabeled test data through online RL with majority voting rewards. However, the paper's experimental comparisons are almost exclusively against the **pretrained backbone model** (the "before TTRL" baseline). It does not empirically compare TTRL against several plausible and simpler alternatives that could also improve performance on unlabeled test data without RL.

**The consequence.** A practitioner wanting to improve a model's performance on a set of unlabeled problems has several options beyond TTRL. They could: (1) **apply majority voting at inference time** without any training — spending the same 64 samples per question to get a consensus answer, which the paper's own Figure 7 shows achieves 33.0% on AIME 2024 (vs. TTRL's post-training avg@64 of 40.0%, a 7-point gain from training); (2) **perform supervised fine-tuning on the initial model's majority-voted answers** (offline self-training), which the paper acknowledges as the "traditional self-training upper bound" (Section 4.1) but never tests head-to-head; or (3) **use few-shot prompting with exemplars** if any labeled examples exist (the paper cites Wang et al., 2025b, showing that "a single training example" can substantially improve reasoning). Without head-to-head comparisons against these baselines, the **marginal benefit of TTRL's online RL over much simpler approaches is unclear.** The paper's central claim that TTRL "exceeds the traditional self-training upper bound" (Section 4.1, Takeaway 1) is supported only by comparing TTRL's final performance to the initial model's maj@n — not by running the offline self-training baseline and showing it performs worse.

The cost tradeoff is also unexamined: test-time majority voting (64 samples per question, no training) costs only inference compute, while TTRL adds 80 episodes of RL training. For AIME 2024 with 30 questions, a user could either spend ~1920 inference samples (30 × 64) for one-time majority voting, or the same ~1920 samples per episode × 80 episodes for TTRL, a 80× increase in compute. The paper does not quantify whether the +7-point gain (40.0 vs. 33.0) justifies the computational premium.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not include an offline self-training baseline, a test-time majority voting baseline as a comparator (the initial maj@n is reported but not presented as a competing method), or a few-shot prompting baseline. The RL Leakage experiment (Figure 8) is the closest to a strong baseline, showing that TTRL approaches supervised performance, but this is an upper bound, not an alternative method. The comparison against "R1-Zero-Like" models (Table 4) is informative but unfair in the opposite direction — those models use large labeled datasets, whereas the simpler alternatives above use no more information than TTRL.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames TTRL's contribution as enabling RL on unlabeled data, which is a genuine capability that none of the simpler alternatives achieve (majority voting alone does not update the model; offline SFT does not create the self-reinforcing cycle). However, the *practical* question — when is the additional complexity and cost of online RL worth it over simpler test-time techniques — is never asked or answered. This is a significant gap for a paper whose primary audience is practitioners deciding how to deploy or improve models on unlabeled data.

### Only a Single Training Run Is Reported for Each Result; Variance and Reproducibility Are Uncharacterized

**The assumption or constraint.** All figures and tables in the paper report results from what appear to be **single training runs** — the accuracy curves (Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 8, Figure 9, Figure 12) show smooth trajectories without error bars or shaded regions, and the tables report single numbers without standard deviations or confidence intervals. The paper never mentions running multiple seeds or reporting variance.

**The consequence.** RL training for LLMs is notoriously high-variance — final performance can vary substantially depending on random seed, data order, and sampling stochasticity. TTRL adds an additional layer of stochasticity through the majority voting reward, which depends on the specific set of N sampled outputs at each step. A single lucky or unlucky training run could produce results that are not representative of expected performance. Without variance estimates, the reader cannot assess whether the reported gains — e.g., +27.3 points on AIME 2024 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B — are reliably achievable or within the noise range of TTRL training. This is particularly concerning for the smaller benchmarks: AIME 2024 has only 30 questions, so a model that happens to get 2–3 additional questions correct in the final evaluation represents a ~7–10 point swing. The 211% headline figure (+27.3 points) could correspond to getting ~8 additional questions correct out of 30 — a relatively small number of discrete successes that might not be robust to retraining.

The failure to report variance also affects the interpretability of the difficulty-bin analysis (Table 3), the cross-task transfer results (Figure 4), and the comparison with RL Leakage (Figure 8). In the RL Leakage comparison, TTRL and the supervised baseline show a ~2–3 point gap — if the standard deviation of either curve is ±2 points, this gap may not be statistically significant. The paper's claim that "TTRL closely approaches" oracle performance would then be weaker than the point estimates suggest.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. All figures show single curves. No mention of multiple seeds, standard deviation, or confidence intervals appears in the experimental setup (Section 3.1), the main results (Section 3.2), or the analysis (Section 4). The appendix figures (Figure 12) similarly show single-run entropy and accuracy curves. This is the paper's most significant methodological weakness from a statistical rigor perspective.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's limitations section (Section 7) mentions plans for "a more in-depth analysis of the impact of prior knowledge and hyperparameter configurations" but does not mention statistical reproducibility or multi-seed evaluation. This is a standard expectation in empirical ML research that the paper does not meet, and it weakens confidence in the precise magnitude of the reported gains, even if the qualitative result (TTRL improves performance) is well-supported by the breadth of model and benchmark testing.
