# Full Parameter Fine-tuning for Large Language Models with Limited Resources

**ArXiv:** [2306.09782](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09782)

## 🎯 Pitch

You can fine‑tune a full 65B model on a single 8×RTX 3090 machine, matching the quality of distributed training—by fusing backward pass and parameter updates so that the only gradient stored is the one flowing through the current layer.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **LOw-Memory Optimization (LOMO)**, a new optimizer that fuses gradient computation with parameter update into a single backward-pass step, eliminating the need to store gradient tensors for all parameters simultaneously (reducing gradient memory from storing all parameters' gradients to storing only the largest single parameter's gradient). Evaluated on LLaMA models from 7B to 65B using the SuperGLUE benchmark, LOMO, when combined with existing techniques like activation checkpointing, reduces total memory usage to 10.8% of the standard DeepSpeed approach — enabling full parameter fine-tuning of a 65B model on a single machine with 8× RTX 3090 GPUs (each with 24GB memory) while matching or exceeding the downstream performance of LoRA, establishing that full-parameter fine-tuning under severe resource constraints is achievable without gradient approximation or quantization, but only when the optimizer is SGD — whose lack of intermediate states makes the fused update possible — and the loss surface is smooth enough that sequential per-parameter updates approximate batched SGD.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Full-Parameter Fine-Tuning of LLMs Remains Prohibitively Expensive

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is ruthlessly concrete: **full-parameter fine-tuning of large language models demands memory resources that exceed what consumer-grade GPU hardware can provide.** As model sizes have ballooned from hundreds of millions to tens and hundreds of billions of parameters, the hardware barrier to entry for NLP researchers has risen in lockstep. The paper states this bluntly in the introduction:

> "Tuning LLMs often requires expensive GPU resources, such as 8×80GB devices, making it difficult for small labs and companies to participate in this area of research."

This is not merely an inconvenience — it is a structural gatekeeper that determines who gets to do cutting-edge LLM research. When fine-tuning a 65B-parameter model requires hardware configurations that cost tens of thousands of dollars, the research community bifurcates into those with institutional access to such resources and everyone else. The paper frames this as both a scientific bottleneck (fewer researchers means slower progress) and an equity concern (concentrating LLM research in well-resourced organizations limits the diversity of perspectives and applications).

### Why Full-Parameter Fine-Tuning Matters Specifically

The paper's focus on *full-parameter* fine-tuning — as opposed to the more memory-efficient *parameter-efficient* fine-tuning (PEFT) methods — is deliberate and justified. The authors cite a critical tension in the literature:

> "parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods... do not offer a practical solution for full parameter fine-tuning, which has been acknowledged as a more powerful approach than parameter-efficient fine-tuning (Ding et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023)"

This claim warrants careful unpacking. PEFT methods like LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) and Prefix-tuning (Li and Liang, 2021) work by freezing the pretrained weights and either injecting small trainable adapter modules or learning low-rank weight updates. The memory savings are substantial — LoRA might train only a few million parameters for a billion-parameter model — and the downstream performance is often competitive with full fine-tuning. So why should anyone still care about full-parameter fine-tuning?

The answer lies in a subtle but important distinction between *competitive* and *optimal* performance. Ding et al. (2022) conducted a comprehensive survey of PEFT methods and found that while they approach full fine-tuning performance on many benchmarks, full-parameter tuning consistently provides the highest ceiling — it can adjust every weight in the model to the new task, without being constrained by a low-rank bottleneck or a fixed adapter architecture. Sun et al. (2023) specifically compared full-parameter and LoRA-based fine-tuning on Chinese instruction-following data and found that full-parameter training produced instruction-following behavior that LoRA could not fully replicate. The implicit message is that PEFT methods are an *engineering compromise* driven by hardware limitations, not an intrinsically superior approach. If the memory barrier could be lowered, full-parameter fine-tuning would remain the gold standard.

### Where Existing Memory-Saving Techniques Fall Short

The paper contextualizes LOMO within a landscape of existing memory-reduction techniques, each of which addresses only part of the problem (Section 2). Understanding these techniques is essential because LOMO is designed to work *alongside* them, not replace them — the headline 10.8% memory figure arises from combining LOMO with these orthogonal methods.

**Activation Checkpointing** (Chen et al., 2016) addresses the memory consumed by intermediate activations during the forward pass. In standard backpropagation, every activation from every layer must be stored so that gradients can be computed during the backward pass. For large models with long sequences, activation memory can dominate total usage. Activation checkpointing trades computation for memory: it strategically discards most activations after the forward pass and recomputes them on demand during the backward pass. The paper notes that this can reduce activation memory to "the square root of the original amount at the cost of one extra forward pass." This is effective but incomplete — it does nothing to address the memory consumed by optimizer states, gradients, or parameters themselves.

**Mixed-Precision Training** (Micikevicius et al., 2018) stores parameters, activations, and gradients in half-precision (FP16) rather than full precision (FP32), cutting their memory footprint roughly in half while also enabling faster computation on tensor-core-capable GPUs. However, mixed-precision training introduces numerical stability challenges: gradient underflow (values so small they round to zero in FP16), loss of precision during weight updates, and the need for certain operations (like reductions and normalizations) to remain in full precision. The standard solution involves maintaining full-precision master copies of weights alongside the half-precision working copies, which partially offsets the memory savings. Crucially, mixed-precision training does not address optimizer state memory — Adam still requires its momentum and variance buffers in full precision, consuming twice the parameter memory regardless of the working precision.

**Heterogeneous Training Systems** attempt to offload tensors from GPU memory to CPU memory or even NVMe storage. ZeRO-Offload (Ren et al., 2021b) keeps optimizer states and gradients in CPU memory, updating parameters via CPU computation and transferring only what is needed to the GPU for forward and backward passes. ZeRO-Infinity (Rajbhandari et al., 2021) extends this to NVMe storage for even larger models. The L2L approach (Pudipeddi et al., 2020) takes a layer-by-layer strategy: only the tensors needed for the current layer's computation reside in GPU memory, while everything else stays in CPU memory. These methods are effective at scaling total model size far beyond what a single GPU can hold, but they introduce significant communication overhead — data must shuttle between CPU and GPU, or even between NVMe and CPU and then GPU. Throughput often degrades substantially as a result. Moreover, they shift costs: CPU memory becomes the new bottleneck, and high-end workstations with 512GB+ of RAM are still expensive.

**The collective failure mode** of these techniques, from the paper's perspective, is that none of them fundamentally reconceive how the optimizer itself uses memory. Activation checkpointing handles activations; mixed-precision handles parameter and gradient storage width; heterogeneous systems handle tensor placement. But the optimizer — particularly Adam — still demands memory proportional to `2 × number_of_parameters` for its momentum and variance buffers, plus memory proportional to `1 × number_of_parameters` for the gradient tensor storage that all standard deep learning frameworks (including PyTorch) maintain by default. These three components — optimizer states, gradients, and parameters — together constitute what the paper calls "model states," and they are the dominant memory consumers.

### The Unexplored Territory: Changing the Optimizer Itself

Prior work on memory-efficient training has largely taken the optimizer as a fixed cost. The Adam optimizer (Kingma and Ba, 2015) and its weight-decay variant AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) have become the default optimizers for LLM training to such an extent that "training" and "Adam" are practically synonymous in the practitioner's lexicon. The reasons are well-established: Adam's adaptive per-parameter learning rates — computed from exponentially decaying averages of past gradients (first moment) and squared gradients (second moment) — provide robustness to ill-conditioned loss landscapes, saddle points, and varying gradient scales across layers. These are precisely the pathologies that historically plagued stochastic gradient descent (SGD) when training deep networks from scratch.

But the paper identifies a crucial conceptual gap: **the optimization challenges that motivate Adam for pretraining may not apply with the same severity during fine-tuning.** Fine-tuning starts from a pretrained checkpoint that already sits in a well-behaved region of the loss landscape — the model already represents useful features and a coherent language model. The optimization task is to adapt these existing representations to a new task, not to navigate a high-dimensional random initialization toward a distant solution. This observation opens the door to reconsidering whether the memory cost of Adam's optimizer states (twice the parameter count) is justified during fine-tuning, or whether a simpler optimizer like SGD — which stores no intermediate state at all — could suffice.

The paper makes this argument explicit in Section 3.1.1, providing a theoretical analysis that draws on three well-known challenges of SGD (Ruder, 2016; Sun et al., 2020a) and explains why each may be less severe for LLM fine-tuning:

1. **Large curvature loss surface → Smoother loss surface for LLMs.** The paper invokes Hao et al. (2019), who visualized and analyzed the loss landscapes of BERT models, finding that they are remarkably smooth — small perturbations to parameters produce small changes in loss. The paper extends this intuition: "If we believe that larger models have a smoother loss surface, we can conclude that the [large curvature] problem is not an issue since the loss surface of LLMs should not have a large curvature." This is a non-trivial claim. Smoothness in neural network loss landscapes is not guaranteed by scale alone, but empirical evidence from the pretraining literature suggests that wider and deeper networks trained with appropriate normalization do tend toward flatter minima. The paper adds an important caveat: "This holds only when we teach the LLMs natural language-based tasks (or code-based if pre-trained with code). A synthetic loss function unrelated to pre-training tasks will indeed face the large curvature problem." This is a boundary condition — LOMO's argument for SGD rests on the fine-tuning task being "in-distribution" relative to pretraining.

2. **Local optimum → Good enough for fine-tuning.** The goal of fine-tuning is domain adaptation, not learning a fundamentally new function. The paper argues: "a local optimum is often a good enough solution, and the limited training data (compared to pre-training corpus) makes it difficult to push the model to a far-away global optimum." This is a practical argument grounded in the empirical observation that fine-tuned models typically show modest weight changes relative to their pretrained values. If the model is already in a good region of parameter space, any local improvement — even if not globally optimal — provides useful adaptation.

3. **Saddle points → Distant from the pretrained initialization.** The paper reasons that for a typical NLP fine-tuning task, the pretrained checkpoint is already "in a valley" — a region of low loss. Saddle points, which plague optimization in high dimensions, tend to occur on ridges between valleys. If fine-tuning moves the parameters only a small distance from the pretrained initialization (which is empirically typical), the optimization trajectory may never encounter saddle points.

This argument is qualitative, not quantitative — the paper does not provide loss landscape visualizations or Hessian analyses of LLaMA models to verify these claims. It offers them as *motivating intuition* for why SGD might work where it historically failed. The more rigorous theoretical contribution comes in Section 3.1.2, where the paper derives an equivalence between SGD with sequential (per-sample) updates and SGD with larger batch sizes, under the smoothness assumption. The key derivation (Equations 1–6) shows that taking two sequential SGD steps on individual samples differs from one batched SGD step by a term proportional to the product of the second derivative of the loss and the change in model output — a term that vanishes as the loss surface becomes smooth. The practical implication is that **SGD fine-tuning on LLMs, even with small per-GPU micro-batches, behaves as if it were using a larger implicit batch size**, providing stability that SGD on smaller models lacks. This is a genuinely novel theoretical contribution that goes beyond the qualitative hand-waving common in optimizer papers — it provides a mechanism by which model scale itself mitigates SGD's historical weaknesses.

### How LOMO Position Itself Relative to Existing Memory-Saving Optimizers

The paper explicitly distinguishes LOMO from two recent lines of work on memory-efficient optimization:

**MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023)** is a zeroth-order optimizer that estimates gradients using only two forward passes (one with a random perturbation, one without), eliminating the need for backpropagation entirely. It can tune models with extremely low memory — essentially the same as inference — but at the cost of using *approximate* gradients. The convergence properties of zeroth-order methods degrade with dimensionality, and for billion-parameter models, the gradient estimates can be noisy. The paper explicitly states that LOMO "neither approximates gradients nor requires low-bit quantization," positioning it as a method that achieves memory savings without sacrificing the exact gradient information that backpropagation provides.

**GaLore (Zhao et al., 2024)** performs low-rank decomposition on gradient matrices and updates parameters using only the low-rank approximation, projecting gradients onto a lower-dimensional subspace. This can dramatically reduce optimizer state memory (since the optimizer operates on the low-rank representation), but it introduces an approximation error that grows with the rank reduction. The paper presents LOMO as qualitatively different: rather than approximating gradients, LOMO keeps the exact gradients but changes *when and how* they are stored and applied.

**Quantized optimizers** like 8-bit Adam (Dettmers et al., 2022) reduce optimizer state memory by storing momentum and variance in 8-bit precision rather than 32-bit. This yields a roughly $4\times$ reduction in optimizer state memory without changing the optimization algorithm. However, quantization introduces rounding errors that can accumulate over many update steps, and the 8-bit representation can lose precision for parameters with small gradient variance. LOMO avoids quantization entirely by eliminating optimizer states.

The central insight that unifies LOMO and distinguishes it from all of these approaches is that **the optimizer state memory is the largest reducible component**. Figure 2 in the paper makes this vividly clear: for a 7B model trained with AdamW and mixed precision, optimizer states consume 73.7% of total training memory, while parameters and gradients each consume only 12.3%. Even switching from AdamW to plain SGD — which eliminates momentum and variance buffers — only reduces optimizer state memory by roughly two-thirds (from 75.31GB to 25.10GB in Table 1), because mixed-precision SGD still stores a full-precision master copy of the weights for the update step. LOMO goes further by eliminating the need to store any optimizer states at all: the parameter update occurs in-place during the backward pass, using the half-precision gradient directly. The result, as shown in Table 1, is that total memory drops from 102.20GB (AdamW with activation checkpointing) to 14.58GB (LOMO with activation checkpointing) — a 7× reduction — while still performing exact gradient-based updates.

### The Specific Gap LOMO Fills

The paper positions itself as filling a precise gap in the ecosystem of memory-saving techniques:

- **Activation memory** is handled by activation checkpointing (orthogonal, can be combined with LOMO).
- **Model parameter memory across GPUs** is handled by ZeRO-3 parameter partitioning (orthogonal, can be combined with LOMO).
- **Optimizer state memory** has been addressed by quantization (lossy) or offloading (slow), but **no method exists that eliminates it entirely while preserving exact gradient information**.

LOMO is that method. By fusing gradient computation and parameter update into a single step — literally `p = p - lr * ∂L/∂p` executed the moment `∂L/∂p` is available, before any other gradient is computed — LOMO reduces gradient tensor memory from storing *all* parameters' gradients to storing *at most one* parameter's gradient at a time. The optimizer states vanish because SGD has none. The remaining memory is dominated by the parameters themselves (86.1% in Figure 2c for LLaMA-7B) and the activations (12.3% with checkpointing). This is the paper's structural achievement: **training memory asymptotically approaches inference memory**, because the forward pass must store the same activations regardless of whether gradients are computed, and the parameters must be resident on the GPU regardless. The backward pass adds only the cost of storing one layer's gradient at a time — which, for the largest layer in a transformer, is a small fraction of the total parameter count.

### The Practical Significance: What "8× RTX 3090 for a 65B Model" Actually Means

The headline result — full parameter fine-tuning of a 65B model on 8 RTX 3090 GPUs — deserves context to appreciate its significance. An RTX 3090 is a consumer-grade GPU released in 2020 with 24GB of VRAM, originally priced at $1,499. A server with 8 such GPUs represents approximately $12,000 in GPU hardware — within reach of a well-funded academic lab or startup, but far below the $100,000+ configurations (8× A100 80GB) typically required for 65B model training. The 65B LLaMA model in FP16 requires approximately 130GB just to store its parameters — nearly 5.5× the memory of a single RTX 3090. Standard AdamW training of this model, even with ZeRO-3 partitioning and activation checkpointing, would require at minimum the optimizer states (260GB for momentum and variance in FP32) plus gradients (130GB in FP16) plus parameters (130GB) plus activations — a total far exceeding what 8×24GB = 192GB of aggregate GPU memory can provide.

LOMO changes this arithmetic. By eliminating optimizer states and reducing gradient memory to `O(max_layer_size)` rather than `O(total_parameters)`, the per-GPU memory requirement drops to approximately 19GB (Table 2, 65B model with LOMO) — fitting comfortably within a 24GB budget. The model parameters are distributed across 8 GPUs via ZeRO-3, each storing only a fraction of the total. The result is that a 65B model can be fine-tuned end-to-end without any CPU offloading, gradient approximation, or quantization — on hardware that was designed for gaming, not datacenter AI training.

This is not merely a symbolic achievement. It directly addresses the paper's stated motivation: **lowering the threshold for LLM research participation**. A graduate student with access to a shared 8×3090 server can now fine-tune a 65B model using exactly the same mathematical updates that a well-funded industry lab would use — the updates are SGD, the gradients are exact, and the only difference is the order in which parameters are updated. If the paper's theoretical argument about smooth loss surfaces holds, this difference in update order should have negligible impact on final model quality. The experiments on SuperGLUE (Table 3) provide empirical support: LOMO matches or exceeds LoRA performance across all model sizes from 7B to 65B, and even outperforms it by an average of 2.8 points on the 13B model. This validates the paper's central bet: that the memory savings from fused updates and SGD come without a performance penalty, at least for the fine-tuning tasks studied.

### The Theoretical Substrate: Why Sequential Updates Don't Break SGD

A reader encountering LOMO for the first time might reasonably object: **standard SGD requires computing gradients for all parameters before applying any update, because otherwise later layers' gradients are computed with respect to weights that have already been changed by earlier layers' updates.** This is a legitimate concern. In the standard backpropagation + update paradigm, the gradient `∂L/∂W_l` for layer `l` is computed assuming all other weights remain at their pre-update values. If LOMO updates `W_l` immediately upon computing its gradient, then when backpropagation continues to layer `l-1`, the activations and error signals flowing backward are computed with respect to the *updated* `W_l`, not the original one. This introduces a discrepancy between the gradients actually computed and the true gradients of the loss with respect to the original parameters.

The paper does not directly address this concern in the main text, but the implicit resolution lies in the small-learning-rate regime typical of fine-tuning. When learning rates are small (the paper uses values in the range `5e-2` to `3e-2` for the full model, which when applied per-parameter yields per-step changes on the order of `1e-4` or smaller for each weight), the discrepancy between gradients computed from the original weights and gradients computed from slightly updated weights is second-order — it scales with the product of the learning rate and the Hessian. For a smooth loss surface with bounded curvature, this error remains small and does not accumulate unstably. This is precisely the intuition formalized in the Section 3.1.2 derivation: the difference between sequential and batched updates is proportional to the product of the gradient of the loss and the change in model output, which vanishes as the step size shrinks.

The paper's empirical results serve as the existence proof: if sequential updates caused significant degradation, the SuperGLUE results in Table 3 would show LOMO underperforming LoRA or zero-shot baselines, not outperforming them. The fact that LOMO achieves 80.8% average on 7B (vs. 78.8% for LoRA and 56.2% for zero-shot) and scales to 89.9% on 65B (vs. 89.0% for LoRA) provides strong evidence that the sequential update approximation is benign in practice.

### The Broader Ecosystem Shift

The paper situates itself within a broader movement toward democratizing LLM research. While the introduction frames this in terms of "small labs and companies," the implications extend further. Full-parameter fine-tuning with consumer GPUs enables:

- **Iterative experimentation cycles** at academic timescales, where researchers can afford to try multiple hyperparameter configurations, training data mixtures, and task formulations without waiting for weeks of cluster scheduling.
- **On-device or edge-server fine-tuning**, where a model can be adapted to user-specific data without shipping it to a central datacenter — relevant for privacy-sensitive applications.
- **Multi-party fine-tuning** where different organizations contribute compute to tune different parts of a shared model, since the per-contributor hardware requirements are lower.
- **Curriculum and pedagogy** in graduate ML courses, where students can gain hands-on experience fine-tuning production-scale models on university hardware budgets.

The paper explicitly connects to this democratization vision in its conclusion and limitations sections, identifying parameter quantization as the next frontier: "The majority of memory are currently occupied by parameters when training with LOMO. Thus, one promising direction is the exploration of parameter quantization techniques, which could significantly reduce memory usage." This suggests a roadmap where LOMO handles gradients and optimizer states, activation checkpointing handles activations, and quantization handles parameters — together potentially pushing the memory requirement for full-parameter fine-tuning below that of inference in FP16, which would be a genuinely transformative milestone.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper proposes a **new optimizer** — a piece of software that determines how a neural network's weights are updated during training — that drastically reduces the GPU memory needed for full-parameter fine-tuning of large language models. The core idea is to **fuse gradient computation and parameter update into a single atomic operation during the backward pass**, so that each parameter's gradient is computed, applied immediately to update that parameter, and then discarded — never needing to store gradients for all parameters simultaneously.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The LOMO system has three major components that work together:

1. **SGD as the base optimizer** (Section 3.1): The standard stochastic gradient descent algorithm, which requires no intermediate state storage (unlike Adam, which stores momentum and variance buffers). This eliminates optimizer state memory entirely.

2. **Fused gradient-update mechanism** (Section 3.2, Algorithm 1): A hook-injected backward pass that intercepts each parameter's gradient the moment it is computed, applies the SGD update rule `p = p - lr * grad` immediately, then releases the gradient tensor from memory. At most one layer's gradient exists in memory at any time.

3. **Stabilization techniques for mixed-precision training** (Section 3.3): Methods to handle gradient normalization, gradient clipping, and precision degradation without needing all gradients stored simultaneously — including a two-pass backward strategy that computes gradient norms in the first pass and updates in the second, plus dynamic loss scaling to prevent FP16 underflow.

Information flows as follows: input batch → forward pass (with activation checkpointing, which is orthogonal to LOMO and handled by existing frameworks) → backward pass with injected hooks → per-layer gradient computation → immediate in-place parameter update → gradient tensor released → backpropagation continues to next layer → repeat until all layers updated.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the theoretical argument for why SGD can work for LLM fine-tuning when it historically failed for smaller models (Section 3.1.1), and the formal derivation showing that sequential per-sample updates approximate larger-batch SGD under smooth loss surfaces (Section 3.1.2). This establishes *why* the optimizer can be simplified.
- **Second**, the core LOMO algorithm itself (Section 3.2) — the fused update mechanism, the hook injection strategy, and why gradient memory drops from storing all parameters' gradients to storing one layer's gradient at most.
- **Third**, the stabilization apparatus (Section 3.3): how gradient clipping, gradient normalization, and mixed-precision loss scaling are adapted to work when you cannot access all gradients simultaneously. This is the engineering that makes the idea practically usable rather than merely theoretically appealing.
- **Fourth**, the integration of LOMO with orthogonal memory-saving techniques (activation checkpointing, ZeRO-3 parameter partitioning) — these are not part of LOMO per se but are essential for the headline 10.8% memory figure and the 65B-on-8×3090 result.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and optimization paper** whose core technical contribution is a memory-efficient implementation of stochastic gradient descent that fuses gradient computation with parameter update to eliminate gradient tensor storage, combined with a theoretical argument for why this fusion is computationally safe during LLM fine-tuning.

---

#### 3.4.1 The Case for SGD During Fine-Tuning (Section 3.1.1)

The fundamental enabling move for LOMO is the decision to use **stochastic gradient descent (SGD) rather than Adam** as the optimizer. This decision is not self-evidently correct — SGD has well-documented failures when training deep networks from scratch — so the paper devotes Section 3.1.1 to arguing that the conditions that make Adam necessary during pretraining are attenuated or absent during fine-tuning.

**The memory arithmetic that motivates the switch.** The optimizer states for Adam (or AdamW) consume memory proportional to `2 × number_of_parameters`, because the optimizer maintains two buffers per parameter: a first-moment estimate (exponentially decaying average of past gradients, often called "momentum") and a second-moment estimate (exponentially decaying average of squared gradients, often called "variance" or "uncentered variance"). In mixed-precision training, these buffers are stored in FP32 (full precision) to preserve numerical accuracy during the update step, while the working parameters and gradients may be stored in FP16. For a 7B-parameter model, this means approximately `7 × 10^9 parameters × 4 bytes/FP32 × 2 buffers = 56 GB` for optimizer states alone. Figure 2(a) in the paper shows that for LLaMA-7B training with AdamW, optimizer states consume 73.7% of total training memory. Moving to SGD eliminates both buffers entirely — SGD needs only the current gradient and the current parameter value, neither of which requires persistent storage beyond the moment of update.

**The three classical challenges of SGD and why they may not apply.** The paper structures its argument around the three pathologies that historically drove the adoption of adaptive optimizers like Adam (Ruder, 2016; Sun et al., 2020a):

**Challenge 1: Large curvature loss surface.** When the loss function has regions of high curvature (sharp valleys, narrow ravines), SGD with a fixed learning rate oscillates or diverges because the gradient direction changes rapidly. Adam addresses this by normalizing each parameter's update by an estimate of the recent gradient magnitude (the second moment), effectively damping oscillations in high-curvature directions. The paper's counterargument is that the loss surface of large pretrained language models is **empirically smooth** — small weight perturbations produce small loss changes. They cite Hao et al. (2019), who visualized BERT loss landscapes and found them to be remarkably flat in most directions. The intuition is that scale itself induces smoothness: with billions of parameters, the model has massive redundancy, and individual weight changes are diluted by the contributions of all other weights. The paper explicitly bounds this claim: "This holds only when we teach the LLMs natural language-based tasks (or code-based if pre-trained with code). A synthetic loss function unrelated to pre-training tasks will indeed face the large curvature problem." In other words, the smoothness is a property of the pretrained manifold, not of arbitrary optimization problems.

**Challenge 2: Local optima.** SGD can become trapped in suboptimal local minima. Adam's adaptive per-parameter learning rates and momentum help escape shallow local minima by continuing to move in directions with consistent gradient sign even when the gradient magnitude is small. The paper's response is that for fine-tuning, a local optimum is often sufficient: the goal is to adapt the model to a new task without destroying the pretrained representations, and "the limited training data (compared to pre-training corpus) makes it difficult to push the model to a far-away global optimum." This is an empirical claim — the fine-tuning dataset is typically orders of magnitude smaller than the pretraining corpus (e.g., 1,000 SuperGLUE examples vs. trillions of pretraining tokens), so the model's weights will not move far from their initial values regardless of the optimizer. Any nearby basin of attraction that improves task performance is a success, whether or not it is the global optimum.

**Challenge 3: Saddle points.** In high-dimensional optimization, saddle points (where the gradient is zero but the point is neither a minimum nor a maximum) are far more common than local minima. SGD can stall near saddle points because the gradient is near zero in all directions. The paper argues that for a pretrained LLM applied to a typical NLP task, "the initial point of LLMs should be in a valley. If the model is pre-trained with instructions (tasks), the phenomenon could be much more apparent since we have more chances of finding pre-trained tasks that are similar to the new task." The geometric intuition is that saddle points exist on ridges — boundaries between valleys — and a pretrained checkpoint already sitting in a task-relevant valley is far from those ridges. As long as fine-tuning does not push the parameters far enough to cross into a different basin, saddle points are never encountered.

**The caveat: this is qualitative motivation, not proof.** The paper does not provide loss landscape analyses of LLaMA models, Hessian eigenvalue spectra, or quantitative smoothness measures. These three arguments are offered as **plausibility reasoning** — they establish that the case against SGD may be weaker for LLM fine-tuning than for training from scratch, which justifies the empirical investigation. The actual validation comes from the downstream task results in Section 4.3.

---

#### 3.4.2 Implicit Batch Size from Sequential SGD Updates (Section 3.1.2)

Beyond the qualitative argument, the paper provides a formal derivation showing that under a smoothness assumption, taking multiple sequential SGD steps on individual training samples approximates a single SGD step on a larger batch. This is significant because it implies that even with small per-GPU micro-batches (which are necessary when memory is constrained), the effective optimization dynamics resemble those of larger-batch training, which is generally more stable.

**The derivation: two sequential steps vs. one batched step.** The paper compares two scenarios:

**Scenario A (batched):** One SGD step on a batch containing two data points `$d_i$` and `$d_j$` simultaneously:

$$\theta' = \theta - \alpha[\nabla L(d_i, f(d_i, \theta)) + \nabla L(d_j, f(d_j, \theta))]$$

where `$\theta$` is the parameter vector before the update, `$\theta'$` is the parameter vector after the update, `$\alpha$` is the learning rate, `$L$` is the loss function, `$f(d, \theta)$` is the model's output on input `$d$` with parameters `$\theta$`, and `$\nabla L(d, f(d, \theta))$` is the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters for data point `$d$`.

**Scenario B (sequential):** Two sequential SGD steps, first on `$d_i$` then on `$d_j$`:

$$\theta_1 = \theta - \alpha\nabla L(d_i, f(d_i, \theta))$$

$$\theta_2 = \theta_1 - \alpha\nabla L(d_j, f(d_j, \theta_1))$$

**The connection via the differential mean value theorem.** The paper applies the mean value theorem to relate `$L(d_j, f(d_j, \theta_1))$` to `$L(d_j, f(d_j, \theta))$`:

$$L(d_j, f(d_j, \theta_1)) = L(d_j, f(d_j, \theta)) + \nabla L(d_j, \xi)(f(d_j, \theta_1) - f(d_j, \theta))$$

where `$\xi$` is some point on the line segment between `$f(d_j, \theta)$` and `$f(d_j, \theta_1)$` in output space, and `$\nabla L(d_j, \xi)$` is the gradient of the loss evaluated at that intermediate point.

Substituting this into the expression for `$\theta_2$` and expanding:

$$\theta_2 = \theta - \alpha\nabla L(d_i, f(d_i, \theta)) - \alpha\nabla L(d_j, f(d_j, \theta)) - \alpha\nabla[\nabla L(d_j, \xi)(f(d_j, \theta_1) - f(d_j, \theta))]$$

**The discrepancy term.** Comparing `$\theta_2$` (the result of two sequential steps) with `$\theta'$` (the result of one batched step), the difference is:

$$\theta_2 - \theta' = -\alpha\nabla[\nabla L(d_j, \xi)(f(d_j, \theta_1) - f(d_j, \theta))]$$

**Why this term is small.** The discrepancy depends on three factors: (1) `$\alpha$`, the learning rate, which is small during fine-tuning (the paper uses values like `$5 \times 10^{-2}$` to `$3 \times 10^{-2}$` for the full model, which translates to per-weight changes on the order of `$10^{-4}$` or smaller); (2) `$\nabla L(d_j, \xi)$`, the gradient of the loss, which is bounded if the loss surface is smooth; and (3) `$f(d_j, \theta_1) - f(d_j, \theta)$`, the change in model output after one SGD step on `$d_i$`, which is small when the learning rate is small and the model is smooth. The product of these three small quantities is a second-order term — it scales with `$\alpha^2$` times the effective curvature of the loss, which vanishes faster than the first-order gradient signal as `$\alpha \to 0$`.

**What this implies operationally.** The derivation shows that performing SGD updates sample-by-sample (or micro-batch-by-micro-batch, which is what LOMO does in practice) is approximately equivalent to accumulating gradients across those samples and applying one update. The approximation error is controlled by the smoothness of the loss surface and the learning rate. Since LLM fine-tuning typically uses relatively small learning rates (to avoid catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge) and the loss surface is empirically smooth, the error is negligible. This means that **the sequential nature of LOMO's updates — each layer is updated before gradients for earlier layers are fully computed — does not fundamentally break the SGD algorithm; it merely reorders the operations in a way that is mathematically almost equivalent under the conditions that hold during fine-tuning.**

**The significance for stability.** A larger effective batch size generally improves training stability because gradient estimates have lower variance. The derivation suggests that even when hardware constraints force a small per-GPU micro-batch (e.g., batch size 1 or 2), the sequential update pattern — combined with the model's smoothness — means the optimization behaves as if the batch size were larger. This provides theoretical justification for why LOMO does not exhibit the instability that historically plagued small-batch SGD.

#### 3.4.3 The Core LOMO Algorithm: Fused Gradient Computation and Update (Section 3.2, Algorithm 1)

The centerpiece of LOMO is Algorithm 1, which describes how the backward pass is modified to update parameters immediately upon gradient computation. This section provides a step-by-step walkthrough.

**The standard (non-fused) gradient descent process.** In standard deep learning frameworks like PyTorch, training proceeds in three sequential phases:

1. **Forward pass:** The input batch flows through the network layer by layer, producing activations and ultimately a scalar loss value. All intermediate activations are stored (unless activation checkpointing is used) to enable gradient computation.
2. **Backward pass:** Starting from the loss, gradients are computed layer by layer in reverse order using the chain rule. For each layer `$l$`, the gradient of the loss with respect to that layer's parameters, `$g_l = \partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \theta_l$`, is computed and stored in a gradient tensor with the same shape as `$\theta_l$`. Crucially, `$g_l$` is not used immediately — it is accumulated into a `.grad` attribute attached to the parameter tensor, and all parameters' gradients are stored simultaneously in GPU memory.
3. **Optimizer step:** After the backward pass completes and all gradients are stored, the optimizer (e.g., SGD, Adam) iterates over all parameters and applies the update rule `$\theta_l \leftarrow \theta_l - \alpha \cdot g_l$` (for SGD) or a more complex rule involving momentum and variance buffers (for Adam). The gradients are then typically zeroed for the next iteration.

**Why this uses so much memory.** The gradient tensor for each parameter has exactly the same shape and memory footprint as the parameter itself. For a transformer model, the largest parameter tensors are the embedding matrix and the feed-forward network weight matrices. For LLaMA-7B, these total approximately 12.55 GB in FP16 (Table 1). Storing all gradients simultaneously therefore requires an additional ~12.55 GB — a 100% overhead on top of the parameters themselves. For AdamW, the optimizer states add another ~75 GB. Together, gradients and optimizer states consume roughly 7× the memory of the parameters alone.

**The fused alternative.** LOMO changes this sequence by eliminating the distinction between phases 2 and 3. The key insight is algorithmic, not hardware-dependent: **a parameter can be updated as soon as its gradient is known, and the gradient can then be freed, because SGD does not require any gradient information from other layers to compute the update for this layer.** This is not true for Adam — Adam needs the full gradient tensor to update its running averages of the first and second moments, and those running averages must persist across iterations. But for SGD, the update rule is `$\theta_l \leftarrow \theta_l - \alpha \cdot g_l$`, which depends only on `$\theta_l$` (already in memory) and `$g_l$` (just computed). Once the update is applied, `$g_l$` is never needed again.

Algorithm 1 from the paper formalizes this:

> **Algorithm 1 Fusion Update in LOMO**
>
> **Require:** model `$f(\cdot)$` with `$L$` layers and `$p$` parameters, parameter `$\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$`, learning rate `$\alpha$`, max step `$T$`, training dataset `$\mathcal{D}$`, loss function `$\mathcal{L}$`
>
> 1: **for** `$t = 1, \ldots, T$` **do**
> 2: &emsp;&emsp; Sample batch `$\mathcal{B} = (x, y) \subset \mathcal{D}$`
> 3: &emsp;&emsp; `$\hat{y} \leftarrow f(x, \theta)$` &emsp;&emsp;&emsp;&emsp;&emsp;&emsp;▷ Forward pass
> 4: &emsp;&emsp; `$\ell \leftarrow \mathcal{L}(y, \hat{y})$`
> 5: &emsp;&emsp; **for** `$l = L, \ldots, 1$` **do** &emsp;&emsp;&emsp;▷ Backward (in reverse order)
> 6: &emsp;&emsp;&emsp;&emsp; `$\theta_l \leftarrow [\theta_i \text{ for } \theta_i \in \text{layer } l]$`
> 7: &emsp;&emsp;&emsp;&emsp; `$g_l \leftarrow \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_l}$`
> 8: &emsp;&emsp;&emsp;&emsp; `$\theta_l \leftarrow \theta_l - \alpha * g_l$`
> 9: &emsp;&emsp;&emsp;&emsp; `$g_l \leftarrow \text{None}$` &emsp;&emsp;&emsp;▷ Clear gradients
> 10: &emsp;&emsp; **end for**
> 11: **end for**

**What each line does in operational terms:**

- **Line 2:** A batch of training data is sampled. The batch consists of input text `$x$` (the prompt with a label candidate inserted) and target `$y$` (the correct label for classification tasks in SuperGLUE).

- **Line 3:** The forward pass computes the model's prediction `$\hat{y}$`. This stores activations at each layer. If activation checkpointing is enabled (which is orthogonal to LOMO and handled by the framework), only a subset of activations are retained; the rest will be recomputed during the backward pass.

- **Line 4:** The scalar loss `$\ell$` (negative log-likelihood for the correct label, averaged or summed over the batch) is computed. This is the quantity whose gradient will be backpropagated.

- **Lines 5–10:** The backward pass proceeds from the last layer `$L$` down to the first layer `$1$`. For each layer `$l$`:
  - **Line 6:** The subset of parameters belonging to layer `$l$` is identified. In a transformer, this includes the attention projection matrices (`$W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O$`), the feed-forward network weights (`$W_1, W_2$`), and the layer normalization parameters (scale and bias). Different layers have different parameter counts — the feed-forward layers are typically the largest (e.g., in LLaMA-7B, the intermediate FFN dimension is 11,008, so `$W_1$` is a `$4096 \times 11008$` matrix).
  - **Line 7:** The gradient `$g_l$` of the loss with respect to `$\theta_l$` is computed via automatic differentiation. This requires the activations from layer `$l$` and the error signal flowing backward from layer `$l+1$`, both of which are available because backpropagation proceeds in reverse topological order.
  - **Line 8:** The SGD update is applied immediately: `$\theta_l \leftarrow \theta_l - \alpha \cdot g_l$`. This is an in-place operation — the parameter tensor `$\theta_l$` is modified directly. The learning rate `$\alpha$` is a scalar hyperparameter (the paper sweeps values in `$\{5 \times 10^{-2}, 3 \times 10^{-2}\}$` for LOMO on SuperGLUE; see Table 4).
  - **Line 9:** The gradient tensor `$g_l$` is explicitly deleted (set to `None` in Python, which releases the underlying GPU memory). This is the critical memory-saving step. As soon as one layer's parameters are updated, its gradient is no longer needed and can be freed. The next iteration of the loop (for layer `$l-1$`) will allocate a new gradient tensor for that layer's parameters. At any given moment during the backward pass, at most one layer's gradient exists in memory.

**The memory bound.** The maximum gradient memory at any point in time is the size of the largest single parameter tensor in the model. For a transformer, this is typically the feed-forward network's `$W_1$` or `$W_2$` matrix. For LLaMA-7B with hidden dimension 4096 and intermediate dimension 11008, `$W_1$` has shape `$[4096, 11008]$`, which in FP16 requires `$4096 \times 11008 \times 2$` bytes ≈ 90 MB. This is dramatically smaller than the full gradient memory of ~12.55 GB (all parameters combined). The paper states that the gradient memory is reduced to `$O(1)$` — in asymptotic notation, constant with respect to the total number of parameters, because only one layer's gradient lives in memory at a time regardless of how many layers the model has. In practice, the constant is the size of the largest layer's parameter tensor.

**Implementation via PyTorch hooks.** The paper notes that a completely faithful implementation of "update immediately upon gradient computation" is not possible with PyTorch's standard APIs. PyTorch's autograd engine computes gradients in a predetermined order and does not expose an interception point where a single parameter's gradient can be captured, used, and released before the rest of the backward pass continues. Instead, LOMO uses **hook functions** — callbacks that PyTorch invokes at specific points during the backward pass. The paper states: "we cannot implement the exact immediate update with current APIs. Instead, we store at most one parameter's gradient in memory and update each parameter one by one along with the backward propagation." The exact hook mechanism and how it interfaces with PyTorch's autograd engine are not detailed in the paper, but the practical outcome is the same: gradient memory is bounded by the largest layer, not the full model.

**Why this must be coupled with SGD.** The fusion in Line 8 — `$\theta_l \leftarrow \theta_l - \alpha * g_l$` — is only valid for optimizers with stateless update rules. For Adam, the update would be:

$$m_l \leftarrow \beta_1 m_l + (1 - \beta_1) g_l$$

$$v_l \leftarrow \beta_2 v_l + (1 - \beta_2) g_l^2$$

$$\theta_l \leftarrow \theta_l - \alpha \cdot \frac{m_l}{\sqrt{v_l} + \epsilon}$$

This requires `$m_l$` and `$v_l$` (the first and second moment estimates) to persist across iterations — they cannot be computed, used, and discarded because they encode the history of all past gradients for this parameter. If you tried to fuse Adam updates, you would need to keep `$m_l$` and `$v_l$` in memory permanently, which defeats the purpose. SGD's statelessness — the update depends only on the current gradient — is what enables fusion. This is why the argument in Section 3.1 (that SGD is sufficient for LLM fine-tuning) is not merely motivational but **architecturally necessary** for LOMO to work.

**Interaction with parameter sharing.** The paper notes one subtlety: "We should inject different hook functions accordingly if some of them share the weight." In transformer models, the embedding matrix and the output projection (language modeling head) are sometimes tied (share the same weight matrix). In such cases, a single parameter tensor contributes to the loss at two different points in the computational graph, and its gradient is the sum of the gradients from both paths. LOMO's hook mechanism must handle this correctly — accumulating the gradient contributions from both paths before applying the update, rather than applying two separate partial updates. The paper does not elaborate on the implementation details, but this is a standard concern in any gradient manipulation framework and is solvable with appropriate hook logic.

#### 3.4.4 Stabilizing Mixed-Precision Training with LOMO (Section 3.3)

The fused update mechanism solves the gradient memory problem, but it creates new challenges for two standard training stabilization techniques: **gradient normalization/clipping** and **mixed-precision loss scaling**. Both of these techniques normally require access to all gradients simultaneously, which LOMO deliberately avoids. Section 3.3 describes how to adapt them.

##### 3.4.4.1 Gradient Clipping and Normalization Without All Gradients (Section 3.3.1)

**Why gradient clipping is necessary.** During training, individual gradient elements can occasionally become very large (the "exploding gradient" problem), especially early in fine-tuning when the model is adapting to a new loss landscape. Unchecked, these large gradients can cause destabilizingly large parameter updates that push the model into a high-loss region from which it cannot recover. The standard solution is **gradient clipping by norm**: compute the L2 norm of the concatenated gradient vector across all parameters, and if it exceeds a threshold `$C$`, rescale all gradients by `$C / \|\mathbf{g}\|_2$` so that the total gradient norm equals `$C$`.

**The problem for LOMO.** Computing the global gradient norm requires knowing the squared magnitude of every parameter's gradient before any update is applied. But LOMO updates each layer immediately upon gradient computation and discards the gradient. By the time the backward pass reaches the first layer, the gradients for all later layers have already been computed, used, and deleted. The global norm cannot be computed.

**Alternative 1: Clipping by value.** Instead of clipping the entire gradient vector by its norm, clip each individual gradient element by its absolute value. If a gradient element exceeds a threshold, it is set to the threshold value (preserving its sign). The paper states:

> "Clipping gradient tensors by their values is a simple but effective solution for gradient explosion before gradient norm approaches."

The concern with value clipping is that it can change the **direction** of the gradient. For example, a gradient vector `$[1.3, 0.8]$` clipped to a maximum absolute value of 1.0 becomes `$[1.0, 0.8]$`. The ratio of the two components changes from `$1.3:0.8$` to `$1.0:0.8$`, which points in a slightly different direction. Norm clipping, by contrast, rescales all components by the same factor, preserving the direction. The paper acknowledges this: "Our experience is that the clipping by values performs worse when the learning rate is high because truncations happened more often in that case. However, clipping by values performs well for medium and small learning rates." The recommendation is to use value clipping when the learning rate is below `$1 \times 10^{-3}$`. Since LOMO typically uses learning rates in the range `$5 \times 10^{-2}$` to `$3 \times 10^{-2}$` (Table 4), this seems contradictory — but those learning rates are for the full model update; the per-parameter effective step size is smaller due to the parameter scale, and the paper considers these to be moderate learning rates.

**Alternative 2: Two-pass gradient norm computation.** To enable true gradient norm clipping, the paper introduces an additional backward pass. The procedure:
1. **First backward pass:** Compute gradients for all parameters but do not update them. Accumulate the squared gradient norm. This pass is purely for measurement — it computes the scaling factor `$C / \|\mathbf{g}\|_2$` (or 1.0 if the norm is below `$C$`).
2. **Second backward pass:** Re-run the backward pass (which requires either recomputing activations via activation checkpointing or storing them from the first pass) and apply the fused LOMO updates, this time scaling each gradient by the factor computed in step 1.

The paper is candid about the cost: "The memory usage leaves unchanged but sacrifices the speed." Two backward passes mean approximately 2× the backward computation time, though the forward pass is still done once. The total training time increases, but memory usage does not — the first pass stores only the scalar gradient norm, not the individual gradients.

**Alternative 3 (flagged as controversial, Section 3.3.1): Grouped gradient norm approximation.** Instead of computing the global gradient norm, approximate it by computing the norm within groups of adjacent layers. Each group's gradients are clipped based on that group's local norm rather than the global norm. The paper acknowledges that this introduces bias:

> "This method is indeed biased, because it results in different update step sizes for different parameters. When updating, the parameters are multiplied by a scale factor according to the gradient norms. Since the gradient norms differ among parameter groups, such an approximation leads to a difference in scale factors."

However, the paper reframes this bias as potentially beneficial: different parameter groups receiving different effective learning rates based on their local gradient statistics is reminiscent of adaptive optimizers, which do exactly this. "Sun et al. (2020a) suggests that it is not always appropriate to use the same learning rate for all parameters in SGD, thus we believe our approach also holds the potential to further benefit SGD." This alternative is left as future work and is not evaluated in the paper.

**What is used in practice.** The paper's experiments use a max gradient norm of 1.0 (Table 4: "Max Grad Norm: 1.0"). The implementation uses the two-pass approach to enable global norm computation when gradient normalization is needed.

##### 3.4.4.2 Mitigating Precision Degradation with Dynamic Loss Scaling (Section 3.3.2)

**Why mixed-precision training needs loss scaling.** FP16 has a limited dynamic range: the smallest positive normal value is approximately `$6 \times 10^{-8}$`. During training, gradient values for some parameters can be smaller than this, causing them to underflow to zero when stored in FP16. These parameters then receive zero updates and stop learning. **Loss scaling** (Micikevicius et al., 2018) addresses this by multiplying the loss value by a large constant `$S$` before the backward pass. Since gradients are computed via the chain rule, all gradients are also multiplied by `$S$`, shifting small gradient values into the representable range of FP16. Before the parameter update, the gradients are divided by `$S$` to restore their correct scale. The scaling factor `$S$` must be chosen carefully: too small and it doesn't prevent underflow; too large and it causes overflow (gradients exceeding the maximum FP16 value of 65,504).

**Dynamic loss scaling.** Rather than choosing a fixed `$S$`, the paper uses **dynamic loss scaling**, which adapts `$S$` during training:
- Start with an initial scaling factor (the paper doesn't specify the initial value, but typical practice starts at `$2^{16} = 65536$` or similar).
- After each backward pass, check whether any gradient overflow occurred (any gradient value had an infinite or NaN value due to exceeding FP16 max).
- If no overflow occurred for a specified number of consecutive iterations, **double** the scaling factor (increase `$S$` to push more precision into small gradients).
- If overflow occurs, **skip the update** for that iteration (the gradients are corrupted), **halve** the scaling factor (reduce `$S$` to prevent future overflow), and proceed to the next iteration.

**The problem for LOMO.** Checking for overflow normally happens after the backward pass completes, when all gradients are available. But LOMO updates parameters during the backward pass — by the time the backward pass finishes, the gradients have already been applied and deleted. If an overflow occurred, the corrupted gradients have already been written into the parameters, and the damage is done.

**Solution: two backward passes for loss scaling too.** The paper adopts the same two-pass strategy used for gradient normalization:

1. **First backward pass:** Compute all gradients but do not apply updates. Monitor for overflow (NaN or Inf values in any gradient tensor). If overflow is detected, record this fact, discard the gradients, halve the scaling factor, and skip to the next iteration. If no overflow, compute the scaling factor adjustment (double after enough clean iterations) and proceed to step 2.
2. **Second backward pass:** Recompute gradients and apply the fused LOMO updates, using the loss scaling factor determined by the first pass.

The paper notes: "These two backward passes for dynamic loss scaling can be executed simultaneously with gradient normalization." This means that when both gradient norm clipping and loss scaling are needed, the two backward passes serve both purposes: the first pass computes the gradient norm and checks for overflow; the second pass applies the clipped, appropriately scaled updates.

**Transitioning computations to full precision.** Even with loss scaling, certain operations are numerically sensitive and should be performed in FP32 rather than FP16. The paper states: "To effectively update parameter and handle gradient for operations like normalization and scaling, the gradient and its associated parameter are converted to full precision within these computations." Specifically:
- When computing the L2 norm for gradient clipping, the squared gradient values are accumulated in FP32 to avoid overflow/underflow in the summation.
- When applying the SGD update `$\theta_l \leftarrow \theta_l - \alpha \cdot g_l$`, the multiplication `$\alpha \cdot g_l$` and the subtraction are performed in FP32 to preserve precision, then the result is cast back to FP16 for storage. This requires temporarily promoting the parameter tensor to FP32, applying the update, and demoting it — which costs a small amount of additional memory for the FP32 copy during the update operation, but this is transient and per-layer, not persistent.

#### 3.4.5 Integration with Orthogonal Memory-Saving Techniques

LOMO achieves its dramatic memory reduction by combining its fused gradient-update mechanism with two existing techniques that address different components of training memory. The paper is explicit that these are orthogonal — LOMO handles gradients and optimizer states, while the other techniques handle activations and parameter distribution.

**Activation Checkpointing** (gradient checkpointing, Chen et al., 2016). During the forward pass of a transformer model, each layer's output activations are typically stored because they are needed during the backward pass to compute gradients for that layer's inputs. For a model with sequence length 512 and batch size 8, the activation memory for LLaMA-7B is approximately 45.61 GB (Table 1, AdamW without checkpointing) — larger than the parameters themselves. Activation checkpointing trades computation for memory: instead of storing all activations, it stores only the activations at strategically chosen "checkpoint" layers. During the backward pass, when the gradients for a non-checkpoint layer are needed, its activations are recomputed from the nearest earlier checkpoint. The paper notes that this reduces activation memory to "the square root of the original amount at the cost of one extra forward pass." In Table 1, this reduces activation memory for LLaMA-7B from 45.61 GB to 1.79 GB.

**ZeRO-3 Parameter Partitioning** (Rajbhandari et al., 2020). ZeRO-3 (Zero Redundancy Optimizer stage 3) partitions model parameters across multiple GPUs, so each GPU stores only a fraction of each parameter tensor. During the forward pass, when a layer's parameters are needed, they are gathered from all GPUs via inter-GPU communication (e.g., all-reduce), the computation is performed, and the full parameters are discarded — only the partition remains. This allows training models that are much larger than a single GPU's memory. LOMO uses ZeRO-3 for the 13B, 30B, and 65B model experiments (Table 2), where the model does not fit on a single GPU. For the 7B model, LOMO can train on a single GPU without ZeRO-3, achieving 769.92 tokens per GPU per second (TGS) vs. 67.37 TGS for AdamW on 8 GPUs — the 11× speedup comes from eliminating inter-GPU communication entirely.

**Why the combination works.** Activation checkpointing reduces activation memory (from ~45 GB to ~2 GB for 7B). LOMO reduces gradient memory (from ~12.55 GB to ~0.24 GB for 7B, the size of the largest layer's gradient) and eliminates optimizer state memory (from ~75 GB to 0 GB). ZeRO-3 partitions the remaining dominant memory consumer — the parameters — across GPUs. The result: total memory per GPU drops from 102.20 GB (AdamW with activation checkpointing) to 14.58 GB (LOMO with activation checkpointing), a 7× reduction. The 10.8% figure in the abstract comes from comparing LOMO's 14.58 GB to the "standard approach" using DeepSpeed (which includes ZeRO-3, activation checkpointing, and AdamW) — presumably 14.58 / (some baseline around 135 GB) ≈ 10.8%, though the exact baseline figure is not explicitly stated in Table 1.

#### 3.4.6 Hyperparameter Configuration and Design Choices Summary

The paper's experimental configuration (Table 4) reveals several deliberate choices:

**Learning rate schedule: Linear decay.** Unlike cosine decay (common in pretraining) or constant learning rate, linear decay gradually reduces the learning rate from its initial value to zero over the course of training. This is a conservative choice appropriate for fine-tuning, where the goal is to make bounded adjustments to already-good weights. The linear schedule ensures that early iterations make larger adaptations while later iterations make progressively smaller refinements, reducing the risk of overfitting to the small fine-tuning dataset.

**Batch size: 16.** This is the total batch size across all GPUs (for LOMO on 7B with a single GPU, this is simply the per-GPU batch size). The paper uses sequence length 1024 for throughput experiments (Table 2) and 512 for memory profiling (Table 1). For SuperGLUE, the sequence length is task-dependent (determined by the prompt templates from MeZO).

**Learning rate sweep: `{5e-2, 3e-2}` for LOMO.** These learning rates are substantially higher than typical AdamW learning rates (which are usually `$5 \times 10^{-5}$` to `$5 \times 10^{-4}$` for fine-tuning). The reason: SGD without momentum and without adaptive per-parameter scaling tends to make slower progress per step than Adam, because the effective step size in each direction is the raw learning rate rather than a normalized quantity. To compensate, SGD typically uses larger nominal learning rates. The paper sweeps only two values and reports the best result per task.

**Warmup ratio sweep: `{0.05, 0.1, 0.2}` for LOMO.** Warmup linearly increases the learning rate from zero to the target value over the first fraction of training steps. This prevents destabilizingly large updates in the first few iterations, when the model has not yet adapted to the new task and gradients may be erratic. The paper sweeps three warmup ratios.

**Epochs: 10, with 1,000 training examples.** The training set is small (1,000 examples per SuperGLUE task, following MeZO's protocol), so 10 epochs means 10,000 total training steps (with batch size 16, 1,000/16 ≈ 62.5 steps per epoch, × 10 = ~625 steps — actually fewer than 10,000 because 1,000/16 is not an integer; the exact number depends on batching). This limited training budget is realistic for resource-constrained settings: training completes quickly even with the two-pass backward strategy.

**Max gradient norm: 1.0.** A standard value for gradient clipping in NLP fine-tuning. When using the two-pass approach, gradients across all parameters are rescaled so that their total L2 norm does not exceed 1.0. This prevents any single iteration from making excessively large parameter changes.

**LoRA configuration (for comparison):** `$r_q = r_v = 2$`, LoRA `$\alpha = 16$`, AdamW optimizer with learning rate `$5 \times 10^{-4}$`, warmup ratio 0.05. LoRA applies low-rank updates to the query and value projection matrices (denoted `$r_q$` and `$r_v$`) with rank 2 — meaning only `$2 \times (d_{model} + d_{head})$` parameters are trained per attention head, a tiny fraction of the full weight matrices. The `$\alpha$` parameter scales the LoRA update relative to the original weight.

**LoRA+LOMO combined configuration:** When both are used together (Section 4.3.2, Figure 3), LoRA modules are trained with AdamW (learning rate `$5 \times 10^{-4}$`) while the full model weights are simultaneously trained with LOMO (learning rates swept from `$\{5 \times 10^{-3}, 1 \times 10^{-3}, 5 \times 10^{-4}\}$`). This demonstrates that LOMO and parameter-efficient methods are complementary — LOMO handles the full parameters with low memory, while LoRA adds trainable capacity for the specific task.

#### 3.4.7 What LOMO Does NOT Change

It is instructive to be explicit about what LOMO preserves, because the paper's claim is not merely that it saves memory but that it saves memory **without compromising the optimization**.

**The loss function is unchanged.** LOMO uses the same negative log-likelihood loss as standard fine-tuning. For SuperGLUE, the model computes the average log-likelihood of each candidate label given the prompt, and the loss encourages the correct label to have higher likelihood.

**The forward pass is unchanged.** The model processes the input exactly as in standard training. Activation checkpointing changes which activations are stored but not how they are computed. The final loss value is identical to what standard training would produce for the same batch.

**The gradient values (before clipping) are mathematically identical to standard backpropagation.** The backward pass uses the same chain rule, the same activation values (recomputed if necessary), and the same loss value. The only difference is the order of applying updates: in standard SGD, all gradients are computed and then all updates are applied; in LOMO, each layer's gradient is computed, used to update that layer, and then the backward pass continues to the next layer. The gradient for layer `$l-1$` is computed with respect to the already-updated layer `$l$` rather than the pre-update layer `$l$`, but as shown in Section 3.1.2, this difference is second-order in the learning rate and vanishes for smooth loss surfaces.

**The parameter update rule is identical to SGD.** Each parameter is updated as `$\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \cdot g$`. There is no approximation, no quantization, no low-rank projection — the update is exact gradient descent (with the caveat about sequential vs. batched updates discussed above).

**The final model is a standard set of weights.** After fine-tuning with LOMO, the model is just a standard LLaMA checkpoint with updated parameters. There are no additional modules, no adapter weights, no modifications to the architecture. This means the fine-tuned model can be deployed using standard inference code with no additional latency or memory overhead — a key advantage over LoRA, which either requires merging the adapter weights into the base weights (losing the ability to switch between tasks) or keeping the adapters separate (adding inference-time computation).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Optimizer as the Primary Memory Bottleneck — and Proving It Can Be Eliminated Entirely

The dominant assumption in the deep learning systems community, reinforced by years of incremental engineering progress, is that **the optimizer is a fixed cost of training**. Adam and AdamW have become so synonymous with LLM training that they are treated as non-negotiable infrastructure — you can offload them (ZeRO-Offload), quantize them (8-bit Adam), or distribute them across GPUs (ZeRO-1/2), but you cannot simply remove them. The memory cost of optimizer states — typically 2× the parameter count in FP32 for Adam's momentum and variance buffers — has been accepted as an unavoidable tax on training large models.

LOMO challenges this assumption at its root. The paper's foundational insight is that **the optimizer's memory cost is an artifact of optimizing from random initialization, not an intrinsic requirement of gradient-based learning**. When fine-tuning rather than pretraining, the optimization landscape is fundamentally different: the model starts in a well-behaved region of parameter space (a pretrained checkpoint), the loss surface is empirically smooth (Hao et al., 2019), and the goal is local adaptation rather than global exploration. Under these conditions, SGD — which stores no intermediate state whatsoever — becomes not merely viable but sufficient.

This reframing has implications far beyond the specific LOMO implementation. It recategorizes optimizer state memory from a *hardware constraint* (something you must accommodate with bigger GPUs or clever engineering) to a *design choice* (something you can opt out of when the optimization problem permits). This conceptual shift is what makes the subsequent engineering — the fused update mechanism, the two-pass backward strategy for gradient normalization — more than a clever trick. It is the intellectual foundation that justifies why the trick is worth doing at all.

The field had prior hints that full-precision optimizer states might be excessive. Dettmers et al. (2022) showed that momentum and variance can be stored in 8-bit precision with minimal accuracy loss. MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) showed that approximate gradients from zeroth-order optimization can work for fine-tuning. But both of these approaches **compromise the optimization signal** — quantization introduces rounding error, and zeroth-order methods introduce estimation variance that scales with dimensionality. LOMO's innovation is to show that under the right conditions (fine-tuning, smooth loss surface), you can use **exact gradients with no optimizer state at all** — achieving memory savings larger than quantization without any approximation. This is the paper's diagnostic contribution: correctly identifying *when* the optimizer tax is unnecessary, not just *how* to reduce it.

The evidence that validates this framing is not merely the memory numbers in Table 1 — those are engineering outcomes — but the downstream performance in Table 3, where LOMO (SGD with fused updates) matches or exceeds LoRA (AdamW with low-rank adapters) across all model sizes from 7B to 65B. If SGD were genuinely insufficient for LLM fine-tuning, the performance gap would be evident. Its absence is the existence proof that the optimizer tax can be eliminated.

---

### Innovation 2: The Implicit Batch Size Derivation as a Theoretical Bridge Between Scale and Optimization Stability

A recurring puzzle in the optimization literature is why large models, which should be harder to optimize due to their high dimensionality, often exhibit **more stable training dynamics** than smaller models. Practitioners have observed this empirically — large transformers are less finicky about learning rates, batch sizes, and other hyperparameters — but the theoretical mechanisms remain poorly understood.

LOMO contributes a concrete, formal mechanism for part of this puzzle: **sequential per-sample (or per-micro-batch) SGD updates on a large, smooth model approximate larger-batch SGD**. The derivation in Section 3.1.2 (Equations 1–6) is not merely a justification for why LOMO's per-layer sequential updates don't break SGD — it is a **standalone theoretical result** about the interaction between model scale and optimization dynamics.

The key insight is that the discrepancy between sequential and batched updates is proportional to the product of the learning rate, the gradient magnitude, and the change in model output induced by the preceding update. As model scale increases (and the loss surface becomes smoother), this third factor — the model's sensitivity to individual weight changes — shrinks. Each parameter contributes less to the overall function, so changing one layer's weights has a smaller impact on the loss computed at subsequent layers. The sequential-vs-batched discrepancy therefore vanishes faster for larger models, all else equal.

This is not an incremental refinement of existing theory. Prior work on large-batch training (Goyal et al., 2017; You et al., 2017) focused on how to scale learning rates linearly with batch size, treating batch size as a tunable knob. LOMO's derivation inverts this: it shows that **effective batch size can emerge from sequential processing under smoothness conditions**, not just from explicit gradient accumulation. This provides a theoretical mechanism for the empirical observation that large models tolerate small per-GPU micro-batches without instability — their smoothness implicitly increases the effective batch size.

The derivation also explains why LOMO does not need the sophisticated gradient synchronization that distributed SGD normally requires. In standard data-parallel training, each GPU computes gradients on a micro-batch, and gradients are averaged across GPUs before the update — this is what enforces consistency. LOMO's per-layer sequential updates break this synchronization (each layer is updated before gradients for other layers on other GPUs are available), yet the model trains stably. The implicit batch size derivation provides the theoretical cover: the asynchrony introduces discrepancy terms that are second-order in the learning rate, controlled by the model's smoothness, and therefore negligible in practice.

This is a **theoretical advance**, not merely an engineering convenience. It connects two previously separate lines of inquiry — the empirical smoothness of large model loss landscapes (Hao et al., 2019; Neyshabur et al., 2017) and the optimization dynamics of SGD (Ruder, 2016; Sun et al., 2020a) — into a unified mechanism. Future work on optimization for large models can build on this insight to design asynchronous or communication-efficient training algorithms with formal guarantees, rather than relying on heuristic justifications.

### Innovation 3: Gradient Storage as an Algorithmic Convention, Not a Mathematical Necessity

Every standard deep learning framework — PyTorch, TensorFlow, JAX — stores gradient tensors for all parameters simultaneously during the backward pass. This is not because backpropagation mathematically requires it; the chain rule computes each parameter's gradient independently from the gradients of downstream layers. It is an **engineering convention** driven by two practical considerations: (1) optimizer states (Adam's momentum and variance) need the full gradient vector to update their running averages, and (2) gradient normalization and clipping need the global gradient norm, which requires all gradients to be available.

LOMO's core systems insight is to identify this convention and systematically dismantle it. By switching to SGD (which eliminates consideration 1) and developing the two-pass backward strategy for gradient normalization (which addresses consideration 2), LOMO demonstrates that **gradient memory can be reduced from `O(P)` to `O(1)` without any mathematical compromise** — where `P` is the total number of parameters and `O(1)` means constant with respect to model size (specifically, the size of the largest single layer's gradient).

This is a fundamental rethinking of the backward pass, not an incremental optimization. Prior work on memory-efficient training accepted the convention that gradients must be stored and focused on reducing other memory components: activation checkpointing (Chen et al., 2016) reduces activation storage; mixed-precision training (Micikevicius et al., 2018) reduces the bit-width of what is stored; heterogeneous systems (Ren et al., 2021b; Rajbhandari et al., 2021) move tensors to cheaper storage tiers. GaLore (Zhao et al., 2024) approximates gradients with low-rank matrices, reducing storage at the cost of information loss. LOMO alone asks: **why are we storing gradients at all?**

The answer — "because Adam needs them" — reveals that the gradient storage convention was always contingent on the choice of optimizer, not dictated by backpropagation itself. By changing the optimizer to one that does not maintain state, the convention can be discarded. The memory savings are not a small percentage improvement but a **categorical elimination** of a major memory consumer. Figure 2(c) shows that gradients drop from 12.3% of total memory (SGD without fusion) to 1.6% (LOMO), and combined with the elimination of optimizer states, total memory drops from 51.99 GB to 14.58 GB (Table 1, comparing SGD with activation checkpointing to LOMO with activation checkpointing).

This insight has implications beyond LOMO. It suggests that the software-hardware co-design of deep learning frameworks should not treat the backward pass as a monolithic phase that must complete before any updates occur. Future optimizers that maintain minimal or no state could adopt similar fusion strategies, and frameworks could expose more flexible APIs for interleaving gradient computation with parameter updates — something the paper notes is not currently possible with PyTorch's standard APIs. The paper's observation that "we cannot implement the exact immediate update with current APIs" is simultaneously a limitation and a call to action for framework developers.

The evidence that this reframing is valid — that the gradient storage convention can be broken without harming training — is in the downstream results (Table 3) and the throughput measurements (Table 2). If breaking the convention caused gradient inconsistency or instability, the 7B LOMO model would not achieve 80.8% average SuperGLUE accuracy (vs. 78.8% for LoRA) or 769.92 tokens per GPU per second throughput (vs. 67.37 for AdamW). The fact that it does both — better performance and dramatically higher throughput — validates the conceptual move.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The SuperGLUE benchmark collection (Wang et al., 2019), specifically six tasks: RTE (recognizing textual entailment; Dagan et al., 2005), BoolQ (boolean question answering; Clark et al., 2019), WSC (Winograd schema challenge; Levesque et al., 2012), WIC (word-in-context; Pilehvar and Camacho-Collados, 2019), MultiRC (multi-sentence reading comprehension; Khashabi et al., 2018), and COPA (choice of plausible alternatives; Roemmele et al., 2011). Following MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023), the authors randomly sample 1,000 training examples from the training set and 1,000 test examples from the validation set per task, reporting the best result obtained using the same random seed. Memory profiling and throughput experiments use LLaMA models on synthetic training runs rather than the downstream evaluation tasks.

- **Base model(s).** LLaMA (Touvron et al., 2023) at four scales: 7B, 13B, 30B, and 65B parameters. The choice of LLaMA is practical — it is an open-source model family with publicly available weights spanning a wide parameter range, enabling the paper to demonstrate scaling behavior from a model that fits on a single consumer GPU (7B with LOMO) to one that pushes the limits of an 8× RTX 3090 server (65B). The authors argue that LLaMA is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (implied by their selection, though not explicitly defended beyond its widespread use in the research community at the time).

- **Metrics.** Two categories of metrics are reported: (1) **Memory usage** in gigabytes (GB), measured as peak GPU memory allocated during training (Table 1, Table 2), broken down by component — parameters, gradients, optimizer states, and activations — with and without activation checkpointing; and (2) **Throughput** in tokens per GPU per second (TGS), measuring the number of tokens processed per second averaged across all GPUs (Table 2). For downstream evaluation, the primary metric is **accuracy** — the fraction of test examples where the model's highest-scoring label matches the ground truth, computed by inserting each candidate label into the prompt, calculating the average log-likelihood, and selecting the label with the highest score (Table 3, Figures 3, 4, 5). No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests are reported for any metric.

- **Baselines.** Three baselines are compared across all experiments: (1) **Zero-shot** — the pretrained LLaMA model evaluated on SuperGLUE without any fine-tuning, serving as the lower bound; (2) **LoRA** (Hu et al., 2022) — parameter-efficient fine-tuning using low-rank adaptation with rank `r_q = r_v = 2`, LoRA α = 16, trained with AdamW at learning rate 5×10⁻⁴ and warmup ratio 0.05; and (3) For memory and throughput comparisons, **AdamW** (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019) and **vanilla SGD** (without gradient fusion) serve as optimizer baselines, both using ZeRO-3 parameter partitioning and activation checkpointing where noted. The "standard approach (DeepSpeed solution)" referenced in the abstract as the 100% memory baseline is not explicitly defined in a single table row, but can be inferred as the AdamW-with-activation-checkpointing configuration from Table 1 (102.20 GB), making LOMO's 14.58 GB approximately 14.3% of that baseline — the abstract's 10.8% figure may reference a different baseline configuration including additional DeepSpeed overhead not detailed in the paper.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For memory profiling, the compute unit is peak GPU memory in GB under a fixed configuration: sequence length 512, batch size 8 (Table 1 and Figure 2). For throughput, the unit is tokens per GPU per second under sequence length 1024 and batch size 1 (Table 2), with parameter partitioning via ZeRO-3 for multi-GPU configurations. For downstream evaluation, the compute budget is not explicitly controlled as a dependent variable — each method is run with its own optimal hyperparameters (learning rate, warmup ratio) swept over a predefined range (Table 4) and trained for 10 epochs on 1,000 examples with batch size 16. The paper reports the best result from hyperparameter sweeps without controlling for total FLOPs or wall-clock time across methods, making the performance comparison between LOMO and LoRA primarily one of final accuracy rather than accuracy-per-compute-dollar.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper follows MeZO's evaluation protocol: 1,000 training examples and 1,000 validation examples are randomly sampled from each SuperGLUE task, and results are reported for a single random seed. The paper states: "we report the highest results of experiments conducted with the same random seed" (Appendix A). This is a best-of-N selection over hyperparameters (learning rate, warmup ratio) rather than a cross-validated estimate — for each configuration, one training run is performed and the best validation accuracy across all sweeps is reported. There is no held-out test set separate from the validation set; the 1,000 sampled validation examples serve as both the development and test set. No multiple-seed averaging, bootstrap confidence intervals, or statistical significance testing is performed.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Memory Profile: LOMO Reduces Training Memory by 7× Compared to AdamW and 3.5× Compared to SGD

Table 1 presents the core memory breakdown for training LLaMA-7B under six configurations (three optimizers × two activation checkpointing settings). All numbers are in GB with sequence length 512 and batch size 8.

**Without activation checkpointing:**

| Setting | Params | Gradients | Optim States | Activations | **Total** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AdamW | 12.55 | 12.55 | 75.31 | 45.61 | **147.02** |
| SGD | 12.55 | 12.55 | 25.10 | 45.61 | **96.81** |
| LOMO | 12.55 | 0.24 | 0.00 | 45.61 | **59.40** |

**With activation checkpointing:**

| Setting | Params | Gradients | Optim States | Activations | **Total** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AdamW | 12.55 | 12.55 | 75.31 | 1.79 | **102.20** |
| SGD | 12.55 | 12.55 | 25.10 | 1.79 | **51.99** |
| LOMO | 12.55 | 0.24 | 0.00 | 1.79 | **14.58** |

The headline comparisons: LOMO reduces total memory from 102.20 GB (AdamW + activation checkpointing, representing the standard DeepSpeed approach) to 14.58 GB — an 85.7% reduction. Compared to SGD with activation checkpointing, LOMO reduces memory from 51.99 GB to 14.58 GB — a 72.0% reduction. The ratio 14.58/102.20 = 14.3% broadly aligns with the abstract's claim of 10.8%, with the discrepancy likely explained by additional overhead in the full DeepSpeed configuration not captured in the simplified Table 1 breakdown.

**Component-by-component analysis of Table 1 and Figure 2:**

- **Optimizer states:** The most dramatic savings. AdamW stores 75.31 GB of optimizer states (FP32 momentum and variance buffers, plus an FP32 master copy of weights for mixed-precision updates). Switching to vanilla SGD reduces this to 25.10 GB — the FP32 master copy of weights remains, but momentum and variance buffers are eliminated. LOMO eliminates optimizer states completely (0.00 GB) because the fused update uses the FP16 gradient directly for the in-place parameter modification, with no persistent FP32 state. Figure 2(a) shows optimizer states as 73.7% of total memory for AdamW; Figure 2(c) shows they have vanished entirely for LOMO.

- **Gradients:** Standard training stores gradient tensors for all parameters simultaneously (12.55 GB for LLaMA-7B). LOMO reduces this to 0.24 GB — an ~52× reduction. The 0.24 GB represents the gradient tensor for the largest single parameter matrix in the model. For LLaMA-7B, this is a feed-forward network weight with shape approximately [4096, 11008] in FP16, requiring `4096 × 11008 × 2 bytes ≈ 90 MB` — the paper's 0.24 GB ($0.24 × 1024^3 ≈ 257$ MB) is larger than this single matrix, suggesting the largest layer may contain multiple parameter tensors (e.g., `W_1`, `W_2`, and `W_3` in a gated FFN) whose combined gradient memory accounts for the 0.24 GB figure.

- **Activations:** Constant across optimizers at 45.61 GB without checkpointing and 1.79 GB with checkpointing. The ~25× reduction from activation checkpointing confirms that activations dominate memory in the forward pass, but the technique is orthogonal to the optimizer choice. The fact that LOMO + activation checkpointing yields a total memory (14.58 GB) where parameters (12.55 GB, 86.1%) dominate — with activations at 1.79 GB (12.3%) and gradients at 0.24 GB (1.6%) — demonstrates the paper's claim that training memory approaches inference memory. Inference requires storing the parameters (12.55 GB) and the activations for the current token (much less than 1.79 GB for a full batch), so the 14.58 GB is indeed close to the theoretical minimum for training with this batch size.

**Implicit in Table 1 but not separately reported:** the memory cost of the two-pass backward strategy for gradient normalization and loss scaling. The second backward pass recomputes activations (reusing the checkpointing infrastructure) and does not store gradients persistently, so its incremental memory cost should be near zero — the same 14.58 GB suffices. However, the paper does not explicitly verify this or report peak memory during the two-pass procedure.

#### Throughput: LOMO Achieves 11× Higher Throughput than AdamW for 7B Models by Eliminating Inter-GPU Communication

Table 2 reports throughput (tokens per GPU per second, TGS) and per-GPU peak memory for LLaMA models from 7B to 65B, all with sequence length 1024 and batch size 1.

**7B model comparisons:**

| Optimizer | GPUs Used | Memory/GPU (GB) | Throughput (TGS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| AdamW | 8 × RTX 3090 | 15.76 | 67.37 |
| SGD | 8 × RTX 3090 | 9.49 | 69.66 |
| **LOMO** | **1 × RTX 3090** | **13.61** | **769.92** |

LOMO achieves 769.92 TGS — approximately 11.4× the throughput of AdamW (67.37 TGS) and 11.1× that of SGD (69.66 TGS) — while using only a single GPU instead of eight. The paper attributes this to the elimination of inter-GPU communication: AdamW and SGD both require ZeRO-3 parameter partitioning across 8 GPUs, incurring all-reduce communication during both forward and backward passes. LOMO fits the entire 7B model on one GPU, so no communication is needed. The slightly higher throughput of SGD over AdamW (69.66 vs. 67.37 TGS) is attributed to SGD not computing momentum and variance updates, a minor computational saving.

The per-GPU memory for LOMO (13.61 GB) is higher than the 14.58 GB reported in Table 1 for the full-training configuration, but Table 2 uses sequence length 1024 (vs. 512 in Table 1) and batch size 1 (vs. 8), so the activation memory differs. With batch size 1 and sequence length 1024, the total tokens per batch is 1024 vs. 4096 for Table 1 (8 × 512), reducing activation memory proportionally.

**13B model comparisons:**

| Optimizer | GPUs Used | Memory/GPU (GB) | Throughput (TGS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| SGD | 8 × RTX 3090 | 15.74 | 32.51 |
| **LOMO** | **2 × RTX 3090** | **15.92** | **66.19** |

AdamW cannot train the 13B model on 8 × RTX 3090 due to out-of-memory — the optimizer states alone would require approximately 2 × 13B × 4 bytes = 104 GB for momentum and variance in FP32, exceeding the aggregate 8 × 24 = 192 GB when combined with parameters, gradients, and activations under ZeRO-3. LOMO succeeds with only 2 GPUs, achieving 66.19 TGS (2.0× SGD's 32.51 TGS on 8 GPUs). The per-GPU memory is comparable (15.92 GB for LOMO vs. 15.74 GB for SGD), but LOMO uses only 2 GPUs vs. 8, reducing communication costs.

**30B model:**

| Optimizer | GPUs Used | Memory/GPU (GB) | Throughput (TGS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LOMO** | **4 × RTX 3090** | **19.78** | **11.61** |

SGD encounters OOM on 8 × RTX 3090 for the 30B model — the FP32 master copy of weights (30B × 4 bytes = 120 GB) plus gradients (30B × 2 bytes = 60 GB) plus parameters (60 GB FP16) total 240 GB before activations, exceeding 192 GB aggregate. LOMO trains on 4 GPUs with 19.78 GB per GPU.

**65B model:**

| Optimizer | GPUs Used | Memory/GPU (GB) | Throughput (TGS) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LOMO** | **8 × RTX 3090** | **19.18** | **4.93** |

This is the headline result: full-parameter fine-tuning of a 65B model on 8 consumer GPUs. Per-GPU memory is 19.18 GB, fitting within the 24 GB available. At 4.93 TGS with sequence length 1024, the paper notes that training on 1,000 samples of 512 tokens each (the SuperGLUE configuration) requires approximately 3.6 hours: 1,000 samples × 512 tokens / (4.93 TGS × 8 GPUs) ≈ 3.6 hours. This is a practically usable training time for a moderately sized fine-tuning dataset, not merely a proof-of-concept.

**What Table 2 does NOT report:** The AdamW or SGD throughput for 30B and 65B models at any batch size, because they OOM. The two-pass backward overhead for LOMO when gradient normalization is used — Table 2 likely reports throughput for the single-pass configuration without gradient norm clipping, but this is not explicitly stated. The throughput impact of activation checkpointing (which adds one extra forward pass) is not isolated.

#### Combining LOMO with LoRA: Complementary Rather than Competitive

Figure 3 evaluates LLaMA-13B on BoolQ and MultiRC, comparing LoRA alone against LoRA+LOMO (where LoRA modules are trained with AdamW while the full model weights are simultaneously trained with LOMO's SGD updates), across varying LoRA attention dimensions (ranks 1, 2, 4, 8, plus a "w/o LoRA" baseline of pure LOMO).

**BoolQ results (Figure 3a):** LoRA+LOMO consistently outperforms LoRA alone at every rank. At rank 2, LoRA achieves approximately 87.1% while LoRA+LOMO achieves approximately 87.5%. At rank 8, LoRA reaches ~87.5% and LoRA+LOMO reaches ~88.1%. The gap is modest (0.4–0.6 points) but consistent. Without LoRA (pure LOMO), accuracy is ~87.7% — higher than LoRA alone at most ranks and competitive with LoRA+LOMO. The key message: adding LoRA to LOMO does not hurt and can help slightly, and vice versa — LOMO does not prevent LoRA from contributing.

**MultiRC results (Figure 3b):** A similar pattern. Without LoRA, pure LOMO achieves ~85.5%. LoRA alone varies from ~86.1% (rank 1) to ~86.7% (rank 8). LoRA+LOMO tracks 0.1–0.3 points above LoRA alone at most ranks but with more variability. At rank 4, LoRA+LOMO reaches ~86.7% vs. ~86.3% for LoRA alone; at rank 8, both methods converge around 86.7%. The performance is largely rank-insensitive for both methods in this range.

**What Figure 3 demonstrates:** LOMO and LoRA are "fundamentally independent" as the paper claims (Section 4.3.2) — combining them does not cause interference or degradation. The full-parameter updates from LOMO and the low-rank adapter updates from LoRA operate on complementary subspaces of the model's parameter space. This means LOMO can be used to achieve the benefits of full-parameter fine-tuning while LoRA adds additional task-specific capacity, without memory conflict because LOMO's memory savings come from the optimizer, not from freezing parameters.

**What Figure 3 does NOT show:** The memory cost of LoRA+LOMO combined — LoRA modules require gradient storage for the adapter parameters plus AdamW optimizer states for those parameters. Since the adapter parameter count is tiny (rank × model dimension × number of adapted matrices), this additional memory is negligible relative to the full model weights. The figure also does not compare against other PEFT methods (prefix-tuning, prompt tuning, adapters) combined with LOMO, which would establish whether the complementarity is specific to LoRA or general.

#### Training Dynamics: LOMO Converges Smoothly and Stably

Appendix B presents training loss curves (Figure 4) and validation accuracy curves (Figure 5) for LLaMA-7B fine-tuned on BoolQ with LOMO and LoRA. These figures are shown for a single training run (the best configuration from hyperparameter sweeps), not averaged across seeds.

**Training loss (Figure 4):** The loss curves for both LOMO and LoRA exhibit qualitatively similar behavior. LOMO's loss drops rapidly in the first ~100 steps from approximately 6.0 to below 2.0, then stabilizes and gradually declines to a final value near 0 at step 600. LoRA follows a similar trajectory. This rapid initial convergence followed by slow refinement is typical of fine-tuning (the model quickly adapts to the new task format and then makes incremental improvements) and shows no sign of the instability or divergence that would indicate SGD failure. The paper states: "During training process with LOMO, the loss converges rapidly in the initial phase and then tends to stabilize and gradually decline."

**Validation accuracy (Figure 5):** Both methods show generally upward trends with training steps, though with visible fluctuations. LoRA appears slightly more stable (narrower oscillations) while LOMO shows larger swings, especially between steps 300–500 where accuracy dips from ~0.86 to ~0.82 and recovers. Both methods end near 0.87–0.88 at step 600. The paper does not comment on whether LOMO's larger fluctuations reflect genuine optimization noise from SGD (vs. Adam's smoother adaptive updates) or are within the range of run-to-run variation.

**Absent from these training dynamics:** Validation loss curves — accuracy alone shows whether the model's predictions are correct but not how calibrated its confidence is. Multi-seed training runs — the single-seed reporting means we cannot distinguish LOMO-specific instability from seed-specific variance. Overfitting diagnostics — with 1,000 training examples and batch size 16 (approximately 62–63 updates per epoch), 10 epochs produce ~625 updates, but the paper does not report whether validation accuracy saturates earlier (suggesting wasted computation) or continues improving (suggesting more epochs could help).

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**LoRA attention dimension (rank) sweep**: Figure 3 serves as an implicit ablation on LoRA rank (1, 2, 4, 8) for both BoolQ and MultiRC on LLaMA-13B. The finding is that performance is relatively insensitive to rank in the range 1–8 for both LoRA alone and LoRA+LOMO — all configurations produce accuracies within a ~1–2 point band. This suggests that even rank-1 adapters can capture much of the task-specific signal, and that LOMO's full-parameter updates are doing substantial work independently of the LoRA rank. However, the narrow rank range (1–8, vs. common practice of testing up to 64 or 256) limits the generality of this observation.

**Implicit optimizer ablation (SGD vs. AdamW for fine-tuning):** While not presented as a standalone table, the comparison between standard SGD (without fusion) and AdamW in Table 1 and Table 2 shows that SGD alone reduces memory from 102.20 GB to 51.99 GB (7B, with activation checkpointing) — a ~50% reduction by eliminating momentum and variance buffers. Table 3 does not include a standalone SGD baseline (without LOMO fusion) for downstream performance, so we cannot assess whether SGD's performance matches LOMO or whether the fusion mechanism itself introduces any performance degradation beyond what SGD would achieve with standard gradient storage. This is a notable missing ablation: the paper's claim that LOMO "does not harm the fine-tuning process" would be stronger if it showed that LOMO matches standard SGD accuracy, not just that it matches LoRA.

**Implicit batch size ablation (via the theoretical derivation):** Section 3.1.2 provides a mathematical argument that sequential per-sample updates approximate larger-batch SGD, but this is never empirically validated. No experiment varies the batch size to measure whether LOMO's effective batch size (as measured by gradient variance or convergence rate) matches the theoretical prediction. No experiment compares LOMO with small micro-batches against standard SGD with larger batches to confirm the equivalence. The derivation remains theoretical motivation rather than an experimentally tested mechanism.

**Two-pass vs. single-pass gradient clipping (not empirically compared):** Section 3.3.1 proposes three alternatives for gradient normalization — clipping by value, two-pass norm computation, and grouped approximation — but the paper states the experiments use "Max Grad Norm: 1.0" (Table 4) without specifying which method was used for which experiments. The throughput measurements in Table 2 are presumably for the single-pass configuration (since the two-pass method would cut throughput roughly in half for the backward pass), but this is not stated. There is no ablation comparing downstream performance with value clipping vs. norm clipping vs. no clipping, which would quantify the importance of proper gradient normalization for LOMO's stability.

**FP32 conversion for updates (not ablated):** Section 3.3.2 mentions that "the gradient and its associated parameter are converted to full precision within these computations" for numerically sensitive operations like normalization and scaling. The paper does not ablate whether this FP32 conversion is necessary — i.e., whether using pure FP16 for the SGD update step degrades accuracy due to precision loss. Given that mixed-precision training typically requires an FP32 master copy of weights for update accumulation, LOMO's elimination of this master copy is surprising and warrants experimental validation that FP16-in-place updates are numerically safe. The paper provides no such validation.

**Learning rate sensitivity (swept but not analyzed across tasks):** Table 4 shows LOMO learning rates swept as `{5e-2, 3e-2}` — only two values. The paper does not report which learning rate was optimal for which task or model size, nor does it show learning rate sensitivity curves. Given that SGD is generally more sensitive to learning rate choice than Adam (which normalizes updates per-parameter), understanding this sensitivity is important for practical adoption. The warmup ratio is swept across `{0.05, 0.1, 0.2}`, but again the optimal values per task are not reported.

**Sequence length and batch size sensitivity (not explored):** All memory profiling uses batch size 8 and sequence length 512 (Table 1); throughput uses batch size 1 and sequence length 1024 (Table 2). The paper does not investigate how LOMO's memory advantage scales with sequence length (which affects activation memory quadratically for self-attention) or batch size (which affects activation memory linearly). For long-sequence fine-tuning tasks (e.g., summarization, document QA), activation memory could dominate even with checkpointing, and LOMO's gradient/optimizer savings would be a smaller fraction of total memory.

**Model architecture sensitivity (single architecture tested):** All experiments use LLaMA, which has a standard decoder-only transformer architecture with SwiGLU activations in the feed-forward layers, rotary position embeddings, and pre-layer normalization. The paper does not test LOMO on encoder-decoder models (T5), mixture-of-experts architectures, or models with different normalization schemes (post-LN), so it is unknown whether the smooth loss surface property that enables SGD fine-tuning generalizes across architectures.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does LOMO Enable Full-Parameter Fine-Tuning of 65B Models on Consumer GPUs? Yes, With an Important Caveat.

The headline claim — "our approach enables the full parameter fine-tuning of a 65B model on a single machine with 8×RTX 3090, each with 24GB memory" — is directly supported by Table 2, which shows 19.18 GB peak memory per GPU for the 65B LLaMA model trained with LOMO on 8 RTX 3090s. This is a genuine engineering achievement. The arithmetic is unambiguous: a 65B model in FP16 requires ~130 GB just for parameters, and with standard AdamW training the optimizer states alone would demand ~260 GB (FP32 momentum + variance), exceeding the aggregate 192 GB of 8×24GB GPUs by a wide margin even before accounting for gradients and activations. LOMO eliminates the optimizer states and reduces gradient memory to the size of the largest layer (~0.24 GB for 7B, presumably ~1–2 GB for 65B), making the problem tractable.

**The caveat:** The 65B result is demonstrated only for throughput measurement, not for downstream task performance. Table 2 reports 4.93 TGS for the 65B model, confirming that training runs without OOM, but Table 3's 65B SuperGLUE results do not specify the hardware configuration used — they could have been run on the same 8×3090 server (as the abstract implies) or on a higher-memory configuration. The paper states "all our experiments are conducted with LLaMA models, ranging from 7B to 65B" (Section 4 opening), implying the 65B downstream results in Table 3 used the same hardware as the throughput tests, but this is not explicitly confirmed. If the 65B SuperGLUE results required different hardware, the democratization claim weakens.

Additionally, training on 1,000 examples with sequence length 512 takes ~3.6 hours (as calculated in Section 4.2), which is for a single task × single seed × single hyperparameter configuration. A full hyperparameter sweep across 3 learning rates, 3 warmup ratios, and 6 tasks could require ~3.6 × 9 = ~32.4 hours per task for 65B — manageable but not trivial. Multi-seed evaluation would multiply this further. The throughput numbers position LOMO as practical but not effortless.

#### Does LOMO Match or Exceed LoRA's Downstream Performance? Yes, but the Comparison Is Incomplete.

Table 3 demonstrates that LOMO's average SuperGLUE accuracy meets or exceeds LoRA at all four model scales (80.8% vs. 78.8% at 7B; 84.2% vs. 81.4% at 13B; 87.1% vs. 86.4% at 30B; 89.9% vs. 89.0% at 65B). This supports the claim that full-parameter fine-tuning via LOMO is at least as effective as the leading parameter-efficient method for these tasks.

**What is missing from this comparison:**

1. **No comparison against full-parameter fine-tuning with AdamW.** The paper's argument is that full-parameter fine-tuning is "more powerful" than parameter-efficient fine-tuning (citing Ding et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023), and LOMO is proposed as a way to achieve full-parameter fine-tuning under resource constraints. But the paper never actually compares LOMO (full-parameter, SGD) against full-parameter fine-tuning with AdamW — the gold standard it claims to approximate. If AdamW full-parameter fine-tuning achieves, say, 85% on 7B while LOMO achieves 80.8%, then LOMO is sacrificing significant performance for memory savings. The paper cannot make this comparison because AdamW full-parameter fine-tuning of 7B requires 102.20 GB (Table 1), exceeding the available hardware. But this means we cannot assess the **performance cost of switching from Adam to SGD**, only the **performance comparison against LoRA** — which is a different axis (full vs. parameter-efficient, not Adam vs. SGD). A 7B model can be fully fine-tuned with AdamW on 4× A100 80GB GPUs, so this comparison is feasible on non-consumer hardware and would substantially strengthen the paper's claims.

2. **No comparison against other memory-efficient full-parameter methods.** MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) and GaLore (Zhao et al., 2024) both aim to reduce memory for full-parameter fine-tuning and are cited in the related work. Neither is included as a baseline in Table 3. MeZO's SuperGLUE results are available in the original paper (using the same 1,000-example sampling protocol that this paper follows), making the omission puzzling. A direct LOMO-vs-MeZO comparison would anchor LOMO's performance relative to the most directly comparable prior work.

3. **Single-seed, best-of-sweep reporting.** The paper "report[s] the best results obtained using the same random seed" (Section 4.3) and sweeps hyperparameters (Table 4). This means the reported numbers are maxima over hyperparameter configurations for a single data split and single random seed. Without multiple seeds, we cannot assess whether LOMO's advantages over LoRA (e.g., +2.8 points at 13B) are statistically reliable or within the range of seed-to-seed variation. Given that LoRA and LOMO differ by only 0.7–2.8 points on average across model sizes, seed variance could plausibly account for the gaps. Standard practice in the fine-tuning literature is to report mean and standard deviation across 3–5 random seeds; the paper's deviation from this practice weakens the performance claims.

4. **No compute-matched comparison.** LOMO uses two backward passes when gradient normalization is enabled (Section 3.3.1), which approximately doubles the backward computation time. LoRA uses a single backward pass with AdamW. If LOMO's two-pass training takes 1.5–2× longer per step than LoRA, a fair comparison would either control for wall-clock time (train LoRA for more epochs) or report throughput alongside accuracy. Table 3 does neither. The throughput advantage in Table 2 (11× for 7B) applies to the single-pass configuration without gradient normalization and without the overhead of activation checkpointing's extra forward pass — the actual throughput during the SuperGLUE experiments (which use gradient norm clipping with max norm 1.0 per Table 4, implying the two-pass method) is unknown.

#### Is SGD Sufficient for LLM Fine-Tuning? The Evidence Is Suggestive but Not Conclusive.

The paper's theoretical argument (Section 3.1) claims that the three classical challenges of SGD — large curvature, local optima, saddle points — are attenuated for LLM fine-tuning. The empirical evidence in Table 3 shows that a model trained with SGD (via LOMO) achieves competitive or superior accuracy compared to a model trained with AdamW (via LoRA). However, this is **not the right comparison for testing the sufficiency of SGD**.

LoRA uses AdamW but trains only a tiny fraction of parameters (low-rank adapters), while LOMO uses SGD but trains all parameters. If LOMO outperforms LoRA, that could be because (a) SGD is sufficient and full-parameter tuning beats parameter-efficient tuning, or (b) full-parameter tuning beats parameter-efficient tuning regardless of optimizer, and SGD's deficiencies are masked by the advantage of training more parameters. Without a full-parameter AdamW baseline, we cannot distinguish these explanations.

The paper's claim that "SGD can successfully fine-tune the full parameters of LLMs" (abstract contribution bullet) is supported only in the weak sense that the resulting model performs well on downstream tasks — not in the strong sense that SGD performs as well as AdamW would on the same task with the same parameter budget. A convincing demonstration would require, at minimum, a smaller-scale experiment (e.g., LLaMA-1B or a distilled model) where both SGD and AdamW can run full-parameter fine-tuning without memory constraints, comparing their final accuracy and convergence speed directly. The absence of such an experiment leaves the central theoretical claim empirically underdetermined.

#### Does the Difficulty Estimation or Implicit Batch Size Mechanism Matter in Practice? These Are Theoretical Contributions, Not Empirically Tested Ones.

The Section 3.1.2 derivation showing that sequential updates approximate larger-batch SGD is elegant and provides theoretical motivation for why LOMO's fused updates should not destabilize training. But no experiment in the paper tests this mechanism. There is no measurement of effective batch size (e.g., by comparing gradient variance between LOMO and standard SGD at different batch sizes), no ablation where the degree of sequentiality is varied (e.g., updating every 2 layers instead of every layer), and no demonstration that the implicit batch size effect becomes stronger with model scale (as the smoothness argument predicts). The derivation stands as a plausibility argument, not a validated mechanism.

Similarly, the difficulty estimation argument in Section 3.1.1 (smooth loss surface, good-enough local optima, distant saddle points) receives no direct empirical support. There are no loss landscape visualizations, no Hessian eigenvalue analyses, no measurements of weight change magnitude during fine-tuning, and no comparison of loss surface curvature between small and large LLaMA models. The arguments are qualitative and rely on citations to prior work (Hao et al., 2019) rather than new measurements on the specific models and tasks used in this paper.

This does not invalidate the paper's practical contribution — LOMO demonstrably saves memory and achieves good downstream performance — but it means the **theoretical framing is largely post-hoc justification** rather than empirically grounded explanation. The paper would be stronger if it acknowledged this explicitly and positioned the theoretical sections as hypotheses that are consistent with the results rather than as validated mechanisms.

#### Scale and Scope Limitations

**All experiments are on SuperGLUE classification tasks with 1,000 training examples.** These are discriminative tasks where the model scores pre-defined label strings and selects the highest-scoring option. This is a narrow slice of the fine-tuning landscape. Generative tasks (summarization, instruction following, dialogue) require autoregressive token generation and use a different loss function (next-token prediction rather than sequence scoring). It is unknown whether LOMO's SGD approach works for generative fine-tuning, where the loss surface may be less smooth (the model must learn to produce entirely new output formats, not just discriminate between existing ones) and the training dynamics differ (teacher forcing creates a different gradient signal than scoring fixed candidate strings).

**The paper tests only one model family (LLaMA).** LLaMA uses specific architectural choices — pre-layer normalization, SwiGLU activations, rotary position embeddings — that may contribute to loss surface smoothness. Models with post-layer normalization (GPT-2 style), different activation functions, or different position encoding schemes might exhibit different optimization characteristics. The claim that SGD is sufficient for LLM fine-tuning in general cannot be extrapolated from a single architecture.

**The evaluation protocol (1,000 random samples, single seed) limits statistical reliability.** The SuperGLUE tasks have varying sizes — RTE has 2,490 training examples, BoolQ has 9,427, COPA has only 400 — so 1,000 samples represents different fractions of the available data. For smaller tasks like COPA, the 1,000 training samples may include the entire training set, making the "sampling" a shuffle rather than a subset. For larger tasks, different random samples of 1,000 examples could yield meaningfully different results. The single-seed reporting means we cannot assess this variance.

**The 10.8% memory claim is difficult to verify from the paper alone.** Table 1's baseline (AdamW + activation checkpointing = 102.20 GB) and LOMO (14.58 GB) give 14.3%, not 10.8%. The abstract's 10.8% likely references a different baseline — possibly the full DeepSpeed configuration including ZeRO-3, mixed-precision, and activation checkpointing applied to the 65B model rather than 7B, or including additional memory overhead from communication buffers that Table 1 does not account for. Without a clear specification of what the "standard approach (DeepSpeed solution)" baseline actually measures, the 10.8% figure is not independently reproducible from the paper's reported data.

#### What Experiments Would Strengthen the Paper

Several experiments could substantially bolster the paper's claims without requiring hardware beyond what was used:

1. **A full-parameter AdamW baseline on a model small enough to fit.** Fine-tune LLaMA-1B (or a distilled 350M-parameter model) on SuperGLUE with both AdamW (full-parameter) and LOMO (full-parameter, SGD), comparing final accuracy and convergence speed. This would directly test whether SGD sacrifices optimization quality relative to AdamW when training the same number of parameters.

2. **Multi-seed evaluation with confidence intervals.** Run LOMO and LoRA with 3–5 random seeds on a subset of tasks and report means ± standard deviations. This is standard practice and would allow readers to assess whether the observed performance differences are statistically meaningful.

3. **Ablation of gradient normalization method.** Train LOMO with value clipping, two-pass norm clipping, and no clipping on a single task, reporting both accuracy and training stability (presence of loss spikes). This would quantify the practical importance of the two-pass strategy and potentially show that value clipping suffices (avoiding the throughput penalty).

4. **Generative fine-tuning experiment.** Fine-tune LLaMA-7B on a small instruction-following or summarization dataset using LOMO and compare against LoRA on generation metrics (ROUGE, human preference). This would extend the scope beyond classification and test whether the smooth-loss-surface assumption holds for generative objectives.

5. **Memory scaling with sequence length.** Profile LOMO memory at sequence lengths 512, 1024, 2048, and 4096 to show how the fraction of memory saved changes as activation memory grows. This is important for practitioners working with long-context tasks.

6. **Comparison with MeZO on the same SuperGLUE protocol.** Since the paper follows MeZO's evaluation setup (1,000 training examples, same prompts), a direct accuracy comparison would position LOMO relative to the most prominent memory-efficient fine-tuning method that also avoids backpropagation memory — and would test whether exact gradients (LOMO) outperform estimated gradients (MeZO) at comparable memory budgets.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For — And Potentially Dominates the Savings

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's decision to use SGD rather than AdamW rests on a theoretical argument that the loss surface of large language models is smooth during fine-tuning (Section 3.1.1), making SGD's historical pathologies — large curvature, local optima, saddle points — less severe. The paper provides a formal derivation showing that sequential per-sample SGD updates approximate larger-batch SGD under smoothness (Section 3.1.2). However, the paper never directly measures or validates this smoothness assumption on the specific models and tasks it uses. The arguments rely on citations to prior work (Hao et al., 2019, which analyzed BERT's loss landscape) and qualitative geometric intuitions about valleys and saddle points, not on new measurements of LLaMA models — no Hessian eigenvalue spectra, no loss landscape visualizations, no curvature measurements at the fine-tuning initialization, and no comparison of loss surface characteristics between different model sizes to verify the claim that "larger models have a smoother loss surface." The paper's central theoretical claim — that SGD is sufficient for LLM fine-tuning because the optimization landscape is benign — is presented as motivating intuition rather than as an empirically grounded finding.

**The consequence.** If the smoothness assumption does not hold for a particular model, task, or fine-tuning regime, the paper provides no diagnostic for detecting this failure mode and no fallback strategy. A practitioner who adopts LOMO based on the paper's argument and finds that training diverges or underperforms has no guidance on whether the problem is: (a) an insufficiently smooth loss surface (violating the core assumption), (b) an inappropriate learning rate (SGD's well-known sensitivity), (c) a task that genuinely requires Adam's adaptive per-parameter scaling, or (d) an implementation bug in the hook injection or two-pass backward strategy. More critically, the smoothness argument imposes a boundary condition that the paper acknowledges but does not test: "This holds only when we teach the LLMs natural language-based tasks (or code-based if pre-trained with code). A synthetic loss function unrelated to pre-training tasks will indeed face the large curvature problem" (Section 3.1.1). This means LOMO's theoretical foundation may not extend to fine-tuning on structured prediction tasks with non-standard loss functions (e.g., ranking losses, contrastive objectives, RL-based fine-tuning with a reward model), multi-modal tasks where the loss combines text and image objectives, or tasks where the fine-tuning data distribution differs substantially from pretraining — all of which are common in contemporary LLM applications. The paper also does not address whether continued pretraining (domain adaptation on large corpora, which involves more data and larger weight changes than the 1,000-example SuperGLUE tasks) satisfies the smoothness assumption, or whether the larger parameter movements involved in domain adaptation push the model far enough from the pretrained initialization to encounter the curvature and saddle-point problems that SGD historically struggles with.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's evidence that SGD works in practice is entirely performance-based: Table 3 shows that LOMO (SGD) matches or exceeds LoRA (AdamW) on SuperGLUE tasks across model sizes. The training loss curves in Figure 4 (Appendix B) show smooth convergence for LOMO on BoolQ with LLaMA-7B, with no loss spikes or divergence. This is suggestive that SGD is stable for these specific configurations, but it does not validate the smoothness mechanism — it could be that the learning rate was well-tuned, that the task is intrinsically easy to optimize, or that full-parameter tuning's advantage over LoRA's low-rank bottleneck outweighs any optimizer deficiency. The paper provides no comparison between LOMO (SGD, full-parameter) and AdamW (full-parameter) on any model size — the one experiment that would directly test whether SGD sacrifices optimization quality relative to Adam when training the same parameters. As noted in the prior analysis, this comparison is feasible on a smaller model (e.g., a 1B-parameter model where AdamW's optimizer states fit in available memory) but is absent from the paper. Without it, the claim that "SGD can successfully fine-tune the full parameters of LLMs" (abstract) is supported in the weak sense that the resulting model achieves good accuracy, not in the strong sense that SGD recovers the performance AdamW would achieve.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to empirically validate the smoothness assumption or provide diagnostics. It presents the theoretical derivation (Section 3.1.2) as justification and the downstream results (Table 3) as evidence, but acknowledges implicitly that the argument is qualitative by hedging with phrases like "If we believe that larger models have a smoother loss surface" and "our intuition is to create a simple and practical solution for fine-tuning LLMs and identify its flaws to continually improve it" (Section 3.1.1). The paper does not suggest future work to measure loss surface properties of LLMs or to characterize when SGD fails for fine-tuning.

### The SuperGLUE Evaluation Is Too Narrow to Establish General-Purpose Viability

**The assumption or constraint.** All downstream performance evaluations (Table 3, Figures 3–5) use the SuperGLUE benchmark with six classification-style tasks (RTE, BoolQ, WSC, WIC, MultiRC, COPA) under a specific evaluation protocol: 1,000 randomly sampled training examples, 1,000 randomly sampled validation examples, scoring pre-defined label strings by average log-likelihood, and selecting the highest-scoring label as the prediction. The paper follows this protocol from MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) and uses the same prompt templates. This experimental design evaluates LOMO exclusively on **discriminative fine-tuning** — the model learns to score fixed candidate strings rather than generate free-form text. The model never produces autoregressive outputs during either training or evaluation.

**The consequence.** This narrow evaluation scope leaves the paper's central claim — that LOMO enables full-parameter fine-tuning of LLMs under resource constraints — unvalidated for the most practically important fine-tuning paradigm in 2023–2024: **generative fine-tuning** for instruction following, dialogue, summarization, and other tasks where the model must produce novel text rather than score pre-defined options. The distinction matters for several reasons. First, the loss function differs: discriminative fine-tuning uses sequence-level log-likelihood scoring of a few candidate strings, while generative fine-tuning uses token-level next-token prediction across potentially long target sequences. The gradient signal for token-level generation is denser (a gradient at every output token) and operates over a much larger output space (the entire vocabulary at each step rather than 2–5 candidate strings). It is unknown whether the smooth loss surface argument (Section 3.1.1) applies to token-level cross-entropy loss over long sequences, or whether the larger effective batch size from sequential updates (Section 3.1.2) behaves similarly when each example contributes hundreds of token-level gradients rather than a single sequence-level gradient. Second, generative fine-tuning typically involves larger weight changes than discriminative fine-tuning because the model must learn new output formats and styles in addition to task-specific knowledge. Larger weight changes increase the discrepancy term in the sequential-vs-batched derivation (Equation 6), potentially invalidating the approximation that LOMO relies on. Third, generative fine-tuning datasets are often larger than the 1,000 examples used here — instruction-tuning datasets routinely contain tens or hundreds of thousands of examples. LOMO's training dynamics over many epochs on larger datasets (in terms of both optimization stability and the two-pass backward overhead for gradient normalization) are unexplored.

Furthermore, even within the discriminative setting, the SuperGLUE tasks represent a narrow slice of language understanding — they test textual entailment, question answering, coreference resolution, and commonsense reasoning, but not other discriminative paradigms like multiple-choice reasoning over long contexts, retrieval-based QA, or structured prediction. The paper does not demonstrate that LOMO's performance advantage over LoRA (+0.7 to +2.8 points on average across model sizes) generalizes beyond these six tasks. The MultiRC task provides a cautionary signal: it is the only task where LoRA consistently matches or slightly outperforms LOMO at multiple model scales (7B: 84.8% LoRA vs. 84.0% LOMO; 13B: 86.1% vs. 85.7%; 65B: 90.0% vs. 89.9%). The paper does not analyze why this task is different, but it suggests that full-parameter tuning with SGD is not universally superior to parameter-efficient tuning with AdamW — there exist tasks where the optimizer choice (SGD vs. AdamW) matters more than the parameter budget (full vs. low-rank).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 reports accuracy on all six SuperGLUE tasks for four model sizes across three methods, providing within-distribution evidence that LOMO works for this specific task family. Appendix B (Figures 4–5) provides training dynamics for BoolQ only. The paper does not conduct any generative fine-tuning experiments, does not evaluate on generation metrics (ROUGE, BLEU, human evaluation), does not test instruction-following or dialogue tasks, and does not benchmark on larger fine-tuning datasets. The statement that "our experiments were limited to a subset of the SuperGLUE benchmark" in the Limitations section of the paper acknowledges this scope limitation explicitly.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges in its Limitations section: "Due to time and resource constraints, our experiments were limited to a subset of the SuperGLUE benchmark, and we did not evaluate LOMO's throughput on advanced GPUs such as A100." It does not propose specific future work to extend LOMO to generative tasks, nor does it discuss whether the approach would need modification for generative fine-tuning. The framing of LOMO as a general solution for "full parameter fine-tuning for large language models with limited resources" (title) is broader than what the experimental evidence supports.

---

### Performance Evaluation Uses Single-Seed Best-of-Sweep Reporting, Making the Claimed Advantages Statistically Unverifiable

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's experimental protocol for downstream evaluation (Section 4.3 and Appendix A) reports "the highest results of experiments conducted with the same random seed" — meaning that for each method, each task, and each model size, multiple hyperparameter configurations are tried (learning rates in {5e-2, 3e-2}, warmup ratios in {0.05, 0.1, 0.2} for LOMO; learning rate 5e-4 and warmup 0.05 for LoRA; LoRA rank in {1, 2, 4, 8} for LoRA+LOMO), and the single best validation accuracy across all configurations is reported. All experiments use a single random seed for data sampling (1,000 training and 1,000 validation examples randomly drawn from each SuperGLUE task, following MeZO's protocol). There is no averaging across multiple random seeds, no reporting of standard deviations or confidence intervals, and no statistical significance testing between methods. The training loss and validation accuracy curves in Appendix B (Figures 4–5) show a single training run, confirming the single-seed protocol.

**The consequence.** The reported performance differences between LOMO and LoRA — +2.0 points average at 7B, +2.8 at 13B, +0.7 at 30B, +0.9 at 65B (Table 3) — cannot be distinguished from noise arising from two sources: (1) sampling variance in the 1,000 training and validation examples (different random draws from the SuperGLUE datasets could produce different accuracy rankings between methods), and (2) seed variance in model initialization and training dynamics (different random seeds for dropout, data ordering, and other stochastic elements could produce different final accuracies for the same method and hyperparameters). Without multiple seeds, a +2.8 point advantage for LOMO over LoRA at 13B could represent a genuine optimizer effect or could be an artifact of a favorable data split and seed for LOMO combined with an unfavorable split and seed for LoRA.

This concern is amplified by the best-of-sweep reporting. If LOMO is tried with 2 learning rates × 3 warmup ratios = 6 hyperparameter configurations, and LoRA is tried with 1 learning rate × 1 warmup ratio = 1 configuration (or more if rank is varied), then LOMO has more opportunities to find a configuration that performs well on the specific validation set by chance. The paper does not control for the number of hyperparameter trials when comparing methods, so the reported maxima are not comparable in the sense of expected generalization performance — LOMO's best-of-6 will be biased upward relative to its true performance by an amount that depends on the variance across configurations and the number of trials. This phenomenon (the "winner's curse" in model selection) is well-documented in the machine learning literature and is typically addressed by using a held-out test set that is not used for hyperparameter selection, or by cross-validation that estimates the variance of the selection procedure. The paper's protocol — selecting the best hyperparameters on the validation set and reporting that same validation set's accuracy as the final result — conflates model selection and evaluation, producing optimistically biased estimates.

Furthermore, the small validation set size (1,000 examples per task) means that the standard error of the accuracy estimate is approximately `√(p(1-p)/1000)`, where p is the accuracy. For a method achieving 85% accuracy, the standard error is approximately `√(0.85 × 0.15 / 1000) ≈ 1.1%`. A 95% confidence interval would span roughly ±2.2 percentage points — meaning that for most tasks and model sizes, the differences between LOMO and LoRA in Table 3 fall within the range of sampling error alone, before accounting for seed variance or hyperparameter selection bias. The 2.8-point average advantage at 13B could easily be consistent with no true difference between the methods.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no multi-seed results, no standard deviations, no confidence intervals, no statistical tests, and no correction for multiple hyperparameter comparisons. Appendix A states: "Due to limited computational resources, we report the highest results of experiments conducted with the same random seed." This is an explicit admission of the limitation, not an oversight. The training dynamics in Figures 4–5 (Appendix B) show visible fluctuations in validation accuracy during training for a single run — LOMO's accuracy on BoolQ oscillates between ~0.82 and ~0.88 during steps 300–500 (Figure 5) — suggesting that final checkpoint accuracy depends on when training stops, adding another source of variance that single-seed reporting does not capture.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the resource constraint driving this limitation but does not discuss its impact on the reliability of the performance claims. It does not provide a power analysis, does not estimate the variance of its measurements, and does not qualify its conclusions with appropriate uncertainty. The stated future work does not include more rigorous statistical evaluation.

### LOMO Introduces a Latency-Versus-Memory Tradeoff That the Throughput Numbers Obscure

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's stabilization techniques for mixed-precision training with LOMO (Section 3.3) require a **two-pass backward strategy** when gradient norm clipping and dynamic loss scaling are both needed — which they are in the SuperGLUE experiments (Table 4: "Max Grad Norm: 1.0"). The first backward pass computes gradients and the global gradient norm (and checks for FP16 overflow for loss scaling) without updating parameters; the second backward pass recomputes the gradients and applies the fused LOMO updates with the correctly scaled and clipped gradients. This means the backward computation — which is already the most computationally expensive part of training — is executed twice per iteration. The forward pass is still executed once (activations from the first backward pass can be reused if stored, or recomputed via activation checkpointing). The paper acknowledges this cost in Section 3.3.1: "The memory usage leaves unchanged but sacrifices the speed" and in Section 3.3.2: "These two backward passes for dynamic loss scaling can be executed simultaneously with gradient normalization." However, the throughput measurements in Table 2 — which show LOMO achieving 11× higher throughput than AdamW for the 7B model (769.92 vs. 67.37 TGS) — almost certainly do not include the two-pass overhead. The 7B LOMO configuration in Table 2 uses a single GPU with batch size 1 and sequence length 1024, and the throughput figure of 769.92 TGS likely represents the single-pass configuration (no gradient normalization, no loss scaling, or using value clipping which does not require a second backward pass). The paper does not specify which gradient clipping method was used for the throughput measurements.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt LOMO based on the headline throughput numbers would be misled if their use case requires gradient norm clipping — which is standard practice in LLM fine-tuning and which the paper itself uses for all downstream experiments (max grad norm 1.0 in Table 4). The actual throughput during SuperGLUE training could be substantially lower than Table 2 suggests — potentially half, if the two-pass backward dominates computation time. Even if the two-pass approach is only 1.5× slower than single-pass (because the forward pass cost remains single, and some backward intermediates can be cached), the 11× throughput advantage over AdamW in Table 2 is not representative of the configuration used to achieve the accuracy numbers in Table 3. This creates a misleading impression: the paper's memory advantage (7× reduction) is genuine and reproducible in any configuration, but the throughput advantage (11×) conflates two separate effects — elimination of inter-GPU communication (genuine, from fitting on a single GPU) and elimination of the two-pass overhead (artifactual, from omitting gradient normalization that the accuracy experiments require).

The latency issue is particularly acute for the 65B model, where Table 2 reports 4.93 TGS and the paper estimates 3.6 hours to train on 1,000 samples of 512 tokens. If the two-pass backward approximately doubles training time, the actual wall-clock time for a SuperGLUE fine-tuning run on 65B would be ~7 hours per task — still practical, but notably less impressive. For hyperparameter sweeps across multiple learning rates, warmup ratios, and tasks, the cumulative cost would be substantial on the very hardware (8× RTX 3090) that the paper positions as accessible to resource-constrained researchers. A full sweep (3 learning rates × 3 warmup ratios = 9 configurations per task, 6 tasks = 54 runs) at ~7 hours each would require ~16 days of continuous server usage — feasible for a dedicated machine but a significant resource commitment for a shared cluster.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 2 reports throughput for LOMO on 7B as 769.92 TGS with a single GPU, but does not specify the gradient clipping method or whether loss scaling was enabled. Table 4 specifies "Max Grad Norm: 1.0" for the downstream experiments, implying the two-pass method was used for SuperGLUE but not for throughput benchmarking. The paper does not report throughput numbers for LOMO with the two-pass strategy enabled, does not measure the wall-clock time of the SuperGLUE experiments, and does not discuss the throughput-memory tradeoff introduced by the two-pass requirement. The throughput comparison against AdamW and SGD in Table 2 is between configurations that differ in multiple dimensions simultaneously — optimizer choice, number of GPUs, communication overhead, and (implicitly) gradient normalization method — making it impossible to attribute the throughput difference to LOMO's algorithmic efficiency alone.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges in its Limitations section that "gradient normalization for LOMO does not increase memory usage, [but] our current implementation necessitates an additional backward pass, which can slow down the training speed in scenarios where gradient normalization is essential." It suggests that grouped gradient norm approximation (Section 3.3.1, "A controversial solution") could avoid the second backward pass by computing approximate norms within layer groups, but does not evaluate this approach and leaves it as future work. The paper does not provide throughput measurements comparable to the accuracy experiments, does not report the actual training time for any SuperGLUE experiment, and does not discuss how the two-pass overhead affects the relative throughput advantage over AdamW when both methods use gradient clipping.

### The Single Model Family and Architecture Limits Generalization of the Smoothness Argument

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments in the paper use the LLaMA model family (Touvron et al., 2023) — a decoder-only transformer with pre-layer normalization (RMSNorm), SwiGLU activations in the feed-forward layers, and rotary position embeddings (RoPE). The paper's theoretical argument for why SGD works during fine-tuning (Section 3.1.1) relies on the loss surface being smooth, but it provides no evidence that this smoothness is a property of large language models in general rather than of LLaMA's specific architectural choices. The paper cites Hao et al. (2019) for smoothness of BERT's loss landscape — but BERT uses post-layer normalization and GELU activations, which are architecturally different from LLaMA. Different normalization schemes, activation functions, and position encoding methods can produce loss landscapes with substantially different curvature properties, conditioning, and optimization dynamics.

**The consequence.** A practitioner attempting to use LOMO on a model with a different architecture — for example, a model with post-layer normalization (GPT-2 style, where normalization occurs after the residual addition rather than before), a model with standard ReLU activations (which can create dead units and non-smooth loss surfaces), or a mixture-of-experts model (where the routing function introduces discrete decisions and discontinuities in the loss landscape) — has no guidance on whether the smoothness assumption holds. Post-layer normalization in particular has been associated with worse optimization characteristics than pre-layer normalization, including gradient explosion in early layers during training. If the loss surface is less smooth for such architectures, the two key mechanisms that LOMO depends on — (1) SGD's sufficiency without adaptive per-parameter learning rates, and (2) the implicit batch size effect from sequential updates (Section 3.1.2), which relies on the discrepancy term being small — could both fail. Training might diverge, require much smaller learning rates (negating throughput advantages), or converge to worse solutions than AdamW.

Similarly, the paper does not test LOMO on encoder-decoder architectures (T5, BART) or encoder-only architectures (BERT), which have different gradient flow patterns (bidirectional attention in the encoder, cross-attention between encoder and decoder). The smoothness argument might still hold for these architectures, but without empirical evidence, the paper's findings cannot be assumed to transfer.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper tests LLaMA at four scales (7B, 13B, 30B, 65B) — all within the same architectural family. There is no ablation across architectures, no comparison of loss surface properties between LLaMA and other model families, and no experiment testing whether LOMO works on a non-LLaMA model of comparable size. The theoretical smoothness argument draws on general principles (parameter count, model redundancy) rather than architecture-specific properties, but the empirical validation is confined to a single architecture. The paper does not discuss whether LLaMA's specific design choices (pre-LN, SwiGLU, RoPE) are expected to produce smoother loss surfaces than alternatives.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. It presents LOMO as a general method for "full parameter fine-tuning for large language models" without qualifying the architectural scope. The paper states that it uses LLaMA because the authors "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (implied in Section 4, though the exact quote appears in prior analysis), but representativeness of capabilities (zero-shot performance, scaling behavior) does not imply representativeness of loss surface geometry or optimization dynamics. The future work section does not mention testing LOMO on other architectures.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

LOMO does not introduce a new optimization algorithm — it uses standard SGD, perhaps the oldest and simplest optimizer in the field. Nor does it introduce a new model architecture, training objective, or memory-saving hardware trick. What it changes is the **conceptual framing of what training memory is and why it exists**. The paper's central reframing is to identify the optimizer — specifically its state — as a *design choice* rather than an *infrastructure cost*, and to demonstrate that this choice can be reversed under the right conditions without sacrificing exact gradient information.

This is a **diagnostic contribution** with systems-level consequences. Prior work on memory-efficient training accepted the assumption that the optimizer's memory footprint is a fixed tax: Adam needs momentum and variance buffers, so training a P-parameter model costs at least 2P in FP32 optimizer states plus P in gradient storage. The field's response was to reduce the tax through engineering — quantize the optimizer states (Dettmers et al., 2022), offload them to CPU (Ren et al., 2021b), or approximate the gradients so that the optimizer has less work to do (Malladi et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024). LOMO instead asks: **what if we eliminate the tax entirely by choosing an optimizer that has no state?** This question is only productive because the paper simultaneously provides a theoretical justification for why the optimizer that historically failed — SGD — might succeed during fine-tuning, via the argument that large pretrained models inhabit smooth loss landscapes where the pathologies that motivate Adam (curvature, saddle points, local optima) are attenuated (Section 3.1.1).

The magnitude of this shift is significant but bounded. It is **not a paradigm shift** in optimization theory — SGD was always known to work in some settings, and the smoothness argument, while elegantly articulated, is qualitative rather than empirically measured. It is **not a new capability** — full-parameter fine-tuning already existed; LOMO makes it cheaper, not better. What LOMO shifts is the **practical economics of who can participate in full-parameter fine-tuning research**. The difference between requiring 8× A100 80GB (~$100,000+) and 8× RTX 3090 (~$12,000) for fine-tuning a 65B model is categorical, not incremental — it moves the capability from "well-resourced industry lab" to "academic research group with a modest GPU server." This is a **democratization shift**, not a performance shift, and the paper is explicit about this framing (Introduction, first paragraph).

The paper also resolves a latent tension in the fine-tuning literature: the gap between what is theoretically optimal (full-parameter fine-tuning, per Ding et al., 2022 and Sun et al., 2023) and what is practically feasible under hardware constraints (parameter-efficient methods like LoRA). Prior to LOMO, researchers with limited hardware had to accept the performance ceiling of parameter-efficient methods not because those methods were intrinsically better, but because full-parameter tuning was prohibitively expensive. LOMO provides evidence that this compromise may be unnecessary — at least for SuperGLUE classification tasks with LLaMA models, full-parameter tuning with SGD (via LOMO) matches or exceeds LoRA across all tested scales (Table 3). This does not mean LoRA is obsolete — the paper demonstrates they are complementary (Figure 3) — but it changes the default calculation: a researcher choosing a fine-tuning method no longer needs to treat full-parameter tuning as categorically inaccessible.

The paper makes certain research directions **more attractive**:
- **Full-parameter fine-tuning studies on consumer hardware** become feasible, enabling academic labs to contribute empirical findings about fine-tuning dynamics, scaling laws for adaptation, and task-specific optimization strategies that were previously the domain of well-resourced industry teams.
- **Simplicity-first optimizer design** — the idea that complex adaptive optimizers may be unnecessary for certain training phases — gains empirical support and theoretical grounding. This could encourage systematic investigation of *when* adaptive optimizers matter, rather than defaulting to Adam for all settings.
- **Gradient storage as an optimization target** rather than a fixed cost opens up new systems-level designs: frameworks that support interleaved gradient computation and parameter updates, optimizers with minimal or no persistent state, and training protocols that dynamically switch between stateful and stateless optimizers depending on training phase.

Conversely, some directions become **less urgent**:
- **Incremental quantization schemes for Adam** (e.g., 6-bit or 4-bit optimizer states) lose some appeal when the states can be eliminated entirely for fine-tuning — though they remain relevant for pretraining, where the smoothness argument does not apply.
- **Pure offloading strategies** (CPU, NVMe) for optimizer states may be less attractive than simply switching to SGD when the optimization problem permits, since offloading incurs communication overhead that LOMO avoids.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Generative fine-tuning benchmarks to establish the boundary conditions of SGD sufficiency.** The paper evaluates LOMO exclusively on discriminative SuperGLUE tasks where the model scores fixed candidate strings — it never generates autoregressive text. The most urgent follow-up is to test LOMO on generative fine-tuning: instruction following (e.g., Alpaca, Dolly, or OpenAssistant datasets), summarization (CNN/DailyMail, XSum), and dialogue. The key question is whether the smooth-loss-surface argument (Section 3.1.1) holds for token-level next-token prediction over long sequences, where the gradient signal is denser and the output space is vastly larger. A strong follow-up would fine-tune LLaMA-7B and LLaMA-13B on a standard instruction-tuning dataset (e.g., 52K Alpaca examples) using LOMO, compare against LoRA and (if hardware permits) full-parameter AdamW on a subset, and report both automated metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore) and human preference win rates. A negative result — LOMO significantly underperforming AdamW on generation quality — would establish a critical boundary: SGD may suffice for discrimination but not for generation, perhaps because the loss landscape for autoregressive decoding has sharper curvature. A positive result would dramatically expand LOMO's applicability.

**Direct LOMO-vs-MeZO-vs-GaLore comparison under matched memory budgets.** The paper cites MeZO (Malladi et al., 2023) and GaLore (Zhao et al., 2024) as related memory-efficient methods but does not compare against them experimentally. MeZO is particularly important because it follows the same SuperGLUE evaluation protocol (1,000 training examples, same prompts) and achieves non-trivial accuracy with only forward passes. A direct three-way comparison — LOMO (exact gradients, no optimizer state, two-pass backward), MeZO (zeroth-order estimated gradients, no backward pass at all), and GaLore (low-rank gradient projection, reduced optimizer state) — on SuperGLUE with memory and throughput reported for each method, at matched per-GPU memory budgets, would establish the Pareto frontier of memory-efficient fine-tuning. The key question: does exact gradient information (LOMO) provide enough benefit over gradient approximation (MeZO, GaLore) to justify the additional computation (backward pass vs. forward-only), or does the approximation error become negligible at large model scales? This comparison is feasible on the same 8× RTX 3090 hardware the paper uses and would anchor LOMO's contribution relative to the most directly comparable prior work.

**Loss landscape measurements to validate (or falsify) the smoothness hypothesis.** The paper's theoretical argument — that LLMs have smooth loss surfaces, making SGD viable — rests on citations to prior work (Hao et al., 2019, on BERT) and qualitative geometric intuitions. It never measures smoothness directly. A follow-up study could compute Hessian eigenvalue spectra, local curvature estimates (via the Hessian-vector product), or loss interpolation plots (linear interpolation between pretrained and fine-tuned checkpoints) for LLaMA models at multiple scales, comparing the curvature at the pretrained initialization against the curvature after fine-tuning and against smaller models where SGD is known to fail. The prediction from Section 3.1.1 is that curvature should decrease with model scale and that fine-tuned checkpoints should remain in wide basins. If this prediction holds, it provides mechanistic validation for LOMO's approach and suggests that other optimizers with minimal state could work for LLM fine-tuning. If it fails — if LLaMA's loss surface is actually quite sharp in certain directions — it would imply that LOMO's success on SuperGLUE is due to some other factor (careful learning rate tuning, the specific tasks, the small number of training examples), and the theoretical framing would need revision. This experiment requires no new training infrastructure, only analysis of existing or fine-tuned checkpoints using standard Hessian computation tools.

**Dynamic difficulty estimation and adaptive strategy switching during fine-tuning.** The paper's implicit batch size derivation (Section 3.1.2) suggests that the discrepancy between LOMO's sequential updates and standard batched SGD is controlled by the learning rate and the loss surface curvature. This opens the possibility of **dynamically switching between SGD and Adam** during fine-tuning: use LOMO (SGD, no optimizer state, low memory) during early epochs when the model is close to the pretrained initialization and the loss surface is smooth, then switch to Adam (with its optimizer state initialized from scratch or carried over) during later epochs when larger weight changes might push the model into regions where adaptive per-parameter scaling becomes beneficial. A concrete experiment: fine-tune LLaMA-7B on a task where LOMO underperforms LoRA (e.g., MultiRC at 7B: LOMO 84.0% vs. LoRA 84.8%, per Table 3) with a hybrid strategy — first 5 epochs with LOMO, then 5 epochs with AdamW (or LoRA+AdamW) — and measure whether the hybrid matches or exceeds either method alone. This would test whether LOMO's memory savings can be realized during the bulk of training while adaptive optimization provides a final performance boost, without the hardware needing to accommodate Adam's optimizer states for the entire training duration.

**Scaling LOMO to continued pretraining and domain adaptation.** The paper's smoothness argument is explicitly bounded: "This holds only when we teach the LLMs natural language-based tasks (or code-based if pre-trained with code). A synthetic loss function unrelated to pre-training tasks will indeed face the large curvature problem" (Section 3.1.1). But what about continued pretraining — fine-tuning on a large domain-specific corpus (e.g., medical literature, legal documents, non-English languages) with hundreds of thousands or millions of examples, where the weight changes are substantially larger than for 1,000-example task adaptation? The smoothness assumption may break down as the model moves farther from the pretrained initialization, and SGD's lack of adaptive per-parameter scaling could become problematic. A stress-test experiment: continued pretraining of LLaMA-7B on a domain corpus of 100K–1M examples, comparing LOMO (SGD) against standard full-parameter AdamW (on hardware that can accommodate it, or on a smaller model) and LoRA. Measure both downstream task performance (on domain-specific benchmarks) and perplexity on held-out domain text. A negative result — LOMO diverging or significantly underperforming on continued pretraining — would establish a clear scope boundary: LOMO is for task-specific fine-tuning with limited data and small weight changes, not for domain adaptation. A positive result would suggest the smoothness assumption is more robust than the paper's caveat implies.

**Framework-level support for fused gradient-update operations.** LOMO's implementation is constrained by PyTorch's autograd API — the paper notes: "we cannot implement the exact immediate update with current APIs" (Section 3.2). The fused update in Algorithm 1 approximates the ideal by storing at most one layer's gradient at a time using hook functions, but true immediate update (where a parameter is modified the instant its gradient is computed, before backpropagation continues to earlier layers) would require deeper integration with the autograd engine. A systems follow-up could implement native support for fused gradient-update operations in PyTorch or JAX, allowing the backward pass to interleave gradient computation with parameter modification at arbitrary granularity. This would benefit not only LOMO but any future optimizer with minimal or no state. A concrete deliverable: a PyTorch pull request or extension that provides a `register_fused_update_hook` API, with benchmarks showing that the overhead of hook injection in the current LOMO implementation is eliminated and that the two-pass backward strategy for gradient normalization can be replaced with a single-pass streaming norm computation (accumulating squared gradient norms as parameters are updated). This would close the throughput gap between LOMO's single-pass and two-pass configurations, making the headline 11× throughput advantage (Table 2) achievable even with gradient clipping.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Academic research labs fine-tuning LLMs for task-specific studies.** An academic NLP group with access to a shared 8× RTX 3090 server (total GPU memory 192 GB, hardware cost ~$12,000) can now fine-tune a 65B-parameter LLaMA model end-to-end on discriminative tasks, where previously this required 8× A100 80GB (~$100,000+) or was simply impossible on their hardware. The paper demonstrates this directly: LOMO trains a 65B model on the same 8×3090 configuration with 19.18 GB peak memory per GPU (Table 2) and ~3.6 hours per 1,000-example task (Section 4.2). For a research project involving 6 SuperGLUE-style tasks, 3 hyperparameter configurations, and 3 random seeds, the total compute time would be approximately 6 × 3 × 3 × 3.6 ≈ 194 GPU-hours — about 24 hours on the 8-GPU server, well within a shared cluster's queue limits. This enables academic researchers to run controlled experiments comparing full-parameter fine-tuning against parameter-efficient methods across model scales, contributing empirical findings to the fine-tuning literature that were previously dominated by industry labs with larger hardware budgets. The key constraint: this currently applies only to discriminative tasks (the paper's scope); generative fine-tuning throughput and performance remain unmeasured.

**Privacy-sensitive on-premise fine-tuning at small organizations.** Organizations that handle sensitive data (healthcare, legal, finance) and cannot ship data to cloud APIs can now fine-tune large models on their own hardware. A legal tech startup with a single 8× RTX 3090 server can take an open-source 65B model and fine-tune it on proprietary legal documents for contract analysis or case retrieval, without the data ever leaving the premises. The 19.18 GB per-GPU memory requirement (Table 2) fits within a single RTX 3090's 24 GB, meaning the 65B model is trainable without GPU upgrades. For smaller models (7B–13B), a single RTX 3090 suffices for training (Table 2: 13.61 GB for 7B, 15.92 GB per GPU for 13B on 2 GPUs), enabling even smaller organizations or individual practitioners with a single consumer GPU to perform full-parameter fine-tuning. The 4.93 TGS throughput for 65B (Table 2) translates to ~3.6 hours per 1,000 examples — practical for overnight training runs on small-to-medium proprietary datasets. The primary risk is the unvalidated performance on generative tasks: a legal tech startup needing the model to *generate* contract summaries, not just classify clauses, would be operating outside the paper's demonstrated scope.

**Cost-efficient multi-task model customization for small-to-medium enterprises.** A company that maintains multiple fine-tuned model variants for different customer-facing tasks (e.g., different product categories, different languages, different tone requirements) traditionally faces a hardware cost proportional to the number of variants if each requires high-end GPUs. LOMO reduces the per-variant hardware requirement from datacenter-grade to consumer-grade. For a 7B model — which LOMO trains on a single RTX 3090 with 13.61 GB memory and 769.92 TGS throughput (Table 2) — a single 8×3090 server can train 8 model variants simultaneously (one per GPU) without inter-GPU communication overhead, achieving aggregate throughput of ~6,160 TGS (8 × 770). At this rate, fine-tuning 8 different task-specific 7B models on 1,000 examples each would require approximately 1.1 hours total (1,000 samples × 512 tokens / (770 TGS) ≈ 665 seconds per variant, parallelized across 8 GPUs). This makes it economically viable to maintain dozens of task-specific full-parameter fine-tuned models rather than relying on a single general-purpose model or parameter-efficient adapters — though the storage cost of separate full model copies (12.55 GB per 7B model in FP16) must be weighed against the disk savings of LoRA adapters (typically a few megabytes).

**Edge-server fine-tuning for personalized language models.** For applications where a language model must be adapted to individual user data (e.g., personalized writing assistants, adaptive tutoring systems, user-specific email auto-completion) without sending that data to a central server, LOMO's low memory footprint enables full-parameter fine-tuning on edge servers with consumer GPUs. A single RTX 3090 in a secure edge server can fine-tune a 7B model on a user's documents or interaction history using LOMO, producing a personalized model without the data leaving the edge. The 769.92 TGS throughput (Table 2) means that 1,000 tokens of user-specific data (e.g., a few dozen emails or documents) can be processed for fine-tuning in under 2 seconds of GPU time per epoch. With 10 epochs, full personalization would require ~20 seconds of GPU time, making it feasible to offer as an on-demand service (e.g., clicking "personalize my assistant" and waiting < 1 minute). The bottleneck shifts from GPU memory (solved by LOMO) to the quality and quantity of personalization data — a separate research challenge that LOMO makes practically explorable.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions LOMO as a method for **full-parameter fine-tuning under GPU memory constraints**, not as a universally superior optimizer. The specific tradeoffs it identifies — against AdamW (higher memory, potentially better optimization) and against parameter-efficient methods like LoRA (lower memory, potentially lower performance ceiling) — suggest the following decision logic, grounded in the paper's empirical evidence:

- **Prefer LOMO when GPU memory is the binding constraint and the task is discriminative classification with limited training data (≤ few thousand examples).** This is the paper's demonstrated success regime: 8× RTX 3090 for 65B models (Table 2), SuperGLUE accuracy matching or exceeding LoRA (Table 3). If your hardware cannot accommodate AdamW's optimizer states for your target model size, LOMO provides full-parameter fine-tuning that would otherwise be impossible.

- **Prefer LOMO when you want the deployment simplicity of a standard fine-tuned checkpoint (no adapter modules, no inference latency overhead).** LOMO produces a standard LLaMA weight file — no LoRA adapters to merge or load separately. If your inference pipeline requires vanilla model weights, LOMO provides full-parameter fine-tuning without the post-hoc merging step that LoRA requires for latency-free deployment.

- **Prefer LoRA (or another PEFT method) when you need to switch between multiple tasks without storing separate full model copies, or when you are working with generative tasks where LOMO's performance is unvalidated.** LoRA adapters are megabytes vs. gigabytes for full checkpoints. For a 7B model, storing 10 task-specific LoRA adapters costs ~10–100 MB vs. ~125 GB for 10 full LOMO checkpoints. The paper also has no evidence for LOMO on generative tasks — LoRA has an established track record for instruction tuning and summarization that LOMO currently lacks.

- **Prefer full-parameter AdamW (on capable hardware) when you need the strongest possible optimization for a challenging task and can afford the hardware.** The paper argues that full-parameter tuning beats parameter-efficient tuning (citing Ding et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023) but never compares LOMO (SGD) against full-parameter AdamW. AdamW's adaptive per-parameter learning rates may provide better final performance, faster convergence, or greater robustness to hyperparameter choice — but the paper provides no evidence either way. If you have the hardware to run full-parameter AdamW, it remains the conservative choice until a direct LOMO-vs-AdamW comparison exists.

- **Consider LOMO+LoRA together when you want to combine the benefits of full-parameter tuning and task-specific adapters.** Figure 3 shows the combination does not hurt and can slightly improve over either method alone on BoolQ and MultiRC. This hybrid approach lets LOMO handle the full-model adaptation while LoRA provides additional task-specific capacity with AdamW optimization.
