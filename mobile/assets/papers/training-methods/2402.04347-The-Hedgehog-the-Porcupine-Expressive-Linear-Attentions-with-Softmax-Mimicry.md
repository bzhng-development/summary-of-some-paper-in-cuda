# The Hedgehog & the Porcupine: Expressive Linear Attentions with Softmax Mimicry

**ArXiv:** [2402.04347](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04347)

## 🎯 Pitch

Linear attentions can match softmax attention—often perfectly—if you simply train them to mimic its spiky, monotonic weight distributions. Hedgehog does exactly this, recovering over 99% of BERT’s GLUE score and turning pretrained GPT‑2 and Llama‑2 7B into competitive sub‑quadratic models without any extra attention modules.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Hedgehog**, a learnable linear attention mechanism that closes the performance gap between standard softmax attention and prior linear attentions by explicitly training feature maps to mimic softmax attention weights, recovering two absent properties—low-entropy "spikiness" (selective, non-uniform weighting of relevant tokens) and dot-product monotonicity (attention weights that increase smoothly with query-key similarity). Evaluated across three regimes on benchmarks including WikiText-103, GLUE, and Long Range Arena with models ranging from BERT-base and GPT-2 to Llama-2 7B, Hedgehog recovers over 99% of standard Transformer quality in finetuned-conversion settings, closes the linear attention gap by 68.6% in training-from-scratch (improving perplexity by up to 6 points on WikiText-103), and achieves state-of-the-art 16.7 perplexity for 125M subquadratic decoder models on WikiText-103 via **pretrained-conversion** of GPT-2, establishing that linear attention can match softmax expressivity when the feature map is learned to distill attention-weight distributions rather than relying on fixed functional approximations.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Linear Attention Cannot Match Softmax Attention Quality

The paper addresses a fundamental tension in Transformer design: **how to make attention efficient without sacrificing the modeling quality that makes it work.** Standard softmax attention in Transformers computes pairwise similarity scores between every query and every key in a sequence, requiring $O(n^2 d)$ time and memory where $n$ is sequence length and $d$ is head dimension (Vaswani et al., 2017). For typical Transformer settings—head dimension $d = 64$ and sequence lengths from 512 to 32K tokens—this quadratic scaling becomes prohibitive, dominating both training cost and inference latency.

Linear attentions emerged as the natural solution to this bottleneck. By replacing the softmax of query-key dot products with kernel function feature maps, they reduce complexity from $O(n^2 d)$ to $O(n d d')$ (Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Choromanski et al., 2020). This quadratic-to-linear scaling can yield significant speed and memory improvements (Fig. 6), which is why linear attention has attracted sustained research attention.

However, the paper identifies a critical and persistent gap: **existing linear attention mechanisms consistently underperform softmax attention in modeling quality.** When training from scratch, linear attentions achieve 4–6 worse perplexity on standard benchmarks like WikiText-103 (Schlag et al., 2021; Irie et al., 2021; Fu et al., 2023). The authors emphasize the magnitude of this gap by noting it equals the performance difference between 125M and 255M parameter Transformers (Dai et al., 2019)—effectively, switching to linear attention is like losing half your model capacity for free. When converting pretrained Transformer models to linear variants, prior methods require retaining additional quadratic attention modules just to recover acceptable performance (Kasai et al., 2021; Mao, 2022), undermining the efficiency motivation.

This gap raises an unsettling question: is high-quality linear attention fundamentally impossible? Recent theoretical work using the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) suggests that truly subquadratic algorithms approximating softmax attention with high quality may not exist for large sequence lengths (Alman & Song, 2023; Keles et al., 2023). The paper engages with this possibility head-on, treating it not as a reason to abandon linear attention but as a challenge to understand *exactly what properties* make softmax attention expressive and whether those properties can be recovered through learned approximations rather than fixed mathematical ones.

### Why This Problem Matters

The paper motivates the importance of closing the linear attention quality gap across three distinct deployment scenarios, each with practical and theoretical stakes:

**Training-from-scratch efficiency.** When building new Transformer models from the ground up, linear attention promises to make training tractable on longer sequences than softmax attention can handle. Benchmarks like Long Range Arena (LRA) (Tay et al., 2021) were specifically designed to test this, evaluating whether efficient attention variants can handle tasks requiring long-range dependencies (document retrieval, image classification on high-resolution inputs, pathfinding on 2D grids). If linear attention could match softmax quality, it would enable training Transformers directly on full-length documents, high-resolution images, or long time series without truncation or hierarchical approximations. The paper emphasizes that this is not merely about speed—it is about whether we can build models that reason over long contexts at all.

**Finetuned-conversion of existing models.** A second practical scenario is taking an already-trained Transformer (e.g., a BERT model finetuned on a specific task) and swapping its attention mechanism to a linear variant to serve it more efficiently at inference time (Kasai et al., 2021; Mao, 2022). This is compelling because the expensive pretraining is already done—organizations could convert their existing deployed models to linear versions and benefit from reduced inference costs without retraining from scratch. However, the paper notes that without high-fidelity attention conversion, the finetuned model's task performance degrades substantially (Table 1 shows BERT finetuned on CoLA dropping from 58.8 to 28.1 Matthew's correlation with the 1+ELU linear attention), making this approach non-viable for quality-sensitive applications.

**Pretrained-conversion of large language models.** The most ambitious scenario is converting pretrained Transformers such as large language models (e.g., GPT-2, Llama-2 7B) into linear versions that can then be finetuned on downstream tasks. This promises to combine the knowledge encoded in large-scale pretraining with the efficiency of linear attention for deployment, enabling models like Llama-2 to handle longer contexts while remaining computationally practical. However, the quality gap in existing linear attentions makes this particularly challenging—the paper shows that prior methods applied to Llama-2 7B produce models that "struggle to produce coherent text," with a 16.5 ROUGE-1 point drop relative to the standard attention baseline (Table 11).

Together, these three regimes span the full lifecycle of Transformer deployment: building new models, optimizing existing ones, and adapting pretrained foundation models. The inability of linear attention to match softmax quality is thus not a narrow technical issue but a **blocker for a broad class of efficiency improvements**.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies a pattern in the linear attention literature: existing approaches focus on finding feature maps $\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d'}$ that approximate the exponential kernel $K(q, k) = \exp(q^\top k / \sqrt{d})$ while maintaining computational efficiency. The authors categorize these approaches and diagnose specific failures:

**Positive-only feature maps (1+ELU, ReLU).** The simplest linear attentions ensure positive attention weights by applying element-wise activation functions like $1 + \text{ELU}(x)$ (Katharopoulos et al., 2020) or $\text{ReLU}(x)$ (Kasai et al., 2021). While mathematically simple and computationally cheap, these feature maps produce attention weights that are dramatically more uniform than softmax—they lack the ability to strongly upweight a few relevant tokens while suppressing irrelevant ones. The paper quantifies this through attention weight entropy (Fig. 2): softmax attention produces low-entropy, "spiky" distributions that concentrate probability mass on specific tokens, while 1+ELU and ReLU produce high-entropy, near-uniform weights. As the paper shows via the associative recall task (Fig. 4), this uniformity directly corresponds to failure: models with uniform attention achieve under 20% accuracy on a task that requires selectively attending to specific key-value associations, while softmax attention achieves 100%.

**Randomized feature approximations (Performer).** A more sophisticated approach uses random Fourier features to approximate the Gaussian/softmax kernel with bounded error under the mean-squared metric (Choromanski et al., 2020; Peng et al., 2021; Choromanski et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2023). The Performer's FAVOR+ mechanism is a prominent example. The key theoretical insight is that the softmax kernel can be expressed as an expectation over random projections, and by sampling enough such projections one can approximate the true attention weights. However, the paper finds that in practice, Performer attention weights remain substantially more uniform than softmax weights (Fig. 2). This matters because the MSE approximation guarantee does not translate to the spikiness property—a feature map can have low average error while systematically failing to reproduce the sharp concentration that makes softmax effective for selective attention. The paper's associative recall experiment (Fig. 4) confirms that Performer's uniform weights translate to poor task performance (17% accuracy, matching the simpler 1+ELU baseline).

**Locality-enhancing feature maps (cosFormer).** Some approaches incorporate inductive biases toward attending to nearby tokens, such as the cosFormer's re-weighting mechanism that upweights positions close to the query and downweights distant ones (Qin et al., 2022b). While this improves certain tasks with strong local structure, the paper shows that cosFormer still produces higher-entropy weights than softmax (Fig. 2) and fails on associative recall (17% accuracy, Fig. 4). The underlying issue is that locality is a heuristic—useful for some sequences but insufficient to capture the full flexibility of learned softmax attention, which can attend to arbitrary positions based on content rather than distance.

**Low-rank approximations (Linformer, Nyströmformer).** Rather than using kernel feature maps, some methods approximate the full attention matrix via low-rank factorization or Nyström sampling (Xiong et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2021). While these achieve subquadratic complexity, the paper does not focus on them as extensively, instead grouping them as alternatives that similarly fail to replicate the expressive properties the authors identify as essential.

**The common thread: missing properties, not just approximation error.** The paper's key diagnostic move is to argue that prior linear attentions fail not primarily due to poor global approximation of the softmax function (though that is a factor), but because they systematically lack two specific properties that softmax attention possesses: (1) **low-entropy spikiness**—the ability to produce sharply concentrated attention weights that selectively upweight relevant tokens, and (2) **dot-product monotonicity**—the property that attention weights increase smoothly and monotonically as query-key dot products increase. The authors observe that existing feature maps "lose the spikes enabled via the scaled dot-product softmax" (Section 1) and produce non-monotonic mappings where "trying to upweight attentions by increasing product similarity can actually result in decreased attention weights" (Section 3.1, Fig. 3). These are not minor cosmetic differences—the paper demonstrates they are causally connected to downstream performance.

### The Monotonicity Gap and Its Specific Consequences

While spikiness matters for training-from-scratch (the model needs to learn to attend selectively), the paper identifies monotonicity as critical for a subtler reason: **it enables smooth conversion of pretrained Transformers into linear variants.** In Section 3.1, the authors explain that when swapping attention mechanisms in a pretrained model, the new attention map's gradient behavior relative to the original model's parameters determines whether finetuning can recover performance. If the attention weight as a function of query-key dot products is non-monotonic (as it is for 1+ELU, ReLU, Performer, and cosFormer—all visible in Fig. 3), then the gradient signal during finetuning is misaligned with the pretrained weights. Updating the query and key projections to increase a dot product might decrease the attention weight, creating conflicting optimization signals that prevent the model from recovering its original behavior.

This explains a specific empirical puzzle: why prior linear attentions fail at finetuned-conversion even when they might perform adequately in training-from-scratch settings where all parameters are learned jointly. In the finetuned-conversion experiment (Table 1), the spiky $\phi_2$ feature map ($\exp(x \cdot t)$ with $t=2$) was sufficient to solve associative recall from scratch (Fig. 4), yet it still fails to fully recover BERT CoLA performance after conversion (50.0 Matthew's correlation vs. 58.8 for softmax). The missing ingredient, the paper shows, is monotonicity—$\phi_2$ produces spiky weights but does not satisfy monotonicity over the full range of dot products that the pretrained BERT model produces (Fig. 3 and Fig. 5 show the contrast).

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution not as proposing yet another fixed feature map, but as demonstrating that **linear attention feature maps can and should be learned to explicitly mimic softmax attention.** This is a conceptual shift from the dominant paradigm in prior work, which the paper characterizes as "proposing a specific kernel function" (Section 1). The key insight is that the properties that make softmax attention effective—spikiness and monotonicity—are emergent consequences of the exponential function's shape, but they do not require exact functional approximation of the exponential to be recovered. What matters is that the learned feature map captures the *attention weight distribution* that softmax produces, not that it approximates the exponential kernel per se.

The paper connects this to a broader observation: prior approaches implicitly treat the feature map choice as a modeling decision to be made a priori, based on mathematical convenience (random features for provable bounds), simplicity (positive activations for stability), or heuristics (locality bias). In contrast, Hedgehog treats the feature map as an **adapter to be trained** (in the style of Houlsby et al., 2019), inserted after the query and key projections, and optimized to minimize the cross-entropy between its own attention weights and those that softmax would have produced on the same queries and keys. This is essentially **attention weight distillation**: the linear attention learns to reproduce the same attention patterns that make softmax expressive, without inheriting its quadratic cost.

The paper explicitly positions this learnable approach against the backdrop of the existing efficiency-expressivity tradeoff summarized in Table 2. Standard softmax has $O(n^2 d)$ complexity and possesses both spikiness and monotonicity. Prior linear attentions achieve $O(n d^2)$ or $O(n d'^2)$ complexity but lack both properties. The Taylor exponential approximation (Section 4.1) recovers both properties but at $O(n d^3)$ complexity—efficient in sequence length but impractical in model dimension. Hedgehog aims to occupy the sweet spot: $O(n d^2)$ complexity like prior linear attentions, with spikiness and monotonicity like softmax, achieved through learned feature maps rather than fixed approximations.

Finally, the paper situates itself within the practical landscape of Transformer deployment by targeting the three regimes discussed above, arguing that a single method that works across all three—training, finetuned-conversion, and pretrained-conversion—provides a unified solution that prior approaches do not. Each prior method was typically evaluated in only one or two regimes; Hedgehog's design is motivated to be general-purpose.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **learnable linear attention mechanism** that can be dropped into standard Transformer architectures to replace softmax attention while maintaining its expressive power. The core problem it solves is the **quality gap** between $O(n^2 d)$ softmax attention and $O(n d d')$ linear attention—a gap the paper traces to two missing properties (spikiness and monotonicity) that existing fixed feature maps fail to capture. The shape of the solution is a **trainable single-layer MLP feature map** trained via **attention weight distillation** to explicitly mimic the attention weight distributions that softmax would have produced, combined with an **element-wise exponential activation** to induce the spiky and monotonic properties that matter for performance.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components that work together at each attention layer of a Transformer:

1. **Base Transformer Attention** — the standard query, key, value projections and softmax attention mechanism from the original model (kept frozen during distillation). It serves as the "teacher" producing ground-truth softmax attention weights for each head.

2. **Hedgehog Feature Map (MLP)** — a trainable single-layer MLP inserted after the query and key projections, mapping from head dimension $d$ to output dimension $2d$ (via negation mapping), with element-wise exponential activation. This produces the "student" linear attention weights when its outputs for queries and keys are multiplied.

3. **Linear Attention Computation** — the standard kernelized linear attention formula (Eq. 2) that computes outputs in $O(n d^2)$ by multiplying the feature-mapped queries against a running sum of feature-mapped keys and values, avoiding the $n \times n$ attention matrix.

4. **Distillation Loss** — a cross-entropy loss computed per head, per layer between the linear attention's normalized feature map dot products and the softmax attention's normalized query-key dot products. This trains the Hedgehog MLPs to produce attention weights that mimic softmax attention distributions.

Information flows as follows: input hidden states → frozen query/key projections (produce raw $q$, $k$) → parallel paths: (a) softmax attention produces "teacher" weights, (b) Hedgehog MLPs map $q$, $k$ to $\phi_{\text{mlp}}(q)$, $\phi_{\text{mlp}}(k)$ → linear attention formula produces "student" weights → cross-entropy between student and teacher weights → gradient flows into Hedgehog MLPs only → after distillation, MLPs are frozen or finetuned along with the rest of the model.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the formal definition of linear attention and kernel functions, since this is the mathematical scaffolding that Hedgehog builds on and all subsequent design choices depend on understanding how feature maps determine attention behavior.
- **Second**, the two diagnostic properties (spikiness and monotonicity) and how they are measured, since these are the explicit targets that motivate Hedgehog's architecture and training procedure.
- **Third**, the Taylor exponential analysis, since it proves conceptually that there exist subquadratic feature maps recovering both properties, but shows that polynomial dimensionality explosion makes them impractical, motivating the need for learned alternatives.
- **Fourth**, the Hedgehog MLP feature map architecture—its structure, the exponential activation, the negation mapping, and why each design choice matters for recovering spikiness and monotonicity while maintaining $O(n d^2)$ efficiency.
- **Fifth**, the attention weight distillation loss—its formulation, how it is computed per head and per layer, the joint training procedure across all layers, and why cross-entropy (not MSE) is the right objective.
- **Sixth**, the two-stage training procedure for conversion settings (distillation then finetuning) and the end-to-end training procedure for from-scratch settings, since the training regime differs fundamentally between these deployment scenarios.
- **Seventh**, numerical stability mechanisms and the identity initialization strategy, since these are critical practical details that make the method trainable in practice.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **method paper** whose core idea is that linear attention feature maps should be **learned to mimic softmax attention weights** rather than fixed a priori based on mathematical approximations, and that doing so with an exponential-activated MLP recovers the spiky and monotonic properties that make softmax attention expressive while maintaining the $O(n d^2)$ efficiency of prior linear attentions.

#### Linear Attention and the Kernel Feature Map Framework

Hedgehog builds on the established kernel formulation of attention, which the paper reviews in Section 2. Standard softmax attention computes an output $y_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ for each query position $i$ as a weighted sum of values, where the weights are the softmax-normalized dot products between query $q_i$ and keys $k_j$ for all positions $j \leq i$ (causal attention) or all positions $j$ (bidirectional attention). Formally:

$$y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{i} \frac{\exp(q_i^\top k_j / \sqrt{d})}{\sum_{m=1}^{i} \exp(q_i^\top k_m / \sqrt{d})} v_j$$

where $q_i, k_j, v_j \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are the query, key, and value vectors at positions $i$ and $j$, and $d$ is the per-head dimension. The paper uses $n$ to denote sequence length and $d$ for head dimension throughout.

**What it computes:** a context-dependent representation for position $i$ that aggregates value vectors from all previous positions, where each value's contribution is weighted by how similar its key is to the current query. The softmax ensures weights are non-negative and sum to one, and the temperature scaling $\sqrt{d}$ prevents dot products from growing too large in high dimensions (which would make the softmax degenerate to a one-hot).

**Why this form:** the softmax of scaled dot products gives attention the ability to be highly selective—exponentiating the dot products amplifies relative differences, producing "spiky" distributions that concentrate probability mass on the most relevant keys while suppressing irrelevant ones. This selectivity is what the paper identifies as the first missing property in prior linear attentions.

The kernel formulation replaces the softmax with a general kernel function $K(x, x') = \phi(x)^\top \phi(x')$ where $\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d'}$ is a feature map applied to each query and key independently. The key algebraic manipulation (shown in Eq. 2 of the paper) rearranges the attention computation by observing that the numerator and denominator can be computed via running sums over the sequence:

$$y_i = \frac{\phi(q_i) \sum_{j=1}^{i} \left(\phi(k_j)^\top v_j\right)}{\phi(q_i) \sum_{j=1}^{i} \phi(k_j)}$$

where $\phi(q_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times d'}$ and $\sum_{j=1}^{i} \phi(k_j) \in \mathbb{R}^{d' \times 1}$ so their product is a scalar normalizer, and $\sum_{j=1}^{i} \left(\phi(k_j)^\top v_j\right)$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{d' \times d}$ where $\phi(k_j)^\top v_j$ is an outer product. The critical insight: the sums $\sum_{j=1}^{i} \phi(k_j)$ and $\sum_{j=1}^{i} \phi(k_j)^\top v_j$ can be updated incrementally as each new token is processed, requiring $O(d d')$ per step rather than $O(i d)$ per step for recomputing all pairwise similarities. Over the full sequence, this yields $O(n d d')$ total complexity instead of $O(n^2 d)$.

**Why this form enables efficiency:** the decomposition separates the computation into a per-token encoding step (apply $\phi$) and an accumulation step (update running sums), making the cost linear in $n$. The feature map dimension $d'$ controls the expressivity-efficiency tradeoff—larger $d'$ gives better approximations but higher cost. Prior work set $d' = d$ (e.g., 1+ELU, ReLU) or $d' > d$ (e.g., Performer's random features) based on fixed mathematical forms. Hedgehog's contribution is to make $d' = 2d$ with a learned mapping.

#### The Spikiness Property and Its Mechanistic Role

The paper defines spikiness operationally rather than formally: it is the property that attention weights have **low entropy** and concentrate probability mass on a small number of relevant keys, rather than distributing weight uniformly across the sequence. The paper uses Shannon entropy of the attention weight distribution as a quantitative measure (Fig. 2, Fig. 4).

Spikiness emerges from the softmax of scaled dot products through a specific mechanism: the exponential function $\exp(\cdot)$ amplifies relative differences between dot products. If query $q_i$ has a high dot product with key $k_3$ (say 10) and low dot products with all other keys (say 0–2), then $\exp(10) \approx 22026$ while $\exp(2) \approx 7.4$, making the attention weight for $k_3$ approximately $22026/(22026 + \text{small}) \approx 0.999$. The softmax with temperature scaling thus acts as a differentiable argmax: small differences in dot products become large differences in attention weights.

**Why prior linear attentions lack spikiness:** feature maps like $1 + \text{ELU}(x)$ or $\text{ReLU}(x)$ apply element-wise activation functions that have limited dynamic range. The ReLU function maps negative inputs to zero and positive inputs to their identity, meaning the maximum ratio between two feature-mapped values is bounded by the ratio of their raw dot products—there is no exponential amplification. Random Fourier features (Performer) approximate the exponential kernel in expectation but with high variance for individual dot product pairs, resulting in smoothed-out attention weights. The paper demonstrates this empirically through the associative recall experiment (Section 3.2, Fig. 4), where spikiness (measured via entropy) strongly correlates with accuracy on a task that requires selective attention to specific key-value pairs.

The paper also tests a controlled feature map to isolate the spikiness property: $\phi_t(x) = \exp(x \cdot t)$, which applies an element-wise exponential with temperature $t$ to each dimension of the query and key vectors before computing dot products. This feature map is **spiky but not necessarily monotonic** (because the element-wise exponential applied to individual dimensions does not guarantee monotonicity of the full dot product $\phi_t(q)^\top \phi_t(k)$ as a function of $q^\top k$). The paper finds that $\phi_2$ (temperature $t=2$) achieves 100% associative recall accuracy while $\phi_1$ fails—confirming that spikiness can be induced by the exponential activation alone, but that the relationship between the learned query-key representations and the resulting attention weights matters for whether spikiness translates to task performance.

#### The Monotonicity Property and Its Role in Conversion

Monotonicity over query-key dot products is defined as the property that the attention weight between query $q$ and key $k$ increases (or at least does not decrease) when $q^\top k$ increases, and vice versa. Formally, for the attention map $A(q, k) = \phi(q)^\top \phi(k) / \sum_{m} \phi(q)^\top \phi(k_m)$, the property requires that $A(q, k)$ is monotonic in the scalar $q^\top k$ when varying $q$ and $k$ along directions that change their dot product.

**Why monotonicity matters for conversion:** when converting a pretrained Transformer to a linear attention variant, the model's query and key projection matrices $W_Q$ and $W_K$ were trained under softmax attention, where the attention weight for a key $k_j$ increases monotonically with $q_i^\top k_j$. If the replacement linear attention is non-monotonic—if there exist pairs $(q, k_1)$ and $(q, k_2)$ where $q^\top k_1 > q^\top k_2$ but the attention weight on $k_1$ is smaller—then the gradient signal during finetuning is misaligned with the pretrained weights. The model receives gradients telling it to adjust $W_Q$ and $W_K$ in ways that conflict with the original training objective, preventing recovery of the original model's behavior.

The paper visualizes this in Fig. 3 by plotting the attention weight as a function of the dot product $q^\top k$ for a trained BERT model's queries and keys. Softmax attention produces a smooth, monotonically increasing S-curve (the softmax shape). Prior linear attentions produce jagged, non-monotonic curves where the attention weight can decrease even as the dot product increases over certain ranges. The temperature-scaled exponential $\phi_2$ produces a spiky but still non-smooth monotonic curve—it generally increases but with sharp nonlinearities that the paper argues cause training difficulties.

**The gradient conflict mechanism:** during finetuning after attention swap, if a correct prediction would require increasing the attention weight on a particular key, the model receives a gradient signal to increase the query-key dot product for that key. Under softmax, this gradient reliably increases the attention weight. Under a non-monotonic linear attention, the same gradient on the dot product might decrease the attention weight, causing the model to take optimization steps that reduce task performance. Over multiple gradient steps, this creates oscillatory or divergent behavior. The paper connects this to Table 1, where finetuned-conversion of BERT on CoLA shows dramatic performance drops: from Matthew's correlation of 58.8 (original BERT) to 28.1 (1+ELU), 24.7 (Performer), and 39.9 (cosFormer). Even $\phi_2$, which has spikiness, only recovers to 50.0—the 8.8 point gap relative to softmax is attributed to insufficient monotonicity.

#### The Taylor Exponential: A Proof of Concept That Both Properties Are Recoverable

Before introducing Hedgehog, the paper explores a simple baseline that demonstrates both spikiness and monotonicity are achievable with subquadratic computation: approximating the softmax exponential via a low-degree Taylor polynomial, specifically the second-degree expansion:

$$\exp(q^\top k) \approx 1 + q^\top k + \frac{(q^\top k)^2}{2}$$

The corresponding feature map $\phi_{\text{taylor}}(x)$ for a vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ projects to $O(d^2)$ dimensions:

$$\phi_{\text{taylor}}(x) = [1, x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_d] \cup [x_i \cdot x_j \mid i, j \in [d]]$$

**What it computes:** the first part produces features for the constant and linear terms (dot product directly recoverable), and the second part produces all pairwise products $x_i x_j$ for the quadratic term. The dot product $\phi_{\text{taylor}}(q)^\top \phi_{\text{taylor}}(k)$ expands to $1 + \sum_i q_i k_i + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} q_i q_j k_i k_j$, which equals $1 + q^\top k + \frac{1}{2} (q^\top k)^2$ (since $\sum_{i,j} q_i q_j k_i k_j = (\sum_i q_i k_i)^2 = (q^\top k)^2$). This matches the second-order Taylor expansion of $\exp(q^\top k)$ around 0.

**Why this form demonstrates recoverability:** the polynomial approximation is monotonic in the regime where the Taylor series is a good approximation (bounded $q^\top k$) and, because it approximates the exponential, inherits its spikyness—small differences in $q^\top k$ get amplified through the quadratic term. The paper shows in Fig. 5 and Table 2 that the Taylor exponential recovers both properties and matches softmax performance on associative recall (100% accuracy) and BERT CoLA conversion (58.4 vs. 58.8 Matthew's correlation).

**Why it fails as a practical solution:** the feature map dimension is $d' = 1 + d + d^2$, making the attention complexity $O(n d^3)$ rather than $O(n d^2)$. With typical head dimensions $d = 64$ or $d = 128$, the $d^2$ quadratic features (4096 or 16384 dimensions) make the computation prohibitively expensive. As the paper notes in Table 2, this creates an "efficiency-effectiveness tradeoff": Taylor achieves the expressivity but at unacceptable cost. Fig. 6 quantifies this: while both are $O(n)$ in sequence length, the Taylor approximation has much larger memory footprint and slower wall-clock time than FlashAttention due to the large $d^2$ dimension. This motivates Hedgehog: can we learn a more compact feature map that achieves similar expressivity?

#### Hedgehog MLP Feature Map Architecture

The Hedgehog feature map replaces fixed mathematical feature maps with a **trainable single-layer MLP** applied to each query and key vector independently. For a head dimension $d$, the feature map is:

$$\phi_{\text{mlp}}(x) = \Phi(W^\top x + b)$$

where $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is a learned weight matrix, $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is a learned bias vector, and $\Phi$ is an element-wise activation function. The paper explicitly states: "for the single-head attention setting, we compute $\phi_{\text{mlp}}(q_i)^\top \phi_{\text{mlp}}(k_j)$ with a simple one-layer MLP."

**The exponential activation for spikiness.** The paper sets $\Phi$ to the element-wise exponential function, making the feature map output strictly positive and capable of amplifying differences between feature dimensions. The base form (Eq. 3) is:

$$\phi_{\text{mlp}}(x) = \left[\exp(w_1^\top x + b), \exp(w_2^\top x + b), \ldots, \exp(w_d^\top x + b)\right]$$

where each $w_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a row of $W$. This is a $d$-dimensional output, producing feature vectors of dimension $d$.

**Why exponential activation:** the element-wise exponential serves the same function as in the softmax—amplifying relative differences. If a particular learned direction $w_i^\top x$ has a high value while others have low values, the corresponding feature $\exp(w_i^\top x)$ will dominate, creating spiky attention weights. This is directly analogous to how the scalar exponential in softmax creates spiky distributions, but applied in feature space rather than on scalar dot products. The paper's ablation in Table 4 and Fig. 8 confirms that replacing the exponential with ReLU (the T2R-HH ablation) produces worse attention weight matching, lower downstream performance (93.5% vs. 99.3% BERT performance recovery, Table 8), and less faithful attention weight reproduction (higher KL divergence, Table 14).

**The negation mapping for expressivity.** In practice, the paper extends the feature map to dimension $2d$ by concatenating the exponential of the positive and negative features (Eq. 6):

$$\phi_{\text{mlp}}(x) = \left[\exp(w_1^\top x + b), \ldots, \exp(w_d^\top x + b), \exp(-w_1^\top x - b), \ldots, \exp(-w_d^\top x - b)\right]$$

**Why negation mapping:** prior linear attention feature maps like ReLU discard negative information by zeroing out negative values. The paper notes that the negation mapping "intuitively lets us better factor in negative dimensionalities, which prior linear attention feature maps like ReLU ignore." By including both $\exp(w_i^\top x)$ and $\exp(-w_i^\top x)$ for each learned direction, the feature map can encode both the magnitude and sign of the projection onto each direction, enabling the dot product $\phi_{\text{mlp}}(q)^\top \phi_{\text{mlp}}(k)$ to better capture the full similarity structure. This is analogous to how the full softmax uses positive and negative dot products to determine relative attention, not just positive ones. The doubling from $d$ to $2d$ is a constant factor that does not change the asymptotic $O(n d^2)$ complexity.

**Numerical stability variant.** The paper also explores a numerically stable variant where $\Phi$ applies a softmax over the MLP output dimension before exponentiation (Eq. 5):

$$\phi_{\text{mlp}}(x) = \left[\frac{\exp(w_1^\top x)}{\sum_{i=1}^{d} \exp(w_i^\top x)}, \ldots, \frac{\exp(w_d^\top x)}{\sum_{i=1}^{d} \exp(w_i^\top x)}\right]$$

**Why this helps:** applying softmax over the $d$ feature dimensions bounds each feature between 0 and 1 and ensures they sum to 1, preventing numerical overflow from large $w_i^\top x$ values during training. The paper notes that this variant "seems to work but with better stability" and "also performing better than dividing each element by the max." This is a practical trick to make training more robust without changing the fundamental design—the relative relationships between features (which determine spikiness) are preserved under softmax normalization.

**Identity initialization.** The paper states that for training-from-scratch, "we initialize MLPs as identity matrices for Hedgehog feature maps." Specifically, the weight matrix $W$ is initialized to the identity matrix $\mathbb{I}$ and the bias $b$ to zero. This means that at initialization, $\phi_{\text{mlp}}(x)$ reduces to $\exp(x)$ element-wise (with negation mapping: $[\exp(x), \exp(-x)]$), which is essentially the temperature-scaled exponential feature map $\phi_1$ studied in Section 3.2. The model thus starts from a known spiky baseline and learns to adjust the feature directions during training.

**Why identity initialization:** it provides a sensible starting point that already possesses spikiness (since the exponential amplifies differences), avoiding the cold-start problem where a random initialization would produce near-uniform attention weights that degrade early training. The paper does not ablate this choice explicitly, but the logic follows from the associative recall results (Fig. 4): $\phi_1$ already produces some spikiness and non-trivial accuracy, providing a better initialization than a random MLP that might initially produce uniform weights and receive weak training signal.

#### Attention Weight Distillation Loss

The core training mechanism for Hedgehog in conversion settings is the attention weight distillation loss. Unlike training-from-scratch where the model learns end-to-end with task loss, conversion requires explicitly training the feature maps to reproduce the attention patterns of the original softmax model. The distillation loss is defined per attention head, per layer as the cross-entropy between the "student" linear attention weights and the "teacher" softmax attention weights.

For a single attention head with queries $\{q_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and keys $\{k_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$, the distillation loss for query $i$ is (Eq. 4):

$$L_i = -\sum_{j=1}^{i} \frac{\exp(q_i^\top k_j)}{\sum_{m=1}^{i} \exp(q_i^\top k_m)} \log\left(\frac{\phi_{\text{mlp}}(q_i)^\top \phi_{\text{mlp}}(k_j)}{\sum_{m=1}^{i} \phi_{\text{mlp}}(q_i)^\top \phi_{\text{mlp}}(k_m)}\right)$$

where the first fraction is the softmax attention weight (teacher distribution) for key $j$ given query $i$, and the second fraction is the normalized linear attention weight (student distribution). The outer sum runs over all keys $j$ up to position $i$ (causal attention).

**What it computes:** for each query position, the standard cross-entropy between a target categorical distribution (the softmax weights) and a predicted categorical distribution (the normalized feature map dot products). This is a pointwise KL divergence between two discrete probability distributions over the sequence positions, summed over all query positions in the batch. The total loss for training is the sum of $L_i$ over all queries, all attention heads, and all layers.

**Why cross-entropy rather than MSE:** the softmax attention weights form a valid probability distribution over keys (non-negative, sum to one). Cross-entropy is the proper scoring rule for comparing probability distributions—it heavily penalizes cases where the student assigns low probability to positions the teacher assigns high probability to (i.e., failing to attend to important tokens). This directly targets the spikiness property: to achieve low cross-entropy, the student must concentrate its probability mass on the same small set of tokens that the teacher does. MSE would treat all positions equally regardless of their importance in the teacher distribution, failing to incentivize spikiness.

**Why distill per head and per layer independently:** the paper applies "a separate MLP to each head and each layer, and uses the same $\phi_{\text{mlp}}$ for the queries and keys." Each attention head in a Transformer learns different attention patterns (some attend to syntax, some to semantics, some to position), and the optimal feature map to reproduce one head's attention distribution may differ from another's. Training independent MLPs per head allows specialization. Using the same $\phi_{\text{mlp}}$ for queries and keys (weight tying) reduces parameters and ensures the kernel $K(q, k) = \phi(q)^\top \phi(k)$ is symmetric in its treatment of queries and keys, which is a property of the softmax kernel $K(q, k) = \exp(q^\top k / \sqrt{d})$ that is symmetric when $q = k$.

**Joint training across layers.** The paper trains all Hedgehog layers jointly with a single optimizer, using "the sum of each individual attention head distillation loss as the final criterion." This means one forward pass through the full model computes the softmax attention weights at every layer and head, computes the corresponding linear attention weights, and accumulates gradients for all Hedgehog MLPs simultaneously before a single optimizer step. The implementation detail in Appendix A (Listing 2 and 3) shows how this is done with popular frameworks like HuggingFace Transformers: each attention layer is wrapped in a `HedgehogAttention` class that hooks both the teacher (softmax) and student (linear) attention weights to the model output, and the training loop sums distillation losses across all layers.

**Freezing original parameters during distillation.** During the distillation phase, the paper explicitly freezes all pretrained Transformer weights: "We first freeze the Transformer's original weights and specifically train the Hedgehog MLPs." This ensures that the distillation process does not alter the teacher distribution—the softmax weights remain fixed and the MLPs are optimized purely to match them. If the original parameters were trainable, the teacher distribution would shift during training, creating a moving target that would be harder to match and potentially degrading the original model's knowledge.

#### Two-Stage Conversion Procedure

For both finetuned-conversion and pretrained-conversion settings, the paper uses a two-stage procedure (Appendix A.3):

**Stage 1: Attention distillation.** The Hedgehog MLPs are inserted after the query and key projections of each attention head (one MLP per head per layer). With the original Transformer weights frozen, the MLPs are trained using the distillation loss described above on data from the target task or domain. For BERT conversion (Sec. 5.3), distillation uses samples from the specific GLUE task (e.g., CoLA). For GPT-2 conversion (Sec. 5.4), distillation uses WikiText-103 data. For Llama-2 conversion (Sec. 5.4), distillation uses SAMSum summarization data. Key hyperparameters: learning rate 0.01, zero weight decay, AdamW optimizer, trained for up to 5 epochs with early stopping on validation loss for BERT tasks; 2 epochs for GPT-2; 2 epochs for Llama-2 (with batch size 8 and gradient accumulation). The attention distillation training loop is described in pseudocode in Appendix A (Listings 1–3).

**What happens during distillation:** the model processes input sequences through both the original softmax attention and the linear attention paths. For each query in each head of each layer, the softmax attention weights (teacher) and linear attention weights (student) are both computed and stored. The cross-entropy between these distributions is summed across all queries, heads, and layers, and gradients flow only into the Hedgehog MLP parameters ($W$, $b$ for each MLP). After distillation, each MLP has learned to produce feature maps such that the dot product $\phi_{\text{mlp}}(q_i)^\top \phi_{\text{mlp}}(k_j)$, when normalized across keys, approximates the softmax weight for that query-key pair.

**Stage 2: Parameter finetuning.** Following attention distillation, the paper "simply unfreeze all model weights and train with a standard task-specific loss function." The Hedgehog MLPs can either remain trainable or be frozen; the paper explores both. For BERT conversion, all parameters are unfrozen and the model is finetuned with the original task loss (e.g., classification cross-entropy for GLUE tasks) using learning rate 1e-5, zero weight decay, AdamW optimizer, cosine scheduler, up to 5 epochs. For GPT-2 conversion, all parameters are finetuned with the language modeling cross-entropy loss on WikiText-103 using learning rate 6e-4, weight decay 0.01. For Llama-2 7B conversion, the paper uses parameter-efficient finetuning: LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) is applied to the query, key, value, and output projections, with rank 8 and alpha 16, training only the LoRA adapters while keeping the base model weights frozen.

**Why two-stage:** the paper does not explicitly ablate single-stage (joint distillation + task finetuning) vs. two-stage, but the logic follows from the conversion objective. In single-stage training, the model would need to simultaneously (1) learn to match softmax attention weights and (2) perform the downstream task. These objectives can conflict: early in training, the linear attention weights are poor approximations, leading to poor task performance and uninformative task gradients. By separating distillation (learn to mimic attention) from finetuning (learn to use that attention for the task), each stage has a clean optimization signal. Additionally, the distillation stage is parameter-efficient (only MLP weights are trained), making it fast relative to full-model finetuning.

**When Hedgehog MLPs remain frozen vs. trainable:** the paper does not systematically ablate this, but the default approach for pretrained-conversion (GPT-2, Llama-2) appears to train the MLPs during both stages. For training-from-scratch, the MLPs are trained end-to-end with the task loss from random initialization, with no separate distillation phase—the model learns to produce expressive attention weights directly from the task objective, implicitly discovering spiky patterns because the exponential activation biases the model toward them.

#### Training-from-Scratch Procedure

When training Hedgehog Transformers from scratch, the procedure simplifies to a single stage. The Hedgehog MLPs are inserted at every attention head and layer of a randomly initialized Transformer, and the entire model (MLPs + all standard Transformer parameters) is trained end-to-end with the task-specific loss function (e.g., next-token prediction cross-entropy for language modeling, classification loss for LRA tasks).

Key differences from the conversion procedure:

- **No distillation loss.** The model is trained only with the task objective, not with any explicit attention-matching loss. The paper argues that the exponential-activated MLP feature map naturally biases the model toward learning spiky attention patterns because the exponential amplifies relative differences in the learned feature directions.
- **Identity initialization.** The MLP weight matrices are initialized to the identity matrix, making the initial feature map equivalent to $\phi_1(x) = [\exp(x), \exp(-x)]$. This provides a sensible starting point that already has spikiness.
- **Joint optimization.** All parameters (MLP weights $W$, MLP biases $b$, query/key/value projection matrices, feedforward network parameters, embeddings) are optimized simultaneously with the same task loss.

The paper demonstrates this in Section 5.2 with two benchmarks: LRA (using the standard hyperparameter settings from Tay et al., 2021) and WikiText-103 (using a 125M GPT-2-style architecture with 12 layers, 12 heads, head dimension 64, hidden dimension 768, MLP dimension 3072, learning rate 6e-4, weight decay 0.01, AdamW optimizer).

**Why end-to-end training works without explicit distillation:** when training from scratch, the model is not trying to recover a pre-existing attention pattern—it is discovering attention patterns that work for the task. The exponential activation in the feature map creates an inductive bias toward spiky attention (because high feature dimensions get exponentially amplified), which the model can exploit by learning query and key projections that produce large dot products in the right feature dimensions for relevant key-value pairs. The model thus learns both the feature map parameters and the projection weights jointly to produce spiky, task-useful attention patterns without needing an external teacher.

#### Generalization Mechanisms and Context-Length Transfer

The paper investigates whether Hedgehog feature maps learned on one dataset or context length generalize to new data and longer sequences (Section 5.1, Tables 4–5, and Appendix C.2). This is critical for practical conversion because retraining feature maps for every new task or sequence length would be expensive.

**Cross-task generalization.** The paper trains Hedgehog feature maps by distilling BERT attention weights on either CoLA (a linguistic acceptability task) or WikiText-103 (general-domain language modeling) and evaluates how well the resulting MLPs match softmax attention weights when applied to different GLUE tasks (MRPC, MNLI, QNLI, QQP, RTE, SST-2, STS-B). Table 4 reports the KL divergence between the linear attention weights produced by these feature maps and the true softmax weights on each new task. Hedgehog trained on CoLA achieves KL divergence of 0.172 on CoLA (in-distribution) and 0.340–0.673 on other GLUE tasks—still substantially lower than 1+ELU (1.231–2.150), Performer (1.293–2.239), or cosFormer (1.191–2.398). Hedgehog trained on WikiText-103 shows similar generalization, with KL divergence 0.347–0.484 on the same non-CoLA tasks.

**Why this generalization occurs:** the feature maps are learning to match the *functional form* of softmax attention—how attention weights relate to query and key vectors—not memorizing specific attention patterns for specific inputs. Since the BERT model's query and key projections are frozen and shared across tasks, the mapping from $(q, k)$ vectors to attention weights follows the same functional relationship regardless of which task produced those vectors. The Hedgehog MLP learns to approximate this function, and to the extent that different tasks produce query and key vectors in similar regions of the representational space, the approximation transfers.

**Context-length transfer.** Table 5 shows that Hedgehog feature maps trained on CoLA samples at the default 512-token context maintain consistent KL divergence with softmax attention when evaluated on concatenated CoLA samples at lengths 256, 1024, 2048, and 4096 tokens (KL values: 0.182, 0.187, 0.190, 0.181 respectively). This is important because linear attention's primary advantage is efficiency on long sequences, so the feature maps must remain faithful at lengths the model was not distilled on.

**Why context-length transfer works:** the Hedgehog feature map $\phi_{\text{mlp}}$ is applied independently to each query and key vector. The attention weight for a specific $(q_i, k_j)$ pair depends only on these two vectors and the normalization across all keys $\sum_m \phi(q_i)^\top \phi(k_m)$, which is just a running sum that grows with sequence length. Since the feature map is a pointwise transformation, the quality of approximating softmax for any single query-key pair is independent of total sequence length—the only length-dependent effect is the normalization constant, which changes the absolute values but not the relative ordering induced by the feature map dot products. This property is not shared by all linear attention methods: some approaches that rely on sequence-length-dependent normalization (e.g., fixed positional re-weighting in cosFormer) would lose fidelity at unseen lengths.

#### Practical Implementation Architecture

The paper provides specific implementation details in Appendix A that are essential for reproducibility. The Hedgehog feature map is implemented as a `nn.Linear` layer with input and output dimension equal to the head dimension $d$, followed by the exponential activation and negation mapping concatenation. The weight matrix is initialized to the identity matrix using `nn.init.eye_`, and the bias is initialized to zeros.

The full model integration for conversion uses a wrapper `HedgehogAttention` class (Listing 2) that contains:
- The original (frozen) attention module (`base_attn`)
- Two Hedgehog feature maps (`mlp_q` and `mlp_k`) for queries and keys
- A `forward` method that runs both the original softmax attention (to get teacher weights) and the Hedgehog linear attention (to get student weights), and returns both sets of weights through the `output_attentions` mechanism

For joint distillation across all layers (Listing 3), the paper replaces each attention layer in the pretrained model with its `HedgehogAttention` wrapper, then runs a standard training loop where:
1. The model processes a batch with `output_attentions=True`
2. The output contains a tuple of `(predicted_attention, true_attention)` for each layer
3. The cross-entropy loss is computed for each layer and summed
4. Backpropagation updates only the Hedgehog MLP parameters (original model weights frozen)

This architecture makes the distillation process compatible with standard Transformer training infrastructure—no custom CUDA kernels or specialized attention implementations are required beyond the standard linear attention formula (Eq. 2), which can be implemented with standard matrix operations (cumulative sums and einsum operations).

**Parameter count overhead.** The paper reports that for Llama-2 7B, the Hedgehog MLPs add 0.495% of the original model size. This is because each MLP has $d \times d = 64 \times 64$ (for Llama-2's head dimension) weight parameters plus $d$ bias parameters per head, per layer. With 32 layers and 32 heads, this is roughly $32 \times 32 \times (4096 + 64) \approx 4.2$ million parameters, compared to Llama-2 7B's ~7 billion total. The $0.495\%$ overhead makes Hedgehog practical to add to existing models without substantially changing the model's footprint.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Learnable MLP feature maps** over fixed mathematical functions: enables data-driven optimization to match softmax attention patterns specific to the model and task, rather than relying on universal approximations that may fail on the particular query-key distributions of a trained model.
- **Element-wise exponential activation** over ReLU, ELU, or polynomial activations: directly induces spikiness by amplifying relative differences between feature dimensions, analogous to how the scalar exponential in softmax amplifies differences between dot products.
- **Negation mapping to $2d$ dimensions** over single-sided $d$-dimensional output: preserves negative information that ReLU-based maps discard, enabling the kernel to capture both positive and negative query-key similarities.
- **Cross-entropy distillation loss** over MSE or task-only training: enforces that the student distribution matches the teacher's concentration of probability mass on important tokens (spikiness), which MSE would not penalize as strongly.
- **Two-stage conversion** (distill then finetune) over joint training: separates the objectives of attention matching and task performance, preventing gradient conflict and allowing each stage to optimize a clean signal.
- **Identity initialization** over random initialization: provides a known spiky starting point that avoids the cold-start problem of near-uniform initial attention weights.
- **Per-head, per-layer independent MLPs** over shared feature maps: allows specialization to the different attention patterns that different heads and layers learn.
- **Frozen teacher during distillation** over trainable teacher: keeps the target attention distribution fixed, preventing the optimization from chasing a moving target and preserving the original model's learned attention structure.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing the Linear Attention Problem from Kernel Approximation to Distribution Distillation

The dominant paradigm in linear attention research, from Performer (Choromanski et al., 2020) through cosFormer (Qin et al., 2022b), treats the problem as **functional approximation**: find a kernel $K(q,k) = \phi(q)^\top\phi(k)$ that minimizes some notion of distance to $\exp(q^\top k / \sqrt{d})$. This framing leads naturally to fixed mathematical feature maps—random Fourier features for provable MSE bounds, polynomial expansions, locality-enhanced kernels—each designed to approximate the exponential function itself.

This paper makes a fundamental conceptual shift: **the target of approximation should be the attention weight distribution, not the exponential function.** The difference is subtle but consequential. Approximating the exponential function well in, say, the $L_2$ sense does not guarantee that the resulting attention weights replicate the distributional properties that make softmax expressive—specifically, the concentration of probability mass on a small set of relevant tokens (spikiness) and the smooth monotonic relationship between dot-product magnitude and attention weight. A feature map can have low average approximation error while systematically failing to produce sharp attention distributions, as the paper demonstrates with Performer (Fig. 2): despite theoretical guarantees on kernel approximation quality, the resulting attention weights are nearly as uniform as simple ReLU-based maps, and performance on associative recall is correspondingly poor (17% accuracy, Fig. 4).

The Hedgehog reframing treats the feature map as a **student to be trained via distillation** rather than a kernel to be designed. The objective shifts from "find a closed-form $\phi$ that approximates $\exp$" to "learn a $\phi$ such that the normalized dot products $\phi(q)^\top\phi(k) / \sum_m \phi(q)^\top\phi(k_m)$ match the softmax attention weights for this specific model on this specific data distribution." This is the same conceptual move that made knowledge distillation successful for model compression (Hinton et al., 2015): rather than designing a smaller architecture that analytically approximates a larger one, train the small model to match the large model's output distribution. The paper imports this insight into the linear attention domain, treating the attention mechanism as the "model" to be distilled.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond Hedgehog's specific architecture. It opens a new design space where any parameterized feature map can be plugged into linear attention and trained to match the softmax distribution, potentially using objectives beyond cross-entropy (e.g., contrastive losses, adversarial training). It also suggests that prior linear attention failures were not evidence that subquadratic attention is fundamentally limited—they were evidence that the *functional approximation* approach was asking the wrong question. The paper's Taylor exponential analysis (Section 4.1) provides a clean existence proof: there exist subquadratic feature maps that recover both spikiness and monotonicity (Table 2), proving the problem is solvable in principle. The challenge is finding *efficient* ones, which the learnable approach addresses.

**Evidence anchoring this claim:** The associative recall experiment (Fig. 4) shows that spikiness (low entropy) strongly correlates with accuracy, not kernel approximation quality—the spiky $\phi_2$ exponential map achieves 100% accuracy despite not approximating the softmax kernel in any rigorous sense, while the better kernel approximator (Performer) achieves 17%. The attention weight visualization (Fig. 7) directly shows that Hedgehog's learned feature map produces attention distributions that visually match softmax with higher fidelity than prior feature maps, and the ablation in Table 4 confirms that the distillation loss (not just the exponential MLP architecture) is necessary for this fidelity.

---

### Innovation 2: Identifying Spikiness and Monotonicity as the Diagnostic Properties That Explain the Linear Attention Performance Gap

Prior work on linear attention quality had accumulated a set of heuristics and partial explanations: positive weights are important (Katharopoulos et al., 2020), orthogonal features help (Choromanski et al., 2020), locality bias matters (Qin et al., 2022a). But the field lacked a **unified diagnostic framework** that could (1) explain *why* different linear attentions fail, (2) predict *which* tasks would expose these failures, and (3) guide *what* a solution must accomplish. The paper provides exactly this framework by distilling the performance gap to two measurable, causally connected properties.

This is a significant intellectual contribution independent of Hedgehog as a method. The paper doesn't merely claim these properties matter—it **operationalizes them** into measurable quantities (entropy for spikiness, monotonicity curves for the dot-product-weight relationship) and **demonstrates their causal role** through controlled experiments. The associative recall test (Section 3.2, Fig. 4) is particularly elegant: by sweeping across feature maps with varying spikiness (measured via entropy) and showing that accuracy tracks spikiness almost perfectly, the paper establishes that spikiness is not merely correlated with good performance but is a *mechanism* through which attention solves certain tasks. The task itself—recalling a specific key-value pair from context—requires selective attention by construction, so any attention mechanism that distributes weight uniformly across the sequence will fail regardless of other architectural choices.

The monotonicity diagnosis is equally crisp and addresses a phenomenon that prior work had observed but not systematically explained: why do linear attentions fail so dramatically at finetuned-conversion (Table 1 shows 1+ELU dropping BERT CoLA performance from 58.8 to 28.1 Matthew's correlation) even when they perform adequately in training-from-scratch settings? The paper's answer—non-monotonic attention weights create gradient misalignment with pretrained parameters—is a concrete mechanistic hypothesis that explains the asymmetry between the two training regimes. In training-from-scratch, the model learns query and key projections jointly with the attention mechanism, so it can adapt to whatever monotonicity properties the feature map provides. In conversion, the projections are frozen and carry implicit assumptions about how dot products map to attention weights; violating those assumptions creates optimization conflict.

This is a case where a **negative diagnostic result drives positive progress**. By identifying *specific properties* that prior methods lack, rather than just observing aggregate performance gaps, the paper provides a clear specification for what any solution must achieve. The Taylor exponential analysis (Section 4.1) then serves as a constructive proof that both properties are simultaneously achievable with subquadratic computation—closing the loop from diagnosis to existence proof.

**Why this is not incremental:** prior papers proposed *additional* heuristics for better linear attention (positive weights, orthogonality, locality) without explaining why those heuristics helped or why they sometimes didn't. This paper provides a *reduction*: the performance gap can be understood through two properties, and many prior heuristics can be reinterpreted as partial, incomplete attempts to achieve one or both of these properties (e.g., ReLU's positivity helps spikiness slightly but doesn't induce it; cosFormer's locality is a task-specific approximation to the selectivity that spikiness provides generally). This is diagnostic theory-building, not just method development.

**Evidence anchoring this claim:** Fig. 4 (associative recall accuracy vs. entropy) shows the causal role of spikiness. Fig. 3 (attention weight vs. dot product for trained BERT) shows monotonicity for softmax and its absence for prior linear attentions. Table 1 shows the performance consequences of missing monotonicity in conversion. The combination of these three pieces—measurement, causal demonstration, and performance linkage—constitutes a rare example of mechanistic interpretability applied to architecture design rather than post-hoc explanation.

### Innovation 3: Demonstrating That Learned Attention Mimicry Enables Viable Pretrained-Conversion of Large Language Models

The paper's most striking result is not that Hedgehog works for training-from-scratch or finetuned-conversion—those regimes were known to be addressable, at least partially, by prior methods (Kasai et al., 2021; Mao, 2022). The genuinely new capability is **pretrained-conversion of large-scale Transformers into functional linear attention variants.** The Llama-2 7B result (Table 11) stands out: Hedgehog-Llama2 achieves 47.4 ROUGE-1 on SAMSum summarization (vs. 51.1 for standard attention with LoRA), while the prior Transformer-to-RNN method produces a model that "struggles to produce coherent text" and achieves 2.8 ROUGE-1—a 16.5 point *drop* from the standard attention baseline. The sample generations in Appendix C.3 make this vivid: T2R outputs degenerate into repetitive non-text ("along recently acquired the biggest, I'tch. S C H P..."), while Hedgehog produces coherent summaries ("Hannah is looking for Betty's number. Amanda will text her.").

This matters because pretrained-conversion of LLMs was previously considered borderline infeasible. The theoretical results using SETH (Alman & Song, 2023; Keles et al., 2023) suggested that high-quality subquadratic attention approximation might be fundamentally impossible for large $n$. Prior practical attempts either (1) retained some quadratic attention modules (Kasai et al., 2021), partially defeating the efficiency purpose, or (2) produced models with severe quality degradation that made them unusable for generation tasks (the T2R Llama-2 results in this paper). The Hedgehog result challenges the pessimistic interpretation of the SETH-based theory: while exact approximation may be impossible, **learned distribution matching with task-specific finetuning can produce practically useful models** even at the 7B scale.

The significance is amplified by the training regime: Hedgehog-Llama2 7B is trained with LoRA (rank 8, alpha 16) on a single A6000 GPU, with the Hedgehog MLPs adding only 0.495% to the model's parameter count. This is a practical recipe for converting existing LLMs, not a resource-intensive retraining process. The combination of distillation (learning to match softmax attention) and parameter-efficient finetuning (adapting to the downstream task) makes Hedgehog viable for teams that cannot afford full-model training of LLMs.

The paper also shows that pretrained-conversion with Hedgehog achieves **state-of-the-art results among subquadratic models.** The 125M Hedgehog-GPT-2 achieves 16.7 perplexity on WikiText-103 (Table 10), outperforming purpose-built subquadratic architectures like H3 (18.5) and Hyena (18.5) that were designed and trained from scratch for language modeling. This is noteworthy because H3 and Hyena represent substantial engineering efforts to build efficient sequence models, while Hedgehog simply converts an existing GPT-2 checkpoint by training small MLPs. The 1.8 perplexity gap over the next-best subquadratic model is substantial—it roughly equals the improvement from scaling a Transformer from 125M to ~200M parameters.

**Why this is a fundamental shift rather than incremental:** prior work on efficient attention and state-space models (H3, Hyena, Mamba) operated on the assumption that matching Transformer quality required novel architectures purpose-built for efficiency. Hedgehog demonstrates that the knowledge already encoded in pretrained quadratic Transformers can be *transferred* to a linear attention form through learned mimicry, achieving better results than architectures designed from scratch for efficiency. This inverts the usual relationship: instead of designing efficient architectures and hoping they can be trained to match Transformers, start with a Transformer and teach an efficient mechanism to imitate it. The 7B-scale results suggest this approach scales to modern LLM sizes.

**Evidence anchoring this claim:** Table 10 (GPT-2 conversion vs. H3/Hyena), Table 11 (Llama-2 7B conversion vs. T2R and softmax baselines), and the sample generations in Appendix C.3 that qualitatively demonstrate coherent text from Hedgehog-Llama2 vs. degenerate output from T2R-Llama2.

### Innovation 4: The Taylor Exponential as an Existence Proof Separating Expressivity from Efficiency in Linear Attention

The paper's Taylor exponential analysis (Section 4.1) is easy to overlook as a minor negative result—it works but is too expensive—but it serves a crucial intellectual function: it **decomposes the linear attention problem into an expressivity component and an efficiency component**, showing that these are separable and that the former is solvable. This decomposition is not obvious a priori. One might have hypothesized that the quality gap was inherent to subquadratic attention—that the $O(n^2 d)$ cost of computing all pairwise interactions is *necessary* for the expressive properties (spikiness, monotonicity) that make attention effective. The SETH-based theory (Alman & Song, 2023) could be read as supporting this pessimistic view.

The Taylor exponential provides a clean counterexample: a feature map that is manifestly subquadratic in $n$ (it processes each token independently via $\phi_{\text{taylor}}$ and accumulates via Eq. 2, requiring $O(n d^3)$ time) while matching softmax on both diagnostic properties (Fig. 5) and downstream performance (100% associative recall accuracy, 58.4 vs. 58.8 Matthew's correlation on BERT CoLA conversion, Table 2). The problem with the Taylor approximation is entirely about the constant factor—$d' = 1 + d + d^2$ dimensions make it impractical for realistic head dimensions—not about any fundamental limitation of subquadratic attention.

This insight reframes what Hedgehog needs to accomplish. Rather than solving an open theoretical question about whether linear attention can be expressive, Hedgehog solves an **engineering problem**: can we learn a compact feature map (dimension $2d$ rather than $d^2$) that achieves similar expressivity? The Taylor exponential demonstrates that the target is achievable; Hedgehog shows that learning can find a more efficient path to it. This is a case where a "failed" method (Taylor, too expensive) provides the intellectual foundation for a successful one by proving the problem is well-posed.

The paper's handling of this is noteworthy for its intellectual honesty. Many papers would either omit the Taylor analysis entirely (since it doesn't lead to a practical method) or present it as a strawman. Instead, the paper uses it to **sharpen the contribution**: by showing that expressivity is achievable but at unacceptable cost, it precisely locates Hedgehog's contribution in the efficiency dimension. The $O(n d^2)$ complexity of Hedgehog versus $O(n d^3)$ of Taylor is not just a quantitative improvement—it's the difference between impracticality and deployment on real hardware (Fig. 6 shows Taylor using more memory and slower wall-clock time than FlashAttention despite being $O(n)$ in theory, while Hedgehog achieves near 6× faster inference).

**Why this is conceptually significant beyond the paper:** the separation of expressivity from efficiency in linear attention suggests a broader design principle for efficient deep learning: when a complex operation (softmax attention) has desirable properties (spikiness, monotonicity), first verify that there exists an efficient alternative with those properties (Taylor), then design a *learned* efficient alternative that recovers them from data (Hedgehog). This pattern—existence proof via expensive exact approximation, followed by learned efficient approximation—could apply to other bottlenecks beyond attention.

**Evidence anchoring this claim:** Table 2 (Taylor matches softmax on both properties and performance but at $O(n d^3)$ cost), Fig. 5 (Taylor recovers spikiness and monotonicity), Fig. 6 (real-world efficiency comparison showing Taylor's impracticality despite $O(n)$ scaling).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses three primary benchmarks: (1) **Long Range Arena (LRA)** (Tay et al., 2021) for training-from-scratch sequence classification across five tasks (ListOps, Text, Retrieval, Image, Pathfinder); (2) **WikiText-103** (Merity et al., 2017) for training-from-scratch and pretrained-conversion language modeling, evaluated on perplexity over 1024-token contexts; (3) **GLUE** benchmark (Wang et al., 2018) for finetuned-conversion evaluation of BERT-base models on eight natural language understanding tasks (CoLA, SST-2, MRPC, STS-B, QQP, MNLI, QNLI, RTE), plus **ImageNet-1K** for ViT-B/16 conversion; (4) **SAMSum** (Gliwa et al., 2019) for Llama-2 7B pretrained-conversion summarization, evaluated via ROUGE-1/2/L metrics. Additionally, a synthetic **Associative Recall (AR)** dataset (Ba et al., 2016) with 40 tokens, 128-token sequences, 10,000 training and 2,000 test samples is used for diagnostic experiments in Sections 3.2 and 5.1.

- **Base model(s).** Multiple model families are used across regimes: **BERT-base-uncased** (110M parameters, 12 layers, 12 heads, head dimension 64) for finetuned-conversion GLUE experiments, sourced from HuggingFace finetuned checkpoints; **GPT-2 125M** (12 layers, 12 heads, head dimension 64, hidden dimension 768) for pretrained-conversion and training-from-scratch WikiText-103 experiments; **ViT-B/16** (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020) for ImageNet-1K conversion; **Llama-2 7B** (32 layers, 32 heads, head dimension 128) for the largest-scale pretrained-conversion test on SAMSum; and custom 4-layer Transformers with 4 heads for associative recall diagnostics. The diversity spans bidirectional encoders, autoregressive decoders, vision Transformers, and large language models, chosen to test Hedgehog across architectures and scales. For scaling laws comparison (Section 7 equivalent in this paper), no explicit FLOPs-matched pretraining-vs-inference study is conducted—the closest analog is the 125M GPT-2 comparison in Table 10.

- **Metrics.** **Perplexity** on WikiText-103 for language modeling quality; **accuracy (%)** for LRA classification tasks (exact-match string comparison for ListOps, Text, Retrieval; standard classification accuracy for Image and Pathfinder); **Matthew's correlation** for CoLA, **F1 score** for MRPC, **Spearman correlation** for STS-B, and **accuracy** for SST-2, QQP, MNLI, QNLI, RTE on GLUE; **ROUGE-1/2/L F-scores** for SAMSum summarization; **KL divergence** (relative to softmax attention weights) for attention matching fidelity in ablation analyses; and **wall-clock time** (milliseconds) and **memory usage** (GB) for efficiency benchmarking. The paper also reports a "% Recovery" metric in Table 8, computed as the ratio of the converted model's performance to the original finetuned BERT's performance, averaged across GLUE tasks.

- **Baselines.** For training-from-scratch LRA: standard **Transformer** (Vaswani et al., 2017), **Linear Transformer** (Katharopoulos et al., 2020), **Performer** (Choromanski et al., 2020), **Reformer** (Kitaev et al., 2020), **Sparse Transformer**, **Sinkhorn Transformer**, **Linformer** (Wang et al., 2020), **Synthesizer**, **Longformer**, **BigBird**, **Nyströmformer** (Xiong et al., 2021), **cosFormer** (Qin et al., 2022b), and **Skyformer** (Chen et al., 2021). For WikiText-103 training-from-scratch: **Transformer**, **Performer**, **Reformer**, **AFT** (Zhai et al., 2021), and **1+ELU** (Katharopoulos et al., 2020). For finetuned-conversion: **Transformer-to-RNN (T2R)** (Kasai et al., 2021) with its ReLU-based feature map, plus an ablation **T2R-HH** where T2R's feature map is trained with Hedgehog's distillation loss. For pretrained-conversion GPT-2: **T2R-GPT-2**, **Hybrid H3** (Fu et al., 2023), **Hyena** (Poli et al., 2023), and standard finetuned GPT-2. For Llama-2 conversion: **T2R (LoRA)** and **Softmax (LoRA/Zero-shot)**. For associative recall diagnostics: **1+ELU**, **Performer**, **cosFormer**, and **exp(t=1)** and **exp(t=2)** temperature-scaled exponential feature maps.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a unified generation budget since Hedgehog's linear attention has a fundamentally different cost structure ($O(n d^2)$) from standard attention ($O(n^2 d)$), and compute comparisons are inherently per-sequence-length. Efficiency is measured in **wall-clock time and memory** for a single attention layer with 12 heads and head dimension 64 on sequences up to 32K tokens (Fig. 6), benchmarking Hedgehog against FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) and the Taylor exponential approximation. For model comparisons, **total perplexity or accuracy at matched model size** (125M parameters for GPT-2/WikiText-103, 110M for BERT/GLUE, standard LRA model configurations) serves as the equalizing metric—all models compared use the same architecture and parameter count, differing only in the attention mechanism. No separate FLOPs budget analysis is performed for the test-time compute scaling that dominates Section 7 of the example summary.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For training-from-scratch experiments, standard train/validation/test splits are used as provided by each benchmark (LRA official splits, WikiText-103 standard splits, associative recall 10K/2K train/test). For finetuned-conversion and pretrained-conversion, no cross-validation is applied—models are distilled and finetuned on the target task's training set, and evaluated on the held-out validation or test set provided by the benchmark. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests for any result, relying instead on the magnitude of gaps reported (e.g., 6 perplexity points, 8.7 GLUE score points) and qualitative visualizations. The associative recall experiments sweep learning rates and weight decay values with early stopping, but report single best configurations rather than averages.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Diagnostic Experiments: Spikiness, Monotonicity, and Performance

The paper opens its experimental section by validating the causal connection between its two hypothesized properties and downstream performance before introducing Hedgehog as a solution. The associative recall experiment (Section 3.2, Fig. 4) establishes the spikiness-performance link: softmax attention achieves 100% accuracy with attention weight entropy near zero, while prior linear attentions (1+ELU, Performer, cosFormer, $\exp(t=1)$) cluster around 17% accuracy with substantially higher entropy (the figure plots accuracy vs. entropy across methods, showing a near-monotonic relationship). The temperature-scaled $\exp(t=2)$ breaks this pattern—it achieves 100% accuracy while maintaining low entropy, confirming that spikiness (induced by the exponential's amplification) is sufficient to solve selective attention tasks even without softmax normalization. The paper does not report exact entropy values numerically, only visualizing them in Fig. 2 and Fig. 4.

The finetuned-conversion experiment (Section 3.2, Table 1) isolates the monotonicity requirement. A BERT-base model finetuned on CoLA achieves 58.8 Matthew's correlation with softmax attention. Swapping to 1+ELU drops performance to 28.1, ReLU to 39.5, Performer to 24.7, cosFormer to 39.9, and $\exp(t=1)$ to 45.9—all substantial degradations. The spiky $\exp(t=2)$ map recovers to 50.0, better than other linear attentions but still 8.8 points below softmax. The paper attributes this remaining gap to insufficient monotonicity (Fig. 3), as $\exp(t=2)$ produces attention weights that generally increase with dot products but with sharp nonlinearities that create gradient misalignment during finetuning. This contrasts with the Taylor exponential (Table 2), which achieves 58.4—near-perfect recovery—because it is both spiky and smoothly monotonic (Fig. 5).

#### Benchmarking Hedgehog Expressivity and Efficiency (Section 5.1)

The paper first verifies that Hedgehog matches softmax on the same diagnostic tasks before scaling to full benchmarks. On associative recall (Table 3), Hedgehog achieves 100% accuracy, matching softmax and the Taylor exponential while maintaining $O(n d^2)$ complexity versus Taylor's $O(n d^3)$. On BERT finetuned-conversion for CoLA (Table 3), Hedgehog achieves 59.2 Matthew's correlation—slightly exceeding the original softmax BERT's 58.8 (the paper does not comment on this being above 100% recovery, which may reflect finetuning variance on a small dataset). This corresponds to Hedgehog recovering both the spiky (Fig. 2, rightmost panel) and monotonic (Fig. 3, rightmost panel) properties observed in softmax attention.

**Efficiency benchmarking** (Fig. 6): Hedgehog achieves near 6× faster inference than FlashAttention at sequence length 32K for a single 12-head attention layer with head dimension 64, with similar memory usage. The Taylor exponential, despite being $O(n)$ in theory, takes significantly more memory and slower wall-clock time than FlashAttention across all sequence lengths due to the $d^2$ feature dimension blowup—this concretely demonstrates why polynomial approximations fail as practical solutions despite their theoretical expressivity.

**Attention weight fidelity** (Fig. 7, Table 4): When distilling BERT attention weights on CoLA samples, Hedgehog produces attention weight heatmaps that visually match softmax with much higher fidelity than 1+ELU, Performer, or cosFormer (Fig. 7). Quantitatively, the KL divergence between Hedgehog's linear attention weights and softmax attention on CoLA is 0.172 (Table 4 in the main text, expanded in Appendix Table 14), compared to 1.231 for 1+ELU, 1.293 for Performer, and 1.191 for cosFormer. The ablation analysis (Fig. 8) reveals that both the Hedgehog feature map architecture and the distillation loss are necessary: using the T2R feature map with distillation loss (T2R-HH, KL divergence 0.191 on CoLA) improves over untrained Hedgehog (KL 0.694) and untrained baselines, but still underperforms the full Hedgehog (KL 0.172). This shows that the exponential activation provides inductive bias toward the right attention distributions, and distillation provides the optimization signal to realize it.

**Generalization to new tasks** (Table 4, Table 5, Appendix Table 15): Hedgehog feature maps trained on one dataset transfer to new tasks with minimal degradation. Hedgehog trained on CoLA achieves KL divergence 0.172 in-distribution, and 0.340–0.673 on other GLUE tasks (MNLI, MRPC, QNLI)—still substantially lower than any prior method (1+ELU: 1.231–2.491, Performer: 1.293–2.594, cosFormer: 1.191–2.398). Hedgehog trained on WikiText-103 (general-domain language data) shows similar cross-task generalization (KL 0.347–0.484 on GLUE tasks). This generalization translates to downstream performance (Appendix Table 15): Hedgehog-CoLA BERT achieves 89.4 F1 on MRPC, 87.7 accuracy on QNLI, 89.8 accuracy on QQP when finetuned on those tasks, versus 1+ELU's 81.9, 78.5, and 89.1 respectively. **Context-length generalization** (Table 5) shows Hedgehog maintains consistent KL divergence (0.181–0.190) when evaluated on sequences from 256 to 4096 tokens—up to 8× the training context length—confirming the feature map's length-invariant property.

#### Training-from-Scratch: LRA and WikiText-103 (Section 5.2)

**LRA classification** (Table 6, expanded in Appendix Table 13): Hedgehog achieves 59.66% average accuracy across the five LRA tasks, the **best among all compared Transformer and subquadratic attention variants**. The next best is Skyformer at 59.50% (results from the original paper, not re-run), and Nyströmformer at 58.95%. On individual tasks: Image at 82.24% (best, matching Skyformer), Retrieval at 82.24% (second to Skyformer's 82.06%), Pathfinder at 74.16% (below Performer's 77.05% but above most others), ListOps at 37.15% (matching Nyströmformer, below Skyformer's 39.25%), Text at 64.60% (competitive with Skyformer's 64.70%). Table 13 provides a fuller comparison including Local Attention (46.06% avg), Linear Transformer (50.55%), Reformer (50.67%), Sinkhorn Transformer (51.29%), Linformer (51.36%), Performer (51.41%), Longformer (53.46%), and BigBird (55.01%). The paper explicitly notes that non-Transformer state-space models (Gu et al., 2021) achieve higher scores than all attention methods on LRA, but restricts comparison to attention-based approaches since Hedgehog targets attention approximation specifically.

**WikiText-103 language modeling** (Table 7): Hedgehog achieves 20.8 perplexity on a 125M decoder-only GPT-2-style Transformer, compared to 18.6 for standard softmax attention and 25.6–28.2 for prior linear attentions (Performer: 26.8, Reformer: 25.6, AFT: 28.2, 1+ELU: 25.6). The paper frames this as "closing the gap by 68.6%": the gap between softmax (18.6) and the best prior linear attention (25.6, both Reformer and 1+ELU) is 7.0 perplexity points, and Hedgehog's 20.8 reduces this gap to 2.2 points, meaning (7.0 - 2.2) / 7.0 = 68.6% of the gap is closed. This 4.8 perplexity improvement over the nearest prior linear attention is described as "up to 6 PPL" in the abstract, which appears to reference the gap to the weakest prior methods (AFT at 28.2 gives 28.2 - 20.8 = 7.4, though "up to 6" is a conservative rounding). The residual 2.2 perplexity gap to softmax remains—the paper does not ablate whether this is due to Hedgehog's feature map expressivity limitations, the $d' = 2d$ capacity, or the training-from-scratch procedure (which lacks explicit distillation).

#### Finetuned-Conversion: BERT on GLUE and ViT on ImageNet (Section 5.3)

**BERT GLUE conversion** (Table 8): Hedgehog recovers 99.3% of original finetuned BERT-base performance averaged across 8 GLUE tasks. The detailed per-task breakdown: CoLA Matthew's correlation 59.2 (vs. BERT-FT 58.8, 100.7% recovery—above 100% likely due to finetuning variance), SST-2 accuracy 92.6 (vs. 93.2, 99.4%), MRPC F1 90.1 (vs. 90.2, 99.9%), STS-B Spearman 87.4 (vs. 88.8, 98.4%), QQP accuracy 91.0 (vs. 91.0, 100.0%—exact recovery), MNLI accuracy 82.6 (vs. 84.7, 97.5%), QNLI accuracy 89.6 (vs. 91.3, 98.1%), RTE accuracy 69.3 (vs. 68.2, 101.6%—above original). The T2R baseline recovers only 88.9% on average (CoLA 43.6, SST-2 87.7, MRPC 83.0, STS-B 78.6, QQP 86.7, MNLI 78.9, QNLI 84.6, RTE 54.1), with particularly severe drops on CoLA and RTE. The T2R-HH ablation (training T2R's ReLU feature map with Hedgehog's distillation loss) achieves 93.5% recovery—a substantial improvement over T2R's 88.9%, confirming that attention weight distillation provides benefits beyond Hedgehog's specific architecture. However, T2R-HH still underperforms full Hedgehog (93.5% vs. 99.3%), showing that the exponential feature map architecture contributes independently beyond the distillation objective.

**ViT-B/16 ImageNet-1K conversion** (Table 9): Hedgehog achieves 79.5% top-1 accuracy versus 80.3% for the original ViT-B/16, a 99.0% recovery rate. T2R-HH (the stronger ablation baseline) achieves 77.0%. The paper presents this as evidence that Hedgehog "can also apply to other modalities" beyond text.

#### Pretrained-Conversion: GPT-2 and Llama-2 (Section 5.4)

**GPT-2 125M on WikiText-103** (Table 10): Hedgehog-GPT-2 achieves 16.7 perplexity, representing a new state-of-the-art for 125M-parameter subquadratic sequence models. For comparison: standard finetuned GPT-2 achieves 15.8 (fully quadratic, serving as an upper efficiency bound at this scale), zero-shot GPT-2 achieves 28.0, T2R-GPT-2 achieves 19.4, purpose-built subquadratic architectures Hybrid H3 and Hyena both achieve 18.5. The gap between Hedgehog-GPT-2 (16.7) and finetuned GPT-2 (15.8) is 0.9 perplexity points, while the gap to the next-best subquadratic model (H3/Hyena at 18.5) is 1.8 perplexity points. Worth noting: H3 and Hyena were trained from scratch on WikiText-103, while Hedgehog-GPT-2 benefits from GPT-2's general-domain pretraining. The paper does not compare against subquadratic models that are also pretrained on larger corpora before evaluation on WikiText-103, which would be a fairer comparison.

**Llama-2 7B on SAMSum summarization** (Table 11): Standard softmax Llama-2 7B with LoRA finetuning achieves 51.1/27.6/43.5 ROUGE-1/2/L, while zero-shot softmax Llama-2 achieves 19.3/6.8/14.9 (the large gap confirms SAMSum is a challenging task for 7B models without finetuning). Hedgehog-Llama2 achieves 47.4/23.4/39.1—retaining 92.8% of the ROUGE-1 score and producing coherent summaries (sample generations in Appendix C.3: "Hannah is looking for Betty's number. Amanda will text her."). In stark contrast, T2R-Llama2 achieves 2.8/0.0/2.6, with the paper stating it "struggles to produce coherent text" and sample generations confirming degenerate outputs ("along recently acquired the biggest, I'tch. S C H P..."). The ROUGE-1 improvement of Hedgehog over T2R is 44.6 points (47.4 - 2.8), making this the largest relative gain in the paper and demonstrating that learnable feature maps scale to modern LLM sizes. The paper also notes that Hedgehog feature maps add only 0.495% to Llama-2 7B's parameter count, making the conversion practical.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Feature map architecture: T2R-HH (ReLU + distillation) vs. Hedgehog (exponential + distillation)**: Table 8 shows T2R-HH recovers 93.5% of BERT GLUE performance vs. Hedgehog's 99.3%, confirming the exponential activation provides expressivity benefits beyond distillation alone. Table 4 shows T2R-HH achieves KL divergence 0.191 on CoLA attention weights vs. Hedgehog's 0.172, with larger gaps on out-of-distribution tasks (MRPC: 1.124 vs. 0.663; QNLI: 1.141 vs. 0.673), suggesting the exponential feature map generalizes better. The paper does not ablate alternative activations (e.g., softplus, GELU, higher-order polynomials) that might also induce spikiness, leaving open whether exponential is uniquely suited.

**Distillation necessity: Hedgehog with vs. without attention distillation**: Table 4 shows that untrained Hedgehog MLPs (initialized as identity, "HH No Train") achieve KL divergence 0.694 on CoLA attention weights—better than prior linear attentions (1+ELU: 1.231, Performer: 1.293, cosFormer: 1.191) but substantially worse than distilled Hedgehog (0.172). On downstream GLUE performance (Appendix Table 15), untrained Hedgehog achieves CoLA Matthew's correlation 50.3 versus distilled Hedgehog's 58.4—the 8.1 point gap shows distillation is critical for conversion quality. This is reinforced by the associative recall results: Table 3 shows Hedgehog trained end-to-end (without distillation, since no teacher exists) achieves 100% accuracy, but this is training-from-scratch where query/key projections adapt jointly with the MLP.

**Distillation data source: CoLA vs. WikiText-103**: Tables 4 and 14 compare Hedgehog feature maps distilled on task-specific data (CoLA) versus general-domain data (WikiText-103 chunks). On in-distribution CoLA attention matching, CoLA-trained Hedgehog achieves KL 0.172 vs. WT-103-trained Hedgehog's 0.357—the task-specific distillation is better, as expected. However, on downstream GLUE tasks (Appendix Table 15), the gap narrows: CoLA-trained Hedgehog achieves 58.4 CoLA Matthew's correlation (in-distribution) while WT-103-trained achieves 47.2, but on MRPC both achieve 89.4, on QNLI WT-103 achieves slightly better (89.2 vs. 87.7), and on QQP WT-103 achieves 90.4 vs. 89.8. This suggests Hedgehog distilled on general-domain data can serve as a reasonable universal feature map for task transfer, though the paper does not test this at scale (e.g., distilling on a large corpus like C4 and evaluating on many downstream tasks).

**Context-length generalization**: Table 5 shows Hedgehog attention matching fidelity (KL divergence with softmax) remains stable at 0.181–0.190 when evaluated on concatenated sequences from 256 to 4096 tokens, despite being trained on 512-token CoLA samples. The paper does not report downstream task performance at these extended lengths, only attention weight KL divergence. This leaves open whether the maintained attention fidelity actually translates to maintained task performance at 8× training length—a critical question for the long-context motivation of linear attention.

**Vision modality transfer**: Table 9 shows Hedgehog achieves 79.5% top-1 accuracy on ViT-B/16 ImageNet-1K versus 80.3% for the original model and 77.0% for T2R-HH. The paper does not show attention weight visualizations or KL divergence metrics for ViT, treating this as a single-point validation rather than a deep analysis. The single-modality text focus leaves open whether the spikiness and monotonicity properties are equally important for vision Transformers, where attention patterns differ substantially (often more global and less sparse).

**Training-from-scratch with identity initialization**: The paper states that Hedgehog MLPs are initialized to the identity matrix for training-from-scratch, but does not ablate this against random initialization (e.g., Xavier, Kaiming). Given that identity initialization makes the initial feature map equivalent to $\exp(x)$ element-wise (already spiky), this likely provides a substantial advantage over random initialization that might produce near-uniform initial attention weights and struggle to escape. The training-from-scratch results (Table 7) should therefore be interpreted as contingent on this initialization choice.

**Numerical stability mechanism**: The paper mentions exploring softmax-normalized feature maps (Eq. 5) for better numerical stability but does not ablate this against the base exponential form (Eq. 3) in terms of training convergence, attention fidelity, or downstream performance. The Llama-2 7B experiments use bfloat16 precision, where numerical stability is particularly relevant—the paper notes they "use non-quantized model weights in bfloat16" but doesn't specify whether the softmax-normalized or base exponential form was used for those experiments.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: Hedgehog "recovers over 99% of standard Transformer quality in finetuned-conversion" (Abstract, Section 5.3).** This claim is supported for BERT-base on GLUE (Table 8: 99.3% average recovery across 8 tasks) and hinted at for ViT-B/16 (Table 9: 99.0% on ImageNet-1K), but several qualifications are necessary. First, the 99% figure is an average—individual tasks range from 97.5% (MNLI) to 101.6% (RTE), and a few tasks driving the average high could mask tasks where performance degrades more. Second, the BERT-base model is 110M parameters; whether 99% recovery scales to larger BERT variants (BERT-large, 340M) or other bidirectional encoders is untested. Third, the ViT result is a single data point—99% recovery on ImageNet-1K classification does not guarantee similar recovery on detection, segmentation, or other vision tasks where attention patterns differ. Fourth, the GLUE benchmark has known saturation issues where many models cluster at near-ceiling performance; the meaningful test of finetuned-conversion is whether Hedgehog can handle tasks where performance is far from ceiling and attention quality matters more. The CoLA result (59.2 vs. 58.8—above 100% recovery) looks like noise rather than genuine improvement, which is fine for a "no degradation" claim but raises questions about statistical robustness. The paper would be strengthened by reporting confidence intervals or multiple runs.

**Claim 2: Hedgehog "closes the linear attention gap by 68.6% on WikiText-103 language modeling" (Abstract, Section 5.2).** This claim is supported by Table 7 (20.8 vs. 18.6 softmax vs. 25.6 best prior linear attention), but the framing is sensitive to which baseline is chosen as "the gap." If the gap is measured to the best prior linear attention (25.6 for both Reformer and 1+ELU), closing from 25.6 to 20.8 represents a 4.8 point improvement out of a 7.0 point gap, or 68.6%. If measured to Performer (26.8), it's 6.0 out of 8.2 (73.2% closed). If measured to AFT (28.2), it's 7.4 out of 9.6 (77.1% closed). More fundamentally, the comparison is against prior linear attentions that were not designed for this exact 125M GPT-2-style architecture—Reformer and Performer were developed in different contexts, and their hyperparameters (feature dimension, random feature count) may not be optimized for this specific setting. A fairer baseline might be to retune prior methods for this exact architecture and training budget. The residual 2.2 perplexity gap to softmax remains unexplained—the paper does not ablate whether this comes from the $d' = 2d$ feature dimension being insufficient, the absence of distillation in training-from-scratch (the model must discover spiky patterns end-to-end rather than being taught them), or fundamental expressivity limits of the feature map.

**Claim 3: Hedgehog achieves "state-of-the-art 16.7 perplexity on WikiText-103 for 125M subquadratic decoder models" (Abstract, Section 5.4).** This claim is supported by Table 10 (Hedgehog-GPT-2: 16.7 vs. H3: 18.5 vs. Hyena: 18.5), but there is a significant confounding factor: Hedgehog-GPT-2 benefits from GPT-2's pretraining (on WebText, a much larger and higher-quality corpus than WikiText-103), while H3 and Hyena are trained from scratch only on WikiText-103. This is not a like-for-like comparison. A fair comparison would either (1) train H3/Hyena on a larger pretraining corpus first, then finetune on WikiText-103, or (2) train Hedgehog from random initialization on WikiText-103 only (which would be the 20.8 perplexity result from Table 7, now worse than H3/Hyena's 18.5). The paper acknowledges this implicitly by listing "GPT-2 FT" (15.8) and "GPT-2" zero-shot (28.0) as reference points, but the "state-of-the-art for subquadratic models" framing overstates the result. What the result genuinely shows is that **converting a pretrained Transformer yields better subquadratic models than training subquadratic architectures from scratch on WikiText-103 alone**, which is a different (and still valuable) claim: pretrained knowledge transfers through Hedgehog's learned feature maps. The paper would be strengthened by converting GPT-2 to H3 or Hyena-like architectures (if possible) to test whether the gain is from pretraining or from Hedgehog specifically.

**Claim 4: "Hedgehog-Llama2 7B achieves 28.1 higher ROUGE-1 points over the base standard attention model" (Abstract, Section 5.4).** This claim comparing Hedgehog to the "base standard attention model" (zero-shot Llama-2 at 19.3 ROUGE-1) conflates the benefits of finetuning with the benefits of Hedgehog. The relevant comparison is Hedgehog-Llama2 with LoRA (47.4) versus Softmax-Llama2 with LoRA (51.1), a gap of 3.7 ROUGE-1 points—Hedgehog is worse than softmax, which is the expected direction. The 28.1 point gain over zero-shot Llama-2 is almost entirely from LoRA finetuning, not from Hedgehog's architecture. The abstract's framing is misleading: "28.1 higher ROUGE-1 points over the base standard attention model" should read "over the base standard attention model **without finetuning**" to be precise, but the intended comparison is clearly against prior linear attentions (where T2R-Llama2 achieves 2.8, meaning Hedgehog gains 44.6 ROUGE-1 points over T2R). The genuine headline is that Hedgehog enables **viable** linear attention Llama-2 where prior methods produce near-zero-quality models—a meaningful advance, but the abstract's phrasing elides the residual gap to standard attention.

**Missing evaluations and limitations:**

- **No long-context task evaluation.** The primary motivation for linear attention is efficiency on long sequences, yet all downstream evaluations use standard context lengths: LRA at its default lengths (up to 4K for Pathfinder), WikiText-103 at 1024 tokens, GLUE at BERT's standard 512 tokens, and SAMSum dialogues at 1024 tokens. The paper demonstrates attention fidelity at 4096 tokens (Table 5) and inference speed at 32K tokens (Fig. 6), but never tests whether Hedgehog's models actually perform well on tasks requiring long-range reasoning beyond the training context length. This is the single most important missing experiment: if Hedgehog linear attention matches softmax at 512 tokens but degrades at 8192 tokens (e.g., due to accumulated approximation error in the running sums), the efficiency gains are moot for the use case that matters most.

- **Single-seed results.** The paper does not report multiple random seeds for any experiment. Results like Hedgehog exceeding softmax on CoLA (59.2 vs. 58.8) and RTE (69.3 vs. 68.2) in Table 8 look like noise—without standard deviations, the reader cannot distinguish genuine improvement from training variance. For GLUE tasks with small test sets (CoLA: 1,043 test examples, RTE: 3,000), 1-point differences are plausibly within single-run noise.

- **No systematic hyperparameter sensitivity.** The paper reports single configurations for distillation (learning rate 0.01, up to 5 epochs, AdamW with zero weight decay) without ablating these choices. The high learning rate (0.01) for distillation is notable—10× higher than the finetuning learning rate (1e-5 for BERT)—and sensitivity to this choice is unexplored. The 0.495% parameter overhead for Llama-2 is for $d' = 2d$ feature maps; what happens with $d' = d$ (no negation mapping) or $d' = 4d$ is not tested.

- **No comparison to other learned attention methods.** The paper compares against a range of fixed feature maps but does not compare to other works that learn attention variants, such as learned kernel approximations or data-dependent pooling. The T2R baseline (Kasai et al., 2021) is the closest, but T2R uses a simple ReLU feature map—more sophisticated learned alternatives exist that are not included.

- **SETH-based impossibility results are engaged but not resolved.** The paper cites Alman & Song (2023) and Keles et al. (2023) as suggesting high-quality subquadratic attention may be fundamentally impossible, but the experimental results do not directly test this theory's predictions (e.g., whether Hedgehog's attention quality degrades at the sequence lengths where the SETH lower bound would bite). The Llama-2 results at 1024-token context and the 4096-token KL divergence measurement are far from the asymptotic regime where these impossibility results apply.

- **Attention distillation cost is unaccounted.** For pretrained-conversion, the two-stage procedure adds the cost of attention distillation before finetuning. For Llama-2 7B on a single A6000 GPU, the paper reports 2 epochs of distillation with batch size 8 and gradient accumulation, but does not report wall-clock time or total FLOPs for this stage. If distillation cost equals or exceeds finetuning cost, the practical efficiency gains of linear attention are partially offset by the conversion overhead.

- **WikiText-103 125M comparison conflates architecture and pretraining.** As noted above, the 16.7 vs. 18.5 (H3/Hyena) comparison is confounded by GPT-2's pretraining. A clean ablation would be training H3 and Hyena starting from the same pretrained GPT-2 weights via some form of attention distillation or architecture conversion equivalent to what Hedgehog does—but such a conversion may not exist for those architectures, making the comparison inherently unfair.

- **No evaluation of training speed or memory during model training.** The efficiency benchmark (Fig. 6) measures inference wall-clock time and memory for a single attention layer, not end-to-end training throughput for full models. Hedgehog claims $O(n d^2)$ complexity, but the constant factors from the feature map computation, the cumulative sum updates, and the need to store running statistics during training could make training slower than FlashAttention for the sequence lengths where most practitioners operate (512–2048). The paper does not report training throughput comparisons, which matters for the training-from-scratch regime where training cost dominates.

**What the experiments genuinely demonstrate:**

The paper convincingly shows that **Hedgehog's learned feature maps can reproduce softmax attention weight distributions with high fidelity**, and that this fidelity translates to recovering softmax-level performance in finetuned-conversion of models up to 110M parameters (BERT-base, ViT-B/16) and enabling viable pretrained-conversion at the 125M (GPT-2) and 7B (Llama-2) scales where prior linear attentions fail catastrophically. The diagnostic experiments (associative recall, monotonicity curves) provide mechanistic evidence that the two hypothesized properties—spikiness and monotonicity—are causally connected to performance, and that Hedgehog recovers both. The Taylor exponential analysis cleanly demonstrates that spikiness and monotonicity are achievable with subquadratic computation, separating expressivity concerns from efficiency concerns.

What the paper does **not** demonstrate (and sometimes overstates) is: (1) that Hedgehog matches softmax performance in training-from-scratch (a 2.2 perplexity gap remains on WikiText-103, and the "68.6% closed" framing obscures this absolute gap); (2) that pretrained-conversion with Hedgehog outperforms purpose-built subquadratic architectures when pretraining is controlled for (the GPT-2 vs. H3/Hyena comparison is confounded); and (3) that Hedgehog's efficiency gains actually translate to practical benefits on long-sequence tasks (no downstream task evaluation beyond standard context lengths).

The most robust and impactful result is the **Llama-2 7B conversion** (Table 11): T2R produces near-zero-quality text (2.8 ROUGE-1), Hedgehog produces coherent summaries (47.4 ROUGE-1) within 3.7 points of standard attention LoRA at only 0.495% parameter overhead. This is a genuine capability unlock—prior methods could not convert LLMs at this scale—and suggests that learned feature maps are on a fundamentally different scaling trajectory from fixed ones. The sample generations in Appendix C.3 make this visceral: T2R outputs are nonsensical garbage, Hedgehog outputs are reasonable summaries. This single result, if replicable on other LLMs (Mistral, Falcon, Llama-3) and tasks (question answering, code generation), changes the calculus for deploying linear attention LLMs.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For and Likely Dominates the Practical Budget

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire Hedgehog framework for conversion settings rests on attention weight distillation: training the feature maps to match the softmax attention weights of a frozen pretrained model. This requires running the full quadratic softmax attention on training data to generate teacher targets. The paper acknowledges this implicitly in describing the two-stage procedure (Section 4.2, Appendix A.3): "We first freeze the Transformer's original weights and specifically train the Hedgehog MLPs" using cross-entropy between linear and softmax attention weights computed from the same inputs. However, the paper never quantifies the computational cost of this distillation stage relative to the subsequent finetuning or deployment stages, nor does it account for this cost when reporting efficiency gains (Fig. 6).

**The consequence.** For pretrained-conversion of large models, the distillation stage requires running the full quadratic attention forward pass on all training data to generate teacher attention weights. For Llama-2 7B on SAMSum, this means every training sample must be processed through Llama-2's 32 layers of standard softmax attention (quadratic in context length) before Hedgehog can begin learning. The distillation cost therefore scales as the *softmax* complexity—the very thing linear attention is meant to avoid—meaning the total end-to-end cost of producing a Hedgehog-converted model may exceed that of simply finetuning the original quadratic model, especially for one-off conversions where distillation cost cannot be amortized over many downstream tasks.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports that distillation takes 2 epochs for Llama-2 7B (Appendix B.5) and up to 5 epochs for BERT GLUE tasks (Appendix B.4), but provides no wall-clock time, FLOP count, or GPU-hour measurement for these distillation runs. Fig. 6 benchmarks *inference* efficiency of the converted model (single-layer wall-clock time and memory at various sequence lengths) but does not include distillation cost in any efficiency accounting. The paper notes that MLPs add only 0.495% to Llama-2 7B's parameter count (Section 5.4), but this measures only the memory overhead of the *learned* feature maps, not the cost of *learning* them. For the GPT-2 pretrained-conversion result (16.7 perplexity, Table 10), the distillation cost is completely unquantified—we know the final model is efficient at inference, but we do not know whether producing it was cheaper than just using the quadratic GPT-2.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper frames efficiency entirely in terms of inference (Fig. 6: "near 6x faster inference" at 32K tokens) and asymptotic complexity ($O(n d^2)$ for Hedgehog vs. $O(n^2 d)$ for softmax), without discussing the one-time cost of distillation for conversion. There is no suggestion of how to reduce distillation cost (e.g., distilling on a subset of training data, using shorter sequences for distillation than for deployment, sharing feature maps across layers to reduce the number of teacher-student pairs, or amortizing distillation across tasks by training a universal feature map on a large corpus). For the training-from-scratch regime, this limitation does not apply (no teacher is needed), which partially explains why the paper uses WikiText-103 and LRA for from-scratch experiments—but for the conversion regimes that the paper highlights as a key contribution, the unaccounted distillation cost is a substantial practical caveat.

### No Long-Context Downstream Task Evaluation Exists, Despite Long Contexts Being the Primary Motivation

**The assumption or constraint.** The core motivation for linear attention is enabling Transformers to handle long sequences that are computationally prohibitive for quadratic softmax attention. The paper states this explicitly in Section 1: "For typical Transformer settings, e.g., with head dimension = 64 and sequence lengths at 512 to 32K, this quadratic-to-linear scaling can result in significant speed and memory improvements (Fig. 6)." However, all downstream task evaluations in the paper use standard or modest context lengths that do not actually *require* linear attention: LRA tasks use their default lengths (up to ~4K for Pathfinder), WikiText-103 uses 1024-token contexts, GLUE tasks use BERT's standard 512-token maximum, GPT-2 conversion uses 1024-token contexts, and SAMSum dialogues are chunked to 1024 tokens. These are all lengths where FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) and other optimized quadratic attention implementations are perfectly viable.

**The consequence.** The paper demonstrates that Hedgehog matches softmax attention quality at context lengths where the efficiency argument for linear attention is weakest. What practitioners actually need to know—and what the paper does not test—is whether Hedgehog attention quality *degrades* at the long sequence lengths (8K, 16K, 32K tokens) that would justify switching from quadratic attention. Several potential failure modes are unexplored: (1) **Accumulated approximation error**: the linear attention running sums $\sum \phi(k_j)$ and $\sum \phi(k_j)^\top v_j$ accumulate over the full sequence, and small per-token approximation errors in the feature map could compound into large attention weight distortions at long lengths. (2) **Numerical instability**: the exponential-activated feature maps produce positive values that grow large, and computing running sums over 32K tokens of $\exp(w^\top x)$ values could produce numerical overflow or underflow in the denominators of Eq. 2, especially at lower precision (bfloat16 is used for Llama-2). (3) **Feature map saturation**: the Hedgehog MLPs are trained via distillation on short sequences (512 tokens for BERT, 1024 for GPT-2/Llama-2); at much longer lengths, the query and key vectors may fall in regions of the feature map's domain that were never encountered during training, producing uncalibrated attention weights.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 5 provides the only long-context measurement: KL divergence between Hedgehog and softmax attention weights on concatenated CoLA samples at 256, 1024, 2048, and 4096 tokens. The KL divergence remains stable (0.181–0.190), which is encouraging but limited in several ways: it measures only one model (BERT-base, bidirectional), one task (CoLA, linguistic acceptability), and only attention weight fidelity, *not* downstream task performance. There is no measurement at 8K, 16K, or 32K tokens—the lengths highlighted in the efficiency benchmark (Fig. 6) and the abstract's "sequence lengths at 512 to 32K." Critically, no experiment tests whether a Hedgehog-converted model *trained* at 1024 tokens can perform a task requiring reasoning over 8K+ tokens of context—the exact use case that motivates linear attention.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges context-length transfer only indirectly, through the generalization experiments in Section 5.1 and the KL divergence measurement (Table 5). The discussion frames these as evidence that Hedgehog "maintains fidelity with softmax attention over context lengths" (Section 5.1), but the claim is only tested to 4096 tokens and only for attention weight KL divergence, not task performance. The paper does not indicate future work on long-context evaluation, nor does it caution that the current results may not extrapolate to the sequence-length regime where linear attention's efficiency advantage is decisive. Of all the limitations discussed here, this is the most significant because it leaves unverified the central premise of the work: that Hedgehog enables *practical* efficiency gains on *long sequences* while maintaining quality.

### All Experiments Use a Single Model Family Per Regime, Limiting Evidence for Cross-Architecture Generalization

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates Hedgehog across diverse settings—BERT-base for bidirectional finetuned-conversion, GPT-2 for autoregressive pretrained-conversion, ViT-B/16 for vision, Llama-2 7B for large-scale conversion—but each regime uses a *single* model architecture and checkpoint. There is no systematic test of whether the spikiness and monotonicity properties, or Hedgehog's ability to recover them, generalize across fundamentally different Transformer variants (e.g., encoder-decoder models like T5, differently-trained BERT variants like RoBERTa, models with different head dimensions or normalization schemes) or across different pretraining corpora and objectives.

**The consequence.** Two specific generalization concerns arise. First, the paper's core diagnostic properties—spikiness and monotonicity—were identified and validated on PaLM-class models in one regime (BERT-base, 110M parameters, 12 heads, head dimension 64). It is unknown whether these properties are equally important for, say, a T5 model with different attention patterns (cross-attention in encoder-decoder), a RoBERTa model trained with dynamic masking, or a vision Transformer with substantially different attention sparsity patterns. If the properties are architecture-specific, Hedgehog's design (exponential-activated MLP, cross-entropy distillation) may be overfit to the specific attention distributions of the tested models.

Second, the pretrained-conversion results depend heavily on the quality of the teacher model's attention weights. The paper distills from pretrained BERT, GPT-2, and Llama-2 checkpoints—all well-trained models where softmax attention has converged to meaningful patterns. It is unknown whether Hedgehog distillation would work from a poorly-trained or differently-trained teacher (e.g., a model trained with distillation itself, or a model with attention dropout that produces noisier attention weights). The identity-initialization strategy (making the initial feature map approximate $\exp(x)$ element-wise) provides a sensible starting point, but if the teacher attention patterns are substantially different from what an exponential feature map can represent, distillation may fail.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper tests three distinct model families (BERT, GPT-2, Llama-2) and one vision model (ViT), which is broader than many linear attention papers. However, each test is a single data point: BERT-base-uncased at 110M parameters, GPT-2 at 125M, ViT-B/16 at 86M, Llama-2 7B. There is no within-family variation (e.g., BERT-base vs. BERT-large to test scaling behavior, or GPT-2 small vs. medium vs. large), and no cross-family comparison with matched size (e.g., comparing BERT-base conversion quality to GPT-2 conversion quality at the same parameter count). The paper does not test encoder-decoder architectures at all, nor does it evaluate whether Hedgehog's feature maps learned on one model can transfer to another model (the cross-task generalization experiments in Appendix C.2 transfer feature maps across *tasks* for the same BERT model, not across *models*).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss architecture generalization as a limitation or suggest that future work should validate on additional model families. The abstract and conclusion present Hedgehog as a general method ("a learnable linear attention") without qualifying that the evidence comes from specific models. The relatively broad set of tested architectures (BERT, GPT, ViT, Llama) provides suggestive evidence of generality, but the single-checkpoint-per-architecture design means that a failure on a different BERT variant or a different decoder-only LLM would not be surprising and is not ruled out by the current results.

### The Residual Gap to Softmax Is Unexplained and Potentially Fundamental for Training-from-Scratch

**The assumption or constraint.** Hedgehog is designed to recover softmax attention's expressive properties, and in finetuned-conversion it achieves near-perfect performance recovery (99.3% on GLUE, Table 8). However, in the training-from-scratch regime—where there is no teacher to distill from and the model must discover spiky attention patterns end-to-end—a significant gap to softmax remains. On WikiText-103 with 125M GPT-2-style models (Table 7), Hedgehog achieves 20.8 perplexity versus softmax attention's 18.6, a 2.2 perplexity point gap. The paper frames this as "closing the linear attention gap by 68.6%" (relative to the best prior linear attention at 25.6), but in absolute terms, 2.2 perplexity points is a meaningful quality difference—roughly equivalent to the gap between a 125M and a ~160M Transformer (extrapolating from Dai et al., 2019).

**The consequence.** The paper does not ablate or diagnose *why* this gap persists, which leaves open several possibilities with different practical implications. If the gap is due to the limited feature dimension ($d' = 2d$ may be insufficient to fully represent the softmax kernel's expressivity), then increasing $d'$ or using deeper MLPs could close it—an engineering solution. If the gap is due to the absence of distillation (the model must learn spiky attention from scratch using only the task loss), then some form of self-distillation or auxiliary spikiness regularization might help. If the gap is fundamental—linear attention with $O(n d^2)$ complexity simply cannot represent certain attention patterns that softmax $O(n^2 d)$ can—then no amount of tuning will fully close it, and the tradeoff between quality and efficiency is irreducible. The paper's own citation of SETH-based impossibility results (Alman & Song, 2023; Keles et al., 2023) lends weight to this third possibility, but the paper does not engage with whether the residual gap is consistent with theoretical limits.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The WikiText-103 result (Table 7) provides the gap measurement. The LRA results (Table 6) show Hedgehog achieving best average accuracy, but this does not directly measure gap to softmax since softmax Transformers are themselves not state-of-the-art on LRA (the paper notes that non-Transformer state-space models outperform all attention methods). The associative recall experiment (Table 3) shows Hedgehog matching softmax at 100% accuracy, but this is a synthetic task with 40 tokens and 128-length sequences—much simpler than WikiText-103 language modeling. There is no ablation varying the feature dimension $d'$, the MLP depth, or the presence/absence of auxiliary distillation-like losses in the training-from-scratch setting.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss the residual training-from-scratch gap as a limitation. Section 5.2 presents the 68.6% closed framing, which emphasizes the improvement over prior linear attentions rather than the remaining gap to softmax. The conclusion (Section 6) states that Hedgehog "leads to competitive performance with softmax-based attention in training from scratch" without quantifying the remaining difference. For practitioners deciding whether to train a Hedgehog Transformer from scratch rather than using standard softmax attention (perhaps with FlashAttention to mitigate the quadratic cost at moderate lengths), the 2.2 perplexity gap is the critical number—and the paper provides no guidance on whether it can be reduced or should be accepted as a fundamental tradeoff.

### Distillation Data Requirements and the Single-Task Generalization Assumption Are Underexplored

**The assumption or constraint.** For finetuned-conversion, the paper distills Hedgehog feature maps on the *same task* that the model will be finetuned on—CoLA for CoLA conversion, each GLUE task individually, SAMSum for summarization. This requires access to labeled task data before conversion, and assumes that attention patterns learned on one task's data transfer to the finetuned model's task. For pretrained-conversion (GPT-2, Llama-2), distillation uses data from the same domain (WikiText-103, SAMSum), making the same assumption. The paper tests cross-task generalization (Appendix C.2) by distilling on CoLA or WikiText-103 and then finetuning on other GLUE tasks, but the results show that task-specific distillation consistently outperforms cross-task distillation (Table 15: Hedgehog-CoLA achieves 58.4 on CoLA vs. Hedgehog-WT103 at 47.2), and the cross-task performance is only tested on small GLUE tasks, not on the larger models (GPT-2, Llama-2) where distillation data requirements would be most expensive.

**The consequence.** If a practitioner wants to convert a pretrained LLM for a specific downstream task, they must either (1) distill on that task's data (requiring the task data to be available and large enough to provide meaningful attention weight targets), or (2) distill on general-domain data and accept a performance degradation whose magnitude is unknown at scale. The paper does not quantify the minimum amount of distillation data needed, the sensitivity of downstream performance to distillation data quantity, or whether distilling on a large general corpus (e.g., C4, The Pile) could serve as a universal feature map initialization for many downstream tasks. For the Llama-2 7B result—the paper's most impressive—we do not know whether the distillation on SAMSum training data is essential, or whether distilling on a generic corpus and then LoRA-finetuning on SAMSum would work nearly as well.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 4 shows that Hedgehog distilled on CoLA achieves KL divergence 0.172 on in-distribution attention weights vs. 0.357 when distilled on WikiText-103—a ~2× increase in approximation error from switching distillation data. Appendix Table 15 shows that this translates to downstream degradation: Hedgehog-CoLA achieves 58.4 on CoLA vs. 47.2 for Hedgehog-WT103, a substantial 11.2 Matthew's correlation point drop. However, on some other GLUE tasks, the gap shrinks or reverses: MRPC is 89.4 for both, QNLI is 89.2 (WT-103) vs. 87.7 (CoLA), QQP is 90.4 (WT-103) vs. 89.8 (CoLA). This mixed pattern suggests cross-task transfer is possible but unpredictable—some tasks benefit from task-specific distillation, others do not—and the paper provides no framework for predicting when task-specific distillation is necessary versus when general-domain distillation suffices. Critically, all cross-task experiments are on BERT-base GLUE tasks; there are no cross-task distillation experiments at the GPT-2 or Llama-2 scale where distillation data costs are highest.

**Mitigation status.** The paper discusses generalization in Section 5.1 and Appendix C.2, presenting the cross-task KL divergence and performance results as evidence that Hedgehog feature maps generalize. However, the paper does not frame the need for task-specific distillation data as a limitation, does not experiment with reducing distillation data (e.g., distilling on 10%, 50%, 100% of task data and measuring the performance curve), and does not suggest future work on universal feature maps trained on large corpora that could serve as initialization for any downstream task. For the pretrained-conversion use case that the paper emphasizes—converting LLMs for new tasks—the distillation data requirement means that the cost of conversion includes not just the computational cost (discussed above) but also the data requirement of having labeled task data available before conversion, which may not be the case in few-shot or zero-shot deployment scenarios.

### The Comparison to Purpose-Built Subquadratic Architectures Conflates Pretraining with Architecture Quality

**The assumption or constraint.** Section 5.4 compares Hedgehog-GPT-2 (converted from a pretrained GPT-2 checkpoint) against Hybrid H3 and Hyena on WikiText-103, reporting Hedgehog's 16.7 perplexity as state-of-the-art for 125M subquadratic models (Table 10). However, H3 and Hyena are trained *from scratch* on WikiText-103 only, while Hedgehog-GPT-2 inherits GPT-2's pretraining on WebText (a much larger and higher-quality corpus). This is not a matched comparison: the pretraining data, pretraining compute budget, and training procedure differ fundamentally. The paper also reports Hedgehog trained from scratch on WikiText-103 at 20.8 perplexity (Table 7)—worse than H3 and Hyena at 18.5—confirming that the 16.7 result is primarily due to GPT-2's pretraining, not Hedgehog's architecture.

**The consequence.** The paper's central claim about pretrained-conversion—that Hedgehog "enables effective transfer to new tasks and efficient scaling to longer contexts, while frequently outperforming modern subquadratic sequence architectures" (Section 5.4)—conflates two separate advantages: (1) the benefit of starting from a pretrained Transformer rather than random initialization, and (2) the benefit of Hedgehog's specific linear attention mechanism. The result genuinely shows that *converting a pretrained Transformer via Hedgehog* works better than *training a subquadratic architecture from scratch on WikiText-103*, but this is a comparison of training paradigms (pretraining + conversion vs. from-scratch) more than a comparison of architectures. Practitioners would need to know: if I train H3 or Hyena on the same pretraining corpus as GPT-2 (WebText) with a comparable compute budget, how does it compare to Hedgehog-GPT-2? That experiment is not done.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 10 provides the comparison, and Table 7 provides the critical from-scratch Hedgehog result (20.8 perplexity) that demonstrates the pretraining effect. The paper lists GPT-2's zero-shot result (28.0) and finetuned GPT-2 (15.8) as reference points, implicitly acknowledging the pretraining advantage, but the abstract and Section 5.4 text do not caveat the H3/Hyena comparison as confounded by pretraining. The paper does not attempt to distill GPT-2 into an H3 or Hyena architecture (which may not be architecturally feasible) or to pretrain H3/Hyena on WebText for fair comparison.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation. The Llama-2 7B comparison (Table 11) does not have this problem because the baseline is T2R applied to the *same* Llama-2 checkpoint—a matched comparison where both linear attention methods start from the same pretrained weights. For the 125M scale, a matched comparison would require converting GPT-2 to H3/Hyena (if architecturally possible) or pretraining H3/Hyena on the same data as GPT-2, neither of which is done. The paper's framing as "state-of-the-art for 125M subquadratic decoder models" (abstract) elides this important qualification and could mislead practitioners into thinking Hedgehog is architecturally superior to purpose-built efficient architectures, when the evidence only supports that pretraining transfer through learned feature maps is effective—a different and narrower claim.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not propose a new attention mechanism in the traditional sense — it proposes a new **paradigm for designing attention mechanisms**: stop trying to approximate the softmax kernel with fixed mathematical feature maps, and instead learn feature maps that explicitly mimic the attention weight distributions that make softmax effective. This is less a new method than a new way of thinking about the problem, and its implications ripple through how the field approaches efficient attention, model conversion, and the relationship between pretraining and inference.

**The magnitude is a reframing, not a revolution.** The paper does not prove that linear attention is universally superior or that softmax attention should be replaced. What it does is break a logjam: prior work treated the linear attention quality gap as a kernel approximation problem, proposing ever-more-elaborate fixed feature maps (random Fourier features, locality-enhanced kernels, low-rank factorizations) that each closed the gap incrementally but never fully. Hedgehog demonstrates that the gap is not fundamentally about kernel approximation quality at all — it is about recovering the **distributional properties** (spikiness, monotonicity) that make softmax attention behaviorally effective, and these properties can be **learned from the teacher model's own attention patterns** rather than engineered from first principles. This reframing opens a new design space: any parameterized feature map that can be trained via attention distillation becomes a candidate linear attention, shifting the research question from "what is the right mathematical form for φ?" to "what is the right training procedure and architecture for learning φ from a teacher?"

**The paper resolves a specific contradiction in the literature.** Prior work contained a puzzling asymmetry: linear attentions performed passably when models were trained from scratch with them (e.g., Katharopoulos et al., 2020; Choromanski et al., 2020), but catastrophically when swapped into pretrained models (Kasai et al., 2021; Mao, 2022). Practitioners learned that "you can't just convert a Transformer to linear attention and finetune" — but nobody could say exactly why, or whether the problem was fundamental. The paper's monotonicity diagnosis (Section 3.1, Fig. 3, Table 1) provides a clean mechanistic explanation: non-monotonic feature maps create gradient misalignment with pretrained query/key projections, making finetuning unstable. Training from scratch avoids this because the projections and feature map are learned jointly from random initialization — there is no pretrained expectation to violate. By showing that Hedgehog recovers monotonicity (Fig. 3, rightmost panel) and consequently achieves near-perfect conversion (Table 8: 99.3% BERT GLUE recovery), the paper demonstrates that the asymmetry is not fundamental — it is a property of specific feature map choices, and learnable feature maps can resolve it.

**Research directions that become more attractive:** (1) Attention distillation as a general strategy for model compression and efficiency, beyond just linear attention — if you can distill a teacher model's attention patterns into a smaller or faster student, the same principle could apply to other attention approximations (sparse attention, local attention + memory, state-space models). (2) Learning attention mechanisms from data rather than designing them analytically — the Hedgehog approach suggests that many attention efficiency problems might be solved by treating the attention mechanism itself as a learned component optimized to match a teacher, analogous to how neural architecture search automated architecture design. (3) Converting pretrained Transformers for long-context deployment — the Llama-2 7B result (Table 11) shows this is possible at scale, making it worthwhile to build robust conversion pipelines for production LLMs rather than training efficient architectures from scratch.

**Research directions that become less attractive:** (1) Proposing yet another fixed kernel feature map with slightly better approximation bounds — the paper's evidence strongly suggests that no fixed functional form will match softmax across diverse models and tasks, because the optimal feature map depends on the specific query-key distributions of the trained model, which vary with architecture, pretraining data, and task. (2) Treating linear attention as a training-from-scratch replacement for softmax — the 2.2 perplexity gap on WikiText-103 (Table 7) and the paper's emphasis on conversion suggest that Hedgehog's main value is in converting existing pretrained models, not in training new models with linear attention from scratch. Researchers who primarily train models from scratch may find better returns with optimized quadratic attention (FlashAttention) at moderate lengths or state-space models for very long sequences.

**The pretrained-conversion result for Llama-2 7B (Table 11) is the most field-altering single finding.** Prior to this paper, converting a 7B LLM to linear attention and finetuning it to produce coherent text was essentially impossible — T2R produced degenerate nonsensical output (Appendix C.3). Hedgehog does it with 0.495% parameter overhead on a single A6000 GPU. This result, if it replicates across other LLMs (Mistral, Falcon, Llama-3) and tasks, changes the calculus for deploying large language models on long contexts: rather than training new efficient architectures at scale (which requires massive compute), organizations can take their existing fine-tuned LLMs, run Hedgehog distillation for a few epochs, and obtain a linear-attention version that is 6× faster at inference (Fig. 6) on long sequences. This is a practical capability unlock, not a theoretical advance — it makes linear attention viable for real LLM deployments in a way that prior methods did not.

**The SETH-based impossibility results (Alman & Song, 2023; Keles et al., 2023) are contextualized but not refuted.** The paper cites these theoretical results suggesting that high-quality subquadratic approximation of softmax attention may be fundamentally impossible for large $n$, and its empirical results do not contradict them — the paper does not claim to achieve exact approximation of softmax attention at arbitrarily long sequences. Instead, the paper demonstrates that *learned distribution matching* at the practical sequence lengths where models are deployed (up to 32K tokens for the efficiency benchmark, though only measured for attention fidelity, not task performance) can achieve good enough quality to be useful. This suggests that the SETH bounds, while theoretically important, may not be the binding constraint for practical deployment at current scales — the gap between what theory says is impossible and what learned approximations can achieve is large enough to enable real applications.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Long-context downstream evaluation of Hedgehog-converted models, especially on tasks requiring reasoning over 8K+ tokens.** The paper's most glaring omission is the absence of any downstream task evaluation at the long sequence lengths that motivate linear attention. The KL divergence measurement on concatenated CoLA samples at 4096 tokens (Table 5) shows attention fidelity is maintained, but does not test whether a Hedgehog-BERT or Hedgehog-Llama2 model can actually *perform* a task — question answering, summarization, retrieval — that requires integrating information across 8K, 16K, or 32K tokens of context. A strong follow-up would take the Hedgehog-Llama2 7B model trained on SAMSum at 1024 tokens, finetune it on a long-document summarization dataset (e.g., GovReport at 8K+ tokens, BookSum at chapter-length), and compare ROUGE scores against standard Llama-2 7B with FlashAttention and against T2R-Llama2 (expecting Hedgehog to produce coherent summaries where T2R fails, but with an unknown quality gap to standard attention). Critically, this would also stress-test Hedgehog's numerical stability at genuine long sequences in bfloat16, the context-length generalization of feature maps trained at 1024 tokens, and whether accumulated approximation error in the running sums causes attention pattern degradation that is invisible to KL divergence but impacts task performance.

**Systematic ablation of feature map dimension, depth, and activation to characterize the expressivity-efficiency Pareto frontier for learned linear attention.** The paper presents Hedgehog as a single architecture choice: single-layer MLP, $d' = 2d$ features, exponential activation, identity initialization. But the design space of learned feature maps is much richer, and we do not know where Hedgehog sits on the tradeoff curve. Specific experiments would include: (1) Varying $d'$ from $d/2$ through $4d$ on the WikiText-103 training-from-scratch setting, measuring how the gap to softmax evolves — does performance saturate at $2d$, or would $4d$ close the remaining 2.2 perplexity gap? (2) Adding a second MLP layer with a nonlinearity before the exponential activation — does deeper feature extraction improve attention weight fidelity on complex tasks? (3) Testing alternative output activations: softplus (smooth approximation to ReLU with exponential tails), learnable temperature parameters in the exponential, or a mixture of exponentials with learned centers and scales. (4) For training-from-scratch, adding an auxiliary spikiness regularization loss (e.g., penalizing the entropy of attention weights) to see whether explicit spikiness encouragement reduces the residual gap to softmax without requiring distillation. The paper's T2R-HH ablation (ReLU + distillation achieves 93.5% recovery vs. 99.3% for exponential) suggests the activation function matters, but we do not know whether exponential is uniquely suited or just the first strong choice tested.

**Universal Hedgehog feature maps trained on large general-domain corpora and transferred across many downstream tasks.** The paper shows Hedgehog-Corpus models (distilled on WikiText-103) generalize to GLUE tasks with some degradation (Appendix Table 15), but the experiments are at BERT-base scale on small GLUE tasks. A practical follow-up would scale this: distill Hedgehog feature maps for a Llama-2 7B or Mistral 7B model on a large general-domain corpus (e.g., 10B tokens from C4 or The Pile), then evaluate the resulting "universal Hedgehog checkpoint" on 10+ diverse downstream tasks (summarization, QA, classification, code generation) with LoRA finetuning, comparing against task-specific Hedgehog distillation for each task. The hypothesis is that a general-domain distillation can capture enough of the teacher model's attention distribution to serve as a good initialization across tasks, eliminating the need for task-specific distillation data. The paper's cross-task results (Table 4, Appendix Table 15) show mixed evidence — general-domain distillation sometimes underperforms task-specific (CoLA: 47.2 vs. 58.4) and sometimes matches (MRPC: 89.4 vs. 89.4) — and scaling to 7B models and more diverse tasks would clarify whether universal feature maps are viable. A negative result (universal distillation consistently and substantially underperforms task-specific) would also be valuable, telling practitioners that the distillation data must be domain-matched, which constrains the deployment scenarios where Hedgehog is practical.

**Testing whether attention distillation can transfer across model architectures — distilling from a large teacher model to a linear-attention student of a different architecture.** The paper always distills Hedgehog feature maps *within the same model*: the teacher and student share the same query/key projections, and only the attention mechanism changes. This is natural for conversion, but a more ambitious question is whether Hedgehog can enable cross-architecture distillation: take a large, expensive Transformer (e.g., a 70B Llama-2 teacher) and distill its attention patterns into a smaller, linear-attention student with a different architecture (e.g., a 7B Hedgehog model with different width/depth, or even a non-Transformer architecture with linear attention). This would require aligning the attention heads and layers between teacher and student — not trivial when architectures differ — but if feasible, it would enable training efficient models that inherit the attention patterns of much larger teachers without ever running the teacher at deployment time. A concrete experiment: distill Llama-2 70B attention weights on a corpus of 1B tokens into a randomly initialized 7B Hedgehog model, then finetune and evaluate on downstream tasks. Compare to (a) the 70B teacher, (b) a 7B Hedgehog model trained on the same corpus from scratch without teacher distillation, and (c) a 7B model trained with standard logit-level knowledge distillation from the 70B teacher (to isolate the benefit of attention-level vs. output-level distillation). This is ambitious but made newly tractable by the paper's demonstration that attention distillation works at the 7B scale.

**Conversion of existing fine-tuned production LLMs to linear attention and measurement of real-world inference latency, throughput, and cost savings in deployment settings.** The paper's efficiency benchmark (Fig. 6) measures single-layer wall-clock time and memory at various sequence lengths, which is informative but far from a real deployment measurement. A deployment-focused follow-up would take a production model (e.g., a fine-tuned Llama-2 7B chat model used in a customer-facing application), run Hedgehog distillation and LoRA recovery-finetuning, then benchmark end-to-end inference latency, throughput (tokens/second), and GPU memory usage serving the converted model on an held-out evaluation set at the target deployment sequence lengths (e.g., 4K, 8K, 16K tokens for long-context chat). Compare against the original model with FlashAttention and against other optimized serving strategies (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, quantization, KV-cache compression). Measure not just speed but also output quality (ROUGE, human preference ratings) to quantify the quality-efficiency tradeoff in a real setting. The paper's 6× faster inference claim at 32K tokens (Fig. 6) is for a single attention layer — end-to-end speedups will be lower because feedforward layers, embedding lookup, and output projection are unchanged and now dominate runtime. Realistic deployment numbers (e.g., "2.3× faster end-to-end at 8K tokens with 2% ROUGE-1 degradation") are what practitioners need to make adoption decisions, and the paper does not currently provide them.

**Stress-testing Hedgehog's monotonicity recovery by evaluating conversion quality on tasks requiring precise gradient alignment with pretrained attention patterns.** The paper diagnoses monotonicity as critical for finetuned-conversion because non-monotonic feature maps misalign gradients with pretrained weights (Section 3.1). The GLUE results (Table 8) show near-perfect recovery, but GLUE tasks are relatively simple and many are near performance ceilings. A sharper test would evaluate Hedgehog conversion on tasks where (a) the pretrained model achieves far-from-ceiling performance (so there is room to observe degradation), (b) the attention patterns are known to be critical for task performance (so violating them measurably hurts), and (c) the conversion process must preserve fine-grained attention distinctions. Candidate tasks: multi-hop question answering (HotpotQA, where attention must link across multiple documents), coreference resolution (where spiky attention to specific entity mentions matters), or code completion (where attention to specific variable declarations and function signatures is essential). If Hedgehog recovers >95% performance on such tasks while T2R drops to near-random, it strongly validates the monotonicity mechanism. If Hedgehog shows unexpected degradation on specific attention patterns (e.g., very long-range dependencies, very precise positional attention), it would reveal limitations of the current architecture — perhaps $2d$ features cannot capture all softmax attention patterns — and motivate extensions like hierarchical feature maps or position-aware feature maps.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-demand conversion of fine-tuned LLMs for long-context customer support and document analysis.** An organization has a Llama-2 7B model fine-tuned on their product documentation and customer chat logs for question answering. The model works well at 4K-token context (product pages + recent chat history), but they want to extend to 16K-token context to include full product manuals and multi-turn conversation histories. Quadratic attention makes 16K-token inference slow and expensive at their serving scale. They run Hedgehog distillation on their existing fine-tuned model using a sample of their own training data (a few hundred examples), then LoRA-finetune to recover any quality loss. The converted model now runs at approximately 3–5× faster wall-clock time at 16K tokens (extrapolating from Fig. 6's single-layer 6× at 32K, accounting for non-attention layers) with an expected quality retention above 90% (extrapolating from Table 11's 92.8% ROUGE-1 recovery for Llama-2 7B on SAMSum). The key benefit is that this conversion uses their existing model and data — no retraining from scratch, no new architecture adoption — and leverages the 0.495% parameter overhead to swap attention mechanisms without changing the model's knowledge.

**Cost-efficient batch inference for document processing pipelines.** A data processing pipeline runs inference over millions of documents (legal contracts, scientific papers, Wikipedia dumps) using a pretrained GPT-2 or BERT model for tasks like entity extraction, summarization, or classification. The documents average 2K–8K tokens in length. They convert their deployed models to Hedgehog variants via one-time distillation on a representative document sample (a few thousand examples, costing a few GPU-hours), then run batch inference on the full corpus using the linear-attention models. At 8K-token average length, Fig. 6 suggests single-layer attention is ~3–4× faster than FlashAttention, and memory usage is similar or lower, allowing larger batch sizes on the same GPU hardware. The quality retention (99.3% for BERT GLUE in Table 8, 99.0% for ViT ImageNet in Table 9) means the pipeline's downstream accuracy is essentially unchanged. The total cost savings scale with the volume of documents processed — if batch inference previously cost $10,000/month in GPU compute, a 3× speedup translates directly to ~$3,300/month, with the one-time distillation cost (~$50–200 for the distillation run) amortizing rapidly.

**Enabling on-device LLM inference for long-form text tasks.** A mobile application wants to run a small language model (e.g., a 125M GPT-2-style model) entirely on-device for privacy-sensitive use cases like email summarization, local document search, or writing assistance. On-device GPUs/NPUs have limited memory and compute compared to server hardware. FlashAttention's $O(n^2 d)$ time complexity makes inference on long emails or documents (>1K tokens) unacceptably slow on mobile hardware. Converting the 125M model to Hedgehog (Table 10: 16.7 perplexity after conversion + finetuning) and deploying it with a linear-attention inference kernel achieves $O(n d^2)$ time and memory, making 4K–8K token inference feasible within mobile latency budgets (sub-second rather than multiple seconds). The 0.495% parameter overhead adds roughly 0.6M parameters to a 125M model, negligible for on-device storage and memory. The quality gap to the server-side quadratic model (Table 10: 16.7 vs. 15.8 perplexity, or ~0.9 PPL) represents a small degradation that may be acceptable for on-device use cases where privacy and latency are the primary constraints.

**Rapid architecture conversion for A/B testing efficient attention mechanisms in production.** An ML team has a standard Transformer model in production and wants to test whether switching to linear attention improves serving costs without degrading user-facing metrics. Rather than training a separate linear-attention model from scratch (which would confound architecture changes with optimization differences) or designing a custom efficient architecture (engineering-intensive), they run Hedgehog distillation on their existing production checkpoint using a sample of production traffic (logged queries), then deploy the converted model alongside the original in an A/B test. The two-stage procedure (distill then finetune) takes a few epochs and can be completed within a day on modest hardware (single A6000 for BERT-scale, a few A100s for Llama-2 7B-scale — exact time depends on distillation data volume, which the paper doesn't report but suggests is modest). The key value is **rapid experimentation velocity**: the team can test whether linear attention works for their specific workload without committing to a new model architecture, training pipeline, or deployment infrastructure. If the A/B test shows latency improvements with acceptable quality degradation, they adopt Hedgehog permanently. If not, they revert with no loss to the original model. The paper's cross-task generalization evidence (Appendix Table 15) suggests that even if distillation is done on slightly different data than production traffic, quality recovery is reasonably robust.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate a formal decision framework or explicit tradeoff matrix against named alternatives (it compares against baselines to demonstrate superiority in specific settings, but does not delineate conditions under which practitioners should choose Hedgehog over, say, FlashAttention for moderate lengths or state-space models for long sequences). The closest the paper comes is the three-regime structure (training-from-scratch, finetuned-conversion, pretrained-conversion), which implicitly frames Hedgehog as suitable for all three, and the efficiency results (Fig. 6) which suggest the efficiency gains are most pronounced at long sequence lengths (>2K tokens) where quadratic attention's $n^2$ factor dominates. However, the paper does not provide explicit guidance like "for sequence lengths below 2K tokens, FlashAttention is preferable; for 2K–8K, Hedgehog conversion offers the best quality-efficiency tradeoff; for >8K, consider state-space models." Such a decision matrix would require head-to-head comparisons at matched compute budgets across sequence lengths, which the paper does not perform. The guidance below is therefore derived from the paper's results and stated motivations, not from an explicit framework the authors provide.

- **Prefer Hedgehog finetuned-conversion when** you have an existing fine-tuned Transformer (BERT, ViT, or similar up to ~100M parameters) that you want to serve more efficiently at inference, can afford 2–5 epochs of task-specific attention distillation on your own task data, and need to recover >99% of the original model's task performance (Table 8: 99.3% GLUE recovery; Table 9: 99.0% ImageNet recovery). Hedgehog outperforms the only comparable prior method (T2R at 88.9% recovery) by a large margin and adds minimal parameters (0.495% overhead).

- **Prefer Hedgehog pretrained-conversion when** you have a pretrained decoder-only LLM (GPT-2 125M through Llama-2 7B) that you want to finetune on a downstream task with improved efficiency for long contexts. Hedgehog is the only demonstrated method that converts LLMs at this scale into viable linear-attention variants (Table 11: 47.4 ROUGE-1 for Hedgehog-Llama2 vs. 2.8 for T2R). The quality gap to standard attention LoRA is present but modest (3.7 ROUGE-1 points, or 7.2% relative), and may be acceptable in exchange for the 3–6× inference speedup at long sequence lengths (Fig. 6). The conversion cost (distillation + LoRA finetuning) fits on a single A6000 GPU.

- **Be cautious about Hedgehog training-from-scratch** if your primary goal is matching softmax Transformer quality on language modeling. The paper closes 68.6% of the perplexity gap to softmax (Table 7: 20.8 vs. 18.6), but the remaining 2.2 perplexity points are meaningful and the paper does not diagnose their source. Unless you specifically need linear attention during training (e.g., to train on sequences longer than FlashAttention can handle), optimized quadratic attention (FlashAttention-2/3) at moderate lengths may be a better quality-efficiency tradeoff. Hedgehog training-from-scratch is most attractive for long-sequence benchmarks like LRA where it achieves best-in-class results among attention methods (Table 6: 59.66% average accuracy), though state-space models (not compared directly) now achieve higher LRA scores.

- **Do not use Hedgehog if** your sequence lengths consistently stay below ~1K tokens, where FlashAttention and other optimized quadratic kernels are already fast and the $n^2$ factor has not yet dominated. Fig. 6 shows Hedgehog's advantage growing with sequence length — at 512 tokens, the wall-clock time gap to FlashAttention is modest, while at 32K tokens it is ~6×. At short lengths, the fixed cost of the feature map computation ($O(n d^2)$ with constants from the MLP forward pass) may even make Hedgehog slower than optimized quadratic attention. The paper does not provide a crossover point, but the efficiency curves suggest it occurs somewhere between 1K and 2K tokens for the 12-head, head-dimension-64 configuration tested.
