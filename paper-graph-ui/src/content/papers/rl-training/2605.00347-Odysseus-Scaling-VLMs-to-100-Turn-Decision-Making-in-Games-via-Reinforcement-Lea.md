# Odysseus: Scaling VLMs to 100+ Turn Decision-Making in Games via Reinforcement Learning

**ArXiv:** [2605.00347](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00347)

## 🎯 Pitch

Vision-language models can learn to play through entire Super Mario Land levels—over 100 consecutive actions—by leveraging RL, but only if you replace the critic-free approaches common in LLM fine-tuning with a lightweight turn-level critic. Without this, popular methods like GRPO completely fail to learn, while Odysseus matches or exceeds frontier proprietary models like GPT-5.4.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper studies how to apply reinforcement learning to train vision-language models for long-horizon decision-making tasks, using the video game *Super Mario Land* as a testbed with Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct as the base model. The core contribution is an adapted variant of PPO featuring a **lightweight turn-level critic** (a small CNN rather than a second full VLM) and **positive-advantage filtering** (discarding samples with negative advantages during training), which together substantially improve training stability and sample efficiency over critic-free methods such as GRPO and Reinforce++ — the latter methods fail to learn effective policies in this 100+ turn setting. The resulting training framework, **Odysseus**, integrates lightweight supervised fine-tuning initialization with multi-task RL across multiple game levels, achieving at least 3× average game progress over frontier models (including GPT-5.4 and GLM-4.6V) and roughly 2× higher sample efficiency than classical deep RL trained from scratch, while maintaining general-domain capabilities. The paper further establishes that pretrained VLMs provide strong action priors that reduce the need for manual action-space engineering and improve sample efficiency relative to training policies from scratch — a benefit that is realized only when RL is properly stabilized with a learned critic for long-horizon credit assignment.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Can RL Effectively Train VLMs for Truly Long-Horizon Tasks?

The fundamental question this paper tackles is whether reinforcement learning can be made to work effectively for training vision-language models (VLMs) on decision-making tasks that require far more interaction steps than what the existing literature has attempted. While RL has been successfully applied to fine-tune LLMs and VLMs in domains like preference alignment (RLHF) and mathematical reasoning, and has even been used for embodied or game-playing agents, **all existing RL-for-VLM efforts operate in settings with relatively short horizons — typically 20–30 interaction turns**. The paper's central hypothesis is that this is not a coincidence: long-horizon settings (100+ turns) introduce qualitatively different challenges that break existing methods, and solving these challenges requires revisiting fundamental algorithmic design choices.

This matters for several reasons that go beyond the specific game environment:

- **Real-world embodied tasks are inherently long-horizon.** Robotic manipulation, autonomous navigation, and multi-step planning problems routinely involve hundreds of sequential decisions where actions taken early in a trajectory determine outcomes much later. If RL cannot be made stable for VLMs beyond 30 turns, the entire vision of VLMs as general-purpose embodied agents is severely constrained.
- **The credit assignment problem becomes exponentially harder with horizon.** In a 20-turn task, a reward signal at the end can be propagated back through the trajectory relatively straightforwardly. In a 100+ turn task, the signal-to-noise ratio of any per-action learning signal degrades dramatically. This means methods that work on short horizons may not simply "scale up" — they may fundamentally fail.
- **The dominant paradigm shifted from RL to SFT for embodied VLMs precisely because RL was unreliable.** Understanding whether this shift was necessary (because RL genuinely doesn't work) or premature (because the right RL recipe hadn't been found) has major implications for the field's research direction.

### Why Super Mario Land?

The choice of environment is deliberate and carefully justified. The paper identifies several properties that make *Super Mario Land* a particularly revealing testbed:

**Genuinely long effective horizons.** Table 1 contrasts *Super Mario Land* (100+ turns) with prior VLM-RL benchmarks: AlfWorld requires only ~10–20 turns per episode, Sokoban and Frozen Lake ~5–30 turns. This is not a small difference — it represents an order-of-magnitude increase in the sequential decision-making burden. The paper's 100+ turn figure refers to *effective horizon* (the number of decisions that meaningfully influence subsequent outcomes), not just raw step count — many steps in other environments are independent or trivially recoverable, whereas in platformer games, a single mistimed jump can erase progress from dozens of prior correct decisions.

**Rich perceptual demands with minimal scaffolding.** Unlike gridworld or text-based environments where state information can be trivially serialized, *Super Mario Land* requires the VLM to extract spatial information — Mario's position, enemy locations, gap distances, platform heights — from rendered pixel frames. This forces the model to use its visual reasoning capabilities, making the task genuinely multimodal rather than a text-only reasoning problem disguised with tokenized state representations.

**Closed-loop control with precise timing.** The paper emphasizes that tasks like "jumping over a gap or avoiding a moving threat at the correct timing remain challenging" for frontier models. This is a qualitatively different type of challenge from, say, navigating to a target object in a static room: it requires the agent to make decisions under dynamic constraints where the window for correct action is narrow and errors are catastrophic (falling into a gap typically ends the episode).

**Sufficiently open yet computationally tractable.** The game has 12 levels of varying difficulty and visual structure, enabling both single-task and multi-task training studies. It's lightweight enough to allow tens of millions of interaction samples — the scale needed for RL training — while being complex enough that zero-shot frontier models "rarely progress beyond the initial stages." The paper explicitly notes this contrasts with large open-world simulators (MineDojo, etc.) that would be computationally prohibitive for controlled RL studies.

**Alignment with human prior knowledge.** A subtle but important point: a human player with no prior experience can achieve non-trivial progress. This establishes a lower bound on what a capable agent should achieve and confirms that the task does not require superhuman capabilities or memorization of level layouts — it requires generalizable perceptual-motor skills that a pretrained VLM might reasonably be expected to acquire or already partially possess.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three categories of prior work and explains why each is insufficient for the long-horizon VLM-RL setting. I'll walk through each in detail because the specific failure modes motivate every architectural choice in Odysseus.

#### Limitation 1: Critic-Free RL Methods Fail on Long Horizons

The most direct prior approach is to apply the RL algorithms that have been successful in fine-tuning LLMs for reasoning and alignment tasks: GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) and Reinforce++ (Hu, 2025). These are **critic-free** methods — they estimate advantages using only the observed returns from sampled trajectories, without learning a value function that predicts expected future reward. Specifically:

- **GRPO with outcome rewards** assigns the same trajectory-level cumulative reward to every turn in a trajectory, computes a batch-normalized advantage from these shared values, and updates the policy. In a 100-turn trajectory, this means that a catastrophic mistake on turn 97 and a brilliant recovery on turn 10 are treated identically from the perspective of per-turn advantage. The temporal credit assignment is effectively reduced to a scalar trajectory score — any structure in *when* good or bad decisions occurred is discarded.
- **GRPO with process rewards** attempts to address this by standardizing per-step rewards and using un-discounted return-to-go, but the signal remains noisy because it doesn't learn to distinguish between states that look similar but have different future potential (e.g., "Mario standing on a platform" could mean safe progress ahead or an imminent pit).
- **Reinforce++** uses a similar approach with discounted returns but still lacks a learned value baseline, relying on batch-level standardization rather than state-conditioned value prediction.

The paper's experimental results (Figure 4a and Figure 7) are stark: **only GRPO with outcome rewards shows any observable improvement**, and even that is modest and unstable. All other critic-free variants essentially flatline. The key insight is that in a 100-turn horizon, the variance of Monte Carlo returns is too high for critic-free methods to produce reliable learning signals — the classical motivation for actor-critic methods (reducing variance via a learned baseline) becomes not just helpful but **essential**.

#### Limitation 2: When Critics Are Used in VLM-RL, They're Impractically Expensive

Prior work that *has* applied actor-critic methods to VLM agents (Zhai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a) uses a **large-model-based token-level critic** — essentially a second full VLM trained to predict per-token values. The paper identifies two problems with this approach:

**Computational intractability.** Training a second VLM as critic "nearly doubles the memory and computation costs compared to critic-free methods." For an 8B-parameter model like Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, this would require maintaining two full copies of the model in memory during training (plus optimizer states for both), making the approach prohibitive for most research groups and all but the most well-resourced industrial labs.

**Token-level granularity may be misaligned with the credit assignment problem.** In a turn-based game where rewards (forward progress) are received at the environment step level, token-level credit assignment forces the critic to learn a mapping from individual text tokens to eventual rewards — but the relationship between generating the token "a" (for jump) and the reward received 20 turns later is mediated by the fact that pressing 'a' at that specific frame produced a specific physical outcome. Tokenizing this relationship introduces unnecessary complexity and may actually degrade the quality of the learned value function.

#### Limitation 3: The Field Has Largely Abandoned RL for Embodied VLMs in Favor of SFT

This is perhaps the most significant gap the paper addresses. Recent high-profile work on embodied VLM agents — including RT-2 (Zitkovich et al., 2023), π0 (Black et al., 2024), SIMA 2 (Bolton et al., 2025), and Lumine (Tan et al., 2025) — relies on **large-scale supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstration trajectories**. The paper acknowledges the impressive results of these approaches but identifies fundamental scalability concerns:

**Expert demonstration data with action labels is expensive and scarce.** For robotic manipulation, collecting expert demonstrations requires teleoperation or human annotation. For game-playing, it requires recordings from skilled human players with corresponding action logs. The paper argues this is "difficult to scale" — a claim supported by the observation that the SFT data used in Odysseus is intentionally lightweight (~5,000 frames annotated by GPT-o3, not human experts) and focuses only on perception/knowledge, not on optimal action control.

**SFT alone cannot exceed the performance of the demonstrator.** A policy trained purely via imitation learning is bounded by the quality of its training data. If the teacher model (GPT-o3 in this case) makes suboptimal decisions — which the paper explicitly demonstrates it does, as shown by the substantial gap between Odysseus-SFT performance and Odysseus after RL — then SFT alone plateaus at this suboptimal level. RL provides a mechanism for the agent to discover better strategies than those demonstrated.

**RL and SFT are complementary, not competing.** The paper's design of Odysseus — lightweight SFT for perception followed by RL for action optimization — suggests that the field's polarization into "SFT-only" and "RL-only" camps is a false dichotomy. The SFT stage injects domain knowledge (what do Koopa Troopas look like? what does a pipe do?) that the pretrained VLM may lack because game environments are underrepresented in pretraining data. The RL stage then optimizes how to act on that knowledge. Neither alone is sufficient; together they enable capabilities neither could achieve independently.

#### Additional Gap: No Systematic Understanding of What Makes RL Work for Long-Horizon VLMs

Beyond the specific limitations of individual prior approaches, the paper identifies a higher-level gap: the field lacks a **systematic investigation of algorithmic components** for VLM-RL in long-horizon settings. Prior work that applied RL to VLMs (Zhai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a) introduced complex, specialized machinery — hierarchical credit assignment, trajectory decomposition, token-level advantage estimation — without controlled ablations demonstrating which components are actually necessary. The paper's approach of starting from a simple PPO baseline and adding modifications incrementally (lightweight critic, positive-advantage filtering) with controlled comparisons against GRPO and Reinforce++ is motivated by the desire to establish **causal evidence** for what matters, rather than presenting yet another complex system whose individual contributions are unclear.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution through several deliberate positioning choices that distinguish it from prior work:

**It treats RL stabilization as the primary research contribution, not the environment or the model.** While *Super Mario Land* and Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct are the empirical vehicle, the paper's abstract and introduction emphasize that the goal is to "identify key ingredients for making RL stable and effective in long-horizon, multi-modal settings" and "provide practical guidance for developing VLMs as embodied agents." This positions the work as a methodological contribution to the RL-for-foundation-models literature, not a game-playing result.

**It draws an explicit parallel to classical deep RL while arguing for a hybrid approach.** Section 5 directly compares VLM-based RL against PPO with a CNN policy trained from scratch — the standard approach that achieved superhuman performance on Atari. The finding that VLM-based RL is roughly 2× more sample-efficient supports the paper's thesis that pretrained VLMs provide useful priors, but the fact that classical RL *can* eventually learn (with action-space engineering) suggests that VLM-based RL is best understood as an acceleration technique, not a fundamentally different capability regime. The paper is careful not to claim that VLMs enable learning tasks that are impossible for classical methods — only that they make learning substantially more efficient and reduce the need for manual design.

**It positions RL as the missing piece in the SFT-dominated embodied VLM paradigm.** The paper explicitly contrasts itself with "approaches [that] heavily depend on Supervised Fine-Tuning with large amounts of high-quality, action-labeled demonstration data" (Section 2). By showing that a lightweight SFT initialization (5000 frames, teacher-annotated, no human action labels) followed by RL can outperform pure SFT approaches, the paper argues that the field's shift away from RL may have been premature — and that with proper stabilization, RL provides capabilities that SFT alone cannot.

**It acknowledges but deliberately simplifies relative to recent specialized architectures.** The paper notes that prior work introduced "specialized machinery for trajectory decomposition, token-level advantage estimation, or hierarchical credit assignment" but argues that "a comparatively simple PPO-based approach with the right critic design is sufficient." This is a deliberate methodological stance: rather than adding complexity to solve problems that may be artifacts of the chosen test environment, the paper strips down to fundamentals and shows that the classical actor-critic recipe — with one key insight about critic architecture (make it lightweight and turn-level) — is enough. This positions the work as providing a **minimal viable recipe** rather than a state-of-the-art system, enabling future work to add complexity only where proven necessary.

**It connects to the broader question of whether foundation models provide useful priors for RL.** Section 5 frames the comparison between VLM-based RL and classical deep RL as testing the hypothesis that "pretrained VLMs provide strong inductive biases that reduce the exploration burden in long-horizon tasks." This connects the paper to a fundamental question in the field: are foundation models merely convenient policy parameterizations, or do they encode knowledge that qualitatively changes the learning problem? The paper's evidence — higher sample efficiency with less action-space engineering — supports the stronger claim, but with an important caveat: this benefit only materializes when the RL algorithm is properly stabilized. A poorly designed RL algorithm (GRPO, Reinforce++) fails to extract any benefit from the VLM prior, suggesting that algorithmic design and model priors are complementary rather than additive.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The system being built is a training pipeline that takes a pretrained vision-language model and, through a combination of supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, transforms it into an agent capable of playing *Super Mario Land* — a platformer video game requiring 100+ sequential decisions with precise timing and spatial reasoning. The core problem it solves is making RL stable and effective for VLMs in long-horizon settings, which it addresses by decomposing the learning problem: a lightweight CNN critic handles temporal credit assignment at the turn level (so the VLM doesn't have to learn value estimation), positive-advantage filtering stabilizes optimization by discarding samples where the agent performed worse than expected, and a supervised initialization stage injects domain-specific visual knowledge without requiring expert action labels.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Odysseus framework has four major components arranged in a pipeline:

1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Initialization** — takes the pretrained VLM (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct) and fine-tunes it on ~5,000 static game frames annotated by a stronger teacher model (GPT-o3) with structured perception-reasoning-action descriptions. This injects domain knowledge about game mechanics, enemy types, and spatial relationships without teaching optimal actions.

2. **VLM Actor** — the fine-tuned VLM generates structured outputs (<perception>, <reasoning>, <answer>) given the current game frame and a system prompt. The <answer> field is parsed into button presses that are executed in the emulator.

3. **Lightweight CNN Critic** — a small convolutional neural network (following the NatureCNN architecture from DQN) that takes only the current game frame as input and predicts the expected cumulative future reward (the value function). This is trained on observed returns from rollout trajectories and runs entirely separately from the VLM, avoiding the memory overhead of a second large model.

4. **PPO Trainer with Positive-Advantage Filtering** — computes advantages by comparing the critic's predicted values against actual observed returns, filters out samples with negative advantages, and updates the VLM's policy weights using a clipped surrogate objective. It also periodically updates the critic using a smooth L1 loss against return-to-go targets.

Information flows through the system in a loop: the VLM actor generates actions → the game environment produces new frames and rewards → the CNN critic evaluates states → advantages are computed and filtered → the VLM is updated → new rollouts are collected with the updated policy → repeat.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the POMDP formulation and the specific instantiation for *Super Mario Land* — state, action, observation spaces, and reward function — because every downstream component depends on these interfaces.
- **Second**, the interaction protocol — how the VLM receives observations, structures its output, and how actions are extracted — because this defines the input-output contract that RL training must optimize.
- **Third**, the adapted PPO algorithm and its two key innovations (turn-level CNN critic and positive-advantage filtering) — the central technical contribution that makes RL stable.
- **Fourth**, the comparison with critic-free alternatives (GRPO, Reinforce++) and classical deep RL — not as separate experiments, but as ablation-like evidence for why each design choice matters.
- **Fifth**, the supervised fine-tuning initialization — what it trains on, how the data is generated, and why it's intentionally lightweight.
- **Sixth**, the multi-task RL training with auto-curriculum — how multiple game levels are balanced during training to prevent easier levels from dominating optimization.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems-building and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that long-horizon VLM-RL requires three ingredients that are individually simple but, when combined correctly, unlock stable training: (1) a learned critic for variance reduction in credit assignment, (2) making that critic lightweight and turn-level rather than a second full VLM, and (3) filtering out negative-advantage samples during policy updates.

#### POMDP Formulation and Environment Instantiation

The paper adopts the standard Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) formalism as the mathematical framework for the agent-environment interaction, but the interesting content is in how this abstraction maps to the concrete *Super Mario Land* setting.

**State space `$\mathcal{S}$`:** The full internal state of the game's RAM — Mario's exact position, velocity, enemy positions and states, timer values, level layout data not visible on screen, and all other variables the Game Boy hardware maintains internally. The agent never observes this directly; it exists only as the ground truth that determines environment dynamics.

**Observation space `$\Omega$`:** The rendered pixel frame — specifically, a single 160×144 resolution game screen upsampled by a factor of 8 to 1280×1152 (Section B), combined with a textual instruction prompt. The upsampling is a practical detail motivated by the observation that "the original game resolution of 160×144 is relatively low compared with the visual resolutions used during modern VLM pre-training" — VLMs like Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct were trained on high-resolution natural images, and feeding them a microscopic 160×144 game frame would be severely out of distribution for the vision encoder. The agent sees only the current frame, not a history buffer — this is described as an "intentionally minimal-scaffolding design" that contrasts with prior work providing richer inputs like observation histories or parsed game state information.

**Action space `$\mathcal{A}$`:** A discrete set of button combinations derived from the Game Boy controller. The agent may press up to two buttons simultaneously from a list of seven: {a, b, up, down, left, right, noop}. The reasoning for the two-button limit is that it maps naturally to human gameplay — running jumps (right + a), crouching (down), climbing (up) — while keeping the combinatorial action space manageable. The classical deep RL baseline in Section 5 uses 22 actions (all valid combinations of up to two buttons), while the VLM uses this same action space but accessed through natural language (the model outputs strings like ['a', 'right'] which are parsed into button presses).

**Reward function `$R(s_t, a_t)$`:** Defined as forward movement at each turn:

$$r_t = x_{t+1} - x_t$$

where `$x_t$` is the x-coordinate of Mario at turn `$t$`, read directly from the game's RAM.

**What it computes:** a scalar indicating how many pixels Mario moved to the right (or left, in which case the reward is negative) during the current turn. This is the distance Mario traversed horizontally in one environment step.

**Why this form:** the paper describes this as "a minimal yet dense learning signal for task progress." The key property is that it is **dense** — every action produces a reward, not just completion of the level — which is critical for credit assignment over 100+ turn horizons because it provides immediate feedback on whether an action produced forward progress. However, it is also **environment-defined** rather than hand-crafted: the reward comes from reading Mario's actual position from RAM, not from a heuristic like "distance to goal." A sparse reward (only rewarding level completion) would make the credit assignment problem dramatically harder, while a more complex shaped reward (rewarding coin collection, enemy defeats, etc.) would embed designer knowledge that might be wrong or myopic. The x-coordinate reward is the simplest signal that correlates with the true objective of "progress through the level."

An important subtlety: the reward can be negative if Mario moves left (which might happen due to knockback from enemy contact or intentional backtracking), and it provides no direct incentive for collecting coins or defeating enemies. The agent must learn these auxiliary behaviors only insofar as they facilitate forward progress — for example, jumping on an enemy that blocks the path yields forward progress after the enemy is removed, which provides positive reward through the subsequent movement, not through an explicit enemy-kill bonus.

**Frame-skipping mechanism:** A single emulator frame (1/60 second) produces negligible visible movement, so the chosen action is applied for multiple consecutive frames: 15 frames when the action includes a jump (button 'a'), and 5 frames otherwise (including noop). This is a standard technique in deep RL for Atari and platformer games — it reduces the effective horizon of the decision problem, makes each action have a visible consequence, and prevents the agent from needing to learn sub-frame timing. The asymmetry (15 frames for jumps, 5 for other actions) accounts for the fact that jumps in Mario games have a characteristic arc duration that longer frame-skips approximately match.

**Policy definition:** In the POMDP formalism, the policy maps from observation history to actions:

$$a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | h_t), \quad h_t = (o_1, a_1, \dots, o_t)$$

where `$\pi_\theta$` is the VLM with parameters `$\theta$`, and `$h_t$` is the interaction history. Crucially, the paper's implementation **does not provide the full history `$h_t$`** to the VLM — it provides only the current frame `$o_t$` and the static system prompt (Section B notes the agent "observes the current game frame and the instruction prompt"). The history dependence is implicit in the VLM's autoregressive generation of the CoT (the <reasoning> section can reference previous states because the VLM remembers its own output context, but there is no explicit memory mechanism or observation history buffer).

#### Interaction Protocol: From Game Frame to Button Press

The interaction protocol defines the exact format of information exchange between the VLM and the game environment. It is decomposed into three stages that execute on every turn:

**Stage 1: Observational Inputs.** The VLM receives exactly two inputs concatenated into its context:

1. A **system prompt** (reproduced in full in Section B) that explains the game's mechanics, lists available buttons with their functions, specifies output format requirements, and states the goal ("progress through levels, collect coins and power-ups when safe, and ultimately finish the game by rescuing Princess Daisy"). The prompt includes explicit mappings like "'a': Jump (used to make Mario jump)" and "'down': Crouch or enter pipes (when standing on a pipe)."

2. The **current game frame** — the rendered pixel image at 1280×1152 resolution, representing what a human player would see on the screen. The paper explicitly notes that the agent does NOT receive: a longer observation history, parsed game state information (Mario's coordinates, enemy positions), or any auxiliary inputs beyond the raw frame and prompt. This minimalist design is a deliberate choice to avoid "scaffolding" — the agent must learn to extract all task-relevant information from pixels alone, using its pretrained visual capabilities.

**Stage 2: Structured Chain-of-Thought Generation.** The VLM generates a response formatted using three XML-style tags — a pattern described as "largely follow[ing] the style of ReAct" (Yao et al., 2022) and "similarly adopted in Chen et al. (2025b); Wang et al. (2025a)." The three required sections are:

- **`<perception>`**: The VLM explicitly describes what it sees on the screen. The paper specifies this should include "Mario's location, nearby obstacles, enemies, and interactive elements such as coins or pipes." This tagging forces the model to ground its visual understanding before acting, rather than skipping directly from pixels to buttons — a form of structured reasoning that the paper hypothesizes "encourage[s] robust spatial-temporal grounding."

- **`<reasoning>`**: The VLM lays out a step-by-step strategy, explaining "the actions required to respond to the current state, such as timing a jump to collect floating coins or moving right to approach a pipe stack." This section can reference knowledge about game mechanics (e.g., "Mario needs to jump on the Goomba to defeat it") and can plan multiple steps ahead.

- **`<answer>`**: The VLM outputs the selected action as a list of button strings formatted as a Python list literal: `['a', 'right']`. The action must be parseable — the system extracts text between `<answer>` and `</answer>` and converts it into button presses.

**Why this format:** The structured CoT serves multiple purposes simultaneously. It provides a training signal (the SFT stage trains the model to produce high-quality perception and reasoning, not just correct actions). It makes the model's decision process interpretable — the qualitative examples in Appendix F (Figures 9 and 10) directly compare the CoT outputs of the base model and Odysseus to diagnose failure modes. It also functions as a form of internal memory: since the VLM is not given an explicit observation history, the <reasoning> section can reference what it noticed in previous turns (e.g., "I saw a gap ahead in the previous frame, so I need to prepare a jump"). The XML tags provide a simple, reliable parsing mechanism — the system can extract specific sections without relying on the VLM producing perfectly formatted output.

**Stage 3: Action Execution.** Once the VLM generates its response and the <answer> field is parsed, the extracted button combination is applied in the game emulator. The frame-skipping logic described above is applied — the button(s) are held for 5 or 15 consecutive frames depending on whether a jump is included. The environment then renders the next frame, computes the reward (x-coordinate change), and the cycle repeats.

A crucial implementation detail: the paper describes the frame-skip in terms of **emulator frames**, not VLM turns. At 60 fps, 15 frames = 0.25 seconds of game time. During these 15 frames, the button combination is continuously applied — the VLM does not re-evaluate its decision partway through the jump. This means the effective decision frequency is 4 Hz during jumps and 12 Hz during other actions, which is substantially lower than the raw frame rate but matches the timescale at which meaningful game state changes occur.

#### Adapted PPO with Lightweight Turn-Level Critic

This is the central technical contribution. The paper starts from standard PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) and modifies it in two specific ways to address the computational and stability challenges of training VLMs in long-horizon environments. The full PPO algorithm (Appendix C.1) uses the standard clipped surrogate objective:

$$L(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{o_t, a_t \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\min\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|o_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|o_t)}\hat{A}_t, \text{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|o_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|o_t)}, 1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}\right)\hat{A}_t\right)\right]$$

where `$\pi_\theta(a_t|o_t)$` is the current VLM's probability of action `$a_t$` given observation `$o_t$` at turn `$t$`, `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|o_t)$` is the probability under the policy that generated the rollout (the behavior policy), `$\hat{A}_t$` is the estimated advantage at turn `$t$`, `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$` and `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$` are asymmetric clipping thresholds, and the expectation is taken over the batch of collected rollout data `$\mathcal{D}$`.

**What it computes:** a scalar training loss for updating the VLM's parameters `$\theta$`. For each observation-action pair in the batch, the loss compares the ratio of new-policy probability to old-policy probability against a clipped version of that ratio, and multiplies the minimum of the two by the advantage estimate. When the advantage is positive (the action was better than expected), the loss encourages increasing the probability of that action in future, but the clipping prevents the new policy from deviating more than `$\epsilon_{\text{high}}$` times the old probability. When the advantage is negative, the loss encourages decreasing the probability, bounded by `$(1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}})$` times the old probability.

**Why this form:** The asymmetric clipping (`$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$`, `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$`) is unusual — standard PPO uses symmetric clipping (typically `$\epsilon = 0.2$`). The paper lists these values in Table 9 but does not explicitly justify the asymmetry. A plausible interpretation is that allowing slightly more optimization in the positive-advantage direction (increasing probability of good actions) is beneficial when combined with positive-advantage filtering, since only positive-advantage samples are used for training anyway — the `$\epsilon_{\text{low}}$` bound on negative samples is effectively irrelevant because negative-advantage samples are filtered out entirely.

The critical question is: **how are the advantages `$\hat{A}_t$` constructed?** This is where the two innovations — lightweight turn-level critic and positive-advantage filtering — operate.

---

#### Innovation 1: Lightweight Turn-Level CNN Critic

**What the critic is:** A value function `$V_\phi(o_t)$` parameterized as a convolutional neural network (CNN) with architecture following the NatureCNN backbone from Mnih et al. (2015) — the same architecture that achieved human-level performance on Atari games. The CNN takes a single game frame `$o_t$` as input and outputs a scalar prediction of the expected discounted cumulative future reward from that state. The hyperparameters (Table 7) are: hidden dimension 512, Adam optimizer with learning rate `$3 \times 10^{-4}$`, no weight decay, gradient clipping at 1.0, constant learning rate schedule, and 1 optimization epoch per training step.

**What "lightweight" means concretely:** The CNN critic has "hidden dimension 512" (Table 7), which — for a standard NatureCNN with three convolutional layers followed by a fully-connected layer — corresponds to roughly 1–2 million parameters. This is approximately **4,000–8,000 times smaller** than the 8-billion-parameter VLM actor. The memory footprint of the CNN critic is negligible relative to the VLM — it adds perhaps 0.01% to the total GPU memory consumption during training, compared to prior approaches that "nearly double" the memory by instantiating a second full VLM as critic.

**What the critic is trained on:** The critic learns to predict discounted return-to-go using a smooth L1 (Huber) loss:

$$L_V(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{o_t \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\text{SmoothL1}\left(V_\phi(o_t) - \hat{R}_t\right)\right]$$

where `$V_\phi(o_t)$` is the critic's predicted value for observation `$o_t$`, `$\hat{R}_t = \sum_{i \geq t} \gamma^{i-t} r_i$` is the discounted return-to-go from turn `$t$` onward (the actual sum of future discounted rewards observed in the trajectory), and `$\gamma = 0.95$` is the discount factor. `$\text{SmoothL1}$` is the Huber loss — quadratic for small errors (|error| < 1), linear for large errors — which provides robustness to outliers in the return signal.

**What this computes:** for each observation in the batch, the loss penalizes differences between the critic's predicted value and the actual sum of discounted future rewards that were subsequently observed. After training, `$V_\phi(o_t)$` estimates the expected future return from state `$o_t$` under the current policy.

**Why this form:** smooth L1 is chosen over MSE because RL returns can have high variance — a single trajectory might have an unusually high or low return due to stochastic events (enemy spawning, lucky timing), and MSE would disproportionately penalize these outliers (squaring the error), potentially destabilizing critic training. The Huber loss is the standard choice in deep RL implementations (Stable-Baselines3, Raffin et al., 2021a).

**Why a turn-level critic rather than token-level:** This is a crucial design decision. In prior VLM-RL work (Zhai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a), the critic operates at the token level — it predicts a value for every generated token in the VLM's output, requiring the critic to understand the relationship between generating the token 'r' in the word 'right' and the game reward received 50 turns later. The paper argues this is misaligned: rewards in *Super Mario Land* are defined at the environment-step level (forward movement per turn), not at the token level. A turn-level critic directly maps from game frame to expected future return, avoiding the need to learn the irrelevant tokenization-to-reward mapping. Moreover, the CNN critic only needs to process pixel data — it doesn't need to handle text tokens, natural language, or the VLM's output format, which dramatically simplifies its architecture.

**Why a CNN rather than a transformer/VLM as critic:** The paper states that "CNN critics, which are sufficient for classical deep RL, can already effectively stabilize training and lead to improved performance." This claim is supported by the experimental results showing PPO with CNN critic outperforming all critic-free methods (Figure 4a). The underlying insight is that, for this environment, the value function depends primarily on visual features (Mario's position relative to obstacles, enemy trajectories, gap locations) that CNNs can extract efficiently. A full VLM would be overkill — it would bring language understanding and reasoning capabilities that are irrelevant for computing a scalar value estimate from pixels. The CNN is also dramatically faster to train (one optimization epoch per PPO training step) and requires no special parallelism or sharding.

**Unstated but important property:** The CNN critic operates as a **privileged information processor**. It sees the same pixel input that the VLM sees, but it is trained specifically for value prediction — it learns which visual features correlate with future success, independent of the VLM's language generation capabilities. This means the critic can potentially learn to recognize subtle visual cues (e.g., the exact distance to a gap that determines whether a jump will succeed) that the VLM might not reliably verbalize in its <perception> section.

#### Innovation 2: Positive-Advantage Filtering

**What it is:** After computing raw advantages, any sample with a negative advantage is discarded from the training batch. The policy is updated only on samples where `$\hat{A}_t > 0$` — that is, only on actions that performed better than the critic expected.

**How advantages are computed with filtering (Equation 1 in Appendix C.1):** The raw turn-level advantage is first computed as:

$$\tilde{A}_t = \hat{R}_t - V_\phi(o_t)$$

where `$\hat{R}_t$` is the observed discounted return-to-go and `$V_\phi(o_t)$` is the critic's predicted value. This is the standard advantage estimator: a positive value means the actual return exceeded the baseline prediction (the action was better than expected), a negative value means it was worse.

Then, with positive-advantage filtering (Equation 1), the advantage fed to the policy update is:

$$\hat{A}_t = \frac{\max(\tilde{A}_t, 0)}{\sigma\left(\{\tilde{A}_{t'} : \tilde{A}_{t'} > 0, o_{t'} \in \mathcal{D}\}\right)}$$

where `$\max(\tilde{A}_t, 0)$` clamps negative advantages to zero, and `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the standard deviation computed over only the positive advantages in the current batch.

**What this computes:** Only the positive raw advantages survive; they are then normalized by dividing by their standard deviation. Negative advantages become zero, and zero-advantage samples contribute nothing to the policy gradient (multiplying the loss by zero neutralizes the update for that sample). The normalization ensures that the remaining positive advantages are on a consistent scale across batches and training steps.

**Why this form:** The paper cites two lines of evidence. First, empirical observations that "negative-advantage samples can destabilize optimization," referenced to Xiong et al. (2025), Deng et al. (2025) for foundation model fine-tuning, and Hämäläinen et al. (2020), Jesson et al. (2023) for classical deep RL. The hypothesized mechanism is that negative advantages encourage the policy to *decrease* the probability of actions it previously took. In a long-horizon setting, an action that led to eventual failure might have been correct at the time it was taken — the failure was caused by a later mistake. Penalizing the early action based on the final outcome introduces noise into the learning signal because the credit assignment is imperfect (the critic cannot perfectly disentangle which actions caused the failure). Positive advantages are more reliable: an action that led to better-than-expected return was genuinely good, even if some later actions were bad.

Second, the paper notes that the role of positive versus negative advantage samples "remains an actively studied question" (citing Carrino et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025 for counterpoints), positioning positive-advantage filtering as an empirical finding that works in this setting rather than a theoretical claim of universal superiority.

**Interaction with the PPO clipping mechanism:** When `$\hat{A}_t = 0$` (due to filtering), the PPO loss becomes zero regardless of the policy ratio, so the sample is effectively ignored. The asymmetric clipping thresholds (`$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$`, `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$`) are then applied only to positive-advantage samples, which means the tighter lower bound (`$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$`) is effectively unused — only the upper clipping bound constrains how much the policy can increase probabilities of good actions.

**Without filtering (standard PPO):** The advantages are batch-normalized over all samples:

$$\hat{A}_t = \frac{\tilde{A}_t}{\sigma\left(\{\tilde{A}_{t'} : o_{t'} \in \mathcal{D}\}\right)}$$

This is the vanilla approach used in the "PPO" baseline in Figure 4a. The experimental comparison (PPO vs. PPO + Pos. Adv. in Figure 4a) shows that positive-advantage filtering "improves training stability" — the filtered version reaches similar or slightly higher game progress with visibly less variance across training steps.

#### Critic-Free Comparison Methods: GRPO and Reinforce++ (Why They Fail)

To establish that the learned critic is genuinely necessary, the paper implements and evaluates three critic-free alternatives under the same experimental conditions. Understanding their mechanics helps clarify why they fail.

**GRPO with Outcome Rewards (Appendix C.1).** The outcome return for a trajectory `$\tau = (o_0, a_0, r_0, \dots, o_{T-1}, a_{T-1}, r_{T-1})$` is:

$$\hat{R}_{\text{out}}(\tau) = \sum_{i=0}^{T-1} r_i$$

which is equivalent to setting the discount factor to `$\gamma = 1$` when computing return-to-go. Every turn `$t$` in the trajectory receives this identical scalar as its reward signal. The advantage is then batch-normalized over trajectories:

$$\hat{A}_t = \frac{\hat{R}_{\text{out}}(\tau) - \mathbb{E}_{\tau' \in \mathcal{D}}[\hat{R}_{\text{out}}(\tau')]}{\sigma\left(\{\hat{R}_{\text{out}}(\tau') : \tau' \in \mathcal{D}\}\right)}$$

**Why this fails in long-horizon settings:** The shared outcome return eliminates all temporal structure. A brilliant jump on turn 5 that collected a crucial coin and a fatal mistimed jump on turn 95 that ended the trajectory receive identical credit — both are treated as contributing to the trajectory's total return. In a 100-turn trajectory, the effective signal-to-noise ratio for any individual action is diluted by a factor of roughly 100, because the return aggregates over all actions. The advantage is essentially telling the policy "this whole trajectory was above/below average" without distinguishing which actions within the trajectory were responsible.

**GRPO with Process Rewards.** Per-turn rewards are first standardized within the batch:

$$\tilde{r}_t = \frac{r_t - \mathbb{E}_{r_{t'} \in \mathcal{D}}[r_{t'}]}{\sigma(\{r_{t'} : r_{t'} \in \mathcal{D}\})}$$

Then the per-turn advantage is the un-discounted return-to-go of these standardized rewards:

$$\hat{A}_t = \sum_{i=t}^{T-1} \tilde{r}_i$$

**Why this also fails:** While this preserves some temporal structure (earlier turns have larger return-to-go sums because they include more future steps), the standardization is performed *across the batch* rather than conditioned on state. This means the same reward value (e.g., moving 5 pixels right) might be treated as positive in one batch context and negative in another, depending on what other trajectories in the batch achieved. The reward is not being compared to what was *expected* from the specific state Mario was in — it's being compared to the average reward across all states in the batch, which is not a meaningful baseline. This is precisely the variance problem that a learned state-conditioned value function solves.

**Reinforce++.** Uses the same turn-level discounted return-to-go as PPO but standardizes it over the batch instead of subtracting a learned baseline:

$$\hat{A}_t = \frac{\hat{R}_t - \mathbb{E}_{o_{t'} \in \mathcal{D}}[\hat{R}_{t'}]}{\sigma\left(\{\hat{R}_{t'} : o_{t'} \in \mathcal{D}\}\right)}$$

where `$\hat{R}_t = \sum_{i \geq t} \gamma^{i-t} r_i$` with `$\gamma = 0.95$`.

**Why this is critiqued:** The batch-level mean `$\mathbb{E}[\hat{R}_{t'}]$` is a constant across all states in the batch, whereas the PPO critic provides a state-conditioned baseline `$V_\phi(o_t)$`. The difference is that the PPO critic can predict that certain states are inherently high-value (e.g., Mario on a safe platform with no enemies nearby) while others are low-value (e.g., Mario about to fall into a gap), and subtract the appropriate baseline for each. Reinforce++ subtracts the same average across all states, so an action taken from an inherently easy state (which would naturally have high return regardless of the specific action) receives an inappropriately high advantage, while an action from a difficult state receives an inappropriately low advantage.

**The experimental evidence (Figure 4a, Figure 7):** Among critic-free methods, only GRPO with outcome rewards shows "observable improvements after training" (roughly 200-400 game progress at 0.38M samples, compared to >600 for PPO variants). All other critic-free configurations remain near zero or show unstable, transient improvements. The paper's interpretation is that "the variance of Monte Carlo returns is too high for critic-free methods to produce reliable learning signals" in a 100+ turn setting.

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning Initialization

The SFT stage is intentionally lightweight and is motivated by the observation that "currently available small open-source VLMs (e.g., Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct) sometimes lack sufficient domain knowledge and perceptual grounding in Super Mario Land" because these environments are "underrepresented in their pre-training data." For example, the base model might not reliably distinguish Mario from enemy sprites or know that pipes can be entered by pressing 'down' — foundational game knowledge that a human player acquires within seconds of playing but that the VLM must learn.

**Data curation:** Approximately 5,000 frames are "sampled across the 10 considered levels from two walkthrough videos that complete the game." The key design choice is that these frames come from *playthrough videos*, not from expert demonstrations with action labels. The paper explicitly contrasts this with prior SFT-based approaches (Tan et al., 2025; Bolton et al., 2025) that require "large amounts of high-quality, action-labeled demonstration data." Here, the frames are static images extracted from videos — there are no corresponding action logs, because the walkthrough videos only show the visual output, not the button presses.

**Teacher annotation:** A stronger model (GPT-o3) generates structured responses for each frame following the same format as the interaction protocol — <perception>, <reasoning>, <answer> tags. The paper qualitatively verifies that these annotations "are of consistently high quality in terms of both game knowledge and visual perception." The teacher model's actions are not necessarily optimal, and the paper explicitly states that the SFT stage "focus[es] on improving domain knowledge and environment perception, rather than optimizing action control, which is deferred to RL." This is a deliberate separation of concerns: SFT teaches the model *what it's looking at*, RL teaches it *what to do about it*.

**Training procedure (Table 8):** The SFT uses standard cross-entropy loss on the model's output tokens. The hyperparameters are: total dataset size 5,058, 1 training epoch, batch size 128, gradient accumulation over 2 steps (effective batch size 256), learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-7}$`, cosine learning rate scheduler, 3% warmup ratio. The extremely low learning rate (`$1 \times 10^{-7}$`) is notable — it suggests the SFT stage is performing a very gentle update, nudging the model's representations without overwriting pretrained knowledge. The single epoch and small dataset also prevent overfitting to the teacher's potentially suboptimal action choices.

**Why SFT matters for RL:** The paper shows that "Odysseus-SFT alone does not provide observable gains over the base model" (Table 2), but "training on top of this initialization, i.e., Odysseus, consistently yields better performance than Odysseus-Zero across all levels." Odysseus-Zero is RL directly on the base model without SFT. This demonstrates that SFT is not useful for immediate performance improvement but is valuable as a *preconditioning* step for RL — it gives the model the vocabulary and visual understanding to reason about the game, which allows RL to focus on learning optimal action timing and selection rather than simultaneously learning perception.

#### Multi-Task RL Training with Auto-Curriculum

The final component extends single-level RL training to multiple game levels simultaneously. In the Odysseus experiments, training is performed on the first five levels of *Super Mario Land* (World 1 Level 1 through World 2 Level 2).

**The problem with uniform level sampling:** If each training batch contains `$M$` trajectories total, and levels are sampled uniformly, then easier levels dominate the optimization objective because trajectories from easier levels are longer (the agent survives further). Consider a batch with `$M_1$` trajectories from an easy level (average length 200 turns) and `$M_2$` trajectories from a hard level (average length 30 turns). With uniform sampling, `$M_1 \approx M_2$`, but the total number of training samples (observation-action pairs) is `$\sum_{m} \text{len}(\tau_{1,m}) \approx 200 M_1$` from the easy level and `$\sum_{m} \text{len}(\tau_{2,m}) \approx 30 M_2$` from the hard level — roughly 6.7× more samples from the easy level. The PPO objective aggregates loss over all samples, so the optimization effectively weights the easy level 6.7× more than the hard level.

**The auto-curriculum mechanism (Figure 5):** For each level `$k$` present in the current batch, the mechanism computes the average trajectory length:

$$N_k = \frac{1}{M_k} \sum_{m \in [M_k]} \text{len}(\tau_{k,m})$$

where `$M_k$` is the number of trajectories from level `$k$` in the current batch, and `$\text{len}(\tau_{k,m})$` is the number of turns in trajectory `$m$` from level `$k$`. Then, for the *next* batch, levels are sampled according to weights inversely proportional to these lengths:

$$w_k \propto \frac{1}{N_k}, \quad \forall k \in [K]$$

where `$K$` is the total number of training levels and `$w_k$` is the sampling probability for level `$k$` in the next batch.

**What this computes:** levels that currently produce short trajectories (the agent struggles and dies quickly) get up-weighted, meaning more of the next batch's trajectories will be collected from those levels. Levels where the agent is already performing well (long trajectories) get down-weighted. Over multiple training steps, this creates a dynamic balancing: as the agent improves on a previously-hard level and trajectories lengthen, that level's weight decreases, shifting training focus to other levels that still need improvement.

**Why this form:** inverse trajectory weighting approximately equalizes the number of training samples contributed by each level. If level 1 has average length 200 and level 2 has average length 30, then `$w_1 \propto 1/200$` and `$w_2 \propto 1/30$`, so level 2 gets approximately 6.7× more trajectories in the next batch. The total training samples become roughly `$200 \cdot (C/200) = C$` from level 1 and `$30 \cdot (6.7 \cdot C/30) = 6.7C$` from level 2 — still not perfectly balanced, but much closer than uniform sampling. The mechanism is adaptive because weights are recomputed from each batch based on current performance, so it responds dynamically as the agent improves or regresses on different levels.

**Why not just fix the weights upfront:** different levels have different intrinsic difficulty and maximum possible trajectory lengths (Table 2 shows maximum progress ranging from 2191 to 2510 across levels). Fixed weights would not adapt to the agent's learning trajectory — a level that is initially hard might become easy after sufficient training, and the weighting should shift accordingly. The auto-curriculum automatically reduces emphasis on levels that the agent has already mastered.

**Implementation in Odysseus:** The RL training uses 1024 trajectories per batch (Table 9), with the auto-curriculum determining how many trajectories come from each level. The remaining hyperparameters for multi-task RL training: 190 training steps for Odysseus (175 for Odysseus-Zero), maximum 400 turns per trajectory (the horizon limit — trajectories are truncated if the agent survives this long), top-p sampling with p=1 (no nucleus filtering), temperature=1, maximum response length 4096 tokens, 1 optimization epoch per training step, mini-batch size 4096, discount factor `$\gamma = 0.95$`, policy clip range `$\epsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28$`, `$\epsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2$`, learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, constant learning rate schedule.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Critic-Free/Critic Dichotomy Is Not About Computation — It's About Temporal Credit Assignment Fidelity

The paper's most consequential intellectual move is reframing the choice between critic-free and actor-critic RL for VLM training as fundamentally about **variance reduction in temporal credit assignment**, not about computational cost. Prior work implicitly treated GRPO-style critic-free methods as the "efficient" default and actor-critic methods as the "expensive" option — a framing that made sense in short-horizon reasoning tasks (20–30 turns) where per-step credit assignment is relatively easy and a learned critic's variance reduction is a luxury, not a necessity. The dominant assumption, carried over from the LLM alignment and reasoning literature (Shao et al., 2024; Hu, 2025), was that critic-free methods work well enough and that adding a critic doubles memory consumption for marginal benefit.

This paper's experiments demonstrate that this assumption catastrophically fails when the horizon extends to 100+ turns. **The critic is not optional — it is the gatekeeper that determines whether RL is even possible.** Figure 4a shows that all critic-free variants (GRPO with outcome rewards, GRPO with process rewards, Reinforce++) either flatline or exhibit unstable, transient gains, while PPO with a learned critic achieves steady, substantial improvement. The mechanism behind this failure is not computational in nature — it's statistical: in a 100-turn trajectory, the Monte Carlo return from a single rollout has such high variance that per-action learning signals from batch-level standardization are essentially noise. The learned critic provides a state-conditioned baseline that subtracts out the expected return for being in a particular state, isolating the component of the return that is *attributable to the specific action taken*.

This insight generalizes beyond *Super Mario Land*. It predicts that any VLM-RL application with effective horizons beyond ~30–50 turns will experience a phase transition where critic-free methods stop working, regardless of how much data or compute is available. The horizon threshold itself likely depends on the stochasticity of the environment (more randomness → earlier failure) and the density of the reward signal. Prior work that successfully applied GRPO to multi-turn agents in AlfWorld or gridworld tasks (~10–20 turns) was operating below this threshold. This paper shows what happens when you cross it, and the result is not a graceful degradation but a qualitative failure mode.

**Significance level:** This is a **fundamental diagnostic reframing**, not an incremental improvement. It changes how researchers should think about algorithm selection for VLM agents: rather than starting from critic-free methods as the default and adding a critic only if needed, the default for long-horizon tasks should be to include a learned critic, with the critic-free option reserved for short-horizon or single-turn settings where variance reduction is unnecessary. The paper provides the first clear empirical boundary for where this transition occurs, even if the exact threshold remains environment-dependent.

**Evidence:** Figure 4a (the algorithmic comparison) and Figure 7 (individual runs) anchor this claim. The stark contrast between PPO variants (reaching 600–800 game progress) and all GRPO/Reinforce++ variants (0–400, mostly flat) at 0.38M samples is the key visualization. The paper's interpretation in Section 4.2 — "critic-free methods exhibit unstable learning dynamics and limited performance gains" — understates the result. The critic-free methods didn't just perform worse; they *failed to learn at all* for several configurations.

---

### Innovation 2: The Critic Can Be Radically Decoupled from the Actor — Architecturally, Computationally, and Representationally

Prior VLM-RL work using actor-critic methods (Zhai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a) operated under an implicit assumption of **architectural symmetry**: the critic that evaluates states should be of comparable capacity to the actor that generates actions, because both are processing the same visual inputs and reasoning about the same environment. This led to designs where the critic is instantiated as a second large VLM — sharing the same backbone, the same multi-modal projector, and the same token vocabulary — trained to predict token-level values. The computational cost of this symmetry (roughly doubling memory and FLOPs) was accepted as the price of actor-critic training for foundation models.

The paper challenges this assumption at its root. By showing that a ~1–2 million parameter CNN with 512 hidden units (approximately **0.01–0.025% of the actor's 8B parameters**) suffices as a critic for long-horizon visual control, the paper establishes that **the value function and the policy operate on fundamentally different representational requirements**. The VLM actor needs to: parse the visual scene into semantic concepts (Mario, enemies, platforms), reason about game mechanics in natural language, maintain implicit memory through its CoT generation, and generate precise action sequences. The critic needs to: extract spatial features from pixels, predict a scalar expected return, and provide a low-variance baseline. These tasks are not just different in complexity — they are different in kind. The semantic understanding that makes the VLM powerful as an actor is *irrelevant* for value prediction, which relies on low-level visual patterns that CNNs process naturally.

This decoupling has three cascading implications:

1. **Computational accessibility.** Actor-critic VLM-RL becomes feasible for academic labs and smaller industry groups. A CNN critic running alongside an 8B VLM adds negligible memory overhead. This removes the primary barrier that made prior actor-critic approaches to VLM-RL impractical.

2. **Representational specialization.** The CNN critic can learn visual features specifically optimized for value prediction, unconstrained by the VLM's pretrained representations. For example, the critic might learn to detect the precise pixel-level indicator of an impending gap that determines whether a jump will succeed — a feature the VLM might not reliably verbalize in `<perception>` but that is crucial for evaluating state quality.

3. **Algorithmic modularity.** Because the critic is architecturally independent, it can be improved, replaced, or augmented without modifying the VLM. An ensemble of CNN critics, a transformer-based critic, or a critic with privileged information (game RAM) could all be plugged into the same training pipeline. This modularity also means that critic improvements benefit all VLM-based agents sharing the same environment, regardless of which specific VLM serves as the actor.

**Significance level:** This is a **fundamental architectural insight** that overturns a costly default assumption. It doesn't just make existing actor-critic methods more efficient — it enables actor-critic methods to be used at all in resource-constrained settings. The finding also has implications beyond gaming: any embodied VLM-RL application where the environment provides pixel observations and scalar rewards can potentially adopt a lightweight visual critic, freeing the VLM to focus on language-conditioned reasoning and action generation.

**Evidence:** Table 7 provides the concrete critic hyperparameters (hidden dimension 512, NatureCNN architecture), and the success of PPO + CNN critic in Figure 4a and Table 2 demonstrates that this decoupling works. The contrast is implicit but stark: prior work (Zhai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a) used large-model critics and evaluated on short-horizon tasks; this paper uses a tiny CNN critic and succeeds on a 100+ turn task.

---

### Innovation 3: Positive-Advantage Filtering as a Stabilization Mechanism, Not a Policy Improvement Trick

The paper's use of positive-advantage filtering — discarding all training samples where the estimated advantage is negative — is not presented as a novel algorithmic contribution (the idea appears in prior RL literature, both classical and LLM-focused). Rather, its innovation is in **the diagnostic function it serves**: the fact that filtering negative advantages substantially improves training stability in long-horizon settings reveals something about the *structure of the credit assignment problem* that was not previously articulated.

The conventional wisdom in RL is that both positive and negative advantages are necessary — the policy needs to learn what *not* to do as much as what to do. The PPO clipping mechanism is explicitly designed to handle negative advantages by preventing the policy from decreasing action probabilities too aggressively. Why, then, would completely discarding negative advantages improve stability?

The paper's results suggest an answer grounded in the temporal nature of long-horizon credit assignment. In a trajectory where the agent takes 100 actions and eventually fails, the critic — even when well-trained — will make errors in attributing the failure to specific actions. An action taken early in the trajectory that was perfectly correct (e.g., a well-timed early jump that collected a coin) might receive a negative advantage because the eventual trajectory outcome was poor, due to a mistake made 50 turns later. When the policy is penalized for this false-negative advantage, it decreases the probability of a good action, making it *less likely* to repeat the successful early behavior in future trajectories. Over many training steps, this noise accumulates and destabilizes learning.

Positive advantages, by contrast, are more reliable: if the observed return exceeded the critic's prediction, the action genuinely contributed to above-expected performance, even if some later actions were mediocre. The positive-advantage signal is robust to the critic's credit assignment errors because errors cannot *create* a positive advantage where none exists — they can only suppress it.

This interpretation positions positive-advantage filtering as a **robustness mechanism against the inevitable imperfection of temporal credit assignment in long horizons**, not as a general-purpose policy improvement. It predicts that the benefit of positive-advantage filtering should grow with the horizon length (since credit assignment degrades with horizon) and diminish as the critic becomes more accurate. The paper provides partial evidence for this prediction: in the algorithmic comparison (Figure 4a), PPO + Pos. Adv. outperforms vanilla PPO, and the gap appears most pronounced during the unstable early phase of training when the critic is still learning.

**Significance level:** This is a **practical insight with theoretical implications**. It provides a simple, implementable stabilization technique, but more importantly it reframes the discussion around positive vs. negative advantages in VLM-RL from "which works better?" to "what does the asymmetry reveal about credit assignment fidelity?" The paper explicitly connects this to the active debate in the field (citing Xiong et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025; Carrino et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2025) and positions its results as "additional empirical evidence that may help shed light on this direction."

**Evidence:** The algorithmic comparison in Figure 4a shows that positive-advantage filtering improves PPO performance (PPO + Pos. Adv. reaches higher game progress with less variance than vanilla PPO). The effect is also visible when added to GRPO with outcome rewards (Figure 7, panels c vs. d), though the benefit is modest because the underlying critic-free advantage estimates are already unreliable. The paper's discussion in Section 4.1 explicitly frames this as a stability mechanism and cites the broader debate.

---

### Innovation 4: Foundation Models as Action Priors — A Qualitative Change in the Learning Problem, Not Just a Warm Start

The comparison between VLM-based RL and classical deep RL (Section 5) is easy to misinterpret as merely demonstrating sample efficiency gains — roughly 2× fewer samples to reach comparable performance. But the deeper insight is about **what kind of prior the pretrained VLM provides and why it matters beyond efficiency**.

Classical deep RL with a CNN policy trained from scratch must solve two coupled problems simultaneously: learning to *see* (extracting task-relevant visual features from pixels) and learning to *act* (mapping those features to optimal button combinations). These problems are coupled because the features useful for perception depend on what actions the agent needs to take, and the optimal actions depend on what features the agent can reliably extract. This coupling is a fundamental source of sample inefficiency in visual RL — the agent spends many samples exploring visual representations that turn out to be useless for action selection.

A pretrained VLM decouples these problems. Its vision encoder has already learned to extract semantically meaningful features (object boundaries, spatial relationships, motion cues) from a diverse pretraining distribution. Its language model has already learned concepts like "jump over," "avoid," and "move toward" that map to action semantics. When RL fine-tunes this model, it operates on a representation space where "Mario is approaching a gap" is already a meaningful distinction from "Mario is on safe ground" — distinctions that a CNN trained from scratch would need thousands of rollouts to discover.

The evidence for this qualitative change is not just the 2× sample efficiency gain, but the **elimination of action-space engineering**. Classical deep RL with the original 22-action combinatorial space makes "only slow progress" and requires manual reduction to an 8-action "engineered" space to learn effectively (Figure 4b). The VLM handles the full 22-action space through natural language without any manual design, because its pretrained knowledge of what button combinations are semantically meaningful (e.g., ['a', 'right'] to "jump while moving right") provides the inductive bias that the engineered action space was hand-coded to provide. The VLM's prior replaces human engineering effort with world knowledge that generalizes — if a new button combination becomes useful, the VLM can discover it through RL, whereas the engineered action space would need to be manually expanded.

**Significance level:** This is a **conceptual reframing of what foundation models contribute to RL**. They are not just providing a warm start (better initialization → faster convergence) — they are changing the structure of the learning problem by providing semantically meaningful representations that decouple perception from action learning. This insight generalizes: any embodied RL task where the environment's action space has structure that aligns with human intuitive knowledge (which covers most tasks designed for human interaction) will benefit from VLM priors beyond what a simple parameter-initialization account would predict.

**Evidence:** Figure 4b shows the comparison. The VLM-based RL curve (reaching ~800 game progress at ~0.4M samples) dominates both classical RL curves. The classical RL with original action space barely reaches 200; with the engineered action space, it eventually approaches VLM performance but requires ~0.8M+ samples. Table 5 documents the engineered action space (8 hand-designed button combinations). The qualitative examples in Appendix F (Figures 9, 10) further illustrate that the base model already generates reasonable CoT reasoning about jumping and enemy avoidance — the prior is not just in the vision encoder but in the language model's understanding of game mechanics.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary benchmark is the Super Mario Land video game, with RL training performed on the first five levels (World 1 Level 1 through World 2 Level 2) and evaluation extended to all 10 levels of the game that share standard control mechanisms (excluding World 2 Level 3 and World 4 Level 3 due to "distinct control mechanisms"). For generalization tests, an additional 32 levels from the separate game Super Mario Bros. are used (Figure 6c). The paper does not use a traditional static dataset with train/test splits; instead, the environment itself generates interaction data during RL training, and evaluation is performed on held-out states and levels.

- **Base model(s).** All VLM experiments use Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025), an 8-billion-parameter vision-language model. The authors argue this model is "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and sits in a regime where zero-shot performance is non-trivial but far from saturation — the base model achieves only 270.23 ± 5.22 average game progress across training levels (Table 2), leaving substantial room for improvement via RL. The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 uses a model with approximately 14× more parameters as the pretraining-scaled baseline. For SFT initialization, GPT-o3 is used as a teacher model to generate CoT annotations.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **game progress**, defined as "the x-axis distance traveled by Mario from the start of each level" (Table 2 caption), measured in pixels read from game RAM. Since each level has a finite length (maximum progress ranging from 2191 to 2510 pixels across levels, as shown in the last row of Table 2), this metric provides a normalized measure of how far the agent advances before dying or timing out. Results are aggregated over multiple runs and reported as mean ± standard error: 8 runs for API-based proprietary models, 256 runs for open-source models (Section E.3). For sample efficiency comparisons (Section 5), game progress is plotted as a function of training samples (state-action pairs). For general-domain evaluations (Table 3), standard benchmark metrics are used: accuracy on MMMUval, MathVision, and RealWorldQA.

- **Baselines.** The paper evaluates against multiple categories:
  - **Frontier proprietary VLMs:** GPT-5.4, Gemini-3-Flash, Claude-Sonnet-4.6 (Table 2).
  - **Frontier open-source VLMs:** Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct, InternVL3.5-241B-A28B, GLM-4.6V (106B-A12B) (Table 2).
  - **Critic-free RL methods:** GRPO with outcome rewards, GRPO with process rewards, both with and without positive-advantage filtering, and Turn-level Reinforce++ (Hu, 2025) (Section 4.2, Figure 4a).
  - **Classical deep RL:** PPO training a CNN policy from scratch (Schulman et al., 2017), implemented via Stable-Baselines3 (Raffin et al., 2021b), with both the original 22-action space and an engineered 8-action space (Section 5, Table 5).
  - **Ablated Odysseus variants:** Odysseus-SFT (SFT only, no RL) and Odysseus-Zero (RL directly on base model, no SFT initialization) (Table 2).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For VLM-based methods, compute is measured in **number of training samples** (state-action pairs). The algorithmic comparison in Section 4.2 uses a limit of 0.38M samples (Figure 4a), while the sample efficiency comparison in Section 5 extends to 2M samples for classical RL (Figure 4b). Multi-task RL training runs for 190 training steps (Odysseus) or 175 steps (Odysseus-Zero) with 1024 trajectories per batch and a maximum of 400 turns per trajectory (Table 9). For evaluation, each model generates 8 or 256 rollouts per level depending on accessibility (API vs. open-source). The frame-skipping mechanism means one "turn" in VLM interaction corresponds to 5 or 15 emulator frames, so the effective sample count in terms of raw environment steps is higher. Classical RL baselines are run for up to 2M state-action pairs with evaluations every 10^4 samples over 128 trajectories.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use cross-validation in the traditional supervised learning sense, since training data is generated online through environment interaction. Instead, statistical reliability is addressed through:
  - **Multiple independent runs:** The algorithmic comparison curves in Figure 4a are averaged over "at least two independent runs" (Figure 4 caption), with individual runs plotted separately in Figures 7 and 8 to show variance.
  - **Large evaluation sample sizes:** 8 runs for API models, 256 runs for open-source models (Section E.3), with standard errors reported in Table 2. For the in-game generalization evaluations (Figures 6a and 6b), performance is averaged over 10 manually collected states per level, providing coverage of different starting conditions.
  - **EMA smoothing:** Training curves in Figure 4 use exponential moving average smoothing with factor 0.85 to reduce noise in visualization.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Algorithmic Comparison: Critic-Free Methods vs. PPO Variants (Section 4.2)

The paper's central algorithmic finding is that **PPO with a learned turn-level CNN critic substantially outperforms all critic-free methods** on a challenging single-level scenario in World 1 Level 1 of Super Mario Land (Figure 4a). The specific scenario requires "jumping over a tall pipe while avoiding two approaching enemies, followed by additional obstacles including platform gaps and additional enemies" (Section 4.2).

At 0.38M training samples:
- **PPO + Positive-Advantage Filtering** achieves approximately 700–800 game progress.
- **PPO without positive-advantage filtering** achieves approximately 600–700 game progress but with visibly higher variance across training steps.
- **GRPO with outcome rewards** — the only critic-free method that shows "observable improvements after training" — reaches approximately 200–400 game progress, with substantial run-to-run variation (Figure 7, panels c and d).
- **All other critic-free variants** (GRPO with process rewards, GRPO with process rewards + Pos. Adv., Reinforce++) remain essentially flat near 0–100 game progress throughout training (Figure 7, panels e–g).

The paper's interpretation: "critic-free methods (GRPO and Reinforce++) exhibit unstable learning dynamics and limited performance gains, regardless of reward design or the use of advantage filtering" (Section 4.2). The failure is not merely quantitative (lower asymptote) but qualitative — several methods show literally zero learning, suggesting a fundamental breakdown rather than slow convergence.

**Positive-advantage filtering provides incremental benefit on top of a working critic.** When added to PPO, it "improves training stability" — the PPO + Pos. Adv. curve in Figure 4a reaches higher performance with less variance. When added to critic-free methods, it does not rescue them: GRPO (outcome) + Pos. Adv. performs similarly to GRPO (outcome) without filtering (Figure 7, panel c vs. d), and GRPO (process) + Pos. Adv. still fails to learn (panel e vs. f). This demonstrates that positive-advantage filtering is complementary to, not substitutable for, a learned critic.

#### VLM-Based RL vs. Classical Deep RL (Section 5)

The hypothesis is that "pretrained VLMs provide strong inductive biases that reduce the exploration burden in long-horizon tasks" and consequently achieve higher sample efficiency than training from scratch (Section 5). The comparison is between VLM-based RL (PPO + turn-level CNN critic + positive-advantage filtering) and classical PPO training a CNN policy from scratch on the same single-level scenario.

At 2M training samples (Figure 4b):
- **VLM-based RL** reaches approximately 800 game progress at roughly 0.4M samples and maintains this performance.
- **Classical RL with the engineered 8-action space** eventually reaches comparable maximum performance (~800 game progress) but requires approximately 0.8–1.0M samples — roughly 2× more.
- **Classical RL with the original 22-action space** makes "only slow progress," barely exceeding 200 game progress even at 2M samples.

The paper reports that "VLM-based RL achieves roughly 2× higher sample efficiency, even without action-space engineering" (Figure 4b caption). The phrase "even without action-space engineering" is crucial: the VLM agent uses the full 22-action combinatorial space accessed through natural language, achieving better efficiency than the classical agent that required manual reduction to 8 carefully designed actions (Table 5). The classical agent with the full action space essentially fails to learn at all, highlighting that the VLM's pretrained knowledge provides a form of automatic action-space structuring that replaces the human engineering effort.

Individual runs are shown in Figure 8, revealing that classical RL with the engineered action space exhibits significant run-to-run variance — one run reaches ~800 while another stalls around 400 — whereas VLM-based RL appears more consistent across runs (though only two runs are shown).

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning Initialization: SFT Alone Doesn't Help, but Enables Better RL (Section 6.1, Table 2)

Table 2 reports performance on the five training levels for the base model, three Odysseus variants, and frontier models. The key comparisons within the Odysseus family:

- **Odysseus-SFT** (SFT only, no RL): achieves 261.77 ± 4.57 average progress, essentially identical to the base model (270.23 ± 5.22). The SFT alone "does not provide observable gains over the base model" (Section 7.1). This validates the paper's claim that the SFT stage teaches perception and domain knowledge but not optimal action control — any performance improvement must come from RL.
- **Odysseus-Zero** (RL directly on base, no SFT): achieves 1354.93 ± 13.68 average progress — roughly 5× the base model. RL alone is already powerful.
- **Odysseus** (SFT followed by RL): achieves 1511.90 ± 8.95 average progress — approximately 5.59× the base model and 1.12× Odysseus-Zero. The consistent improvement over Odysseus-Zero "across all levels" supports the paper's claim that "SFT initialization enables more effective RL training" (Section 7.1), with the benefit most pronounced on levels where the base model showed weakest perceptual grounding.

This is worth examining level-by-level from Table 2:
- **World 1 Level 2:** Base 129.14, Odysseus-Zero 1222.69, Odysseus 1430.88 — a gap of 208.19 between Zero and Odysseus.
- **World 2 Level 2:** Base 195.33, Odysseus-Zero 1192.71, Odysseus 1528.51 — a gap of 335.80.

The levels showing the largest gaps (W1-L2, W2-L2) are also those where the base model performed worst, supporting the interpretation that SFT is most beneficial when the base model most lacks domain knowledge.

#### Comparison with Frontier Models (Section 7.1, Table 2)

The headline finding from Table 2 is that Odysseus (1511.90 ± 8.95 average progress) dramatically outperforms all tested models:

| Model | Average Progress |
|---|---|
| **Odysseus (Ours)** | **1511.90 ± 8.95** |
| GLM-4.6V (best frontier) | 512.91 ± 4.46 |
| Claude-Sonnet-4.6 | 348.19 ± 22.61 |
| Gemini-3-Flash | 341.14 ± 23.09 |
| GPT-5.4 | 310.47 ± 25.95 |
| Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct | 279.45 ± 4.36 |
| Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct (base) | 270.23 ± 5.22 |
| InternVL3.5-241B-A28B | 259.13 ± 4.36 |

The improvement ratios: 5.59× over the base model, 2.95× over the best frontier model (GLM-4.6V). Notably, the largest open-source model (Qwen3-VL-235B, a 235B MoE model) performs only marginally better than the 8B base model (279.45 vs. 270.23), and several proprietary frontier models (GPT-5.4, Gemini-3-Flash) underperform the base model. This suggests that raw model scale without environment-specific training provides little benefit for this task — the capability gap is primarily about training methodology, not parameter count.

Level-by-level, the pattern varies:
- On **World 1 Level 1**, even weaker models achieve non-trivial progress (all are above 400), and GLM-4.6V reaches 731.28 — the only frontier model to break 500 on any level. Odysseus reaches 1644.43, roughly 2.25× GLM-4.6V.
- On **World 1 Level 2**, most frontier models collapse — GPT-5.4 achieves only 67.62, Gemini-3-Flash 255.88, base model 129.14. Odysseus reaches 1430.88, roughly 5.6× the best frontier model on this level (GLM-4.6V at 364.85, with unusually high standard error of 9.97 suggesting high variance).
- On **World 2 Level 1**, GLM-4.6V (478.46) and GPT-5.4 (252.75) show substantial variance, with Odysseus reaching 1352.30.

The across-the-board superiority on every individual level supports the paper's claim that the gains are robust rather than being driven by outlier performance on a single easy level.

#### Generalization Evaluations (Section 7.2, Figure 6)

The paper evaluates generalization under three progressively more challenging settings, using improvement relative to the base model as the metric to isolate the effect of training:

**In-game off-policy states (Figure 6a):** 50 manually curated states from the five training levels (10 per level), inducing a state distribution shift since these states were "sampled independently of the agent's trajectories." Odysseus achieves an average improvement of 32.2% over the base model. The per-level improvements show significant variation — World 1 Level 2 shows the largest gain, consistent with the largest absolute improvement in Table 2, but the paper does not provide numerical values per level in this figure (only histogram bar heights are shown).

**In-game unseen levels (Figure 6b):** 50 manually collected states from the five held-out levels of Super Mario Land (10 per level), which are "entirely unseen during RL training but share the same game mechanics and visual structure." Odysseus achieves an average improvement of 41.5% — notably higher than the 32.2% on off-policy states from training levels. This counterintuitive result (better generalization to unseen levels than to off-policy states from seen levels) may reflect that off-policy states include challenging edge cases that the agent encountered less frequently during training, whereas unseen levels share visual structure that the agent's learned features transfer to effectively. The paper does not explicitly discuss this discrepancy.

**Cross-game generalization (Figure 6c):** All 32 levels of Super Mario Bros., a different game with "more substantial domain shift in terms of level design and visual appearance." Odysseus achieves an average improvement of 23.1% over the base model across all 32 levels. The per-level histogram shows substantial variance — most levels cluster around the 23.1% average, with some levels showing near-zero improvement and some showing gains above 50%. The paper notes that "despite being trained on only five levels of Super Mario Land, Odysseus exhibits clear signs of both in-game and cross-game generalization" (Section 7.2).

A comparison across the three Odysseus variants (SFT, Zero, Odysseus) within Figure 6c shows that Odysseus-Zero and Odysseus achieve similar cross-game generalization, both outperforming Odysseus-SFT. This suggests that the RL training, not the SFT initialization, drives generalization. The SFT model's improvement over base on the cross-game setting is not explicitly quantified but appears minimal from the histogram.

#### General-Domain Capability Retention (Section 7.3, Table 3)

Table 3 reports performance on three general multi-modal benchmarks:

| Model | MMMUval | MathVision | RealWorldQA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct) | 69.00 | 54.64 | 71.11 |
| Odysseus-SFT | 70.44 | 55.00 | 71.37 |
| Odysseus-Zero | 70.22 | 54.44 | 70.72 |
| Odysseus (RL on SFT) | 70.77 | 53.52 | 71.11 |

The key finding: "compared to the base model, the Odysseus-series models retain comparable performances." MMMUval shows slight improvements (69.00 → 70.44–70.77), MathVision shows slight degradation (54.64 → 53.52–55.00), RealWorldQA is essentially flat (71.11 → 70.72–71.37). The maximum deviation from base is 1.77 percentage points on MMMUval (improvement) and 1.12 points on MathVision (degradation for Odysseus), both within typical benchmark variance.

This result addresses the "natural concern" that "extensive training in the game environment (over tens of millions of interaction samples) may cause the model to overfit and lose its general capabilities" (Section 7.3). The paper interprets the negligible degradation as evidence that the training pipeline preserves the pretrained model's general-purpose strengths. However, the evaluation is limited to three visual reasoning benchmarks — it does not test language-only capabilities, instruction following, or other domains where catastrophic forgetting might manifest differently.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Critic architecture: CNN vs. no critic (Figure 4a, Figure 7).** The comparison of PPO with CNN critic against all critic-free methods (GRPO variants, Reinforce++) serves as a de facto ablation establishing that the critic is necessary. Removing the critic entirely (i.e., using any critic-free method) causes performance to collapse to near-zero or unstable values (0–400 game progress vs. 600–800 with PPO). This is the strongest ablation signal in the paper — the learned critic is not an incremental improvement but a binary success/failure gate.

- **Positive-advantage filtering: PPO with vs. without (Figure 4a, Figure 7).** PPO + Pos. Adv. achieves approximately 700–800 game progress at 0.38M samples, while vanilla PPO reaches approximately 600–700 with higher variance. The benefit is stability rather than asymptote — both methods eventually reach comparable performance, but the filtered version gets there more consistently and with less run-to-run variation. When added to GRPO with outcome rewards, the effect is minimal (Figure 7, panel c vs. d), suggesting filtering amplifies but does not substitute for a good underlying advantage estimator.

- **SFT initialization: RL with vs. without (Table 2).** Odysseus (RL with SFT) outperforms Odysseus-Zero (RL without SFT) by 1511.90 vs. 1354.93 on average — a consistent ~12% relative improvement across all five training levels. This is presented as evidence that "SFT initialization enables more effective RL training" (Section 7.1). The SFT-only model (Odysseus-SFT) at 261.77 is essentially identical to base (270.23), confirming that SFT alone does not improve action control — it only conditions the model for more effective RL.

- **Multi-task training with auto-curriculum: uniform vs. inverse-weighted sampling (Section 6.2).** The paper describes the auto-curriculum mechanism but does not provide an explicit ablation comparing uniform sampling against inverse-weighted sampling on multi-task performance. The results in Table 2 are reported only for auto-curriculum training; we cannot quantify how much the curriculum contributes versus uniform sampling. This is a notable omission — given the paper's emphasis on the auto-curriculum as a key component of Odysseus, an ablation would have been informative.

- **Process rewards vs. outcome rewards for GRPO (Figure 7).** GRPO with outcome rewards (panels c, d) shows modest learning (200–400 game progress), while GRPO with process rewards (panels e, f) shows essentially none. This negative result reveals that process-level reward standardization (per-step reward normalization across the batch) is actively harmful compared to trajectory-level outcome feedback. The paper does not provide a mechanistic explanation for why, but one hypothesis is that standardizing per-step rewards destroys the scale information about which actions produce large vs. small forward progress — all steps become relative to the batch mean, eliminating the signal that some moves are genuinely more productive than others.

- **Classical RL action space: original 22-action vs. engineered 8-action (Figure 4b).** The original action space yields "only slow progress" (barely exceeding 200 at 2M samples), while the engineered action space reaches ~800. This ablation establishes that combinatorial action spaces are a fundamental challenge for classical RL in this domain and that human action-space engineering provides outsized benefits. The VLM agent handling the full 22-action space through natural language without manual engineering is the key comparison — the VLM effectively provides an automatic form of action-space structuring through pretrained knowledge of which button combinations are semantically meaningful.

- **Hidden dimension of the CNN critic (Table 7).** The critic uses hidden dimension 512. No sweep over hidden dimensions (e.g., 128, 256, 1024) is reported, so we cannot assess whether this is a sweet spot or whether the critic performance is robust to this choice.

- **Discount factor (Tables 4, 9).** Both the single-level experiment and multi-task training use discount factor γ = 0.95. No sweep over γ is reported. The choice of 0.95 rather than 0.99 (common in classical RL for long-horizon tasks) affects the effective horizon of credit assignment — with γ = 0.95, the contribution of a reward 40 turns in the future is 0.95^40 ≈ 0.128, meaning the critic discounts rewards beyond ~40 turns quite heavily. Whether this matters for a 100+ turn task is not explored.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper collectively demonstrate that a specific training recipe — lightweight SFT initialization followed by PPO with a small CNN turn-level critic and positive-advantage filtering — enables stable and effective RL training of an 8B VLM on a 100+ turn platformer game, producing agents that substantially outperform both the base model and all tested frontier models. The paper's central claims are largely supported by the evidence, but the strength of support varies considerably across claims, and several important boundaries of the findings remain untested.

**Claim: "An adapted PPO algorithm with a lightweight turn-level critic substantially improves training stability and sample efficiency over critic-free methods."** The evidence for this claim is the strongest in the paper. Figure 4a provides a clean, controlled comparison across seven algorithm variants at matched sample budgets. The result is not marginal — it's binary. Critic-free methods essentially fail to learn (flat curves, high variance), while PPO with CNN critic learns reliably. This is unlikely to be a statistical artifact because: (1) the gap is large (700+ vs. 0–400 game progress), (2) multiple critic-free variants were tested (outcome rewards, process rewards, with/without filtering), and (3) individual runs in Figure 7 confirm that no critic-free run achieved PPO-level performance. The finding that process-reward GRPO is *worse* than outcome-reward GRPO is itself informative and non-obvious, suggesting that naive per-step reward shaping can be actively harmful.

However, the evidence is limited to a single environment and a single model family. We cannot conclude that critic-free methods will *always* fail at 100+ turn horizons — only that they fail under the specific conditions tested (Mario platformer, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, forward progress reward, frame-skip mechanics). The failure mode — high variance in Monte Carlo returns — is theoretically well-understood as a function of horizon length, which gives the result some generalizability, but the exact horizon threshold at which the failure occurs is not characterized.

**Claim: "The critic can be designed as a remarkably lightweight module (CNN with 512 hidden units) rather than a second full VLM."** The evidence supports that the CNN critic *works* — PPO + CNN critic successfully trains the VLM. But the paper does not compare the CNN critic against a large-model token-level critic (as used in Zhai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a). This means we cannot conclude that the CNN critic is *equally effective* to a large-model critic, only that it is *sufficient*. A full-model critic might achieve even better sample efficiency or final performance — or it might be worse due to overfitting or optimization difficulties. The paper's claim is about efficiency and practicality, not about asymptotic performance parity, and the evidence for practical sufficiency is strong. But a reader interested in maximizing performance rather than minimizing cost would want to see at least a small-scale comparison against a token-level or VLM-based critic to understand the tradeoff.

Additionally, the CNN critic is trained only on pixels. The paper does not test whether providing the critic with additional information (e.g., the VLM's generated CoT text, a history of recent frames, or privileged game state) would improve credit assignment quality. The finding that raw pixels suffice for value estimation in a visually simple game may not transfer to more complex visual environments.

**Claim: "Positive-advantage filtering further improves training stability."** This claim is supported (Figure 4a shows PPO + Pos. Adv. outperforming vanilla PPO, particularly in the noisy early-training regime) but the effect size is modest and the evidence is limited. Only two independent runs are shown for each method (Figure 7), and the PPO without filtering runs show significant variance — one run reaches comparable performance to the filtered version while the other stalls lower. This could reflect genuine instability that filtering mitigates, or it could be insufficient statistical power to distinguish two methods that perform similarly on average. A larger-scale comparison with more seeds would be needed to confidently claim that filtering provides a systematic benefit rather than occasionally preventing unlucky runs.

The paper frames positive-advantage filtering as a stabilization mechanism whose benefit derives from the unreliability of negative advantages in long-horizon credit assignment. This is theoretically plausible but not directly tested — we don't know, for example, whether the benefit of filtering correlates with horizon length (the mechanism predicts it should) or whether filtering becomes unnecessary with a better-trained critic. An experiment varying horizon length and measuring the filtering benefit would strengthen the mechanistic claim.

**Claim: "VLM-based RL is significantly more sample-efficient than training agents from scratch with classical deep RL, highlighting the value of encoded knowledge priors."** The evidence supports this claim with qualifications. Figure 4b shows approximately 2× better sample efficiency for VLM-based RL compared to classical PPO with an engineered action space. The claim that VLMs "reduce the need for manual designs such as action space engineering" is strongly supported — the VLM handles the full 22-action space while classical RL requires human engineering to 8 actions to learn at all.

However, the classical RL baseline may not be the strongest possible. The paper uses PPO with a standard CNN policy from Stable-Baselines3 — a solid baseline, but not necessarily state-of-the-art for sample-efficient visual RL. Methods like DrQ, SPR, or EfficientZero have demonstrated substantially better sample efficiency on Atari than vanilla PPO. The 2× figure should be understood relative to a specific classical baseline, not relative to the best possible classical approach. Additionally, the classical RL baseline uses a fixed number of training steps (2M), while the VLM-based RL uses the same number of *state-action pairs* — but the VLM's samples are generated by a policy with strong priors, so early training samples are of higher quality (the agent survives longer, sees more diverse states). This creates a virtuous cycle not available to the randomly-initialized classical agent. The comparison is fair in terms of raw sample count, but the "sample efficiency" advantage may partly reflect better exploration from the pretrained prior rather than faster convergence per informative sample.

A missing comparison: classical RL initialized with a pretrained visual encoder (e.g., a frozen ResNet or ViT backbone) rather than training the CNN from scratch. This would isolate whether the VLM's benefit comes from its visual representations, its language understanding, its CoT reasoning, or some combination. The paper claims the VLM provides "action priors" that reduce the need for action-space engineering, implying that language understanding specifically (not just visual pretraining) is responsible for this benefit. Testing a visual-encoder-only initialization would distinguish these hypotheses.

**Claim: "Odysseus achieves at least 3× average game progress over frontier models."** This claim is quantitatively supported by Table 2 (1511.90 vs. 512.91 for best frontier model = 2.95×), but interpreting what this comparison *means* requires caution. The frontier models are evaluated zero-shot — they receive the system prompt and game frame but undergo no training of any kind. Odysseus undergoes SFT + ~190 steps of multi-task RL with ~10^8 interaction samples. The comparison demonstrates that RL training produces a dramatically better agent than prompting alone, which is expected. The more meaningful comparison is against what frontier models could achieve with the same training pipeline — but this is not tested (and would be infeasible for proprietary API-only models).

The comparison also conflates multiple factors: the training methodology (RL vs. zero-shot prompting), the amount of environment interaction, and the model architecture. The fact that a 106B-A12B MoE model (GLM-4.6V) outperforms a 235B MoE model (Qwen3-VL-235B) in zero-shot evaluation suggests that parameter count alone does not predict game-playing ability — model-specific factors (training data mix, vision encoder quality, instruction tuning) dominate. This makes the Odysseus-to-frontier comparison difficult to interpret causally.

**Claim: "The trained models exhibit consistent improvements under both in-game and cross-game generalization settings."** The evidence (Figures 6a–6c) supports that Odysseus improves over the base model in all three generalization settings, with average relative improvements of 32.2%, 41.5%, and 23.1%. However, the generalization evaluation has several limitations:

1. **Small evaluation sets:** 10 manually collected states per level for in-game evaluations means 50 total states per setting. Performance on these 50 states may not represent performance across the full state distribution.
2. **No trajectory-level evaluation:** The in-game generalization tests use static starting states — the agent takes one action from a fixed frame. This measures reactive decision quality but not the closed-loop performance that matters in actual gameplay, where each action produces a new state that may be out of distribution relative to the hand-picked starting states. An evaluation that runs the agent from level start on unseen levels and measures full-trajectory progress would be more convincing.
3. **Cross-game is tested only from level start:** Figure 6c evaluates from the beginning of each Super Mario Bros. level, which allows trajectory-level assessment. The 23.1% average improvement is meaningful, but the per-level variance (some levels show near-zero improvement) suggests that generalization is uneven — the agent transfers well to levels that visually resemble training levels and poorly to levels with novel mechanics or appearances.

The cross-game result is the most impressive generalization signal, showing that the trained agent extracts transferable skills (jumping over gaps, avoiding enemies) rather than memorizing level layouts. But the fact that improvement over base is smaller (23.1%) than for unseen levels of the same game (41.5%) confirms that there is a domain gap that RL training does not fully bridge.

**Claim: "The resulting agents retain their general-domain capabilities."** Table 3 shows minimal degradation on three benchmarks. This is a positive result, but three benchmarks — all in the visual reasoning domain — do not constitute a comprehensive capability retention evaluation. The model might have forgotten language-only skills (translation, summarization, coding), instruction-following abilities, or safety alignment that are not captured by MMMU, MathVision, and RealWorldQA. The paper's claim that training "does not compromise the model's general-purpose strengths" is stated more strongly than the evidence warrants. A broader evaluation suite would be needed to support this claim confidently.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Horizon-length sweep for critic-free vs. critic methods.** Testing at 10, 30, 50, 100, 200 turns would characterize where the failure threshold lies and validate the theoretical mechanism.
2. **Comparison against a token-level VLM critic.** Even a small-scale experiment (e.g., a LoRA-based critic rather than full fine-tuning) would quantify the performance gap or lack thereof between lightweight CNN and full VLM critics.
3. **Ablation of the auto-curriculum.** Uniform vs. inverse-weighted level sampling in multi-task training.
4. **Classical RL with pretrained visual backbone.** Isolates visual priors vs. language priors.
5. **Full-trajectory evaluation on unseen levels.** Rather than static starting states, run the agent from level start on held-out levels.
6. **More independent runs.** Two runs per method (Figures 7, 8) provides limited statistical power; 5+ runs would allow meaningful uncertainty quantification.
7. **Larger-scale positive-advantage filtering comparison.** Does the benefit persist with more runs, or is it within noise?
8. **Broader general-capability evaluation.** Include language-only benchmarks, instruction-following tests, and safety evaluations.

**Summary of evidential strength:** The paper's strongest contribution — that a lightweight CNN critic is sufficient and necessary for stable VLM-RL in long-horizon settings — is well-supported by a clean algorithmic comparison with multiple baselines. The full Odysseus system's performance gains over base models and frontier models are substantial and consistent across levels. The generalization and capability-retention results are positive but limited in scope. The sample efficiency comparison against classical RL is suggestive but would benefit from stronger baselines and more ablation of what specifically the VLM prior contributes. The paper's methodological contribution is clear; its claims about practical state-of-the-art performance should be interpreted with an understanding of the specific (and limited) zero-shot baselines it compares against.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Not Accounted For in the Proposed Framework

**The assumption or constraint.** The core of the Odysseus training framework does not involve explicit difficulty estimation (unlike the compute-optimal scaling paper in the reference example). However, the paper does rely on an **auto-curriculum mechanism** (Section 6.2) that dynamically adjusts level sampling weights based on average trajectory length `$N_k$`, computed from each batch. While this mechanism is adaptive and computationally cheap (it only requires tracking trajectory lengths), the paper **never accounts for any overhead of multi-task coordination** in its efficiency claims. More significantly, the paper acknowledges in Section 6.1 that the SFT initialization stage requires a stronger teacher model (GPT-o3) to annotate ~5,000 frames, stating:

> "a stronger model (in our case, GPT-o3) is used to generate teacher responses"

This teacher model's inference cost — approximately 5,000 calls to a proprietary API model with structured CoT generation — is **not amortized** into any of the reported sample efficiency or compute comparisons. The paper treats this as a fixed upfront cost, but for a practitioner attempting to replicate or extend the pipeline to a new domain, this cost is a prerequisite that must be paid before any RL training can begin.

**The consequence.** A practitioner seeking to apply Odysseus to a new game or environment must either: (1) have API access to a model at least as capable as GPT-o3, (2) pay the inference cost for annotating thousands of frames, or (3) develop an alternative method for initializing domain knowledge. For environments where no sufficiently strong teacher model exists (niche games, proprietary simulators, novel robotic tasks), the SFT initialization stage may be impossible or require expensive human annotation instead. The paper's claim that this approach is "inherently more scalable" because "game-play videos are far more readily available than collecting expert trajectories with actions" (Section 6.1) is true for video availability but does not address the dependence on a strong *teacher model* — the bottleneck shifts from data collection to model capability.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper demonstrates in Table 2 that SFT-only (Odysseus-SFT, 261.77 average progress) does not improve over the base model (270.23), but that RL trained on top of SFT (Odysseus, 1511.90) outperforms RL without SFT (Odysseus-Zero, 1354.93) by approximately 12%. This establishes that the SFT stage provides value. However, **no ablation studies vary the teacher model or the quantity of SFT data** — we do not know whether a weaker teacher model would suffice, whether 1,000 frames would be enough, or whether the quality threshold for SFT annotations is high. The paper does not report the cost (in API calls or dollars) of generating the 5,000 GPT-o3 annotations.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation beyond framing the SFT stage as "intentionally lightweight compared with previous works" (Section 6.1). It does not propose methods for reducing teacher model dependence, such as using a smaller open-source model for annotation or self-supervised pretraining on gameplay videos. The paper's suggestion that "game-play videos are far more readily available" points toward future work on learning from video without action labels, but this is not explored.

### Classification: All Results Are on a Single Game Genre with a Single Model Family

**The assumption or constraint.** Every experiment in the paper uses exactly one environment (*Super Mario Land*) and one base model family (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct). The algorithmic comparison (Section 4.2), the classical RL comparison (Section 5), the multi-task training (Section 6), and all generalization evaluations (Section 7.2) are conducted within this narrow configuration. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by positioning *Super Mario Land* as "a compact but appealing testbed" (Section 2) and by testing cross-game generalization to *Super Mario Bros.* (Section 7.2), but the **core algorithmic findings — that a lightweight CNN critic is necessary and sufficient, that positive-advantage filtering stabilizes training, that critic-free methods fail — are validated on only one game and one VLM.**

**The consequence.** Several aspects of the paper's findings could be specific to the environment or model and fail to transfer:

- **The critic's sufficiency:** The CNN critic succeeds because *Super Mario Land* is a side-scrolling platformer where forward progress (the x-coordinate reward) is visually correlated with specific pixel patterns (Mario's position relative to the left edge, scrolling speed). In a game with more abstract state (e.g., an RPG with inventory management) or a task where value depends on non-visual context (e.g., dialogue choices), a CNN processing only the current frame may be insufficient — the critic might need memory, text understanding, or longer observation history.
- **The action space:** The VLM's "strong action priors" (Section 5) leverage the fact that button combinations in a platformer map intuitively to language ("jump right" → `['a', 'right']`). In domains where actions are not linguistically natural (e.g., continuous control of a robotic arm with joint angles, or a game with dozens of abstract keyboard shortcuts), the pretrained VLM's knowledge may provide little or no useful prior.
- **The reward density:** The x-coordinate reward provides a dense signal at every turn — every correct action produces immediate, measurable forward progress. In sparse-reward environments (e.g., completing a puzzle, winning a strategy game), the credit assignment problem becomes dramatically harder, and it is unclear whether the lightweight CNN critic would still suffice or whether the advantage of critic-based methods over critic-free methods would be even larger or smaller.
- **The horizon structure:** The paper's central finding — that critic-free methods fail at 100+ turns — is demonstrated on a game where the effective horizon is genuinely long but where each turn's reward is local and predictable. In environments with long horizons but delayed, stochastic, or compositional rewards, the failure threshold for critic-free methods might occur at different horizon lengths or manifest differently.

The paper's cross-game generalization to *Super Mario Bros.* (Section 7.2, Figure 6c) provides partial evidence that the learned skills transfer within the same genre (2D platformers), but this is still a narrow transfer: both games share side-scrolling mechanics, similar visual structure, and identical control concepts (jumping, running, avoiding enemies). The 23.1% improvement over the base model confirms some generalization but does not address whether the algorithmic recipe would work for a different game genre, let alone a non-game embodied task.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** All results in Figures 4, 7, 8 and Tables 2, 3 are from *Super Mario Land* with Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct. The cross-game evaluation in Figure 6c uses *Super Mario Bros.* but only tests the final trained model's zero-shot performance — it does not evaluate whether the *training pipeline* (SFT + PPO with CNN critic) would work if applied to *Super Mario Bros.* directly. We know the trained policy transfers somewhat, but we do not know whether the training algorithm itself transfers to new games.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to test the training pipeline on additional environments. It acknowledges the narrowness of the testbed by describing *Super Mario Land* as a "compact but appealing testbed" and arguing it is "representative" of long-horizon embodied control, but this claim is not empirically supported. The cross-game evaluation is presented as evidence of generalization capability in the *agent*, not as evidence of the training recipe's portability. A practitioner interested in applying Odysseus to a novel domain must treat the algorithmic findings as suggestive rather than validated.

### Classification: The CNN Critic's Architecture and Input Are Not Ablated, Leaving the "Lightweight" Claim Underdetermined

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's headline architectural insight is that the critic can be "a remarkably lightweight module" (Section 4.1) — specifically, a CNN with 512 hidden units following the NatureCNN architecture. The paper contrasts this with prior work that uses "a large-model-based token-level critic, which nearly doubles the memory and computation costs" (Section 4.1). However, the paper **never compares the CNN critic against a larger critic** — not a token-level VLM critic, not a larger CNN, not a CNN with additional inputs (frame history, text features from the VLM's CoT). The claim that the CNN critic is *sufficient* is well-supported (Figure 4a shows PPO + CNN critic works). The claim that a larger critic is *unnecessary* is not tested.

**The consequence.** This has two implications for practitioners attempting to extend the approach:

1. **The critic might be leaving performance on the table.** A more powerful critic — perhaps with a deeper architecture, frame stacking for motion detection, or access to the VLM's CoT embeddings — could produce better value estimates, enabling more efficient credit assignment and faster or more stable training. The paper's results establish a lower bound on what is possible with a lightweight critic but do not characterize the performance ceiling. For a practitioner with sufficient compute to train a larger critic, the paper provides no guidance on whether that investment would yield returns.

2. **The critic's success may depend on the specific visual simplicity of *Super Mario Land*.** The game has low-resolution pixel art with clear visual indicators of state (Mario's position, distinct enemy sprites, blocky platforms). A CNN with 512 hidden units may be adequate for this visual complexity but insufficient for photorealistic environments, 3D worlds, or tasks where value depends on fine-grained visual details (e.g., reading text on signs, identifying specific objects in cluttered scenes). The paper provides no characterization of how critic capacity requirements scale with visual complexity.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 7 lists the critic hyperparameters (hidden dimension 512, NatureCNN backbone) but provides no sweep over architecture sizes or input modalities. The comparison in Figure 4a is between CNN critic and *no critic* (critic-free methods), not between CNN critic and alternative critic designs. Section 4.1 states that "CNN critics, which are sufficient for classical deep RL, can already effectively stabilize training and lead to improved performance" — this implies sufficiency but explicitly avoids claiming optimality or characterizing the tradeoff curve.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. It presents the CNN critic as a practical finding (it works and is cheap) rather than as a claim of optimality. The paper's framing — "by delegating turn-level value estimation to a small module, we can drastically reduce the memory and computational bottlenecks" (Section 4.1) — emphasizes the efficiency benefit over prior large-model critics but stops short of claiming that no larger critic could improve performance. A practitioner reading the paper should understand that 512 hidden units in a NatureCNN is a demonstrated *working configuration*, not a proven *optimal configuration*.

### Classification: Generalization Evaluations Use Static Starting States Rather Than Closed-Loop Trajectory Rollouts

**The assumption or constraint.** The in-game generalization evaluations (Figures 6a and 6b) use a protocol where the agent is tested on "10 manually collected states per level" (Section 7.2) and generates a single action from each static frame. The paper explicitly describes these as states that are "from the same training levels [but] sampled independently of the agent's trajectories" (off-policy) or from "the remaining five levels of Super Mario Land... entirely unseen during RL training" (in-game unseen). However, **the evaluation measures only single-step decision quality from hand-picked frames, not the closed-loop trajectory performance** that determines actual game progress. After the agent takes one action from the test state, the resulting new state is not evaluated — the episode ends.

**The consequence.** Single-step evaluation from curated states can overestimate or underestimate true closed-loop capability:

- **Overestimation risk:** A hand-picked state might be one where the correct action is obvious (e.g., Mario is already in the air approaching a platform — pressing 'right' is trivially correct), but the agent's real weakness is in the sequence of decisions that *led* to that state or that follow from it. The agent might correctly identify what to do when shown a frame of an impending gap but fail to execute the correct sequence of jumps in real-time gameplay because it cannot maintain consistent visual grounding across multiple frames.
- **Underestimation risk:** Conversely, a hand-picked state might be an edge case (e.g., Mario in an unusual position that rarely occurs during normal play) where even a capable agent struggles, deflating the measured generalization performance.
- **Compounding errors:** The defining challenge of long-horizon decision-making is that small errors compound — a slightly mistimed jump leads to a slightly worse landing position, which makes the next jump harder, which leads to falling into a gap 10 turns later. Single-step evaluation cannot capture this compounding dynamic, which is precisely the phenomenon that makes long-horizon RL difficult and that the paper's algorithmic contributions are designed to address.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper's descriptions of the evaluation in Section 7.2 and Section E.3 make clear that these are static-state evaluations. For in-game settings (Figures 6a, 6b): "10 manually collected states per level from the five training levels" and "10 manually collected states per level from the five held-out levels." The reported improvements (32.2% and 41.5%) are computed as the average over these 50 static states. The cross-game evaluation (Figure 6c) is different — it "test[s] the agent on all 32 levels of another game Super Mario Bros." and reports performance "from the start of each level" (Figure 6 caption), which does capture closed-loop trajectory performance. However, this trajectory-level evaluation is only done for cross-game generalization, not for the held-out *Super Mario Land* levels that share mechanics and visual structure with the training environment — the setting where closed-loop performance would be most informative.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses this by providing trajectory-level evaluation for the **training levels themselves** (Table 2, which reports full-trajectory game progress from level start) and for the **cross-game setting** (Figure 6c, from level start). But the in-game generalization to unseen *Super Mario Land* levels — the most direct test of whether RL training produces transferable skills within the same game — is evaluated only on static states. The paper does not report what happens when Odysseus runs from the beginning of World 3 Level 1, which would be the natural closed-loop generalization test. This omission is notable because unseen-same-game evaluation is the setting most likely to succeed (shared mechanics, similar visuals) and would provide the strongest evidence that the agent has learned generalizable skills rather than memorizing training-level layouts.

### Classification: Positive-Advantage Filtering's Benefit Is Not Statistically Validated and Its Mechanism Is Not Directly Tested

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper presents positive-advantage filtering as a key algorithmic ingredient that "improves training stability" (Section 4.1) and demonstrates this via the comparison between PPO and PPO + Pos. Adv. in Figure 4a. However, the evidence for this benefit comes from **only two independent runs per method** (Figure 7 shows Run 1 and Run 2 for each configuration). With such a small sample size, it is impossible to distinguish a genuine systematic benefit from run-to-run variance — the fact that the two PPO + Pos. Adv. runs both reached high performance while one of the two vanilla PPO runs stalled could reflect filtering's effectiveness, or it could reflect that vanilla PPO happened to have one unlucky seed.

**The consequence.** A practitioner implementing Odysseus must decide whether to include positive-advantage filtering. The paper's recommendation is clear (include it), but the evidence supporting this recommendation is fragile:

- **If the benefit is real and systematic:** Filtering is a low-cost addition (it requires only a sign check on computed advantages, negligible computation) that improves stability. Including it is a pure win.
- **If the benefit is spurious or small:** The filtering introduces a potential bias — the policy is never explicitly penalized for bad actions, which could lead to suboptimal convergence in some environments or reward structures. The paper's theoretical motivation (Section 4.1) relies on the claim that "negative-advantage samples can destabilize optimization," but this claim is supported by citations to prior work (Xiong et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2025) studying different settings (LLM reasoning, not VLM game-playing), not by experiments within this paper.

Moreover, the paper does not test the claimed mechanism — that negative advantages are unreliable due to long-horizon credit assignment errors. An experiment that varied the horizon length and measured the filtering benefit would test this mechanism. An experiment that compared the critic's advantage estimates against ground-truth action values (obtained through exhaustive rollouts) would quantify how often negative advantages are genuinely "wrong." Neither experiment is performed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4a shows the aggregate comparison: PPO + Pos. Adv. curve is higher and smoother than PPO. Figure 7 shows the individual runs: for PPO + Pos. Adv., both runs reach ~800 game progress; for vanilla PPO, Run 1 reaches ~800 but Run 2 stalls around ~400. Table 2 uses positive-advantage filtering in both Odysseus-Zero and Odysseus (the RL training configurations). The algorithmic ablation in Section 4.2 compares PPO with and without filtering and states that "positive-advantage filtering improves training stability when combined with PPO." The paper acknowledges uncertainty by noting that "the role of positive versus negative advantage samples in RL training remains an actively studied question" (Section 4.1), citing Carrino et al. (2026) and Zhu et al. (2025) as counterpoints.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the limited evidence: it acknowledges the broader debate on positive vs. negative advantage samples and positions its results as "additional empirical evidence that may help shed light on this direction" (Section 4.1) rather than as a definitive resolution. The filtering mechanism is simple and computationally cheap, so its inclusion in the Odysseus recipe is a low-risk recommendation even if the benefit turns out to be modest. However, the paper's decision to include only two runs per method substantially weakens the statistical basis for claiming that filtering is a necessary or consistently beneficial component of the training recipe — rather than a useful heuristic that happened to help in these specific runs.

### Classification: The Training vs. Inference Efficiency Tradeoff Is Not Analyzed

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper measures sample efficiency during training (Figures 4a, 4b) and reports final game progress at inference time (Table 2), but it **never analyzes the relationship between these two regimes**. Specifically, as the VLM agent improves through RL training, the length of its trajectories increases dramatically — the base model averages ~270 pixels of progress (roughly 10–20 turns before dying), while Odysseus averages ~1,512 pixels (potentially exceeding 100 turns and approaching full level completion). This means that **each training rollout becomes progressively more expensive as the agent improves**, because the VLM must perform forward passes for every turn of a longer trajectory. The paper's sample efficiency metrics count "state-action pairs" or "training samples" but not wall-clock time or total FLOPs, which are the resources that constrain practical training.

Additionally, at inference time, a trained Odysseus agent generating a full-level trajectory of 100+ turns must perform 100+ VLM forward passes — each generating a structured CoT with perception, reasoning, and action tags. This inference cost is orders of magnitude higher than classical deep RL agents (a single CNN forward pass per action) or even zero-shot prompted VLMs (which die quickly and thus incur few forward passes). The paper does not discuss this inference-time cost or compare it against alternatives.

**The consequence.** The paper's claims about efficiency must be interpreted carefully:

- **Training efficiency:** The reported 2× sample efficiency advantage over classical deep RL (Figure 4b) counts state-action pairs. But as training progresses, VLM-based RL generates samples from longer trajectories (the agent survives longer), while classical RL generates samples from consistently short trajectories early in training. A fair wall-clock comparison would account for the fact that VLM forward passes are ~4,000–8,000× more expensive than CNN forward passes (8B parameters vs. ~1–2M for the CNN policy), potentially negating or reversing the sample-count advantage when measured in FLOPs or GPU-hours. The paper does not report training wall-clock time for either method.

- **Inference efficiency:** A deployed Odysseus agent that reaches World 1 Level 2 and survives 100+ turns executes perhaps 100–150 VLM forward passes, each generating 500–1,000 tokens of CoT. For an 8B model, this represents a substantial per-episode inference cost — likely seconds to minutes of GPU time depending on hardware. A classical CNN policy processes each frame in microseconds. For applications where inference latency or throughput matters (real-time gameplay, large-scale evaluation), the VLM agent's computational cost may be prohibitive regardless of its superior game progress.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not provide:
- Wall-clock training time for any experiment.
- FLOP counts for training or inference.
- Inference latency measurements for Odysseus agents.
- Any comparison of total computational cost (training + inference) between VLM-based and classical approaches.

The only compute-related metrics are "training samples" (Figures 4, 7, 8) and "generations" (the number of turns in a trajectory, Tables 4 and 9). Table 9 specifies that multi-task RL training runs for "190 training steps" with "1024 trajectories per batch" and "max turns = 400" — this gives an upper bound on the number of VLM forward passes (~190 × 1024 × 400 ≈ 78 million token-generation calls) but the actual number is lower because most trajectories terminate before 400 turns.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation at all. It is focused on demonstrating that RL *can work* for VLM training in long-horizon settings, not on characterizing the practical cost of doing so. The paper's emphasis on the lightweight CNN critic as reducing "memory and computational bottlenecks" (Section 4.1) addresses the *marginal* cost of adding a critic to VLM training but does not address the *total* cost of VLM training relative to alternatives. A practitioner deciding whether to invest in VLM-based RL versus classical methods or SFT-only approaches receives no guidance from this paper on the practical resource requirements.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around VLM-based embodied agents from a methodological monoculture — where supervised fine-tuning on expert demonstrations was treated as the only reliable path — toward a hybrid paradigm where RL is not merely viable but **quantitatively superior** for action optimization, provided it is properly stabilized. Before this work, the field had largely converged on an implicit consensus: VLMs are powerful perception and reasoning engines, but teaching them to *act* in environments requires curated expert trajectories because RL is too unstable, too sample-inefficient, or too computationally expensive for the long-horizon settings that matter. This consensus was not unfounded — it was supported by the observation that RL-for-VLM efforts consistently operated in 20–30 turn sandboxes and that attempts to push beyond those horizons either failed silently or required such complex machinery (hierarchical credit assignment, token-level critics doubling memory) as to be impractical.

Odysseus breaks this consensus by demonstrating that the instability was not an inherent property of RL-for-VLMs but rather a symptom of applying the **wrong RL algorithms** to the wrong horizon regime. The key intellectual move is the realization that the critic-free methods (GRPO, Reinforce++) that succeeded brilliantly in short-horizon reasoning and alignment tasks — where per-step credit assignment is relatively easy and a learned baseline is a luxury — undergo a **qualitative failure mode** when the horizon stretches to 100+ turns. Figure 4a makes this failure mode concrete: several critic-free configurations show literally zero learning, not merely slower convergence. The learned critic is not an incremental improvement; it is the **gatekeeper** that determines whether RL is even possible.

This reframing has several cascading implications for how the field should think about algorithm selection:

**It changes the default starting point for VLM-RL research.** Prior to this work, a researcher beginning a new VLM-RL project would reasonably start with GRPO — it is simpler, widely used, and computationally cheaper. The paper argues this is the wrong default for long-horizon settings. The new default should be actor-critic methods with a learned value function, with the critic-free option reserved only for short-horizon tasks where the horizon is demonstrably below the failure threshold. The paper does not precisely locate this threshold (it is likely environment-dependent), but the binary nature of the failure in Figure 4a — flat lines versus steady improvement — provides a diagnostic: if critic-free methods show zero or unstable learning, a learned critic is likely necessary, not optional.

**It decouples the actor and critic architectures as a design principle, not just an efficiency trick.** Prior actor-critic VLM work (Zhai et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025a) implicitly assumed architectural symmetry — the critic should be of comparable capacity to the actor because both process the same inputs and reason about the same environment. The paper's demonstration that a ~1–2 million parameter CNN suffices as a critic for an 8B-parameter VLM actor overturns this assumption. More importantly, it reveals that the value function and the policy operate on **fundamentally different representational requirements**: the VLM needs semantic understanding, language grounding, and multi-step reasoning; the critic needs to extract spatial features from pixels and predict a scalar. These tasks are not merely different in complexity — they are different in kind, and forcing them into the same architecture wastes capacity. This insight should guide future system design: the critic should be chosen to match the *structure of the value estimation problem*, not the *size of the policy model*.

**It provides a unifying explanation for conflicting prior results.** The paper implicitly reconciles why GRPO succeeds for mathematical reasoning (Guo et al., 2025) and preference alignment (Ouyang et al., 2022) but the RL-for-embodied-VLM literature gravitated toward SFT. The key variable is **effective horizon**. Mathematical reasoning problems typically involve generating a single solution (effective horizon ~1, even if the solution contains many tokens, because the reward is outcome-based and there is no environment feedback), or at most a few sequential refinements. GRPO's batch-level advantage normalization works well in this regime because the variance of the return is manageable when the number of decisions per trajectory is small. Embodied tasks with 100+ turns of closed-loop interaction represent a fundamentally different credit assignment problem that GRPO was never designed to handle. This explanation is testable — it predicts a phase transition in GRPO performance as horizon increases, which future work could characterize.

**It reopens the RL-versus-SFT debate with a more nuanced resolution.** The paper's design — lightweight SFT for perception and domain knowledge, followed by RL for action optimization — suggests that the field's polarization into "SFT-only" and "RL-only" camps was a false dichotomy. Table 2 shows that neither alone is sufficient: SFT-only (Odysseus-SFT) performs identically to the base model, while RL-only (Odysseus-Zero) achieves substantial gains but is consistently outperformed by the combined approach (Odysseus). The SFT stage teaches the model *what it's looking at* (distinguishing enemies from power-ups, understanding pipe mechanics); the RL stage teaches it *what to do about it* (optimal jump timing, when to run versus wait). This division of labor is both conceptually clean and empirically validated, and it provides a template for future embodied VLM systems: use SFT to inject domain knowledge from readily available sources (gameplay videos, instruction manuals) and RL to optimize the decision policy that SFT alone cannot perfect.

**It redirects attention from specialized RL machinery to fundamental algorithmic hygiene.** The paper explicitly contrasts itself with prior work that "often introduce specialized machinery for trajectory decomposition, token-level advantage estimation, or hierarchical credit assignment" (Section 2). By showing that classical PPO with a properly designed critic and a simple filtering heuristic suffices, the paper argues — implicitly but clearly — that the field has been overcomplicating its solutions. Many of the specialized techniques developed for short-horizon VLM-RL may have been patches for problems that disappear when the underlying credit assignment is handled properly via a learned value function. This suggests a research strategy of **starting simple and adding complexity only where proven necessary**, with the PPO + CNN critic + positive-advantage filtering combination serving as the new minimal viable baseline against which more complex methods must justify themselves.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Characterizing the horizon-dependent phase transition between critic-free and critic-based RL.** The paper establishes that at ~100 turns, critic-free methods fail while PPO with a learned critic succeeds. But where exactly is the threshold? An experiment that sweeps effective horizon (10, 20, 30, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200 turns) in a controlled environment — perhaps by truncating trajectories at fixed lengths while keeping all other factors constant — would map the failure curve of GRPO and Reinforce++ as a function of horizon. The prediction is that performance degrades smoothly at first (variance increases) and then collapses sharply when the Monte Carlo return variance exceeds the learning rate's capacity to extract signal. Characterizing this threshold as a function of environment stochasticity, reward density, and action space size would transform the paper's empirical observation into a generalizable diagnostic tool: given an environment description, a practitioner could predict whether critic-free methods are viable or whether a learned critic is likely necessary.

**Ablating what specifically the VLM prior contributes beyond visual pretraining.** The paper's claim that pretrained VLMs provide "strong action priors" (Section 5) is supported by the comparison against classical deep RL — the VLM agent handles the 22-action space through natural language while the classical agent requires manual engineering to 8 actions (Table 5). But this comparison conflates multiple factors: visual pretraining, language understanding, CoT reasoning, and world knowledge about game mechanics. A targeted ablation would test: (1) a classical RL agent with a frozen or fine-tuned pretrained vision encoder (e.g., DINOv2 or CLIP ViT) but no language model — this isolates visual priors; (2) a VLM-based agent with the CoT ablated (outputting only actions, no perception/reasoning tags) — this isolates the value of structured reasoning; (3) a VLM fine-tuned on a scrambled action space (where button names are randomly reassigned, e.g., 'a' now means 'left') — this tests whether the prior is genuinely about action semantics or merely about the VLM's ability to represent combinatorial spaces more efficiently. The paper's qualitative examples in Appendix F (Figures 9, 10) already hint at what the CoT contributes — the base model fails because it misidentifies objects or applies wrong game mechanics, while Odysseus generates factually correct perception and reasoning. A systematic ablation would quantify these contributions.

**Testing the CNN critic's capacity limits in visually complex environments.** The paper demonstrates that a NatureCNN with 512 hidden units suffices for value estimation in *Super Mario Land* — a low-resolution, 2D, side-scrolling game with distinct visual features. Would the same architecture work for: (a) a 3D platformer (e.g., *Super Mario 64*), (b) a first-person shooter with photorealistic graphics, (c) a robotic manipulation task with egocentric camera views and occlusions? A systematic study that varies visual complexity (resolution, dimensionality, clutter, texture diversity) while keeping the task structure (forward progress reward, similar horizon) constant would reveal how critic capacity requirements scale. The hypothesis — that a CNN critic is sufficient whenever the value-relevant visual features are spatially local and translation-invariant — could be tested by environments where value depends on non-local or relational features (e.g., counting items in an inventory, reading text on distant signs). For such environments, a critic with attention or memory mechanisms might become necessary, and characterizing this boundary would make the paper's architectural insight more actionable.

**Combining the lightweight critic with the VLM's CoT features for improved credit assignment.** The paper's design keeps the critic and actor entirely separate: the CNN critic sees only pixels, while the VLM actor generates structured CoT text. A natural extension would be to provide the critic with features derived from the VLM's internal representations — for example, the hidden states of the language model at the point where it generates the <answer> tag, or embeddings of the <perception> text. This would allow the critic to condition its value estimates on the VLM's *interpretation* of the visual scene, not just on raw pixels. The hypothesis is that this would improve credit assignment because the critic could distinguish between states that look similar but are semantically different (e.g., Mario standing on a platform that is safe versus one that is about to collapse — visually identical at the current frame but distinguished by the VLM's text description). The risk is that coupling the critic to the actor's representations reintroduces the computational overhead the paper sought to avoid. A study comparing (1) pixel-only CNN critic, (2) CNN critic with frozen VLM visual features, and (3) CNN critic with CoT text embeddings would characterize the performance-efficiency Pareto frontier.

**Stress-testing generalization through fine-tuning on held-out levels and measuring catastrophic forgetting of training levels.** The paper's generalization evaluation (Figures 6a–6c) tests zero-shot transfer from training levels to unseen states and games. But a more realistic deployment scenario is **continued training**: the agent is deployed on a new set of levels, collects interaction data, and is fine-tuned further. This raises the question of whether the RL recipe enables continual learning without catastrophic forgetting. An experiment that trains on levels 1–5 (as in Odysseus), then fine-tunes on levels 6–10 (held-out during initial training), and measures both (a) performance on levels 6–10 and (b) retained performance on levels 1–5, would test whether the VLM's pretrained representations provide a form of natural regularization against forgetting (because the visual and linguistic features are grounded in a broad pretraining distribution) or whether RL fine-tuning overwrites level-specific strategies. The paper's Table 3 shows minimal forgetting on general-domain benchmarks, but this does not address forgetting of specific game skills.

**Validating the approach on a non-gaming embodied task with sparse rewards.** The paper's environment has a dense forward-progress reward — every correct action produces immediate, measurable feedback in the x-coordinate. This reward density is likely a significant contributor to the success of the CNN critic, because the value function has a relatively smooth structure (nearby states have similar expected future progress). A critical stress-test would be to apply the same recipe — lightweight SFT + PPO with CNN critic + positive-advantage filtering — to a task with sparse rewards, such as a robotic manipulation task where the only reward is binary task completion, or a navigation task where reward is only received upon reaching the goal. In sparse-reward settings, the critic must predict value accurately for states that are far from any reward signal, which may require deeper networks, longer training, or auxiliary objectives (e.g., predicting future states or learning a latent dynamics model). The paper's findings may not transfer to this regime, and characterizing where they break would define the scope of the approach.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Training VLM agents for classic game libraries without human annotation.** The paper's SFT data generation pipeline — sampling ~5,000 frames from gameplay videos and annotating them with a strong teacher model (GPT-o3) — is intentionally lightweight and does not require human action labels. This makes it feasible to apply Odysseus to any game for which walkthrough videos exist on YouTube, which is essentially all commercially released games. A practical application would be to train a single VLM agent on a curated set of 2D platformers (e.g., the NES and Game Boy Mario libraries, Mega Man, Metroid) using the same pipeline, aiming for a generalist platformer agent. The cross-game generalization result in Figure 6c (23.1% improvement on *Super Mario Bros.* after training only on *Super Mario Land*) provides initial evidence that transfer is possible. The key practical benefit is eliminating the bottleneck of expert demonstration collection — the most expensive and least scalable component of prior SFT-based approaches like SIMA 2 (Bolton et al., 2025) — while still achieving better-than-zero-shot performance through RL optimization.

**Cost-efficient fine-tuning of open-source VLMs for domain-specific visual control.** For an organization deploying VLMs in a specific visual control domain — such as a manufacturing robot that must navigate a factory floor, or an assistive technology that interprets screen content for users with visual impairments — the paper provides a concrete, implementable recipe. The key practical insight is that a CNN critic with ~1–2 million parameters can be trained alongside an 8B VLM with negligible additional memory overhead (Table 7), meaning that existing open-source VLM fine-tuning infrastructure (based on frameworks like VeRL, which Odysseus itself uses) can be extended to actor-critic RL without requiring a second GPU for the critic. The SFT stage can use domain-specific images (photos of the factory floor, screenshots of the application interface) annotated by a capable VLM, and the RL stage can use environment interaction (real or simulated) to optimize the action policy. Table 3 shows that general-domain capabilities are largely preserved through this process, meaning the fine-tuned model remains useful for general-purpose tasks rather than becoming a brittle specialist.

**Accelerating RL research on VLM agents by providing a stable baseline.** Before this work, a researcher wanting to experiment with novel RL algorithms for VLM agents in long-horizon settings faced a bootstrapping problem: the existing algorithms (GRPO, Reinforce++) didn't work reliably, but implementing a full actor-critic pipeline with a token-level VLM critic was prohibitively expensive. The paper's demonstration that PPO with a lightweight CNN critic provides stable, reliable learning on an 8B model using standard open-source infrastructure lowers the barrier to entry substantially. A researcher can now take the Odysseus codebase, swap in their proposed algorithm (e.g., a novel advantage estimation method, a different policy gradient estimator), and compare against the PPO + CNN critic + positive-advantage filtering baseline on *Super Mario Land* — knowing that the baseline itself works and provides a meaningful signal. The paper's detailed hyperparameter tables (Tables 4, 7, 8, 9) and appendices provide enough information to replicate the setup without guesswork, which is a practical contribution in itself given how sensitive RL training can be to seemingly minor configuration details.
