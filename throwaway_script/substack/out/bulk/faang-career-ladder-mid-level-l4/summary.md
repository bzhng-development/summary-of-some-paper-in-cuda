Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article on the FAANG career ladder, comparing mid-level (L4) and senior (L5) engineers:

**Team-Level Influence as the Core Difference**

The L4 → L5 gap is larger than L3 → L4 because L5 promotion requires significant behavior changes—L5s are expected to lead and influence their teams. Ryan illustrates this shift across three concrete dimensions: improving the codebase (L4 initiates refactoring and code cleanups; L5 "identifies areas of improvement, influences the team to take goals on improving it together, then leads the charge"), production excellence (L4 participates in oncall and mitigating outages; L5 "creates an 'oncall improvement' workstream and builds a process for everyone to make the team's oncall better"), and project direction (L4 owns project management of a medium-to-large feature; L5 "drives team planning, and builds a roadmap of several medium-to-large features"). The key distinction is behavioral, not technical: "I wouldn't say the L5 examples are harder. They just require a mindset shift to own things at the team level." Soft skills start becoming more important at L5 precisely because you're expected to lead and influence others.

**Project Scope Criteria**

L5s handle larger-scope projects. Big tech companies measure scope along several criteria, but Ryan emphasizes these "aren't a checklist. Your work can be L5 scope by meeting only some of these criteria." The practical implication is that your manager will use these criteria to argue your work is L5-level during calibration, which is why "it's important to align with your manager on your work's scope. If you only work on L4 scope, you won't get any closer to promotion, no matter how good your work is."

**Mentorship and Team Culture**

L5 is the first level where engineers are expected to grow others—L4s just need to be good team players, while L5s are responsible for mentoring and building team culture. Example L5 culture contributions include knowledge sharing (writing wikis, organizing knowledge-sharing presentations, contributing to Q&A groups), recruiting activities (interviewing, outreach, attending college career fairs), and team activities (organizing happy hours, social activities, bug bashes). These contributions are "necessary, but not the top priority. Influence and impact are much stronger drivers of promotion to L5." For L4s aiming for L5, Ryan recommends focusing on influence and impact first—"if you do that, you'll get promoted quickly (~2-3 halves)."

---

Ryan's framing of the L4→L5 leap centers on a single **behavioral shift to team-level ownership**—a thread that runs through codebase influence, production-excellence processes, and roadmap planning alike. His emphasis that scope criteria "aren't a checklist" complements his **manager-alignment principle**: promotion requires doing L5-scoped work *and* having a manager who can argue that scope in the calibration room. The mentorship layer, while "necessary," is explicitly subordinate to the influence-and-impact engine—a useful prioritization signal for mid-level engineers navigating the path to senior.
