# DoLa: Decoding by Contrasting Layers Improves Factuality in Large Language Models

**ArXiv:** [2309.03883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03883)

## 🎯 Pitch

Subtracting the next-token probabilities of an LLM's early layers from its later layers makes the model 12–17 points more truthful on TruthfulQA—without any retrieval or fine-tuning. This simple decoding trick works because knowledge neurons concentrate in higher layers, while lower layers focus on syntax.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Decoding by Contrasting Layers (DoLa)**, a simple decoding strategy for reducing hallucinations in pretrained large language models without requiring external knowledge retrieval or additional fine-tuning. The method contrasts next-token logits from later ("mature") transformer layers against those from dynamically selected earlier ("premature") layers—for example, subtracting the lower-layer distribution from the higher-layer distribution to amplify factual knowledge that emerges in the model's topmost layers while downplaying syntactic patterns encoded earlier. Evaluated across LLaMA family models (7B to 65B) on TruthfulQA, FACTOR, StrategyQA, and GSM8K, DoLa improves TruthfulQA open-ended generation truthfulness by 12–17 absolute percentage points (e.g., LLaMA-7B rising from 30.4% to 42.1% on the %Truth∗Info metric), establishing that contrasting internal layer representations can surface factual knowledge only when the base model possesses distinct knowledge localization across layers—small models like GPT2-Medium show no such benefit.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Hallucinate, and We Don't Fully Understand Why

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is **hallucination in large language models**—the tendency of LLMs to generate content that deviates from real-world facts observed during pretraining. As the authors state in Section 1, this represents a "major bottleneck in their deployment especially for high-stakes applications (e.g., clinical/legal settings) where reliable generation of trustworthy text is crucial."

The problem is both practically urgent and theoretically puzzling. Practically, LLMs are being deployed in contexts where factual reliability matters—medical advice, legal reasoning, educational content—and hallucinations undermine trust. Theoretically, we don't fully understand *why* models that have seen enormous volumes of factual text during pretraining nonetheless produce incorrect statements. The paper identifies one possible root cause (Section 1): the maximum likelihood language modeling objective minimizes the forward KL divergence between data and model distributions, which "potentially results in a model with mass-seeking behavior which causes the LM to assign non-zero probability to sentences that are not fully consistent with knowledge embedded in the training data." In other words, the model learns to spread probability mass across plausible-sounding sequences—including factually incorrect ones—rather than concentrating it exclusively on factual outputs.

Empirically, the paper notes that an LM trained with next-word prediction on finite data "has been shown to result in a model that uses linguistic knowledge to recognize the superficial patterns, instead of recognizing and generating the real-world facts extracted from the training corpus." This is a crucial distinction: the model may learn the *surface form* of factual statements (syntax, discourse patterns, named entity co-occurrence) without learning the underlying factual associations with sufficient precision to override statistical shortcuts.

### The Gap: Leveraging Internal Model Structure Without External Intervention

By the time this paper was written, there were roughly three families of approaches for addressing hallucinations, each with significant practical limitations:

**1. Fine-tuning with human feedback (RLHF).** Techniques like reinforcement learning from human feedback (Ouyang et al., 2022) train models to produce outputs that humans prefer, which can reduce certain types of hallucination. However, RLHF requires substantial human annotation effort, is expensive to run, and can introduce its own failure modes (e.g., models learning to produce answers that *sound* authoritative to human raters without being more factually accurate). Moreover, RLHF is a training-time intervention—it requires modifying the model weights, which is impractical when only API access is available or when the base model is too large to fine-tune.

**2. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).** Approaches that ground LLM outputs in retrieved external knowledge (Izacard et al., 2022; Borgeaud et al., 2022; Ram et al., 2023) can substantially improve factuality by providing the model with relevant documents at inference time. However, they introduce significant infrastructure complexity (maintaining a retrieval index, running a retrieval pipeline alongside generation) and face the fundamental challenge that retrieval quality is often the bottleneck—if the retriever fails to find relevant documents, the grounding benefit evaporates. The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation in Section 6: "our method relies on the model's internal knowledge without using external retrieval modules... thus, it cannot correct misinformation acquired during training."

**3. Inference-time intervention with trained classifiers (ITI).** Li et al. (2023) proposed Inference-Time Intervention, which trains a linear classifier on top of a model's internal activations to predict whether a statement is truthful, then uses this classifier during decoding to shift attention heads toward truthfulness-promoting directions. While effective (the paper reports ITI improves LLaMA-7B on TruthfulQA from 26.9% to 43.5% truthfulness), ITI requires supervised training data with truthfulness labels—TruthfulQA itself in their experiments. This creates a circular dependency where truthfulness improvements are tied to a specific labeled dataset and may not generalize to fact patterns not covered by the training labels. The paper notes this limitation: "We rely on existing models and pre-trained parameters, not using human labels or factual knowledge bases for fine-tuning, limiting possible improvements."

**4. Contrastive decoding between models.** Li et al. (2022) proposed Contrastive Decoding (CD), which contrasts the output distributions of a strong "expert" LM against a weaker "amateur" LM. CD was designed to improve fluency and coherence, not factuality. The paper's experiments (Table 1) show that applying CD to factuality tasks yields mixed results: on TruthfulQA open-ended generation, CD often causes the model to refuse to answer entirely (e.g., LLaMA-33B with CD generates "I have no comment" over 60% of the time), inflating truthfulness scores at the expense of informativeness. The paper hypothesizes this is because CD contrasts LLaMA-7B against larger models, and the larger models are better at instruction-following—they learn from the TruthfulQA prompt that "I have no comment" is an acceptable response and overuse it.

### The Specific Opportunity: Factual Knowledge Is Localized Across Layers

The paper's central insight—the gap it exploits—comes from the model interpretability literature, which had already established that transformer LMs encode different types of information at different depths. The paper cites three key findings (Section 1 and Section 5):

- **Classical NLP pipeline in BERT:** Tenney et al. (2019) showed that BERT mimics a classical NLP pipeline, with earlier layers handling syntactic information (part-of-speech tags, constituency structure) and later layers handling semantic information (coreference, semantic roles). This suggests a natural hierarchy where lower layers capture linguistic form and higher layers capture meaning.

- **Knowledge neurons in upper layers:** Dai et al. (2022) identified "knowledge neurons"—individual neurons whose activations strongly correlate with factual knowledge—as being distributed predominantly in the topmost layers of pretrained BERT. This is direct evidence that factual associations are not spread evenly throughout the model but are concentrated in specific late-stage computations.

- **Factual knowledge can be edited by manipulating specific layers:** Meng et al. (2022) demonstrated that factual associations in autoregressive LMs can be edited by manipulating a specific set of feedforward layers. This further confirms the modular encoding of factual knowledge and suggests that intervening on specific layers can alter the model's factual behavior.

However, none of this prior work connected these localization findings to *decoding strategy*. The gap was this: **we know factual knowledge lives in later layers, but no one had proposed using this fact at inference time to improve factuality without retraining or external data.** This is the precise gap DoLa fills. The paper describes the intuition through a concrete example (Figure 1): when answering "Where is the capital of Washington State?", the probability of the plausible-but-wrong answer "Seattle" remains high across all layers (it's syntactically correct and statistically frequent), but the probability of the correct answer "Olympia" gradually increases from lower to higher layers as more factual knowledge is incorporated. Contrasting the difference between an early layer and a late layer can therefore surface the factual answer by amplifying the *differential* contribution of the higher layers.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper situates DoLa at the intersection of two research threads: **model interpretability (knowledge localization)** and **contrastive decoding**. It draws directly on the contrastive decoding mechanism from Li et al. (2022)—subtracting log-probabilities between two distributions—but fundamentally redefines what is being contrasted. Instead of contrasting different *models* (expert vs. amateur), DoLa contrasts different *layers within the same model* (mature vs. premature). This shift in perspective has several important consequences that the paper emphasizes:

**No external model needed.** CD requires selecting an appropriately sized amateur model (e.g., a smaller LLaMA variant), which introduces hyperparameter sensitivity—the amateur model's size must be carefully chosen, as the paper's own CD experiments show (Table 5 in Appendix B, where CD performance varies dramatically with amateur model choice). DoLa works entirely within a single model, removing this dependency.

**No training required.** Unlike ITI, which requires training a linear classifier on labeled truthfulness data, DoLa is purely inference-time: it requires no additional training data, no fine-tuning, and no supervised labels. This makes it applicable to any pretrained transformer LM out of the box.

**Dynamic adaptation per token.** The paper introduces a key innovation beyond standard contrastive decoding: the premature layer is not fixed but *dynamically selected at each decoding step* based on which earlier layer's output distribution is most different from the final layer. The motivation, illustrated in Figure 3, is that different tokens require factual knowledge to different degrees—function words like "was" and "the" have their output distributions stabilize in early layers, while named entities and dates continue evolving through later layers. A fixed premature layer would either under-contrast (picking too late a layer for easy tokens) or over-contrast (picking too early a layer for hard tokens). Dynamic selection adapts to token difficulty.

**Complementary to other approaches.** The paper explicitly positions DoLa as a foundational method that could be combined with RLHF, retrieval augmentation, or ITI (Section 6): "the limitations listed above could be potentially addressed through future work combining the corresponding elements with our decoding strategy." This is not a claim to replace those methods but to provide an orthogonal, cost-free improvement that stacks with them.

### Reconciling Conflicting Evidence in the Literature

An important piece of intellectual context that the paper addresses only indirectly (but which helps explain its contribution) is the tension between two observations in the literature. On one hand, prior work on contrastive decoding (Li et al., 2022; O'Brien & Lewis, 2023) showed that contrasting *models* improves fluency and reasoning but did not study factuality. On the other hand, concurrent work by Gera et al. (2023) on Autocontrastive Decoding (ACD)—which contrasts layers within small LMs like GPT-2—found that this approach *increased* hallucinations. The paper addresses this tension directly in its limitations and analysis (Section 5, Appendix N): when DoLa is applied to GPT2-Medium (335M parameters), it fails to improve factuality (Table 17). The resolution is that "large LLMs storing distinct knowledge across layers is key for DoLa to work"—small models lack the capacity to develop the hierarchical knowledge representation that DoLa exploits. This reconciliation is important because it establishes a boundary condition: the method is not universally applicable but specifically targets models large enough to exhibit knowledge localization, which is precisely the regime where hallucination is both most problematic and hardest to address through fine-tuning (due to model size).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents a **decoding strategy**—a method for choosing which word to generate next during text generation—that makes large language models produce more factually correct outputs without any retraining, fine-tuning, or access to external knowledge bases. The system solves the problem of LLM hallucinations by exploiting a property of transformer architectures: factual knowledge is encoded predominantly in the model's later layers, while syntactic and surface-level patterns are encoded earlier. The solution's shape is a **contrastive mechanism at each generation step**: the model computes two probability distributions over the next token—one from an early "premature" layer and one from the final "mature" layer—and then amplifies the difference between them, effectively isolating the factual knowledge that emerges in the final layers while canceling out surface-level patterns that are common to both.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The DoLa system has four major components arranged as a pipeline that executes at every single token generation step:

1. **Base Language Model (the backbone):** Any pretrained transformer-based autoregressive LM (tested on LLaMA 7B–65B and MPT-7B) with `$N$` stacked transformer layers. This model is used exactly as-is—no weights are modified. During decoding, it computes hidden states at every layer for the current context.

2. **Early-Exit Distribution Computer:** For a set of candidate earlier layers `$\mathcal{J} \subset \{0, \ldots, N-1\}$` (only even-numbered layers for efficiency, partitioned into 2–4 buckets depending on total layer count), this component applies the model's vocabulary projection head directly to the hidden state at that layer, producing an alternative next-token probability distribution `$q_j(x_t \mid x_{<t})$`. This is standard early-exit computation—no special training is needed because residual connections make intermediate hidden states already interpretable as token probabilities.

3. **Dynamic Premature Layer Selector:** At each decoding step, this component computes the Jensen-Shannon Divergence between the final-layer distribution `$q_N(\cdot)$` and each candidate early-exit distribution `$q_j(\cdot)$`. It selects the layer `$M$` with the maximum divergence as the "premature layer"—the layer whose outputs differ most substantially from the final layer, indicating that significant processing (ideally factual knowledge injection) occurred after this layer.

4. **Contrastive Decoder with Adaptive Plausibility Constraint:** This component takes the log-probability difference between the mature layer `$N$` and the dynamically selected premature layer `$M$`, applies an adaptive plausibility constraint that zeroes out tokens with too-low probability from the mature layer (preventing contrastive amplification of noise), and optionally applies a repetition penalty. The resulting distribution `$\hat{p}(x_t \mid x_{<t})$` is used to select the next token via greedy decoding or sampling.

Information flows sequentially: the prompt enters → the base model computes all layer hidden states → the premature layer selector identifies layer `$M$` using JSD → the contrastive decoder computes `$\hat{p}$` by subtracting `$\log q_M$` from `$\log q_N$` → the next token is sampled and appended to the context → the process repeats for the next token.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the formal layer-wise probability computation**, which establishes how individual layers produce next-token distributions and why early-exit predictions are meaningful—this is the foundation that makes DoLa possible.
- **Second, the dynamic premature layer selection mechanism**, which is the core algorithmic innovation: how JSD is computed, why it is the right distance metric, and how candidate layers are organized into buckets to balance adaptivity with robustness.
- **Third, the contrastive operation itself**, including the log-domain subtraction formula, the adaptive plausibility constraint (APC) borrowed from Contrastive Decoding, and the repetition penalty—explaining what each component does, why it's necessary, and what would break without it.
- **Fourth, the motivation for DoLa from the model interpretability evidence**, specifically the two patterns of JSD behavior (factual tokens vs. function words) that justify dynamic selection and the quantitative NER experiment in Appendix A.
- **Fifth, the hyperparameter design space and validation strategy**, covering bucket partitioning rules, validation protocol, and how the hyperparameter search is dramatically reduced compared to a static layer selection baseline (DoLa-static).

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is **a decoding strategy paper** whose core idea is that contrasting the output distributions of a later ("mature") transformer layer against a dynamically selected earlier ("premature") layer amplifies factual knowledge that emerges in upper layers while canceling surface-level linguistic patterns common to both, thereby reducing hallucinations without training or retrieval.

---

#### Formalizing Layer-Wise Next-Token Distributions

The starting point is a standard autoregressive transformer language model with an embedding layer, `$N$` stacked transformer layers, and an affine vocabulary projection head `$\phi(\cdot)$` that maps the final hidden state to logits over the vocabulary `$\mathcal{X}$`. Given a prefix of previously generated tokens `$x_{<t} = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_{t-1}\}$`, the embedding layer produces initial hidden vectors `$\mathbf{H}_0 = \{\mathbf{h}_0^{(1)}, \ldots, \mathbf{h}_0^{(t-1)}\}$`. These are processed successively through each transformer layer `$j$`, producing hidden states `$\mathbf{H}_j$` at each depth. For standard decoding, only the final layer's representation `$\mathbf{h}_N^{(t)}$` (the last-position hidden state at layer `$N$`) is projected through `$\phi$` to produce the next-token distribution:

$$p(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \text{softmax}\left(\phi\left(\mathbf{h}_N^{(t)}\right)\right)_{x_t}, \quad x_t \in \mathcal{X}$$

where `$\phi(\cdot)$` is the learned affine transformation (the "language model head" or "vocabulary head"), `$\mathbf{h}_N^{(t)}$` is the hidden state at the final layer `$N$` for the last token position, and `$\text{softmax}(\cdot)_{x_t}$` extracts the probability assigned to token `$x_t$`.

**What it computes:** This is the standard autoregressive generation equation: it takes the model's deepest representation of the current context, projects it to vocabulary-sized logits, normalizes to a probability distribution, and reads off the probability for a specific token `$x_t$`. The computation happens once per generation step and determines which token is sampled next.

**Why this form:** The final layer representation `$\mathbf{h}_N$` aggregates information from all layers via residual connections, so it contains both syntactic and semantic features. Standard decoding trusts this single representation to capture everything needed. The key insight of DoLa is that this trust is misplaced for factual knowledge—the final layer may still contain syntactic patterns that dominate the probability mass, obscuring factual information that exists in the representation but at lower probability.

The DoLa innovation is to also compute *early-exit* distributions from intermediate layers. For any layer `$j \in \mathcal{J}$` where `$\mathcal{J}$` is a set of candidate premature layers (subset of `$\{0, \ldots, N-1\}$`), the model's vocabulary head is applied directly to the hidden state at that layer:

$$q_j(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \text{softmax}\left(\phi\left(\mathbf{h}_j^{(t)}\right)\right)_{x_t}, \quad j \in \mathcal{J}$$

where `$q_j(\cdot)$` is the early-exit next-token distribution from layer `$j$`, `$\mathbf{h}_j^{(t)}$` is the hidden state at that intermediate layer for the final token position, and `$\phi(\cdot)$` is the same vocabulary head used for the final layer—no separate projection head is trained.

**What it computes:** This takes a *partially processed* representation of the context (only the first `$j$` transformer layers have acted on it) and asks: what would the model predict if it stopped here? Because residual connections make hidden states evolve gradually, `$\phi(\mathbf{h}_j^{(t)})$` produces a legitimate probability distribution even though the model was never trained for early exiting. The distribution `$q_j$` typically captures lower-level patterns (syntax, local collocations) that are already determined by layer `$j$`, before deeper semantic and factual processing occurs.

**Why this form:** The paper's foundational claim is that `$q_N$` (final layer) and `$q_j$` (early layer) encode qualitatively different information—`$q_N$` includes factual knowledge injected in later layers, while `$q_j$` captures syntactic plausibility. By contrasting them, the factual component can be isolated. This works because the exact same vocabulary head `$\phi$` is applied to both representations; any difference in output distributions must come from the different hidden states, which in turn reflect the different processing depths. If separate heads were trained per layer, differences could be confounded by head-specific biases.

A subtle but important point: layer 0 is defined as the word embedding layer (before any transformer blocks), so `$q_0$` uses raw token embeddings before any self-attention or feedforward processing. This is included as a candidate layer in some experiments (Appendix C) and serves as a baseline representing purely lexical-level predictions with zero contextual processing.

#### Dynamic Premature Layer Selection

The core algorithmic contribution of DoLa is not just contrasting layers, but **dynamically selecting which layer to use as the premature layer at each decoding step**. The motivation is illustrated in Figure 2 and Figure 3: different tokens require different amounts of processing depth. Function words like "was," "the," "in" stabilize early—the model knows what to generate by the middle layers, and later layers barely change the distribution. Named entities, dates, and other fact-intensive tokens continue to evolve through the final layers as factual knowledge is injected. A fixed premature layer would be suboptimal for some fraction of tokens: too early for easy tokens (wasting the contrastive signal) or too late for hard tokens (subtracting away relevant factual information).

The selection mechanism works as follows. At each decoding step `$t$`, for every candidate premature layer `$j \in \mathcal{J}$`, the system computes the distributional distance between the final-layer distribution `$q_N(\cdot \mid x_{<t})$` and the early-exit distribution `$q_j(\cdot \mid x_{<t})$`:

$$d\left(q_N(\cdot \mid x_{<t}), q_j(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\right) = \text{JSD}\left(q_N(\cdot \mid x_{<t}) \,\|\, q_j(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\right)$$

where `$\text{JSD}(P \| Q)$` is the Jensen-Shannon Divergence between distributions `$P$` and `$Q$`. The premature layer `$M$` is then selected as the one with maximum divergence:

$$M = \arg\max_{j \in \mathcal{J}} \text{JSD}\left(q_N(\cdot \mid x_{<t}) \,\|\, q_j(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\right)$$

where `$\mathcal{J}$` is the set of candidate early layers (constrained to even-numbered layers for efficiency and bucketed as described below), and `$M$` is the chosen premature layer, satisfying `$0 \leq M < N$`.

**What JSD is and why it's used:** The Jensen-Shannon Divergence is a symmetric, bounded measure of distance between two probability distributions. It is defined as:

$$\text{JSD}(P \| Q) = \frac{1}{2}\text{KL}(P \| R) + \frac{1}{2}\text{KL}(Q \| R)$$

where `$R = \frac{1}{2}(P + Q)$` is the midpoint distribution, and `$\text{KL}(P \| Q) = \sum_x P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$` is Kullback-Leibler divergence.

**What JSD computes in this context:** At each token position, the final layer produces a distribution `$q_N$` over the vocabulary, and each early layer `$j$` produces its own distribution `$q_j$`. JSD measures how "far apart" these two distributions are, in a symmetric way. A high JSD means the model's prediction changed substantially between layer `$j$` and the final layer—implying significant processing (and potentially factual knowledge injection) occurred. A low JSD means the prediction stabilized early and later layers barely modified it.

**Why JSD over alternatives:** JSD has two properties that make it superior to raw KL divergence for this task. First, it is **symmetric**: `$\text{JSD}(P \| Q) = \text{JSD}(Q \| P)$`, whereas `$\text{KL}(P \| Q) \neq \text{KL}(Q \| P)$` in general. This symmetry matters because there is no natural directionality—we don't know a priori whether the final layer is "more correct" than the early layer, only that they differ. Second, JSD is **bounded** between 0 and `$\log 2$` (approximately 0.693), which makes the divergence magnitudes comparable across different tokens and layers. KL divergence is unbounded above, which could cause a few outlier tokens to dominate the layer selection. The paper also uses the fact that JSD scales naturally for visualization (multiplied by `$10^5$` in Figure 2 for readability), but the ranking (argmax) is independent of scaling.

**What `$\arg\max$` achieves:** By selecting the layer with maximum JSD, the system identifies the point in the layer stack where the model's prediction underwent its **most dramatic change**. The intuition is that this change represents the injection of new information—ideally factual knowledge—that was not present in earlier layers. Contrasting against this layer rather than a fixed early layer means the system adaptively finds the best "before" snapshot for each token, maximizing the contrastive signal where factual knowledge exists and minimizing it where the prediction is already stable.

**Candidate layer set `$\mathcal{J}$` and bucketing.** Rather than selecting from all `$N$` layers (which would be computationally expensive and potentially noisy), DoLa constrains `$\mathcal{J}$` to a **pre-defined bucket of even-numbered layers**. The bucketing strategy depends on the total number of layers in the model:

- **LLaMA-7B** (32 layers): 2 buckets — `$[0, 16)$` and `$[16, 32)$`
- **LLaMA-13B** (40 layers): 2 buckets — `$[0, 20)$` and `$[20, 40)$`
- **LLaMA-33B** (60 layers): 3 buckets — `$[0, 20)$`, `$[20, 40)$`, `$[40, 60)$`
- **LLaMA-65B** (80 layers): 4 buckets — `$[0, 20)$`, `$[20, 40)$`, `$[40, 60)$`, `$[60, 80)$`

Within each bucket, only **even-indexed layers** (0th, 2nd, 4th, etc.) are considered as candidates, halving the computation. The 0th layer is the word embedding layer (before any transformer block). The best bucket for a given task and model is selected using a validation set (two-fold validation for TruthfulQA and FACTOR; a randomly sampled 10% subset of GSM8K training data for StrategyQA and GSM8K).

**Why bucketing:** If `$\mathcal{J}$` were all layers `$[0, N)$`, the JSD-maximizing layer would be a moving target potentially sensitive to noise in individual layer outputs. By restricting to a contiguous range (e.g., only low layers `$[0, 16)$` or only high layers `$[16, 32)$`), the method forces the premature layer to come from a specific depth regime. This matters because the optimal depth regime differs across tasks. The paper finds empirically (Table 10 in Appendix F) that:

- **TruthfulQA** (short-answer factuality) selects **higher buckets**: `$[16, 32)$` for 7B, `$[20, 40)$` for 13B, `$[40, 60)$` for 33B, `$[60, 80)$` for 65B. The interpretation (Section 3.2) is that for fact-critical short answers, contrasting against higher layers works better because the key factual knowledge appears in the topmost layers.
- **FACTOR, GSM8K, StrategyQA, and Vicuna QA** (long-paragraph factuality, chain-of-thought reasoning, open-ended chatbot) select **lower buckets**: `$[0, 16)$` for 7B and `$[0, 20)$` for 13B/33B/65B. The interpretation is that these tasks involve many non-fact tokens (function words, discourse markers, arithmetic steps) whose predictions are already stable in early layers. Contrasting against higher layers would over-amplify small fluctuations on these tokens.

**Hyperparameter selection cost:** The bucketing strategy reduces the hyperparameter search from examining all individual layers (32–80 possibilities with DoLa-static) to testing only 2–4 buckets—a roughly **10× reduction** in validation runs. This is a practical engineering advantage: for a new model or task, an experimenter needs only a handful of validation runs to select the appropriate bucket, rather than exhaustively sweeping all possible premature layer indices.

**DoLa-static as an ablation baseline.** The paper defines DoLa-static as a variant where a single fixed layer is chosen for contrasting throughout the entire decoding process, selected based on best validation performance. Figure 5 (GSM8K with LLaMA-7B) and Figure 6 (FACTOR with all models) compare DoLa against DoLa-static across all possible premature layer choices. The key findings:

- DoLa-static can sometimes *outperform* DoLa when the optimal layer is found (e.g., layer 10 in GSM8K subset #1, Figure 5 left).
- However, the optimal layer is **sensitive to data distribution**: across two different 10% subsets of GSM8K (Figure 5 left vs. right), the optimal layer shifts from 10 to 2, and using subset #1's optimal layer on subset #2 causes performance degradation.
- DoLa maintains consistently high performance across both subsets, nearly matching the best DoLa-static layer without requiring per-dataset optimization.

This establishes DoLa's robustness claim: by dynamically selecting the premature layer per token, it adapts to the data distribution implicitly, whereas DoLa-static would require an in-distribution validation set that may not be available in deployment.

**Random layer selection ablation (Appendix J, Table 15).** To verify that the JSD-based selection is genuinely better than arbitrary layer choice, the paper tests a "random" baseline where the premature layer is randomly selected at each step (rather than via JSD argmax). On FACTOR, random selection performs *worse* than the original baseline (e.g., LLaMA-13B drops from 61.1/62.6 baseline to 53.8/54.8 on News/Wiki). This confirms that the JSD signal captures meaningful information about where factual processing occurs—random selection introduces noise that harms more than it helps, making it "essential to apply our JSD-based layer selection strategy."

#### The Contrastive Operation: Amplifying Mature Layer Knowledge

Once the premature layer `$M$` is dynamically selected, DoLa computes the next-token probability distribution by contrasting the mature (final) layer against the premature layer in log space:

$$\hat{p}(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \text{softmax}\left(\mathcal{F}\left(q_N(x_t), q_M(x_t)\right)\right)_{x_t}$$

where the contrastive operator `$\mathcal{F}(\cdot, \cdot)$` is defined as:

$$\mathcal{F}\left(q_N(x_t), q_M(x_t)\right) =
\begin{cases}
\log \frac{q_N(x_t)}{q_M(x_t)}, & \text{if } x_t \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{head}}(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \\
-\infty, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}$$

**What it computes:** For each token `$x_t$` in the vocabulary, the operation takes the log-ratio of its probability under the mature layer versus its probability under the premature layer. If the mature layer assigns higher probability than the premature layer (the token became *more* likely after deeper processing), the log-ratio is positive, amplifying that token. If the premature layer assigned higher probability (the token became *less* likely after deeper processing), the log-ratio is negative, suppressing that token. The result is a new set of logits where the contribution of the premature layer is "subtracted out" of the mature layer's prediction. A softmax then normalizes these corrected logits into a proper probability distribution.

**Why log-ratio subtraction:** This follows the Contrastive Decoding formulation from Li et al. (2022). The intuition comes from the fact that a language model's log-probability `$\log q(x_t)$` can be decomposed into contributions from different factors (syntax + semantics + world knowledge). If we assume the premature layer captures mostly syntax and surface patterns (contributing `$\log q_{\text{syntax}}(x_t)$`) while the mature layer captures both syntax and factual knowledge (contributing `$\log q_{\text{syntax}}(x_t) + \log q_{\text{factual}}(x_t)$`), then subtracting them isolates the factual component:

$$\log \frac{q_N(x_t)}{q_M(x_t)} \approx \log q_{\text{factual}}(x_t)$$

This is an approximation—in practice, the layers are not cleanly factorizable—but the empirical results suggest it is good enough to produce substantial factuality gains.

**Why not use raw probability ratio:** Subtracting probabilities directly `$q_N(x_t) - q_M(x_t)$` would be problematic because probabilities are on different scales at different layers—a token might go from 0.001 to 0.01 (a 10× increase) or from 0.1 to 0.2 (a 2× increase); the former is a much stronger signal of knowledge injection but would look smaller in absolute difference. Working in log-space captures multiplicative changes, which are more appropriate for probability ratios. This is standard in contrastive decoding (Li et al., 2022).

**The Adaptive Plausibility Constraint (APC).** The conditional in `$\mathcal{F}$` defines a filtering step: tokens whose mature-layer probability is too low are set to `$-\infty$` (zero probability after softmax), effectively removing them from consideration. The filtered set `$\mathcal{V}_{\text{head}}(x_t \mid x_{<t})$` is defined as:

$$\mathcal{V}_{\text{head}}(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \left\{ x_t \in \mathcal{X} : q_N(x_t) \geq \alpha \max_w q_N(w) \right\}$$

where `$\alpha \in [0, 1]$` is a threshold parameter (set to 0.1 in all experiments) and `$\max_w q_N(w)$` is the probability of the mature layer's most-likely token.

**What it computes:** This identifies the set of tokens that have non-negligible probability under the mature layer. Specifically, a token is retained only if its probability is at least `$\alpha$` times the probability of the most-likely token. For example, if the top token has probability 0.5 and `$\alpha = 0.1$`, any token with probability below 0.05 is excluded.

**Why this constraint is necessary (two failure modes):**

1. **False positives (implausible tokens getting high contrastive scores):** The contrastive operation involves division by `$q_M(x_t)$`. If `$q_M(x_t)$` is extremely small for some token (e.g., `$10^{-8}$`) while `$q_N(x_t)$` is merely small (e.g., `$10^{-4}$`), the ratio `$q_N / q_M$` becomes enormous (`$10^4$`), giving that implausible token a very high contrastive score. The paper calls this "an implausible token with an extremely low score may be rewarded with a high score after contrast, due to the unstable low probability range on these implausible tokens from different layers." APC prevents this by only considering tokens that the mature layer actually thinks are plausible.

2. **False negatives (confident predictions getting suppressed):** If the model is very confident about a prediction (e.g., the word "the" has probability 0.99 in both the premature and mature layers), the contrastive ratio `$q_N / q_M \approx 1.0$` and the contrastive score `$\log(1.0) = 0$`—low compared to tokens with large ratios. The paper notes: "when the model is very confident about an easy decision, the output probability of a high-score token does not change much in different layers and results in low scores after contrast, so we need to force the model still select from these high-score tokens in this case." APC ensures these confident predictions remain in the candidate set, even though their contrastive score is low.

**Why `$\alpha = 0.1$`:** The value follows prior work by Li et al. (2022) and balances inclusiveness (allowing genuinely emerging tokens through) against strictness (filtering noise). Too high an `$\alpha$` would restrict to only the top-1 or top-2 tokens, removing the diversity needed for factual disambiguation. Too low would admit noise tokens that get spuriously high contrastive scores. The paper inherits this value without extensive tuning, suggesting it is relatively robust.

**Post-softmax (or not) for multiple-choice evaluation.** For multiple-choice tasks like TruthfulQA MC, the paper finds that **omitting the final softmax** and using the raw contrastive scores directly as likelihood scores improves performance (Table 6 in Appendix C). The scores are:

- DoLa with post-softmax on TruthfulQA MC (LLaMA-7B): MC1 31.9, MC2 52.2, MC3 28.2
- DoLa without post-softmax: MC1 32.2, MC2 63.8, MC3 32.1

The authors state this was found empirically for TruthfulQA only and that "both implementations (with and without softmax) are much better than baseline scores." For open-ended generation, the softmax is applied as normal to produce a proper sampling distribution. The discrepancy likely arises because multiple-choice scoring requires comparing scores across answer choices, where softmax can distort the relative magnitudes that the contrastive operation produces.

**Handling `$-\infty$` for likelihood-based evaluation.** For TruthfulQA MC and FACTOR, where the model must assign a likelihood score to answer choices (not generate text), setting excluded tokens to `$-\infty$` would make the entire sequence score zero if any token falls outside `$\mathcal{V}_{\text{head}}$`. Instead, the paper replaces `$-\infty$` with `$-1000$`—a large negative log-probability that effectively zeros out those tokens without making the full sequence score degenerate. This applies only to multiple-choice evaluation; during generation, `$-\infty$` tokens are simply never sampled.

#### Repetition Penalty

Empirically, the paper found that DoLa can have "a higher tendency to repeat previously generated sentences, especially during generation of long sequences of chain-of-thought reasoning" (Section 2.3). This is a known issue with contrastive decoding approaches: by sharpening the distribution (amplifying peaks, suppressing tails), the model becomes more deterministic and can get stuck in loops.

The mitigation is a standard **repetition penalty** introduced by Keskar et al. (2019). At each generation step, the logits for tokens that have appeared in the previous context are divided by a temperature-like parameter `$\theta$`:

$$\text{logits}[x_t] = \text{logits}[x_t] \, / \, \theta \quad \text{if } x_t \in \text{previous\_context}$$

where `$\theta = 1.2$` in all experiments. By dividing the logits of repeated tokens by a value greater than 1, their effective probability is reduced, discouraging the model from generating the same token again.

**Why `$\theta = 1.2$`:** This is the value from the original Keskar et al. paper and is used without tuning. Values much larger would suppress repetition too aggressively, potentially harming fluency; values closer to 1.0 would have negligible effect.

**Interaction with DoLa:** The repetition penalty is applied *after* the contrastive operation and APC filter—it modifies the already-contrasted distribution. This ordering ensures that DoLa's factuality improvements are not confounded by the repetition penalty's effect on fluency. The paper's ablation in Appendix K (Figures 7–8) shows that the repetition penalty improves DoLa's performance on StrategyQA and GSM8K (long-form reasoning tasks), while slightly *hurting* the baseline's performance. This asymmetry suggests that DoLa's distribution sharpening makes it more susceptible to repetition, and the penalty compensates for this specific side effect rather than universally improving generation quality.

#### The Motivation for DoLa: Why Factual Knowledge Emerges in Later Layers

The paper grounds its approach in empirical observations about how next-token predictions evolve through transformer layers. This section unpacks the evidence that justifies the architectural choice of contrasting early vs. late layers rather than, say, contrasting adjacent layers or using layer ensembles.

**Qualitative evidence: JSD heatmaps (Figure 2).** The paper presents a detailed heatmap tracking the JSD between each even-numbered layer and the final (32nd) layer of LLaMA-7B, computed token-by-token during the generation of a factual answer. The input is: "Who was the first Nigerian to win the Nobel Prize, in which year?" The generated output is: "Wole Soyinka was the first Nigerian to win the Nobel Prize, in 1986."

The heatmap reveals two distinct patterns:

- **Pattern #1: Factual tokens show sustained high JSD through the top layers.** When predicting the named entity "Wole" (second column), the JSD between the 30th layer and the final layer is still at 2.97 (scaled by `$10^5$`), indicating that the model's prediction is still changing substantially even between the second-to-last and last layers. Similarly, when predicting "Soyinka" (third column), layer 30 shows JSD of 4.78 compared to the final layer. The date "1986" also shows elevated JSD in the highest layers. The interpretation: the model is still incorporating factual knowledge in the very last layers for these tokens—the factual association is not "settled" until near the output.

- **Pattern #2: Function words and copied tokens show low JSD from middle layers.** When predicting "was" (fourth column), the JSD drops to 0.03 by layer 14 and stays near zero through all higher layers. For "the" (eighth column), JSD is essentially zero from layer 12 onward. For tokens copied from the input question ("first," "Nigerian," "Nobel," "Prize"), JSD becomes negligible from the middle layers. The interpretation: the model "decides" these tokens early and does not revise its prediction in later layers—they are syntactic or surface-level choices that don't benefit from deep semantic processing.

**What this implies for contrastive decoding:** If factual tokens have their predictions change dramatically in the topmost layers while function tokens stabilize early, then contrasting a high-mid layer (e.g., layer 24) against the final layer (32) will produce a strong signal for factual tokens (large difference) and a weak signal for function tokens (small difference). This is exactly the desired behavior: amplify the factual components, leave the syntactic structure intact. Contrasting too low a layer (e.g., layer 8) would produce large differences for *all* tokens, potentially destabilizing the syntactic skeleton of the output.

**Quantitative evidence: NER experiment (Appendix A, Table 4).** To support the qualitative claims more rigorously, the paper conducts a controlled experiment using the CoNLL-2003 named entity recognition validation set (3.25K examples). For each token, the paper identifies the "critical layer"—the layer with the maximum JSD from the final layer when predicting with teacher forcing (feeding ground-truth context, not generated context). The results are split by whether the ground-truth token is an entity (e.g., "London," "John Smith") or a non-entity (e.g., "the," "was," "reportedly"):

| Layer | Entity Tokens | Non-Entity Tokens |
|-------|---------------|-------------------|
| 0     | 35.56%        | 75.55%            |
| 22    | 9.69%         | 1.76%             |
| 24    | 10.38%        | 2.62%             |
| 28    | 10.06%        | 2.11%             |
| 30    | 25.40%        | 12.98%            |

**What the table shows:** For non-entity tokens, the critical layer is overwhelmingly layer 0 (the word embedding layer) in 75.55% of cases—meaning the prediction barely changes after the embedding layer because it's already determined by lexical-level statistics. For entity tokens, only 35.56% of critical layers are at layer 0, while over 50% of critical layers are in the high layers (22–30). This quantitatively confirms the qualitative observation: factual predictions (entities) are more likely to undergo significant revision in later layers than non-factual predictions.

**Why this supports dynamic selection:** The distribution of critical layers is not uniform—it varies by token type. This validates the decision to dynamically select the premature layer per token rather than using a fixed layer. A fixed layer 0 would capture the contrast for 75% of non-entity tokens but would miss the contrast for the majority of entity tokens whose critical changes occur in layers 22–30. Dynamic selection via JSD adaptively finds whichever layer provides the strongest contrastive signal for the current token.

**A caveat on the NER experiment:** The paper acknowledges that this experiment uses teacher forcing with ground-truth tokens rather than model-generated tokens, which introduces a "mismatch" when the model would have predicted a different token than the ground truth. The authors note that manual labeling of model-generated outputs would be more accurate but is substantially more expensive. The asymmetry in the results (entity tokens spread across high layers, non-entity tokens concentrated at layer 0) is strong enough to support the qualitative claim despite this noise.

#### Hyperparameters and Validation Protocol

The paper makes several concrete design choices that affect practical deployment:

**Adaptive plausibility constraint threshold `$\alpha = 0.1`$.** Set following Li et al. (2022) without tuning. This controls how many tokens survive the APC filter. With `$\alpha = 0.1$`, any token with probability at least 10% of the top token's probability is retained. For a top token at probability 0.5, the cutoff is 0.05, which typically retains 10–100 tokens depending on the distribution's entropy. Lower `$\alpha$` would admit more tokens (risk of false positives from noise amplification), higher `$\alpha$` would admit fewer (risk of false negatives from suppressing genuine factual alternatives that have moderate probability). The paper does not ablate `$\alpha$` extensively, inheriting it from prior work.

**Repetition penalty `$\theta = 1.2`$.** Set following Keskar et al. (2019). Applied to all experiments for consistency, even though some tasks (TruthfulQA short-answer) generate short outputs where repetition is unlikely to be an issue. The paper's ablation in Appendix K shows it helps DoLa specifically on StrategyQA and GSM8K (where long CoT reasoning chains are generated) while having minimal impact on baseline performance.

**Even-numbered layer restriction.** Only layers 0, 2, 4, ... are considered as premature candidates, halving the per-step computation. The paper justifies this implicitly: neighboring layers (e.g., 20 vs. 21) are likely to have very similar outputs due to residual connections, so including odd layers would add computation without adding much information. This is standard practice in early-exit work.

**Bucketing and validation.** The bucket for each model-task pair is selected via:
- **TruthfulQA and FACTOR:** Two-fold cross-validation. The dataset is split into two folds; the best bucket (by MC3 score for TruthfulQA, by accuracy for FACTOR-News/Wiki) is selected on one fold and evaluated on the other, with results averaged. The paper notes that both folds consistently select the same bucket (Table 10), indicating the choice is stable.
- **GSM8K and StrategyQA:** A randomly sampled 10% subset of the GSM8K training set serves as the validation set for both tasks. The best bucket for GSM8K is reused for StrategyQA (since StrategyQA lacks its own training set).
- **Vicuna QA:** No validation set exists. The paper reuses the best bucket from GSM8K/FACTOR (lower layers for all models).

This means the full hyperparameter search for a new model-task combination requires testing only 2–4 configurations (one per bucket), each evaluated on a validation subset—a small fraction of the 16–40 configurations needed for DoLa-static, which would enumerate all even-numbered layers individually.

**Decoding strategy per task type:**
- **Multiple-choice (TruthfulQA MC, FACTOR):** The model computes likelihood scores for each answer choice using the contrastive formula. Greedy decoding is not used; instead, the answer with the highest score under `$\hat{p}$` is selected. The post-softmax is **omitted** for TruthfulQA MC (as discussed above, per empirical validation), while standard `$\hat{p}$` is used for FACTOR.
- **Open-ended generation (TruthfulQA Gen, StrategyQA, GSM8K, Vicuna QA):** Greedy decoding is used for TruthfulQA, StrategyQA, and GSM8K. For Vicuna QA, random sampling with temperature 0.7 and max new tokens 1024 is used to produce diverse, chatbot-style responses.

**Model and hardware configuration.** All experiments use 16-bit floating point inference with batch size 1. LLaMA 7B runs on 1 NVIDIA V100 GPU, 13B on 2 GPUs, 33B on 4 GPUs, and 65B on 8 GPUs, with model weight sharding handled by Huggingface Accelerate. These are not hyperparameters per se but establish the practical feasibility: DoLa adds only 1–8% latency overhead (Table 2) and 1.1% additional GPU memory (Appendix E, Table 9) compared to standard decoding, making it deployable on the hardware already required to run the base model.

**Latency and throughput analysis (Section 4.2, Table 2).** The paper measures decoding latency on TruthfulQA prompts (average input length 250.3 tokens after 6-shot prompting) with forced 50-token generation:

| Model | Baseline (ms/token) | DoLa (ms/token) | Overhead |
|-------|--------------------|--------------------|----------|
| 7B    | 45.4               | 48.0               | ×1.06    |
| 13B   | 77.3               | 83.1               | ×1.08    |
| 33B   | 146.7              | 156.7              | ×1.07    |
| 65B   | 321.6              | 324.9              | ×1.01    |

The overhead decreases with model size because the additional forward passes for early exits (which require computing hidden states at intermediate layers) become a smaller fraction of total computation as the per-layer cost increases. In all cases, the overhead is under 8%, which the paper characterizes as "negligible cost." The throughput reduction is proportionally similar (93–99% of baseline throughput), confirming that DoLa is practical for production deployment.

**Memory overhead (Appendix E, Table 9).** The additional GPU memory required by DoLa (storing intermediate hidden states and computing early-exit distributions) is 1.1% for 7B, 0.8% for 13B, 0.6% for 33B, and 0.6% for 65B—essentially negligible compared to the memory already occupied by model weights and KV caches. This is because the early-exit computation reuses existing hidden states that are already computed during the forward pass; the only additional memory is for storing the JSD computation and the contrastive logits, which are vocabulary-sized (32K–50K floats, or <1MB).

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications: A Summary

- **Contrasting layers rather than models:** Avoids the need for a second model (as in Contrastive Decoding), which introduces hyperparameter sensitivity in amateur model selection (Appendix B shows CD performance varies dramatically with amateur model choice) and requires loading two models into memory. DoLa works within a single model, making it simpler to deploy.

- **Jensen-Shannon Divergence for layer selection over KL divergence or L2 distance:** JSD is symmetric and bounded, making the layer selection stable across tokens with different entropy. KL divergence could be dominated by outlier tokens where one distribution assigns near-zero probability. L2 distance in probability space would be sensitive to the absolute scale of probabilities rather than the relative shape of the distributions.

- **Dynamic per-token selection over static fixed layer:** Accounts for token-level variation in how much processing depth is needed (factual tokens require later layers; function words stabilize early). The DoLa-static ablation (Figures 5–6) shows that the optimal static layer is data-dependent and that dynamic selection is more robust across dataset shifts, while requiring 10× fewer hyperparameter trials.

- **Bucketing into layer ranges over selecting from all layers:** Prevents the premature layer from being selected in inappropriate depth regimes for the task type (e.g., selecting a very high layer for a function word would produce a near-zero contrastive signal). Bucketing also dramatically reduces the hyperparameter search space. The empirical finding that different tasks select different buckets (high for short-answer factuality, low for long-form reasoning) validates this design.

- **Adaptive Plausibility Constraint (APC) with `$\alpha = 0.1$`:** Prevents two failure modes of naive contrast: noise amplification from low-probability tokens with extreme ratios, and suppression of confident predictions that don't change across layers. Without APC, the contrastive distribution would be dominated by spurious tokens. The specific value `$\alpha = 0.1$` follows prior work and is not tuned, suggesting robustness.

- **Repetition penalty `$\theta = 1.2$`:** Compensates for DoLa's tendency to sharpen the distribution (which can cause repetitive generation on long sequences) without fundamentally changing the factual amplification mechanism. The ablation shows it benefits DoLa more than the baseline, confirming it addresses a DoLa-specific side effect.

- **Even-numbered layer restriction:** Halves the computation with negligible information loss, since adjacent layers have nearly identical outputs due to residual connections. Standard in early-exit literature.

- **Two-fold validation for bucket selection:** Ensures the bucket choice is not overfit to a particular data split. The consistency of bucket selection across folds (Table 10) suggests the choice is genuinely task-dependent, not an artifact of the specific validation set.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Hallucination Reduction as an Internal Contrast Problem Rather Than an External Knowledge Problem

The dominant paradigm for improving LLM factuality when this paper was written—and still today—falls into two categories: (1) **bring in external knowledge** (retrieval-augmented generation, web search, knowledge bases) to ground the model's outputs in verifiable sources, or (2) **modify the model's training** (RLHF, fine-tuning on factuality datasets, supervised intervention) to change what the model learns to produce. Both approaches treat the model as a black box that needs outside correction—either at inference time (retrieval) or at training time (fine-tuning). DoLa's fundamental conceptual move is to argue that **the factual knowledge already exists inside the model**, encoded in its later layers, but is being *drowned out* by surface-level statistical patterns that dominate the output distribution. The problem is not missing knowledge—it's a signal-to-noise problem in how the model's internal representations get aggregated into a final prediction.

This reframing matters because it changes what a solution looks like. If hallucinations are caused by missing knowledge, the fix must be external (add knowledge) or expensive (retrain). If hallucinations are caused by the decoding process failing to isolate factual knowledge that's already present, the fix can be **purely internal and inference-time**. DoLa doesn't add anything to the model—no new parameters, no training data, no retrieval corpus. It reweights the signal the model already computes. This is a fundamentally different category of intervention, one that the paper establishes can work even when the model's internal knowledge is imperfect (the 12–17 point gains on TruthfulQA aren't ceiling-level performance, but they're substantial and "free" in terms of training/infrastructure cost).

The paper supports this reframing with the layer-wise JSD analysis (Figure 2 and Appendix A). The fact that factual tokens show sustained high JSD with the final layer through layers 28–30, while function words stabilize by layer 12, is diagnostic evidence that the model *does* distinguish fact-critical from surface-level tokens internally—it just doesn't act on this distinction in standard decoding because the final-layer distribution blends everything together. DoLa's contrastive operator can be understood as a **signal separation** technique: it subtracts out the early-layer surface signal, leaving the late-layer factual differential. This is conceptually analogous to differential signaling in electrical engineering (where you transmit the difference between two wires to cancel common-mode noise), but applied to the layer stack of a transformer.

What makes this an innovation rather than an obvious extension of prior layer-analysis work (Tenney et al., 2019; Dai et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2022) is the leap from *observing* knowledge localization to *operationalizing* it in a decoding algorithm. Those prior works established *that* knowledge is localized; DoLa answers the question "so what can we do with that fact at inference time?" The answer—contrast the distributions, not the representations—is non-obvious. One might have imagined intervening on activations directly (as ITI does with trained probes), or averaging layer outputs, or using early-exit for efficiency (as Schuster et al., 2022 did). Contrasting output distributions is a specific design choice that rests on the insight that the *difference* between an early and late prediction is more informative than either prediction alone, because the common-mode signal (plausible syntax) cancels and the differential-mode signal (emerging factual knowledge) remains.

The boundary condition established by the GPT2-Medium failure (Appendix N, Table 17) strengthens this reframing: small models don't benefit from DoLa because they lack the internal knowledge stratification that makes the contrast meaningful. DoLa doesn't create factuality—it surfaces what's already there. This is both a limitation (it can't correct knowledge the model never learned) and a conceptual clarity: DoLa is a *decoding strategy*, not a knowledge injection strategy, and it works precisely when the model has encoded factual knowledge in a layer-differentiated way.

---

### Innovation 2: Dynamic, Per-Token Premature Layer Selection as a Form of Implicit Difficulty Estimation

The paper's second distinctive conceptual move is recognizing that **the optimal "before" snapshot for contrast varies by token**, and that this variation can be exploited automatically through a distributional distance metric rather than requiring manual specification, training, or per-task optimization. The field had existing contrastive decoding methods (Li et al., 2022) and layer-contrast methods (Gera et al., 2023), but all used **fixed** amateur models or fixed early layers. The paper's insight is that a fixed choice is fundamentally suboptimal because different tokens need different amounts of processing depth—a claim they substantiate with the NER analysis (Appendix A) showing that entities have critical layers in the 22–30 range while non-entities have critical layers at position 0 in 75% of cases.

The dynamic selection via JSD argmax is more than an engineering convenience. It's a form of **implicit lexical difficulty estimation**. Tokens that the model finds "hard" (requiring deep factual processing) will naturally have their predictions change most in later layers, producing a high JSD against the final layer. Tokens that are "easy" (determined by surface statistics) will have low JSD from early layers onward, and the argmax will select an appropriately early premature layer. The system doesn't need to know *why* a token is hard—it just measures, via JSD, *how much the prediction changed* and uses the point of maximum change as the contrast anchor. This is elegant because it requires zero external annotation (no labeling of which tokens are entities, which are function words) and operates purely on the model's own internal signal.

Compared to the alternative of exhaustive static layer selection (DoLa-static), the dynamic approach makes a qualitatively different claim about robustness. DoLa-static can overfit its layer choice to the validation distribution—the paper shows this concretely in Figure 5, where two different 10% GSM8K subsets yield different optimal static layers (layer 10 vs. layer 2), and using one subset's optimal layer on the other degrades performance. Dynamic selection sidesteps this by grounding the layer choice in a property of the specific token being decoded, not a property of the dataset. This means DoLa is more likely to transfer across data distributions without re-validation—a practically important property for deployment where the incoming query distribution may shift over time.

The random selection ablation (Appendix J, Table 15) is a critical negative result that elevates this from "dynamic selection works" to "JSD-based dynamic selection works for a theoretically grounded reason." Random premature layer selection—even when averaged over many samples—*hurts* performance relative to the baseline (e.g., LLaMA-13B drops from 61.1/62.6 to 53.8/54.8 on FACTOR News/Wiki). This means the JSD signal is not just picking a convenient layer; it's capturing something causally relevant about where factual processing occurs. If any layer worked about as well as any other, random selection would be fine. The fact that it's actively harmful means the premature layer choice is consequential, and JSD is an effective heuristic for making it.

---

### Innovation 3: Empirical Demonstration That Knowledge Localization Is a Prerequisite for Layer-Contrast Methods to Work

This is less a "method innovation" and more a **diagnostic finding with broad implications for the research direction**. The paper doesn't just claim DoLa works on large models—it explicitly tests and shows that it fails on small ones (GPT2-Medium, 335M parameters, Appendix N, Table 17). On TruthfulQA MC, GPT2-Medium baseline scores are MC1 23.5, MC2 41.9, MC3 20.0; with DoLa, scores become MC1 22.9, MC2 41.4, MC3 16.4—flat or worse. On FACTOR, the degradation is dramatic: News drops from 41.0 to 22.2, Wiki from 31.6 to 20.9.

This negative result matters because it establishes a **sharp boundary condition** that was not obvious a priori. Prior work on contrastive decoding between models (Li et al., 2022) used model size difference as the contrast axis and showed benefits for fluency/coherence, not factuality. The concurrent ACD work (Gera et al., 2023) found that layer-contrast on small models *increased* hallucinations—a result that could have been interpreted as "layer contrast is harmful for factuality." DoLa's finding resolves this tension: layer contrast *can* improve factuality, but only when the model is large enough to have developed distinct knowledge representations across layers. The paper explicitly attributes ACD's failure to model scale: "Large LLMs storing distinct knowledge across layers is key for DoLa to work."

This diagnostic has forward-looking implications for the field. As researchers develop ever-larger models, the knowledge localization property that DoLa exploits may become more pronounced (bigger models have more capacity to develop specialized layer functions), suggesting DoLa's benefits may scale with model size. Conversely, for techniques like model distillation or pruning that compress large models into smaller ones, this finding warns that layer-differentiated knowledge may be lost in the compression process, potentially making contrastive decoding approaches ineffective on compressed models. The paper doesn't explore this directly, but the logic follows from the boundary condition they establish.

The finding also provides a partial explanation for *why* hallucinations occur in large models: if factual knowledge is concentrated in late layers but surface statistics dominate the output distribution (because they're reinforced at every layer), then standard decoding is effectively underweighting the factual signal relative to the surface signal. DoLa corrects this imbalance. The fact that the correction works better for larger models suggests the imbalance is a structural property of how transformer LMs allocate representational capacity across depth, not just a quirk of individual training runs.

---

### Innovation 4: Verifying That Task Type Determines Which Depth Regime to Contrast Against

The paper's fourth conceptual contribution is the empirical discovery that **the optimal layer range for contrast is task-dependent in a principled way**, not just a hyperparameter to tune blindly. The validation results (Table 10) show a clean pattern:

- **Short-answer factuality tasks (TruthfulQA):** All model sizes select the **highest bucket** of layers—`[16, 32)` for 7B, `[20, 40)` for 13B, `[40, 60)` for 33B, `[60, 80)` for 65B. When the task is to answer a direct factual question with a brief response, contrasting against the very top layers works best, suggesting that the key factual disambiguation happens in the model's final processing stages.

- **Long-form generation, reasoning, and open-ended chat (FACTOR, GSM8K, StrategyQA, Vicuna QA):** All model sizes select the **lowest bucket**—`[0, 16)` for 7B, `[0, 20)` for all larger models. When the output involves many tokens, only some of which are fact-critical, contrasting against very early layers (near the embedding layer) prevents over-amplification of noise on non-factual tokens.

This pattern is not obvious a priori. One might have guessed that more complex tasks (reasoning) would need contrast against higher layers (more processing depth), or that the optimal layer would scale monotonically with task difficulty. Instead, the finding is that tasks with **dense factual content per token** (short answers where every token matters) benefit from late-layer contrast, while tasks with **sparse factual content per token** (long paragraphs where many tokens are syntactic/structural) benefit from early-layer contrast. The paper's interpretation (Section 3.2) is that "contrasting with higher layers works better for key facts, while contrasting with the lower layers can better take care of all the tokens if they include many non-fact tokens that do not require to be contrasted with higher layers."

This has practical significance because it provides a **decision rule for practitioners**: if your task involves short, fact-dense outputs (QA, fact completion), use later-layer buckets; if your task involves long, structured outputs (essays, reasoning chains, dialogue), use earlier-layer buckets. The rule isn't proven to generalize beyond the tested tasks, but the consistency across all four model sizes for each task type is striking and suggests a genuine task-dependent property rather than noise.

The finding also connects to the broader interpretability observation in Figure 2 and Appendix A: factual tokens evolve through late layers, while non-factual tokens stabilize early. For short-answer tasks, nearly every token is fact-critical, so contrasting late layers amplifies the signal on all tokens. For long-form tasks, most tokens are structural, and contrasting late layers would produce near-zero contrastive signals on those tokens (since their predictions already stabilized) while strongly amplifying the few factual tokens—potentially creating an imbalance where factual tokens dominate the distribution and degrade fluency or coherence. Early-layer contrast provides a milder signal that affects all tokens more uniformly, maintaining output quality while still providing some factual boost.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on five datasets spanning multiple choice and open-ended generation. **TruthfulQA** (Lin et al., 2022) tests short-answer factuality with 817 questions, each with multiple true/false answer choices for MC evaluation and an open-ended generation split rated by fine-tuned GPT-3 on truthfulness and informativeness. **FACTOR** (Muhlgay et al., 2023) tests long-paragraph factuality with News and Wiki subsets; each example contains a paragraph and four completions, one correct. **StrategyQA** (Geva et al., 2021) requires implicit multi-hop chain-of-thought reasoning for yes/no questions. **GSM8K** (Cobbe et al., 2021) consists of grade-school math word problems requiring both factual knowledge and arithmetic reasoning. **Vicuna QA** (Chiang et al., 2023) provides 80 open-ended chatbot questions with GPT-4-based pairwise evaluation. Dataset splits: TruthfulQA and FACTOR use their standard test sets; GSM8K uses the full test set (1,319 examples); StrategyQA uses its test set; Vicuna QA uses 80 evaluation examples.

- **Base model(s).** The LLaMA family (Touvron et al., 2023) across four scales: 7B (32 layers), 13B (40 layers), 33B (60 layers), and 65B (80 layers). These were chosen as "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" and because they are open-weight models enabling full internal access for layer-wise computation. MPT-7B (MosaicML, 2023) is tested in Appendix G for cross-family generalization. GPT2-Medium (335M parameters) is tested in Appendix N as a small-model counterfactual. All models use 16-bit floating point inference, batch size 1, with 1/2/4/8 V100 GPUs for the four LLaMA sizes respectively (Appendix F).

- **Metrics.** For TruthfulQA multiple-choice, three metrics: **MC1** (accuracy of the single most likely answer, "winner takes all"), **MC2** (normalized total probability mass assigned to all true answers), and **MC3** (fraction of questions where the model assigns highest probability to a true answer among all true/false choices). For TruthfulQA open-ended generation, two fine-tuned GPT-3 scores: **%Truth** (is the answer truthful?), **%Info** (is the answer informative?), and their product **%Truth∗Info** (the primary metric, balancing the two). **%Reject** measures how often the model outputs "I have no comment," which would trivially achieve 100% truth but 0% info. For FACTOR, standard multiple-choice **accuracy** on News and Wiki subsets separately. For StrategyQA and GSM8K, **exact match accuracy** of the final answer. For Vicuna QA, GPT-4 assigns **scores on a scale of 1–10** for each response and determines **win/tie/lose** counts in pairwise comparisons.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three approaches: **(1) Original decoding:** greedy decoding for TruthfulQA, StrategyQA, and GSM8K; random sampling with temperature 0.7 for Vicuna QA; likelihood scoring for multiple-choice tasks. **(2) Contrastive Decoding (CD)** (Li et al., 2022): contrasts an amateur LLaMA-7B against larger expert models (LLaMA-13B/33B/65B), with the adaptive plausibility constraint `α = 0.1`. Appendix B also explores CD with smaller amateur models (OpenLLaMA-7B/3B, Sheared-LLaMA-2.7B/1.3B) as stronger baselines. **(3) Inference-Time Intervention (ITI)** (Li et al., 2023): uses LLaMA-7B with a linear classifier trained on TruthfulQA labels to shift attention heads during decoding. ITI results are reported for TruthfulQA only, drawn from Li et al. (2023).

- **Generation budget and compute accounting.** Since DoLa is a decoding strategy rather than a search method, the paper does not measure compute in "generations" or "samples." Instead, all comparisons are at the **same decoding length** (same number of generated tokens) between DoLa and the baseline. The additional compute from DoLa's early-exit forward passes is accounted for via latency and throughput measurements (Table 2, Section 4.2), which confirm an overhead of 1–8% across model sizes. For multiple-choice tasks, no generation occurs—likelihood scores are computed under each method and compared directly. For open-ended tasks, the same prompts and decoding parameters are used (greedy or sampling), with only the next-token distribution computation differing. There is no "budget sweep" as in search-based papers; each task uses a single decoding configuration per method.

- **Cross-validation and statistical protocol.** For TruthfulQA multiple-choice and FACTOR, the paper uses **two-fold validation** to select the best candidate layer bucket: the dataset is split into two folds, the best bucket (by MC3 for TruthfulQA, by accuracy for FACTOR-News/Wiki) is selected on one fold and evaluated on the other, with results averaged. The authors note both folds consistently select the same bucket across all model sizes (Appendix F, Table 10). For GSM8K and StrategyQA, a **randomly sampled 10% subset of GSM8K training data** serves as a single validation set, with the best bucket reused for StrategyQA (which lacks its own training set). For Vicuna QA (no validation set available), the best bucket from GSM8K/FACTOR is reused. DoLa-static experiments enumerate all even-numbered layers as premature candidates and report validation-selected performance. The paper does not report confidence intervals or statistical significance tests, relying on the consistency of improvements across model sizes and the large gaps on TruthfulQA (12–17 percentage points) to argue for robustness. The 500-question TruthfulQA test set and the 80-example Vicuna QA set are relatively small for estimating precision of the reported gains.

### Main Quantitative Results

The paper organizes its main experiments into three categories: multiple-choice factuality, open-ended generation factuality, and instruction-following chat quality. I present each in turn.

#### Multiple-Choice Factuality: TruthfulQA and FACTOR

**TruthfulQA MC (Table 1, left columns).** The headline finding is that DoLa substantially improves over baselines across all four LLaMA sizes, with the gains concentrated in the more stable MC2 and MC3 metrics (which aggregate over all true/false answers) rather than MC1 (winner-takes-all, more sensitive to single-token fluctuations):

- **LLaMA-7B:** MC1 rises from 25.6 to 32.2 (+6.6), MC2 from 40.6 to 63.8 (+23.2), MC3 from 19.2 to 32.1 (+12.9). This is a massive improvement on MC2—nearly a 60% relative increase—indicating that DoLa dramatically redistributes probability mass toward true answers even when the single most-likely answer doesn't change.
- **LLaMA-13B:** MC2 rises from 43.3 to 64.9 (+21.6), MC3 from 20.8 to 34.8 (+14.0). MC1 shows a modest gain from 28.3 to 28.9 (+0.6), which the paper attributes to MC1's sensitivity to fluctuations.
- **LLaMA-33B:** MC2 rises from 49.5 to 62.3 (+12.8), MC3 from 24.2 to 34.0 (+9.8). MC1 drops slightly from 31.7 to 30.5, which the paper does not explain in detail but is consistent with the "fluctuation sensitivity" narrative.
- **LLaMA-65B:** MC2 rises from 46.9 to 64.6 (+17.7), MC3 from 22.7 to 34.3 (+11.6). MC1 is essentially flat (30.8 → 31.1).

Compared to ITI (which uses supervised training on TruthfulQA labels), DoLa matches or exceeds ITI's TruthfulQA MC1 on 7B (32.2 vs. 25.9) and does so without any training data. Compared to CD, DoLa consistently outperforms on MC2 and MC3 across all model sizes, often by large margins (e.g., 13B MC2: 64.9 DoLa vs. 41.0 CD; 65B MC2: 64.6 DoLa vs. 47.0 CD). The CD baseline mostly underperforms even the vanilla decoding baseline on MC scores, which the paper does not explicitly analyze in Section 3.2 but attributes generally to the amateur model (7B) being too different from the expert to provide useful contrast for factuality.

**The chosen candidate layer buckets** (Appendix F, Table 10) show a consistent task-dependent pattern: all four LLaMA model sizes select their **highest** bucket for TruthfulQA—`[16, 32)` for 7B, `[20, 40)` for 13B, `[40, 60)` for 33B, `[60, 80)` for 65B. The paper interprets this as reflecting that TruthfulQA's short, fact-critical answer choices benefit from contrasting against the layers where factual knowledge is most concentrated (the topmost layers). This contrasts sharply with the long-form tasks discussed below.

**FACTOR (Table 1, middle columns).** For long-paragraph factuality, DoLa provides consistent but more modest gains of 2–4% across model sizes:

- **LLaMA-7B:** News 58.3 → 62.0 (+3.7), Wiki 58.6 → 62.2 (+3.6)
- **LLaMA-13B:** News 61.1 → 62.5 (+1.4), Wiki 62.6 → 66.2 (+3.6)
- **LLaMA-33B:** News 63.8 → 65.4 (+1.6), Wiki 69.5 → 70.3 (+0.8)
- **LLaMA-65B:** News 63.6 → 66.2 (+2.6), Wiki 72.2 → 72.4 (+0.2)

These are smaller relative gains than TruthfulQA, which the paper attributes (Section 3.2) to FACTOR involving longer paragraph choices where "many non-fact tokens do not require to be contrasted with higher layers." The gains come from choosing a different bucket: all models select their **lowest** bucket for FACTOR—`[0, 16)` for 7B, `[0, 20)` for 13/33/65B. Contrasting against very early layers provides a broader, milder signal that lifts factuality across all tokens without the risk of over-amplifying individual factual tokens that could distort the longer text. CD is less effective on FACTOR, with DoLa outperforming it on most model-subset combinations (the exception being 13B on Wiki, where CD reaches 64.4 vs. DoLa's 66.2—a narrow DoLa win, but the paper acknowledges CD outperforms in the text).

**MPT-7B verification (Appendix G, Table 11).** To check cross-family generalization, MPT-7B is tested on TruthfulQA and FACTOR. On TruthfulQA open-ended generation, %Truth∗Info rises from 26.6 to 46.0 (+19.4), an even larger gain than LLaMA-7B's +11.7. On FACTOR, News improves from 67.4 to 68.5 (+1.1) and Wiki from 59.0 to 62.3 (+3.3). The paper takes this as evidence that "DoLa can still improve the performance for the LLMs beyond LLaMA," though the FACTOR gains are mixed (News improvement is marginal) and no systematic sweep of model families is conducted.

#### Open-Ended Generation Factuality: TruthfulQA, Chain-of-Thought, and Chat

**TruthfulQA open-ended generation (Table 1, right-of-center columns).** This is the paper's most dramatic result and the core of its factuality claim. The metrics are %Truth, %Info, %Truth∗Info (product), and %Reject (fraction of "I have no comment" responses). The primary metric is %Truth∗Info, which penalizes models that achieve high truthfulness by refusing to answer:

- **LLaMA-7B:** %Truth from 30.4 to 42.1 (+11.7), %Info from 96.3 to 98.3, %Truth∗Info from 26.9 to 40.8 (+13.9), %Reject from 2.9 to 0.6. This means DoLa produced truthful answers 42.1% of the time while remaining informative 98.3% of the time—a substantial increase in truthfulness without sacrificing informativeness.
- **LLaMA-13B:** %Truth∗Info from 32.4 to 44.6 (+12.2), %Reject from 6.7 to 2.1
- **LLaMA-33B:** %Truth∗Info from 31.7 to 49.1 (+17.4), %Reject from 38.1 to 8.2
- **LLaMA-65B:** %Truth∗Info from 34.8 to 49.2 (+14.4), %Reject from 19.1 to 4.8

The 12–17 absolute percentage point gains in %Truth∗Info are the headline numbers. Notably, these improvements come with **reduced rejection rates** across all model sizes, meaning DoLa both makes the model more truthful *and* more willing to answer rather than defaulting to "I have no comment." This contrasts sharply with CD, which shows inflated %Truth scores but abysmal %Info because it frequently refuses to answer—LLaMA-33B with CD achieves %Truth of 81.5 but %Info of only 45.0 (and %Reject of 62.7), yielding a %Truth∗Info of 36.7, substantially below DoLa's 49.1. The paper attributes CD's refusal behavior to the fact that CD uses LLaMA-7B as the amateur model for all larger experts; the larger models are better at instruction-following and learn from the TruthfulQA prompt that "I have no comment" is an acceptable response, so CD amplifies this tendency. DoLa avoids this because it contrasts within the same model, where both layers share the same instruction-following capacity.

Compared to ITI on LLaMA-7B, DoLa achieves 40.8 %Truth∗Info vs. ITI's 43.5—slightly lower, but achieved without any supervised training on TruthfulQA labels. This is a strong validation point: a completely unsupervised decoding strategy approaches the performance of a method that required training a classifier on labeled truthfulness data specific to the evaluation task.

**Chain-of-thought reasoning: StrategyQA and GSM8K (Table 1, rightmost columns).** For tasks requiring multi-step reasoning, the improvements are more modest but consistent:

- **StrategyQA (yes/no reasoning):**
  - LLaMA-7B: 60.1 → 64.1 (+4.0)
  - LLaMA-13B: 66.6 → 67.6 (+1.0)
  - LLaMA-33B: 69.9 → 72.1 (+2.2)
  - LLaMA-65B: 70.5 → 72.9 (+2.4)

  Gains range from 1–4% across model sizes. CD mostly *worsens* performance on StrategyQA (e.g., 13B drops from 66.6 to 60.3), which the paper attributes to CD impairing the reasoning ability of large LMs by contrasting with the 7B model that "has a certain level of reasoning ability."

- **GSM8K (math word problems):**
  - LLaMA-7B: 10.8 → 10.5 (−0.3, essentially flat)
  - LLaMA-13B: 16.7 → 18.0 (+1.3)
  - LLaMA-33B: 33.8 → 35.5 (+1.7)
  - LLaMA-65B: 51.2 → 54.0 (+2.8)

  The 7B model shows no improvement, while gains of 1.3–2.8% appear for 13B–65B. The paper's conclusion that "contrasting layers by DoLa is still helpful" is technically true for the larger models, but the 7B null result is worth noting—at the smallest scale tested within the LLaMA family, the method provides no benefit on this task. This is not inconsistent with the paper's narrative (larger models have more pronounced knowledge localization), but it does suggest a minimum scale threshold even within the LLaMA family for some task types.

The chosen candidate layer buckets for both StrategyQA and GSM8K are the **lowest** (same as FACTOR)—`[0, 16)` for 7B, `[0, 20)` for 13B/33B/65B. This aligns with the paper's task-type hypothesis: long reasoning chains contain many structural/arithmetic tokens that don't benefit from late-layer contrast, and using early-layer contrast provides a uniform mild boost without destabilizing the chain structure.

**Vicuna QA instruction following (Figure 4, Section 3.3).** For open-ended chatbot evaluation using GPT-4 judgments, DoLa uses the lower-layer buckets (reusing GSM8K/FACTOR validation results since Vicuna QA lacks its own validation set). Pairwise comparisons between LLaMA+DoLa and LLaMA baseline (80 questions) show:

- **7B:** DoLa wins 43 games, ties 4, loses 33
- **13B:** DoLa wins 52, ties 3, loses 25
- **33B:** DoLa wins 42, ties 3, loses 35
- **65B:** DoLa wins 39, ties 3, loses 38

The overall scores (left panel) show DoLa notably outperforming the baseline for 13B and 33B models, with smaller margins for 7B and 65B. The 65B result (39 wins, 38 losses) is essentially a tie, suggesting that at the largest scale tested, DoLa's benefits on open-ended chat may diminish. The authors attribute DoLa's success in the qualitative examples (Appendix M, Tables 18–20) to providing "more detailed, accurate, and relevant" responses, while acknowledging that DoLa sometimes generates less relevant answers (Tables 21–22) that cause GPT-4 to prefer the baseline.

**GPT-4 evaluation of text quality excluding factuality (Appendix D, Table 8).** To check whether DoLa improves or degrades general text quality beyond factuality, the paper reruns GPT-4 evaluation with a modified prompt instructing GPT-4 to "rate by the grammaticality and cohesiveness of their responses, but not factuality." Results (1–10 scale, averaged over 80 examples):

- 7B: 6.44 baseline → 6.96 DoLa (+0.52)
- 13B: 7.06 → 7.98 (+0.92)
- 33B: 6.89 → 7.84 (+0.95)
- 65B: 8.04 → 8.01 (−0.03, essentially tied)

DoLa improves or maintains grammaticality/cohesiveness for 7B/13B/33B, with a small drop for 65B. This counters a potential concern that the contrastive operation might produce ungrammatical or incoherent text by disrupting the syntactic scaffold—the results suggest DoLa's text quality is on par or slightly better than standard decoding even when factuality is excluded from evaluation.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Dynamic vs. static premature layer selection (Section 4.1, Figures 5–6, and Appendix H, I):** The paper presents DoLa-static as the primary ablation variant, where a single fixed layer (selected via validation performance) is used for all tokens instead of dynamic per-token JSD-based selection. On GSM8K validation subsets (Figure 5), DoLa-static can outperform DoLa when the optimal layer is found (e.g., layer 10 in subset #1 achieves ~0.13 accuracy vs. DoLa's ~0.10), but the optimal layer shifts across data subsets (layer 10 in subset #1 vs. layer 2 in subset #2). DoLa maintains consistent performance near the best DoLa-static layer across both subsets without requiring per-subset tuning, supporting the robustness claim. On FACTOR (Figure 6), DoLa-static with validation-selected layers can slightly outperform DoLa on some model-subset combinations (Table 13: e.g., 13B News 63.6 DoLa-static vs. 62.5 DoLa), but DoLa requires testing only 2–4 bucket configurations versus 16–40 individual layer tests for DoLa-static. The paper argues this validates DoLa as "more robust and generalizable, only requiring a very small hyperparameter search space."

**Random layer selection baseline (Appendix J, Table 15):** Selecting the premature layer randomly at each decoding step (rather than via JSD argmax) causes performance to drop *below* the vanilla baseline on FACTOR. For example, LLaMA-13B with random selection scores 53.8/54.8 on News/Wiki vs. the baseline of 61.1/62.6, while DoLa achieves 62.5/66.2. This confirms the JSD signal is essential—arbitrary layer selection introduces harmful noise.

**Contrasting with layer 0 (word embedding) vs. all layers (Appendix C, Table 7):** On TruthfulQA MC, the paper compares DoLa against two restricted variants: (a) always contrasting with the 0-th (embedding) layer, and (b) dynamically selecting from all even-numbered layers without bucketing. Both variants improve over the baseline but underperform DoLa. For LLaMA-7B, MC2 scores are: baseline 40.6, layer-0-only 61.7, all-layers 63.9, DoLa 63.8. For LLaMA-13B, MC3 scores are: baseline 20.8, layer-0-only 30.2, all-layers 31.0, DoLa 34.8. The bucketing approach (DoLa) matches or exceeds the unbucketed variant while constraining the premature layer to a task-appropriate depth regime, and substantially outperforms the layer-0-only variant on larger models.

**Adaptive plausibility constraint (APC) `α = 0.1`:** The paper inherits this value from Li et al. (2022) without ablating it. The mechanism (described in Section 2.3) is analyzed qualitatively: without APC, "an implausible token with an extremely low score may be rewarded with a high score after contrast" (false positives) and "when the model is very confident about an easy decision, the output probability of a high-score token does not change much in different layers and results in low scores after contrast" (false negatives). No quantitative ablation of α values is provided, which is a limitation—the reader cannot assess how sensitive results are to this parameter.

**Repetition penalty `θ = 1.2` (Appendix K, Figures 7–8):** The impact of varying `θ` from 1.0 (no penalty) to 2.0 is tested on StrategyQA and GSM8K for all model sizes. Key patterns: (1) DoLa with no repetition penalty (`θ = 1.0`) underperforms DoLa with `θ = 1.2` on both tasks, confirming the need for the penalty. (2) For DoLa, performance peaks at `θ = 1.2` and degrades slightly at higher values. (3) For the baseline, the repetition penalty generally *hurts* performance—it's optimal at `θ = 1.0` (no penalty). This asymmetry confirms that DoLa's sharpened distribution creates a repetition tendency that the baseline doesn't have, and the penalty is a targeted fix rather than a universal improvement. (4) For CD, the penalty provides small gains but CD remains below baseline and below DoLa.

**Post-softmax omission for TruthfulQA MC (Appendix C, Table 6):** On TruthfulQA multiple-choice, omitting the final softmax after the contrastive operation improves LLaMA-7B's MC2 from 52.2 to 63.8 and MC3 from 28.2 to 32.1. This is an empirical finding without deep theoretical justification; the paper states it was "found that not applying the softmax function... can make the performance even better" and applies this only to TruthfulQA MC. Whether this generalizes to other multiple-choice tasks is untested (FACTOR uses standard softmax).

**GPT2-Medium: failure on small models (Appendix N, Table 17):** Applying DoLa to GPT2-Medium (335M parameters, 24 layers) produces no improvement or degradation on TruthfulQA MC (MC2: 41.9 baseline → 41.4 DoLa; MC3: 20.0 → 16.4) and severe degradation on FACTOR (News: 41.0 → 22.2; Wiki: 31.6 → 20.9). The paper attributes this to small models lacking "distinct knowledge across layers" for DoLa to exploit, establishing model scale as a necessary condition for the method to work.

**MPT-7B cross-family generalization (Appendix G, Table 11):** DoLa improves MPT-7B's TruthfulQA %Truth∗Info from 26.6 to 46.0 (+19.4), FACTOR News from 67.4 to 68.5, Wiki from 59.0 to 62.3, and StrategyQA from 59.5 to 60.3, but *reduces* GSM8K from 8.3 to 8.0. The pattern of gains on factuality with mixed results on math reasoning mirrors the LLaMA results, suggesting some cross-family transfer, but the single model tested (MPT-7B) and the small sample (one model from one alternative family) limit the strength of this generalization claim.

**CD with varied amateur models (Appendix B, Table 5):** This is technically a baseline exploration rather than a DoLa ablation, but it's informative. Using smaller amateur models (down to 1.3B parameters) improves CD performance on GSM8K compared to using LLaMA-7B as the amateur, but CD still generally falls below the vanilla baseline and well below DoLa. For example, LLaMA-33B baseline is 33.81, best CD variant is 34.87 (Sheared-LLaMA-1.3B as amateur), while DoLa reaches 35.41. This confirms that the amateur model selection problem in CD is non-trivial and that even optimized CD underperforms DoLa, supporting the paper's claim that intra-model contrast is simpler and more effective than inter-model contrast for factuality.

**Latency and memory overhead analysis (Section 4.2, Table 2; Appendix E, Table 9):** While not an ablation of the method's components, these measurements establish the practical cost. DoLa increases latency by 1–8% across model sizes (7B: 1.06×, 13B: 1.08×, 33B: 1.07×, 65B: 1.01×) and increases GPU memory overhead by 0.6–1.1% relative to baseline. The overhead shrinks with model size because the additional computation for early-exit forward passes becomes a smaller fraction of the total. These numbers are small enough that the paper characterizes DoLa as having "negligible cost," though the 8% latency increase on 13B is non-trivial in high-throughput settings.

### Critical Assessment

**Does DoLa improve factuality across diverse tasks?** The evidence supports a **conditional yes**, with the condition being that the model is large enough to exhibit layer-differentiated knowledge. On TruthfulQA—the primary benchmark for the paper's factuality claim—the gains are large and consistent: 12–17 absolute percentage points in %Truth∗Info across all four LLaMA sizes (Table 1). These numbers are striking enough that random variation or test-set overfitting are unlikely explanations. On FACTOR, gains are smaller (1–4%) but consistent. On MPT-7B (Appendix G), TruthfulQA gains replicate but FACTOR gains are mixed (News +1.1, Wiki +3.3). On GSM8K, gains are modest (0–2.8%) and absent for the 7B model. On StrategyQA, gains are similarly modest (1–4%). The paper's headline claim that "DoLa consistently improves truthfulness across multiple choice tasks and open-ended generation tasks" is technically true but masks significant variation in effect size across tasks—the improvement on TruthfulQA is qualitatively different from the improvement on FACTOR or GSM8K, and the paper does not deeply interrogate why some factuality tasks see 12+ point gains while others see 1–2 point gains.

**What was actually tested vs. what was claimed?** The paper's primary empirical claim is about **truthfulness/factuality**. However, the tasks used to demonstrate factuality improvements are mostly about **factual recall** (TruthfulQA asks about real-world facts; FACTOR tests whether a completion is consistent with a given paragraph). The reasoning tasks (StrategyQA, GSM8K) mix factual knowledge with logical inference and arithmetic. DoLa's gains on reasoning are substantially smaller and non-existent for 7B on GSM8K, suggesting that the method primarily helps with **retrieving stored facts** rather than with reasoning per se. This is consistent with the mechanism (contrasting surface patterns from deeper semantic knowledge) but is not explicitly bounded by the paper—the abstract claims DoLa "reduces hallucinations" broadly, while the evidence more precisely supports "improves factual recall when the model already knows the fact." The Vicuna QA results (Figure 4) show that DoLa can improve open-ended chatbot responses judged by GPT-4, but the paper's own qualitative examples (Tables 21–22) acknowledge that DoLa sometimes generates less relevant answers, and at 65B scale the win/loss count is essentially tied (39–38). This suggests the method's benefits for open-ended generation may saturate or reverse at larger scales.

**Genuine weaknesses in experimental design:**

1. **Single model family for primary results.** All main experiments use LLaMA models (Touvron et al., 2023). The MPT-7B test (Appendix G) provides weak cross-family evidence—it's a single model at a single scale. The paper's claim that DoLa "could potentially be applied to any transformer-based LLMs" (Section 6) is speculative based on the current evidence. Models with different architectural choices (e.g., non-residual architectures, different normalization schemes, different training objectives) might not exhibit the same layer-wise knowledge localization that DoLa depends on. A single alternative model at 7B scale does not establish general applicability.

2. **No systematic scale analysis within LLaMA family for all tasks.** The paper tests four LLaMA sizes, which is good, but the relationship between scale and DoLa's effectiveness is not analyzed. On some metrics, gains shrink at larger scales (FACTOR Wiki: +3.6 at 7B but +0.2 at 65B; Vicuna QA: large win margins at 13B/33B but near-tie at 65B). On others, gains are roughly constant (TruthfulQA %Truth∗Info: gains of 12–17 points across all sizes). The paper does not discuss whether DoLa's benefits asymptote, peak at a particular scale, or continue to grow. This is a missed opportunity given the paper's central claim that knowledge localization (which should be more pronounced at scale) is the mechanism.

3. **Validation set selection introduces potential data leakage concerns.** For GSM8K and StrategyQA, the validation bucket is selected using a 10% subset of GSM8K training data. This is standard practice, but the paper then evaluates on the GSM8K test set. For StrategyQA, the GSM8K validation set is reused, meaning the bucket choice for a fundamentally different task (multi-hop reasoning vs. math word problems) is informed by a dataset that may not be representative. The paper justifies this by noting StrategyQA lacks its own training set, but the circularity (using math performance to tune a reasoning model) is not examined. For Vicuna QA, where no validation set exists at all, the GSM8K/FACTOR buckets are simply reused—this means the Vicuna QA results are technically zero-shot with respect to the bucket hyperparameter, but the bucket was chosen using a correlated task, making it unclear how this would work on a truly novel task with no related validation data.

4. **No ablation of `α` (adaptive plausibility constraint threshold).** The value `α = 0.1` is inherited from Li et al. (2022) without any sensitivity analysis. Given that APC prevents two specific failure modes (false positives from noise amplification, false negatives from confident predictions), the threshold likely matters for the tradeoff between these modes. The paper provides no evidence that 0.1 is near-optimal for factuality tasks, or that results are robust to variations in this parameter. This is a significant gap—a skeptical reader might wonder whether tuning α per task would substantially change the reported gains.

5. **Small test sets for some evaluations.** TruthfulQA has 817 test questions, which is reasonable. FACTOR has unknown test set size (the paper doesn't report the number of News/Wiki test examples), but the two-fold validation implies it's large enough to split. StrategyQA's test set size is not reported. GSM8K has 1,319 test examples. Vicuna QA uses only 80 examples for GPT-4 evaluation, making those win/loss counts noisy—a few borderline judgments by GPT-4 could flip several win/loss outcomes. The paper does not report statistical significance for any comparison, which is common in LLM evaluation but limits the strength of claims about small-margin improvements (e.g., DoLa's 0.2 point gain on FACTOR Wiki for 65B).

6. **The TruthfulQA open-ended metric (%Truth∗Info) conflates truthfulness and informativeness multiplicatively.** A model that is truthful 60% of the time and informative 100% of the time gets the same score (60) as a model that is truthful 100% of the time and informative 60% of the time. These represent very different behaviors. The paper reports the individual components, so the reader can disaggregate, but the headline gains of "12–17 absolute points" on %Truth∗Info are primarily driven by truthfulness improvements (with info staying high or improving). This is genuinely good, but the metric's multiplicative structure means the gains appear larger in percentage-point terms than they would in an additive metric.

**Experiments that would have strengthened the paper:**

- **Ablation of model scale with a single architecture family across a wider range**, including models smaller than 7B (e.g., LLaMA-3B if available, or OPT-350M/1.3B/2.7B/6.7B) to precisely locate where the knowledge localization threshold kicks in and how gains scale with parameter count. The GPT2-Medium failure (335M) and LLaMA-7B success (7B) leave a large gap in between.
- **Testing on a factuality dataset with fine-grained difficulty categories** (e.g., by question type, by frequency of the tested fact in training data, by entity popularity) to understand *which kinds of factual knowledge* DoLa surfaces and which it doesn't. The method's mechanism (contrasting surface patterns from deep knowledge) should work better for facts where the model has strong deep representations but weak surface co-occurrence—but this prediction is untestable with the current evaluation setup.
- **Systematic comparison with RAG baselines.** The paper positions DoLa as an alternative to retrieval-based approaches (Section 6), but never compares against them directly. A simple retrieval baseline (e.g., retrieving relevant Wikipedia passages and conditioning on them) would contextualize DoLa's gains—is a 12-point TruthfulQA improvement competitive with retrieval augmentation on the same model, or does retrieval provide substantially larger gains at higher infrastructure cost?
- **Combination with ITI or CD.** The paper claims DoLa is complementary to other interventions, but never tests combinations. Would DoLa + ITI on LLaMA-7B surpass ITI alone? Would DoLa + CD (contrasting layers *and* models) provide additional gains? These combination experiments would test the paper's modularity claim.
- **Human evaluation of factuality for open-ended generation.** The TruthfulQA open-ended metric relies on fine-tuned GPT-3, and Vicuna QA on GPT-4. While these are reasonable automated proxies, they introduce the possibility that the evaluator models share biases with the evaluated models. Human factuality judgments, at least on a subset, would strengthen the claims.

**Where claims hold conditionally:**

- **"DoLa consistently improves truthfulness":** Holds strongly for short-answer factuality (TruthfulQA), weakly-to-moderately for long-paragraph factuality (FACTOR), weakly for reasoning tasks (StrategyQA, GSM8K), and not at all for small models or LLaMA-7B on GSM8K. The consistency is in the *direction* of improvement (rarely negative on large models) but not in the *magnitude*, which varies by an order of magnitude across tasks.
- **"DoLa reduces hallucinations without external knowledge or fine-tuning":** Holds for the tested tasks and models, but the paper does not evaluate on the kind of hallucination that retrieval-augmented methods typically address—generating claims about specific entities or events that can be verified against a knowledge base. TruthfulQA tests common misconceptions and "common sense" factuality, not the kind of detailed factual accuracy needed in clinical or legal settings (the very applications the paper mentions in the introduction). The claim that DoLa addresses hallucinations "in high-stakes applications" is aspirational based on the current evidence.
- **"Dynamic selection is more robust than static selection":** Supported for GSM8K (Figure 5) where the optimal static layer shifts across data subsets while DoLa remains stable. However, on FACTOR (Table 13), DoLa-static with validation-selected layers actually slightly outperforms DoLa on several model-subset pairs. The robustness claim holds in the sense that DoLa requires less hyperparameter tuning, but not necessarily in the sense that DoLa always matches or exceeds the best possible static layer when that layer is optimally chosen for the specific distribution.
- **"DoLa can be widely applied with negligible cost":** Supported for latency (1–8% overhead) and memory (0.6–1.1% overhead), but the "widely applied" part requires assuming that knowledge localization is a general property of large transformers—an assumption the paper itself shows fails for GPT2-Medium. The boundary condition (model must be large enough) limits "widely" to models above some unknown size threshold, likely in the billions of parameters.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: Knowledge Localization Is a Prerequisite, Not a Guarantee

**The assumption or constraint.** DoLa's entire mechanism depends on the assumption that factual knowledge is encoded differentially across transformer layers—specifically, that later layers contain factual information not present in earlier layers, and that subtracting early-layer predictions from late-layer predictions can isolate this factual signal. The paper makes this assumption explicit in Section 2.1 ("Factual knowledge evolves across layers") and grounds it in prior interpretability work (Tenney et al., 2019; Dai et al., 2022; Meng et al., 2022). However, the authors also acknowledge in Section 5 that this property is not universal: contemporaneous work by Gera et al. (2023) on Autocontrastive Decoding found that layer contrast on small models *increased* hallucinations.

**The consequence.** DoLa provides no benefit—and can actively harm performance—when applied to models that lack distinct knowledge stratification across layers. This means the method is not a universal decoding strategy that can be applied to any pretrained LM; it is effective only above some model scale threshold where layer-differentiated factual knowledge emerges. A practitioner with a model in the hundreds of millions of parameters (a common deployment scale for on-device or low-latency applications) cannot expect DoLa to help and may see degraded outputs. More subtly, even for large models, DoLa's effectiveness depends on *which* facts are localized and *how strongly*—facts that are diffusely encoded across many layers may not be surfaced by the contrastive operation, while facts that are strongly localized benefit most. The paper provides no diagnostic for determining whether a given model or fact type will benefit.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Appendix N (Table 17) provides direct negative evidence: applying DoLa to GPT2-Medium (335M parameters) produces no improvement on TruthfulQA MC (MC2 drops from 41.9 to 41.4; MC3 drops from 20.0 to 16.4) and severe degradation on FACTOR (News falls from 41.0 to 22.2; Wiki from 31.6 to 20.9). The paper interprets this as confirming that "small LMs do not learn enough factual knowledge due to their limited capacities" and that "different layers in small LMs do not have enough distinctions." The LLaMA-7B GSM8K result (Table 1: accuracy drops from 10.8 to 10.5) hints that even at 7B parameters, some task-model combinations may be near the threshold where DoLa is ineffective. However, the paper does not systematically map out where this threshold lies—the gap between GPT2-335M (failure) and LLaMA-7B (success on most tasks) is large, and untested intermediate scales (1B, 3B, 6B parameters) could reveal the transition point.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially acknowledges this limitation in Section 6: "DoLa also has limitations:... our method relies on the model's internal knowledge." However, the scale-dependence is framed as an observation about small models rather than a fundamental constraint on the method's applicability. The authors do not provide a diagnostic test, a minimum scale recommendation, or a method for predicting which model-task pairs will benefit. Future work on automated detection of knowledge localization strength (e.g., by measuring inter-layer JSD variance on a held-out factual probe set) would address this gap.

### Limitation 2: The Difficulty Estimation Cost (Early-Exit Computation) Is Significant for Some Deployment Regimes

**The assumption or constraint.** DoLa requires computing the vocabulary head `φ(·)` on the hidden states of multiple intermediate layers at every decoding step—specifically, for all even-numbered layers in the selected candidate bucket. For LLaMA-7B with the low-layer bucket `[0, 16)`, this means computing `φ(h_j)` for j ∈ {0, 2, 4, ..., 14} (8 early-exit forward passes) plus the standard final-layer forward pass at each token. The paper measures this cost and reports it as "negligible" in Section 4.2, with latency increases of 1–8% and throughput decreases of 1–7% across model sizes (Table 2). However, the measured scenario (single example, batch size 1, 50-token forced generation on TruthfulQA) may not represent all deployment settings.

**The consequence.** In high-throughput production environments where batch sizes are large and latency is tightly constrained (e.g., real-time chatbots serving thousands of queries per second), a 5–8% per-token overhead translates directly to either 5–8% higher serving costs or 5–8% fewer queries served per GPU. For the 13B model, which shows the highest overhead (1.08× latency), this is non-trivial at scale. More importantly, the *relative* overhead depends on the ratio of early-exit computation to standard forward pass cost, which is affected by sequence length, batch size, and hardware utilization. The paper's measurements (single-example, short context, forced 50-token generation) likely represent a worst case for relative overhead, since the standard forward pass is fast under these conditions. In high-batch, long-context scenarios, the absolute overhead increases while the relative overhead may decrease or increase depending on whether the early exits can be batched efficiently. The paper does not profile these scenarios.

Additionally, the memory overhead (0.6–1.1%, Appendix E Table 9) is measured as GPU memory consumed *during forward passes*, but this does not account for the fact that intermediate hidden states must be *retained* for the early-exit computation. In standard decoding, hidden states at intermediate layers can be freed after they are used as inputs to the next layer. DoLa requires retaining them (or recomputing them, which would increase latency further) until the JSD is computed and the premature layer is selected. The paper's memory measurements appear to include this overhead, but the implementation details are not provided.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The latency and throughput measurements in Table 2 and Appendix F use TruthfulQA's 817 examples with 6-shot prompting (average input length 250.3 tokens) and forced 50-token generation, on single V100 GPUs with batch size 1. The paper reports overheads ranging from 1.01× (65B) to 1.08× (13B). The memory overhead analysis in Appendix E (Table 9) reports peak GPU memory during forward passes and shows absolute overheads of 1–3% above baseline. No measurements are provided for batched inference, variable sequence lengths, or constrained-memory edge deployment scenarios.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this cost—it reports it and characterizes it as acceptable. The observation that overhead *decreases* with model size (from 1.08× at 13B to 1.01× at 65B) suggests that for the largest models (where hallucinations are most problematic and fine-tuning is most expensive), the overhead is truly minimal. However, for mid-size models in cost-sensitive deployments, the overhead may be a meaningful fraction of the inference budget. The paper suggests no optimizations (e.g., caching early-exit projections, using a subset of layers, early-stopping the JSD computation when a sufficiently divergent layer is found). Future work on reducing the number of candidate layers (perhaps adaptively, by learning which layers are most informative for different token types) would address this.

### Limitation 3: Task-Type Dependence Requires Per-Task Validation, Limiting Zero-Shot Applicability

**The assumption or constraint.** DoLa's performance depends critically on selecting the correct candidate layer bucket for the task at hand. The paper finds that different tasks require different depth regimes: short-answer factuality (TruthfulQA) selects the highest bucket across all model sizes, while long-form generation and reasoning tasks (FACTOR, GSM8K, StrategyQA, Vicuna QA) select the lowest bucket (Appendix F, Table 10). The bucket is selected via a validation set—two-fold cross-validation for TruthfulQA and FACTOR, a 10% GSM8K training subset for GSM8K and StrategyQA, and reused GSM8K/FACTOR buckets for Vicuna QA. The paper argues that bucketing reduces the hyperparameter search space from 16–40 individual layer choices (DoLa-static) to 2–4 bucket choices.

**The consequence.** DoLa is not a zero-shot method in practice. To deploy it on a new task, a practitioner needs a labeled validation set representative of the deployment distribution to select the appropriate bucket. This requirement is modest (a few hundred examples) but non-zero, and it introduces the risk of distribution shift: if the validation set does not match the deployment distribution (e.g., a chatbot deployed for a new domain), the bucket choice may be suboptimal. The paper's DoLa-static analysis (Figure 5, right) demonstrates exactly this failure mode: the optimal static layer on one 10% GSM8K subset (layer 10) underperforms when applied to a different 10% subset (where layer 2 is optimal). While DoLa's dynamic selection is more robust than static selection, the *bucket* within which dynamic selection operates is still fixed per task.

More subtly, the paper's finding that different tasks select opposite ends of the layer stack (highest vs. lowest bucket) means there is no universal default. A practitioner cannot simply "use the middle layers" and expect reasonable performance—using the wrong bucket can produce results worse than the baseline. The Vicuna QA results (Figure 4) are particularly instructive: the bucket was chosen using GSM8K/FACTOR validation, not Vicuna QA validation (since none exists). The 65B model shows essentially tied performance (39 wins, 38 losses), which could mean either that DoLa provides no benefit at this scale for chat, or that the reused bucket choice is suboptimal for this task.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 10 (Appendix F) shows the consistent but task-dependent bucket patterns. Figure 5 shows DoLa-static optimal layer sensitivity across GSM8K subsets. The Vicuna QA experiment (Section 3.3) explicitly acknowledges using GSM8K/FACTOR buckets "since Vicuna QA lacks a validation set," making it a zero-shot test of bucket transfer. The MPT-7B results (Appendix G, Table 11) implicitly test bucket transfer across model families. However, the paper does not systematically evaluate how performance degrades when using the "wrong" bucket—e.g., applying the TruthfulQA high-layer bucket to FACTOR or vice versa. This ablation would directly quantify the cost of bucket mismatch.

**Mitigation status.** The paper implicitly mitigates this through the bucketing strategy itself—by restricting the search to 2–4 buckets rather than 16–40 individual layers, the validation burden is already reduced 10× compared to DoLa-static. However, the fundamental need for a validation set is not eliminated. The paper does not propose a method for zero-shot bucket selection (e.g., based on task metadata, prompt characteristics, or unsupervised signal from the model's layer-wise behavior on a few unlabeled examples). This is a clear avenue for future work, especially given the paper's framing of DoLa as broadly applicable without task-specific tuning.

### Limitation 4: DoLa Surfaces Existing Knowledge but Cannot Correct Misinformation Absorbed During Pretraining

**The assumption or constraint.** DoLa operates entirely within the model's existing parameters, contrasting internal representations to amplify factual knowledge that is already encoded. It does not introduce new information, consult external sources, or modify model weights. The paper is explicit about this in Section 6: "Not grounding on external knowledge: Our method relies on the model's internal knowledge without using external retrieval modules. Thus, it cannot correct misinformation acquired during training."

**The consequence.** For any factual question where the model's training data contained systematic misinformation, contradictions, or no coverage at all, DoLa cannot help—and may actually amplify incorrect "knowledge" if the model learned a false association that is encoded in its later layers. The TruthfulQA benchmark specifically targets common misconceptions (e.g., "How long should you wait before filing a missing person report?" where the baseline incorrectly answers "24 hours"), which the model may have learned from imperfect web text. DoLa succeeds on many of these (Table 3 shows it correctly answers "immediately"), but this success is limited to facts where the *correct* information is present in the model's later layers and the *incorrect* information is a surface-level statistical pattern. If both the correct and incorrect information are encoded at similar depths (or if only the incorrect information exists), DoLa has no mechanism to discriminate between them.

This limitation fundamentally bounds DoLa's applicability in high-stakes domains. For medical questions where training data contains outdated or conflicting guidelines, for legal questions where jurisdiction-specific facts are blended in pretraining, or for news events after the model's knowledge cutoff, DoLa provides no safeguard. A retrieval-augmented system could surface up-to-date documents; DoLa cannot.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not directly test DoLa on questions with known training-time misinformation or temporal knowledge cutoff failures. The TruthfulQA dataset implicitly tests some of this (it includes questions designed to probe common human misconceptions that may appear in web text), but the paper does not stratify results by whether the model's error is due to missing knowledge vs. suppressed knowledge. The GPT2-Medium failure (Appendix N) is indirect evidence: small models lack both the correct factual knowledge *and* the layer differentiation, so the failure mode conflates the two. The qualitative examples in Table 3 show DoLa correcting the baseline's errors (Q1: "July 4, 1776" → "August 2, 1776"; Q2: "wait 24 hours" → "report immediately"), but also show a counterexample (Q3: where DoLa provides detailed but incorrect information while the baseline correctly says "I have no comment"). This confirms that DoLa can both correct and introduce errors, depending on what knowledge is present in the model.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation in Section 6 and suggests future work combining DoLa with retrieval augmentation or fine-tuning on factual knowledge bases. However, no combination experiments are conducted. The limitation is framed as a scope constraint—"our method relies on internal knowledge"—rather than a failure mode per se. For practitioners, this means DoLa should be understood as improving the *extraction* of existing knowledge, not the *accuracy* of that knowledge. If the base model systematically believes something false, DoLa may make it state that false belief more confidently.

### Limitation 5: No Systematic Combination with Complementary Methods, Leaving the Upper Bound Unknown

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper evaluates DoLa as a standalone decoding strategy, comparing against baselines (CD, ITI, vanilla decoding) but never combining DoLa with them. It also never combines DoLa with retrieval-augmented generation, fine-tuning, or other hallucination reduction techniques. The authors explicitly acknowledge this gap in Section 6: "the limitations listed above could be potentially addressed through future work combining the corresponding elements with our decoding strategy."

**The consequence.** While the paper demonstrates that DoLa alone provides meaningful improvements (12–17 points on TruthfulQA %Truth∗Info), it leaves unanswered whether these gains are **additive** with other methods or **redundant** with them. If, for example, RLHF training already sharpens the model's factual outputs in later layers, DoLa might provide no additional benefit on top of an RLHF-tuned model—the contrastive signal would already be implicitly incorporated. Conversely, if DoLa's mechanism (surfacing internal knowledge) is orthogonal to retrieval augmentation (adding external knowledge), the combination might yield substantially larger gains than either alone. Without combination experiments, the paper cannot distinguish between "DoLa is a useful component in a broader factuality pipeline" and "DoLa is superseded by stronger methods."

This also limits the paper's practical recommendations. A practitioner reading this paper learns that DoLa improves over vanilla decoding, but does not learn whether they should deploy DoLa *instead of* or *in addition to* retrieval augmentation, RLHF, or ITI. The FLOPs-matched comparison framework that would answer this (e.g., "DoLa on a 13B model vs. retrieval augmentation on a 7B model, at equal inference cost") is absent.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides standalone comparisons against CD and ITI (Table 1), showing DoLa outperforms CD and approaches ITI's performance without training. However, no DoLa+CD or DoLa+ITI experiments are conducted. The paper tests DoLa on top of LLaMA models that have not undergone RLHF (LLaMA is a base pretrained model), so the interaction with alignment techniques is unknown. The appendix explores CD with varied amateur models (Appendix B), improving CD's performance, but still does not combine with DoLa.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly calls for combination experiments in Section 6 as future work. This is a fair acknowledgment, but it means a key practical question—"what is the best factuality strategy given access to all available tools?"—remains unanswered by the current results. A minimal additional experiment would be applying DoLa on top of an ITI-modified model or a retrieval-augmented generation pipeline to test additivity.

---

### Limitation 6: The Adaptive Plausibility Constraint Threshold `α` Is Inherited Without Sensitivity Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** The adaptive plausibility constraint (APC) filters the contrastive distribution to exclude tokens whose mature-layer probability falls below `α` times the maximum probability. The paper sets `α = 0.1` throughout all experiments, following Li et al. (2022) without tuning or ablating the value. APC prevents two failure modes: false positives from noise amplification on very low-probability tokens, and false negatives from suppressing confident predictions that don't change across layers (Section 2.3).

**The consequence.** The choice of `α` directly controls the tradeoff between these two failure modes. A lower `α` (e.g., 0.01) admits more tokens into the contrastive distribution, potentially including genuine factual alternatives that have moderate probability in the mature layer but also admitting noise tokens that receive spuriously high contrastive scores. A higher `α` (e.g., 0.5) restricts the distribution to only the most probable tokens, reducing noise but potentially excluding the very factual alternatives that DoLa is designed to surface—if the correct factual token has probability 0.3 while the top (incorrect) token has probability 0.7, an `α` of 0.5 would exclude the correct token (0.3 < 0.5 × 0.7 = 0.35) and DoLa would have no effect.

Without an ablation of `α`, the reader cannot assess whether the reported gains are robust to this parameter or whether they depend on a specific, potentially brittle value. If the optimal `α` varies by task, model size, or even token position, the current fixed setting may be leaving performance on the table for some tasks while providing suboptimal tradeoffs for others. The paper's qualitative analysis of APC (Section 2.3) explains *why* the constraint is necessary but does not explore *how much* it matters or *what happens* at different thresholds.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No ablation of `α` is provided anywhere in the paper or appendix. The value 0.1 is mentioned once in Section 3.1 ("We set adaptive plausibility constraint (α) to 0.1") and once in the method description (Section 2.3), but no experiments vary it. The paper does report that on TruthfulQA MC, omitting the post-softmax improves performance (Appendix C, Table 6), which interacts with how the contrastive scores are used but is not an `α` ablation.

**Mitigation status.** Not mitigated. The paper inherits the value from prior work without validation. For practitioners deploying DoLa, this means the method includes an untuned hyperparameter that could affect results in unknown ways. A sensitivity analysis across `α ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5}` on at least one task and model size (e.g., TruthfulQA with LLaMA-7B) would establish whether the 0.1 default is near-optimal or whether tuning `α` per task could yield significant additional gains. The fact that the paper carefully validated the bucket choice (via two-fold cross-validation) but did not apply the same rigor to `α` suggests this was an oversight rather than a considered decision.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

DoLa represents a **methodological reframing** rather than a paradigm shift: it demonstrates that hallucinations can be partially addressed by changing *how a model reads out its own internal knowledge* rather than by changing what knowledge it has access to. This is a genuine conceptual contribution because the dominant approaches to factuality when this paper was written—retrieval augmentation (Borgeaud et al., 2022; Izacard et al., 2022) and supervised intervention (Li et al., 2023; Ouyang et al., 2022)—all operate on the assumption that the model needs *external correction*. DoLa shows that for large enough models, the factual knowledge is already present but is being **systematically underweighted** in the final output distribution because surface-level statistical patterns (syntax, collocations, common but wrong answers) dominate the probability mass. The contrastive operation can be understood as a **variance-reduction technique**: it subtracts the common-mode signal shared across layers (plausible-sounding completions) to reveal the differential-mode signal (factual knowledge that emerges only in later layers).

The practical significance of this reframing is that it opens a new category of hallucination interventions that are **zero-cost in terms of training and infrastructure**. DoLa requires no labeled data, no fine-tuning, no retrieval index, and no second model—just a modification to the decoding loop that adds 1–8% latency overhead. For the many deployment scenarios where factuality matters but the resources for RLHF or retrieval pipelines are unavailable (open-source models running on consumer hardware, API-based models where only logit access is provided, models too large to fine-tune), this is a meaningful new capability. The paper's 12–17 point gains on TruthfulQA %Truth∗Info across all four LLaMA sizes (Table 1) establish a concrete baseline for what this category of method can achieve.

The paper also **reconciles a tension in the layer-contrast literature**. Prior work on contrastive decoding between models (Li et al., 2022) showed benefits for fluency and coherence but not factuality. Concurrent work on Autocontrastive Decoding (Gera et al., 2023), which contrasts layers within small models, found that it *increased* hallucinations. DoLa's negative result on GPT2-Medium (Appendix N, Table 17) clarifies the boundary condition: layer contrast helps factuality only when the model is large enough to have developed layer-differentiated knowledge representations. Small models encode facts diffusely; large models concentrate them in upper layers. This resolution is important because it prevents the field from prematurely concluding that "layer contrast doesn't work for factuality" and instead directs attention to *when* and *why* it works. The prediction is clear: as models continue to scale, the knowledge localization property DoLa exploits should become more pronounced, and contrastive decoding methods should become more effective—a hypothesis the paper does not test but which follows directly from its analysis.

The finding that **different tasks require contrast against different depth regimes** (Table 10: TruthfulQA selects the highest layer bucket, while FACTOR/GSM8K/StrategyQA/Vicuna QA select the lowest) introduces a more nuanced understanding of how factual knowledge is accessed during generation. Short-answer factuality tasks, where nearly every output token is fact-critical, benefit from contrasting against the very top layers where factual disambiguation occurs. Long-form tasks, where most output tokens are structural, benefit from contrasting against very early layers to provide a mild, uniform boost without destabilizing the syntactic skeleton. This task-type dependence was not predicted by prior interpretability work (which established *that* knowledge is localized but not *how* to use that fact during generation) and provides a practical decision rule for practitioners: match the contrast depth to the density of factual content in the output.

However, the paper does **not** shift the fundamental ceiling on factuality. DoLa surfaces existing knowledge; it cannot correct misinformation absorbed during pretraining, fill gaps in the model's knowledge, or reason about facts the model never learned. The paper is explicit about this (Section 6: "it cannot correct misinformation acquired during training"), and the boundary is empirically visible in the hard-cap on TruthfulQA performance (even with DoLa, %Truth∗Info peaks at ~49% for the 65B model—far from 100%). In this sense, DoLa changes the *efficiency* of knowledge extraction but not the *quantity* of knowledge available, which remains bounded by pretraining. This distinguishes it from retrieval-augmented methods, which can in principle access knowledge beyond the model's training data, and from fine-tuning methods, which can in principle correct false associations. The practical implication is that DoLa is best understood as a **complementary method**—a free improvement to stack on top of other interventions rather than a replacement for them.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Automated zero-shot bucket selection via task metadata or prompt analysis.** The most immediate practical limitation of DoLa is the need for a labeled validation set to select the candidate layer bucket. The paper shows that bucket choice is task-dependent (highest layers for short-answer factuality, lowest for long-form generation) but provides no method for choosing a bucket without validation data. A follow-up could train a lightweight classifier to predict the optimal bucket from task metadata (average output length, presence of factual keywords in the prompt, task type taxonomy) or from the model's own layer-wise JSD statistics on a few unlabeled examples. The specific experiment: collect layer-bucket validation results across 20–50 diverse tasks, extract features from the task description and from DoLa's JSD pattern on 10–20 unlabeled examples, and train a bucket predictor. The metric would be whether zero-shot bucket selection achieves ≥90% of the validation-optimal bucket's performance. Given the paper's finding that two-fold validation consistently selects the same bucket within each task type (Table 10), this may be a relatively easy classification problem.

**Systematic scale-law analysis of knowledge localization and DoLa effectiveness.** The paper tests four scales within the LLaMA family (7B→65B) and one small model (GPT2-335M), but does not characterize how DoLa's benefits scale with parameter count. The observation that GPT2-335M fails while LLaMA-7B succeeds leaves a large untested gap (models in the 1B–6B range) where the knowledge localization threshold likely lies. A follow-up study using a single architecture family across a dense scale sweep (e.g., OPT models at 125M, 350M, 1.3B, 2.7B, 6.7B, 13B, 30B, 66B, or the Pythia suite) could map out the scaling curve of DoLa's effectiveness and identify where the layer-differentiation threshold emerges. The key measurements would be: (1) the relationship between model size and inter-layer JSD variance on factual vs. non-factual tokens (a direct measure of knowledge localization), and (2) the relationship between this variance and DoLa's factuality gains on TruthfulQA and FACTOR. The prediction from the paper is a sigmoidal curve—no benefit below some critical size, then rapid improvement as layer differentiation emerges, then saturation as most factual knowledge becomes localizable. This would both validate the paper's mechanistic explanation and provide practical guidance for the minimum model size at which DoLa is worth deploying.

**Layer-contrast on top of retrieval-augmented generation and RLHF-tuned models.** The paper explicitly calls for combining DoLa with complementary methods (Section 6) but conducts no combination experiments. Two specific combinations are high-priority: (1) DoLa on top of a retrieval-augmented model (e.g., LLaMA with retrieved documents prepended to the prompt), testing whether layer contrast provides additional gains when external knowledge is already available, and (2) DoLa on top of an RLHF-trained model (e.g., LLaMA-2-Chat or Vicuna), testing whether alignment training already implicitly performs something similar to layer contrast (by sharpening the model's factual outputs) and thus reduces or eliminates DoLa's benefit. The experiment design is straightforward: apply DoLa to LLaMA-2-Chat at multiple scales on TruthfulQA and FACTOR, compare against vanilla decoding and against the base LLaMA-2 + DoLa results. If RLHF already surface factual knowledge effectively, DoLa's gains on chat models should be substantially smaller than on base models. If the gains are additive, it confirms orthogonal mechanisms.

**DoLa as a diagnostic tool for detecting knowledge suppression during decoding.** The paper's central claim is that DoLa works by amplifying factual knowledge that exists in later layers but is suppressed in the final output distribution. This mechanism can be repurposed as a **probing tool**: for any given prompt and generated output, the difference between the contrastive distribution `p̂` and the standard final-layer distribution `q_N` indicates *which tokens* had their probabilities substantially altered by DoLa's contrastive operation. Tokens with large positive differences are candidates for "suppressed knowledge"—facts the model knows (high probability in later layers) but doesn't surface under standard decoding (low probability in the final distribution because surface patterns dominate). A follow-up study could use DoLa as an offline hallucination detector: for generated text, compute the KL divergence between `q_N` and `p̂` at each token position; tokens with high divergence are flagged as potentially hallucinated under standard decoding. The evaluation would compare this detector against existing hallucination detection methods (e.g., SelfCheckGPT; Manakul et al., 2023) on a benchmark like FactCC or a synthetic hallucination dataset. This would extend DoLa from a decoding strategy to a diagnostic tool, broadening its utility beyond generation-time intervention.

**Layer-level intervention via direct activation manipulation rather than output contrast.** DoLa operates at the output distribution level—it contrasts the probabilities from two layers after the vocabulary projection. An alternative approach suggested by the knowledge localization literature (Meng et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2022) is to intervene directly on the hidden state representations in the upper layers, amplifying the directions in activation space that encode factual knowledge. Specifically: using the same JSD-based dynamic layer selection, identify which layer shows the maximum divergence from the final layer, then add a scaled version of the *difference* in hidden states between that layer and the final layer to the final hidden state before the vocabulary projection. This would be a representation-space analog of DoLa's probability-space contrast. The experiment would compare DoLa against this activation-intervention variant on TruthfulQA and FACTOR, measuring both factuality and fluency. If activation-space intervention matches or exceeds DoLa's gains, it would suggest that the factual signal is accessible as a direction in representation space, opening the door to more fine-grained control (e.g., scaling the factual signal by a learned coefficient rather than the binary contrast operation).

**Cross-architecture replication to test the generality of the knowledge localization hypothesis.** The paper tests LLaMA (standard decoder-only transformer) and MPT-7B (also decoder-only, with a different training recipe). The claim that DoLa works because knowledge is localized to later layers in large transformers predicts that it should work on any sufficiently large transformer architecture, but may fail or require adaptation for non-standard architectures (encoder-decoder models, mixture-of-experts models, models with shared layer weights, models with different normalization schemes). A systematic replication across architectures—T5 (encoder-decoder), Mixtral (MoE), and a vision-language model—would test the scope condition. For encoder-decoder models, the question is whether knowledge localization occurs in the decoder's layers (DoLa would apply as-is) or in the cross-attention from the encoder (requiring a modified contrast operation). For MoE models, the question is whether sparse expert routing disrupts the smooth layer-wise knowledge evolution that DoLa depends on. Negative results on some architectures would refine the theory of *why* layer contrast works, moving it from an empirical observation about LLaMA to a principled claim about transformer depth hierarchies.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Factuality improvement for open-source LLM deployments in resource-constrained settings.** The most direct application is improving the factual reliability of open-source models running on consumer hardware or in small-organization deployments where RLHF and retrieval augmentation are infeasible. A team deploying LLaMA-7B for customer support QA can add DoLa decoding with approximately 6% latency overhead and 1.1% GPU memory overhead (Tables 2 and 9), gaining roughly +6.6 points on TruthfulQA MC1 and +14 points on TruthfulQA %Truth∗Info without any additional infrastructure. The implementation is a modification to the generation loop—no model weights change, no additional dependencies. The validation burden is light: test 2–4 bucket configurations on a few hundred in-domain validation examples, select the best bucket, deploy. For applications where factual accuracy directly impacts user trust (educational Q&A, technical support, medical triage where incorrect answers have high costs), this is a high-ROI change.

**Improving data quality for self-supervised fine-tuning pipelines.** When LLMs are used to generate training data for themselves or for smaller models (as in distillation, synthetic data generation, or self-improvement loops like STaR/ReST), the factuality of the generated data is critical—errors propagate through subsequent training rounds. Applying DoLa during the generation phase of such pipelines can improve the factual quality of the synthetic data at near-zero additional cost. For example, when using LLaMA-65B to generate answers to factual questions for a distillation dataset, switching from standard greedy decoding to DoLa decoding would boost truthfulness from ~50% to ~54% (TruthfulQA %Truth) and reduce the "I have no comment" rejection rate from 19.1% to 4.8% (Table 1), producing both more factual and more informative training examples. The paper's finding that DoLa reduces the refusal rate (which is a known problem in synthetic data generation where models learn to hedge) is particularly valuable here—it increases both the quality and the yield of the generation process.

**Cost-efficient factuality improvement for API-based models where only logit access is available.** Many commercial LLM APIs provide access to output logits or token probabilities but do not allow fine-tuning or architecture modification. DoLa can be applied as a post-processing step on the received logits if the API provider exposes the vocabulary projection at intermediate layers—or, for APIs that only expose final-layer logits and token probabilities, a proxy early-exit distribution could be approximated by running a smaller open-source model on the same prompt and treating its output as the "premature" distribution in a CD-style setup, combined with DoLa's JSD-based selection logic to choose which smaller model layer to contrast against. While this is not the exact DoLa algorithm (it requires a second model, moving back toward CD), the paper's framework provides the rationale for *which* layer of the smaller model to use as the contrast target. The practical setup: receive final-layer logits from the proprietary model, compute early-exit distributions from a smaller open-source model on the same prompt, use JSD to select the best early layer from the small model, contrast, and decode. The paper's CD experiments (Table 1, Appendix B) show that naive CD with LLaMA-7B as amateur underperforms, but the JSD-based layer selection logic from DoLa could be adapted to select a better amateur layer—a testable hypothesis.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not present DoLa as part of an explicit tradeoff decision against named alternatives with well-defined switching conditions. It positions DoLa as a complementary, zero-cost improvement that can be applied on top of any pretrained transformer LLM, rather than as a replacement for retrieval augmentation, RLHF, or ITI. The paper's explicit statement in Section 6—"the limitations listed above could be potentially addressed through future work combining the corresponding elements with our decoding strategy"—frames DoLa as additive, not competitive. The practical decision is therefore **whether to apply DoLa in addition to existing factuality measures**, not whether to choose DoLa instead of them. No conditional preference matrix is warranted based on the paper's own positioning.
