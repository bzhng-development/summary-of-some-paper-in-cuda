# DelTA: Discriminative Token Credit Assignment for Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards

**ArXiv:** [2605.21467](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21467)

## 🎯 Pitch

Standard RLVR updates dilute discriminative signals by averaging over all token-gradient vectors, letting frequent but uninformative patterns like formatting tokens dominate the update direction. DelTA instead reweights each token based on how much it differentiates high-reward from low-reward responses, sharpening the implicit discriminator that decides which token probabilities rise or fall. This token-level credit assignment yields gains of 2.6–3.3 average points on math benchmarks over the strongest same-scale baselines.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **DelTA** (**D**iscriminative signal-guided **T**oken Credit **A**ssignment), a method that reweights token-level terms in the RLVR surrogate to make policy-gradient updates more discriminative. Training on DeepMath-103K with Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen3-14B-Base, DelTA reshapes the side-wise centroids that implicitly act as a linear discriminator over token-gradient vectors—amplifying directions that distinguish high-reward from low-reward responses while downweighting shared patterns like formatting tokens. On seven mathematical benchmarks, DelTA outperforms the strongest same-scale baselines by 3.26 and 2.62 average points on the 8B and 14B backbones respectively, with additional gains on code generation, a different model family, and out-of-domain evaluations, establishing that token-level discriminative reweighting improves sequence-level RLVR only when the reweighting compares each token-gradient vector against both the positive- and negative-side centroids rather than relying on within-side centrality alone.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Sequence-Level Rewards Don't Tell You Which Tokens Matter

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is a **granularity mismatch** at the heart of reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR). When training an LLM to solve math problems, the reward signal is typically binary and sequence-level: the model gets a 1 if the final answer is correct, and a 0 otherwise. But the policy gradient update that optimizes the model operates at the **token level**—each token in the generated response contributes a gradient term to the parameter update. The question is: given that every token in a correct response shares the same positive reward, and every token in an incorrect response shares the same negative reward, **which tokens actually drive the learning, and why?**

This is not a purely academic question. Recent empirical work—cited by the authors in Section 1—has revealed a striking pattern: RLVR induces **sparse, targeted shifts** in the model's token-level output distributions. Only a small fraction of token positions undergo substantial probability changes during training, while most remain nearly unchanged (Meng et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026). This sparsity suggests that RLVR contains an **implicit token selection mechanism**—something is determining which token probabilities get increased and which get decreased—but that mechanism is not directly specified by the reward signal. The reward only says "this whole answer was good" or "this whole answer was bad." It provides no per-token guidance.

This gap has direct practical consequences. If the implicit token selection is suboptimal—for example, if the update expends gradient mass on tokens that don't actually distinguish good reasoning from bad reasoning—then training is inefficient. The model may learn to reinforce surface-level patterns (formatting, common phrases) rather than the reasoning steps that actually lead to correct answers. Understanding and improving this implicit selection is therefore central to making RLVR more sample-efficient and more capable of producing genuine reasoning improvements.

### Why This Matters: The Stakes of Token-Level Credit Assignment

The importance of this problem extends in several directions:

**Training efficiency.** Modern RLVR training runs are enormously expensive, often requiring thousands of GPU-hours. If a significant fraction of each gradient update is "wasted" on tokens that don't discriminate between good and bad reasoning, then improving token-level credit assignment directly translates to faster convergence, higher final performance, or both—without requiring more training data, larger models, or additional reward signals.

**Reasoning quality and length.** Figure 2 in the paper reveals a concrete symptom of suboptimal credit assignment: under standard DAPO training, response lengths shrink and entropy rises over time, while reward plateaus. This is consistent with a scenario where the policy update reinforces shared, low-information tokens (formatting, boilerplate) at the expense of the diverse, high-information tokens that characterize genuine reasoning. A better credit assignment mechanism could sustain long, coherent reasoning chains rather than collapsing to shorter, higher-entropy outputs.

**Interpretability.** The paper's discriminator view provides a **causal explanation** for which token probabilities change during training. This is valuable beyond performance gains: it gives researchers a lens for diagnosing *why* RLVR works, when it fails, and how different design choices (clipping, normalization, advantage estimation) shape the learning dynamics. Currently, most RLVR methods are designed and tuned empirically; a principled understanding of the token-level update mechanism could guide more systematic method development.

**Foundation for future methods.** If the paper's central claim is correct—that the RLVR update acts as an implicit linear discriminator whose quality depends on how well the side-wise centroids separate positive from negative token gradients—then this opens a design space. Any technique that improves the discriminative quality of these centroids (better token representations, more sophisticated reweighting, learned projections) could further improve RLVR. The discriminator view is thus not just an analysis tool but a **generative principle** for new algorithms.

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies and critiques several existing strategies for addressing the token-level credit assignment problem:

**Uniform token weighting is the dominant default.** Methods like DAPO (Yu et al., 2025) apply the same sequence-level advantage to every token in a response, normalizing only by the total token count. Similarly, GRPO normalizes by response length first, then averages over responses. Both approaches treat all tokens as equally responsible for the outcome. From the discriminator view, this means the positive- and negative-side centroids are simple advantage-weighted means of *all* token-gradient vectors—including ones that are shared across both good and bad responses (formatting tokens, problem restatements, common phrases). The authors show formally (Section 3.1, Appendix D) that these centroids minimize within-side squared distance—they are optimal summaries of each side independently. But a summary that is good for describing one side is not necessarily good for *distinguishing* it from the other side. This is a classic insight from discriminative modeling (Cohen et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2024; Khosla et al., 2020): Fisher's linear discriminant, for example, explicitly optimizes between-class separation, not within-class compactness. RLVR, as currently practiced, uses the equivalent of class-conditional means—which can be pulled toward shared background structure.

**Entropy-based token filtering (forking tokens).** DAPO with forking tokens (Wang et al., 2025) keeps only high-entropy tokens in the policy-gradient loss, based on the intuition that low-entropy tokens (formatting, stop words) contribute little useful gradient signal. This is a step toward selective token weighting, but it has a key limitation: *entropy is not discriminative*. A token can have high entropy (the model is uncertain about it) but still be equally likely in both good and bad responses—in which case filtering on entropy alone doesn't help separate positive from negative gradients. Conversely, a token can have low entropy (the model is confident) but be strongly diagnostic of correct reasoning. The discriminator view suggests that what matters is not how uncertain the model is about a token, but whether that token's gradient vector systematically differs between high-reward and low-reward responses.

**Future-influence weighting (FIPO).** FIPO (Ma et al., 2026) reweights tokens by a discounted estimate of how much each token influences the subsequent trajectory—essentially, a forward-looking importance score. This addresses a different question: *which tokens are causally important for the outcome?* rather than *which tokens distinguish good from bad outcomes?* A token can have high future influence (it strongly shapes what comes next) but still be equally present in correct and incorrect trajectories (e.g., a step that says "Let's think about this carefully"). Conversely, a token can have low future influence (it's near the end of the response and doesn't shape much subsequent text) but be highly discriminative (e.g., the final answer token). The discriminator view targets the latter case explicitly.

**Process reward models and learned value functions.** Another line of work (Cui et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b) trains separate models to assign dense, per-step rewards or value estimates, which then provide token-level or step-level supervision. This is effective but fundamentally different in approach: it requires additional model training, careful data curation (often with human annotations or Monte Carlo rollouts), and the resulting dense rewards may themselves be imperfect. DelTA is complementary—it operates entirely within the sequence-level reward regime, using no additional supervision, auxiliary models, or human labels. Instead, it extracts discriminative token-level information *from the structure of the RLVR update itself*.

**Token-level reweighting without discriminative comparison.** Some prior work reweights tokens based on their similarity to a single reference direction (e.g., Kazemnejad et al., 2025). The authors explicitly test this in Section 5.1 (Q1 analysis): they construct a variant that assigns larger weights to tokens closer to their *own-side* centroid, without comparing to the opposite side. This variant performs **worse** than both DelTA and the DAPO baseline (Table 2). The result is revealing: simply upweighting tokens that are "typical" of good responses doesn't help, because those typical tokens may include shared formatting or boilerplate that also appears in bad responses. The opposite-side comparison is essential—what matters is whether a token gradient is *relatively* more characteristic of its own side.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions DelTA as occupying a distinct niche in the credit assignment landscape: **discriminative reweighting derived from the RLVR update itself, without external signals**. The key intellectual move is the **discriminator view** (Section 3.1): the realization that a policy-gradient update vector ∆θ can be interpreted not just as a movement in parameter space, but as an implicit linear classifier in token-gradient space. Specifically, for any candidate token with gradient vector v, the update increases its log-probability if v aligns more with the positive-side centroid ¯µ+ than with the negative-side centroid ¯µ−, and decreases it otherwise. The quality of this discriminator depends on how well ¯µ+ and ¯µ− separate positive- from negative-side token gradients.

This view reframes the credit assignment problem: rather than asking "which tokens caused the reward?" (a causal attribution question), it asks "which token-gradient directions best separate the two sides?" (a discriminative feature selection question). The paper argues that this shift in perspective directly suggests an algorithmic improvement: reshape the centroids to be more discriminative by upweighting token-gradient vectors that are side-characteristic and downweighting those that are shared.

Notably, the discriminator view is not specific to DAPO. Appendix E shows that several representative RLVR variants (GRPO, DAPO with forking tokens, FIPO) can all be expressed as modifications to the effective token weights ρ_(i,t) used in the centroid construction. GRPO changes per-token weights inversely with response length; forking tokens zeroes out low-entropy tokens; FIPO multiplies by future-influence estimates. In each case, the method's update direction is determined by which tokens contribute to the positive and negative centroids. DelTA's contribution is to make this weighting explicitly discriminative—tokens contribute more when their gradient vectors are more characteristic of their own side than of the opposite side.

The paper also positions the discriminator view as a **purely local, first-order analysis**—it examines the update direction at the current parameter θ_old, assuming clipping is locally inactive (a reasonable assumption at the start of each optimization epoch when importance ratios are near 1). This is explicitly not a claim about the full nonlinear training trajectory. Rather, it is presented as a design principle: if we can shape the local update direction to be more discriminative, the resulting trajectory should produce better policies. The experiments then test whether this principle holds in practice under full clipped RLVR training.

Finally, the paper positions DelTA as **lightweight and compatible** with existing RLVR infrastructure. The token coefficients λ_(i,t) are computed as stop-gradient quantities once per rollout batch, the weighted surrogate uses the same asymmetric clipping as DAPO, and the only additional hyperparameters are the coefficient range [λ_min, λ_max] and the number of refinement iterations K (set to [0.8, 1.2] and K=1 in all main experiments). The computational overhead is modest (approximately 10% additional forward passes for coefficient estimation, per Appendix L.1), and the method requires no changes to the reward model, data pipeline, or optimizer. This positions DelTA not as a wholesale replacement for existing RLVR methods, but as a drop-in modification to the surrogate objective that reshapes the effective update direction.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops **DelTA**, a method that reweights individual token contributions inside the standard RLVR training objective so that the resulting gradient update better separates tokens characteristic of correct reasoning from tokens characteristic of incorrect reasoning. The system solves the problem that sequence-level rewards give every token in a response the same credit or blame, which causes the gradient update to be diluted by shared, non-discriminative tokens—DelTA fixes this by estimating per-token coefficients that amplify directions distinguishing high-reward from low-reward responses and downweight directions that appear similarly in both.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has five major components connected in a pipeline that runs once per rollout batch during RLVR training:

1. **Base LLM Policy** (`π_θ`)—the autoregressive language model being trained (e.g., Qwen3-8B-Base). It generates groups of responses given math prompts, and its parameters are updated by the RLVR objective.

2. **Token-Gradient Proxy Extractor**—for every generated token in the rollout batch, computes a lightweight vector representation that approximates the token's full-parameter gradient. Uses only the final-layer LM head hidden states and output probabilities (not the full gradient) to keep computation feasible.

3. **Centroid Initializer**—splits token-gradient vectors by whether their response had positive or negative advantage, then computes advantage-weighted centroids (mean vectors) for each side. These centroids define the initial "reference directions" for what a typical good-response token and bad-response token look like in gradient space.

4. **Centroid Refinement Loop** (K iterations, default K=1)—alternates between (a) computing a discriminative score for each token based on how much closer its gradient vector is to its own side's centroid vs. the opposite side's centroid, and (b) recomputing each centroid as a score-weighted average, giving more influence to tokens with high discriminative scores. This loop is stop-gradient: no parameters are updated.

5. **Self-Normalized Weighted DAPO Surrogate**—takes the final discriminative scores, maps them to bounded coefficients λ_(i,t) in [0.8, 1.2], normalizes them so the average coefficient is 1, and uses them to reweight each token's contribution in the standard DAPO clipped objective. The LLM parameters are then updated using this reweighted objective.

Information flows as follows: a prompt batch enters → the current policy generates responses → rewards are computed and group-normalized to produce advantages → token-gradient proxies are extracted from the policy's final layer → advantage-weighted centroids are initialized from all tokens → the refinement loop produces discriminative scores → scores are mapped to coefficients → coefficients reweight the DAPO surrogate → the optimizer updates the policy parameters. The coefficients are computed fresh for each rollout batch and held fixed during optimization over that batch.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the discriminator view of RLVR updates—deriving the local update equation, showing how it induces an implicit linear discriminator over token-gradient vectors, and identifying the side-wise centroids as the objects that determine which token probabilities increase or decrease. This is the conceptual foundation that motivates everything else.

- **Second**, the limitation of uniform aggregation—why simple advantage-weighted centroids can be pulled toward shared, high-frequency token directions (formatting, boilerplate) that don't distinguish good from bad reasoning, diluting the discriminator's ability to separate the two sides.

- **Third**, the DelTA coefficient estimation procedure—how discriminative scores are computed from the contrast between positive and negative centroids, why the entropy-regularized assignment with opposite-side comparison is essential (and not own-side centrality alone), and the alternating refinement loop that sharpens the centroids.

- **Fourth**, the self-normalized weighted surrogate—how the discriminative scores are mapped to bounded coefficients, how the coefficients reweight the DAPO objective, and how the normalization preserves the effective learning rate while reshaping the update direction.

- **Fifth**, the token-gradient proxy—how the full-parameter token-gradient vector is approximated for computational feasibility, what the approximation captures and what it loses, and why it's sufficient for the coefficient estimation task.

- **Sixth**, the implementation details—adaptive temperatures, lagged statistics, numerical stability, and the connection to Fisher's linear discriminant as an interpretive lens.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily an **algorithm design paper** whose core idea is that the policy-gradient update in sequence-level RLVR can be viewed as an implicit linear discriminator over token-gradient vectors, and that reshaping this discriminator to be more contrastive—by reweighting token contributions based on their side-characteristicness—produces better policy updates.

#### The Discriminator View: RLVR Updates as Implicit Token-Gradient Classification

The starting point is a first-order Taylor expansion: if we perturb the model parameters by a small update vector `Δθ`, the change in log-probability of any candidate token `x` under context `c` is approximately the inner product between that token's gradient vector and the parameter update.

Formally, for any generation context `c` and candidate next token `x`:

$$\Delta \log \pi(x \mid c) := \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}} + \Delta\theta}(x \mid c) - \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(x \mid c) \approx \left( \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(x \mid c) \big|_{\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}} \right)^\top \Delta\theta$$

where `Δlog π(x|c)` is the (approximate) change in the model's log-probability for token `x` after the update, `∇_θ log π_θ(x|c)` is the token-gradient vector—a vector in parameter space indicating how each parameter affects the log-probability of token `x`—and `Δθ` is the actual parameter update applied by RLVR.

**What it computes:** the local first-order approximation to how much the model's probability of emitting token `x` in context `c` will change after a parameter update. If the inner product is positive, the probability increases; if negative, it decreases. The magnitude of the change is proportional to the alignment between the token's gradient direction and the update direction.

**Why this form:** the first-order Taylor expansion is the standard tool for analyzing local behavior of smooth functions. It is exact in the limit of infinitesimal updates and provides a good approximation for small-but-finite gradient steps. The paper uses it not to claim exactness of the full training trajectory, but as an analytical lens: if we can understand and improve what the local update does to token probabilities, we can design better objectives whose full training trajectories produce better policies.

Now we need `Δθ`—the parameter update induced by the RLVR objective. The paper uses DAPO as the concrete example. Let `{o_i}` be a group of `G` sampled responses, each consisting of tokens `o_{i,1}, ..., o_{i,|o_i|}`. Let `R_i` be the sequence-level reward, and let `Â_i` be the group-normalized advantage:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{R_i - \mu_R}{\sigma_R + \epsilon_A}$$

where `μ_R` and `σ_R` are the mean and standard deviation of rewards within the group, and `ε_A > 0` is a small constant preventing division by zero.

Around `θ_old`, the importance ratio `r_{i,t}(θ_old) = 1` for all tokens, so clipping is locally inactive. The local policy-gradient update from the DAPO surrogate is therefore:

$$\Delta\theta_{\text{RLVR}} \propto \sum_{i=1}^{G} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_i v_{i,t}$$

where `v_{i,t} = ∇_θ log π_θ(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})|θ=θ_old` is the token-gradient vector for the `t`-th token of response `i`—a vector in parameter space with the same dimensionality as the model.

**What it computes:** an advantage-weighted sum of all token-gradient vectors in the rollout batch. Positive-advantage tokens pull the parameters in one direction (encouraging those tokens); negative-advantage tokens pull in the opposite direction (suppressing those tokens). The proportionality omits the learning rate and the global normalizer `1/(∑|o_i|)`, which scale the update uniformly without changing its direction.

**Why this form:** this is the standard policy-gradient theorem applied to the unclipped DAPO surrogate at the point where importance ratios are 1. Derivation provided in Appendix C: the gradient of the DAPO surrogate with respect to `θ` at `θ=θ_old` is exactly this sum divided by the token count, because `∇_θ r_{i,t}(θ)|θ=θ_old = ∇_θ log π_θ(o_{i,t})|θ=θ_old = v_{i,t}`. The derivation confirms that at the start of each optimization epoch (before any parameter update within that epoch), the clipped surrogate gradient equals the unclipped gradient, so the analysis captures the actual update direction applied by the optimizer.

The crucial move is to **separate this sum by the sign of the advantage**:

$$\Delta\theta_{\text{RLVR}} \propto \sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_i v_{i,t} - \sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} |\hat{A}_i| v_{i,t}$$

**What it computes:** the positive-advantage aggregate (sum of token-gradient vectors from correct or high-reward responses, weighted by advantage magnitude) minus the negative-advantage aggregate (sum from incorrect or low-reward responses). Responses with zero advantage contribute nothing.

**Why this form:** it reveals that the update direction is the difference of two aggregates, one from each side. This is the same structure as a linear classifier learned by comparing class-conditional means. The sign and magnitude of the update at each parameter dimension are determined by whether that dimension is more strongly associated with positive-advantage tokens or negative-advantage tokens.

Now define the total masses and normalized centroids:

$$M_+ = \sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_i, \quad M_- = \sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} |\hat{A}_i|$$

$$\bar{\mu}_+ = \frac{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_i v_{i,t}}{M_+}, \quad \bar{\mu}_- = \frac{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} |\hat{A}_i| v_{i,t}}{M_-}$$

where `M_+` is the total positive advantage mass (sum of all positive advantages, weighting each token by its response's advantage), `M_-` is the total negative advantage mass, `\bar{μ}_+` is the positive-side centroid (a vector in parameter space representing the average token-gradient direction from correct responses), and `\bar{μ}_-` is the negative-side centroid.

**What it computes:** `\bar{μ}_+` is the average of all token-gradient vectors from positive-advantage responses, weighted by how positive each response's advantage is (more correct responses get proportionally more weight). `\bar{μ}_-` is the analogous weighted average for negative-advantage responses. These are the "prototypical" gradient directions for good and bad tokens.

**Why this form:** Appendix D proves that `\bar{μ}_+` is exactly the minimizer of the weighted sum of squared Euclidean distances to all positive-side token-gradient vectors: `\bar{μ}_+ = argmin_μ ∑_{i:Â_i>0} ∑_t Â_i ‖v_{i,t} - μ‖²₂`. So the centroid is the optimal within-side summary under squared-error loss—the single vector that best represents all positive-advantage token gradients. `\bar{μ}_-` has the same property for the negative side.

Substituting this decomposition back into the update gives:

$$\Delta\theta_{\text{RLVR}} \propto M_+ \bar{\mu}_+ - M_- \bar{\mu}_-$$

**What it computes:** the update direction as the difference between two scaled reference vectors. The masses `M_+` and `M_-` control the overall strength, while `\bar{μ}_+` and `\bar{μ}_-` control the direction.

**Why this form:** it cleanly separates the update into two factors: the total "pull" from each side (masses) and the direction of that pull (centroids). Modifying either factor changes the update. In particular, reshaping the centroids (changing *which* tokens contribute most to defining `\bar{μ}_+` and `\bar{μ}_-`) changes the update direction without necessarily changing the total mass.

Now the key insight: plugging the decomposed update back into the Taylor expansion yields the **discriminator view**:

$$\Delta\log \pi(x \mid c) \propto M_+ \cdot \left( \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(x \mid c) \right)^\top \bar{\mu}_+ - M_- \cdot \left( \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(x \mid c) \right)^\top \bar{\mu}_-$$

**What it computes:** the local change in token `x`'s log-probability as the difference between two scores: (1) how well `x`'s token-gradient vector aligns with the positive centroid (`v^⊤ \bar{μ}_+`), scaled by the positive mass `M_+`, and (2) how well it aligns with the negative centroid (`v^⊤ \bar{μ}_-`), scaled by `M_-`. The token probability increases if the positive-side score exceeds the negative-side score, and decreases otherwise.

**Why this is called a discriminator:** for any pair of token-gradient vectors `v` and `v'`, the update direction `Δθ` implicitly classifies them: `v` gets its probability increased if `M_+ v^⊤ \bar{μ}_+ > M_- v^⊤ \bar{μ}_-`. This is exactly a linear decision rule with the decision boundary defined by the plane orthogonal to `Δθ` in token-gradient space. The update direction `Δθ` itself serves as the weight vector of this implicit classifier. The authors formalize this: "In parameter space, it is a policy-update direction; in token-gradient space, it acts as an implicit linear discriminator over candidate token-gradient vectors. This discriminator is not explicitly parameterized or separately trained; it is induced by the policy-gradient update itself."

This duality is the intellectual engine of the paper. Since `Δθ` induces a discriminator, and the quality of that discriminator depends on how well `\bar{μ}_+` and `\bar{μ}_-` separate the two sides, we can improve the update by **redesigning how we compute the centroids** to be more discriminative. This is the reverse-design principle: "rather than asking whether the update direction is a good summarizer of each side, we ask whether it is a good discriminator between the two sides."

#### The Limitation: Within-Side Summarization ≠ Between-Side Discrimination

The centroids `\bar{μ}_+` and `\bar{μ}_-` are optimal within-side summaries (minimizing within-side squared distance), but this is the wrong objective for their role in the discriminator. The paper articulates this as a classic insight from the discriminative modeling literature: "good within-side summaries are not necessarily good between-side discriminators" (citing Cohen et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2024; Khosla et al., 2020).

Concretely, consider what happens when positive- and negative-advantage responses share substantial common structure. In mathematical reasoning, both correct and incorrect responses include:

- **Formatting tokens**: "Let's think step by step", "We need to compute", "Therefore", newlines, LaTeX delimiters.
- **Problem-specific entities**: numbers, variable names, the problem statement itself.
- **Common reasoning patterns**: "First, we note that...", "By the Pythagorean theorem...", even when the subsequent application is wrong.

These shared tokens appear frequently on both sides. Their token-gradient vectors will be similar across correct and incorrect responses because they occur in similar contexts with similar model probabilities. When we compute `\bar{μ}_+` and `\bar{μ}_-` as advantage-weighted averages over *all* tokens, these shared high-frequency directions dominate the sum—there are many of them, and they contribute large aggregated gradient mass. The result: both centroids get **pulled toward the same background directions**, making them less discriminative.

The consequence for the discriminator is severe: a candidate token `x` whose gradient vector is similar to these shared directions will score similarly against both centroids, meaning its probability won't change much. Meanwhile, tokens that *are* genuinely discriminative—for example, a correct algebraic manipulation vs. an incorrect one—may be rare (appearing in only a few responses) and their gradient directions get diluted in the aggregate. The discriminator ends up "overemphasizing task-agnostic commonalities and undermining sparse directions that better distinguish higher- from lower-reward responses."

This analysis explains several empirical observations:

- **Training plateaus**: if the update direction is pulled toward shared background structure, the model learns to reinforce formatting and common phrases rather than the reasoning steps that discriminate correct from incorrect solutions. The reward plateaus (Figure 2, left) while response length shrinks and entropy rises (Figure 2, middle and right)—consistent with the model collapsing to high-probability boilerplate.

- **The forking-tokens limitation**: filtering by entropy (Wang et al., 2025) removes low-entropy tokens like formatting, but doesn't distinguish between high-entropy tokens that are discriminative (rare correct reasoning steps) and high-entropy tokens that are shared uncertainty (the model is equally confused about a variable name in both correct and incorrect responses). Entropy measures the model's uncertainty, not the token's discriminative value.

- **The failure of own-side centrality**: if we simply upweight tokens close to their own-side centroid (the "within-side only" variant in Section 5.1), we actually *amplify* the problem, because tokens closest to the centroid are likely to be the shared, high-frequency ones that define the background direction. Table 2 confirms this variant performs worse than even the uniform DAPO baseline.

The paper's diagnosis is clear: the centroids need to be **contrastive**—they should emphasize token-gradient directions that are characteristic of their own side relative to the opposite side, and downweight directions that are common to both. This is exactly what DelTA does.

#### DelTA Coefficient Estimation: Discriminative Scores from Centroid Contrast

DelTA estimates a discriminative score `α_{i,t} ∈ [0,1]` for each token `(i, t)` in the rollout batch. The score is high when the token's gradient vector `v_{i,t}` is much closer to its own side's centroid than to the opposite side's centroid, and low when the distances are similar (i.e., when the token gradient is shared or non-discriminative).

The computation proceeds in three phases: initialization, alternating refinement, and final coefficient mapping.

##### Initialization: Advantage-Weighted Centroids

The positive and negative centroids are initialized exactly as in standard DAPO—from the original advantage-weighted token-gradient means:

$$\mu_+^{(0)} = \frac{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_i v_{i,t}}{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_i}, \quad \mu_-^{(0)} = \frac{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} |\hat{A}_i| v_{i,t}}{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} |\hat{A}_i|}$$

where superscript `(0)` denotes iteration 0, `v_{i,t}` is the token-gradient proxy vector (approximating the full-parameter gradient, detailed later), and denominators are clamped by a small `ε > 0` for numerical stability when a side has zero mass (which can happen in degenerate rollout groups).

**What it computes:** the exact same centroids that the standard DAPO update would use to define its update direction. DelTA starts from the standard centroids and refines them.

**Why this initialization:** it ensures DelTA's initial discriminator is identical to the standard RLVR discriminator. The refinement then sharpens it; if refinement fails or is degenerate, DelTA gracefully falls back to the standard update.

##### Squared-Distance Margins: Quantifying Side-Characteristics

For each token `(i, t)` at refinement iteration `k`, define the **squared-distance margin**:

$$\Delta_{i,t}^{+,(k)} = \|v_{i,t} - \mu_-^{(k)}\|_2^2 - \|v_{i,t} - \mu_+^{(k)}\|_2^2, \quad \hat{A}_i > 0$$

$$\Delta_{i,t}^{-,(k)} = \|v_{i,t} - \mu_+^{(k)}\|_2^2 - \|v_{i,t} - \mu_-^{(k)}\|_2^2, \quad \hat{A}_i < 0$$

where `‖v - μ‖²₂` is the squared Euclidean distance between the token-gradient vector `v` and the centroid `μ` in parameter space, `Δ⁺` is the margin for positive-side tokens (positive when `v_{i,t}` is closer to `μ_+` than to `μ_-`), and `Δ⁻` is the symmetric margin for negative-side tokens.

**What it computes:** for a token from a correct response, `Δ⁺` measures how much closer its gradient vector is to the positive centroid than to the negative centroid. A large positive value means the token gradient is much more characteristic of correct responses; a value near zero or negative means it's equally close to both centroids or closer to the wrong side. For incorrect-response tokens, `Δ⁻` is the analogous measure.

**Why squared Euclidean distance:** Appendix G shows an important algebraic equivalence:

$$\|v - \mu_-\|_2^2 - \|v - \mu_+\|_2^2 = 2v^\top(\mu_+ - \mu_-) + \|\mu_-\|_2^2 - \|\mu_+\|_2^2$$

So the squared-distance margin is (up to a centroid-dependent constant offset) proportional to the inner product `v^⊤(μ_+ - μ_-)`—the alignment of `v` with the centroid contrast direction `μ_+ - μ_-`. This is exactly the linear score that the discriminator view uses to classify token-gradient vectors. Using squared Euclidean distances is therefore consistent with the inner-product discriminator while also connecting naturally to the least-squares geometry of centroid construction (Appendix D proves centroids minimize within-side squared distances).

##### Adaptive Side-Specific Temperatures

Before computing scores, DelTA estimates a temperature `γ_+^{(0)}` and `γ_-^{(0)}` for each side from the empirical distribution of squared-distance margins under the initial centroids:

$$\gamma_+^{(0)} = \sqrt{\max\left(\text{Var}\left(\{\Delta_{i,t}^{+,(0)} : \hat{A}_i > 0\}\right), \varepsilon_\gamma\right)}$$

where `Var(·)` is the sample variance, `{Δ⁺}` is the set of all positive-side margin values in the batch, and `ε_γ > 0` is a small constant ensuring the temperature is always positive (preventing division by zero in the sigmoid). `γ_-^{(0)}` is defined symmetrically from the negative-side margins.

**What it computes:** the temperature is the standard deviation of the squared-distance margins within each side. It adapts to the scale of the margin distribution—when token-gradient vectors are widely dispersed in their distances to the centroids, the temperature is large; when they are tightly clustered, it's small.

**Why adaptive:** a fixed temperature would need to be tuned per model scale, training stage, and dataset. Using the empirical standard deviation makes the sigmoid assignment self-calibrating: the temperature automatically normalizes the margins to a unitless scale where differences are measured relative to the spread of the batch. This means tokens that are "one standard deviation more characteristic of their own side" get the same sigmoid score across different batches, even as the absolute margin magnitudes change during training. The square root converts variance to standard deviation, matching the natural scale of the margin values.

##### Soft Assignment via Entropy-Regularized Optimization

For fixed centroids `μ_+^{(k)}`, `μ_-^{(k)}` and temperature `γ_+^{(k)}`, the discriminative score `α_{i,t}^{(k)}` for a positive-side token is defined as the solution to:

$$\alpha_{i,t}^{(k)} = \arg\max_{\alpha \in [0,1]} \left[ \alpha \cdot \Delta_{i,t}^{+,(k)} + \gamma_+^{(k)} \cdot h(\alpha) \right], \quad \hat{A}_i > 0$$

where `h(α) = -α log α - (1-α) log(1-α)` is the binary entropy function, and `γ_+^{(k)}` is the side-specific temperature controlling the softness of the assignment.

**What it computes:** the optimization balances two terms. The first term `α · Δ⁺` encourages the score `α` to be large when the margin is positive (token is closer to its own centroid) and small when the margin is negative. The second term `γ · h(α)` is an entropy regularizer that penalizes extreme values of `α`: at `α=0` and `α=1`, the entropy is 0 (no penalty); at `α=0.5`, the entropy is maximum (log 2, the largest penalty). The temperature `γ` controls the tradeoff—small `γ` makes the assignment nearly hard (α close to 0 or 1), while large `γ` pushes α toward 0.5 (uncertain).

**Why this form:** the entropy regularizer serves two purposes. First, it prevents the assignment from being too brittle: if a token's margin is only slightly positive, hard assignment would give it full weight (α=1) while a nearly identical token with a slightly negative margin would get zero weight (α=0), creating a discontinuity that could destabilize the centroid refinement. The entropy smooths this, giving tokens with near-zero margins scores near 0.5 regardless of sign—they contribute roughly equally to both centroids. Second, it provides a principled probabilistic interpretation: the solution is a logistic function, meaning the score can be interpreted as the model's confidence that the token-gradient vector is more characteristic of its own side, under a logistic noise model with scale `γ`.

The closed-form solution (derived in Appendix G) is:

$$\alpha_{i,t}^{(k)} = \sigma\left( \frac{\Delta_{i,t}^{+,(k)}}{\gamma_+^{(k)}} \right) = \sigma\left( \frac{\|v_{i,t} - \mu_-^{(k)}\|_2^2 - \|v_{i,t} - \mu_+^{(k)}\|_2^2}{\gamma_+^{(k)}} \right), \quad \hat{A}_i > 0$$

and symmetrically for negative-side tokens:

$$\alpha_{i,t}^{(k)} = \sigma\left( \frac{\|v_{i,t} - \mu_+^{(k)}\|_2^2 - \|v_{i,t} - \mu_-^{(k)}\|_2^2}{\gamma_-^{(k)}} \right), \quad \hat{A}_i < 0$$

where `σ(z) = 1/(1 + exp(-z))` is the sigmoid function.

**What it computes:** for a positive-side token, `α_{i,t}` is the sigmoid of the standardized margin—the larger the margin (token is more characteristic of correct responses), the closer `α` is to 1. For a margin of zero (token is equidistant from both centroids), `α = σ(0) = 0.5`. For a negative margin (token is actually closer to the wrong centroid), `α < 0.5`. For negative-side tokens, the sign convention is flipped so that `α` is still high when the token is characteristic of its own (negative) side.

**Why the sigmoid:** Appendix G proves this is the unique maximizer of the entropy-regularized objective. Derivation: the objective `f(α) = αΔ + γ h(α)` is strictly concave on (0,1) because `f''(α) = -γ/(α(1-α)) < 0` for all `α ∈ (0,1)` and `γ > 0`. Setting the derivative to zero, `f'(α) = Δ + γ log((1-α)/α) = 0`, yields `α/(1-α) = exp(Δ/γ)`, which rearranges to the sigmoid form. The solution is unique and global because of strict concavity.

##### Centroid Refinement: Score-Weighted Re-Averaging

Given the discriminative scores `α_{i,t}^{(k)}`, DelTA recomputes the centroids as **score-weighted within-side averages**:

$$\mu_+^{(k+1)} = \frac{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_i \alpha_{i,t}^{(k)} v_{i,t}}{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \hat{A}_i \alpha_{i,t}^{(k)}}, \quad \mu_-^{(k+1)} = \frac{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} |\hat{A}_i| \alpha_{i,t}^{(k)} v_{i,t}}{\sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} |\hat{A}_i| \alpha_{i,t}^{(k)}}$$

**What it computes:** the new centroid for each side is still an average of token-gradient vectors from that side, but now each token `(i, t)` is weighted by `Â_i · α_{i,t}` instead of just `Â_i`. Tokens with `α` near 1 (highly characteristic of their own side) contribute their full advantage-weighted gradient to the centroid. Tokens with `α` near 0.5 (shared or ambiguous) contribute at half strength. Tokens with `α` near 0 (more characteristic of the opposite side, despite being on this side—which can happen for tokens that are actually errors within a correct response) contribute almost nothing.

**Why this refinement:** it directly implements the design principle: make the centroids more contrastive by emphasizing token-gradient directions that distinguish the two sides. The `α` weights are discriminative by construction—a token gets high weight only if its gradient vector is substantially closer to its own centroid than to the opposite centroid. After re-averaging, the new centroids will be pulled toward the discriminative directions and away from the shared background. The refinement is **not** trained or optimized; it is a fixed-point-style update that sharpens the centroids toward the tokens that best characterize each side.

The refinement also updates the temperatures in a **lagged** manner: during the pass that computes `α_{i,t}^{(k)}`, DelTA also accumulates the empirical variances of the new margins `Δ_{i,t}^{+,(k)}` and `Δ_{i,t}^{-,(k)}`, computes new temperatures `γ_+^{(k+1)}` and `γ_-^{(k+1)}`, and caches them for the next iteration's score computation. This ensures that at each iteration, the temperature reflects the current scale of the margins, adapting as the centroids shift.

The paper experiments with `K = 1, 2, 3` refinement iterations (Table 11). `K = 1` gives the best average performance; `K = 2` and `K = 3` degrade, likely because excessive refinement overfits to the batch-level token-gradient geometry—the centroids become too specific to the current batch's noise rather than capturing stable discriminative directions. The default is therefore `K = 1`: initialize, compute scores once, re-average once.

##### Final Coefficient Mapping

After the `K` refinement iterations, DelTA does one final pass: compute the squared-distance margins using the refined centroids `μ_+^{(K)}` and `μ_-^{(K)}` and the latest cached temperatures `γ_+^{(K)}` and `γ_-^{(K)}`, producing final raw scores:

$$\alpha_{i,t}^{\star} = \sigma\left( \frac{\|v_{i,t} - \mu_-^{(K)}\|_2^2 - \|v_{i,t} - \mu_+^{(K)}\|_2^2}{\gamma_+^{(K)}} \right), \quad \hat{A}_i > 0$$

with the symmetric form for negative-side tokens.

These raw scores are then mapped to **bounded coefficients**:

$$\lambda_{i,t} = \lambda_{\min} + (\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}) \cdot \alpha_{i,t}^{\star}$$

where `λ_min` and `λ_max` are hyperparameters controlling the range. The paper uses `[λ_min, λ_max] = [0.8, 1.2]` in all main experiments. For zero-advantage responses (which don't belong to either side), `λ_{i,t} = λ_min`.

**What it computes:** a linear remapping of `α` from `[0, 1]` to `[λ_min, λ_max]`. A token with `α = 1` (maximally discriminative for its side) gets coefficient `λ_max = 1.2` (20% above average). A token with `α = 0.5` (non-discriminative, equidistant from both centroids) gets coefficient `(λ_min + λ_max)/2 = 1.0` (average). A token with `α = 0` (actually more characteristic of the opposite side) gets coefficient `λ_min = 0.8` (20% below average). Zero-advantage tokens get `λ_min`.

**Why bounded remapping:** directly using the raw sigmoid scores `α ∈ (0,1)` as coefficients would create extreme reweighting—tokens with scores near 0 would contribute almost nothing, and the effective token count would vary dramatically across batches. The bounded range `[0.8, 1.2]` ensures that all tokens still contribute to the update (no token is completely zeroed out), while still providing a meaningful relative ranking: the most discriminative tokens get 1.2/0.8 = 1.5× the weight of the least discriminative tokens. The ablation in Table 3 shows that removing the range mapping (`w/o range map`, using raw `α` directly) reduces the average score from 23.27 to 20.78, confirming that bounded coefficients are more stable.

#### The Self-Normalized Weighted Surrogate

The final step is to use the coefficients `λ_{i,t}` to reweight the DAPO surrogate objective. The standard DAPO objective (with token-count normalization) is:

$$J_{\text{DAPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^G |o_i|} \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \min\left( r_{i,t}(\theta) \hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}) \hat{A}_i \right) \right]$$

DelTA replaces the uniform `1/∑|o_i|` normalizer with a self-normalized weighted version:

$$J_{\text{DelTA}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \lambda_{i,t}} \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \lambda_{i,t} \cdot \min\left( r_{i,t}(\theta) \hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}) \hat{A}_i \right) \right]$$

where `λ_{i,t}` are the stop-gradient coefficients, `r_{i,t}(θ) = π_θ(o_{i,t}|q, o_{i,<t})/π_{θ_old}(o_{i,t}|q, o_{i,<t})` is the importance ratio, `Â_i` is the group-normalized advantage, and the clip parameters `ε_low = 0.2`, `ε_high = 0.28` follow the DAPO paper (asymmetric clipping, lower bound more permissive than upper bound).

**What it computes (implementation form):** in practice, the authors implement this equivalently by defining normalized coefficients `\bar{λ}_{i,t} = λ_{i,t} · N / Z`, where `N = ∑|o_i|` is the total token count and `Z = ∑ λ_{i,t}` is the sum of coefficients. Then `∑ \bar{λ}_{i,t} / N = 1`, so the normalized coefficients can be used as multipliers under the standard DAPO token-count normalizer:

$$J_{\text{DelTA}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \bar{\lambda}_{i,t} \cdot \min\left( r_{i,t}(\theta) \hat{A}_i, \text{clip}(r_{i,t}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon_{\text{low}}, 1 + \epsilon_{\text{high}}) \hat{A}_i \right) \right]$$

**What it computes:** each token `(i, t)` contributes `\bar{λ}_{i,t} · [clipped advantage-weighted ratio]` to the loss, and the sum is divided by `N` as in standard DAPO. Since `∑ \bar{λ}_{i,t} = N`, tokens with `\bar{λ} > 1` (discriminative) contribute more than average, and tokens with `\bar{λ} < 1` (shared/non-discriminative) contribute less.

**Why self-normalization:** dividing by `∑ λ_{i,t}` rather than `∑|o_i|` ensures that the overall scale of the loss is invariant to the `λ` distribution—if all `λ` were doubled, the normalized coefficients `\bar{λ}` would remain unchanged. This prevents the bounded range `[0.8, 1.2]` from changing the effective learning rate relative to DAPO. The normalization changes only the **relative** weighting of tokens within the batch, not the total gradient magnitude. The ablation in Table 3 (`w/o λ-norm`) confirms this is important: removing the self-normalization (using `λ` in the numerator but dividing by `∑|o_i|` as in DAPO) reduces the average score from 23.27 to 21.39.

**Why this reshapes the update direction (local analysis):** around `θ_old` where clipping is inactive, the gradient of the DelTA surrogate is:

$$\nabla_\theta J_{\text{DelTA}}(\theta)\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}} \propto \sum_{i=1}^G \sum_{t=1}^{|o_i|} \bar{\lambda}_{i,t} \hat{A}_i v_{i,t} = \sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_t \bar{\lambda}_{i,t} \hat{A}_i v_{i,t} - \sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_t \bar{\lambda}_{i,t} |\hat{A}_i| v_{i,t}$$

Comparing to the standard DAPO update (where `\bar{λ}_{i,t} = 1` for all tokens), the effective positive and negative centroids become:

$$\mu_+^{\text{eff}} \propto \sum_{i: \hat{A}_i > 0} \sum_t \bar{\lambda}_{i,t} \hat{A}_i v_{i,t}, \quad \mu_-^{\text{eff}} \propto \sum_{i: \hat{A}_i < 0} \sum_t \bar{\lambda}_{i,t} |\hat{A}_i| v_{i,t}$$

**What it computes (effective centroids):** the effective centroids are now weighted by `\bar{λ}` in addition to `Â`. Tokens with high discriminative scores (gradients characteristic of their side) get `\bar{λ} > 1`, amplifying their influence on the centroid direction. Tokens with low scores (shared background) get `\bar{λ} < 1`, reducing their influence. The result: the effective positive centroid is pulled toward token-gradient directions that are not just present in correct responses, but **relatively more present** in correct responses than in incorrect ones. The effective negative centroid is pulled toward the complementary directions.

**Why this is "reshaping the discriminator":** the induced discriminator from the DelTA update will have reference directions `μ_+^{\text{eff}}` and `μ_-^{\text{eff}}` that are more contrastive—the positive centroid emphasizes what makes correct tokens *different* from incorrect ones, not just what makes them *typical*. A candidate token's probability increases only if its gradient aligns more with the contrastive positive direction than with the contrastive negative direction, which is a stricter and more discriminative test than alignment with the simple average.

The coefficients `λ_{i,t}` are **stop-gradient** quantities: they are computed once from the rollout batch using the current `θ_old`, held fixed across all optimization epochs over that batch, and recomputed only when new trajectories are sampled. No gradients flow through the coefficient computation, so the refinement loop and the sigmoid assignment do not add any terms to the policy gradient beyond the reweighting of the existing DAPO loss terms.

#### The Token-Gradient Proxy: Approximating Full-Parameter Gradients at Scale

Computing the full-parameter token-gradient vector `v_{i,t} = ∇_θ log π_θ(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})` for every token in a rollout batch is prohibitive: it requires backpropagating through the entire model for each token position, which at LLM scale (billions of parameters, thousands of tokens per batch) would multiply the training cost many times over.

DelTA instead uses a **layer-restricted proxy** based on the standard linear classification view of the LM head. Let `h_t ∈ ℝ^d` be the final hidden state at step `t` (output of the last transformer layer), `W ∈ ℝ^{|V|×d}` be the LM head weight matrix mapping from hidden states to vocabulary logits, and `z_t = W h_t` be the logits with softmax probabilities `p_t = softmax(z_t)`. For the actually-generated token `y_t = o_{i,t}`, the token log-probability is:

$$\log p_t(y_t) = W_{y_t}^\top h_t - \log \sum_j \exp(W_j^\top h_t)$$

where `W_{y_t}` is the `y_t`-th row of `W`, corresponding to the token `y_t`.

**What it computes:** the log-probability of the generated token, decomposed into a "score" for the correct token (`W_{y_t}^⊤ h_t`) minus the log-sum-exp of scores for all tokens (the normalizer).

Differentiating with respect to the LM head row `W_{y_t}` gives:

$$\nabla_{W_{y_t}} \log p_t(y_t) = (1 - p_t(y_t)) \cdot h_t$$

**What it computes:** the gradient of the token's log-probability with respect to the output embedding for that token is the final hidden state scaled by `(1 - p_t(y_t))`, the model's current "error" for that token prediction. If the model is very confident (`p_t(y_t) ≈ 1`), the gradient is near zero—the token is already high-probability and requires little adjustment. If the model is uncertain (`p_t(y_t) ≈ 0`), the gradient is approximately `h_t`—a strong signal to increase this token's embedding toward the hidden state direction.

DelTA uses `(1 - p_t(y_t)) · h_t` as the token-gradient proxy vector `v_{i,t}`. This is a vector of dimension `d` (the hidden state dimension, e.g., 4096 for Qwen3-8B), not the full parameter count. It represents the token's gradient restricted to the LM head output embedding row.

**Why this proxy:** the full-parameter gradient `∇_θ log π_θ(y_t)` has components for all layers—attention weights, MLP weights, layer norms, embeddings—but the LM head row is the **direct interface** between the hidden representation and the output probability. Under a frozen-representation approximation (assuming hidden states don't change when we adjust just the output embeddings), this proxy captures the first-order effect of the token gradient. Prior work on gradient-based influence functions (Pruthi et al., 2020) and data influence (Yeh et al., 2022) has used similar layer-restricted approximations when full-parameter computation is infeasible. The paper acknowledges this is an approximation: "This restriction should therefore be understood as an approximation, not an exact reconstruction of the full gradient signal."

**What the proxy captures vs. what it loses:** the proxy captures (a) the direction in hidden-state space that the model would need to adjust to increase this token's probability, and (b) the magnitude of that adjustment (scaled by the model's current error). It loses information about how earlier layers contribute to the gradient through the chain rule—for example, adjusting attention patterns or MLP activations to make the hidden state `h_t` itself more favorable for the target token. However, for the purpose of **comparing token-gradient vectors** (which is what DelTA does—it checks whether `v_{i,t}` is closer to `μ_+` or `μ_-`), the proxy needs to capture relative similarities, not exact magnitudes. If two tokens require similar adjustments in the output embedding space, their proxy vectors will be similar; if they require different adjustments, their proxy vectors will differ. The discriminative signal that DelTA exploits is in these relative similarities.

**Proxy ablations (Appendix F, Table 4):** the paper tests an alternative proxy using the top-K hidden-gradient: `\tilde{v}_{i,t} = W_{y_t} - ∑_{j∈K_t} \tilde{p}_t(j) W_j`, where `K_t` is the set of top-K vocabulary indices by current probability, `\tilde{p}_t` is the softmax renormalized within `K_t`, and `W_j` is the `j`-th row of `W`. This proxy computes the gradient of log `p_t(y_t)` with respect to the hidden state `h_t` (not the LM head row), approximated over the top-K competing tokens. This variant explicitly incorporates the competing high-probability tokens, which the default proxy captures only through the scalar `(1 - p_t(y_t))`.

The top-K proxy achieves **better** performance than the default: average score 24.29 vs. 23.27 across four benchmarks (Table 4). This suggests that incorporating local competition among top vocabulary candidates provides a stronger discriminative signal—tokens that are similarly embedded to many high-probability alternatives look different from tokens with few competitors, and this difference helps separate positive from negative gradient directions. However, the paper keeps the default proxy in main experiments because it is the "minimal and most direct layer-restricted instantiation"; the stronger top-K variant shows DelTA can benefit from richer proxies without changing the underlying mechanism.

A **random-coefficient baseline** (Appendix F, Table 4) is also tested: `λ_{i,t}` sampled randomly from `[λ_min, λ_max]`, with the same normalization and weighted DAPO objective. This achieves an average score of 18.34, substantially below both the default DelTA (23.27) and the DAPO baseline (19.05 per Table 2). This confirms that the benefit comes from the **structure** of the coefficients (discriminative ranking), not from arbitrary reweighting or stochastic perturbation of the loss.

#### Implementation Pipeline: Step-by-Step Per Batch

The complete DelTA computation for a single rollout batch proceeds as follows (Appendix H provides full details):

**Step 1: Generate rollout batch.** Sample `G` responses per prompt from the current policy `π_{θ_old}`. Compute sequence-level rewards `R_i` (binary: 1 if final answer correct, 0 otherwise, verified by `math-verify`). Compute group-normalized advantages `Â_i = (R_i - μ_R)/(σ_R + ε_A)`.

**Step 2: Extract token-gradient proxies.** For each valid response token `(i, t)`, compute the final hidden state `h_{i,t}` and the output probability `p_{i,t}(o_{i,t})` from the forward pass (already done for log-probability computation). Form the proxy vector `v_{i,t} = (1 - p_{i,t}(o_{i,t})) · h_{i,t}`.

**Step 3: Initialize centroids and temperatures.** Compute `μ_+^{(0)}` and `μ_-^{(0)}` as advantage-weighted averages of proxy vectors, skipping groups where one side has zero mass. Compute initial temperatures `γ_+^{(0)}` and `γ_-^{(0)}` as the standard deviations of the squared-distance margins under these centroids.

**Step 4: Refinement loop (K iterations, default K=1).** For `k = 0, ..., K-1`:
- 4a. Compute discriminative scores `α_{i,t}^{(k)}` using the sigmoid formula with current centroids and cached temperatures.
- 4b. Recompute centroids `μ_+^{(k+1)}` and `μ_-^{(k+1)}` as `α`-weighted within-side averages.
- 4c. Accumulate squared-distance margin statistics under the new centroids and cache the updated temperatures `γ_+^{(k+1)}` and `γ_-^{(k+1)}` for the next iteration. The temperature update is **lagged**: temperatures used in iteration `k` were computed from the margin statistics at iteration `k-1`.

**Step 5: Final coefficient computation.** Compute final squared-distance margins using the refined centroids `μ_+^{(K)}`, `μ_-^{(K)}` and latest temperatures `γ_+^{(K)}`, `γ_-^{(K)}`. Compute raw scores `α_{i,t}^⋆`, map to `[λ_min, λ_max]`, normalize to `\bar{λ}_{i,t}`, and use as multipliers in the standard DAPO token-level loss.

**Step 6: Optimize.** Run multiple epochs of gradient descent on the weighted DAPO surrogate with the fixed coefficients `\bar{λ}`. On the next rollout batch, resample from the updated policy and recompute coefficients from scratch.

**Computational cost:** With `K=1`, the coefficient estimation requires `K+2 = 3` additional actor forward passes (one for initial temperature scale, one for the single refinement iteration, one for final coefficient computation). These passes recompute hidden states from the rollout batch; they do not require backpropagation through the model. The paper measures this overhead at approximately 10.2% of total first-step wall-clock time (37 seconds out of ~363 seconds on 8×NVIDIA B200 GPUs). After the first training step, response lengths diverge between DelTA and DAPO (Figure 2 shows DelTA maintains longer responses), making later-step comparisons confounded. The 10.2% figure represents the pure coefficient estimation overhead.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications: A Summary

- **Entropy-regularized assignment with opposite-side comparison** over hard assignment or own-side centrality: the entropy regularizer prevents brittle discontinuities and provides a probabilistic interpretation; the opposite-side comparison ensures scores reflect discriminative value (Section 5.1 shows own-side centrality alone is worse than DAPO).

- **Squared Euclidean distance** as the margin metric: algebraically equivalent to the inner product with the centroid contrast direction `μ_+ - μ_-`, which is the natural score for the linear discriminator view. Consistent with least-squares centroid geometry (Appendix D).

- **Adaptive side-specific temperatures** from margin standard deviations: self-calibrates the sigmoid across different model scales, training stages, and batch compositions. Avoids per-setting temperature tuning. Ablation in Table 3 (`w/o adaptive γ`) shows degradation from 23.27 to 21.19 when fixed temperatures are used.

- **Bounded coefficient range `[0.8, 1.2]`** over raw scores or wider ranges: prevents extreme reweighting that could destabilize training, while preserving discriminative ranking. Ablation (`w/o range map`) shows raw `α` scores reduce average from 23.27 to 20.78. Sensitivity analysis (Table 11) shows robustness to range choices (`[0.5, 1.2]`, `[0.8, 1.5]`, `[0.5, 1.5]` all perform similarly).

- **Self-normalization by coefficient sum** over token-count normalization: keeps the effective learning rate invariant to the `λ` distribution. Ablation (`w/o λ-norm`) shows the DAPO normalizer `1/∑|o_i|` reduces performance from 23.27 to 21.39.

- **One refinement iteration (K=1)** over multiple: Table 11 shows `K=2` and `K=3` degrade (average 22.15 and 22.20 vs. 23.27), likely due to overfitting the batch-level token-gradient geometry. One iteration provides the essential discriminative sharpening without over-adapting to noise.

- **Stop-gradient coefficients** computed once per rollout batch: no gradients flow through the sigmoid, the centroid refinement, or the temperature computation. The only trainable parameters are the policy weights updated by the reweighted DAPO loss. This avoids complex meta-learning dynamics and keeps training stable.

- **Layer-restricted LM-head proxy** over full-parameter gradients: makes computation feasible at LLM scale (billions of parameters). The proxy captures the first-order discriminative signal in output embedding space; Appendix F shows DelTA is robust to proxy choice and can benefit from richer proxies (top-K hidden-gradient variant improves further).

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: RLVR Updates as an Implicit Linear Discriminator — A New Analytical Lens for Token-Level Credit Assignment

The paper's most fundamental contribution is not an algorithm but a **conceptual reframing**: showing that the policy-gradient update in sequence-level RLVR can be understood as an implicit linear classifier operating in token-gradient space. Prior work treated the RLVR update as a black-box parameter movement — improvements in token-level credit assignment came from heuristics (entropy filtering, future-influence weighting) or from training auxiliary dense reward models. DelTA's discriminator view provides something genuinely new: a **principled diagnostic for why** a sequence-level update increases or decreases particular token probabilities, expressed through a simple geometric relationship between the update direction `Δθ` and the positive/negative side-wise centroids in token-gradient space.

What makes this distinctive is that it **converts a correlation into a causation**: the paper shows not just *that* some tokens experience large probability changes and others don't (an empirical observation by Meng et al., 2026 and Ma et al., 2026), but *why* — because the RLVR update direction acts as a weight vector scoring each candidate token by its alignment with the positive-minus-negative centroid contrast. A token's probability increases when its gradient vector aligns more with `μ_+` than with `μ_-`, and decreases otherwise. This is a **first-principles explanation** for the sparse token-level shifts that prior work could only document empirically.

This is a **fundamental conceptual advance** rather than an incremental refinement. Before this paper, the dominant framework for reasoning about token credit in RLVR was causal attribution ("which tokens caused the reward?"), which naturally led to solutions like learned value functions or future-influence estimates. DelTA's discriminator view replaces this with a discriminative-feature-selection framework ("which token-gradient directions best separate the two sides?"), which is computationally lighter and geometrically cleaner. It is also **method-agnostic**: Appendix E demonstrates that GRPO, DAPO, forking tokens, and FIPO can all be expressed as modifications to the effective token weights in the centroid construction. This unifies seemingly disparate techniques under a single geometric analysis, making the landscape of RLVR credit assignment methods much more interpretable and suggesting a common design principle (reshape the centroids) that none of the prior methods explicitly targeted.

The evidence for this innovation is primarily analytical and structural rather than a single benchmark number: the discriminator view is derived, not discovered, from the first-order Taylor expansion of the DAPO objective (Section 3.1, Appendix C). Its explanatory power is validated indirectly by the failure of the "within-side only" variant (Table 2), which confirms that the discriminator's quality depends specifically on between-side contrast — if the centroids were merely within-side summaries, own-side centrality would suffice. It doesn't. The discriminator view *predicts* this failure, which is stronger evidence than post-hoc rationalization.

---

### Innovation 2: Identifying the Within-Side Summary vs. Between-Side Discrimination Mismatch as the Bottleneck

The paper's second foundational contribution is diagnosing **exactly why** uniform token aggregation degrades the RLVR update. The centroid construction in standard DAPO (and GRPO, and related methods) minimizes within-side squared distance — it finds the single vector that best summarizes all token gradients on a given side. But the discriminator induced by the update uses these centroids for a fundamentally different purpose: separating positive-side and negative-side token gradients. The paper explicitly invokes a classic insight from the discriminative learning literature (Fisher's linear discriminant, contrastive learning) to argue that within-class compactness and between-class separation are distinct and often conflicting objectives. In RLVR, the former objective (implicitly optimized by uniform aggregation) can actively undermine the latter by allowing shared, high-frequency token directions to dominate both centroids and wash out the contrast.

This is a **fundamental diagnostic move** that changes how someone thinks about RLVR failures. Before this paper, the field knew that RLVR training sometimes plateaus or degrades (e.g., response length collapse, entropy increase), but attributing this to the centroid construction is a new causal claim. The paper makes the case explicit: formatting tokens, problem-restatement phrases, and common reasoning patterns appear in both correct and incorrect responses, so their gradient vectors pull `μ_+` and `μ_-` toward the same background direction. The effective discriminator ends up emphasizing "task-agnostic commonalities" rather than the sparse reasoning-specific directions that actually separate good from bad solutions.

The empirical signature of this mismatch is clearly visible in Figure 2. Under DAPO, reward plateaus while response length shrinks and entropy rises — consistent with the update direction being pulled toward low-information shared tokens (formatting, boilerplate) at the expense of diverse reasoning content. DelTA's training dynamics diverge: longer responses are sustained with lower entropy and higher reward, exactly what one would predict if discriminative reweighting preserves reasoning-specific gradient signals that the standard update dilutes.

This diagnosis is **complementary to, not competing with**, prior explanations for RLVR training challenges. Entropy-based filtering (Wang et al., 2025) identified that low-entropy tokens dominate the update, but entropy is not discriminative — a low-entropy formatting token can appear equally in both correct and incorrect responses. DelTA's diagnosis explains *why* filtering low-entropy tokens only partially helps: it removes the most obvious background signals but doesn't actively promote discriminative ones. Future-influence weighting (Ma et al., 2026) addresses a different question altogether (causal importance, not discriminative value). The paper thus provides a **unifying diagnostic**: the centroid mismatch is a structural limitation of sequence-level RLVR that existing methods mitigate only partially and implicitly, and which DelTA addresses directly and explicitly.

The evidence is strongest in the Q1 analysis (Section 5.1, Table 2): a variant that upweights tokens close to their own centroid (within-side centrality) performs *worse* than both DelTA and the uniform DAPO baseline. This is precisely the failure mode the diagnosis predicts — own-side centrality amplifies the very shared directions that make the centroids poor discriminators. The Q2 analysis (Section 5.2, Figures 3–4) provides convergent evidence: training on the top 50% of tokens ranked by DelTA coefficients outperforms full-token DAPO (even with half the gradient terms), while training on the bottom 50% causes complete collapse. This shows that the coefficients meaningfully separate high-value from harmful token-gradient directions, consistent with the diagnosis that some token contributions actively weaken the discriminator.

---

### Innovation 3: The Discriminative Token Coefficient as a Self-Supervised Selection Signal (Not a Learned Reward)

DelTA's third contribution is demonstrating that **discriminative token-level information can be extracted entirely from the structure of the RLVR update itself,** without external dense rewards, auxiliary models, or human annotations. The coefficients `λ_{i,t}` are computed from the contrast between the positive and negative centroids — objects that are already implicitly present in the standard RLVR update (they are the advantage-weighted token-gradient aggregates). DelTA merely makes this implicit discriminative structure explicit by sharpening the centroids, and then feeds it back into the surrogate objective through reweighting.

This is **conceptually distinct** from all major prior approaches to token-level credit assignment in RLVR. Process reward models (e.g., Cui et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b) train separate models to predict per-step correctness, requiring additional data, model capacity, and training infrastructure. Future-influence models (FIPO, Ma et al., 2026) estimate discounted downstream impacts, incurring additional forward passes and hyperparameter choices. Entropy-based filtering (Wang et al., 2025) uses a heuristic statistic of the current policy. DelTA's coefficients, by contrast, come from **no additional supervision signal** — they are derived from the same rollout batch and the same advantages that drive the RLVR update. The information is already there; DelTA just reorganizes it for discriminative purposes.

The significance of this goes beyond performance. It establishes that **sequence-level rewards contain implicit discriminative structure** that can be surfaced without densifying the reward signal. This is a counterpoint to the prevailing assumption in the process reward model literature that sequence-level supervision is fundamentally insufficient for learning fine-grained token-level behaviors. DelTA shows that, at the level of the gradient update, the contrast between high-reward and low-reward responses already encodes which token directions are discriminative — it's just that this structure is diluted by uniform aggregation. The method thus operates in a **computationally minimal regime**: the coefficients are stop-gradient, computed once per batch, and require no trainable parameters beyond the policy itself. The overhead is ~10% additional forward passes (Appendix L.1), and the method integrates directly with existing DAPO training pipelines.

The Q2 analysis (Figures 3–4) is the strongest piece of evidence for this innovation. By using `λ_{i,t}` solely for hard token selection (training only on the top 50% of tokens), the paper isolates the ranking quality of the coefficients from any continuous reweighting effect. The fact that top-λ training outperforms both full DAPO and random selection — and that bottom-λ training collapses — confirms that the coefficients identify token-gradient directions with genuine learning value, using no information beyond what the standard RLVR update already contains. The proxy ablation (Appendix F, Table 4) further supports the self-supervised nature: the coefficients work with a minimal LM-head proxy, improve with a richer top-K proxy, and fail under random assignment. The ranking signal is real and derives from the structure of the policy's own gradient space.

---

### Innovation 4: A Principled Empirical Demarcation of When and Why Token-Level Reweighting Works

The paper's fourth contribution is less about DelTA specifically and more about the **boundary conditions** it establishes for token-level credit assignment. Through its ablation design, the paper provides unusually clear evidence about *what kind* of token reweighting works and *what kind doesn't,* generating a set of negative and positive results that together form a principled understanding.

The Q1 analysis (Section 5.1) proves that **own-side centrality is insufficient and can be harmful**. This is not obvious a priori — one could reasonably argue that tokens more typical of correct responses should be upweighted. The fact that within-side weighting underperforms the uniform DAPO baseline is a strong negative result that rules out a whole class of token-reweighting methods.

The Q2 analysis (Section 5.2) demonstrates that **the bottom-ranked tokens actively harm learning**. Training on only the bottom 50% causes a steep collapse (Figure 3, red curve), far worse than random selection. This means the coefficients aren't just identifying "less useful" tokens — they're identifying tokens whose gradient directions, when included in the update, drag the policy in the wrong direction. This is a sharper claim than "some tokens are uninformative"; it suggests that the standard RLVR update contains gradient components that are *actively counterproductive*, consistent with the diagnosis that shared background directions dilute the discriminator.

The Q3 ablation (Table 3) decomposes DelTA's design into five components and shows each contributes — but with a clear hierarchy. The largest drop comes from removing centroid refinement (`w/o refinement`, 23.27 → 19.97), indicating that one-shot coefficients from the initial centroids are insufficient and that the iterative sharpening is the mechanism's core. Removing the range mapping (`w/o range map`, → 20.78) and removing the entropy regularizer (`w/o h(α)`, → 20.93) both cause substantial degradation, confirming that bounded soft coefficients are more stable than raw scores or hard assignments. Removing adaptive temperatures (`w/o adaptive γ`, → 21.19) and removing coefficient-mass normalization (`w/o λ-norm`, → 21.39) show smaller but consistent drops, indicating that scale adaptation and normalization are useful stabilizers.

This set of results is **more informative than a single ablation study** because it maps the design space: which components are essential (refinement, soft bounded assignment) vs. which are helpful stabilizers (adaptive temperatures, normalization). The sensitivity analysis (Table 11) further shows that DelTA is robust to its hyperparameters (`λ_min`, `λ_max`, `K`) within reasonable ranges, with only the number of refinement iterations (`K`) showing a clear pattern (more iterations hurt). This provides practical guidance for practitioners and researchers extending the method.

The negative result on `K` (Table 11) is itself insightful: excessive refinement overfits the batch-level token-gradient geometry. This is a concrete instance of a general tension in discriminative methods — sharper discrimination can come at the cost of overfitting to noise. That DelTA works best with a single refinement step suggests the discriminative signal is strongest at the coarsest level (which tokens are broadly side-characteristic vs. shared), and that finer-grained distinctions add noise rather than signal. This finding has implications beyond DelTA: it suggests that simple centroid contrast may be the right level of analysis for RLVR token credit, and that more complex parametrizations (learned projections, deeper iterative refinement) may not help — a useful negative result for future work.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** Training uses DeepMath-103K (He et al., 2025), a large-scale, decontaminated, verifiable mathematical dataset. Evaluation uses seven mathematical benchmarks: AIME24, AIME25, AIME26 (Zhang & Math-AI, 2024, 2025, 2026), HMMT25 (February and November), HMMT26 (February) (Balunović et al., 2025), and Brumo 25. The specific training split and data preprocessing details are not elaborated in the paper beyond the dataset citation.

- **Base model(s).** Two backbones: **Qwen3-8B-Base** and **Qwen3-14B-Base** (Yang et al., 2025). These are chosen as representative contemporary LLMs at two scales, both starting from base (pre-trained, not instruction-tuned) checkpoints. An additional experiment uses **Olmo3-7B-Base** (Olmo et al., 2025) to test cross-family generalization. The paper states the models are "representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" but does not provide the pass@1 rates of the base models on MATH to contextualize the difficulty distribution.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **evaluation accuracy** — the fraction of problems for which the model's generated answer matches the ground truth, verified by `math-verify` (which handles mathematical expression equivalence). For each problem, 16 responses are sampled (temperature 1.0, top-p 0.7, maximum 30,000 tokens) and the paper reports "the average performance over all samples" (Section 4.1) — this phrasing is ambiguous: it could mean `pass@1` averaged over the 16 samples, or `avg@16` (average of the 16 individual correct/incorrect judgments), or `pass@16` (at least one correct). The statistical significance protocol in Appendix K clarifies: "each repetition runs the model over the evaluation suite and produces one question-count-weighted aggregate score," with 16 repetitions, suggesting the evaluation produces a single aggregate accuracy per run, but the exact estimator (best-of-16? average of 16?) is not explicitly stated for the main results. For checkpoint selection, the AIME25 avg@8 score is used uniformly.

- **Baselines.** Four methods are compared (all trained with identical hyperparameters and dynamic sampling disabled):
  - **DAPO** (Yu et al., 2025): the group-relative critic-free RLVR method with asymmetric clipping and token-level normalization, serving as the primary baseline.
  - **DAPO w/ Forking Tokens (DAPO w/ FT)** (Wang et al., 2025): DAPO with only the top 20% high-entropy tokens kept in the policy-gradient loss.
  - **SAPO** (Gao et al., 2025): replaces hard clipping with smooth, temperature-controlled soft gates with separate temperatures for positive- and negative-advantage tokens.
  - **FIPO** (Ma et al., 2026): reweights token-level advantages using discounted Future-KL influence weights, clipped for variance control.
  Method-specific hyperparameters for SAPO and FIPO are in Tables 7 and 8 (Appendix J). The choice of disabling dynamic sampling across all methods is deliberate: "We disable dynamic sampling for all methods to isolate the effect of the policy-update objective" (Section 4.1).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** All methods use the same rollout configuration: batch size 128, micro batch size 32, 16 rollouts per prompt, maximum response length 20,480 tokens during training, temperature 1.0, top-p 1.0. Learning rate is 1e-6, KL coefficient is 0.0 (no KL penalty). Qwen3-8B-Base trains for 220 steps; Qwen3-14B-Base trains for 300 steps. The paper does not report total training FLOPs or wall-clock time, but does measure the per-step overhead of DelTA's coefficient computation: approximately 37 seconds on the first step out of ~363 seconds total (~10.2% overhead, measured on 8×NVIDIA B200 GPUs; Appendix L.1). This overhead is measured only on the first step to avoid confounding from diverging response lengths during training (DelTA maintains longer responses than DAPO, making later-step comparisons unfair).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** Checkpoint selection uses a held-out evaluation on AIME25 avg@8, applied uniformly to all methods. Statistical significance is assessed via a **one-sided Mann–Whitney U test** on 16 repeated evaluation runs per method (Appendix K), comparing DelTA against the strongest same-scale baseline (SAPO for 8B, FIPO for 14B). The test treats each of the 16 scores per method as independent samples, which captures stochasticity from repeated generation but not from training (full training runs are not repeated due to cost). The pre-specified alternative hypothesis is that DelTA outperforms the baseline. The paper reports that DelTA significantly outperforms the strongest baselines at both model scales under this protocol, but exact p-values are not provided in the main text.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Benchmark Performance (Table 1)

DelTA achieves the highest average accuracy across all seven benchmarks on both model scales. On Qwen3-8B-Base, DelTA reaches **28.40 average points**, compared to the strongest same-scale baseline SAPO at 25.14 — a gain of **3.26 points** (Table 1). On Qwen3-14B-Base, DelTA achieves **39.91 average points**, compared to FIPO at 37.29 — a gain of **2.62 points**. These averages are "question-count weighted" across benchmarks, meaning larger benchmarks contribute proportionally more to the aggregate.

Notably, DelTA achieves the **best result on every single benchmark** at both scales, suggesting the gain is systematic rather than concentrated on a few benchmarks. The magnitude of improvement varies across benchmarks: on Qwen3-8B-Base, the spread ranges from +3.38 points on HMMT26 (Feb.) (17.05 → 20.27) to +6.46 points on AIME25 (23.33 → 26.46), with the largest relative improvement often on harder benchmarks (e.g., HMMT25 Feb. from 14.58 to 18.33, HMMT25 Nov. from 16.04 to 18.54).

The second-best method varies by scale: on 8B, SAPO is second at 25.14 (a soft-clipping method), while on 14B, FIPO is second at 37.29 (a future-influence method). The baseline ranking is not preserved across scales — DAPO ranks last on 8B (22.95) but is competitive on 14B (35.09, third place). The forking-tokens variant is consistently second or third (24.80 on 8B, 36.77 on 14B).

The paper does not report standard deviations or confidence intervals for these benchmark scores in Table 1, so the within-benchmark reliability of individual numbers cannot be assessed. The statistical significance test (Appendix K) is performed on the aggregate scores over repeated evaluation runs, not per-benchmark.

#### Training Dynamics (Figure 2)

Figure 2 compares reward, response length, and entropy trajectories over 220 training steps on Qwen3-8B-Base.

**Reward (left panel):** DelTA and DAPO track closely for the first ~50 steps, reaching a reward of approximately 0.55–0.60. After this point, the trajectories diverge. DAPO plateaus and then slightly degrades, ending around 0.58 at step 220. DelTA continues to improve, reaching approximately 0.72 at step 220. The gap at the end of training is roughly 0.14 reward units. The divergence onset around step 50 suggests that the discriminative reweighting effect accumulates: early in training, the centroids are not well-separated because the model produces similar responses (few correct answers), so DelTA's coefficients are near-uniform and the update is similar to DAPO. As the model improves and generates a meaningful mix of correct and incorrect responses, the centroid contrast sharpens and DelTA's reweighting begins to matter.

**Response length (middle panel):** Both methods start with responses of roughly 2,000–2,500 tokens. After step 25–50, DAPO's response length trends downward, ending around 1,200–1,500 tokens. DelTA's response length rises, then stabilizes with some fluctuation, ending around 3,000–4,500 tokens — roughly 2–3× longer than DAPO. The paper interprets this as evidence that DelTA "maintains longer responses" and that the length difference is not simply a byproduct: "The response-length and entropy curves suggest that this divergence is not merely due to shorter answers."

**Entropy (right panel):** Both methods start with entropy near 0.3–0.4. DAPO's entropy rises throughout training, ending around 0.9–1.0 — high entropy, indicating uncertain, diffuse token distributions. DelTA's entropy initially rises to approximately 0.6, then gradually declines to around 0.4–0.5 by the end of training, ending substantially below DAPO and near its own starting entropy. The combination of longer responses with lower entropy and higher reward is the paper's key dynamic signature: DelTA produces "more stable and confident long-reasoning behavior" compared to DAPO's shift toward "shorter responses with rising entropy."

The paper attributes this pattern to the discriminator view mechanism. Under DAPO, shared background directions (formatting, common phrases) dominate the centroids, so the update pulls the model toward high-probability, low-information tokens — explaining the response length collapse, since generating many diverse reasoning tokens requires going against the dominant centroid pull. Under DelTA, discriminative tokens are upweighted, so the update preserves gradient signals for reasoning-specific tokens that would otherwise be washed out. The model can therefore sustain longer reasoning chains without drifting toward boilterplate.

A subtlety: the paper does not ablate whether the length difference is *causal* for the reward improvement or *correlated*. It is possible that DelTA's reweighting independently improves both reward and response length, or that the length maintenance enables better reasoning (longer chains allow more problem-solving steps), or that a third factor (e.g., the discriminative update pushing the model toward more diverse output distributions) drives both. The discriminator view suggests the reweighting directly improves reward by focusing the update on reasoning-relevant directions, with length as a downstream consequence, but this causal chain is not experimentally isolated.

#### Token Selection Analysis (Figures 3–4, Section 5.2)

**Figure 3** plots training reward curves for four token-selection regimes: full-token DAPO, random 50% of tokens, top 50% of tokens by DelTA λ coefficients, and bottom 50% by λ coefficients. All regimes use the standard DAPO loss on the selected tokens, isolating the ranking effect of the coefficients from the continuous reweighting.

The **top-λ** curve (green) closely tracks full-token DAPO (blue) for the first ~50 steps, then diverges upward, ending at approximately 0.80 reward — above both the full DAPO baseline (~0.72 at step 220, per Figure 2 left) and substantially above the random 50% baseline (orange, ~0.62). This is striking: using half the tokens outperforms using all tokens, meaning the discarded half contains gradient directions that actively weaken the policy update.

The **random 50%** curve (orange) is consistently below full DAPO, ending around 0.62. The gap between random 50% and full DAPO (~0.10) reflects the cost of losing half the gradient mass; the gap between top-λ and random 50% (~0.18) reflects the value of discriminative selection over random selection. Since top-λ also uses only half the tokens, the full 0.18 improvement over random 50% is attributable entirely to which tokens are selected.

The **bottom-λ** curve (red) is the most dramatic: reward rises slightly to ~0.55 by step 25, then collapses to near 0.35 by step 100, far below the starting point. This is a strong negative result. It demonstrates that low-λ tokens aren't simply uninformative — their gradient directions actively harm the policy, dragging it toward worse behavior. The paper interprets this as evidence that "low-λ tokens are not simply uninformative; their gradient directions can actively harm the RLVR update."

**Figure 4** translates the training curves to evaluation accuracy on four benchmarks (AIME25, AIME26, HMMT25-Nov, HMMT26-Feb). Top-λ training achieves the highest accuracy across all four, with an average over the four benchmarks of approximately 24.7 (computed from the bar heights: roughly 27, 28, 20, 22 for the four benchmarks, visually). Full-token DAPO averages approximately 19.0 (values from Table 2 DAPO row: 23.33, 24.17, 12.08, 16.86, averaged = 19.11). Random 50% is close to DAPO or slightly below on three of four benchmarks, consistent with discarding half the gradient mass without compensating selectivity. Bottom-λ evaluation is not shown in Figure 4, presumably because the training collapse in Figure 3 rendered the model unevaluable or its scores were negligible.

The paper does not provide the exact numeric values in Figure 4 for the random and top-λ bars, only the visual. The DAPO full-token baseline values are reported separately in Table 2. This makes precise comparison difficult without extracting numbers from the figure.

#### Code Generation (Table 10)

On code generation tasks, DelTA improves over DAPO on all three benchmarks tested, using Eurus2-RL-Code as the training dataset with DAPO as baseline. On HumanEval+, DelTA achieves 84.6 vs. DAPO 83.0 (+1.6). On MBPP+, 73.2 vs. 72.1 (+1.1). On LiveCodeBench, 35.6 vs. 33.4 (+2.2). The weighted average (benchmarks weighted by number of problems) increases from 47.7 to 49.5 (+1.8 points). The training uses two epochs with the same recipe as the math experiments (no task-specific tuning). The improvement is modest compared to the math benchmarks but consistent — every benchmark improves. The small magnitude may reflect that code generation has different discriminative token patterns (e.g., syntax tokens may be more evenly distributed across correct and incorrect solutions, reducing the discriminative signal available to DelTA's coefficient estimation), or that two epochs is insufficient for the discriminative reweighting effect to compound as it does in the 220-step math training. The paper does not explore these hypotheses.

#### Cross-Model Family Generalization (Table 9)

On Olmo3-7B-Base, DelTA beats DAPO on all seven benchmarks. The average score improves from 19.01 to 22.80 (+3.79 points). The improvement is larger in absolute terms on this smaller, weaker model compared to Qwen3-14B (+2.62) but smaller than Qwen3-8B (+3.26), though the baseline accuracy differs substantially (Olmo3 DAPO at 19.01, Qwen3-8B DAPO at 22.95 — similar baselines, similar DelTA gains of 3.79 vs. 3.45 points respectively). This suggests the DelTA mechanism transfers across model families without architecture-specific adjustments, using identical hyperparameters.

#### Out-of-Domain Generalization (Table 12)

On GPQA-Diamond and MMLU-Pro (evaluated with avg@5 over 5 samples), DelTA improves over DAPO on both benchmarks and both backbone sizes. On Qwen3-8B-Base: GPQA-D 43.43 → 50.00 (+6.57), MMLU-Pro 63.97 → 66.47 (+2.50), weighted average 58.87 → 62.38 (+3.51). On Qwen3-14B-Base: GPQA-D 54.55 → 56.67 (+2.12), MMLU-Pro 70.80 → 72.27 (+1.47), weighted average 66.77 → 68.40 (+1.63). The gains are larger on GPQA-D (science questions) than on MMLU-Pro (broad knowledge), and larger on the smaller model (3.51 vs. 1.63 weighted average gain). This suggests DelTA's benefit is not limited to mathematical reasoning and transfers to broader reasoning domains, though with domain- and scale-dependent magnitude.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Within-side only vs. opposite-side comparison (Q1, Table 2):** The variant that assigns coefficients based on distance to own-side centroid only — `α_i,t = σ(-‖v - μ_+‖²/γ)` — achieves an average score of **17.94**, which is **worse than both DelTA (23.27) and the DAPO baseline (19.05)**. The degradation is consistent across all four benchmarks. The paper uses this falsification test to establish that opposite-side comparison is not a helpful add-on but the mechanism's core — own-side centrality alone is counterproductive because it upweights shared, non-discriminative tokens that pull the centroid toward the background. This is a clean negative result that sharply delimits the design space.

**Coefficient signal quality via hard token selection (Q2, Figures 3–4):** Top-50%-by-λ training outperforms full-token DAPO in reward and evaluation accuracy, despite using half the tokens. Random 50% underperforms full DAPO. Bottom 50% collapses training. This isolates the ranking quality of λ_i,t and demonstrates the coefficients separate high-value from harmful gradient directions. The collapse of bottom-λ is foregrounded as evidence that some tokens in the standard DAPO update are actively counterproductive — the paper frames this as supporting the diagnosis that shared background directions dilute the effective discriminator.

**Component ablations (Q3, Table 3):** Five components are ablated against the full DelTA (average 23.27):

- **w/o refinement (K=0):** coefficients estimated from initial centroids only, no iterative sharpening. Average drops to **19.97** (largest drop, −3.30). One-shot discriminative scores are insufficient; the refinement loop is the mechanism's core operational component.
- **w/o adaptive γ:** fixed temperatures instead of side-specific standard deviations. Average drops to **21.19** (−2.08). Scale adaptation matters — fixed temperatures misnormalize the sigmoid input, either over- or under-sharpening the assignment.
- **w/o h(α):** hard 0/1 assignment instead of soft sigmoid scores. Average drops to **20.93** (−2.34). The entropy regularizer's smoothing is important for stability, preventing brittle discontinuities at the decision boundary.
- **w/o λ-norm:** λ coefficients in numerator but divided by token count `∑|o_i|` instead of coefficient sum `∑ λ_i,t`. Average drops to **21.39** (−1.88). Self-normalization maintains effective learning rate invariance and prevents the coefficient distribution from inadvertently scaling the loss.
- **w/o range map:** raw `α ∈ (0,1)` used as weights instead of mapping to `[λ_min, λ_max]`. Average drops to **20.78** (−2.49). Bounded coefficients are more stable — raw sigmoid scores create extreme reweighting.

All five ablations show non-trivial drops, but the ranking is informative: refinement is the most important single component, followed by soft bounded assignment (range map + entropy regularizer), with adaptive normalization (temperatures + λ-norm) as helpful stabilizers.

**Proxy choice (Appendix F, Table 4):** The default LM-head row proxy `(1-p_t)·h_t` achieves average 23.27. A top-K hidden-gradient proxy (gradient of log-probability with respect to hidden state, approximated over top-K vocabulary) achieves **24.29**, improving on the default. This suggests the discriminative signal is robust to proxy choice and can benefit from richer approximations that capture local token competition. A random coefficient baseline (λ sampled uniformly from `[λ_min, λ_max]`, same normalization) achieves **18.34**, below both DelTA variants and the DAPO baseline (19.05), confirming that the structure of the coefficients matters, not just the fact of reweighting.

**Hyperparameter sensitivity (Q4, Table 11, Appendix L.4):** The coefficient range `[λ_min, λ_max]` is tested at `[0.5, 1.2]`, `[0.8, 1.5]`, `[0.5, 1.5]`, and the base `[0.8, 1.2]`. Average scores are 23.22, 22.77, 23.07, and 23.27 respectively — all within ~0.5 points and none substantially worse than the base. The number of refinement iterations `K` is tested at 1 (base), 2, and 3. Averages: 23.27, 22.15, 22.20 — `K=2` and `K=3` both degrade by ~1.1 points, with `K=3` not further degrading beyond `K=2`, suggesting the degradation plateaus rather than monotonically worsening. The paper interprets this as overfitting to batch-level token-gradient geometry, but does not test whether this overfitting can be mitigated by larger batch sizes or averaging across batches.

**Cross-domain: Code and OOD (Tables 10, 12):** DelTA improves over DAPO on code generation (+1.8 weighted average, Table 10) and two out-of-domain knowledge/reasoning benchmarks (+3.51 on 8B, +1.63 on 14B, Table 12). The paper does not conduct component ablations in these domains, so it is unknown whether the same components matter equally or whether domain-specific patterns emerge.

**Negative results:** The paper reports three notable negative results:
1. The "within-side only" variant (Table 2) is worse than DAPO, establishing a clear boundary.
2. The bottom-50%-by-λ training collapse (Figures 3–4) shows active harm from low-scoring tokens — the coefficients don't just rank, they separate beneficial from harmful.
3. Increasing refinement iterations beyond K=1 degrades performance (Table 11), providing a practical guideline and suggesting overfitting.

### Critical Assessment

#### Claim 1: "DelTA outperforms the strongest same-scale baselines by 3.26 and 2.62 average points"

This claim is **directly supported by Table 1** for both Qwen3-8B-Base (28.40 vs. 25.14 SAPO) and Qwen3-14B-Base (39.91 vs. 37.29 FIPO). The statistical significance test (Appendix K) confirms these gaps are significant at the 0.05 level under the repeated-generation protocol. However, several limitations apply:

- The numbers are based on a **single training run per method per model scale**. Full training is not replicated with multiple seeds due to cost. The statistical test captures generation-level stochasticity (16 evaluation runs) but not training-level stochasticity (initialization, data order, optimization noise). The training dynamics in Figure 2 suggest substantial run-to-run variation is possible — the gap between DelTA and DAPO in reward varies from near-zero (step 25) to large (step 220). A single training run cannot establish that DelTA consistently outperforms across random seeds, only that it did in this run.

- The **strongest baseline varies by scale** (SAPO at 8B, FIPO at 14B), and neither baseline was specifically tuned for these settings — method-specific hyperparameters are taken from their respective papers (Tables 7–8). It is possible that additional tuning could close some of the gap.

- The checkpoint selection criterion (best AIME25 avg@8) is the same for all methods but the metric being optimized is not the same as the evaluation metric (average over seven benchmarks). A method that happens to peak on AIME25 earlier or later in training could be disadvantaged if its best average-across-benchmarks checkpoint lies elsewhere. The paper does not report the correlation between AIME25 performance and average performance at each step.

- **Benchmark coverage is narrow in domain:** all seven are competition mathematics. The code and OOD results (Tables 10, 12) provide supporting evidence for generalization, but with smaller absolute gains (+1.8 on code, +1.6–3.5 on OOD) and without comparative baselines (only DAPO, not SAPO or FIPO). The paper's title and framing emphasize mathematical reasoning, but the broader claim of improving "RLVR" requires more diverse task validation.

#### Claim 2: "DelTA reshapes the side-wise centroids to be more contrastive, thereby improving the update direction"

This claim is **indirectly supported** — the paper provides no direct measurement of centroid contrast or update direction quality. The discriminator view is an analytical lens; the experimental evidence for it is the success of DelTA (which implements the centroid-reshaping design principle) and the failure of the within-side-only variant (which removes the discriminative comparison). Neither of these directly verifies that the centroids are *actually* more contrastive under DelTA, or that the update direction changes in the predicted way. To directly support this claim, the paper would need to report measurements like:

- The cosine similarity between `μ_+` and `μ_-` under DAPO vs. DelTA over training (lower similarity = more contrastive centroids).
- The distribution of token-level probability changes under DAPO vs. DelTA updates, showing that DelTA produces larger changes on discriminative tokens and smaller changes on shared tokens.
- The classification accuracy of the induced discriminator on held-out token-gradient vectors (how well does the DAPO discriminator vs. DelTA discriminator separate tokens from correct vs. incorrect responses?).

Without such measurements, the centroid-reshaping mechanism is a **plausible and consistent explanation** for the observed performance gains, but not a directly verified causal mechanism. The Q2 analysis (top-λ outperforms full DAPO) supports that the coefficients *rank* useful token-gradient directions, but ranking is weaker evidence than demonstrating that the reweighted centroids are geometrically more discriminative.

#### Claim 3: "The discriminator view provides a principled explanation for RLVR token selection"

This claim is **analytically supported** by the derivation in Section 3.1 and Appendix C, which shows that the first-order update of DAPO can be decomposed into positive/negative centroid contrast. The extension to other methods (Appendix E) further supports the generality of the view. The empirical evidence is that methods consistent with the discriminator view's diagnosis (DelTA) outperform methods that are not (within-side-only, uniform DAPO). However, the paper does not test alternative explanations for the same empirical patterns:

- The training dynamics in Figure 2 (DAPO: shorter responses, higher entropy, lower reward) could be explained by known RLVR pathologies unrelated to centroid contrast: reward hacking (the model learns to game the verifier rather than improve reasoning), distributional collapse (the policy entropy drops sharply then the model can't explore), or optimization instability from the clipped surrogate. The discriminator view offers one explanation; the paper does not rule out others.
- The Q2 finding (bottom-λ training collapses) could be explained by low-λ tokens corresponding to tokens with high gradient noise or adversarial gradient directions, rather than specifically non-discriminative ones. The paper equates "low discriminative score" with "hurts the discriminator" but the collapse could have other causes.

#### Claim 4: "DelTA generalizes across backbones, domains, and benchmarks"

This claim is **supported with qualifications**. Cross-backbone: Olmo3-7B-Base shows gains (Table 9), but only DAPO is compared — no head-to-head with the other baselines (SAPO, FIPO, forking tokens) on this model family, so it's unknown whether DelTA remains the strongest method. Cross-domain: code generation (Table 10) shows gains but the experiment uses two training epochs vs. the math experiments' 220 steps, and no information is given about whether convergence was reached or whether the gap would widen with longer training. OOD evaluation (Table 12) shows consistent gains but the absolute magnitude is smaller on the 14B model (+1.63 weighted average vs. +3.51 on 8B), and the paper does not test whether this pattern holds across more than two OOD benchmarks. Cross-benchmark within math: DelTA wins on all seven, which is the strongest generalization evidence.

#### What's Missing

Several experiments would have strengthened the paper substantially:

1. **Multiple training seeds per method:** even 2–3 seeds on the 8B model would provide error bars for Table 1 and distinguish method variance from seed variance.

2. **Direct measurements of the discriminator:** centroid cosine similarity over training, classification accuracy of the induced discriminator, and visualization of which token types receive high vs. low λ coefficients as a function of their semantic role (the token cloud in Figure 5, Appendix L.6, is qualitative and context-agnostic — it averages coefficients over all occurrences of a token type, losing the context-dependence that is central to DelTA's mechanism).

3. **Comparison against an ORM/PRM baseline:** the paper positions DelTA as complementary to dense reward approaches (process reward models, learned value functions), but provides no head-to-head comparison. Training a process reward model on the same data and using it for token-level rewards vs. DelTA's self-supervised coefficients would help calibrate the value of the discriminative approach relative to the dominant alternative.

4. **Difficulty-stratified analysis:** the discriminator view predicts that DelTA's benefit should vary with problem difficulty — when most responses in a batch are either all correct or all incorrect, the positive-negative centroid contrast is weak (one side has little mass), and DelTA's coefficients should be near-uniform. Breaking down results by pass@1 difficulty (as in the reference paper's analysis) would test this prediction and reveal whether DelTA's gains concentrate on problems where the model produces a meaningful mix of correct and incorrect responses.

5. **Ablation of the training data distribution:** the discriminative signal DelTA exploits depends on the presence of both correct and incorrect responses in each batch. If the policy becomes too strong (most responses correct), the negative centroid loses mass and discriminative signal degrades. If the policy is too weak (few correct), the positive centroid degrades. Understanding this dependence would help practitioners know when DelTA is most useful.

6. **Longer training:** Figure 2 shows DelTA reward still rising at step 220 (8B) — the training was stopped at a fixed step count, not at convergence. DAPO's reward has plateaued or declined by step 220, but it's possible DAPO would recover with different hyperparameters or simply different random dynamics. Training to convergence (or for a fixed number of tokens processed) would provide a more definitive comparison.

7. **Direct comparison to the strongest published RLVR results:** the baseline numbers in Table 1 (DAPO at 22.95 on 8B, 35.09 on 14B) are substantially below some published results for similar-scale models trained with RLVR on math. This could reflect the paper's decision to disable dynamic sampling and to use a fixed training recipe without per-method tuning. The paper positions its contributions as about the update mechanism rather than achieving state-of-the-art scores, but understanding how DelTA interacts with stronger baseline training recipes (including dynamic sampling, longer training, or different data mixtures) is important for practical adoption.

Overall, the experimental evidence is **consistent with and well-explained by** the discriminator view and DelTA's design, but the paper's strongest contribution is the **conceptual reframing and the algorithm that operationalizes it**, rather than an exhaustive empirical validation of every mechanistic claim. The experiments demonstrate that discriminative token reweighting improves performance across a meaningful range of settings, with thoughtful ablations that rule out simpler explanations (random reweighting, own-side centrality, uniform selection). The limitations are primarily about depth of mechanistic validation and breadth of conditions tested, not about the existence or direction of the effect.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Discriminator View Is a Local, First-Order Approximation—Not a Verified Causal Mechanism for the Full Training Trajectory

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire analytical framework rests on a first-order Taylor expansion around `θ_old`: the local change in token log-probability is approximated as `∇_θ log π_θ(x|c)ᵀ Δθ`, and the update direction `Δθ` is derived from the unclipped surrogate gradient at the point where importance ratios are 1. The authors are explicit and transparent about this scope:

> "We use this local characterization as an analysis and design principle for shaping the policy-update direction, rather than as an exact description of the full nonlinear clipped RLVR training trajectory." (Section 3.1)

This is not presented deceptively—the paper is clear that the discriminator view is a design tool, not a trajectory-level theory. However, the practical implications of this limitation are significant and not fully explored.

**The consequence.** The local analysis only describes the very first gradient step within an optimization epoch—before any clipping becomes active, before importance ratios deviate from 1, and before the optimizer's preconditioner (Adam) modifies the raw gradient direction. After that first step, the policy parameters change, the importance ratios `r_{i,t}(θ)` diverge from 1, and clipping becomes active on some tokens—the actual gradient diverges from the idealised unclipped form. Moreover, the fundamental objects in the analysis (`μ_+`, `μ_-`) are computed from token-gradient vectors at `θ_old`, but after even a single parameter update, the token-gradient vectors for the same responses would be different if recomputed—the centroids shift continuously during training.

The consequence is that DelTA's coefficient estimation, which is based on the local centroids, may not target the globally optimal update direction. The refinement loop sharpens centroids based on the current batch's token-gradient geometry, but there is no guarantee that this sharpened direction remains beneficial under clipping, over multiple epochs, or as the policy drifts. This is not a theoretical quibble—it means that DelTA's coefficients could be locally optimal but globally misaligned, especially in later epochs of a batch when the policy has been updated several times and the original token-gradient vectors are stale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides indirect evidence that the local design principle translates to full-training benefits: DelTA outperforms DAPO across all evaluations (Table 1, Figures 2–4), and the training dynamics diverge favorably after step 50 (Figure 2). However, the paper does **not** provide any measurement of:

- How the cosine similarity between `μ_+` and `μ_-` evolves over training, which would directly test whether DelTA's centroids are more contrastive as training progresses.
- Whether the per-token probability changes predicted by the local discriminator (Equation 4) correlate with the actual token-level probability changes observed after a full training step (which includes clipping, multiple epochs, and Adam's preconditioning).
- Whether the coefficients `λ_{i,t}` computed at `θ_old` remain correlated with token-level learning value in later epochs of the same batch (the coefficients are fixed across epochs within a batch, but the policy changes).

Without these measurements, a plausible alternative explanation remains: DelTA's reweighting may function as a form of **variance reduction** (giving more stable gradient estimates by downweighting high-variance token directions) rather than as a discriminative-centroid shaper. The Q2 analysis (top-50%-by-λ outperforms full DAPO) shows the coefficients identify useful token-gradient directions, but this could be because the coefficients filter out noisy or high-variance gradient components, not because they specifically sharpen the centroids in the way the discriminator view describes.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap directly, but the robustness of the results to proxy choice (Table 4), hyperparameter ranges (Table 11), and model scale (8B, 14B, 7B-Olmo) provides circumstantial evidence that the mechanism generalises beyond the local approximation. The authors frame the discriminator view explicitly as "an analysis and design principle" rather than a performance guarantee, which is intellectually honest but leaves the causal chain unverified. A stronger validation would involve measuring centroid geometry directly or testing whether the token-level probability changes match the discriminator's predictions.

### Gradient Staleness: Coefficients Are Computed Once Per Rollout Batch but Reused Across Multiple Optimization Epochs

**The assumption or constraint.** DelTA computes token coefficients `λ_{i,t}` from the token-gradient vectors at `θ_old` (the policy that generated the rollout batch) and holds them fixed across all optimization epochs over that batch. The RLVR training loop typically performs multiple gradient steps on the same batch of trajectories—in this paper's setup, the exact number of epochs per batch is not specified, but standard practice in DAPO/GRPO training involves several epochs of optimization before resampling. The paper states:

> "The coefficients are stop-gradient quantities computed once per rollout batch, fixed across optimization epochs, and recomputed after new trajectories are sampled." (Section 3.2)

**The consequence.** As the policy is updated during the multi-epoch optimization, the actual token-gradient vectors for the rollout tokens change—if we were to recompute `v_{i,t}` from the updated policy, they would differ from the original vectors used to estimate the coefficients. The fixed coefficients therefore reflect the discriminative structure at the **beginning** of the batch's optimization, but are applied to gradient steps throughout the batch—potentially several parameter updates later.

This creates a systematic bias: in the first epoch, the coefficients are exactly correct for the local discriminator. In the second epoch, the policy has changed, the importance ratios `r_{i,t}(θ)` have moved away from 1, clipping may be active on some tokens, and the effective token-gradient vectors (if recomputed) would have shifted. The stale coefficients may:

- Continue upweighting tokens that were discriminative under the old policy but are no longer discriminative under the updated policy (or vice versa).
- Fail to adapt to the fact that the policy's output distribution has changed—tokens that were characteristic of correct responses at `θ_old` may no longer be characteristic after several gradient steps.

The practical effect is that coefficient staleness may cause the effective update direction in later epochs to degrade relative to the first epoch, potentially undoing or diluting the benefits of the reweighting. The paper provides no analysis of how the effective update direction changes across epochs within a batch.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does **not** measure or ablate the number of optimization epochs per batch, nor does it report how performance varies with different numbers of epochs. The training recipe (Table 5) specifies batch size 128, micro batch size 32, and 220/300 total steps for 8B/14B, but the number of epochs per batch is not stated. The reward curves in Figure 2 show smooth improvement for DelTA over training steps, but these are at the step granularity (each step presumably processes one micro-batch of 32), not the batch granularity. If gradient staleness were severe, one would expect the reward improvement per batch to saturate or decline within the multi-epoch optimization window—but this is not measured. The paper's strong results suggest that staleness is not catastrophic in practice, but the absence of measurement means there is no evidence about how many epochs per batch would be optimal for DelTA specifically versus DAPO.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially mitigates this by keeping coefficients in a bounded range `[0.8, 1.2]`, which limits the damage from stale coefficients—even maximally misweighted, a token's contribution is only 20% above or below average. This bounded reweighting is more forgiving than hard selection (which the Q3 ablation shows performs worse). However, the fundamental issue of stale coefficients remains unaddressed. Recomputing coefficients within each epoch would add computational overhead (approximately `K+2` additional forward passes per epoch, not just per batch), which the paper does not explore. The tradeoff between coefficient freshness and computational cost is left as an open question.

### The Difficulty Estimation Gap: The Paper Provides No Analysis of When DelTA's Discriminative Signal Is Strong vs. Weak

**The assumption or constraint.** DelTA's coefficient estimation depends fundamentally on the presence of **both positive-advantage and negative-advantage responses in each rollout batch**, because the discriminative score `α_{i,t}` compares distances to both centroids. If a batch contains only correct responses (all `Â_i > 0`), the negative centroid has zero mass and cannot be computed. If a batch contains only incorrect responses (all `Â_i < 0`), the positive centroid has zero mass. The paper handles this formally:

> "If one advantage side is empty... such a group contributes zero to the local policy-gradient update and is therefore skipped in the positive-negative decomposition." (Appendix C)

The authors also note that under group-normalized advantages, if all responses in a group are correct, all advantages are zero, and the entire group contributes nothing to the update—this is inherent to DAPO's advantage normalization, not specific to DelTA.

**The consequence.** DelTA provides **no discriminative signal on batches where the policy is uniformly correct or uniformly incorrect**, because there is no between-side contrast to exploit. On uniformly-correct batches, all advantages are zero and no update occurs anyway. On uniformly-incorrect batches, the policy needs to be pushed away from those responses, but DelTA cannot compute a positive centroid to contrast against—the best it can do is fall back to the standard DAPO uniform update (which it does, by treating empty sides as zero and skipping centroid computation).

This has direct implications for **when DelTA is most useful in training**. Early in training, when the policy is weak and few responses are correct, the positive centroid is estimated from a small number of tokens and may be noisy. Late in training, when the policy is strong and most responses are correct, the negative centroid is sparse and the discriminator loses contrast—there are few incorrect responses to define what separates good from bad. DelTA is most effective in the **intermediate regime** where the policy produces a meaningful mix of correct and incorrect responses, providing rich between-side contrast for coefficient estimation.

This also implies that DelTA's benefit should be **difficulty-dependent**: the mechanism works best on problems of intermediate difficulty where the policy sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails. On trivially easy problems (nearly always correct), there is no negative side to learn from. On impossibly hard problems (nearly always incorrect), there is no positive side to contrast against. The paper does not test this prediction—there is no difficulty-stratified analysis as was done in the reference paper's Figure 3 (right panel) showing that beam search helps on medium-difficulty problems but hurts on easy ones.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The reward curves in Figure 2 show that DelTA and DAPO track closely for the first ~50 steps—this is consistent with the prediction that early in training (when few responses are correct), the discriminative signal is weak and DelTA approximates DAPO. The divergence after step 50 coincides with the policy reaching a non-trivial success rate (reward ~0.5–0.6), which would provide meaningful positive-negative contrast. However, this correlation is not explicitly tested—the paper does not measure the fraction of batches that contain both correct and incorrect responses over training, nor the relationship between batch composition and DelTA's coefficient distribution.

The paper also **does not** provide a difficulty-stratified breakdown of DelTA's gains. The seven math benchmarks vary in difficulty (AIME is generally harder than some HMMT instances), and the absolute accuracy varies from ~18% to ~57% for DelTA on the 14B model (Table 1). The gain over the best baseline varies from +1.45 points (HMMT25 Nov., 14B: 32.92 vs. 31.67 baseline) to +6.67 points (HMMT25 Feb., 14B: 26.04 vs. 19.79—though the strongest baseline here is actually DAPO w/ FT at 20.42, not the 19.79 used in the "strongest same-scale baseline" claim, making exact gain comparisons ambiguous). Without systematic difficulty binning, it is impossible to test whether DelTA's gains concentrate on intermediate-difficulty problems as the discriminator mechanism predicts.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap. The authors do not discuss the dependence of discriminative signal strength on batch composition, do not provide difficulty-stratified analysis, and do not suggest strategies for improving coefficient estimation when one side is sparse (e.g., using a historical buffer of token-gradient vectors from previous batches, or interpolating between discriminative and uniform weighting based on batch composition). This is a significant gap for practitioners who want to know whether DelTA will help on their specific problem distribution.

### The Scope of Validation Is Narrow: One Task Family (Mathematics), One Training Dataset, and Training-Level Variance Is Unmeasured

**The assumption or constraint.** All main experiments train on **DeepMath-103K** and evaluate on seven **mathematical reasoning benchmarks**. The code generation experiment (Table 10) uses a different dataset (Eurus2-RL-Code) and only two training epochs, which is substantially less training than the math experiments (220–300 steps). The out-of-domain evaluation (Table 12) tests the math-trained models on GPQA-Diamond and MMLU-Pro, but does not train on OOD data—it measures transfer, not in-domain RLVR improvement in non-math domains. The Olmo3-7B experiment (Table 9) uses the same math data and training recipe testing cross-architecture generalization, not cross-task generalization.

The paper's training protocol further compounds this: only **one training run per method per model scale** is performed. The statistical significance test (Appendix K) uses 16 repeated evaluation runs to capture generation-level stochasticity, but:

> "Due to the high cost of RLVR training, we do not repeat full training runs with multiple random seeds." (Appendix K)

**The consequence.** The paper cannot distinguish between method variance and seed variance. If DelTA's single training run happened to be a favorable random seed and the baseline runs happened to be unfavorable, the reported gaps (3.26 and 2.62 points) could overstate the method's advantage. Conversely, if DelTA's run was unfavorable, the true advantage could be larger. A single training run per method means the error bars on the main results (Table 1) reflect only generation stochasticity, not the substantially larger variance from different random initialisations, data orders, and optimisation trajectories.

From a practical deployment perspective, the narrow task scope means a practitioner training on a different problem domain (code generation, scientific reasoning, multi-turn dialogue, tool use) has very limited evidence about whether DelTA will help. The code generation result (+1.8 points weighted average) is positive but substantially smaller than the math gains (+3.26 on 8B), and the experiment uses only two epochs—it is unknown whether the gap would widen with longer training (as in math, where the gap widened after step 50 in Figure 2) or whether two epochs is representative of convergence-level performance. The OOD results show transfer—the math-trained model does better on science and knowledge tasks—but this is not a test of DelTA's effectiveness when trained directly on those tasks with domain-appropriate RLVR rewards.

Additionally, all experiments use **base models** (Qwen3-Base, Olmo3-Base), not instruction-tuned models. The interaction between DelTA's discriminative reweighting and instruction tuning (which already shapes the token distribution toward helpful, formatted outputs) is unexplored. Instruction-tuned models may have different token-gradient geometries—more uniform across correct and incorrect responses because both are well-formatted—which could reduce the discriminative signal available to DelTA.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The cross-domain evidence is:

- **Code generation (Table 10):** +1.8 weighted average over DAPO, all three benchmarks improve. Training is only two epochs, which the paper does not justify or compare to convergence. No comparison to SAPO, FIPO, or forking tokens in this domain.
- **OOD evaluation (Table 12):** Math-trained DelTA outperforms math-trained DAPO on GPQA-Diamond and MMLU-Pro by +1.6–3.5 points (weighted average). These are zero-shot transfer results, not RLVR training results in the OOD domains.
- **Cross-architecture (Table 9):** Olmo3-7B-Base shows +3.79 average point gain over DAPO on math, confirming the mechanism transfers across model families under identical hyperparameters. This is the strongest cross-validation evidence, but it remains within the math domain.

The paper has **no experiments** training on non-math verifiable reward tasks from scratch, no experiments with instruction-tuned base models, and no multi-seed training validation.

**Mitigation status.** The paper is transparent about the training protocol (single run) and the rationale (cost). The Olmo3-7B result partially mitigates the architecture-specificity concern, and the code/OOD results partially mitigate the math-only concern, but the mitigations are limited in scope. Future work on broader task families, instruction-tuned models, and multi-seed validation would substantially strengthen confidence in the method's generality. The authors do not speculate about domain dependence or suggest task-specific adaptations that might be needed.

### Verification Bias: The Method Assumes a Verifiable Binary Reward but Does Not Explore Reward Noise or Granularity

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use a **binary verifiable reward**: 1 if the final answer is exactly correct (verified by `math-verify`), 0 otherwise. The paper states this explicitly:

> "We use a simple binary verifiable reward: a response receives reward 1 if its final answer is correct, and 0 otherwise." (Appendix I)

The group-normalized advantages `Â_i` are then computed from these binary rewards, producing a continuous-valued advantage that separates correct from incorrect responses within each batch. The entire discriminative mechanism depends on there being a clean separation between responses with `Â_i > 0` (correct or relatively better) and `Â_i < 0` (incorrect or relatively worse).

**The consequence.** A binary reward is the **cleanest possible discriminative signal**—there is no ambiguity about which responses are "good" and "bad." This maximises the contrast between the positive and negative centroids, making DelTA's coefficient estimation as informative as possible. In many realistic RLVR settings, rewards are not binary:

- **Partial credit**: a math solution that arrives at the wrong final answer but performs correct intermediate steps might deserve partial reward. The paper's setting ignores this—all incorrect answers receive reward 0 regardless of how close they were.
- **Continuous rewards**: code generation might be scored by the fraction of unit tests passed (0% to 100%), producing a continuous reward signal where the boundary between "good enough" and "not good enough" is fuzzy.
- **Noisy or inconsistent rewards**: in some domains, the verifier itself may be imperfect (e.g., a learned reward model, human preference scores, or execution-based verification with environmental stochasticity), introducing noise into the reward signal.

Under partial credit or continuous rewards, the positive-negative split would be less clean—responses with intermediate rewards may not clearly belong to either side, and forcing a split based on `Â_i > 0` vs. `Â_i < 0` could assign nearly-identical responses to opposite sides based on small reward differences. This would degrade the discriminative signal because the token-gradient vectors of responses near the decision boundary would be similar, pulling the centroids toward each other and reducing contrast. Under noisy rewards, incorrect responses might occasionally receive reward 1 and correct responses reward 0, directly contaminating the centroid estimates.

Furthermore, the binary reward assumption interacts with the difficulty-dependence issue (Limitation 3): when the model is very strong, most batches will have all responses correct (all `Â_i = 0` under group normalization) and no update occurs. This creates a natural ceiling—DelTA cannot improve the policy beyond the point where it consistently produces only correct answers in each batch, because there is no negative side to learn from and no discriminative signal to exploit.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides **no experiments** with non-binary rewards, no analysis of reward noise sensitivity, and no discussion of how the method would extend to continuous or multi-level reward signals. The code generation experiment (Table 10) uses execution-based verification (pass/fail on test cases), which is also binary in practice, so this experiment does not test reward granularity. The paper's positioning as "RLVR from verifiable rewards" in the title suggests broad applicability, but all empirical validation uses the simplest case (binary verification).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. A natural extension would be to generalise the discriminative score computation to handle continuous rewards: for example, by partitioning responses into reward quantiles rather than a hard positive/negative split, or by using the reward values directly as regression targets in the coefficient estimation (tokens whose gradient magnitude correlates with reward would get higher weight). The paper does not propose or discuss such extensions, leaving the method's applicability to non-binary reward settings as an open question. For practitioners with graded or continuous rewards, the paper provides no guidance on whether or how to adapt DelTA.

### The Token-Gradient Proxy Approximation: Unexplored Information Loss from Restricting to the LM Head

**The assumption or constraint.** DelTA computes the token-gradient vectors `v_{i,t}` as a layer-restricted proxy using only the final LM head: `v_{i,t} = (1 - p_t(o_{i,t})) · h_t`, where `h_t` is the final hidden state and `p_t` is the softmax probability. The full-parameter token-gradient vector `∇_θ log π_θ(o_{i,t} | q, o_{i,<t})` contains gradient components for **all** layers—attention weights in every transformer block, MLP weights, layer norms, and input embeddings. The proxy discards all of this except the LM head output embedding row. The paper is transparent:

> "We do not use this proxy as an exact reconstruction of the full-parameter gradient. Instead, we use it as a scalable layer-restricted representation for estimating relative token coefficients." (Appendix F)

**The consequence.** The proxy captures only the **output-level** discriminative signal: which tokens require the model to adjust its output embedding in a direction characteristic of correct vs. incorrect responses. It misses the **internal** discriminative signal: which attention patterns, intermediate representations, or computational pathways within the transformer distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning. Two tokens might have identical proxy vectors (because they occur in similar hidden-state contexts with similar model confidence) but very different full-parameter gradients (because they engage different attention heads or MLP activations that are differentially important for correct reasoning). In such cases, DelTA would assign them identical coefficients, missing a source of discriminative signal.

The practical consequence is that DelTA's coefficient estimation may be **blind to deep reasoning structure** that manifests in earlier layers. For example:

- An attention head that consistently attends to the correct numerical value in correct responses but attends to distractors in incorrect responses would produce different full-parameter gradients for tokens influenced by that head. The proxy—which only sees the final hidden state—may not capture this difference if the hidden state itself is similar (the model's internal processing differed, but the output representation converged to similar values).
- An MLP activation pattern that computes an intermediate quantity (e.g., a factored expression) correctly in some responses and incorrectly in others would have different gradients in earlier layers even if the final token-level probability is similar.

The proxy ablations (Table 4) partially address this by testing a richer proxy (top-K hidden-gradient, which incorporates competition among top vocabulary candidates). This richer proxy performs **better** than the default (24.29 vs. 23.27 average), confirming that the default proxy's information is not complete—there is recoverable discriminative signal that the default proxy misses. The fact that a better (but still layer-restricted) proxy improves performance implies that even stronger proxies (e.g., incorporating selected intermediate-layer gradients) might yield further gains, and conversely, that the default proxy is leaving discriminative value on the table.

The information loss is also **scale-dependent**. In smaller models with shallower networks, most of the token-level discriminative signal may be concentrated in the final layers (where it directly affects the output), making the proxy adequate. In larger models with deep reasoning chains spread across many layers, the proxy may miss an increasing fraction of the relevant discriminative structure. The paper does not test this—the results on 8B vs. 14B show similar relative gains (+3.26 vs. +2.62), but this comparison confounds model scale with baseline accuracy and does not isolate the proxy quality.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The proxy ablation (Appendix F, Table 4) is the only evidence about proxy quality. It shows:

- Default proxy: 23.27 average
- Top-K hidden-gradient proxy: 24.29 average (+1.02)
- Random λ: 18.34 average (−4.93, below DAPO at 19.05)

This establishes three things: (1) the proxy matters—better proxies lead to better performance; (2) the default proxy is not saturated—there is room for improvement; and (3) the coefficients must be structured—random coefficients fail. However, the paper does not test other proxy variants (e.g., incorporating intermediate-layer hidden states, using gradient information from attention layers, or ensembling multiple layer-restricted proxies), so the ceiling for proxy-driven improvement is unknown.

**Mitigation status.** The paper notes that "exploring richer and more efficient token-gradient approximations is a promising direction for future work" (Appendix A, Limitations), acknowledging this gap. The practical mitigation for deployment is that the default proxy already provides gains over DAPO, so the information loss is not catastrophic—but practitioners willing to invest additional computation in coefficient estimation could potentially achieve better results with richer proxies. The paper provides no guidance on how to select or design such proxies, and the 10.2% overhead of the default method would increase with richer proxy computation (the top-K proxy likely requires additional top-K extraction and weighted averaging, adding computational cost not measured in the paper).

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a new analytical lens for understanding token-level credit assignment in RLVR: the **discriminator view**. The core idea—that a policy-gradient update vector `Δθ` can be reinterpreted as an implicit linear classifier in token-gradient space, with the positive- and negative-side centroids `μ_+` and `μ_-` serving as reference directions—is not an incremental refinement of existing credit assignment methods. It is a **fundamental reframing** of what the RLVR update does at the token level: it determines whether a candidate token's probability increases or decreases based on whether its token-gradient vector aligns more with the positive-side or negative-side centroid. This converts what was previously an opaque, empirically observed phenomenon (sparse token-level distributional shifts documented by Meng et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2026) into a **geometrically interpretable mechanism with a clear causal structure**.

The magnitude of this shift should not be overstated—it is a conceptual and analytical contribution, not a new training paradigm. The discriminator view does not replace RLVR; it explains and improves it within the existing sequence-level reward framework. But it changes the conversation around token-level credit assignment in several concrete ways.

**First, it reorients the design space for future methods.** Before this paper, the dominant approaches to addressing the sequence-level reward granularity mismatch fell into three categories: (1) filtering tokens by a heuristic statistic (entropy in forking tokens, Wang et al., 2025; future influence in FIPO, Ma et al., 2026), (2) training auxiliary dense reward models (process reward models, Cui et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b), or (3) accepting uniform weighting and compensating with better clipping or normalization (DAPO, Yu et al., 2025; SAPO, Gao et al., 2025). The discriminator view unifies the first category under a single geometric interpretation (Appendix E shows that these methods are all modifications to the effective token weights `ρ_{i,t}` in the centroid construction) and **provides a new design target**: make the centroids more contrastive. DelTA is one concrete instantiation of this principle; the design space it opens—manipulating the positive and negative reference directions to improve the induced discriminator—is broader than DelTA itself and suggests many possible algorithmic variants (different contrast functions, learned projections, batch-level normalization strategies).

**Second, it provides a falsifiable diagnostic for why RLVR training plateaus or degrades.** The paper's diagnosis—that standard uniform aggregation allows shared, high-frequency token-gradient directions (formatting, boilerplate, problem-specific entities) to dominate both centroids, diluting the discriminative contrast—is not just a post-hoc explanation. It generates a specific, testable prediction: training on only the tokens with the highest discriminative scores should outperform training on all tokens, because the excluded tokens contain gradient directions that actively weaken the discriminator. The paper validates this prediction in the Q2 analysis (Section 5.2, Figures 3–4): top-50%-by-λ training outperforms full-token DAPO, while bottom-50%-by-λ training collapses completely. This is a **strong, directional prediction** that goes beyond "DelTA works better"; it demonstrates that the coefficients meaningfully separate beneficial from harmful gradient directions. The diagnostic thus gives practitioners and researchers a concrete tool: if a model's RLVR training is plateauing, measure whether the centroids have collapsed toward each other (high cosine similarity between `μ_+` and `μ_-`), and if so, consider discriminative reweighting.

**Third, it reconciles contradictory intuitions about token-level weighting.** The paper provides a clear negative result (Section 5.1, Table 2) showing that own-side centrality—assigning larger weights to tokens closer to their own side's centroid—performs *worse* than the uniform DAPO baseline. This resolves a natural but incorrect intuition: one might think that tokens more "typical" of correct responses should be upweighted. The discriminator view explains exactly why this fails: the tokens closest to the positive centroid are likely to be the shared, high-frequency ones that also appear in incorrect responses, and upweighting them amplifies the very background directions that weaken the discriminator. The opposite-side comparison is not a helpful add-on; it is **the core mechanism**. This finding has implications for the broader contrastive learning literature (where similar own-class vs. between-class weighting tradeoffs are well-studied, e.g., Khosla et al., 2020) and suggests that future token-level weighting methods in RLVR should always incorporate between-side contrast, not just within-side typicality.

**Fourth, it demonstrates that sequence-level rewards contain more information than is typically exploited.** The prevailing assumption in the process reward model literature is that sequence-level supervision is fundamentally insufficient for learning fine-grained token-level behaviors—that dense, per-step rewards are necessary to capture which tokens contribute to reasoning success. DelTA provides a counterpoint: the contrast between correct and incorrect responses, expressed through the token-gradient geometry, already encodes which token directions are discriminative. This information is latent in the standard RLVR update; DelTA merely surfaces it by sharpening the centroids. This does not render process reward models obsolete—dense rewards provide stronger and more direct supervision—but it does suggest that the gap between sequence-level and token-level supervision is narrower than previously assumed, at least for the purpose of deciding *which tokens to emphasize* in the policy gradient. Research that previously assumed dense rewards were necessary for token-level credit assignment may now explore whether self-supervised discriminative signals from the RLVR update itself can achieve comparable gains at lower cost.

**Research directions that become more attractive:**

- **Geometric analysis of RLVR training dynamics**: The discriminator view provides concrete objects to measure—centroid cosine similarity, margin distributions, token-level probability changes—that can be tracked over training to diagnose when and why RLVR succeeds or fails. This makes RLVR training more interpretable and debuggable.
- **Discriminative reweighting variants beyond DelTA**: The paper open-sources a specific implementation (centroid refinement with sigmoid assignment, bounded coefficients, self-normalization), but the design space is much larger. Different contrast functions (cosine similarity instead of squared Euclidean distance), different assignment mechanisms (softmax over batch instead of per-token sigmoid), and learned projections (training a small network to map token-gradient proxies to discriminative scores) are all natural extensions.
- **Integrating the discriminator view with process reward models**: Rather than treating discriminative reweighting and dense rewards as competing approaches, one could use a process reward model to provide a stronger discriminative signal (tokens with high per-step rewards are more likely to be discriminative) and combine it with DelTA's self-supervised coefficients for robustness.
- **Understanding the relationship between gradient geometry and reasoning quality**: The paper shows that DelTA maintains longer responses with lower entropy and higher reward (Figure 2). Investigating whether the centroid contrast direction `μ_+ - μ_-` correlates with specific reasoning behaviors (e.g., verification steps, backtracking, self-correction) could connect the geometric analysis to the cognitive science of reasoning.

**Research directions that become less attractive:**

- **Purely heuristic token filtering**: The paper shows that entropy-based filtering (forking tokens) is outperformed by DelTA (Table 1: 28.40 vs. 24.80 on 8B), and the Q1 analysis shows that within-side centrality filtering is actively harmful. This suggests that heuristic filtering without a discriminative criterion is a limited approach—future work on token selection should incorporate between-side contrast or accept that it will be outperformed by discriminative methods.
- **Uniform token weighting as a default**: The consistent gains of DelTA over DAPO across three model families, two task domains, and multiple benchmarks establish that uniform weighting is suboptimal in a way that can be systematically improved. New RLVR methods should not default to uniform token aggregation without justification; the burden of proof now shifts to showing that uniform weighting is sufficient for a given setting.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Directly measuring centroid geometry to validate the discriminator mechanism.** The paper's central claim—that DelTA improves RLVR by making the side-wise centroids more contrastive—is supported indirectly by the performance gains and by the failure of the within-side-only variant, but the centroids themselves are never measured. A direct follow-up would instrument the training loop to compute, at regular intervals: (1) the cosine similarity between `μ_+` and `μ_-` under DAPO vs. DelTA, predicting that DelTA's centroids are less similar (more contrastive); (2) the effective rank or explained variance of the centroid contrast direction `μ_+ - μ_-`, testing whether DelTA concentrates discriminative signal into fewer, more informative dimensions; (3) the per-token probability changes predicted by the local discriminator (Equation 4) vs. the actual probability changes observed after a full training step, quantifying how well the first-order approximation holds under clipping, multiple epochs, and Adam preconditioning. This experiment would use the same Qwen3-8B-Base + DeepMath-103K setup as the paper, adding instrumentation but no new hyperparameters. A positive result (DelTA centroids are measurably more contrastive, and the predicted probability changes correlate with observed changes) would validate the causal mechanism. A negative result (centroids are not more contrastive despite performance gains) would suggest that DelTA helps through a different mechanism—perhaps variance reduction or implicit regularization—and would redirect theoretical work.

**Difficulty-stratified analysis to test the "intermediate difficulty" prediction.** The discriminator view predicts that DelTA's benefit should be largest on problems of intermediate difficulty, where the policy produces a meaningful mix of correct and incorrect responses per batch, providing rich between-side contrast. On very easy problems (nearly always correct), the negative centroid has little mass and the discriminative signal is weak; on very hard problems (nearly always incorrect), the positive centroid is sparse. This is a falsifiable prediction that the paper does not test. A follow-up would: (1) bin the seven math benchmarks (or individual problems within them) by the base model's pass@1 rate into difficulty quintiles, following the methodology of the reference paper; (2) evaluate DelTA vs. DAPO separately within each quintile; (3) test the specific prediction that the DelTA-minus-DAPO gap is largest in quintiles 2–4 (intermediate difficulty) and smallest in quintiles 1 (easy) and 5 (hard). This experiment requires no new training—the existing Qwen3-8B-Base and Qwen3-14B-Base checkpoints can be evaluated with difficulty-stratified sampling. A positive result would provide strong mechanistic evidence for the discriminator view and give practitioners clear guidance on when to use DelTA. A negative result (uniform gains across difficulty) would suggest the mechanism is more complex than simple positive-negative contrast and might involve other factors (gradient noise reduction, exploration benefits from longer responses).

**Multi-seed training to quantify method variance vs. seed variance.** The paper's main results come from a single training run per method per model scale, which conflates method performance with random seed effects (initialization, data order, optimization noise). A follow-up would perform 3–5 training runs of DAPO, DelTA, and the strongest baseline (SAPO for 8B, FIPO for 14B) on Qwen3-8B-Base with the same DeepMath-103K dataset and identical hyperparameters, varying only the random seed. The key metric is the **rank consistency**—does DelTA outperform the baselines in every run, or is there crossover?—and the **gap-to-variance ratio**—is the average gap between methods larger than the within-method standard deviation? This experiment is expensive (3–5× the compute of the paper's main experiments, approximately 15–25 training runs at 220 steps each) but provides the critical missing information for practitioners deciding whether to adopt DelTA. If the method variance is large relative to the seed variance, the paper's claims are robust. If seed variance is comparable to or larger than the method gap, the single-run results are less informative and the community needs larger-scale replication.

**Combining DelTA with process reward models or learned value functions.** The paper explicitly positions DelTA as complementary to dense reward approaches: "Instead of relying on external dense rewards, value estimates, or auxiliary token-selection rules, DelTA reweights the RLVR surrogate using token coefficients derived from the positive-negative discriminator induced by the update itself" (Section 6). A natural follow-up tests whether DelTA's self-supervised discriminative coefficients provide gains *on top of* a process reward model or learned value function. The experiment would: (1) train a process reward model on DeepMath-103K using Monte Carlo rollout supervision (following the PRM training recipe from the reference paper, adapted to Qwen3 models); (2) use the PRM's per-step scores as token-level advantages in a DAPO-style training loop; (3) additionally apply DelTA's discriminative reweighting to the PRM-derived token-level losses; (4) compare PRM-only vs. DelTA-only vs. PRM+DelTA. The prediction—following the discriminator view—is that PRM+DelTA should outperform either alone, because the PRM provides a stronger base signal for *which* tokens matter, while DelTA sharpens the *contrast* between token-gradient directions on the two sides. A negative result (PRM+DelTA doesn't improve over PRM-only) would suggest that the discriminative signal DelTA extracts is largely redundant with the PRM's per-step scores, implying that DelTA is most useful specifically when dense rewards are unavailable.

**Extending DelTA to non-binary and continuous reward settings.** All of the paper's experiments use binary verifiable rewards (answer correct: 1, incorrect: 0). Many practical RLVR settings involve continuous or multi-level rewards—code generation with partial test-case credit, dialogue with preference scores, or tool-use with environment feedback. A follow-up would adapt DelTA's coefficient estimation to these settings and measure whether the gains persist. The natural extension replaces the hard positive/negative split with a **soft assignment** based on reward quantiles: partition each batch's responses into, say, quartiles by reward, define the positive centroid from the top quartile and the negative centroid from the bottom quartile, and compute discriminative scores using the same squared-distance margin and sigmoid assignment as in the original DelTA. Tokens from intermediate reward quartiles could be assigned a coefficient based on interpolation between the two centroids, or weighted by their reward relative to the batch distribution. The experiment would use a code generation dataset (Eurus2-RL-Code, already tested with binary rewards in Table 10) but modify the reward to be the fraction of test cases passed, and compare DelTA (with the quartile-based adaptation) vs. DAPO vs. the binary-reward DelTA baseline. A positive result would substantially broaden DelTA's applicability; a negative result would establish that the binary discriminative structure is load-bearing and that continuous-reward settings need different approaches.

**Probing the internal representations that drive the discriminative signal.** The paper uses a token-gradient proxy based on the final LM head hidden states and output probabilities. The top-K hidden-gradient proxy (Appendix F, Table 4) outperforms the default, suggesting that richer gradient information improves coefficient quality. A follow-up would systematically probe **which layers** of the transformer contribute the most discriminative signal. The experiment would: (1) compute token-gradient proxies from different layers—the input embedding layer, middle transformer blocks, and the final hidden state—each capturing gradient information from different stages of computation; (2) evaluate DelTA with each proxy independently; (3) test whether ensembling proxies from multiple layers (e.g., concatenating hidden states from layers 4, 8, 12, and the final layer) further improves performance. The prediction is that deeper layers (closer to the output) contribute more discriminative signal because they directly shape the token probabilities, but that intermediate layers may capture reasoning-specific patterns (e.g., attention to numerical values, intermediate computation results) that the final layer abstracts away. This experiment would use the Qwen3-8B-Base setup with the existing training recipe, varying only the proxy computation. The results would guide practitioners on the computational cost vs. discriminative signal tradeoff for proxy design and could reveal whether the discriminative structure is concentrated in the output layer (in which case the default proxy is near-optimal) or distributed across the network (in which case more expensive multi-layer proxies are warranted).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Training stronger reasoning models with the same compute budget.** The most direct application of DelTA is as a drop-in replacement for the standard DAPO (or GRPO) objective in existing RLVR training pipelines. On Qwen3-8B-Base trained with DeepMath-103K, DelTA achieves **28.40 average accuracy** across seven math benchmarks vs. **25.14 for SAPO** (the strongest baseline, Table 1)—a **3.26-point improvement** at the same training budget (220 steps, identical batch size and rollout configuration). The coefficient computation adds approximately **10.2% overhead** to the first training step (37 seconds out of ~363 seconds on 8×NVIDIA B200 GPUs, Appendix L.1), and this overhead shrinks as training progresses and response lengths grow (since rollout generation, not coefficient computation, dominates wall-clock time). For teams already running DAPO-style RLVR training on math or code tasks with binary verifiable rewards, switching to DelTA requires changing only the loss computation (replace the uniform token average with the self-normalized weighted surrogate) and adding the coefficient estimation pipeline (3 additional forward passes per rollout batch with K=1). No changes to the reward model, data pipeline, optimizer, or hyperparameter schedule are needed. The cost is the engineering effort to implement the proxy extraction, centroid refinement, and coefficient normalization—all of which use standard tensor operations (Euclidean distance, sigmoid, weighted averaging) and are well within the capabilities of existing RLVR frameworks like VeRL (Sheng et al., 2024), which the paper uses. The gain of 3.26 points on an 8B model is substantial enough to justify this engineering investment for most teams training reasoning models at scale.

**Improving sample efficiency for smaller models in cost-sensitive deployments.** The paper's results on Olmo3-7B-Base (Table 9: **22.80 vs. 19.01**, a **3.79-point gain**) and Qwen3-8B-Base (Table 1: **28.40 vs. 25.14 for SAPO**, a **3.26-point gain**) demonstrate that DelTA's benefit is not limited to a specific model family or the largest available scale. For organizations with limited compute budgets who must train smaller models (7B–8B parameters) and cannot afford the largest pretrained backbones, DelTA provides a way to extract more reasoning capability from the same training data and compute. The 3.26–3.79 average point improvement on math benchmarks represents a meaningful capability upgrade at zero additional training FLOPs (the coefficient overhead is in forward passes, not gradient computation). This is particularly relevant for on-device or edge deployment scenarios where model size is constrained by hardware (memory, latency) but training can be done in a datacenter with modest additional forward-pass cost. Moreover, the out-of-domain evaluation (Table 12) shows that the math-trained DelTA model transfers better to GPQA-Diamond and MMLU-Pro than the math-trained DAPO model (**+3.51 weighted average on 8B**), suggesting that the improved token-level credit assignment produces more general reasoning capabilities, not just math-specific improvements. For a team building a general-purpose small reasoning model, training with DelTA on math data may yield better transfer to non-math reasoning tasks than training with DAPO on the same data.

**Bootstrap self-improvement pipelines with better data generation.** Section 8 of the paper mentions "distilling the outputs of applying additional test-time compute back into the base LLM, enabling an iterative self-improvement loop" as a future direction. DelTA is directly applicable to such pipelines. In a typical self-improvement loop (e.g., ReST^EM, Singh et al., 2024; or the STaR approach, Zelikman et al., 2022), the model generates responses, correct responses are used as training targets, and the model is fine-tuned on these targets. DelTA can be integrated at **both** the RLVR training stage (to improve the policy that generates the self-improvement data) and the supervised fine-tuning stage (to reweight token-level losses based on discriminative value, though this would require adapting DelTA's coefficient estimation to work with supervised rather than RL losses). The paper's Q2 result—that training on only the top 50% of tokens by DelTA's coefficients outperforms training on all tokens (Figure 4)—is particularly relevant here: when generating self-improvement training data, one could use DelTA coefficients to filter which tokens from correct responses are used as targets, focusing the supervised loss on the tokens most characteristic of correct reasoning rather than all tokens (including formatting and boilerplate). This would produce cleaner training signals and potentially more robust self-improvement, especially in early iterations where the model's reasoning is fragile and correct responses are rare (the regime where DelTA's discriminative signal is strongest, per the difficulty-dependence prediction).

**Debugging and monitoring RLVR training runs.** Beyond performance gains, the discriminator view provides a set of **interpretable metrics** that practitioners can monitor during training to diagnose issues. The paper does not develop these metrics into a monitoring toolkit, but the objects are well-defined and computable with minor instrumentation: (1) **centroid cosine similarity** (`μ_+^T μ_- / (‖μ_+‖ ‖μ_-‖)`)—if this rises during training, the discriminator is weakening and the update direction is losing contrast; (2) **effective margin** (average `Δ^+_{i,t}` over positive-side tokens)—if this shrinks, token-gradient vectors from correct and incorrect responses are becoming harder to distinguish; (3) **coefficient entropy** (entropy of the `λ_{i,t}` distribution within a batch)—if this approaches zero, all tokens receive near-uniform weights and DelTA is not providing discriminative reweighting, which could signal that the policy is too strong (all responses correct in each batch, no negative side) or too weak (few correct responses, sparse positive side). These metrics require no additional forward passes beyond what DelTA already computes for coefficient estimation, and they can be logged per-step alongside reward and response length. For teams running expensive RLVR training runs (often thousands of GPU-hours), these metrics provide early warning of training plateaus or degradation—if centroid similarity is rising while reward is flat, the discriminator may be collapsing, and interventions (adjusting the batch composition to ensure a mix of correct and incorrect responses, increasing the coefficient range, or switching to a stronger baseline) could be applied before wasting additional compute. The paper's Figure 2 shows that DAPO's reward plateaus around step 100 while response length declines and entropy rises; these existing metrics diagnose that *something is wrong*, but centroid-based metrics would diagnose *why*—specifically, whether the update direction is being pulled toward shared background structure.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate a formal decision rule or tradeoff matrix against named alternatives. DelTA is positioned as a modification to the DAPO surrogate that can be applied whenever DAPO-style RLVR is used—there is no case made for *not* using DelTA, and no explicit comparison against process reward models or other credit assignment paradigms in terms of when each is preferable. The paper's ablation studies establish that certain design variants (within-side-only weighting, raw coefficient scores, hard assignment) perform worse than DelTA, but these are variants of DelTA itself, not competing credit assignment methods in a broader landscape. The paper also does not claim that DelTA is universally superior—the limitations section (Appendix A) acknowledges restricted task validation and the need for richer gradient proxies—but does not specify conditions under which a practitioner should choose an alternative method.

Given this, a forced decision matrix would be speculative rather than grounded in the paper's explicit claims and evidence. The paper's contribution is better understood as establishing the discriminator view and demonstrating one effective implementation (DelTA) of its design principle, rather than as providing a comprehensive comparison framework for credit assignment methods.
