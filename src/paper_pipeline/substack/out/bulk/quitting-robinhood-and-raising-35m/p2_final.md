Here are the takeaways from this Jayendra Jog interview (went from jaded Robinhood engineer coasting through promotions to founding Sei Labs, a $1B+ crypto startup):

**The Facebook internship lottery**

Jay’s career began with a stroke of luck he traces to a single Friday night. As a UCLA sophomore, he went into “hermit mode” grinding for the brand-name internships his social circle had deemed the cream of the crop: Facebook and Google. A friend organized “LeetCode and Chill,” where CS students solved problems together. One was the classic “number of islands” graph problem. “That ended up being the exact question I got during my onsite Facebook internship interview. If I had not gone to that event, I would not have gotten my Facebook internship. Without that Facebook internship, I don’t think any of the things that happened afterward in my career would have materialized.”

He frames his entire trajectory around **step-function improvements**—“life is not linear for anyone… there are ups, but they tend to be step function improvements versus just linear growth.” The internship itself, however, was impersonal and not fun. Facebook felt too large, which steered him toward a smaller-company internship at Pinterest the next summer—a much better experience because of the tighter intern class and San Francisco culture (he’s still “super close” with four or five people from that class, versus almost no one from Facebook). Ryan notes the early demonstration of following the social consensus on prestige: “What you value is a function of what other people around you value and are talking about.” Had Jay attended a different college where high-frequency trading shops were the topic, his path might have looked entirely different.

**Joining Robinhood (Series C on paper, Series D in reality)**

Graduating in 2018 with options to return to Pinterest, pursue a UCLA fifth-year master’s, or go elsewhere, Jay optimized explicitly for **growth over compensation**—“I didn’t care as much about money as I did about growth, both from the learning standpoint and also from the career growth standpoint.” At the Greylock Tech Fair (where they rent out Oracle Arena and invite interns), Robinhood’s line was the longest—a couple hundred people waiting. Despite knowing nothing about trading or managing a brokerage account, he was drawn to the team. He signed when Robinhood was Series C (~100 people). By the time he started, it was Series D; headcount had roughly doubled, and the office still had people working out of standing desks in a literal house across the street—bedrooms converted to workspaces.

Ryan asks whether big tech is the right first move. Jay offers a more nuanced view than “pick small companies”: he’d already gotten the résumé stamp from Facebook and Pinterest. His real heuristic: **surround yourself with smart people**. “Probably the biggest thing you can control is the kind of person you’re going to be working with, because you don’t know what the career prospects are going to be for any startup.” Robinhood validated that rule—around a third of his new-grad cohort went on to raise money and start venture-backed companies, an astronomically high ratio. “When you’re at a company that’s scaling quickly, you run into scaling problems, and you get exposed to a lot more stuff.”

**Getting jaded in nine months—and the golden handcuffs**

The career-growth story soured quickly. His first performance review happened at nine months (he’d missed the three-month cutoff by a week or two), and by then he’d been working brutally hard: multiple 2:00 AM nights and an on-call rotation centered on an “overnight batch” process for trade settlement after market close. The batch system was fragile and rife with tech debt; a deployment introducing bugs could trigger cascading failures, and any job failure required paging the author, fixing the code, and restarting the chain. “There were several times it went to like 5:00 AM, and it was dangerously close to causing a business issue for Robinhood.” He earned a top rating—five out of five, atypical for anyone. But “there was nothing else tied to that. No promotion, no compensation change. I was just like, why did I do this?” At 22, the tradeoff between effort and reward felt broken. He resolved to check out and “go through the motions of playing the game,” becoming deeply jaded about the big-tech career ladder.

Why stay nearly three more years? Two reasons. A death in the family made leaving emotionally harder. And Robinhood’s equity created a practical trap: exercising his options would have cost $400,000, money he didn’t have. His strategy became waiting for the IPO, which arrived in July 2021, removing the handcuffs. Financially, this was a windfall—“an order of magnitude higher” than three-and-a-half years at Google or Facebook. He also acknowledges the luck: his 2018 graduating class hit a historically favorable IPO window; graduates just two years later faced a much colder market.

**Coasting into promotions through domain expertise**

Even while emotionally disengaged, Jay still got promoted in his second performance cycle and was up for another before leaving. He attributes this almost entirely to **becoming a load-bearing domain expert by default** in a hyper-growth environment. Robinhood grew 10x after he joined, and he quickly became “the only person that knew how to do like 10 different things.” When the team and the work are both expanding rapidly, mere competence at the things nobody else understands generates promotion packets almost automatically. He was given mentoring responsibility (his first mentee at ~1.5 years in, something that might take much longer at a larger company) and an engineering-lead scope simply because he was the person who understood multiple systems.

Ryan crystallizes the through-line: Jay sought talented people and high-growth environments, and promotions followed—“even though you weren’t really trying for that.” Jay confirms: “I was putting in the bare minimum to make sure that I was close to average, then spending my time elsewhere.”

**GameStop from the inside: the infrastructure you never see**

January 28, 2021—the day Robinhood turned off buys on GameStop and other meme stocks—Jay woke up to a firestorm. Friends and acquaintances financially invested in the squeeze were messaging him: “What the hell is going on?” The public narrative assumed market manipulation, but the reality was a **T+2 settlement liquidity crisis**: trades took two days to settle, and during that window Robinhood had to post collateral for all the positions being opened. On January 28th, that meant billions of dollars they didn’t have. “Normal people don’t know about any of that stuff. It exists because of the ramifications of the traditional financial system that was built 40 years ago.”

This moment directly seeded the idea for Sei Labs. The archaic plumbing of finance—and the powerlessness of being an insider who couldn’t change it—convinced him that “we will need much better financial rails in the future.” He acknowledges Robinhood may have deliberately miscommunicated during the crisis because they were simultaneously trying to raise billions to survive; they were “fighting for their company’s existence,” and the comms suffered. “All things considered, they navigated it in an okay way, but they could have communicated to the public a lot better.”

**Leaving to start something—and why the risk is overblown**

After the 2019 promotion disappointment, Jay and his co-founder began nights-and-weekends building an AWS cost-management startup. It targeted companies that needed to cut cloud spend—customers who were atrophying, a horrible market. “Honestly, thank God for that because I don’t think I would have liked spending several years working on that.” In 2021, they pivoted to building a decentralized Robinhood analogue, reasoning that decentralized infrastructure could prevent a GameStop-style collateral crisis from recurring. That initial exchange concept evolved into building the underlying infrastructure, which became Sei. With the IPO behind him and his four-year grant winding down, his bar for leaving was low: “Financially, there wasn’t much downside.”

When Ryan asks whether a normal compensation scenario would have produced the same conviction, Jay says “it would have been even easier to make that decision.” His core argument: **software engineers wildly overestimate the risk of leaving**. “Most of the time, if you’re competent, things will work out anyway. I don’t know too many people that are good at their jobs at company A that are not able to find any work again after that.” Even a failed startup makes you *more* valuable to early-stage companies—you pick up product validation, marketing, go-to-market, and business development skills that a pure engineer never touches. Ryan probes the bear case (no PMF, no marketable milestone), and Jay concedes that big companies won’t give much credit for a failed exploration, but seed and pre-seed startups will actively prefer a founder-turned-engineer for the zero-to-one muscle. It biases your career smaller, but “that’s also because that’s the environment those founders want to find themselves in.”

**Raising $35M during a crypto winter**

Jay started raising Sei’s seed round three weeks after the Terra blockchain collapsed—one of the worst times in crypto history to fundraise. “We learned a lot about rejection, obviously, because most investors said no to us.” He describes the pattern: investors initially skeptical, staying on the sidelines and offering delay-tactic responses (“we’re still doing diligence”) to preserve optionality. The dynamics flip when a single top-tier lead commits. For Sei, that was **Multicoin**, the largest Solana investor and a top-five-to-ten crypto fund. Once Multicoin invested (a $5M seed round; strategic rounds later brought the total to $35M), “everything else is super easy after that.” The stamp of approval cascades into BD, future fundraising, and launch support.

He also draws a distinction: crypto fundraising is **not ARR-driven like web2**. “In crypto, it’s much more about how much excitement there is around this project and how many teams are actually building with this.” It’s reminiscent of 1995–1997 internet investing, when alternate metrics like eyeballs substituted for revenue. In a field where the core product may be the blockchain itself, fundamental financial metrics simply don’t apply in the same way.

**The crypto thesis: verifiability for money**

Ryan asks the skeptical-engineer question directly: with all the grift, what’s the genuine value? Jay’s response grounds crypto in the same adolescent-phase pattern as the early internet: the mid-‘90s web’s largest use case was pornography, yet the internet transformed society. “It does take time, and there tends to be a lot of grift in any new industry getting started.” Regulatory clarity and social policing are gradually squeezing out the worst actors.

His economic thesis is narrower than “crypto changes everything”: **the only use case that justifies the cost of a distributed ledger is finance**, because verifiability matters when money is at stake. Crypto already excels at two things—payments and trading—and stablecoins are driving real adoption for international remittances. In countries with 30%-inflation currencies or capital controls that block dollar access, holding USDC/USDT is a practical solution. The endgame: “some version of a completely decentralized Wall Street that will be globally accessible, allowing anyone to trade and make use of it.” The verifiability that crypto offers enables computation between parties who don’t trust each other, unlocking financial use cases worldwide.

**Startup growth vs. big-tech scaling problems**

Since 2022, Jay says he’s learned more every single year than he did during any year working for someone else—and the steepest growth was non-engineering. “The engineering side of things is generally pretty easy. Most businesses are solving pretty mundane problems. If you’re a competent L4, then you can do perfectly fine as a founder from any kind of engineering side.” His team has grown from 5–7 people in 2022 to roughly 60 across entities, a deliberately slow pace. “If you grow slowly, it allows you to maintain the culture and prevents the scaling issues you see with bureaucracy and people being clueless about what to do.”

**“Don’t be so scared”**

The advice Jay would give his graduating self: “Pull the trigger quickly.” He’s naturally risk-averse, and in retrospect the hesitation around leaving Robinhood was unnecessary. In an ideal world, he’d have left earlier—though he immediately adds that earlier departure might have locked him into the cloud-cost startup instead of crypto, so the timing ended up right. The synthesis is a balanced one: think through decisions carefully, but once the thinking is done, the fear is largely an illusion. If you know what you want to build, even as a new grad, “I think you should just go for it.”

---

His **step-function improvements** lens—where career leaps trace to single events like a LeetCode and Chill session or a Multicoin lead—shapes everything from his internship lottery story to his fundraising playbook. That pattern feeds directly into his conviction that **software engineers wildly overestimate the risk of leaving**: the downside is mostly illusory, and the real step-function upside lives outside the big-tech ladder. His own arc—becoming a **load-bearing domain expert by default** while emotionally checked out—underscores how much career velocity in hyper-growth environments is about being competent in the right room, not about grinding for the next rating. The through-line is a career philosophy that treats fear as the main bottleneck and smart, high-growth peers as the only reliable compass.
