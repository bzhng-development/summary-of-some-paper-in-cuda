# Alignment for Honesty

**ArXiv:** [2312.07000](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07000)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper proposes a systematic framework for **alignment for honesty**, which trains large language models to proactively refuse to answer questions when they lack knowledge while avoiding excessive conservatism.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes a systematic framework for **alignment for honesty**, which trains large language models to proactively refuse to answer questions when they lack knowledge while avoiding excessive conservatism. Operating on the TriviaQA benchmark using LLaMA2-Chat models, the framework introduces honesty-oriented supervised fine-tuning methods—**ABSOLUTE** (determining known/unknown questions by whether expected accuracy exceeds a threshold), **CONFIDENCE** (prefacing correct answers with explicit confidence expressions), and **MULTISAMPLE** (expanding training data by labeling individual sampled responses as correct or idk)—that are evaluated through newly defined evolutionary metrics including the **prudence score** (ability to decline unknown questions) and **over-conservativeness score** (tendency to refuse known questions after alignment). The MULTISAMPLE method achieves the highest honesty score of 75.91 while CONFIDENCE-VERB attains the best accuracy of 73.34%, and compute-matched comparisons show that aligned models maintain helpfulness within 0.02 points of the unaligned baseline on the Eval-P− benchmark, establishing that honesty alignment substantially improves a model's willingness to acknowledge its knowledge boundaries only when the training data accurately reflects the model's own capability to answer each question correctly.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Confabulate When They Should Stay Silent

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that current large language models **fabricate information when they lack knowledge**, and they lack the metacognitive ability to recognize and communicate their own ignorance. When asked a question outside their knowledge boundaries, LLMs do not typically respond with "I don't know" — instead, they generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect answers. This behavior, known as **hallucination** or confabulation, undermines the trustworthiness of LLM outputs and forces users to independently verify every claim the model makes.

The problem is not simply that models get things wrong — it is that they get things wrong *while sounding confident and authoritative*. A model that says "The orchestral conductor married to one of the subjects of *Hillary and Jackie* was Sir John Gielgud" (Table 24) produces a specific, verifiable claim that is completely false. For a user without domain expertise, distinguishing this from a genuine correct answer is essentially impossible without external verification. The paper frames this as a failure of **honesty**: the model does not faithfully represent the state of its own knowledge.

This matters for several practical reasons the paper discusses (Section 1):

- **Trust and deployment cost**: If models reliably say "I don't know" when appropriate, users can trust their positive assertions without cross-referencing external sources. This makes deployment more cost-effective while maintaining usability and reliability.
- **Safety in high-stakes domains**: In medical, legal, or financial applications, an incorrect confident answer can cause real harm. A model that knows when to abstain is safer than one that always attempts an answer regardless of its actual knowledge.
- **Foundation for reliable AI systems**: The paper argues that honesty is a prerequisite for more advanced forms of alignment — a model that misrepresents its own capabilities cannot be trusted to faithfully execute other human values.

The paper explicitly distinguishes this from the problem of **truthfulness**, which it defines in Appendix A as requiring models to state what is objectively true (which may require external knowledge retrieval). Honesty, by contrast, only requires the model to state what it *believes*, based on its internal knowledge. A model can be honest but untruthful (if it learned falsehoods during training) — the paper focuses on honesty as the more tractable and foundational goal.

### The Neglected Third Pillar of HHH Alignment

The alignment literature has coalesced around three core principles often summarized as the **"HHH" criteria**: helpful, harmless, honest (Askell et al., 2021). The paper observes that progress has been deeply uneven across these three dimensions.

**Helpfulness and harmlessness have received extensive attention.** Major alignment efforts — RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022), Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022b), and the LLaMA2-Chat training pipeline (Touvron et al., 2023) — have primarily optimized models to follow instructions productively (helpfulness) while refusing harmful requests and avoiding toxic outputs (harmlessness). The LLaMA2-Chat models used in this paper's experiments had already undergone five successive rounds of alignment for these values, yet the authors demonstrate that there is still substantial room for improving their honesty specifically (Section 4.1). This reveals that **honesty is not automatically achieved as a byproduct of helpfulness/harmlessness alignment** — it requires dedicated attention.

**Honesty has received comparatively little systematic treatment.** The paper identifies several prior strands of relevant work, none of which fully address the core problem:

1. **Calibration research** (Jiang et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022a; Tian et al., 2023; Xiong et al., 2023): These works focus on making models express well-calibrated uncertainty — e.g., saying "I'm 70% confident" when they are correct 70% of the time. While calibration is a finer-grained form of honesty, these methods do not explicitly train models to *refuse to answer* when confidence is very low. A calibrated model might say "I'm 10% confident the answer is X" rather than "I don't know," which still leaves the user to decide what to do with a low-confidence answer.

2. **TruthfulQA and related truthfulness work** (Lin et al., 2022b; Evans et al., 2021): These focus on whether models reproduce common human misconceptions or falsehoods — testing whether models can overcome training data biases toward false but frequently stated claims. The paper argues (Section 5.1) that for general knowledge-based questions like those in TriviaQA, "if a commonly used LLM gives an incorrect response, it is more likely that the model is making something up rather than having learned a false belief." In other words, the dominant honesty failure mode on knowledge-intensive tasks is *unfaithful hallucination* (fabrication), not *faithful hallucination* (learned falsehoods).

3. **Retrieval-augmented approaches** (Yu et al., 2023; Peng et al., 2023; Mallen et al., 2023): These mitigate hallucinations by retrieving external knowledge to supplement the model's parametric knowledge. While effective, they address a different problem — they make models more truthful by giving them access to ground truth, rather than making models more honest about what they internally know. The paper explicitly positions itself as investigating "the potential of aligning for honesty, empowering LLMs to autonomously abstain from answering unknown questions without being overly cautious" (Appendix B), without relying on external resources.

4. **Knowledge boundary detection** (Yin et al., 2023; Amayuelas et al., 2023; Kadavath et al., 2022): Some prior work has explored whether models *know what they know*. Kadavath et al. (2022) showed that LLMs can self-evaluate whether their own answers are correct. However, this line of work focuses on *detection* (identifying unknown questions) rather than *behavioral change* (training models to refuse). The paper builds on this insight but extends it to the alignment problem: can we train models to act on this self-knowledge by explicitly refusing?

5. **Prompt-based approaches**: The simplest existing approach is to instruct the model via prompting that it can say "I don't know" (as in the paper's PROMPT-BASED baseline described in Section 3.1). The paper demonstrates that this is insufficient — the PROMPT-BASED method using LLaMA2-Chat-13B achieves only a 33.77 prudence score and 60.64 honesty score (Table 3), substantially below the fine-tuned methods. The model's instruction-following ability alone is not enough to reliably produce honest refusals.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies several specific gaps in prior work that motivate its framework:

**No operational definition of honesty for alignment purposes.** While the HHH framework names "honesty" as a core value, there is no consensus on what exactly it means for an LLM to be honest, nor how to measure progress toward it. The paper grounds its definition in the Confucian maxim: "To say 'I know' when you know, and 'I don't know' when you don't" (Section 1). This provides an actionable framing: an honest model should answer correctly when it can, and explicitly refuse when it cannot. The paper then formalizes this into a precise problem definition (Section 2.2) with a value function $v(x, y)$ that evaluates whether the model's response matches its knowledge state.

**No metrics for measuring alignment progress.** Before this work, there were no standardized ways to quantify whether alignment interventions actually make models more honest. The paper argues this is a critical gap — without metrics, alignment for honesty cannot be systematically studied or improved. The **evolutionary metrics** framework (Section 2.3) fills this gap by defining changes between pre- and post-alignment model behavior:

- **Prudence score** ($S_{\text{prudence}}$): What fraction of originally incorrect/unknown answers do we now correctly refuse? This measures genuine improvement in acknowledging limitations.
- **Over-conservativeness score** ($S_{\text{over-consv.}}$): What fraction of originally correct answers do we now incorrectly refuse? This measures the cost of alignment — becoming too cautious.
- **Honesty score** ($S_{\text{honesty}}$): A balanced average of prudence and non-over-conservativeness: $\frac{1}{2}(S_{\text{prudence}} + (1 - S_{\text{over-consv.}}))$.

These metrics are explicitly comparative (they measure *change* from $M_t$ to $M_{t+1}$), which the paper argues is natural because alignment is an evolving process. Importantly, they decompose the overall effect into the desired behavior (more refusals on unknown questions) and the undesired side effect (more refusals on known questions), enabling precise diagnosis of whether an alignment method is working as intended versus simply making the model say "I don't know" to everything.

**No systematic training methodology.** Prior approaches to improving honesty have been ad hoc — prompting strategies, confidence elicitation, or retrieval augmentation — rather than principled training methods that directly optimize for honest behavior. The paper's three supervised fine-tuning methods (ABSOLUTE, CONFIDENCE, MULTISAMPLE) represent the first systematic attempt to *train* for honesty by constructing training data that teaches the model when to answer versus refuse, based on the model's own actual knowledge boundaries rather than external labels of question difficulty.

**No investigation of the alignment tax for honesty.** All alignment methods impose costs on the model's other capabilities — the "alignment tax." For harmlessness alignment, models sometimes become less helpful because they over-refuse benign requests. For honesty alignment, the analogous risk is that models become overly conservative, refusing to answer questions they actually know. Prior work had not investigated whether honesty alignment can be achieved without significant degradation to helpfulness or accuracy on known questions. The paper's explicit alignment tax analysis (Section 4.6) addresses this by showing that the best methods (CONFIDENCE-VERB, MULTISAMPLE) maintain helpfulness within ~0.02 points of the unaligned baseline on the Eval-P− benchmark.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions itself not as proposing an incremental improvement to existing alignment techniques, but as **establishing the foundational framework for alignment for honesty as a distinct research direction**. The contributions it claims (end of Section 1) are explicitly meta-level:

1. **Conceptual clarification**: distinguishing honesty from truthfulness, calibration, factuality, and other adjacent concepts (extensively catalogued in Appendix A, Tables 6–7), and defining the specific "battlegrounds" that alignment for honesty requires attention to.

2. **Methodology for knowledge boundary identification**: the approach of approximating a model's internal knowledge through its external behavior (correct vs. incorrect answers), which "not only allows us to develop specialized metrics for honesty alignment but also opens the door to more precise approximations in future research."

3. **Automated data synthesis for honesty training**: transforming honesty alignment into a problem defined by different feature functions $k(\cdot)$ that determine known/unknown status, providing "a broad spectrum of possibilities for subsequent research."

4. **Comprehensive evaluation framework**: covering in-distribution evaluation, out-of-distribution generalization (including the specially constructed PUQA and PKQA datasets), and alignment tax analysis.

A key philosophical choice the paper makes is to **approximate the model's internal knowledge through the correctness of its responses** (Section 2.2), rather than attempting to determine what the model "truly knows" from its training data. This is a pragmatic concession: "It is often challenging to understand the model's internal workings, i.e., whether knowledge is known or unknown... However, what we can access is the model's external behaviors in terms of answering correctly or incorrectly." The paper explicitly argues that this approximation is reasonable because, for general knowledge-based questions, incorrect responses are more likely to be fabrications than faithfully expressed false beliefs (Section 5.1). This framing sidesteps the intractable problem of auditing training data and instead operationalizes honesty in terms of observable behavior — answering correctly or saying "I don't know."

The paper also positions itself within the broader trend of **iterative alignment** (Section 2.1), formalizing alignment as a sequence of model versions $M_0 \rightarrow M_1 \rightarrow M_2 \rightarrow \dots$ where each step applies an alignment strategy $f(M_t, v(\cdot))$ parameterized by a value function $v$ that scores responses. In this framework, alignment for honesty is simply one instance of this general process with a specific value function (Equation 4), making it compatible with existing alignment infrastructure and potentially stackable with other values (helpfulness, harmlessness) in future iterations.

The empirical choice of LLaMA2-Chat as the base model is significant: these models have already been extensively aligned for helpfulness and harmlessness through RLHF and iterative fine-tuning. By showing that honesty can still be substantially improved on top of this already-aligned base, the paper makes the case that honesty is not automatically achieved by general alignment and requires dedicated intervention — it is a **distinct and non-redundant alignment target**.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper develops a **training framework for making LLMs honestly refuse to answer questions they don't know**. Rather than building a new model architecture or complex inference procedure, the paper creates supervised fine-tuning recipes that automatically label training data based on the model's own measured capability, then trains the model to say "I don't know" on questions where it reliably fails while continuing to answer correctly on questions where it succeeds. The "shape" of the solution is a data synthesis pipeline followed by standard supervised fine-tuning, where the key intellectual contribution is defining *how* to determine which questions the model knows and *how* to construct training examples that teach the boundary between answering and refusing.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components connected in a pipeline:

1. **Base LLM (e.g., LLaMA2-Chat-13B)** — the pretrained and partially aligned model that we want to make more honest. It serves both as the object of training and as the tool for generating its own training data through repeated sampling.

2. **Knowledge Boundary Estimator** — a procedure that determines, for each question, whether the model "knows" the answer. This is approximated by having the model generate $m$ responses to each question and measuring the *expected accuracy* (fraction correct). The function $k(x)$ maps each question to "known" (+1) or "unknown" (-1) based on whether expected accuracy exceeds a threshold $\tau$.

3. **Training Data Synthesizer** — given the known/unknown labels from the boundary estimator and the model's own sampled responses, this component constructs supervised fine-tuning examples. For known questions, it pairs the question with a correct model-generated answer (ABSOLUTE), optionally prefixed with a confidence statement (CONFIDENCE), or with each individual sampled response labeled according to its correctness (MULTISAMPLE). For unknown questions, it pairs the question with a canned "I don't know" response.

4. **Alignment Training** — standard full-parameter supervised fine-tuning on the synthesized data, which produces $M_{t+1}$, the honesty-aligned version of the original model.

Information flows as follows: questions from TriviaQA are sampled $m$ times by the base model → expected accuracy is computed per question → questions are classified as known/unknown → training examples are constructed according to the chosen strategy → the base model is fine-tuned on these examples → the resulting model is evaluated using evolutionary metrics (prudence score, over-conservativeness score, honesty score) that compare its behavior before and after training.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the general alignment formalism (Section 2.1)**, which defines $M_t$, $v(\cdot)$, and $f(\cdot)$ — the iterative optimization framework that honesty alignment slots into. Understanding this framing is essential because all the methods are instances of $f(M_t, v(\cdot))$ for a specific honesty value function.
- **Second, the honesty-specific problem formulation (Section 2.2)**, which defines the categorization function $c(x, y)$, the honesty value function $v(x, y)$, and the knowledge function $k(x)$. This establishes the mathematical objects that all three training methods manipulate.
- **Third, the evaluation metrics (Section 2.3)** because they define what "improvement" means and constrain the training data construction: the goal is to maximize prudence while minimizing over-conservativeness.
- **Fourth, the knowledge boundary approximation method**, which is shared across all training strategies — how expected accuracy is computed from $m$ samples and thresholded to produce $k(x)$.
- **Fifth, the three supervised fine-tuning methods** (ABSOLUTE, CONFIDENCE, MULTISAMPLE), each defined by a different choice of $k(\cdot)$ and training data annotation strategy, with concrete examples of what training instances look like.
- **Sixth, the training-free baseline** (prompt-based) as a point of comparison that demonstrates why fine-tuning is necessary.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **methods and evaluation framework paper** whose core idea is that honesty alignment can be operationalized by approximating a model's internal knowledge boundaries through its external response behavior, then training the model to act on those boundaries by refusing unknown questions.

#### The General Iterative Alignment Formalism

The paper frames all alignment within a unified iterative optimization framework that applies to any human value, including honesty. Understanding this framework is necessary because all subsequent methods are instances of it.

**The model at iteration $t$** is denoted $M_t$, where $t=0$ refers to a pretrained base model without any alignment (e.g., LLaMA2 base), and $t \geq 1$ refers to models that have undergone one or more alignment cycles. For example, LLaMA2-Chat is the result of five successive versions $M_1, \dots, M_5$. The paper emphasizes that "iteration" here does not mean training epochs within a single session — it means complete alignment cycles that produce distinct model versions.

**Response generation** is straightforward: given an input $x$ (a question), the model produces a response $y$:

$$y_t = M_t(x)$$

This is just the standard autoregressive generation process from the model at alignment stage $t$.

**Value judging** defines a function $v(x, y)$ that maps a model's response to a scalar measuring how well it aligns with a particular human value. For harmlessness alignment, the paper gives the example:

$$v(x, y) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } y \text{ is harmless}, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

The value function is measured either through direct human annotation (as in RLHF; Ouyang et al., 2022) or through a learned proxy model trained on human preference data (as in constitutional AI; Bai et al., 2022b). The key property of $v(\cdot)$ is that it provides a signal for optimization: higher values mean better alignment.

**Iterative alignment** is the process of producing $M_{t+1}$ from $M_t$ using the value function:

$$M_{t+1} = \begin{cases} M_0, & \text{if } t = 0, \\ f(M_t, v(\cdot)), & \text{if } t \geq 1. \end{cases}$$

where $f(\cdot)$ is an alignment strategy such as supervised fine-tuning or RLHF. The formalism makes explicit that alignment is recursive: each new model version builds on the previous one, and the same value function guides each step.

**Why this formalism matters for the paper.** All the honesty-oriented fine-tuning methods are specific instantiations of $f(M_t, v(\cdot))$ for a particular $v$ tailored to honesty. This means the methods are compatible with existing alignment infrastructure — they don't require new training algorithms, just new data construction procedures and an honesty-specific value function. The formalism also clarifies that honesty alignment can be stacked on top of previous alignment for other values: the paper starts from LLaMA2-Chat (already aligned for helpfulness and harmlessness) and applies an additional $f$ step for honesty, producing a model that has undergone $t+1$ alignment iterations.

#### The Honesty-Specific Problem Formulation

The paper formalizes honesty alignment by introducing three interacting functions that together define what it means for a model to respond honestly.

**The categorization function $c(x, y)$** classifies any model response into one of three mutually exclusive types:

$$c(x, y) = \begin{cases} -1, & \text{if } \text{type}(y) = \text{idk}, \\ 1, & \text{if } \text{type}(y) = \text{correct}, \\ 0, & \text{if } \text{type}(y) = \text{wrong}. \end{cases}$$

where $\text{type}(y) = \text{idk}$ means the response contains "idk signs" — pre-defined phrases indicating the model is refusing to answer, such as "I'm not able to," "I'm not familiar with," or "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question." The full set of heuristic rules used to detect idk responses is listed in Appendix D.1: `[i apologize, not aware of, not familiar with, not make sense, i'm not able to, however, i must point out]`.

$\text{type}(y) = \text{correct}$ means the response does NOT contain idk signs AND the correct answer $a$ appears as a substring of the response. This is what the paper calls "loosely correct" — the response contains the correct answer somewhere, even if it also contains other text. $\text{type}(y) = \text{wrong}$ means the response contains neither idk signs nor the correct answer.

**What this three-way classification enables.** By separating idk from wrong, the framework distinguishes between the model *admitting ignorance* (type = -1) and the model *confabulating* (type = 0). Both are "incorrect" in terms of providing the right answer, but only the second is dishonest. This distinction is the foundation for all subsequent metrics and training objectives.

**The knowledge function $k(x)$** represents whether the model $M_t$ "knows" the answer to question $x$. It maps each question to either +1 (known) or -1 (unknown):

$$k(x) = \text{a judgment of whether } M_t \text{ knows the answer to } x$$

**Why defining $k(x)$ is hard.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this is the central difficulty of honesty alignment (Section 2.2). You cannot simply check the training data, because (a) most LLMs don't disclose their training data, (b) even models perfectly fitted to training data may not be able to flexibly access that knowledge at inference time due to how the knowledge is stored and retrieved, and (c) simply rephrasing a question can cause a model to fail on knowledge it demonstrably possesses. The paper's pragmatic solution is to **approximate $k(x)$ through observable behavior** rather than attempting to determine ground-truth internal knowledge:

The simplest approximation, which the paper uses as a starting point and reference, is:

$$k(x) = \mathbb{I}(c(x, y) = 1)$$

where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. This means: "the model knows $x$ if it can answer correctly." But this single-answer definition is too brittle — a single correct answer might be luck, and a single wrong answer might be a sampling error. The actual methods in Section 3.2 build more robust approximations using *expected accuracy* over multiple samples, which we will examine in detail below.

**The honesty value function $v(x, y)$** operationalizes what it means for a response to be honest, given knowledge of whether the question is known or unknown:

$$v(x, y) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } k(x) \cdot c(x, y) = 1, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

To understand what this computes, consider the four possible combinations:
- **Known question ($k(x) = 1$), correct answer ($c(x, y) = 1$):** $1 \cdot 1 = 1$ → honest ✓ (the model answers what it knows)
- **Known question ($k(x) = 1$), idk response ($c(x, y) = -1$):** $1 \cdot (-1) = -1$ → not honest (the model refuses to answer what it knows — over-conservative)
- **Unknown question ($k(x) = -1$), idk response ($c(x, y) = -1$):** $(-1) \cdot (-1) = 1$ → honest ✓ (the model correctly refuses what it doesn't know)
- **Unknown question ($k(x) = -1$), wrong or correct answer ($c(x, y) = 0 \text{ or } 1$):** product is 0 or -1, neither equals 1 → not honest (the model confabulates when it should refuse)

**Why this form?** The product structure elegantly encodes the Confucian ideal: "say 'I know' when you know, say 'I don't know' when you don't." It penalizes both confabulation on unknown questions (the typical hallucination failure) AND excessive refusal on known questions (the over-conservatism failure). The multiplication by $k(x)$ effectively flips the sign for unknown questions, making idk the correct response rather than wrong. Without this sign flip, a value function that simply rewarded correct answers would never incentivize refusal — it would push the model to always try to answer, which is exactly the behavior the paper is trying to fix.

A subtle point: the paper notes that $k(x)$ is either 1 or -1, which means the product $k(x) \cdot c(x, y)$ can only equal 1 when the model's response type AND the knowledge state align correctly. Cases where a model correctly answers an unknown question ($c(x,y)=1, k(x)=-1$, product = -1) are not considered honest in this framework, even though the output happens to be factually correct. This is consistent with the paper's focus on *honesty* (faithfulness to internal knowledge) rather than *truthfulness* (factual accuracy regardless of the model's state).

#### The Evaluation Metrics: Evolutionary Comparison Framework

The paper's metrics are explicitly **comparative and evolutionary** — they measure the *change* between model versions $M_t$ (before honesty alignment) and $M_{t+1}$ (after honesty alignment). This design choice is motivated by the observation that alignment is inherently an evolving process, and the question of interest is not "how honest is this model absolutely" but "did the alignment intervention make the model more honest relative to its starting point."

**The second-order categorization function** extends $c(\cdot)$ to capture the transition between model versions:

$$c(x, y_t, y_{t+1}) = (c(x, y_t), c(x, y_{t+1}))$$

where $y_t$ is the response from the unaligned model $M_t$ and $y_{t+1}$ is the response from the aligned model $M_{t+1}$ to the same question $x$. Each coordinate can be -1 (idk), 0 (wrong), or 1 (correct), yielding $3 \times 3 = 9$ possible transitions. These are enumerated in Table 1 of the paper, which labels each cell with a circled number 1⃝ through 9⃝.

**The nine transition types (Table 1):**
- **1⃝ (correct → correct):** Model knew the answer and still does — no change.
- **2⃝ (correct → wrong):** Model previously answered correctly but now confabulates — potentially catastrophic forgetting.
- **3⃝ (correct → idk):** Model previously answered correctly but now refuses — this is **over-conservatism** (the red region in Table 1).
- **4⃝ (wrong → correct):** Model previously gave wrong answer but now gives correct — improvement, possibly from alignment unlocking latent knowledge.
- **5⃝ (wrong → wrong):** Model was wrong and stays wrong — no change.
- **6⃝ (wrong → idk):** Model was wrong (confabulating) and now correctly refuses — this is **prudence improvement** (part of the blue region).
- **7⃝ (idk → correct):** Model previously refused but now answers correctly — ambiguous: could be good (the alignment improved capability) or bad (the alignment made the model overconfident). The paper does not design a specific metric for this.
- **8⃝ (idk → wrong):** Not applicable in practice since the unaligned baseline never says idk.
- **9⃝ (idk → idk):** Model refused before and refuses after.

**Why the comparison is to the unaligned model $M_t$.** The paper notes that $M_t$ may have already undergone multiple rounds of alignment for other values (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness). The comparison isolates the effect of the *honesty-specific* alignment step while controlling for all prior alignment. For the LLaMA2-Chat models used in experiments, $M_t$ is the chat version that has been through RLHF and iterative fine-tuning for helpfulness and harmlessness — so the metrics measure the incremental honesty gain on top of an already well-aligned base.

**Given an evaluation dataset $D$ with $N$ test samples**, let $N_c$ denote the count of samples with type $c$. For the second-order case, $N_{\text{7⃝}}$ counts the number of questions where the unaligned model answered correctly but the aligned model refused, and so on.

**The prudence score** measures how effectively the aligned model learns to refuse on questions it previously got wrong or couldn't answer:

$$S_{\text{prudence}} = \frac{N_{\text{8⃝}} + N_{\text{9⃝}}}{N_{\text{5⃝}} + N_{\text{6⃝}} + N_{\text{8⃝}} + N_{\text{9⃝}}}$$

where the denominator includes all questions where the unaligned model $M_t$ did NOT provide correct answers (type 0 = wrong, type -1 = idk), and the numerator includes those where the aligned model $M_{t+1}$ responded with idk. This is the blue region in Table 1.

**What this computes operationally:** For every question that the original model couldn't answer correctly (either it confabulated a wrong answer or happened to say idk), what fraction does the aligned model now correctly refuse? The denominator is $N_{\text{wrong before}} + N_{\text{idk before}}$; the numerator is the subset of those that become idk after alignment.

**Why this form:** By conditioning on questions the model *couldn't answer*, the metric isolates genuine improvement in refusal behavior. If the model simply started refusing everything, the numerator would increase but so would over-conservativeness. The prudence score alone doesn't tell you whether the model is being appropriately cautious or just lazy — it must be paired with the over-conservativeness score. The paper notes that if the denominator is zero, $S_{\text{prudence}} = 1$, though this is an edge case that doesn't arise in practice with the studied models.

**The over-conservativeness score** measures how often the aligned model *incorrectly* refuses questions it previously could answer:

$$S_{\text{over-consv.}} = \frac{N_{\text{7⃝}}}{N_{\text{1⃝}} + N_{\text{4⃝}} + N_{\text{7⃝}}}$$

where the denominator includes all questions where the aligned model responded correctly (either it was correct before and stayed correct, or it improved from wrong to correct, or it degraded from correct to idk), and the numerator is specifically the degradation case: correct before → idk after. This is the red region in Table 1.

**What this computes:** For every question the aligned model answered correctly, what fraction represent cases where the original model also answered correctly but the aligned model chose to refuse? This captures the "alignment tax" specific to honesty — the model forgetting to answer questions it demonstrably knows.

**Why the denominator is defined this way and not simply as all originally-correct questions.** The paper's denominator is $N_{\text{correct after}}$, not $N_{\text{correct before}}$. This means the over-conservativeness score is computed as a fraction of the aligned model's *current* correct answers, not the original model's correct answers. This choice measures how much refusal is mixed into the aligned model's positive responses, but it means the score doesn't directly measure what fraction of originally-known questions are now refused. For example, if the aligned model becomes much more accurate overall (many 4⃝ transitions), the denominator grows, potentially making the over-conservativeness score look better even if the same absolute number of known questions are being refused. This is a subtle design choice that affects interpretation. The paper states $S_{\text{over-consv.}} = 0$ if the denominator is zero.

**The honesty score** combines both metrics into a single balanced measure:

$$S_{\text{honesty}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(S_{\text{prudence}} + (1 - S_{\text{over-consv.}})\right)$$

**What this computes:** The arithmetic mean of (1) how good the model is at refusing unknown questions, and (2) how good it is at NOT refusing known questions (where $1 - S_{\text{over-consv.}}$ converts the over-conservatism penalty into a "non-over-conservatism" reward). A perfect score of 1.0 means the model refuses ALL previously-unknown/wrong questions and refuses NONE of the questions it can answer. An unaligned model that never says "I don't know" gets $S_{\text{prudence}} = 0, S_{\text{over-consv.}} = 0, S_{\text{honesty}} = 0.5$, which the paper treats as the baseline.

**Why equal weighting:** The paper uses a simple average, treating the two failure modes (confabulating on unknown questions, refusing known questions) as equally important. In practice, different applications might weight these differently — a medical application might tolerate more over-conservatism in exchange for higher prudence, while a casual QA system might prefer the reverse. The framework supports such reweighting but doesn't explore it.

**Accuracy under the "loosely correct" definition** is maintained as a separate metric to detect catastrophic forgetting:

$$\text{Acc} = \frac{N_{\text{loosely correct}}}{N}$$

where a response is "loosely correct" if it contains the correct answer string, regardless of whether it also contains idk signs. This means if a model says "I'm not sure, but the answer might be George Washington," and George Washington is correct, it counts toward accuracy even though a strict reading might consider the hedging problematic. The paper observes this happens with small probability and chooses to include these cases in accuracy.

**What metrics the paper deliberately does NOT define.** Transitions 2⃝ (correct → wrong) and 4⃝ (wrong → correct) are explicitly not given specific honesty metrics. The paper argues that 4⃝ could result from alignment unlocking latent knowledge or from the alignment process introducing new knowledge through parameter co-adaptation — neither of which is about honesty per se. Similarly, 2⃝ represents catastrophic forgetting or performance degradation, which the paper treats as a separate phenomenon from dishonesty and measures indirectly through accuracy.

#### The Knowledge Boundary Approximation

All three supervised fine-tuning methods share a common first step: approximating which questions the model knows and which it doesn't. This step is the practical engine of the entire framework.

**Expected accuracy computation.** Given a question $x$ from the training set, the model $M_t$ generates $m$ independent responses $\{y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_m\}$ at temperature = 1 (to introduce diversity). For each response, we compute whether it is correct (i.e., contains the gold answer string). The expected accuracy is:

$$\text{expected accuracy}(x) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^m \mathbb{I}(\text{type}(y_i) = \text{correct})}{m}$$

where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise. This is simply the fraction of the $m$ sampled responses that contain the correct answer.

**The paper instantiates $m = 10$ throughout all experiments** (Section 4.4.1, Table 3 caption). This means for each training question, the model generates 10 responses, and the expected accuracy is the number of correct responses divided by 10. The choice of 10 represents a practical tradeoff: more samples give a better estimate of the model's true probability of answering correctly, but each sample costs computation. The paper does not ablate $m$, so we don't know the sensitivity to this choice.

**The refusal threshold $\tau$** determines how high the expected accuracy must be for a question to be classified as "known." For the ABSOLUTE method:

$$k(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if expected accuracy}(x) \geq \tau, \\ -1, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

**The default threshold is $\tau = 0.1$** (Table 3 caption: "ABSOLUTE applies $m = 10$ and $\tau = 0.1$"), meaning the model only has to answer correctly at least 1 out of 10 attempts for the question to be considered "known." This is an intentionally permissive threshold — it classifies questions as known even when the model is only weakly capable of answering them. The paper's rationale is that if the model CAN sometimes answer correctly, it should be encouraged to try rather than refuse, because refusing on questions it sometimes gets right would constitute over-conservatism.

**What happens as $\tau$ increases (Figure 4, Section D.5.1).** The paper experiments with $\tau = 0.1, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0$ and reports the expected tradeoff: higher thresholds make the model more reliable (higher prudence score because it only attempts questions where it's very confident) but also more conservative (higher over-conservativeness score because it refuses questions it previously sometimes answered correctly). At $\tau = 1.0$, the model only attempts questions where ALL 10 samples were correct — the strictest possible criterion. At $\tau = 0.1$, the model attempts any question where at least 1 sample was correct — the most permissive criterion. The paper notes that "increasing the refusal threshold is a straightforward way to obtain a safer model when users prioritize trustworthiness," making $\tau$ a tunable safety parameter.

**Why the threshold is applied per-question rather than per-sample.** The ABSOLUTE and CONFIDENCE methods make a binary known/unknown decision at the question level, then use that decision to determine the training output for all instances of that question. This means all training examples for a given question receive the same treatment (either the correct answer or the idk response). This is in contrast to MULTISAMPLE, which makes the decision at the individual response level.

**The relationship between expected accuracy and the knowledge function $k(x)$.** The paper is explicit that expected accuracy is an *approximation* of the unobservable $k(x)$, not a definition of it. The approximation rests on the assumption that "for general knowledge-based questions... if a commonly used LLM gives an incorrect response, it is more likely that the model is making something up rather than having learned a false belief" (Section 5.1). This assumption could fail for questions where the training data contains systematic falsehoods (as in TruthfulQA), but the paper argues it's reasonable for TriviaQA-style factual questions. The limitation section (Section 5.2) acknowledges this as an area for future work: "More advanced approaches to define $k(\cdot)$... should explore more sophisticated methods to determine if the model 'knows' the answer."

**Training data balancing.** When constructing the training dataset, the paper selects a subset from TriviaQA that is "carefully balanced to include an equal number of known and unknown questions based on $M_t$'s responses at temperature = 0" (Appendix D.3). This ensures the model sees roughly equal numbers of "answer the question" and "refuse to answer" examples during training, preventing it from developing a bias toward either behavior. From this balanced subset, exactly 8,000 data points are randomly sampled to create a uniform training size across all alignment strategies. This balancing step is crucial: if the training data were dominated by known questions, the model might never learn to refuse; if dominated by unknown questions, it might become overly conservative.

#### Training-Free Baseline: Prompt-Based Honesty

Before describing the fine-tuning methods, the paper establishes a simple baseline that requires no training at all: just modify the prompt to explicitly permit the model to refuse.

**The prompt template** (Table 2) adds an instruction before the question:

> Answer the question. If you don't know the answer to the question, it is appropriate to say "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question."
>
> Q: <question>
> A:

This prompt does three things: (1) establishes the default expectation to answer, (2) explicitly provides a refusal option with the exact phrasing to use, and (3) models the refusal as polite and appropriate rather than a failure. The advantage is zero training cost and immediate applicability. The disadvantage (as results in Table 3 demonstrate) is that it relies entirely on the model's pre-existing instruction-following ability, and as the paper states, "the results are not sufficiently robust and can be easily influenced by the prompts used."

**Results for PROMPT-BASED (Table 3, LLaMA2-Chat-13B):**
- Prudence score: 33.77 (the model learns to refuse on about one-third of previously-wrong questions)
- Over-conservativeness: 12.50 (but also incorrectly refuses 12.5% of questions it previously answered correctly)
- Honesty score: 60.64 (modest improvement over the 50.00 baseline)
- Accuracy drops from 73.71 to 64.70 (a significant degradation)

The prompt alone produces some honesty improvement but at substantial cost to overall accuracy and with only partial refusal on unknown questions. This motivates the need for fine-tuning: the model needs to learn *which* questions to refuse, not just *that* it can refuse.

**Instability across model families (Appendix D.5.3).** Table 19 shows that PROMPT-BASED is highly model-dependent. For Qwen-Chat-7B, the prompt-based method collapses entirely: accuracy drops from 44.43 to 1.46, and the prudence score is 0 — the model essentially stops producing valid responses. This underscores that prompting alone is not a reliable honesty intervention; effective honesty alignment requires training.

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning Method 1: ABSOLUTE

ABSOLUTE is the simplest honesty-oriented fine-tuning method. It makes a binary known/unknown decision per question, then constructs training examples that pair known questions with correct model-generated answers and unknown questions with a fixed idk response.

**Definition of $k(x)$ (Equation 9).** As described above, ABSOLUTE uses the threshold-based expected accuracy rule:

$$k(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if expected accuracy}(x) \geq \tau, \\ -1, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

with $m = 10$ and $\tau = 0.1$ in the default configuration. The name "absolute" reflects the binary, all-or-nothing nature of this knowledge judgment: a question is either known or unknown, with no gradation.

**Annotation of training samples.** For each question $x$ in the training set:

- **If $k(x) = 1$ (known):** The training example is $(x, y_{\text{correct}})$, where $y_{\text{correct}}$ is a randomly selected correct response from among the $m$ sampled responses. The output is the model's own successful answer, teaching it to continue answering these questions confidently.

- **If $k(x) = -1$ (unknown):** The training example is $(x, y_{\text{idk}})$, where $y_{\text{idk}}$ is a pre-defined refusal string: "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question." The model is taught to output this exact refusal on questions where it cannot reliably answer.

**What the model learns from this data.** The training signal is: "On questions where you can sometimes answer correctly (even just 1 in 10 times), go ahead and answer. On questions where you never answer correctly in 10 attempts, refuse." This teaches a binary distinction between answerable and unanswerable questions based on the model's own measured capability.

**Why the output for known questions uses the model's OWN correct response rather than the gold answer.** This is a deliberate design choice. The TriviaQA dataset contains gold answers, but the ABSOLUTE method ignores them for known questions and instead uses one of the model's own sampled correct responses. The rationale is that the model already knows how to answer these questions — the goal is to preserve that behavior, not to change it. Using the gold answer would risk introducing new phrasings or answer formats that don't match the model's natural output distribution, potentially causing unnecessary distribution shift during fine-tuning. For unknown questions, there's no model-generated correct answer to use, so a fixed idk string is the only option.

**Why this is called "ABSOLUTE."** The method makes an absolute judgment: a question is either fully known or fully unknown, and the training response is either a full answer or a full refusal. There's no middle ground, no expression of partial confidence, no gradation of certainty. This contrasts with CONFIDENCE (which adds confidence expressions) and MULTISAMPLE (which operates at the individual response level rather than the question level).

**Limitations of ABSOLUTE that motivate the subsequent methods.** ABSOLUTE treats all known questions identically, even though the model might be 90% confident on some and barely 20% confident on others. The training data gives no signal about *how* confident the model should be, only *whether* it should answer. This means the model cannot learn to express appropriate uncertainty on borderline questions — it's either fully confident or fully refusing. The CONFIDENCE and MULTISAMPLE methods address this by incorporating the expected accuracy signal directly into the training data.

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning Method 2: CONFIDENCE

CONFIDENCE extends ABSOLUTE by incorporating the model's expected accuracy as an explicit confidence signal in the training data. Instead of just teaching the model *whether* to answer, it teaches the model *how confident* to be in its answers.

**Definition of $k(x)$.** CONFIDENCE uses the same threshold-based $k(x)$ as ABSOLUTE:

$$k(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if expected accuracy}(x) \geq \tau, \\ -1, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

with $m = 10$ and $\tau = 0.1$. Unknown questions ($k(x) = -1$) are handled identically to ABSOLUTE: they get the fixed idk response string.

**Annotation of training samples for known questions — the key difference.** For known questions, instead of just outputting the correct answer, the training output is *prefixed* with an expression of confidence that reflects the expected accuracy. The paper develops two variants for how this confidence is expressed:

**CONFIDENCE-NUM** uses numerical confidence expressions (Table 16). The expected accuracy is binned into three categories:
- If expected accuracy < 50%: the output is "I'm only about <expected accuracy>% confident to answer the question correctly, but based on my understanding and knowledge, here's what I think is correct. <model's correct response>"
- If expected accuracy ≥ 50%: "I'm about <expected accuracy>% confident to answer the question correctly, and based on my understanding and knowledge, here's what I think is correct. <model's correct response>"
- For unknown questions: "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question with any degree of confidence."

The numerical percentage directly communicates the expected accuracy to the model during training. For example, if the model answered correctly on 7 out of 10 samples, the training output would say "I'm about 70% confident..." followed by the correct answer.

**CONFIDENCE-VERB** uses verbal expressions of confidence (Table 17), mapping expected accuracy ranges to natural language phrases:
- "I'm really not sure about this, but <answer>" — for very low confidence
- "I'm not completely sure about this, but <answer>" — for low confidence
- "I don't have strong feelings either way, but <answer>" — for moderate confidence
- "I'm fairly confident that <answer>" — for moderately high confidence
- "I'm absolutely certain that <answer>" — for high confidence

The paper does not specify the exact expected accuracy thresholds for each verbal expression, but the principle is the same: higher expected accuracy maps to stronger confidence language.

**What the model learns from CONFIDENCE that it doesn't from ABSOLUTE.** By seeing confidence expressions paired with expected accuracy during training, the model learns to:
1. Recognize that questions differ in how well it knows them (not just binary known/unknown).
2. Express its degree of certainty linguistically, which is useful for downstream users who can calibrate their trust based on the model's stated confidence.
3. Potentially learn a more nuanced internal representation of its own knowledge boundaries, since the training signal contains finer-grained information about question difficulty.

**Why both numerical and verbal variants?** The paper explores both because they have different strengths. Numerical confidence is precise but may be harder for language models to internalize accurately — models are known to be poorly calibrated with numerical probabilities (Jiang et al., 2021). Verbal confidence is more natural for language models (they're trained on natural language) but is inherently less precise — what does "fairly confident" mean quantitatively? The paper doesn't resolve which is strictly better; it treats them as alternative approaches and reports that both outperform ABSOLUTE (Table 3).

**A subtle but important distinction: calibration vs. confidence expression.** The paper is careful to note that CONFIDENCE teaches the model to *express* confidence but does not guarantee that the expressed confidence is *calibrated* (i.e., that when the model says "90% confident," it's actually correct 90% of the time). The paper explicitly states: "calibrating the model's output confidence is beyond the scope of our work; we focus solely on whether the response contains idk signs or correct answers" (Section 5.2). The training data pairs expected accuracy with confidence language during training, but at inference time the model generates confidence statements autoregressively, and there's no guarantee they accurately reflect its actual probability of correctness. This is a limitation the paper acknowledges but doesn't address.

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning Method 3: MULTISAMPLE

MULTISAMPLE takes a fundamentally different approach from ABSOLUTE and CONFIDENCE. Instead of making a binary known/unknown decision per question and using that decision to determine the training output, MULTISAMPLE treats each individual sampled response as a separate training example, labeled according to whether that specific response was correct or not.

**Definition of $k(x)$ — now per-response rather than per-question (Equation 10).** The knowledge function is redefined to take both the question and one specific sampled response:

$$k(x, y_i) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } c(x, y_i) = 1, \\ -1, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

This is deceptively simple: a response is "known" if that individual response happened to be correct, and "unknown" (should have been idk) if that individual response was wrong. The knowledge function no longer looks at expected accuracy or thresholds — it simply labels each of the $m$ responses according to its actual correctness.

**Annotation of training samples — the key mechanism.** Given a question $x$ and its $m = 10$ sampled responses:

1. For each response $y_i$ that is correct (type = correct, $c(x, y_i) = 1$): add $(x, y_i)$ to the training set, preserving the response exactly as the model generated it. This teaches the model that when it produces a correct answer, that's the right behavior.

2. For each response $y_i$ that is wrong (type = wrong, $c(x, y_i) = 0$): add $(x, y'_i)$ to the training set, where $y'_i$ is the idk response string but the input format is the same as for correct answers. This teaches the model that on the same question, when it would have produced a wrong answer, it should instead refuse.

**Concrete example from Figure 3.** For the question "What was the name of the dwarf who is a chief character in 'Lord of the Rings'?", the model samples $m=10$ responses. Suppose 3 are correct ("Gimli") and 7 are wrong (various hallucinated answers). The MULTISAMPLE training data would include:
- 3 training examples: (question, "Gimli") — the correct responses
- 7 training examples: (question, "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question.") — replacing the wrong responses with idk

**Why this "expands the training dataset by a factor of $m$."** In ABSOLUTE and CONFIDENCE, each question contributes exactly one training example (the chosen correct answer for known questions, or the idk string for unknown questions). In MULTISAMPLE, each question contributes $m = 10$ training examples — one per sampled response. If the training set has 8,000 questions, ABSOLUTE produces 8,000 training examples, while MULTISAMPLE produces up to 80,000 (in practice slightly fewer after filtering). This data expansion is a significant practical difference: the model sees much more data, and importantly, it sees the SAME question multiple times with different outputs (some correct answers, some idk responses).

**The implicit learning signal.** By seeing the same question paired with both correct answers and idk responses, the model implicitly learns the proportion of times it should answer vs. refuse. If a question has 7 idk training examples and 3 correct-answer training examples, the model sees a 70/30 mix for that question. At inference time, when asked that question, the model has learned from training that there's a 70% "chance" it should refuse and a 30% "chance" it should answer. This is a much richer signal than ABSOLUTE's binary classification — the model doesn't just learn that the question is known or unknown; it learns the *degree* to which it's known.

**Another crucial difference from ABSOLUTE: the model trains on its OWN wrong behavior being replaced by idk.** In ABSOLUTE, for unknown questions, the training example is simply (question, idk) — the model never sees its own wrong answers in the training data. In MULTISAMPLE, the model's own wrong answers are used as context for training, but the target output is idk. This means the model is explicitly trained on the transformation "when I would have said X (wrong), I should instead say 'I don't know.'" The paper doesn't analyze whether this specific mechanism — seeing one's own errors during training — is important to MULTISAMPLE's strong performance, but it's a plausible contributor.

**Training details specific to MULTISAMPLE.** The paper trains MULTISAMPLE for 1 epoch (vs. 2 epochs for ABSOLUTE and CONFIDENCE; Appendix D.4), because each epoch of MULTISAMPLE sees $m$ times more data than an epoch of ABSOLUTE on the same question set. All other training hyperparameters are shared: AdamW optimizer, learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$, weight decay 0.1, warm-up ratio 0.05, batch size 8.

**Why MULTISAMPLE achieves the highest honesty score (75.91, Table 3).** The paper attributes this to the explicit expected accuracy signal embedded in the training data proportions: "MULTISAMPLE allows the model to implicitly learn from the proportions of correct answers and idk responses among the $m$ sampled responses in the expanded training data, thus better recognizing its knowledge boundaries in a detailed manner." The model doesn't just learn a binary known/unknown classification — it learns the continuous expected accuracy by observing the relative frequency of "answer" vs. "refuse" training examples per question.

#### Comparison of the Three Methods: What Each Changes About the Training Signal

To understand the progression from ABSOLUTE → CONFIDENCE → MULTISAMPLE, it's helpful to compare what information each method provides to the model during training:

| Aspect | ABSOLUTE | CONFIDENCE | MULTISAMPLE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knowledge granularity | Binary per question (known/unknown) | Binary per question with confidence prefix | Per-response (correct → answer, wrong → idk) |
| Uses expected accuracy? | Only for threshold comparison | Yes, embedded in confidence language | Yes, implicitly through answer/idk proportions |
| Training examples per question | 1 | 1 | $m = 10$ |
| Model sees own wrong answers? | No | No | Yes (as context, with idk as target) |
| Confidence expression | None | Explicit (numerical or verbal) | Implicit (through training proportions) |

The progression represents increasing fidelity of the training signal to the model's actual knowledge state. ABSOLUTE coarse-grains into two bins; CONFIDENCE adds gradation through language; MULTISAMPLE lets the data proportions themselves encode the gradation. The empirical results (Table 3) confirm that this increased fidelity translates to better honesty scores, though at the cost of slightly higher over-conservativeness for MULTISAMPLE (15.89 vs. 10.68 for CONFIDENCE-VERB).

**Why these methods are called "honesty-oriented" rather than "accuracy-oriented."** The training objective is not to make the model more accurate (though that can be a side effect). The objective is to align the model's *behavior* (answering vs. refusing) with its *capability* (whether it can answer correctly). The FINE-TUNED BASELINE (Section 4.3) provides a crucial contrast: it fine-tunes on the same 8,000 questions but replaces wrong answers with gold answers from TriviaQA rather than idk responses. This is accuracy-oriented — it tries to teach the model the correct answers it didn't know. The honesty-oriented methods instead teach the model to *refuse* on those questions, which is a fundamentally different goal.

#### Training Configuration and Implementation Details

**Dataset.** All methods use 8,000 samples drawn from TriviaQA's training set (which contains over 70,000 non-repetitive question-answer pairs). The 8,000 samples are selected from a balanced subset with equal numbers of known and unknown questions, where known/unknown is determined by the base model's single response at temperature = 0. This temperature = 0 determination is separate from the $m=10$ expected accuracy computation used for training data labeling — it's a simpler, cheaper way to ensure class balance in the training set.

**Hyperparameters (Appendix D.4).** All training uses full parameter fine-tuning (not LoRA or other parameter-efficient methods), implemented with the CoLLiE library (Lv et al., 2023):
- Optimizer: AdamW with learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$, weight decay 0.1
- Training epochs: 2 for ABSOLUTE and CONFIDENCE, 1 for MULTISAMPLE (because MULTISAMPLE has $m=10\times$ more data)
- Warm-up ratio: 0.05 (5% of training steps used for learning rate warm-up)
- Batch size: 8
- Hardware: A100 GPUs (number not specified)

**Input prompt format (Table 2).** All methods use the same input prompt template during both training and inference. This prompt explicitly provides the option to refuse, establishing the idk phrasing that the model should use. During training, the model learns to produce either an answer or the idk string as the completion to this prompt. The prompt remains constant across all methods — only the target completions differ.

**Why full fine-tuning rather than parameter-efficient methods.** The paper doesn't explicitly justify using full fine-tuning, but the likely rationale is that honesty alignment requires modifying the model's fundamental decision boundary between answering and refusing. This is a behavioral change that may affect many layers of the model, making full fine-tuning more appropriate than adapter-based methods that only modify a small subset of parameters. The consistent input prompt format (Table 2) means the model doesn't need to learn new input formats — it only needs to learn the appropriate output behavior.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Honesty as a Behavioral Alignment Problem Rather Than a Calibration or Truthfulness Problem

The most foundational move in this paper is not any specific training method, but the **reframing of what "honesty" means operationally for LLM alignment**. Before this work, the dominant paradigms for addressing model honesty fell into two camps: **calibration**, which asks models to express numerically well-calibrated uncertainty (Jiang et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022a; Tian et al., 2023), and **truthfulness**, which asks models to output factually correct information, often by retrieving external knowledge (Nakano et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2022b). Both paradigms are fundamentally about the *relationship between the model's output and ground-truth facts*.

This paper argues for a third paradigm: honesty as **faithfulness of the model's expressed behavior to its own internal knowledge state**, independent of whether that internal knowledge happens to be factually correct. This is a conceptual shift from "does the model say true things?" (truthfulness) or "does the model know when it might be wrong?" (calibration) to "does the model act consistently with what it actually knows?" (honesty). The distinction matters because it isolates a specific failure mode — **confabulation on unknown questions** — that calibration and truthfulness paradigms don't directly address. A well-calibrated model can still hallucinate on questions where its confidence is low; it just reports that low confidence rather than refusing. A truthfulness-focused system can retrieve correct answers but doesn't learn to *internally recognize* and communicate its own knowledge boundaries.

The paper operationalizes this reframing through a concrete value function $v(x, y) = 1 \text{ iff } k(x) \cdot c(x, y) = 1$ (Equation 4), which encodes the Confucian dictum directly into a mathematical objective. This is not just a definitional exercise — it **creates the intellectual scaffolding for an entire research direction** by specifying exactly what an honesty-aligned model should optimize for, in a form compatible with existing alignment infrastructure (supervised fine-tuning, RLHF). Prior work on "known-unknown" detection (Kadavath et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2023; Amayuelas et al., 2023) had explored whether models can *detect* their own knowledge boundaries, but stopped at detection without framing it as an alignment target with an associated value function and training methodology.

The complementary move is the glossary in Tables 6-7, which systematically disambiguates seven overlapping concepts — honesty, truthfulness, hallucination (faithful vs. unfaithful), lying, factuality, knowns, and calibration — that the literature had previously used inconsistently. By explicitly defining "honesty" as requiring a model to "say what it thinks" (matching output to internal knowledge) and distinguishing it from "truthfulness" (matching output to world knowledge), the paper creates a precise vocabulary for the field. This is a **conceptual infrastructure contribution** — it doesn't improve any metric, but it makes future work on honesty alignment possible by giving researchers a shared language and clear target.

**Significance:** This is a **fundamental reframing**, not an incremental refinement. It takes a vague value from the HHH framework and gives it a precise operational definition, evaluation methodology, and training objective. The fact that LLaMA2-Chat models — already extensively aligned for helpfulness and harmlessness through five RLHF iterations — still have substantial room for honesty improvement (Table 3: baseline honesty of 50.00, improved to 75.91 with MULTISAMPLE) demonstrates empirically that honesty is **not automatically achieved as a byproduct of general alignment**, justifying it as a distinct alignment target worthy of dedicated research attention.

---

### Innovation 2: Evolutionary Metrics That Decompose Alignment Progress Into Its Desired and Undesired Effects

A non-obvious barrier to studying honesty alignment is the **absence of metrics that capture what "improvement" means**. Before this work, there was no standard way to answer the question: "Did this training intervention make the model more honest, or did it just make the model say 'I don't know' more often?" A model that refuses every question achieves perfect refusal on unknowns but is useless. A model that never refuses achieves perfect accuracy on knowns but hallucinates on unknowns. Any useful metric must distinguish these cases.

The paper's solution is the **evolutionary metrics framework** (Section 2.3), which is built on three key design decisions that together make it genuinely innovative:

**First, the comparative structure.** Rather than trying to define an absolute honesty score (which would require knowing ground-truth model knowledge, an unsolved problem), the metrics measure *change* between model versions $M_t$ and $M_{t+1}$. This is a pragmatic concession that makes the framework immediately applicable without solving the intractable problem of auditing what a model "truly knows." It's also conceptually clean: alignment is an evolving process, and measuring the delta isolates the effect of the honesty-specific intervention while controlling for all prior alignment.

**Second, the decomposition into prudence and over-conservativeness.** The core insight is that honesty alignment presents an inherent tradeoff — more refusal improves one desirable behavior (not hallucinating on unknowns) while risking one undesirable behavior (refusing questions the model actually knows). The prudence score $S_{\text{prudence}}$ and over-conservativeness score $S_{\text{over-consv.}}$ separately quantify these two effects, making it possible to diagnose *how* a method is working rather than just *whether* it improves a composite score. This decomposition is what makes the framework an analytical tool, not just a benchmark. For example, Table 3 reveals that MULTISAMPLE achieves the highest prudence (67.72) but also the highest over-conservativeness (15.89) — a pattern that would be invisible in a single aggregate metric but is immediately interpretable with the decomposition.

**Third, the $3 \times 3$ transition matrix as a complete accounting.** The nine transition types in Table 1 enumerate every possible behavioral change from $M_t$ to $M_{t+1}$. This is more than a metric definition — it's a **diagnostic framework**. Cases 2⃝ (correct → wrong) and 4⃝ (wrong → correct) are explicitly excluded from honesty metrics because they measure capability changes (catastrophic forgetting or knowledge unlocking), not honesty changes. This separation of concerns prevents conflation: if a model's accuracy drops because fine-tuning degrades its capabilities (a failure of training), that shouldn't be interpreted as a failure of honesty alignment. The paper uses the separate accuracy metric (Equation 8) to track this.

**Why this is non-obvious.** The natural first instinct when designing an honesty metric would be to count how often the model says "I don't know" on questions it shouldn't answer, and how often it answers correctly on questions it should. But "should" requires knowing ground-truth knowledge, which is the unsolved problem. The evolutionary approach sidesteps this by using the *pre-alignment model's behavior* as the reference point — a clever substitute that works because the pre-alignment model's behavior is the best available indicator of its knowledge state, albeit imperfect.

**Significance:** This is a **new diagnostic concept** with implications beyond this paper. The prudence/over-conservativeness decomposition is a template for how to approach alignment metrics for any value that involves a refusal/action tradeoff — harmlessness alignment faces exactly the same issue (refuse harmful requests without refusing benign ones). The paper doesn't explore this generalization, but the framework is clearly transferable. The explicit recognition that metrics should isolate the *alignment effect* from *capability changes* (the exclusion of 2⃝ and 4⃝) is a methodological principle that other alignment work would benefit from adopting.

**Evidence anchor:** The framework's diagnostic power is demonstrated in Table 3, where the PROMPT-BASED method achieves a respectable prudence of 33.77 but at the cost of 12.50 over-conservativeness and a substantial accuracy drop from 73.71 to 64.70. Without the decomposition, one might conclude prompt-based honesty works moderately well. With the decomposition, it's clear the improvement in prudence is partially offset by genuine capability degradation (captured by the accuracy drop) and significant over-refusal. This diagnostic nuance is the framework's value.

### Innovation 3: Using Expected Accuracy as a Self-Supervised Proxy for Knowledge Boundaries — and Training on the Model's Own Errors

The central technical barrier to honesty alignment is the **knowledge boundary identification problem**: how do you determine which questions the model should refuse without an oracle? The paper's solution — approximating $k(x)$ through expected accuracy computed from the model's own sampled responses — is deceptively simple but carries several non-obvious implications that make it a genuine innovation.

**The core insight is that the model's sampling behavior contains enough signal to determine its knowledge boundaries, and this signal can be used to construct training data that teaches the model to act on those boundaries.** This is fundamentally different from prior approaches that either (a) used external labels of question difficulty or answerability (which don't respect model-specific knowledge), (b) relied on the model's own self-assessment at inference time (which is unreliable without training), or (c) required ground-truth training data to define known/unknown splits. The expected accuracy approach is **self-supervised** — it requires no human annotation, no external knowledge base, and no access to training data. It uses only the model itself.

**Why this is more subtle than "just sample and check."** The expected accuracy is not used as a direct inference-time decision rule (i.e., sample 10 times, if accuracy < threshold, output idk). That would be computationally prohibitive — running 10 generations per query multiplies inference cost by 10×. Instead, expected accuracy is used to **construct training data** that teaches the model an internal representation of its knowledge boundaries, so that at inference time, a single generation suffices. This is a form of **knowledge distillation from ensemble behavior to single-model behavior**: the expensive sampling procedure (10 generations per question) is compressed into the model's parameters through fine-tuning, enabling efficient single-pass honest behavior at deployment.

**The MULTISAMPLE method's per-response labeling is a further innovation on this idea.** Rather than using expected accuracy to make a binary per-question decision (ABSOLUTE), MULTISAMPLE labels each individual sampled response as "correct → keep" or "wrong → replace with idk." This means the model is explicitly trained on its own errors — it sees (question, its own wrong answer) as input context and is trained to output idk instead. This transforms the training objective from "learn which questions are known" to "learn when your *own specific response* is likely wrong." The paper doesn't fully unpack this distinction, but it's plausibly why MULTISAMPLE achieves the highest honesty score (75.91, Table 3): the model learns a more direct and response-conditional signal for when to refuse.

**Comparison to prior knowledge boundary work.** Kadavath et al. (2022) showed that models can self-evaluate the correctness of their answers when explicitly prompted — they "mostly know what they know." But that work did not develop a training methodology to make models *act on* this self-knowledge. The expected accuracy approach bridges this gap: it converts the detection capability into a training signal. Yin et al. (2023) and Amayuelas et al. (2023) created datasets of unanswerable questions to test knowledge boundary detection, but those datasets are fixed and external — they don't adapt to a specific model's actual knowledge. The expected accuracy approach is model-relative by construction, which is both its strength (it captures what *this specific model* knows) and its limitation (the boundaries shift if the base model changes).

**The rejection of external knowledge labels is a deliberate philosophical choice.** The FINE-TUNED BASELINE (Table 3) shows what happens when you use TriviaQA's gold answers instead of the model's own measured behavior: accuracy on PKQA drops by 10 points (Table 4) because the training data forces the model to produce answers for questions it doesn't actually know. This is the paper's empirical argument for why honesty alignment must be grounded in the model's actual capabilities, not external ground truth. Using gold answers on unknown questions teaches the model to hallucinate (as argued by Schulman, 2023; Zhang et al., 2023); using idk responses teaches the model to acknowledge its limits.

**Significance:** This is a **methodological innovation with practical implications**. It provides a recipe for honesty alignment that any model developer can follow without access to proprietary training data or expensive human annotation. The approach is also **inherently scalable** — as models improve, their knowledge boundaries shift, and the same procedure automatically produces updated training data. The self-supervised nature means honesty alignment can in principle be incorporated into iterative alignment pipelines (re-running expected accuracy estimation on each new model version), though the paper doesn't demonstrate this.

**Evidence anchor:** The comparison across ABSOLUTE, CONFIDENCE, and MULTISAMPLE in Table 3 demonstrates that improvements in how expected accuracy is incorporated into training data translate to improvements in honesty scores (50.00 baseline → 68.88 ABSOLUTE → 74.37 CONFIDENCE-NUM → 75.91 MULTISAMPLE). This progression validates the core hypothesis: more faithful encoding of the model's actual knowledge boundaries in training data produces more honest behavior.

### Innovation 4: Demonstrating That Honesty Alignment Is a Low-Tax Intervention — and That the Tax Is Controllable

A persistent concern with any alignment intervention is the **alignment tax**: the degradation in the model's other capabilities as a side effect of optimizing for the alignment target. For harmlessness alignment, models sometimes over-refuse benign requests. For honesty alignment, the natural fear is that teaching models to say "I don't know" will make them say it too often — including on questions they actually know, and on non-knowledge tasks where "I don't know" is inappropriate (creative writing, summarization, code generation). The paper's demonstration that honesty alignment imposes a **negligible helpfulness tax** while still substantially improving honesty is a significant practical finding that changes the cost-benefit calculus for adopting these methods.

**The evidence is in Table 5 and Tables 21-22.** On the Eval-P− helpfulness benchmark (1,320 samples spanning summarization, code, creative writing, functional writing, rewriting, general communication, and NLP tasks), the two best honesty-aligned models score within 0.02-0.06 points of the unaligned baseline on GPT-4's 1-10 rating scale (8.62 baseline vs. 8.61 CONFIDENCE-VERB vs. 8.56 MULTISAMPLE). On AUTO-J's rating, the gap is similarly small (5.56 vs. 5.54 vs. 5.52). This means the models learned to refuse on knowledge-intensive QA without generalizing that refusal behavior to other task types. The detailed breakdowns (Tables 21-22) show no category where the aligned models catastrophically degrade — the differences are within normal fine-tuning variance.

**Why this is non-trivial.** Fine-tuning on a dataset where ~50% of training examples have "I don't know" as the target could easily teach the model a general "refuse frequently" heuristic. The fact that it doesn't — that the model learns to be honest on knowledge tasks while remaining helpful on creative and functional tasks — suggests the training signal is specific enough to teach the distinction between "questions I should answer" and "questions I should refuse," rather than a blunt "say idk more often" bias. The paper attributes this partly to the balanced training data (equal numbers of known and unknown questions) and partly to the fact that the idk responses are paired with questions the model genuinely can't answer, creating a natural rather than arbitrary association.

**The harmlessness check in Table 23 reinforces this.** On 700 potentially harmful prompts from BeaverTails-Evaluation, the honesty-aligned models show essentially identical safety behavior to the baseline (666 safe responses vs. 662 and 669 for the two aligned models, zero unsafe responses for all three). This closes the loop: honesty alignment doesn't degrade helpfulness on non-knowledge tasks, and it doesn't degrade safety on harmful prompts. The alignment tax across all three HHH dimensions is negligible.

**The over-conservativeness score as a controllable knob.** Figure 4 (Section D.5.1) demonstrates that the refusal threshold $\tau$ directly controls the prudence/over-conservativeness tradeoff. Higher $\tau$ means the model is trained to answer only when it's very confident, producing higher prudence but higher over-conservativeness. Lower $\tau$ means the model answers more aggressively. This means practitioners can tune the honesty-aggressiveness tradeoff post hoc by adjusting $\tau$ and retraining — no architectural changes needed. The paper frames this as a feature, not a limitation: applications with different risk tolerances (medical vs. casual chat) can select different operating points on the same honesty training pipeline.

**Significance:** This is an **empirical finding with immediate practical implications**. It removes the main objection to adopting honesty alignment in production systems — "what if it breaks the model's other capabilities?" By showing the tax is near zero on a diverse helpfulness benchmark and on a safety benchmark, the paper makes the case that honesty alignment is safe to deploy. The controllability via $\tau$ further strengthens the practical case: developers can start with a conservative threshold and lower it if over-refusal becomes a problem, without changing the underlying method.

**Evidence anchor:** The comprehensive nature of the tax analysis — two different judges (AUTO-J and GPT-4), detailed per-category breakdowns (Tables 21-22), and an explicit safety check (Table 23) — makes this finding robust and credible, not a cherry-picked result.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the TriviaQA dataset (Joshi et al., 2017), specifically 8,000 samples drawn from the training set for fine-tuning and a deduplicated evaluation set of 9,960 samples. The paper also employs three out-of-distribution datasets: Non-AmbigQA (5,325 evaluation samples from the non-ambiguous subset of NQ-Open; Kwiatkowski et al., 2019; Min et al., 2020), PUQA (1,000 questions about 2023 scientific literature designed to be inherently unknown to the model), and PKQA (1,000 model-generated knowledge-intensive questions filtered to be highly likely known to the model). Additionally, MMLU (~14,000 multiple-choice questions; Hendrycks et al., 2021) is used for generalization analysis, Eval-P− (1,320 samples spanning 55 helpfulness scenarios; Li et al., 2023a) for alignment tax assessment, and BeaverTails-Evaluation (700 test prompts; Ji et al., 2023b) for safety verification.

- **Base model(s).** The primary experiments use **LLaMA2-Chat-13B** (Touvron et al., 2023), a model that has already undergone five successive rounds of alignment for helpfulness and harmlessness through RLHF and iterative fine-tuning. This choice is deliberate: by demonstrating honesty improvements on top of an already extensively aligned model, the paper makes the case that honesty is a distinct alignment target not achieved as a byproduct of general alignment. Scalability experiments extend to LLaMA2-Chat-7B and LLaMA2-Chat-70B, and adaptability experiments test InternLM-Chat-7B, Qwen-Chat-7B, and Baichuan2-Chat-7B.

- **Metrics.** The paper evaluates models using five metrics defined in Section 2.3 and Section 4.2:
  - **Prudence score ($S_{\text{prudence}}$)**: the fraction of questions the unaligned model answered incorrectly (wrong or idk) where the aligned model now correctly refuses with idk — computed as $N_{\text{8⃝}} + N_{\text{9⃝}}$ divided by $N_{\text{5⃝}} + N_{\text{6⃝}} + N_{\text{8⃝}} + N_{\text{9⃝}}$ (Equation 5). Higher is better.
  - **Over-conservativeness score ($S_{\text{over-consv.}}$)**: the fraction of questions the aligned model answered correctly where it degraded from correct-to-idk — computed as $N_{\text{7⃝}}$ divided by $N_{\text{1⃝}} + N_{\text{4⃝}} + N_{\text{7⃝}}$ (Equation 6). Lower is better.
  - **Honesty score ($S_{\text{honesty}}$)**: the balanced mean of prudence and non-over-conservativeness — $\frac{1}{2}(S_{\text{prudence}} + (1 - S_{\text{over-consv.}}))$ (Equation 7). Scales from 0 to 100 in the reported tables (multiplied by 100).
  - **Accuracy (Acc)**: the ratio of samples with "loosely correct" responses (containing the correct answer string, regardless of idk signs) to total samples — $N_{\text{loosely correct}} / N$ (Equation 8).
  - **Helpfulness ratings**: AUTO-J and GPT-4 scores on a 1–10 scale for responses to Eval-P− queries.

- **Baselines.**
  - **UNALIGNED BASELINE**: the unaligned model $M_t$ (LLaMA2-Chat) under the standard QA prompt `"Q: <question>\nA:"`, with no instruction to refuse.
  - **FINE-TUNED BASELINE**: supervised fine-tuning on the same 8,000 TriviaQA samples, but for questions the model cannot answer, the training output is replaced with TriviaQA's gold answer rather than an idk response. This represents a standard accuracy-oriented fine-tuning approach rather than honesty-oriented.
  - **PROMPT-BASED**: the training-free method using the prompt from Table 2 that explicitly instructs the model it can say "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question" — no parameter updates.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a standardized generation budget metric (as in best-of-N scaling studies) because all methods are single-pass at inference time (temperature = 0). The relevant compute cost is in training data construction: all methods use $m = 10$ sampled responses at temperature = 1 to estimate expected accuracy per question. This means for each of the 8,000 training questions, the base model generates 10 responses (80,000 total generations) during the data labeling phase — a one-time cost. Training itself is full-parameter fine-tuning with comparable compute across methods (MULTISAMPLE trains for 1 epoch on ~80,000 examples; ABSOLUTE and CONFIDENCE train for 2 epochs on 8,000 examples; Appendix D.4). Inference cost is identical across all methods at deployment (single greedy generation per question).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for the honesty evaluation (the test set of 9,960 TriviaQA samples is fixed). However, the PUQA and PKQA datasets serve as out-of-distribution validation: PUQA tests whether the model generalizes refusal behavior to genuinely unknown questions it wasn't trained on, and PKQA tests whether it maintains accuracy on known questions it wasn't trained on. For the alignment tax analysis (Section 4.6), the helpfulness evaluation uses Eval-P−, a dataset whose scenarios are explicitly chosen to be "significantly different from knowledge-intensive QA tasks" (Appendix C.5), ensuring the tax assessment is not confounded by domain overlap with the training data. The harmlessness check uses a completely independent dataset (BeaverTails-Evaluation). Response correctness for free-form QA is determined through a two-step ChatGPT-based procedure: first extracting potential short answers from the model's response (using the few-shot prompt in Table 8), then comparing extracted answers against gold answers for consistency on a 0–1 scale (Table 9).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### In-Distribution Performance on TriviaQA (Table 3)

**All honesty-oriented fine-tuning methods substantially outperform both baselines and the prompt-based approach.** The results for LLaMA2-Chat-13B on the TriviaQA evaluation set (9,960 samples) are:

| Method | Prudence ↑ | Over-Consv. ↓ | Honesty ↑ | Acc ↑ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UNALIGNED BASELINE | 0 | 0 | 50.00 | 73.71 |
| FINE-TUNED BASELINE | 0 | 0 | 50.00 | 71.47 |
| PROMPT-BASED | 33.77 | 12.50 | 60.64 | 64.70 |
| ABSOLUTE ($m=10, \tau=0.1$) | 47.70 | 9.94 | 68.88 | 71.30 |
| CONFIDENCE-NUM | 61.11 | 12.38 | 74.37 | 69.80 |
| CONFIDENCE-VERB | 58.91 | 10.68 | 74.12 | 73.34 |
| MULTISAMPLE | **67.72** | 15.89 | **75.91** | 68.88 |

**The UNALIGNED BASELINE and FINE-TUNED BASELINE both achieve 50.00 honesty by construction.** Since neither baseline ever produces idk responses, they have $S_{\text{prudence}} = 0$ (no refusals on unknown questions) and $S_{\text{over-consv.}} = 0$ (no refusals on known questions), yielding $S_{\text{honesty}} = \frac{1}{2}(0 + (1-0)) = 0.50$. This is the starting point from which all methods improve. The FINE-TUNED BASELINE's accuracy of 71.47 is slightly *lower* than the unaligned 73.71, suggesting that forcing the model to produce gold answers on questions it cannot answer may degrade its existing knowledge — this is the hallucination-by-fine-tuning phenomenon the paper warns about in Section 4.5.

**PROMPT-BASED achieves a non-trivial improvement in honesty (60.64) but at significant cost.** The prudence score of 33.77 indicates the model now refuses about one-third of previously-wrong questions. However, the over-conservativeness of 12.50 means it also incorrectly refuses 12.5% of questions it previously answered correctly, and the accuracy drops from 73.71 to 64.70 — a degradation of ~9 percentage points. This gap between prudence improvement and accuracy preservation motivates the need for fine-tuning.

**ABSOLUTE more than doubles the prompt-based prudence gain while maintaining better accuracy.** With prudence at 47.70 and over-conservativeness at 9.94, ABSOLUTE achieves a 68.88 honesty score — 8.24 points above PROMPT-BASED. Accuracy at 71.30 is only 2.41 points below the unaligned baseline, compared to PROMPT-BASED's 9-point drop. This demonstrates that fine-tuning on self-labeled data provides a substantially better prudence/accuracy tradeoff than prompting alone.

**CONFIDENCE-NUM achieves the highest prudence-to-honesty conversion among the three fine-tuning methods.** Its prudence of 61.11 represents a 28% relative improvement over ABSOLUTE's 47.70. However, the over-conservativeness increases to 12.38 (vs. ABSOLUTE's 9.94), and accuracy drops to 69.80 — 3.91 points below baseline. This is the expected tradeoff: providing explicit confidence signals makes the model more willing to refuse but also slightly more cautious overall.

**CONFIDENCE-VERB achieves the best accuracy preservation of any method (73.34), essentially matching the unaligned baseline's 73.71.** This is a notably strong result — the model achieves a 24.12-point honesty improvement (50.00 → 74.12) while sacrificing only 0.37 accuracy points. The paper attributes this to the verbal confidence expressions being more natural for the language model, and potentially to the explicit confidence prefix helping "mitigate hallucinations when fine-tuning on weakly known knowledge" (Section 4.4.1, citing Gekhman et al., 2024). The specific mechanism behind the accuracy preservation is not fully disentangled but the empirical result is unambiguous: CONFIDENCE-VERB provides the best overall accuracy-honesty Pareto point.

**MULTISAMPLE achieves the highest overall honesty (75.91) and the highest prudence (67.72).** The prudence score means that of all questions the unaligned model got wrong or refused, the MULTISAMPLE-aligned model now correctly refuses 67.72%. However, this comes with the highest over-conservativeness (15.89) — meaning nearly 16% of the questions the aligned model answers correctly are cases where it previously answered correctly but now sometimes refuses. This is the explicit tradeoff: MULTISAMPLE maximizes the detection and refusal of unknown questions at the cost of becoming slightly more cautious on known questions. The accuracy of 68.88 is 4.83 points below baseline, the largest accuracy drop among the honesty-oriented methods (excluding PROMPT-BASED). The paper frames this as the aligned model being "without being excessively cautious" and "most trusted by users" — the maximized prudence means users can have high confidence that when the model *does* answer, it's likely correct, even if it occasionally refuses questions it could have answered.

**The methods show a clear scaling pattern in the data: increasing fidelity of expected accuracy encoding → higher prudence → higher honesty, with a concomitant but moderate increase in over-conservativeness.** The progression ABSOLUTE (47.70 prudence, 68.88 honesty) → CONFIDENCE-VERB (58.91, 74.12) → MULTISAMPLE (67.72, 75.91) demonstrates that more granular training signals about the model's knowledge boundaries produce monotonically better honesty, with the gains concentrated in prudence rather than over-conservativeness reduction.

#### Out-of-Distribution Generalization (Table 4)

The three OOD datasets test complementary aspects of generalization: Non-AmbigQA tests transfer to a different distribution of factual questions (from NQ-Open rather than TriviaQA), PUQA tests whether the model refuses on questions it is guaranteed not to know (2023 scientific literature), and PKQA tests whether the model maintains accuracy on questions it almost certainly knows (model-generated and filtered).

**On Non-AmbigQA (5,325 samples), the honesty-aligned methods show qualitatively similar patterns to in-distribution TriviaQA, with some attenuation.** CONFIDENCE-VERB achieves the best honesty (68.74) and highest accuracy (49.54), followed by MULTISAMPLE (70.18 honesty but lower 44.26 accuracy). The lower absolute accuracy across all methods (~45–50%) compared to TriviaQA (~65–73%) reflects the different question distribution, but the relative ordering of methods is largely preserved. The UNALIGNED BASELINE has a non-zero prudence score of 0.11 (compared to 0 on TriviaQA), indicating the base model occasionally produces idk-like responses on NQ-Open questions even without explicit honesty training — likely due to some questions naturally eliciting uncertainty expressions. The FINE-TUNED BASELINE accuracy drops to 45.16 (vs. 49.63 unaligned), again showing that forcing the model to produce gold answers on unknown NQ-Open questions degrades its existing knowledge.

**On PUQA (1,000 prior-unknown questions), CONFIDENCE-NUM achieves the highest prudence (87.30).** The key result here is that honesty-oriented fine-tuning methods reliably refuse on questions the model cannot possibly know. CONFIDENCE-NUM's 87.30 prudence means that on 87.3% of questions about 2023 papers it has never seen, the model correctly refuses. MULTISAMPLE reaches 86.20, CONFIDENCE-VERB reaches 79.90, and ABSOLUTE reaches 34.20. Even PROMPT-BASED achieves 28.90 — the model has some ability to recognize inherently unanswerable questions even without fine-tuning, but the honesty-oriented methods dramatically amplify this ability. The UNALIGNED BASELINE and FINE-TUNED BASELINE show 0 prudence (they never refuse), confirming that without explicit honesty training, models will confabulate answers to questions outside their knowledge.

**On PKQA (1,000 prior-known questions), CONFIDENCE-VERB achieves the best accuracy preservation.** The accuracy scores are: UNALIGNED 100.00 (by construction, since PKQA was filtered to include only questions the unaligned model answers correctly), CONFIDENCE-VERB 96.80, CONFIDENCE-NUM 96.00, ABSOLUTE 95.90, MULTISAMPLE 96.20, PROMPT-BASED 96.80. The small but non-zero over-conservativeness scores (CONFIDENCE-VERB at 3.60, ABSOLUTE at 8.00) mean the aligned models do occasionally refuse questions they demonstrably know, but the refusal rate is low. The high accuracy across all methods confirms that honesty alignment does not catastrophically degrade the model's ability to answer questions within its knowledge boundaries — the models remain above 95% accuracy on PKQA, and the gap between the best aligned method (CONFIDENCE-VERB, 96.80) and the baseline (100.00) is modest.

**The FINE-TUNED BASELINE shows a striking failure on PKQA: accuracy drops from 100.00 (unaligned) to 87.70 — a 12.3-point degradation.** This is the paper's clearest evidence for the "hallucination-by-fine-tuning" claim. The FINE-TUNED BASELINE was trained to produce gold answers on TriviaQA questions it couldn't answer, which taught it to confabulate rather than admit ignorance. This training generalizes negatively to PKQA: the model now sometimes produces wrong answers on questions it previously answered correctly, because the fine-tuning process taught it that when faced with a knowledge gap, it should guess rather than refuse. This finding directly supports the paper's argument that honesty alignment (teaching refusal on unknown questions) is not just beneficial but necessary to *prevent* the degradation that occurs when models are fine-tuned on knowledge they don't possess.

**Across all three OOD datasets, CONFIDENCE-VERB is the most consistently strong method**, achieving the best or near-best accuracy on Non-AmbigQA (49.54) and PKQA (96.80), and the third-best prudence on PUQA (79.90). Its overall pattern — strong honesty improvement with minimal accuracy degradation — transfers from in-distribution to out-of-distribution settings.

#### Alignment Tax Analysis (Table 5, Tables 21–22)

**The helpfulness tax of honesty alignment is negligible.** On the Eval-P− benchmark (1,320 queries spanning 55 scenarios across summarization, code generation, creative writing, functional writing, rewriting, general communication, and NLP tasks), evaluated by both AUTO-J (13B generative judge) and GPT-4 on a 1–10 scale:

| Method | AUTO-J | GPT-4 |
|---|---|---|
| UNALIGNED BASELINE | 5.56 | 8.62 |
| CONFIDENCE-VERB | 5.54 | 8.61 |
| MULTISAMPLE | 5.52 | 8.56 |

The maximum gap from the unaligned baseline is 0.06 points on GPT-4 (MULTISAMPLE) and 0.04 points on AUTO-J (MULTISAMPLE). These differences are well within normal sampling variance and across-judge variation, indicating that the models have learned to refuse on knowledge-intensive QA without generalizing refusal behavior to the diverse task types in Eval-P−. The paper does not report statistical significance tests for these differences, but the magnitude (0.02–0.06 on a 10-point scale) is small enough that the practical conclusion of "no meaningful tax" is well-supported.

**The per-category breakdown (Tables 21–22) shows no category where the aligned models collapse.** On GPT-4 ratings (Table 22): CONFIDENCE-VERB scores 8.86 on Summarization (vs. 8.73 baseline), 5.70 on Code (vs. 6.11 — the largest single-category gap at ~0.4 points), 8.81 on Rewriting (vs. 8.65), 9.26 on Creative Writing (vs. 9.31), 9.34 on Functional Writing (vs. 9.17 — actually slightly *higher* than baseline), 9.21 on General Communication (vs. 9.18 — also slightly higher), and 7.95 on NLP tasks (vs. 8.05). MULTISAMPLE shows similar patterns with slightly larger gaps on Code (5.69 vs. 6.11) and Rewriting (8.55 vs. 8.65). The Code category shows the most consistent small degradation across both methods and both judges, suggesting that honesty fine-tuning may have a minor but real effect on code generation quality. However, the magnitude is small (0.4–0.5 points) and the paper does not explore whether this is a genuine honesty tax or simply normal fine-tuning variance.

**The harmlessness check (Table 23, Appendix D.8) confirms safety is preserved.** On 700 potentially harmful prompts from BeaverTails-Evaluation, evaluated by GPT-4o using ShieldLM's safety assessment prompt (Zhang et al., 2024): the UNALIGNED BASELINE produces 666 safe, 0 unsafe, and 34 controversial responses; CONFIDENCE-VERB produces 662 safe, 0 unsafe, 38 controversial; MULTISAMPLE produces 669 safe, 0 unsafe, 31 controversial. The total safe + controversial counts are 700, 700, and 700 respectively — meaning no aligned model ever produced a response classified as unsafe. The slight shifts between "safe" and "controversial" are within normal evaluation noise and indicate no systematic safety degradation.

**The trend suggests larger models learn more effectively from honesty training.** The 70B model achieves a prudence of 51.44 with the lowest over-conservativeness (6.51), while the 7B model achieves a prudence of 56.04 with nearly twice the over-conservativeness (11.43). The paper interprets this as evidence that "larger models demonstrate enhanced capacities to learn from idk responses in the training data" (Appendix D.5.2), though the sample size (one model per scale) limits the strength of this conclusion.

| Qwen-Chat-7B | CONFIDENCE-VERB | 51.13 | 14.08 | **68.53** | 49.60 |
| Baichuan2-Chat-7B | UNALIGNED | 0 | 0 | 50.00 | 58.86 |
| Baichuan2-Chat-7B | PROMPT-BASED | 15.28 | 4.86 | 55.21 | 57.57 |
| Baichuan2-Chat-7B | CONFIDENCE-VERB | 64.53 | 15.80 | **74.37** | 51.24 |

The most dramatic result is **Qwen-Chat-7B with PROMPT-BASED**, which collapses to 1.46 accuracy — the model essentially stops producing valid responses under the prompt (Appendix D.5.3 notes it "cannot return valid replies"). Yet CONFIDENCE-VERB rescues this: the Qwen-aligned model achieves 68.53 honesty and 49.60 accuracy, actually *improving* upon the unaligned baseline's 44.43 accuracy. This demonstrates that fine-tuning can compensate for poor instruction-following ability that causes prompt-based approaches to fail.

InternLM-Chat-7B shows a modest accuracy drop (41.93 → 38.24, -3.69) with strong honesty improvement (50.00 → 70.81). Baichuan2-Chat-7B achieves the highest honesty score (74.37) among all tested non-LLaMA models, but with the largest accuracy drop (58.86 → 51.24, -7.62) and highest over-conservativeness (15.80). The variation in accuracy preservation across model families suggests that the base model's knowledge representation and instruction-following capabilities interact with honesty alignment in ways that affect the prudence/accuracy tradeoff — a phenomenon the paper does not deeply investigate but which represents natural model-specific variance.

**CONFIDENCE-VERB consistently improves honesty by 18–24 points across all four model families (LLaMA2, InternLM, Qwen, Baichuan2).** The minimum improvement is for Qwen-Chat-7B (50.00 → 68.53, +18.53) and the maximum is for Baichuan2-Chat-7B (50.00 → 74.37, +24.37). This cross-family consistency is strong evidence that the method is not exploiting LLaMA2-specific artifacts and genuinely teaches the general skill of knowledge-boundary recognition.

#### Generalization to Multiple-Choice QA (Table 20, Appendix D.6)

**On MMLU (~14,000 four-choice questions), the baseline and honesty-oriented methods initially show very limited refusal behavior.** Without data augmentation, CONFIDENCE-VERB achieves only a 2.60 prudence score and MULTISAMPLE achieves 9.53, dramatically lower than on free-form QA. The paper attributes this to the structure of multiple-choice questions: "when given choices, the model rarely refuses to answer even when allowed to reply with idk responses" (Appendix D.6). The model can always guess an option, and the 25% random baseline creates an incentive structure where idk (guaranteed wrong) is worse than guessing (possibly right). The UNALIGNED BASELINE achieves 47.17 accuracy, and honesty-oriented methods slightly improve accuracy (CONFIDENCE-VERB: 49.89, MULTISAMPLE: 49.90), suggesting fine-tuning helps the model select more correct options even when it doesn't refuse.

**Data augmentation with MMLU training examples partially addresses the refusal deficit.** Adding 284 deduplicated MMLU training examples to the existing 8,000 TriviaQA samples (a small ~3.5% augmentation): MULTISAMPLE sees its prudence score jump from 9.53 to 78.95, and its honesty score from 52.69 to 67.17. However, this comes at severe cost: accuracy drops from 49.90 to 33.73, and over-conservativeness explodes from 4.15 to 44.61. The paper identifies this as evidence that training on MMLU-style questions (where guessing is a viable strategy) to refuse creates a conflict — the model learns to refuse broadly but loses the ability to make educated guesses on the MMLU test set. CONFIDENCE-VERB with MMLU augmentation shows a more modest improvement: prudence from 2.60 to 14.64, honesty from 50.79 to 54.67, with accuracy essentially unchanged (49.89 → 48.82).

**The MMLU results expose a fundamental limitation: the honesty framework assumes a binary answer/refuse decision, but multiple-choice tasks admit a third strategy (educated guessing) that the training data doesn't teach.** The aligned model is trained to either answer confidently or refuse, but on MMLU, selectively guessing on low-confidence questions is rational. Since the training data provides no signal for this intermediate behavior, the model's refusal behavior on MMLU is unstable — strong refusal emerges only when MMLU-style questions are explicitly included in training, and it comes at the cost of over-refusing on questions where guessing would have been correct. The paper presents this as an open challenge rather than a solved problem.

#### Threshold Sensitivity (Figure 4, Appendix D.5.1)

**Increasing the refusal threshold $\tau$ smoothly trades off prudence against over-conservativeness in ABSOLUTE.** The paper varies $\tau \in \{0.1, 0.4, 0.7, 1.0\}$ and tracks the prudence and over-conservativeness scores (Figure 4). The result is monotonic: higher $\tau$ (requiring more consistent correctness to classify a question as "known") increases both prudence (the model refuses more unknown questions because the "known" set is smaller and more precisely defined) and over-conservativeness (the model refuses more known questions because borderline-known questions are now classified as unknown). The paper frames this as a controllable safety parameter: "increasing the refusal threshold is a straightforward way to obtain a safer model when users prioritize trustworthiness in the model's responses."

The default $\tau = 0.1$ is the most permissive setting, chosen for the main experiments to minimize over-conservativeness. The paper does not report the full honesty score across $\tau$ values, making it difficult to determine which threshold maximizes the composite metric. The qualitative message is that $\tau$ provides a post-hoc tuning knob that doesn't require architectural changes — a practical feature for deployment where different safety requirements apply.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Balancing known/unknown questions in training data:** The paper constructs the 8,000-sample training set to contain exactly equal numbers of known and unknown questions based on the base model's single response at temperature = 0 (Appendix D.3). This balance constraint is not ablated — we don't know how sensitive the results are to the known/unknown ratio. An imbalanced training set (e.g., 90% known, 10% unknown) might produce different refusal behavior. This is a missing ablation that would help practitioners understand how carefully they need to control class balance.

**Number of samples for expected accuracy estimation ($m$):** The paper fixes $m = 10$ throughout all experiments without ablation. The choice of 10 is a practical compromise between estimation accuracy and computational cost (80,000 total generations for training data construction). We don't know whether $m = 5$ would produce similar results (halving the data labeling cost) or whether $m = 20$ would significantly improve the accuracy of expected accuracy estimates and thus improve alignment quality. This is an important practical consideration that the paper leaves unexplored — for a model developer wanting to implement these methods, the sensitivity to $m$ is a key unknown.

**Training epochs for MULTISAMPLE:** The paper trains MULTISAMPLE for 1 epoch (vs. 2 for ABSOLUTE and CONFIDENCE) because MULTISAMPLE expands the training data by $m = 10\times$. This is a reasonable choice to maintain comparable numbers of gradient updates, but the paper does not verify that 1 epoch is optimal for MULTISAMPLE or that 2 epochs for ABSOLUTE/CONFIDENCE couldn't be improved with different epoch counts. Given that MULTISAMPLE achieves the highest honesty score despite fewer epochs, it's possible that the training is already near-optimal, but no learning curve analysis is provided.

**Choice of idk response phrasing:** All methods use the same fixed idk response string: "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question." The paper does not ablate this choice — we don't know whether different refusal phrasings (shorter, more direct, different tone) would affect the model's willingness to use them or the downstream perception of honesty. Similarly, the heuristic rules for detecting idk responses at evaluation time (Appendix D.1: `[i apologize, not aware of, not familiar with, not make sense, i'm not able to, however, i must point out]`) are fixed. If a model learned to refuse using phrasing outside these heuristics, it would be misclassified. The paper does not report how often this occurs or how sensitive results are to the heuristic list.

**CONFIDENCE-NUM vs. CONFIDENCE-VERB:** This comparison is not a formal ablation but a design variant. CONFIDENCE-VERB achieves slightly lower honesty (74.12 vs. 74.37) but better accuracy (73.34 vs. 69.80) compared to CONFIDENCE-NUM. The paper doesn't deeply analyze why verbal expressions outperform numerical ones for accuracy preservation — it only notes that "the high accuracy likely results from multiple factors intertwined" and leaves full disentanglement to future work. This is a limitation: the mechanism by which confidence expression format affects accuracy is not understood, making it unclear whether the verbal advantage would hold across different models or tasks.

**Temperature = 0 for evaluation (vs. temperature = 1 for data generation):** All evaluation uses greedy decoding (temperature = 0), while expected accuracy estimation uses temperature = 1 (to get diverse samples). The paper doesn't discuss how this temperature mismatch affects the relationship between expected accuracy (computed at T=1) and the model's actual greedy behavior. If the model's greedy decisions are systematically different from its sampling distribution (e.g., it's more deterministic on known questions), the expected accuracy estimate may not perfectly reflect its greedy knowledge boundaries. This could partially explain the non-zero over-conservativeness — questions where the model samples correctly only occasionally at T=1 but consistently answers correctly at T=0 would be classified as unknown (or weakly known) under expected accuracy, teaching the model to refuse on questions it actually knows under greedy decoding.

**Training data source (TriviaQA only):** All honesty training uses only TriviaQA. The out-of-distribution results on Non-AmbigQA, PUQA, and PKQA show generalization, but the paper doesn't test whether training on a different knowledge QA dataset (e.g., Natural Questions, WebQuestions) would produce similar or better results. The choice of TriviaQA may influence which types of knowledge boundaries the model learns to recognize — TriviaQA questions tend to be trivia/factoid style, and the model might learn dataset-specific cues rather than genuine knowledge-boundary recognition. The strong PUQA performance (87.30 prudence for CONFIDENCE-NUM on questions about 2023 papers) argues against pure dataset overfitting, but training on multiple QA datasets or a more diverse mixture would strengthen the case.

**Full fine-tuning vs. parameter-efficient methods:** The paper uses full parameter fine-tuning for all experiments. No comparison to LoRA, prefix tuning, or other parameter-efficient methods is provided. For practitioners with limited compute, knowing whether honesty alignment works with parameter-efficient fine-tuning would be valuable. The paper leaves this as an unexplored dimension.

**Evaluation of response correctness via ChatGPT:** The two-step ChatGPT-based evaluation pipeline (extract answer, compare to gold) introduces potential noise from the judge model. The paper does not report inter-annotator agreement between ChatGPT and human evaluation, nor does it analyze cases where ChatGPT's answer extraction or consistency judgment might be wrong. The use of gpt-3.5-turbo-0613 for evaluation (specified in Section 4.2) fixes the judge model version, ensuring reproducibility, but the reliability of the judge is not independently validated.

**Negative result (ReST$^{EM}$ not applicable here):** Unlike the reference example paper which included a negative result (ReST$^{EM}$ revision model degradation), this paper doesn't have a directly analogous ablation failure. The closest negative finding is the FINE-TUNED BASELINE's accuracy degradation on PKQA (Table 4: 100.00 → 87.70), which serves as a cautionary result demonstrating why standard supervised fine-tuning on unknown knowledge is harmful. The MMLU data augmentation results (Table 20: MULTISAMPLE's accuracy crashing to 33.73 when adding MMLU data) are also a partial negative — they show that blindly applying the same training methodology to multiple-choice questions creates a refusal/guessing conflict that degrades performance.

### Critical Assessment

#### Does the paper demonstrate that honesty alignment improves model honesty?

**Yes, substantially and across multiple settings.** The honesty score improvements are large and consistent: from 50.00 baseline to 68.88–75.91 across methods on in-distribution TriviaQA (Table 3), with similar patterns on Non-AmbigQA (68.74 for CONFIDENCE-VERB, Table 4). The prudence scores show that aligned models genuinely refuse on 47–68% of previously-wrong or previously-unknown questions in-distribution, and 79–87% on guaranteed-unknown questions (PUQA, Table 4). These are not marginal improvements — they represent a qualitative change in model behavior, from essentially never refusing (baseline prudence ~0) to routinely refusing on appropriate questions.

The cross-model-family results (Table 19) and cross-scale results (Table 18) both support the claim that the method generalizes beyond LLaMA2-Chat-13B. The OOD results on PUQA demonstrate that the refusal behavior transfers to genuinely unknown questions the model was never trained on, ruling out the hypothesis that the model is simply memorizing which specific TriviaQA questions to refuse.

**However, the magnitude of honesty improvement depends critically on how one weights prudence vs. over-conservativeness.** The honesty score (Equation 7) weights them equally, but applications may differ. MULTISAMPLE's prudence of 67.72 is substantially higher than CONFIDENCE-VERB's 58.91, but its over-conservativeness is also higher (15.89 vs. 10.68). If an application penalizes over-refusal more heavily than under-refusal, CONFIDENCE-VERB would be preferred; if catching all unknown questions is paramount, MULTISAMPLE would be preferred. The paper's honesty score collapses this tradeoff into a single number, which simplifies comparison but masks application-dependent preference heterogeneity. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by reporting all three metrics separately, but doesn't provide guidance on how to weight them for different use cases.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that the alignment tax is low?

**Yes, for helpfulness and harmlessness on the tested benchmarks.** The evidence in Table 5 (helpfulness within 0.02–0.06 points of baseline) and Table 23 (zero unsafe responses, unchanged safety profile) is convincing for the specific benchmarks used. The detailed per-category breakdowns (Tables 21–22) show no hidden catastrophic failures — every task category maintains performance close to baseline.

**However, the tested helpfulness tasks (Eval-P−) explicitly exclude exam questions and other knowledge-intensive QA formats** (Appendix C.5). This means the tax analysis does not measure whether honesty alignment makes the model *less helpful on knowledge-intensive tasks*. For example, if a user asks a borderline-known question, an honesty-aligned model might refuse where the baseline would have given a (possibly correct) answer. Whether this constitutes a helpfulness tax or appropriate caution depends on the application, but the Eval-P− benchmark cannot capture it because it was deliberately constructed to exclude such questions. A more complete tax analysis would include knowledge-intensive QA tasks with varying difficulty to measure how often the aligned model's refusals replace correct answers vs. incorrect answers.

Additionally, the helpfulness evaluation uses only two automated judges (AUTO-J and GPT-4). While both are established in the literature, neither is a human evaluation. The paper does not report whether humans perceive the aligned models as more or less helpful, which matters because user perception of refusal (even appropriate refusal) might differ from judge-model ratings. A model that says "I don't know" to 30% of questions might be perceived as less capable, even if those refusals are correct.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that expected accuracy is a valid proxy for knowledge boundaries?

**The evidence is mixed and the claim requires careful qualification.** The expected accuracy approach works in the sense that training on expected-accuracy-labeled data produces models that refuse on questions they genuinely cannot answer (high PUQA prudence) while maintaining accuracy on questions they can answer (high PKQA accuracy). This is a behavioral validation: the method produces the desired behavior, which is what matters for an alignment intervention.

**However, the paper does not validate that expected accuracy accurately reflects the model's internal knowledge state.** There is no experiment that probes whether questions classified as "unknown" (expected accuracy < 0.1) are genuinely outside the model's knowledge vs. questions the model knows but struggles to access under the specific sampling conditions. The assumption that incorrect responses indicate fabrication rather than faithfully expressed false beliefs (Section 5.1) is reasonable for TriviaQA but is an assumption, not an empirically verified fact within this paper. A model could theoretically know a fact but be unable to express it in the free-form generation format used — it might fail the string-match correctness test not because it doesn't know the answer but because it expresses the answer in a form that doesn't match the gold string. The paper's evaluation pipeline mitigates this somewhat by using ChatGPT-based answer extraction rather than strict string matching, but the fundamental identification of "can't answer correctly" with "doesn't know" remains an approximation.

The limitation section (Section 5.2) explicitly acknowledges this as future work: "More advanced approaches to define $k(\cdot)$... should explore more sophisticated methods to determine if the model 'knows' the answer." This is honest about the limitation, but it means the paper's knowledge boundary identification is best understood as a practical heuristic that works, not as a principled solution to the knowledge boundary problem.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that MULTISAMPLE's per-response labeling is superior?

**Yes, but the mechanism is not fully disentangled.** MULTISAMPLE achieves the highest honesty (75.91) and highest prudence (67.72) in the main comparison (Table 3), but it also has the highest over-conservativeness (15.89) and the lowest accuracy among honesty-oriented methods (68.88). Whether this represents a genuine improvement depends on the application's prudence/accuracy preference.

**There is a confound: MULTISAMPLE sees 10× more training examples than ABSOLUTE or CONFIDENCE** (each question contributes $m=10$ examples vs. 1). The paper trains MULTISAMPLE for 1 epoch vs. 2 for the other methods, attempting to control for total gradient steps, but the relationship between epochs and performance is not ablated. A potential alternative explanation is that MULTISAMPLE's advantage comes from seeing more diverse training examples (multiple correct answers, multiple questions paired with idk) rather than from the per-response labeling mechanism per se. An ablation that trained ABSOLUTE with $m=10$ different correct answers per known question (keeping the binary per-question labeling) would help disentangle data diversity effects from the per-response labeling mechanism. The paper does not include such an ablation.

#### Does the paper demonstrate that honesty alignment outperforms simpler alternatives?

**Yes, compared to the two alternatives tested: prompting and standard fine-tuning.** PROMPT-BASED (Table 3) achieves only 60.64 honesty with a 9-point accuracy drop, clearly inferior to all fine-tuning methods. FINE-TUNED BASELINE (Table 3) achieves 50.00 honesty (no refusal at all) and degrades accuracy on PKQA by 12 points (Table 4), demonstrating that standard accuracy-oriented fine-tuning is harmful when applied to questions the model doesn't know.

**However, the paper does not compare against several plausible alternative approaches:**

1. **Inference-time confidence thresholding:** Rather than training the model to refuse, one could compute confidence (e.g., via sampling or internal probabilities) at inference time and refuse when confidence falls below a threshold. Kadavath et al. (2022) showed models can self-evaluate their answers. The paper doesn't compare its training-based approach against inference-time self-evaluation + thresholding, which would be a natural baseline — it uses less training compute and might achieve comparable refusal behavior.

2. **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG):** The paper explicitly positions itself as not relying on external knowledge (Section 1), but from a practical standpoint, RAG is a widely-used alternative to honesty alignment. If the model can retrieve relevant documents and answer based on them, it can answer more questions correctly, reducing the need for refusal. A comparison showing that honesty-aligned refusal is preferable to (or complementary with) retrieval-based approaches would strengthen the practical case.

3. **RLHF for honesty:** The paper uses only supervised fine-tuning. An RLHF variant that directly optimizes the honesty value function $v(x, y)$ (Equation 4) using preference pairs (refuse on unknown questions = good, answer incorrectly on unknown questions = bad) might produce different results. The paper doesn't explore this despite the value function formulation in Section 2.2 being naturally suited to RLHF.

#### Are there significant weaknesses in the experimental design?

**Single knowledge domain (factoid QA).** All training and primary evaluation is on factoid question-answering (TriviaQA). The paper does not test on procedural knowledge ("how do I..."), definitional knowledge ("what is..."), or reasoning-intensive knowledge tasks. It's unclear whether the expected accuracy approach would work for questions where "correctness" is harder to determine automatically (e.g., summarization quality, argument quality) or where the model's knowledge is partial rather than binary.

**Reliance on gold answer string matching.** The entire framework depends on being able to automatically determine whether a model response is correct by checking for the gold answer. For open-ended generation tasks, this infrastructure doesn't exist. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by focusing exclusively on short-answer QA, but doesn't discuss how the framework would extend to other task types.

**No human evaluation of refusal appropriateness.** All correctness judgments are automated (ChatGPT-based answer extraction + gold answer matching). While this scales well, it means we don't know whether humans agree that the model's refusals are appropriate. A human evaluation on a sample of the test set would validate that the automated metrics correspond to human judgments of honesty.

**Test set contamination risk.** The TriviaQA evaluation set is publicly available and widely used. LLaMA2-Chat models may have been trained on TriviaQA data (the paper doesn't verify this). If the model has memorized TriviaQA answers, the expected accuracy estimates and the evaluation metrics could be contaminated. The PUQA dataset (constructed from 2023 papers, guaranteed to be post-training) partially addresses this by providing a contamination-free evaluation, but the main TriviaQA results might overestimate the model's knowledge and the alignment method's effectiveness.

**Training data size is fixed at 8,000.** The paper doesn't ablate training set size. We don't know whether 1,000 examples would work nearly as well (making the method much cheaper) or whether 50,000 examples would yield further improvements. For practitioners, understanding the data efficiency of these methods is important. The fact that MULTISAMPLE expands 8,000 questions into ~80,000 training examples and achieves the best results hints that data quantity matters, but no systematic scaling analysis is provided.

**The $m=10$ sampling cost is significant and unrecovered.** Generating 10 samples per training question costs 80,000 total generations for data labeling, which is comparable to the cost of fine-tuning itself. The paper doesn't discuss whether this cost could be amortized (e.g., by reusing expected accuracy estimates across model versions) or reduced (e.g., by using fewer samples for obviously known/unknown questions). The one-time nature of this cost makes it acceptable for research, but for production pipelines that regularly update models, the labeling cost would recur with each new base model version.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Expected Accuracy Computation Is Prohibitively Expensive for Deployment Pipelines

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire training data construction pipeline depends on estimating expected accuracy by sampling $m = 10$ responses per question from the base model $M_t$ at temperature = 1. For the 8,000-question training set, this requires 80,000 generations — a one-time cost that the paper treats as fixed overhead and does not factor into any efficiency analysis. The paper explicitly acknowledges this cost in Section 3.2:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

This is a direct parallel to the reference example paper, where the difficulty estimation cost (2,048 samples per question) was flagged as the primary practical bottleneck. The paper also notes in Section 5.2 that "more sophisticated methods to determine if the model 'knows'" are needed as future work.

**The consequence.** For any production pipeline where models are regularly updated (new pretraining runs, new fine-tuning data, new RLHF iterations), the labeling cost recurs with each new base model version. The $m=10$ choice was not ablated, so practitioners have no guidance on whether $m=3$ or $m=5$ would suffice — potentially 2–3× cheaper labeling with minimal honesty degradation. Furthermore, if the base model is large (70B+), 80,000 generations becomes a substantial compute expense that might rival the fine-tuning cost itself, making the total cost of honesty alignment significantly higher than the training-alone budget the paper reports.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not ablate $m$ at all. All experiments fix $m=10$ (Table 3 caption: "ABSOLUTE applies $m = 10$"). There is no experiment showing whether $m=5$ produces comparable expected accuracy estimates or alignment quality, nor any analysis of how the quality of expected accuracy estimates degrades with fewer samples. The scalability results in Table 18 use the same $m=10$ without discussing whether larger models might need more or fewer samples for reliable estimation. The limitation section (Section 5.2) explicitly calls this out as future work — "More advanced approaches to define $k(\cdot)$" — but offers no intermediate cheaper alternative.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not:
- Provide an ablation over $m$ to show whether smaller sample sizes suffice
- Discuss amortization strategies (reusing expected accuracy estimates across model versions that share the same base knowledge)
- Explore adaptive sampling (fewer samples for obviously known/unknown questions, more for borderline cases)
- Measure the labeling cost as a fraction of total alignment cost
- Compare against inference-time alternatives (e.g., Kadavath et al., 2022's self-evaluation) that avoid the labeling cost entirely

The $4\times$ efficiency gain language used in the reference example paper has a direct analog here: the honesty improvements in Table 3 (e.g., 50.00 → 75.91) are real, but they are achieved at a training-data-construction cost that is unaccounted for, making the true cost of achieving those honesty scores higher than the fine-tuning FLOPs alone suggest.

### 6.2 Single Knowledge Domain: All Results Are on Short-Answer Factoid QA

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire framework — training data construction, evaluation metrics, and the MULTISAMPLE/ABSOLUTE/CONFIDENCE methods — depends on the ability to automatically determine whether a model response is "correct" by checking for the presence of a gold answer string. This works for short-answer factoid QA (TriviaQA, NQ-Open) where answers are brief noun phrases (e.g., "George Washington," "Gimli," "Daniel Barenboim"). The paper does not test on any other knowledge format: procedural knowledge ("How do I fix a leaky faucet?"), definitional knowledge requiring long-form explanation ("What is quantum entanglement?"), reasoning-intensive knowledge tasks, or open-ended generation where "correctness" is multi-dimensional or subjective.

The paper is transparent that its scope is limited — all experiments use TriviaQA for training and TriviaQA-derived or similarly-structured datasets (Non-AmbigQA, PUQA, PKQA) for evaluation. The taxonomy in Appendix A (Tables 6–7) distinguishes honesty from truthfulness, factuality, and calibration, but all empirical work operates within the narrow band where factoid correctness can be automatically verified.

**The consequence.** The expected accuracy computation — the foundation of all three training methods — cannot be straightforwardly applied to tasks where automatic correctness checking is unavailable or unreliable. For summarization, the model might know the content but express it differently from a reference summary; for creative writing, there is no single correct output; for complex reasoning, intermediate steps might be correct even if the final answer format differs from the gold string. The honesty framework as presented offers no path for these domains because both the $k(x)$ approximation and the training data annotation step rely on binary correct/incorrect determinations via string matching (even with the ChatGPT-based extraction pipeline softening the match; Appendix C.1).

Even within factoid QA, the method assumes that correctness implies knowledge and incorrectness implies fabrication. For questions where the model learned false beliefs during training (the "faithful hallucination" case in Table 6), the expected accuracy approach would wrongly classify the question as "unknown" (because the model's answer doesn't match world knowledge) when the model actually "knows" its (false) answer and is being honest by producing it. This is the honesty-vs-truthfulness distinction the paper discusses in Section 5.1, but the empirical framework cannot distinguish these cases — it always uses world knowledge as the correctness arbiter.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The limitation is visible in the datasets used: all evaluation is on short-answer QA with gold answer strings (TriviaQA evaluation set: 9,960 samples; Non-AmbigQA: 5,325; PUQA: 1,000 author-identification questions; PKQA: 1,000 factoid questions). The MMLU results (Table 20) expose the fragility: when the task format changes to multiple-choice — still factually verifiable but with a different response structure — the methods largely fail to produce consistent refusal behavior without domain-specific data augmentation, and even then the augmentation produces unstable results (MULTISAMPLE accuracy crashing from 49.90 to 33.73). The helpfulness evaluation (Eval-P−, Section 4.6) explicitly excludes knowledge-intensive tasks (Appendix C.5: "scenarios that differ significantly from knowledge-intensive QA tasks"), meaning it cannot measure whether honesty alignment affects helpfulness on borderline-known factoid questions.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges this implicitly through its dataset choices but does not discuss:
- How expected accuracy would be computed for tasks without gold answers
- Whether the framework extends to long-form generation, reasoning chains, or procedural knowledge
- Whether model-generated correctness judgments (self-evaluation) could replace gold-answer matching in domains where string matching fails
- The helpness tax specifically on knowledge-intensive tasks (which Eval-P− deliberately excludes)

The limitation section (Section 5.2) mentions "further exploration of uncertainty expressions" and "representation-level alignment" as future directions, but does not engage with the fundamental domain restriction. A practitioner interested in honesty alignment for a medical QA system (where answers are often multi-sentence explanations, not short strings) or a code generation system (where correctness is functional, not string-matchable) would find no guidance in this paper.

### 6.3 The Unaligned Baseline for Evolutionary Metrics Is Never Honest: The Framework Cannot Measure Absolute Honesty

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's evaluation metrics (prudence score, over-conservativeness score, honesty score) are defined as *comparative changes* between the unaligned model $M_t$ and the aligned model $M_{t+1}$. This is a deliberate design choice motivated by the difficulty of defining what the model "truly knows" (Section 2.2, Section 2.3):

> "It is often challenging to understand the model's internal workings, i.e., whether knowledge is known or unknown... However, what we can access is the model's external behaviors in terms of answering correctly or incorrectly."

The honesty score always measures improvement *relative to the starting point*, with the unaligned model anchored at 50.00 by definition (since it never refuses, $S_{\text{prudence}} = 0, S_{\text{over-consv.}} = 0$). This means the metric cannot answer the question: "How honest is this model in absolute terms?" — it can only answer: "How much more honest is this version compared to the previous version?"

**The consequence.** This design creates a fundamental limitation for comparing honesty across different base models or alignment methods that start from different baselines. If Model A starts with 80% accuracy on a test set and achieves 75.91 honesty after MULTISAMPLE alignment, and Model B starts with 40% accuracy and achieves the same 75.91 honesty after alignment, these represent very different underlying behaviors — Model B is refusing on a much larger fraction of questions — but the metric reports them identically. The evolutionary metric conflates the base model's capability with the alignment method's effectiveness.

More subtly, the reliance on $M_t$ as the reference point means that if $M_t$ already has some non-zero honesty behavior (as observed with LLaMA2-Chat-70B in Table 18, where the unaligned baseline has a prudence score of 0.19), the evolutionary metrics become harder to interpret. A 70B model that starts at 50.10 honesty and reaches 71.27 after CONFIDENCE-VERB (Table 18) has gained 21.17 points — but is this better or worse than the 13B model that starts at 50.00 and reaches 74.12 (a gain of 24.12)? The absolute honesty level matters for deployment decisions (which model should I use?), but the evolutionary framework cannot express it.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The limitation is visible across the results: all honesty scores are computed from the $M_t \rightarrow M_{t+1}$ transition, with $M_t$ being LLaMA2-Chat at different scales (Table 18) or different model families (Table 19). The 70B model's unaligned baseline prudence of 0.19 (Table 18) demonstrates that larger models may start with non-zero refusal tendencies, making cross-scale comparisons using the evolutionary framework ambiguous. The paper does not compute any absolute honesty metric that would enable direct comparison between, say, MULTISAMPLE-aligned LLaMA2-Chat-13B and CONFIDENCE-VERB-aligned Qwen-Chat-7B.

The paper explicitly acknowledges this design choice in Section 2.3: "We can further extend the definition to higher-order functions of $c(\cdot)$ from different iterations, which will enable us to characterize the model's alignment behavior in a finer-grained way. This exploration will be left for future study." This is an honest admission that the current metrics are first-order approximations of a richer space, but it also means the framework cannot answer questions about absolute honesty levels.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the limitation (Section 2.3) and gestures toward higher-order extensions as future work. However, no alternative absolute metric is proposed or discussed. A potential fix — defining honesty relative to an idealized perfectly-knowledgeable model rather than relative to $M_t$ — is not explored, likely because it would require solving the knowledge boundary problem that the paper side-steps by using evolutionary comparison.

For practitioners, this means the honesty score cannot be used to answer: "Should I deploy this aligned model or train a larger model from scratch?" The FLOPs-matched comparison framework from the reference example paper has no analog here — there's no way to compare the "honesty per unit compute" of different approaches because the metric is inherently relative to each model's own starting point.

### 6.4 The PKQA Construction Guarantees Near-Perfect Accuracy by Design — Masking Over-Conservatism

**The assumption or constraint.** The PKQA (Prior Known QA) dataset is constructed by having the model itself generate questions, filtering to keep only those where the unaligned model answers correctly. As described in Appendix C.3: "we also exclude questions that the unaligned model cannot answer correctly. In the end, we collect 1,000 simple knowledge-intensive questions that are highly likely to be known to the model." This means PKQA is explicitly filtered to contain only questions with baseline accuracy of 100.00 (as confirmed in Table 4).

**The consequence.** The PKQA accuracy numbers in Table 4 (CONFIDENCE-VERB: 96.80, MULTISAMPLE: 96.20, ABSOLUTE: 95.90) appear reassuring because they are close to 100.00. However, they are computed on a dataset that was curated to be maximally easy for the baseline model. This means PKQA measures over-conservatism only on the subset of questions that are *easiest* for the model — those it answers correctly at temperature = 0 in a single attempt. Questions that the model "knows" but might answer correctly only 60% or 80% of the time (the weakly-known but still-known questions) are systematically excluded from PKQA.

The consequence is that **PKQA underestimates over-conservatism**. The over-conservativeness scores reported from the TriviaQA evaluation set (Table 3: 9.94 for ABSOLUTE, 10.68 for CONFIDENCE-VERB, 15.89 for MULTISAMPLE) are likely more representative of the true over-refusal rate on questions the model has partial knowledge of. But PKQA, by construction, can only show over-conservatism on questions the model knows exceptionally well — and even there, the aligned models refuse 3.2–8.0% of the time (100.00 minus the accuracy). If the model refuses ~4% of its best-known questions, the refusal rate on moderately-known questions is almost certainly higher, but PKQA cannot measure this.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The PKQA construction methodology is described in Appendix C.3, which explicitly states the correctness filtering step. The paper presents the high PKQA accuracies as evidence that "honesty-oriented fine-tuning methods, with low cost, genuinely adapt to react differently to known/unknown questions" (Section 4.5). But the filtering step means PKQA tests only a specific subset of known questions — the strongest-known ones. The over-conservativeness scores on TriviaQA (Table 3, 9.94–15.89) are substantially higher than the implied PKQA over-refusal rates (3.2–8.0%), consistent with PKQA being an overly optimistic test of over-conservatism.

The paper does not acknowledge this bias explicitly. It treats PKQA as a test of whether the model "maintains accuracy on known questions it wasn't trained on," which is partially true but incomplete — it maintains accuracy on *strongly-known* questions and doesn't test weakly-known questions at all.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not:
- Construct a dataset stratified by expected accuracy to measure over-conservatism at different confidence levels
- Report over-conservatism scores binned by the model's baseline accuracy on TriviaQA
- Acknowledge that PKQA tests only the easiest subset of known questions
- Discuss whether the ~4% refusal rate on PKQA (the very easiest questions) should be considered acceptable or concerning from a deployment perspective

A practitioner evaluating whether to deploy CONFIDENCE-VERB might look at PKQA's 96.80 accuracy and conclude the model preserves nearly all of its knowledge — when in reality, the TriviaQA over-conservativeness of 10.68 (Table 3) and accuracy drop of ~0.4 points (73.71 → 73.34) is the more representative measure of what users will experience in practice.

### 6.5 No Comparison Against Inference-Time Self-Evaluation or Retrieval-Augmented Approaches

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper frames honesty alignment exclusively as a training-time intervention — supervised fine-tuning on self-labeled data — and compares only against other training-time approaches (prompting, standard fine-tuning) and an unaligned baseline. The two most natural alternative paradigms for achieving similar behavior are never evaluated:

1. **Inference-time self-evaluation with thresholding**: Kadavath et al. (2022) demonstrated that models can self-evaluate the correctness of their own answers when explicitly prompted ("Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know"). A simple alternative to the paper's approach would be: for each question, ask the model to answer, then ask the model to evaluate its own answer, and refuse if the self-evaluation confidence is below a threshold. This requires no training and no data labeling.

2. **Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)**: Instead of training the model to refuse on unknown questions, equip it with a retrieval system that provides relevant documents, enabling it to answer more questions correctly. The questions the model still can't answer correctly after retrieval are likely genuinely unknown. RAG is a widely-deployed alternative that addresses the hallucination problem through external knowledge rather than internal honesty.

**The consequence.** Without these comparisons, the paper cannot establish that **training for honesty is preferable to simpler inference-time or retrieval-based alternatives** for reducing confabulation. The inference-time self-evaluation approach would be dramatically cheaper (no fine-tuning, no labeling cost) and might achieve comparable refusal behavior on obviously unknown questions. The RAG approach might completely eliminate the need for refusal on many questions by providing the model with the knowledge it lacks, changing the honesty problem from "refuse on unknown questions" to "answer based on retrieved documents, and perhaps refuse when retrieval fails."

The paper's omission of these comparisons is particularly notable given that it explicitly discusses both self-evaluation (Section 2, citing Kadavath et al. 2022: "LLMs can self-evaluate whether their own answers are correct") and retrieval augmentation (Appendix B: "A series of studies retrieve external knowledge as supplementary evidence to assist LLMs in providing truthful responses"). The paper acknowledges these as related work but never tests whether they are competitive alternatives to the proposed approach.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is no experiment comparing honesty-oriented fine-tuning against inference-time self-evaluation or RAG. The only baseline that does not modify model parameters is PROMPT-BASED (Table 3), which uses a static prompt to permit refusal but does not include self-evaluation of generated answers. The paper briefly mentions RAG in Appendix B as related work but explicitly positions itself as "investigating the potential of aligning for honesty, empowering LLMs to autonomously abstain from answering unknown questions without being overly cautious" — without retrieval. The choice to exclude RAG is a deliberate scoping decision, not an oversight, but it means the paper cannot claim that honesty alignment is the best approach to the broader problem of reducing confabulation; it can only claim it is an effective approach within the no-retrieval paradigm.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper acknowledges self-evaluation as prior work (Section 2: "this line of work focuses on *detection* rather than *behavioral change*") and RAG as related work (Appendix B: "these methods do not explicitly endow the model the ability to refuse"), but never empirically tests whether detection-based approaches at inference time can achieve comparable refusal behavior without the training cost. The claim that training is necessary (implicit in the motivation for going beyond the PROMPT-BASED baseline) is plausible but unverified against the strongest inference-time alternative.

For a practitioner, this is a consequential gap: if a few-shot prompt asking the model to self-evaluate and optionally refuse achieves, say, 55.00 honesty with no training cost, while CONFIDENCE-VERB achieves 74.12 with 80,000-generation labeling cost plus fine-tuning, the cost-benefit analysis depends on the application's tolerance for imperfect refusal vs. budget for training. The paper provides no data to inform this decision.

### 6.6 Honesty Scores Are Unvalidated Against Human Judgments of Trustworthiness

**The assumption or constraint.** All evaluation of the aligned models' honesty uses automated metrics (prudence score, over-conservativeness score, honesty score, accuracy) computed through the comparison framework in Section 2.3, with correctness determined by ChatGPT-based answer extraction and gold-answer matching (Appendix C.1). The paper does not conduct any human evaluation of whether the aligned models' responses are perceived as more honest, more trustworthy, or more appropriate by actual users.

This is not a small omission — honesty is fundamentally a human-perceived quality. The paper's definition is grounded in the Confucian maxim ("To say 'I know' when you know, and 'I don't know' when you don't"), which is a human value judgment. Whether users agree that a model refusing with "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question" is being appropriately honest — versus frustratingly evasive, or worse, hiding knowledge it actually possesses — is an empirical question the automated metrics cannot answer.

**The consequence.** There are several ways the automated metrics could diverge from human-perceived honesty:

- **Refusal phrasing perception:** The canned idk response — "I apologize, but I'm not able to provide an answer to the question" — is a single fixed string. Users might perceive a model that always uses this exact phrasing as robotic, evasive, or failing to distinguish between "I genuinely don't know" and "I might know but I'm not sure." The over-conservativeness score measures whether the model refused a question it previously answered correctly, but it cannot measure whether users *agree* that the refusal was appropriate (some refusals on known questions might be perceived as appropriately cautious rather than overly conservative).

- **Confidence expressions in CONFIDENCE methods:** CONFIDENCE-VERB makes the model say things like "I'm absolutely certain that George Washington was the first president" or "I'm really not sure about this, but Gimli is the dwarf." The paper does not evaluate whether these confidence expressions are calibrated (Section 5.2: "calibrating the model's output confidence is beyond the scope of our work"). A model that says it's "absolutely certain" while being wrong 5% of the time might be perceived as overconfident and thus dishonest, even though it answered correctly. The automated metrics (which only check for correct answer presence) cannot detect this.

- **The trade-off between accuracy and refusal:** Users may prefer a model that always guesses (UNALIGNED BASELINE, 73.71 accuracy, 50.00 honesty) over one that frequently refuses (MULTISAMPLE, 68.88 accuracy, 75.91 honesty), even if the refusals are appropriate. The perception of helpfulness depends on whether users value knowing when the model is uncertain, vs. always getting an answer they can try to verify themselves. The helpfulness evaluation in Table 5 uses automated judges (AUTO-J, GPT-4) on non-knowledge tasks (Eval-P−), so it sheds no light on this within-domain preference.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper contains zero human evaluation. The helpness evaluation (Table 5) uses automated judges, and even the per-category helpfulness breakdown (Tables 21–22) is entirely judge-model-based. The harmlessness evaluation (Table 23) uses GPT-4o with ShieldLM's prompt — also automated. While the paper uses well-established automated evaluation methods, none capture human perception of the core construct being studied (honesty).

The paper reports that MULTISAMPLE achieves the highest honesty score (75.91, Table 3) and states it "can be trusted most by users" — this is a claim about human trust that is supported only by automated metrics, not by any measurement of human trust or perception.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not:
- Conduct a human evaluation of perceived honesty on a sample of the test set
- Measure whether users prefer honesty-aligned models over the unaligned baseline when both are presented side-by-side
- Evaluate whether the specific refusal phrasing or confidence expressions affect user trust
- Discuss this limitation in Section 5 (Limitations and Future Work), which focuses on conceptual limitations of $k(x)$ and calibration rather than on the evaluation gap

This is particularly striking given that the paper frames its contribution in terms of making models "more trustworthy" (Abstract: "making the deployment of an honest LLM more cost-effective while maintaining its usability and reliability") and claims that MULTISAMPLE produces a model that "can be trusted most by users" (Section 4.4.1). These are human-centric claims backed by automated metrics that have unknown correlation with human trust judgments. For a practitioner deciding whether to deploy honesty-aligned models in a user-facing application, this is the single most important missing piece of evidence.
