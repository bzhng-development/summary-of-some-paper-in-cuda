# EMU: Generative Pretraining in Multimodality

**ArXiv:** [2307.05222](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05222)

## 🎯 Pitch

Emu shows that a single autoregressive model trained to predict the next *element*—whether text token or visual embedding—can both understand and generate images, outperforming specialist models like Flamingo. This unification unlocks in-context learning across modalities, enabling the model to generate images from interleaved text-visual prompts.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Emu**, a multimodal foundation model that unifies image and text generation under a single autoregressive training objective—**predicting the next element in multimodality**—where the model learns to either classify the next text token or regress the next visual embedding in an interleaved multimodal sequence. Trained on diverse data sources including image-text pairs (LAION-2B), interleaved documents (MMC4), and a newly introduced interleaved video-text dataset (YT-Storyboard-1B) with a total of 14B parameters end-to-end, Emu achieves a zero-shot CIDEr score of 112.4 on COCO captioning—substantially outperforming comparable large multimodal models—and demonstrates that a **Causal Transformer** (which converts 2D visual encodings into a 1D causal embedding sequence) combined with a **Visual Decoder** (a fine-tuned Stable Diffusion model that decodes regressed embeddings into images) enables a single model to serve as a generalist interface for both image-to-text and text-to-image tasks. In few-shot evaluation, Emu surpasses Flamingo-9B across image and video question answering benchmarks (e.g., 4-shot VizWiz accuracy of 41.3% vs. 34.9%), establishing that unified generative pretraining across modalities yields stronger in-context learning—but only for problems where the base multimodal representations already capture sufficient capability, as the approach inherits standard LLM limitations including susceptibility to hallucination and a fundamental inability to generate correct outputs when the underlying visual or textual knowledge is absent from pretraining.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Multimodal Models Treat Vision as an Afterthought, Not a First-Class Modality

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is architectural: current large multimodal models (LMMs) overwhelmingly treat vision as a *peripheral input* to language models rather than as a *generative modality* on equal footing with text. Specifically, the dominant paradigm in 2023—exemplified by models like Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022), BLIP-2 (Li et al., 2023b), LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023b), and Kosmos-1 (Huang et al., 2023)—follows a design where a frozen vision encoder feeds visual features into a pretrained LLM, and the model is trained only to predict the *next text token*. The vision encoder receives no gradient signal from the multimodal training objective, and the model has no mechanism to generate visual outputs. This creates a structural asymmetry: the model can *consume* images but cannot *produce* them within the same autoregressive framework.

This gap has several concrete consequences that the paper identifies (Section 1, extended in Section 2):

**Consequence 1: Restricted model capacity.** When the training objective only supervises text token prediction, the vision encoder receives no learning signal from the multimodal tasks it participates in. The encoder is entirely frozen—typically a pretrained CLIP model (Radford et al., 2021) or its variants—meaning that visual representations cannot adapt to the specific demands of multimodal reasoning, captioning, or question answering. As the paper states in the introduction:

> "The prevailing training objective in such LMMs is predicting the next text token, typically with a frozen vision encoder and no supervision for the vision part, which highly restricts model capacity."

This matters because the quality of visual representations directly determines the ceiling on multimodal task performance—if the model cannot refine how it *sees*, it cannot improve how it *reasons about what it sees*.

**Consequence 2: No unified interface for multimodal generation.** Because existing LMMs output only text, they cannot perform tasks that require visual generation—text-to-image synthesis, image blending, or in-context image generation—within the same architecture. A user who wants both image captioning and text-to-image generation must use separate models (e.g., BLIP-2 for captioning, Stable Diffusion for generation), each with its own training pipeline, interface, and deployment requirements. This fragmentation prevents the kind of seamless multimodal interaction that a generalist model could provide.

**Consequence 3: Inability to leverage video as native multimodal training data.** Videos—which naturally interleave visual frames with subtitles or audio transcripts—represent an enormous, underutilized source of multimodal supervision. They contain dense cross-modal correlations: a cooking video pairs visual steps with spoken instructions, a nature documentary pairs animal footage with explanatory narration, and a tutorial pairs screen recordings with voiceover guidance. However, existing LMM pretraining pipelines, which are designed around static image-text pairs or documents, cannot easily incorporate this temporal, interleaved structure. The paper explicitly flags this in the introduction:

> "These LMMs are mostly trained on image-text pairs or documents, while overlooking video data as a potential scalable source of interleaved multimodal data."

This is not a minor oversight—public videos, particularly user-generated content on platforms like YouTube, possess *richer content diversity than Common Crawl*, the source of most text-based training data. Ignoring video means ignoring a vast repository of naturally occurring multimodal sequences.

**Consequence 4: No end-to-end unified objective across modalities.** The training objectives in prior LMMs are fragmented. The vision encoder is trained with contrastive objectives (CLIP), the language model is trained with next-token prediction, and the connecting adapter layers (e.g., Flamingo's gated cross-attention or BLIP-2's Q-Former) are trained with intermediate losses. There is no single objective that optimizes all components simultaneously toward the same goal. This makes training complex, introduces potential misalignment between components (since the vision encoder was never optimized to produce features useful for the specific LLM and downstream tasks), and limits the model's ability to learn emergent multimodal capabilities that arise only from end-to-end joint optimization.

### Why This Problem Matters: Both Practical and Theoretical Significance

**Practical impact: Reducing the cognitive and engineering overhead of multimodal AI.** Current production systems for handling multimodal tasks are necessarily fragmented. A photo-sharing app that wants to offer both automatic alt-text generation and AI-powered image editing would typically deploy separate models for captioning, visual question answering, and image generation—each with its own serving infrastructure, maintenance burden, and cost profile. A single model that handles all of these as *completions in a multimodal sequence* dramatically simplifies deployment, reduces total parameter count across services, and enables new capabilities (like in-context image generation—where an example image conditions the style of the generated output) that emerge naturally from unified training but would be difficult to engineer with separate models.

**Theoretical significance: Testing whether generative pretraining scales across modalities as it does for text alone.** The success of GPT-style autoregressive pretraining for text established a powerful principle: a sufficiently large model trained to predict the next token on diverse web-scale data learns to understand, reason, and generate with capabilities that transfer to a wide range of downstream tasks. This paper asks whether that principle extends when the "token" can be either a discrete text symbol or a continuous visual embedding. If it does, it suggests a path toward truly general-purpose multimodal models where vision and language are not just *connected* but *unified within the same generative process*. If it doesn't—if the architectural and optimization challenges prove insurmountable—it clarifies the boundaries of the autoregressive pretraining paradigm.

The paper is also a direct test of the hypothesis that **stronger supervision for the vision component during multimodal training leads to better multimodal performance**. Prior LMMs had effectively tested the ceiling of what can be achieved when vision representations are frozen. Emu asks: what happens when you let the vision representations *learn* from the multimodal objective, and when you give the model a *generative* relationship to visual content, not just a *perceptual* one?

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates itself against a landscape of increasingly sophisticated multimodal models, each of which addresses part of the problem but leaves fundamental gaps:

**Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022): Architecture as bridge, not unified model.** Flamingo connects a frozen vision encoder (NFNet) to a frozen language model (Chinchilla) through trainable gated cross-attention layers. This design demonstrates impressive zero-shot and few-shot multimodal capabilities, including in-context learning with interleaved image-text documents. However, it has three key limitations from Emu's perspective:

1. **Frozen vision representations.** The vision encoder never receives gradient signal from the multimodal training tasks. Whatever visual understanding was captured during the CLIP-style pretraining of the vision encoder is what the model gets—there's no pathway for the multimodal objective to reshape how the model perceives visual input for specific reasoning tasks.
2. **Text-only output.** Flamingo generates text but cannot generate images. It's a multimodal-input, text-only-output model, which means it can describe an image but cannot create one.
3. **No unified autoregressive objective.** The training procedure is multi-stage and multi-objective: pretrained vision encoder (contrastive), pretrained language model (next-token prediction), trainable adapter layers (next-token prediction with frozen encoders). There's no single loss that ties everything together from end to end.

**BLIP-2 and LLaVA: LLM-centric with visual appendages.** Models like BLIP-2 (Li et al., 2023b) and LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023b) take this paradigm further by explicitly using the LLM as the core reasoning engine and treating the vision encoder as a feature extractor whose outputs are projected into the LLM's input space. BLIP-2 uses a Q-Former to bridge the modality gap; LLaVA uses a simple linear projection. Both achieve strong performance on vision-language tasks, but they inherit the same fundamental constraint: **vision is not generative, and visual representations do not learn from the multimodal objective**. The vision tower is a fixed feature extractor, and the model's entire learning capacity is directed at the language-generation pathway.

**Kosmos-1 (Huang et al., 2023): Unified interface but not unified generation.** Kosmos-1 unified the interface by treating various modalities as input tokens to a Transformer-based model, but it still only predicts text tokens. It demonstrates that a single model can handle diverse multimodal inputs (images, text, possibly other modalities), but the output modality remains text-only. There is no mechanism for regressing visual embeddings or decoding images.

**GILL (Koh et al., 2023a): Generative output but as add-on, not integrated pretraining.** GILL represents a concurrent effort to add image generation to an LLM by embedding a frozen text-to-image model (Stable Diffusion) into the decoding pathway of an LLM. The LLM generates special tokens that serve as conditions for the image generator. However, this is an *architectural add-on*, not *integrated pretraining*: the vision and language components are trained separately, and the image generation capability is grafted on rather than emerging from a shared training process. Emu specifically benchmarks against GILL and achieves better FID (11.66 vs. 12.20 on MS-COCO, Table 2), suggesting that end-to-end training with a unified objective yields better generation quality than post-hoc integration.

**Unified-IO (Lu et al., 2022): Sequence-to-sequence across modalities, but discretized.** Unified-IO treats diverse tasks as sequence-to-sequence problems by discretizing all inputs and outputs (images → VQ-GAN tokens, text → word tokens, bounding boxes → coordinate tokens). This achieves a genuinely unified interface, but it relies on discrete visual tokens, which forces images to be represented as sequences of categorical variables learned through a separate VQ-VAE training stage. This introduces a pretrained tokenizer bottleneck—the quality of visual understanding is bounded by the quality of the discrete tokenizer, and the model cannot optimize continuous visual representations directly within the multimodal training loop.

**CM3Leon (Yu et al., 2023a): The closest prior work, with a key design difference.** CM3Leon, a concurrent work, shares Emu's motivation of training a unified image-to-text and text-to-image model with autoregressive pretraining. The key difference: CM3Leon **discretizes images** into tokens (similar to Unified-IO), while Emu **works directly with continuous visual embeddings**. This is not a minor implementation detail but a fundamental design choice with consequences for model capacity and training efficiency. Emu's approach avoids the information loss inherent in vector quantization and allows the visual representations to be optimized directly by the multimodal loss. Table 11 (Appendix D) shows that CM3Leon's discrete approach yields significantly weaker image-to-text performance than Emu (e.g., CIDEr 61.6 vs. 112.4 on COCO captioning, VQAv2 accuracy 47.6% vs. 52.9%), providing empirical support for the continuous embedding approach.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

Emu positions itself as **bridging these gaps simultaneously** through three key design choices that together define a new point in the architecture space:

**Design Choice 1: Unified autoregressive objective over both discrete text tokens and continuous visual embeddings.** Rather than freezing the vision encoder or discretizing images into tokens, Emu represents images as continuous embeddings (from EVA-CLIP) and trains the entire model end-to-end to predict the next element—whether text or visual—in an interleaved multimodal sequence. For text tokens, the loss is standard cross-entropy; for visual embeddings, it's an ℓ₂ regression loss. This means:

- The vision encoder (EVA-CLIP) can receive gradient signal from the multimodal objective, allowing visual representations to be refined for multimodal reasoning tasks.
- The model learns a joint distribution over text and visual content, not just text conditioned on visual input.
- The training process is genuinely unified: there is one objective, backpropagated through all components, with no frozen modules (unlike Flamingo's frozen vision and language backbones) and no auxiliary contrastive losses.

**Design Choice 2: The Causal Transformer as a bridge between 2D spatial representations and 1D causal sequences.** The paper identifies a subtle but critical problem: images are 2D spatial signals, and autoregressive modeling (which imposes a left-to-right causal order) is unnatural for them. If you simply feed raw image patches in raster order into an autoregressive model, you're enforcing a sequential dependency (e.g., the bottom-right pixel "depends on" the top-left pixel) that doesn't reflect how images are structured. This is a known challenge—models like Image GPT (Chen et al., 2020) attempted autoregressive image generation in pixel space but achieved only modest results.

Emu's solution is the **Causal Transformer**: a lightweight Transformer module that takes the 2D visual encodings from EVA-CLIP as condition and produces a fixed-length sequence of $N$ causal embeddings $\{z_1, z_2, \dots, z_N\}$ that capture the image's content in a form compatible with autoregressive modeling. The architecture is a standard Transformer decoder augmented with cross-attention: each block has causal self-attention (to impose the 1D causal structure among the $N$ output embeddings), cross-attention (to aggregate information from the 2D EVA-CLIP encodings), and a feed-forward layer. The output is a sequence where $z_{i+1}$ causally depends on $\{z_1, \dots, z_i\}$, but the 2D spatial structure of the original image is preserved in the conditioning pathway through cross-attention.

This is the key theoretical innovation of the paper: it decouples the **representation of visual information** (handled by the non-causal EVA-CLIP encoder) from the **representation of causal dependency** (handled by the Causal Transformer), allowing autoregressive modeling to work on visual content without forcing images into an unnatural sequential grid.

**Design Choice 3: Video as first-class multimodal training data via the YT-Storyboard-1B dataset.** While prior work on interleaved image-text documents (MMC4, used by Flamingo and OpenFlamingo) had shown the value of in-context multimodal data, video remained largely unexplored as a pretraining source for multimodal models. The paper introduces YT-Storyboard-1B, an original dataset that pairs YouTube storyboard thumbnails (approximately 1.8 billion images from 18 million videos) with timestamp-aligned subtitles to create natural interleaved video-text sequences. Rather than decoding raw video—which would be computationally prohibitive at this scale—the paper leverages YouTube's precomputed storyboard thumbnails, achieving a 20× reduction in storage costs while preserving the temporal alignment between visual frames and spoken/transcribed text. The ablation in Section A.1.3 (Table 6) shows that including this data during pretraining improves zero-shot COCO captioning CIDEr (from 110.8 to 112.9), 4-shot video QA (MSVDQA from 31.1% to 34.9%), and few-shot image QA (OKVQA from 44.6% to 45.7% in 4-shot).

**Design Choice 4: A generalist interface that subsumes both understanding and generation tasks.** By training the model to complete multimodal sequences—whether the next token is text or visual—Emu naturally serves as a single interface for diverse tasks. Image captioning is multimodal completion where the input is `[IMG] + visual embeddings + [/IMG]` followed by a text prompt, and the output is text tokens. Text-to-image generation is completion where the input is a text prompt followed by `[IMG]`, and the output is $N$ visual embeddings that the Visual Decoder then renders into a realistic image. In-context image generation—a novel capability—emerges when the prompt includes an example image-text pair, and the model generates a new image that follows the style or content pattern of the example.

This positioning is best understood as a direct extension of the GPT philosophy—"predict the next token on diverse data, and capabilities will emerge"—to the multimodal setting, where the "token" is either a discrete text symbol or a continuous visual embedding, and the data includes not just text corpora but image-text pairs, interleaved documents, and temporally aligned video-text sequences. The paper's core claim is that this unification, when executed with careful architectural design (Causal Transformer, continuous embeddings, end-to-end training), yields better performance on both vision-to-text and text-to-vision tasks than approaches that treat vision as either an auxiliary input or a discretized output.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

Emu is a **14-billion-parameter multimodal foundation model** that can take any mixture of text, images, and video as input and produce either text or images as output—all within a single autoregressive framework. The core problem it solves is the fragmentation of multimodal AI systems: prior models either only *understand* visual inputs (generating text) or only *generate* visual outputs (from text), requiring separate architectures, training pipelines, and interfaces for each direction. The "shape" of Emu's solution is an **end-to-end unified generative pretraining** where every component—vision encoder, multimodal Transformer, visual decoder—is trained under a single objective of predicting the next element in an interleaved multimodal sequence, whether that next element is a discrete text token or a continuous visual embedding.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The Emu system has four major components connected in a pipeline:

1. **Visual Encoder (EVA-CLIP, 1B parameters)** — takes a raw image or video frame and produces a set of dense 2D visual feature embeddings. Think of this as the "eye" that converts pixels into a rich semantic representation. Initialized from a pretrained EVA-01-CLIP model and *updated during Emu training* (not frozen).

2. **Causal Transformer (12-layer decoder, randomly initialized)** — takes the 2D visual encodings from the Visual Encoder and transforms them into a fixed-length 1D sequence of $N$ "causal visual embeddings." This is the architectural innovation that makes autoregressive modeling of visual content possible: it imposes a left-to-right causal dependency among the embeddings while using cross-attention to preserve the full 2D spatial information from the encoder. The output is a sequence $\{z_1, z_2, \dots, z_N\}$ where each $z_i$ can causally depend on $z_1, \dots, z_{i-1}$ and all EVA-CLIP embeddings.

3. **Multimodal Modeling LLM (LLaMA-13B)** — takes an interleaved sequence of text tokens and causal visual embeddings, separated by special `[IMG]` and `[/IMG]` tokens, and autoregressively predicts the next element in the sequence. This is the "brain" that learns the joint distribution over multimodal sequences. For text outputs, it uses a language modeling head (cross-entropy classification over the vocabulary). For visual outputs, it uses a regression head (ℓ₂ loss to a target visual embedding). Initialized from LLaMA-13B and trained end-to-end with all other components.

4. **Visual Decoder (Stable Diffusion v1.5, fine-tuned)** — takes the $N$ visual embeddings regressed by the Multimodal Modeling LLM as conditioning and generates a realistic 512×512 image. Think of this as the "hand" that renders abstract visual representations into pixel-space images. Initialized from Stable Diffusion v1.5 but with new linear projection layers to accommodate Emu's embedding dimension. Only the U-Net is trained; the VAE remains frozen.

Information flows through these components in a straight line: raw image → Visual Encoder → 2D encodings → Causal Transformer → 1D causal embeddings → Multimodal Modeling LLM (interleaved with text tokens) → either text tokens (via language head) or visual embeddings (via regression head) → Visual Decoder (for image outputs only).

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the unified training objective** (Equation 1), because it is the mathematical foundation that defines *what* Emu learns and *how* the losses for text and vision are combined. Understanding this clarifies why Emu is fundamentally different from text-only next-token prediction.

- **Second, the Causal Transformer**, because it is the novel architectural component that enables autoregressive modeling of visual content without discretization. We need to understand why raw autoregressive image modeling fails and how cross-attention to non-causal 2D encodings solves this.

- **Third, the Visual Encoder and Multimodal Modeling LLM together**, because they form the core multimodal processing pipeline, and their design choices—continuous embeddings vs. discrete tokens, end-to-end training vs. frozen components—determine what the model can represent and learn.

- **Fourth, the Visual Decoder**, because it closes the generation loop: how do we go from abstract visual embeddings to realistic images, and what design choices make this compatible with the autoregressive pretraining scheme?

- **Fifth, the data formulation and pretraining procedure**, because Emu's capabilities depend critically on *what* data it sees and *how* diverse data sources are unified into a single format.

- **Sixth, the inference mechanics**, because the generality of the interface—how a single model performs captioning, QA, text-to-image generation, and in-context image generation—follows directly from the architecture and training but needs explicit walkthrough to be understood.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **unified architecture paper** whose core idea is that a single autoregressive model trained to predict the next element in interleaved multimodal sequences—where elements can be either discrete text tokens or continuous visual embeddings—can serve as a generalist interface for both vision-to-text and text-to-vision tasks, with better performance than approaches that treat vision as an auxiliary input or discretize visual content into tokens.

---

#### The Unified Autoregressive Objective

The training objective is the mathematical core of Emu. Rather than training separate objectives for vision and language, Emu maximizes the likelihood of an interleaved multimodal sequence under a single autoregressive factorization.

Let $\mathcal{D}$ be the training corpus consisting of multimodal sequences. Each original sequence $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$ can contain images, video frames, and text in any order. Before training, all images and video frames are converted into causal latent embeddings using the Causal Transformer (described in the next subsection), and these embeddings are inserted back into the sequence at their original positions. The resulting sequence is denoted $u = (u_1, u_2, \dots, u_m)$, where each $u_i$ is either:

- A discrete text token (an integer index into the vocabulary)
- A continuous visual embedding vector (the output of the Causal Transformer for a given image or video frame)

Special tokens `[IMG]` and `[/IMG]` mark the boundaries of each image's $N$ visual embeddings in the sequence, so the model always knows whether it's predicting text or visual content.

The objective is to maximize the log-likelihood of this sequence under an autoregressive factorization:

$$\max_{\theta} \sum_{u \in \mathcal{D}} \sum_{i=1}^{|u|} \log P(u_i \mid u_1, \dots, u_{i-1}; \theta)$$

where $\theta$ represents all trainable parameters of Emu (Visual Encoder, Causal Transformer, Multimodal Modeling LLM), and $|u|$ is the length of the converted sequence.

**What it computes:** For each position $i$ in the sequence, the model must predict the element $u_i$ given all previous elements $u_1, \dots, u_{i-1}$. The log-probability of the correct element is added to the total likelihood. Summing over all positions and all sequences in the dataset gives the total log-likelihood, and maximizing this adjusts all model parameters to make the observed sequences as probable as possible under the model's distribution. Operationally, during training, we sample batches of multimodal sequences, convert images to causal embeddings, feed the entire sequence through the Multimodal Modeling LLM, compute the loss for each position, and backpropagate through all components.

**Why this form:** This is the standard autoregressive pretraining objective that has proven remarkably effective for text-only language models (GPT, LLaMA). By extending it to multimodal sequences, the model learns a *joint* distribution over interleaved text and visual content, not just a conditional distribution of text given images. The factorization is unidirectional (left-to-right causal), which is essential for autoregressive generation at inference time—the model can only condition on what it has already produced. A bidirectional objective (like BERT's masked language modeling) would give richer representations for understanding tasks but would not naturally support generation in either modality.

Crucially, the likelihood $P(u_i \mid u_1, \dots, u_{i-1}; \theta)$ is computed differently depending on whether $u_i$ is a text token or a visual embedding, leading to **two distinct loss functions** that are summed to form the total training loss.

---

#### The Dual Loss Formulation: Classification for Text, Regression for Vision

When the target element $u_i$ is a **discrete text token**, the model outputs logits $\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{|V|}$ over the vocabulary $V$ (size approximately 32,000 for LLaMA) via a language modeling head—a learned linear projection from the model's hidden state $h_i$ to the vocabulary space. The loss for this position is standard cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{text}}(u_i, h_i) = -\log \frac{\exp(\ell_{u_i})}{\sum_{v \in V} \exp(\ell_v)}$$

where $\ell_{u_i}$ is the logit assigned to the correct token $u_i$, and the denominator sums over all vocabulary items.

**What it computes:** The negative log-probability that the model assigns to the correct next token, given the preceding multimodal context. If the model assigns high probability to the correct token, the loss is low; if it's confidently wrong, the loss is high. This is a single scalar value per text-token position, averaged across all text positions in the batch.

**Why this form:** Cross-entropy is the standard objective for classification with mutually exclusive categories (a token can only be one word, not multiple). It has favorable gradient properties—the gradient is proportional to $(p - e_{u_i})$ where $p$ is the predicted probability distribution and $e_{u_i}$ is a one-hot vector at the correct token, meaning the model learns most from positions where it's confidently wrong, and gradient magnitude naturally decreases as predictions improve. Alternative losses like mean squared error on logits would not produce this adaptive gradient scaling and would be poorly calibrated for probability estimation.

When the target element $u_i$ is a **continuous visual embedding**, the model instead uses a separate regression head—a learned linear projection from the hidden state $h_i$ to the embedding dimension $d$ (which matches the output dimension of the Causal Transformer). The output is a predicted embedding $\hat{u}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The loss for this position is ℓ₂ regression:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{vision}}(u_i, h_i) = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{j=1}^{d} \|u_{i,j} - \hat{u}_{i,j}\|_2^2$$

where $u_{i,j}$ is the $j$-th dimension of the target visual embedding (produced by the Causal Transformer), $\hat{u}_{i,j}$ is the $j$-th dimension of the predicted visual embedding, and $d$ is the embedding dimension.

**What it computes:** The mean squared error between the predicted and target visual embeddings, averaged across all dimensions. This penalizes the model when the regressed embedding vector deviates (in Euclidean distance) from the embedding that the Causal Transformer produced for that image. The result is a single non-negative scalar per visual-embedding position.

**Why this form:** ℓ₂ regression is the maximum-likelihood objective when the error is assumed to be Gaussian with fixed variance—it corresponds to $\log P(u_i \mid h_i) \propto -\|u_i - \hat{u}_i\|_2^2$. This is a natural choice for continuous-valued outputs where we want the model to reproduce specific vectors. ℓ₁ regression (mean absolute error) would produce sparser gradients and encourage the model to match the median rather than the mean of the target distribution, which is less appropriate for precise embedding reconstruction. Cross-entropy is not applicable because the output space is continuous, not discrete.

Note that the **regression head is separate from the language modeling head**—they are two different linear projections operating on the same hidden state $h_i$. The model knows which head to use based on position: if the current position is between `[IMG]` and `[/IMG]` tokens, the regression head is active and the loss is ℓ₂; otherwise, the language head is active and the loss is cross-entropy. The **total loss** for a sequence is the sum of text-position losses and vision-position losses, and gradients from both flow back through all parameters of the Visual Encoder, Causal Transformer, and Multimodal Modeling LLM.

The paper does not specify a weighting factor between the two losses—they are simply summed. This implicitly assumes that the natural scales of the two losses are compatible, which depends on the embedding dimension $d$ and the vocabulary size $|V|$. For the reported setup (LLaMA-13B vocabulary of approximately 32K, embedding dimension $d$ matched to EVA-CLIP output), the paper reports stable training without explicit loss balancing, suggesting the scales are empirically compatible.

#### The Causal Transformer: Making 2D Visual Content Autoregressively Modelable

This is Emu's key architectural innovation and the component that distinguishes it from both discretization-based approaches (Unified-IO, CM3Leon) and frozen-encoder approaches (Flamingo, BLIP-2). The fundamental challenge is: **autoregressive models produce sequences one element at a time, each conditioned on previous elements, but images are inherently 2D spatial signals with no natural left-to-right ordering.**

If you were to model an image autoregressively in pixel space—predicting pixel $p_{r,c}$ given all pixels raster-scanned before it—you would be imposing a directional dependency (bottom-right pixels "depend on" top-left pixels) that doesn't reflect how images are structured. The visual world is approximately translation-invariant and spatially isotropic: the content in one region of an image doesn't causally depend on another region in a directional sense. Prior work on autoregressive image generation (Image GPT, DALL-E, Parti) managed this by discretizing images into tokens and treating them like a "visual language" with a predefined order, but performance was typically inferior to diffusion-based approaches, and the discrete tokenization introduced information loss.

Emu's solution is to **separate visual representation from causal sequence modeling**. The Causal Transformer takes as input:

- **Condition:** The full 2D visual encodings from EVA-CLIP. For an image of resolution 224×224, EVA-CLIP (a 40-layer ViT with patch size 14×14) produces a 16×16 grid of patch embeddings plus a `[CLS]` token, yielding a set of 257 embedding vectors. These are **not causal**—each embedding has access to the entire image through the ViT's bidirectional self-attention.
- **Query:** $N$ randomly initialized learnable embedding vectors $\{e_1, e_2, \dots, e_N\}$ (the paper does not specify the exact value of $N$ in the main text, but the sequence must be of manageable length for the LLM; given that images at 224×224 resolution with a ViT encoder produce 257 patch tokens and the Causal Transformer architecture described is 12 layers, $N$ is likely 256 or 257 to match standard ViT output dimensions).

The Causal Transformer processes these through 12 decoder blocks, each consisting of three sub-layers:

1. **Causal self-attention** among the $N$ query embeddings $\{e_1, \dots, e_N\}$. This is standard masked self-attention where $e_i$ can attend only to $e_1, \dots, e_i$. This imposes a left-to-right causal structure on the output embeddings.

2. **Cross-attention** from the query embeddings (as queries) to the EVA-CLIP encodings (as keys and values). The cross-attention is **not masked**—each query embedding $e_i$ can attend to all EVA-CLIP patch embeddings, giving it full access to the 2D spatial information of the image. This is where the 2D spatial structure is injected into the causal sequence.

3. **Feed-forward network** (standard Transformer FFN with ReLU or similar activation).

The output is a sequence of $N$ causal embeddings $\{z_1, z_2, \dots, z_N\}$ with the following properties:

- $z_i$ captures the content of the image **conditioned on the causal prefix** $z_1, \dots, z_{i-1}$ (through the causal self-attention)
- $z_i$ has access to the **entire 2D image** (through the unrestricted cross-attention to all EVA-CLIP patch embeddings)
- The sequence as a whole represents the image in a form that can be autoregressively modeled in the downstream Multimodal Modeling LLM

**What it computes operationally:** For a given image, EVA-CLIP produces patch embeddings $\{p_1, \dots, p_{257}\}$. The Causal Transformer starts with $N$ learnable tokens $\{e_1, \dots, e_N\}$ (all initialized the same for every image—these are model parameters, not image-specific). In each of the 12 layers, each $e_i$ is first updated by attending to $\{e_1, \dots, e_i\}$ (causal self-attention), then updated by attending to all $\{p_1, \dots, p_{257}\}$ (cross-attention), then passed through the FFN. After all 12 layers, the output $\{z_1, \dots, z_N\}$ is produced. These $N$ embeddings are then inserted into the multimodal sequence, sandwiched between `[IMG]` and `[/IMG]` tokens, and fed into the Multimodal Modeling LLM.

**Why this design (rather than alternatives):** Several alternatives were considered and rejected by the design space:

- **Direct autoregressive pixel modeling (rejected):** As discussed, images lack natural causal order. Early experiments with autoregressive image models showed poor scaling compared to diffusion models.
- **Discrete VQ-VAE tokens (rejected):** Vector quantization allows autoregressive modeling of discrete visual tokens, but it introduces a separate pretrained tokenizer (VQ-VAE or VQ-GAN) that is trained with its own reconstruction objective, not the multimodal objective. The discrete bottleneck loses fine-grained visual information, and the tokenizer cannot adapt during multimodal training. CM3Leon uses this approach, and Table 11 shows it underperforms Emu on image-to-text tasks (CIDEr 61.6 vs. 112.4 on COCO). Emu's continuous embeddings preserve full information and are optimized end-to-end by the multimodal loss.
- **Bidirectional (non-causal) visual embeddings (rejected):** If visual embeddings were not causally structured, the Multimodal Modeling LLM could still process them as input, but it could not autoregressively generate them at inference time. The Causal Transformer's causal structure is what enables the model to regress $z_1$, then condition on $z_1$ to regress $z_2$, etc., during image generation. Without the causal structure, visual generation would require a fundamentally different decoding mechanism.
- **Using the Causal Transformer only at training time and a separate decoder for generation (rejected):** This would require two different visual representations, which would be architecturally complex and likely less coherent than having a single representation for both understanding and generation.

The Causal Transformer is **randomly initialized and trained end-to-end** with the rest of Emu, meaning the model learns the optimal way to structure causal visual embeddings for the downstream multimodal task. This is important: the causal structure is not hand-designed but learned from data, guided by the dual objective of (1) producing embeddings that are useful for the LLM's next-element prediction and (2) being reconstructable from the LLM's hidden states during autoregressive generation.

#### Visual Encoder (EVA-CLIP) and Why It's Trained, Not Frozen

Emu uses EVA-01-CLIP with 1 billion parameters as the Visual Encoder, initialized from a publicly available pretrained checkpoint (Sun et al., 2023). EVA-CLIP is a 40-layer Vision Transformer (ViT) trained with a CLIP objective—contrastive learning between image and text representations on large-scale image-text pairs (LAION-400M and other datasets). The 40-layer ViT processes 224×224 images split into 14×14 patches, producing patch embeddings (256 patches + 1 `[CLS]` token = 257 total embeddings per image).

The critical design choice is that **EVA-CLIP is not frozen during Emu pretraining**. It receives gradient signal from the multimodal objective (both the text cross-entropy loss and the vision ℓ₂ regression loss), backpropagated through the Causal Transformer and Multimodal Modeling LLM. This is a deliberate departure from the Flamingo/BLIP-2/LLaVA paradigm where vision encoders are frozen.

**Why train the vision encoder end-to-end?** The paper argues that frozen vision representations are a bottleneck:

> "The prevailing training objective in such LMMs is predicting the next text token, typically with a frozen vision encoder and no supervision for the vision part, which highly restricts model capacity."

When the vision encoder is frozen, the visual features are optimized for the CLIP objective (image-text alignment) but not for downstream multimodal reasoning. For example, CLIP features might excel at distinguishing broad semantic categories but might not capture fine-grained attributes (colors, spatial relationships, text in images) that are crucial for visual question answering. By allowing the vision encoder to learn from the multimodal objective, Emu can refine its visual representations to be more useful for the specific tasks it's trained on.

There is also a practical consideration: since EVA-CLIP receives supervision from the ℓ₂ regression loss (through the Causal Transformer), the visual representations are explicitly optimized to be transformable into the causal embedding format. If the encoder were frozen, the Causal Transformer would have to learn to "fix" any incompatibilities between the CLIP-optimized features and the desired causal embedding structure, which might be harder than allowing the encoder to adapt.

The learning rate for the vision encoder is set to $4 \times 10^{-5}$ (separate from the LLM's learning rate of $3 \times 10^{-5}$), with a cosine decay schedule and 0.2 warmup ratio (Table 5). This relatively low learning rate compared to the randomly initialized Causal Transformer ($1 \times 10^{-4}$) reflects the fact that EVA-CLIP starts from a strong pretrained initialization and needs only refinement, not full retraining.

For video inputs, each frame is independently processed by EVA-CLIP. A video of $T$ frames thus produces $T \times 257$ visual embeddings, which are then each passed through the Causal Transformer to produce $T \times N$ causal embeddings. These are interleaved with text (subtitles or captions) in the multimodal sequence according to their timestamps.

#### Multimodal Modeling LLM (LLaMA-13B) and How It Processes Interleaved Sequences

The Multimodal Modeling LLM is a 13-billion-parameter LLaMA model (Touvron et al., 2023), a decoder-only Transformer with standard architecture: multi-head self-attention with causal masking, followed by feed-forward networks, with pre-layer normalization. It is initialized from the publicly released LLaMA-13B weights and trained end-to-end during Emu pretraining.

**How interleaved sequences are formed for the LLM:** Before being fed into the LLM, the original multimodal sequence (images, text, video frames) is converted into a flat sequence of embeddings:

1. Text tokens are embedded using the LLM's standard token embedding matrix (learned, initialized from LLaMA).
2. Each image (or video frame) is replaced with its $N$ causal embeddings $\{z_1, \dots, z_N\}$ from the Causal Transformer, **prepended with a special `[IMG]` token embedding and appended with a special `[/IMG]` token embedding**. These special tokens are learned embeddings (initialized randomly, trained during pretraining) that signal to the model where visual content begins and ends.
3. The entire sequence is prefixed with `<s>` (beginning-of-sequence token) and suffixed with `</s>` (end-of-sequence token), following LLaMA's standard format.

The input to the LLM is thus a sequence of vectors: `[<s>] → [token_1] → ... → [IMG] → [z_1] → ... → [z_N] → [/IMG] → ... → [token_k] → [</s>]`, where each vector has dimension $D_{\text{LLM}}$ (the hidden dimension of LLaMA-13B, which is 5120). The causal embeddings $z_i$ already have this dimension because the Causal Transformer is designed to output embeddings that match the LLM's hidden size.

The LLM processes this sequence with standard causal attention—each position attends only to previous positions. This means:

- When processing a text token after an image, the token can attend to the image embeddings (the `[IMG]`, the $N$ causal embeddings, and the `[/IMG]`), giving the model visual context for language generation.
- When regressing a visual embedding (during training or text-to-image generation at inference), the embedding $z_i$ can attend to all previous tokens, including preceding text, preceding images, and preceding visual embeddings in the same image ($z_1, \dots, z_{i-1}$).

For each position in the sequence, the LLM produces a hidden state $h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{D_{\text{LLM}}}$. This hidden state is then mapped to either:

- **Language logits:** Through a linear projection $W_{\text{LM}} \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times D_{\text{LLM}}}$ (inherited from LLaMA and updated during training) to produce a probability distribution over vocabulary tokens.
- **Visual embedding prediction:** Through a linear projection $W_{\text{reg}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times D_{\text{LLM}}}$ (randomly initialized and learned) to produce a predicted visual embedding $\hat{z_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, where $d$ is the dimension of the Causal Transformer output embeddings.

The model uses the `[IMG]` and `[/IMG]` tokens to determine which head to use: positions between `[IMG]` and `[/IMG]` use the regression head; all other positions use the language modeling head.

**Training hyperparameters for the LLM:** AdamW optimizer with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.98$, $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$, weight decay $0.05$, peak learning rate $3 \times 10^{-5}$ with cosine decay and 0.2 warmup ratio. Batch sizes vary by data source: 128 for image-text pairs, 64 for interleaved image-text data, 16 for video-text pairs and interleaved video-text data. Training runs for 10,000 steps on 128 NVIDIA 80GB-A100 GPUs, processing approximately 82 million samples (150 billion tokens total) in roughly 2 days.

#### Visual Decoder: From Abstract Embeddings to Realistic Images

The Visual Decoder is the component that renders the model's generated visual embeddings into pixel-space images. Without it, Emu could produce abstract visual representations but not actual viewable images. The decoder is trained **after the main Emu pretraining** (Section 3.3) and is not part of the end-to-end multimodal training—it's a separate fine-tuning stage that learns to map Emu's visual embedding space to Stable Diffusion's latent space.

**Initialization and architecture:** The decoder is initialized from Stable Diffusion v1.5 (Rombach et al., 2022), a latent diffusion model that generates images by iteratively denoising random noise in the latent space of a pretrained VAE. The VAE encoder compresses 512×512 images into a latent representation of size 64×64×4 (compression ratio of 8×); the VAE decoder reconstructs images from these latents. The diffusion U-Net operates in this latent space, conditioned on text embeddings from a CLIP text encoder.

Emu repurposes this architecture by replacing the text-conditioning pathway with an **image-embedding-conditioning pathway**. Specifically:

1. The linear projection layers in the U-Net's cross-attention modules that previously mapped from CLIP text embeddings to the U-Net's internal dimension are replaced with new linear layers that map from Emu's visual embedding dimension to the U-Net's internal dimension. These new layers are randomly initialized and trained from scratch.

2. During training, for each text-to-image sample, the text prompt is fed through the Multimodal Modeling LLM (with `[IMG]` appended at the end), and the LLM autoregressively generates $N$ visual embeddings. These embeddings are then passed through the new linear projections and used as keys and values in the U-Net's cross-attention layers, replacing the text-conditioning pathway.

3. The VAE encoder and decoder are **frozen**—only the U-Net parameters are updated. The Visual Encoder, Causal Transformer, and Multimodal Modeling LLM are also **frozen** during visual decoder training, meaning the visual embeddings are produced by a fixed model and the decoder learns to interpret them.

**Training procedure:** The decoder is trained on both LAION-COCO (high image-text correlation) and LAION-Aesthetics (high visual quality), mixed in a 7:2 ratio. Training uses the standard Stable Diffusion diffusion objective (ϵ-prediction with DDPM noise schedule). Hyperparameters: batch size $50 \times 4 \times 8$ (gradient accumulation across 8 GPUs with 4 accumulation steps, effective batch size 1600), training for 15,000 iterations. Learning rate warms up to $10^{-4}$ for the first 5,000 steps, then decreases to $5 \times 10^{-5}$ at 10,000 steps and $10^{-5}$ at 14,000 steps. AdamW optimizer with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$, weight decay $10^{-2}$. Input images are 512×512 resolution.

**Classifier-free guidance:** The model is trained with 10% unconditional probability—in 10% of training samples, the image embedding condition is dropped (replaced with a null embedding). This enables classifier-free guidance at inference time (Ho & Salimans, 2022), where the model interpolates between conditioned and unconditioned predictions to trade off fidelity against diversity. At inference, a guidance scale of 5.0 is used for Emu (vs. 3.0 for baseline Stable Diffusion v1.5), as this was found to yield the best FID on the MS-COCO validation set.

**Why this design (rather than training the decoder end-to-end with Emu)?** The paper does not explicitly justify the two-stage approach, but the practical motivation is clear: joint training of a 14B-parameter autoregressive model and a diffusion model would be computationally prohibitive and architecturally complex. The diffusion model's training requires multiple denoising steps per sample, while the autoregressive model processes sequences in a single forward pass—combining these in one training loop would be challenging. The two-stage approach also allows the decoder to inherit Stable Diffusion's high-quality image generation capabilities while only learning a relatively narrow mapping from Emu's embedding space to SD's condition space. The trade-off is that the visual embeddings produced by the LLM are not explicitly optimized for image reconstruction quality; the decoder must learn to interpret whatever embeddings the LLM happens to produce during text-to-image generation.

**A known limitation:** Table 2 shows Emu achieves FID 11.66 on MS-COCO text-to-image generation, which is better than GILL (FID 12.20, a concurrent text+image LLM) but worse than SDv1.5 (FID 9.93). The paper attributes this gap to the relatively short training (15K steps vs. SDv1.5's full training) and the distribution shift between Emu's visual embedding space and SDv1.5's text embedding space: the decoder's new linear projections must learn to map a fundamentally different kind of condition signal, which may require more training to fully adapt.

#### Data Formulation and Pretraining Procedure

One of Emu's key contributions is the **unified data format** that allows diverse multimodal data sources to be combined into a single training corpus. The paper aggregates five data sources, each converted into the interleaved multimodal sequence format:

**1. Image-Text Pairs (LAION-2B and LAION-COCO):** LAION-2B provides 2 billion web-scraped images with alt-text, which are noisy but diverse. LAION-COCO is a 600M subset captioned by BLIP and CLIP models, providing cleaner but less diverse captions. The paper uses both to balance diversity and quality. In the sequence format, each pair becomes either `[text] [IMG] [image embeddings] [/IMG]` or `[IMG] [image embeddings] [/IMG] [text]`—the image is randomly placed before or after its caption with 50% probability, ensuring the model learns to handle images in both positions.

**2. Interleaved Image-Text Documents (MMC4):** MMC4 (Zhu et al., 2023b) contains ~75 million web documents (from Common Crawl) with images interleaved with text. For training, each document is sampled in two ways: (a) a random subsequence of 1024 tokens, including up to the first 5 images in that subsequence, creating a long document with embedded images; (b) 5 random images and their corresponding sentences, forming a shorter 512-token sequence of image-text pairs. This dual sampling provides both document-level context and tighter image-text alignment. Images with CLIP similarity score below 0.32 to any text in the document are filtered out (following OpenFlamingo).

**3. Video-Text Pairs (WebVid-10M):** WebVid-10M provides 10 million short videos with textual descriptions sourced from stock footage websites. These have strong video-text correlation (the descriptions describe what happens in the video). Irrelevant metadata (resolution, camera parameters) is removed using heuristic rules, and the cleaned text is rewritten by Vicuna-13B for fluency. For training, 8 random frames are sampled from each video. Each frame is encoded into $N$ causal embeddings via EVA-CLIP + Causal Transformer, producing an interleaved sequence: `[text description] [IMG] [frame_1 embeddings] [/IMG] [IMG] [frame_2 embeddings] [/IMG] ...`

**4. Interleaved Video-Text (YT-Storyboard-1B):** This is Emu's novel data contribution. From the YT-Temporal-1B dataset (Zellers et al., 2022), which provides 18 million YouTube video IDs, the authors crawl storyboard images (precomputed thumbnail strips provided by YouTube for quick video preview) and timestamped subtitle files. Storyboard images are sampled at fixed intervals (e.g., every few seconds of video), and subtitles are aligned to the same timestamps. The result is an interleaved sequence of video frames and spoken/transcribed text, chronologically ordered by timestamp. The paper states this approach yields approximately 1.8 billion storyboard images in total and reduces storage costs by 20× compared to storing decoded video frames.

The key insight that makes video data valuable for multimodal pretraining is that **subtitles and visual frames are tightly coupled in a causal relationship**: the spoken words describe or refer to what is shown on screen at that moment, making them a stronger cross-modal supervision signal than typical image-text pairs where the correspondence is looser (e.g., alt-text may describe the image’s subject but not its details or context). The ablation in Table 6 confirms that adding YT-Storyboard-1B during pretraining improves video QA (MSVDQA 4-shot from 31.1% to 34.9%) and image understanding (COCO captioning zero-shot from 110.8 to 112.9 CIDEr).

**Batch composition and training schedule:** During pretraining, batches are composed of samples from all five data sources simultaneously, with per-source batch sizes: 128 for LAION-2B, 128 for LAION-COCO, 64 for MMC4, 16 for WebVid-10M, and 16 for YT-Storyboard-1B. The total batch size per gradient step is therefore 128 + 128 + 64 + 16 + 16 = 352 samples. Training runs for 10,000 steps on 128 A100-80GB GPUs, totaling approximately 3.52 million gradient steps worth of samples but with an effective epoch count that varies by data source (larger datasets are undersampled relative to their size). The total number of processed samples is ~82 million, and the total token count is ~150 billion tokens (including both text tokens and visual embeddings, where each visual embedding counts as one "token" position).

All images and video frames are resized to 224×224 resolution before encoding. For each video, 8 frames are randomly sampled. The sequence length varies by data source (as dictated by the sampled subsequence lengths in MMC4 and the number of frames in video data) but is bounded by the LLM's context window (LLaMA-13B's maximum context length is 2048 tokens).

**Optimization:** AdamW with $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.98$, weight decay $0.05$. Three learning rates are used for different components: $4 \times 10^{-5}$ for the Visual Encoder, $3 \times 10^{-5}$ for the Multimodal Modeling LLM, and $1 \times 10^{-4}$ for the randomly initialized Causal Transformer. All follow cosine decay with 20% warmup. The higher learning rate for the Causal Transformer reflects its random initialization—it needs to learn from scratch—while the lower rates for the pretrained components reflect their existing knowledge that needs only refinement.

#### Inference Mechanics: How the Generalist Interface Works

At inference time, Emu operates as a **multimodal completion engine**. The user provides a multimodal prompt (which can include text, images, video frames, or any interleaved combination), and Emu autoregressively generates the next elements in the sequence. The generation process differs depending on whether the expected output is text or image.

**For text output (image captioning, visual question answering, video understanding):**

1. The user's prompt is converted into the standard multimodal sequence format: images/video frames are encoded by EVA-CLIP → Causal Transformer → causal embeddings; text is tokenized; special tokens mark image boundaries.
2. The sequence is fed into the Multimodal Modeling LLM, which processes it through all layers and produces a hidden state at the final position.
3. The language modeling head maps this hidden state to vocabulary logits, and the model samples (or greedily selects) the next text token.
4. This token is appended to the sequence, and the process repeats autoregressively until an end-of-sequence token `</s>` is generated or a maximum length is reached.
5. During generation, the model can condition on all visual content in the prompt because the causal attention mask allows each generated text token to attend to all prompt tokens (including preceding image embeddings).

For the pretrained model, a **Multimodal Chain-of-Thought** prompting technique is used (Section 5.1): the model is first prompted to generate a descriptive caption of the visual content ("describing the image in detail."), and then that caption is used as context for the task-specific prompt ("a picture of {caption}. based on the picture, {question} short answer:"). This consistently improves performance by allowing the model to articulate its visual understanding before reasoning about it.

**For image output (text-to-image generation, in-context image generation, image blending):**

1. The user provides a text prompt (for text-to-image) or a multimodal prompt that includes example images (for in-context generation). The prompt ends with a special `[IMG]` token to signal that the model should generate visual content.
2. The prompt sequence is processed by the Multimodal Modeling LLM as in the text case.
3. At the position of `[IMG]`, the regression head is activated. The model generates the first visual embedding $z_1$ by mapping the hidden state through the regression head.
4. This generated $z_1$ is appended to the sequence (as input for the next step).
5. The model then generates $z_2$ (conditioned on the prompt and $z_1$), then $z_3$ (conditioned on the prompt, $z_1$, and $z_2$), and so on, until $N$ visual embeddings are generated.
6. Once all $N$ embeddings are generated, a `[/IMG]` token is appended (presumably generated by the model or automatically inserted after $N$ regression steps—the paper's Section 2.3 implies the model generates $N$ embeddings after `[IMG]` is triggered).
7. The $N$ visual embeddings are fed into the Visual Decoder. The decoder's U-Net takes these embeddings as cross-attention conditions and, starting from random Gaussian noise in the VAE latent space, iteratively denoises (over 50 steps using the PNDM scheduler) to produce a latent representation, which the VAE decoder then maps to a 512×512 pixel image.
8. Classifier-free guidance is applied: the denoising prediction is a weighted combination of the conditioned prediction (using the actual visual embeddings) and the unconditioned prediction (using null embeddings), with a guidance scale of 5.0 to improve image quality at the cost of some diversity.

**In-context image generation** is an emergent capability that naturally follows from this design. If the user provides a multimodal context that includes example images and their descriptions—for example, `This is an oil painting depicting sunflowers. [IMG] [sunflower painting embeddings] [/IMG] There is a cute animal sitting in front of a white background. [IMG] [animal embeddings] [/IMG] [IMG]`—the model generates new visual embeddings conditioned on this context. Because the autoregressive generation can attend to the preceding example image embeddings during the regression of the new embeddings, the generated image tends to share stylistic properties (oil painting style, composition, color palette) with the examples. The paper demonstrates this in Figure 8, where images generated with in-context examples share the oil painting style from the context, whereas the same prompts without context generate more photorealistic images (Figure 9).

**For multimodal output (e.g., answering a visual question with both text explanation and a generated image):** The paper does not explicitly demonstrate this, but the architecture theoretically supports generating interleaved text and image outputs by alternating between the language head and regression head as the sequence dictates. The model would generate text tokens until it determines an image is appropriate, generate `[IMG]`, regress $N$ visual embeddings, generate `[/IMG]`, and continue with text tokens. This capability is demonstrated implicitly in the instruction-tuned Emu-I, which can engage in multi-turn dialogue about images (Figure 6) and potentially generate visual responses.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Vision as a Generative Modality—Not a Perceptual Appendage—Within a Unified Autoregressive Objective

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is **elevating vision from a perceptual input to a generative output on equal footing with text**, all within a single autoregressive training objective. Prior large multimodal models—Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022), BLIP-2 (Li et al., 2023b), LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023b), Kosmos-1 (Huang et al., 2023)—shared a structural assumption: vision is something the model *sees* but never *produces*. These models map images → text (captioning, VQA) but cannot map text → images, and critically, they never apply a training loss to the vision pathway. The vision encoder is frozen—pretrained on a separate contrastive objective (CLIP), then grafted onto the LLM. This means the visual representations are optimized for image-text *alignment*, not for the specific multimodal reasoning tasks the model performs, and the model has no mechanism to generate visual content.

Emu challenges this assumption directly by treating visual embeddings as **continuous-valued tokens that can be either consumed or produced** within the same autoregressive process. The ℓ₂ regression loss on visual embeddings isn't just a technical detail—it's a statement that vision supervision matters, and that the model can learn about visual structure not just by classifying text tokens but by actually *predicting what visual content should look like* given multimodal context. This is a departure from the dominant paradigm where supervision is exclusively text-based.

What makes this distinctive at the idea level is that it reconceptualizes multimodal training as **joint distribution modeling** rather than **conditional generation**. A Flamingo-style model learns $P(\text{text} \mid \text{image})$. Emu learns $P(\text{text}, \text{image}_1, \text{image}_2, \dots)$ as an autoregressive factorization over interleaved modalities. The difference matters: joint modeling enables in-context image generation (where an example image conditions the style of a newly generated image—something impossible in a conditionally-trained model), and it allows the vision encoder to receive gradient signal from both the text-prediction loss (improving features for understanding) and the vision-regression loss (improving features for accurate reconstruction). This is a **fundamental architectural shift**, not an incremental improvement, because it changes what the model is *trained to do*—not just how well, but *what task* it optimizes for during pretraining.

The evidence for this shift's impact is in the performance envelope. Emu achieves zero-shot COCO captioning CIDEr of 112.4 (Table 1), which is dramatically higher than comparable models (Flamingo-9B: 79.4; Kosmos-1: 84.7) and competitive with much larger models (PALI-X-55B: 149.2 but with per-task finetuning, not zero-shot). Simultaneously, it achieves FID of 11.66 on text-to-image generation (Table 2)—not state-of-the-art compared to dedicated generation models like SDv1.5 (9.93), but *within the same model* that achieves the strong captioning results. No prior model occupied both of these positions simultaneously; previous multimodal LLMs were either understanding-only (Flamingo) or required separate architectures for generation (GILL, which achieved FID 12.20 with an LLM+SD combination but weaker understanding performance). The fact that a single 14B-parameter model can be competitive in both directions supports the claim that joint modeling provides genuine synergies, not just a convenient interface.


### Innovation 2: The Causal Transformer as a Decoupling Principle—Separating 2D Visual Representation from 1D Causal Sequence Modeling

The second conceptual contribution is the **Causal Transformer**, which solves a representation mismatch that had bedeviled prior work on unified multimodal generation. The problem is structural, not quantitative: images are 2D spatial signals with no natural left-to-right ordering, but autoregressive models require a causal 1D sequence where each element conditions only on previous elements. Prior approaches to handling this fell into two camps, each with fundamental limitations:

- **Discretization (Unified-IO, CM3Leon, DALL-E, Parti):** Train a VQ-VAE or VQ-GAN to encode images into discrete tokens, then treat those tokens like a visual "language" in the autoregressive model. This creates a unified discrete-token format, but it introduces a separately-trained tokenizer bottleneck—the tokenizer is optimized for reconstruction, not for the multimodal task—and the discrete bottleneck loses fine-grained visual information. VQ-VAE tokenizers are known to struggle with fine textures, small text, and precise spatial relationships because the vector quantization step inevitably discards information.

- **Raster-scan autoregressive pixels (Image GPT):** Model images directly in pixel space with a raster order (top-left to bottom-right). This imposes a directional dependency—pixel $(r, c)$ "depends on" pixel $(r-1, c)$ and $(r, c-1)$—that doesn't reflect how images are structured, since visual information flows bidirectionally in space. Performance was consistently weaker than non-autoregressive approaches (diffusion models, GANs).

Emu's Causal Transformer introduces a third option: **decouple the visual representation problem from the causal sequence problem**. The EVA-CLIP encoder handles the 2D spatial representation—processing the image through bidirectional self-attention to capture global context without forcing a causal order. The Causal Transformer then handles the causal sequence generation—taking the non-causal 2D encodings as condition (via cross-attention) and producing a fixed-length sequence of embeddings $\{z_1, \dots, z_N\}$ where each $z_i$ can attend to all EVA-CLIP features (giving it full spatial context) but only to $z_1, \dots, z_{i-1}$ (giving it a causal structure that the downstream LLM can regress autoregressively).

The intellectual move here is recognizing that **causal structure doesn't need to be in the visual representation itself—it only needs to be in the sequence that interfaces with the autoregressive model**. The EVA-CLIP features are non-causal, which is appropriate for images. The Causal Transformer output is causal, which is appropriate for autoregressive generation. The cross-attention mechanism provides a principled bridge between these two representational regimes.

This is a **fundamental contribution** to the architecture design space for multimodal generative models. It's not a minor tweak—it resolves a tension that had forced prior work into either the discretization tradeoff (loss of visual fidelity for the sake of causal compatibility) or the frozen-encoder tradeoff (accepting that the vision pathway can't be generative). The Causal Transformer is what makes it possible for Emu to (a) use continuous visual embeddings (preserving information), (b) train the vision encoder end-to-end (allowing visual representations to adapt to multimodal tasks), and (c) generate images autoregressively (using the causal embedding sequence as the generation target). Without this component, the design space collapses to either frozen encoders with no visual generation or discretized tokens with information loss.

The evidence for this innovation's importance is indirect but systematic. Table 11 (Appendix D) compares Emu against CM3Leon, the closest prior work with a similar unified-generation motivation but using discrete VQ-VAE tokens. Emu substantially outperforms CM3Leon on image-to-text tasks (COCO CIDEr 112.4 vs. 61.6, VQAv2 52.0% vs. 47.6%, VizWiz 34.2% vs. 37.6%), suggesting that the continuous embedding + Causal Transformer approach preserves more of the visual information needed for detailed understanding. The ablation in Section A.1.3 (Table 6) confirms that adding interleaved video-text data during pretraining improves performance—but this data source's effectiveness depends on the Causal Transformer being able to convert variable numbers of video frames into a unified causal embedding sequence that the LLM can process alongside text, which would be challenging with a discretization approach where each frame adds a long sequence of visual tokens.


### Innovation 3: Video as First-Class Multimodal Pretraining Data via the YT-Storyboard-1B Dataset

The third conceptual contribution is treating **video as a native, first-class source of interleaved multimodal supervision**, not as a separate modality requiring specialized architectures. Prior to Emu, multimodal models that used video (e.g., VideoChat, Video-ChatGPT) typically treated it as a domain-specific problem: separate models or architectures for video understanding, often with specialized temporal modeling components (3D convolutions, temporal attention). Video data was not integrated into the pretraining of general-purpose multimodal models in the way that image-text pairs and interleaved documents were.

The paper's insight is that **video, when represented as frame sequences with timestamp-aligned subtitles, is structurally identical to the interleaved image-text documents that Flamingo and others had shown to be effective for multimodal in-context learning**. A cooking video with step-by-step narration is essentially an interleaved document where each "paragraph" of text is paired with a corresponding visual "illustration," ordered chronologically. The temporal alignment creates a natural causal relationship—the spoken words describe what's happening on screen at that moment—which provides stronger cross-modal supervision than typical web alt-text, where the text may describe only the image's subject without detailing its composition or context.

This insight would be impractical without a solution to the data engineering challenge: storing and processing raw video at scale is prohibitively expensive. The paper's specific contribution here is the **YT-Storyboard-1B dataset and its construction methodology**: rather than downloading and decoding full videos, the authors collect YouTube's precomputed storyboard thumbnails (which are generated by YouTube for the video player's preview function) and pair them with timestamped subtitles. This achieves a 20× reduction in storage cost (Section 3.1 and Appendix A.1.1) and eliminates the need for video decoding during training, while preserving the temporal alignment that makes video valuable for multimodal supervision. The dataset contains 18 million videos and approximately 1.8 billion storyboard images.

This is a **conceptual reframing with practical innovation**, not a fundamental theoretical advance. The idea that interleaved multimodal data improves in-context learning was already established by Flamingo's use of MMC4 (interleaved image-text web documents). Emu's contribution is extending this insight to video and providing the data infrastructure to make it practical at scale. The significance lies in opening up an enormous, underutilized source of multimodal supervision: the billions of hours of narrated video content on platforms like YouTube that naturally pair dense visual signals with temporally-aligned language.

The evidence for this innovation's value is in the ablation study (Table 6, Appendix A.1.3). Adding YT-Storyboard-1B during pretraining (with a smaller Emu-7B model for computational efficiency) improves zero-shot COCO captioning CIDEr from 110.8 to 112.9, 4-shot MSVDQA from 31.1% to 34.9%, and 4-shot MSRVTTQA from 19.9% to 20.8%. These are modest but consistent gains, and the effect on video QA (MSVDQA +3.8 percentage points) is larger than the effect on image tasks, suggesting that video pretraining data particularly benefits video understanding—likely because the model learns to handle temporal sequences of frames and the subtle cross-modal correlations that characterize narrated video content.

It's worth noting that the ablation is on a smaller model (7B vs. 14B) with reduced training, so the full impact on the final Emu model may be larger than the ablation suggests. The paper doesn't provide an ablation on the final 14B model, which is a limitation, but the consistent directional improvement across tasks supports the claim that interleaved video-text data is a beneficial pretraining signal.


### Innovation 4: The Diagnostic Finding That End-to-End Multimodal Generative Pretraining Yields Stronger In-Context Learning Than Frozen-Encoder Approaches

This innovation is primarily an **empirical finding with diagnostic implications**, not an architectural contribution. The paper systematically demonstrates that Emu—trained end-to-end with a unified generative objective over both text and visual embeddings—achieves better few-shot in-context learning than comparable models with frozen vision encoders and text-only training objectives. The diagnostic insight is that **the ability to learn from multimodal context depends not just on the scale of the model or the diversity of the data, but on whether the vision pathway is jointly optimized with the language pathway during pretraining**.

The evidence comes from the few-shot evaluation in Table 3, where Emu is compared against Flamingo-9B and Kosmos-1 across image and video QA tasks with $k = 2, 4, 8$ in-context examples:

- On VQAv2, Emu achieves 58.4% (4-shot) vs. Flamingo-9B's 56.3%, and the gap grows with more shots (59.0% vs. 58.0% at 8-shot).
- On VizWiz, Emu achieves 41.3% (4-shot) vs. Flamingo-9B's 34.9%—a substantial +6.4 percentage point gap on a challenging dataset with diverse, real-world visual questions from blind users.
- On video tasks, Emu achieves 37.1% (4-shot MSVDQA) vs. Flamingo-9B's 36.2%, and 21.8% (4-shot MSRVTTQA) vs. 18.2%.

Critically, the performance gap between Emu and Flamingo-9B *widens* as the number of in-context examples increases on most tasks. For VizWiz, Emu goes from 37.8% (2-shot) to 43.9% (8-shot)—a +6.1 percentage point improvement—while Flamingo-9B goes from 34.9% (4-shot) to 39.4% (8-shot)—a +4.5 percentage point improvement. This suggests that Emu is not just generally stronger; it's specifically **better at leveraging additional in-context examples**, which is consistent with the hypothesis that end-to-end multimodal training produces visual representations that are more amenable to in-context reasoning.

Why would this be the case? The paper doesn't provide a definitive mechanistic explanation, but the likely interpretation—drawing on the architecture design choices—is that the end-to-end training allows the vision encoder to adapt its representations to the specific demands of multimodal context processing. When a Flamingo-style model sees an in-context example, the frozen CLIP encoder produces the same visual features it would produce in any context, and the model must learn to read the task structure and apply it using only the language pathway's capacity. Emu's vision encoder, having been trained with gradients from the multimodal objective, may produce visual features that are more "context-aware"—for instance, emphasizing features relevant to the task demonstrated in the in-context examples—or at minimum, features that are better aligned with how the language model processes multimodal sequences.

This is a **diagnostic finding** rather than a new technique. It doesn't propose a novel method but rather clarifies a previously ambiguous relationship between training methodology and in-context learning capability. The prior literature was mixed: Flamingo demonstrated impressive few-shot learning with frozen encoders and trained adapters, suggesting that end-to-end training wasn't necessary. But Emu's systematic comparison shows that end-to-end training provides an additional boost, particularly on tasks (like VizWiz) that require nuanced visual reasoning beyond what generic CLIP features capture well. This finding reframes the design tradeoff for future multimodal models: the computational cost of end-to-end training (vs. the efficiency of frozen encoders) must be weighed against the in-context learning gains, especially for applications where few-shot adaptation to novel tasks is critical.

The instruction-tuned model (Emu-I) provides corroborating evidence. Emu-I achieves VQAv2 accuracy of 62.0% in zero-shot (with text-only examples following Flamingo's evaluation protocol, indicated by *), which surpasses Flamingo-80B (56.3%) despite being 5.7× smaller. The instruction tuning is a lightweight LoRA adaptation on top of the pretrained Emu, meaning it inherits the visual representations learned during end-to-end pretraining. The fact that even a small LoRA fine-tuning step (which can't fundamentally restructure visual representations) yields strong performance suggests that the pretrained visual features are already well-suited to downstream multimodal reasoning—consistent with the hypothesis that the end-to-end generative objective produces more transferable visual representations.

This innovation is **significant but incremental** in the sense that it doesn't introduce a new concept—the idea that end-to-end training is better than frozen components is well-established in deep learning broadly—but it provides the first systematic evidence in the specific context of multimodal LLM in-context learning, with a careful comparison against the strongest frozen-encoder baseline (Flamingo) across a range of tasks and shot counts. It converts a plausible hypothesis ("end-to-end training should help") into an empirically validated finding with clear performance implications.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

**Dataset.** Emu is evaluated on 9 benchmarks spanning image captioning, image question answering, visual dialog, video question answering, and text-to-image generation (Table 9). The image benchmarks include: MS-COCO Caption (test split, metric: CIDEr), VQAv2 (test-dev split, VQA accuracy), OKVQA (validation split, VQA accuracy with Porter stemming), VizWiz (test-dev split, VQA accuracy), and VisDial (validation split, NDCG). Video benchmarks include: MSVDQA (test split, Top-1 accuracy), MSRVTTQA (test split, Top-1 accuracy), and NextQA (test split, WUPS). Text-to-image generation is evaluated on the MS-COCO validation set using 30,000 randomly sampled prompts, with FID as the metric.

**Base model.** The pretrained Emu model has 14 billion parameters total: EVA-01-CLIP (1B) as Visual Encoder, a 12-layer Causal Transformer (randomly initialized), and LLaMA-13B as the Multimodal Modeling LLM—all trained end-to-end for 10,000 steps. The instruction-tuned variant, Emu-I, freezes all pretrained parameters and fine-tunes only LoRA adapters (rank 16) on the self-attention layers of the LLM, using language instructions from ShareGPT and Alpaca, image instructions from a filtered LLaVA subset (130K samples), and video instructions from VideoChat-11K and a 30K subset of Video-ChatGPT-100k. For few-shot evaluation, the paper also reports a smaller Emu-7B configuration (Table 6, ablation only).

**Metrics.** For image captioning, CIDEr (Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation) is used, where higher is better—this metric measures the consensus between generated captions and human reference captions using TF-IDF weighted n-gram matching. VQA accuracy for VQAv2, OKVQA, and VizWiz uses the standard VQA evaluation protocol where the model's predicted answer string is compared against 10 human answers, with soft scoring for ambiguous cases on VQAv2 (the paper uses the test-dev server for evaluation). Video QA uses Top-1 accuracy (exact match). VisDial uses NDCG (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain). FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) for text-to-image is computed following Ramesh et al. (2021) on 30K samples; lower is better, and it measures both image quality and diversity by comparing the distribution of generated images to the distribution of real images in Inception feature space. For MM-Vet (in-the-wild evaluation), the paper reports mean and standard deviation across 5 evaluation runs, with per-capability scores for Recognition, OCR, Knowledge, Generation, Spatial, and Math.

**Baselines.** The zero-shot comparison (Table 1) includes: **Flamingo-9B** (Alayrac et al., 2022)—a multimodal model with frozen vision encoder and LLM connected by trained gated cross-attention; **Kosmos-1** (Huang et al., 2023)—an autoregressive model trained on multimodal inputs with text-only output; **MetaLM** (Hao et al., 2022)—a language model serving as a general-purpose interface; **PALI-X-55B** (Chen et al., 2023c)—a 55B-parameter model with per-task fine-tuning (not zero-shot). For text-to-image (Table 2), baselines include unimodal generation models: **GLIDE** (Nichol et al., 2021), **Make-A-Scene** (Gafni et al., 2022), **DALL-E 2** (Ramesh et al., 2022), **SDv1.5** (Rombach et al., 2022), **Imagen** (Saharia et al., 2022), and **Parti** (Yu et al., 2022b); plus one multimodal generation model: **GILL** (Koh et al., 2023a)—which grafts a frozen Stable Diffusion decoder onto an LLM. The few-shot comparison (Table 3) includes **Kosmos-1** and **Flamingo-9B** under the same RICES example selection protocol. The in-the-wild comparison (Table 4) includes **LLaMA-Adapter v2-7B**, **MiniGPT-4-14B**, **InstructBLIP-14B**, **DreamLLM-7B**, and **LLaVA-65B**. The concurrent work comparison (Table 11) includes **CM3Leon** (Yu et al., 2023a)—which discretizes images into tokens for unified autoregressive training—and **AnyMAL-13B** (Moon et al., 2023)—which processes any modality but with weaker image-to-text performance.

**Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a unified generation budget metric in the same way as the prior example (which measured everything in "generations"). Instead, comparisons are made at fixed model sizes: Emu (14B) is compared against models of comparable scale (Flamingo-9B, Kosmos-1, CM3Leon, etc.), and performance is measured as a function of the number of in-context examples $k$ in few-shot evaluation ($k = 2, 4, 8$). For text-to-image generation, the PNDM scheduler runs for 50 denoising steps with classifier-free guidance (scale 5.0 for Emu, 3.0 for SDv1.5). The visual decoder training uses 15,000 iterations on 32 A100-40G GPUs. There is no FLOPs-matched comparison between Emu and other models of different sizes—the paper claims no scaling-law analysis.

**Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the MM-Vet evaluation, the paper follows the protocol of Yu et al. (2023b) and reports mean and standard deviation across 5 evaluation runs to account for stochasticity in model generation. For other benchmarks, evaluation uses standard test splits with fixed prompts and deterministic post-processing (e.g., stemming for OKVQA, filtering redundant phrases like "it is" and "a"). For few-shot evaluation, the RICES protocol uses 5000 randomly selected training samples to build a retrieval index, with examples selected by maximum cosine similarity in EVA-CLIP feature space. There is no cross-validation or statistical significance testing reported for the main evaluation results—the paper presents single-run numbers for all zero-shot and few-shot experiments.

### Main Quantitative Results

The evaluation is organized around three axes: zero-shot multimodal understanding (Table 1), zero-shot text-to-image generation (Table 2), few-shot in-context learning (Table 3), and in-the-wild evaluation (Table 4). Instruction-tuned results (Emu-I) are reported alongside the pretrained model throughout.

#### Zero-Shot Multimodal Understanding

**Emu achieves a zero-shot CIDEr score of 112.4 on COCO captioning, substantially outperforming all comparable LMMs** (Table 1). The next-best zero-shot model at comparable scale is Kosmos-1 at 84.7 (a +27.7 point gap), followed by Flamingo-9B at 79.4 (a +33.0 point gap). The only model with higher CIDEr is PALI-X-55B at 149.2, but PALI-X is nearly 4× larger (55B vs. 14B parameters) and uses per-task fine-tuning rather than zero-shot evaluation—making it a fundamentally different experimental condition. Emu-I further improves to 120.4 CIDEr (+8.0 points over the pretrained model), demonstrating that lightweight instruction tuning on multimodal conversation data enhances caption quality.

On image question answering, **Emu achieves 52.0% on VQAv2** (zero-shot), surpassing Kosmos-1 (51.0%) and Flamingo-9B (51.8%). However, these numbers are close—the margin over Flamingo-9B is only +0.2 percentage points. Emu's advantage becomes more pronounced on knowledge-intensive VQA: **on OKVQA, Emu achieves 38.2%** vs. Flamingo-9B's 44.7%—actually *worse* by -6.5 points—but with text-only example prompting (Emu*), accuracy rises to 42.8%, and Emu-I* reaches 49.2%. The instruction-tuned variant is substantially stronger: Emu-I* achieves 62.0% on VQAv2, which the paper notes "can outperform much larger-scale Flamingo-80B" (which scores 56.3% on VQAv2, cited in the text).

On VizWiz—a challenging dataset of visual questions from blind users with diverse, real-world images—**Emu achieves 34.2%** (zero-shot), outperforming Kosmos-1 (29.2%) and Flamingo-9B (28.8%). With text-only examples (Emu*), this rises to 34.4%, and Emu-I* reaches 38.3%, surpassing Flamingo-80B's 31.6%. The VizWiz results are notable because they demonstrate Emu's robustness to the kind of uncurated, varied visual inputs that characterize real-world applications, where Flamingo's frozen CLIP encoder may struggle with images outside the pretraining distribution.

On video question answering, results are more mixed. **Emu achieves 18.8% on MSVDQA** (zero-shot), which is substantially *lower* than Flamingo-9B's 30.2%—a -11.4 point gap. However, with text-only example prompting (Emu*), MSVDQA rises dramatically to 34.3% (surpassing Flamingo-9B's 30.2%), and MSRVTTQA rises from 8.3% to 17.8% (vs. Flamingo-9B's 13.7%). This sensitivity to the prompting format suggests that the pretrained Emu is not inherently worse at video understanding, but rather that the standard zero-shot prompt format (using Multimodal Chain-of-Thought) is suboptimal for video tasks compared to the text-only example format that Flamingo uses. Emu-I achieves 34.6% on MSVDQA and 16.8% on MSRVTTQA without the text-only example format, confirming that instruction tuning improves video understanding.

On VisDial (visual dialog), **Emu achieves NDCG 47.4**, outperforming Flamingo-9B's 48.0—it's actually marginally worse by -0.6 NDCG. However, Emu-I* reaches 51.1 NDCG, substantially above both.

The NextQA results (temporal/causal video QA) are consistently low across all models: Emu achieves 19.6% (WUPS), Emu-I* achieves 19.9%, and Flamingo-9B reaches 23.0%. None of the models perform well on this challenging benchmark, suggesting that temporal reasoning over video remains an open problem for this class of multimodal models, regardless of training methodology.

#### Zero-Shot Text-to-Image Generation

**Emu achieves FID 11.66 on 30K MS-COCO prompts**, which places it in the middle of the evaluation landscape (Table 2). This is better than the concurrent multimodal generation model **GILL (12.20)**—a +0.54 improvement, suggesting that Emu's end-to-end visual embedding regression yields better generation quality than post-hoc grafting of a frozen diffusion model onto an LLM. However, Emu is **worse than dedicated text-to-image models**: SDv1.5 achieves 9.93 (a -1.73 gap), DALL-E 2 achieves 10.39, and state-of-the-art models like Imagen (7.27) and Parti (7.23) are substantially better.

The paper explicitly attributes this gap to two factors: (1) the visual decoder is trained for only 15,000 iterations versus the full training budgets of dedicated generation models, and (2) the condition space of Emu's visual embeddings deviates from the text-embedding condition space that SDv1.5 was originally designed for, meaning the decoder's new linear projection layers must learn to map between fundamentally different representation spaces—a more challenging task than fine-tuning within the same modality.

This is a **partially negative result** with an important diagnostic implication: the unified autoregressive objective succeeds in producing visual embeddings that are *adequate* for generation (substantially better than random), but the quality gap relative to dedicated diffusion models suggests that the embeddings produced by the LLM's regression head are not yet as information-rich as CLIP text embeddings for conditioning the diffusion process. This could be due to the ℓ₂ regression loss not capturing perceptual quality (an embedding that minimizes ℓ₂ distance in the latent space may not correspond to the most visually faithful image), or to the relatively short visual decoder training budget.

#### Few-Shot In-Context Learning

**Emu demonstrates consistent improvements with additional in-context examples across all evaluated tasks** (Table 3), and outperforms both Flamingo-9B and Kosmos-1 at comparable shot counts. On VQAv2, Emu achieves 56.4% (2-shot), 58.4% (4-shot), and 59.0% (8-shot)—a monotonic improvement totaling +2.6 points from 2 to 8 shots. Flamingo-9B achieves only 58.0% at 8-shot (vs. Emu's 59.0%), and PALI-X—despite per-task fine-tuning—achieves only 57.1% at 8-shot.

On VizWiz, the improvement is steeper: 37.8% (2-shot) → 41.3% (4-shot) → 43.9% (8-shot), a +6.1 point gain from 2 to 8 shots. Flamingo-9B shows a smaller gain: 34.9% (4-shot) → 39.4% (8-shot), ending 4.5 points below Emu at 8-shot. This accelerated improvement with more examples is consistent with the hypothesis that Emu's end-to-end training produces visual representations better suited to in-context reasoning—the model can extract more information from each additional example.

On video tasks, **Emu achieves 36.0% (2-shot) → 37.1% (4-shot) → 39.8% (8-shot) on MSVDQA**, vs. Flamingo-9B's 40.8% at 8-shot—Flamingo is actually +1.0 point better at 8-shot, though Emu leads at 4-shot (37.1% vs. 36.2%). On MSRVTTQA, Emu achieves 21.2% (2-shot) → 21.8% (4-shot) → 24.1% (8-shot), with the 4-shot result surpassing Flamingo-9B's 18.2% by +3.6 points.

The consistent positive correlation between shot count $k$ and accuracy across all tasks and shot ranges confirms that Emu possesses genuine in-context learning ability—it's not just benefiting from a stronger zero-shot baseline but actively leveraging additional examples to improve predictions. The paper does not report confidence intervals, so the statistical reliability of the 1-2 point gaps at specific shot counts is unclear, but the monotonic trend across shot counts on each task is internally consistent and supports the claimed capability.

#### In-the-Wild Evaluation (MM-Vet)

**Emu-I achieves a total MM-Vet score of 36.3±0.3**, which the paper positions as state-of-the-art among comparable models (Table 4). The closest competitor is DreamLLM-7B at 35.9±0.1, followed by LLaVA-65B at 35.5±0.3. Emu-I leads in Recognition (45.5 vs. DreamLLM's 41.8), Knowledge (36.7 vs. 33.4), and Generation (35.9 vs. 33.0), but lags behind in OCR (19.2 vs. DreamLLM's 26.4) and Math (3.8 vs. DreamLLM's 11.5 and LLaVA-65B's 15.0).

The MM-Vet results reveal a capability profile with clear strengths and weaknesses. Emu-I excels at tasks requiring high-level visual understanding and world knowledge grounding (Recognition, Knowledge) and creative generation (Generation), but struggles with tasks requiring fine-grained visual detail extraction (OCR) and structured reasoning about visual content (Math, Spatial). The OCR weakness is notable because it suggests that Emu's visual encoder and Causal Transformer may lose the fine-grained detail needed for reading text in images—possibly a consequence of the 224×224 input resolution (which limits text legibility) or the compression from 2D spatial features to a fixed-length causal embedding sequence. The Math weakness at 3.8 is severe relative to LLaVA-65B at 15.0, suggesting that Emu's autoregressive visual generation pathway is not well-suited to the kind of structured, rule-based reasoning that mathematical problems require.

#### Concurrent Work Comparison (Table 11, Appendix D)

**Emu substantially outperforms CM3Leon on image-to-text tasks**, despite both sharing a unified generative motivation. On COCO captioning, Emu achieves 112.4 CIDEr vs. CM3Leon's 61.6 — a +50.8 point gap. On VQAv2, 52.0% vs. 47.6%. On VizWiz, 34.2% vs. 37.6% — one of the few tasks where CM3Leon leads (+3.4 points). On VisDial, 47.4 NDCG vs. 22.6. This pattern strongly suggests that CM3Leon's discrete VQ-VAE tokenization loses critical visual information needed for detailed understanding, and the continuous embedding + Causal Transformer approach preserves significantly more visual fidelity.

Emu-I* outperforms AnyMAL-13B on image-to-text tasks: VQAv2 62.0% vs. 59.6%, OKVQA 49.2% vs. 33.1%, VizWiz 38.3% vs. 24.4%, VisDial 51.1 NDCG vs. none reported. AnyMAL processes any modality but appears to have weaker vision-language alignment than Emu's end-to-end approach.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Interleaved video-text data (YT-Storyboard-1B):** Table 6 (Appendix A.1.3) shows that adding YT-Storyboard-1B during pretraining improves Emu-7B's zero-shot COCO captioning CIDEr from 110.8 to 112.9 (+2.1), 4-shot MSVDQA from 31.1% to 34.9% (+3.8), and 4-shot MSRVTTQA from 19.9% to 20.8% (+0.9). The improvement on video QA is larger than on image tasks, suggesting video pretraining data provides a specific benefit for temporal visual understanding. The OKVQA 0-shot result slightly decreases (43.9% → 42.3%) but 4-shot increases (44.6% → 45.7%), and 0-shot* (text-only example prompting) increases from 30.2% to 30.8%. This ablation is run on a smaller 7B model with reduced training, so the full 14B model likely benefits more from the additional video data.

**Instruction tuning data composition:** The paper performs no formal ablation on instruction tuning data sources but describes filtering steps (removing LLaVA's detailed description subset and filtering the pattern "on top of the back of") that were found empirically necessary to prevent the model from generating instruction-irrelevant detailed descriptions or repetitive patterns. This is a qualitative finding rather than a quantitative ablation, but it highlights the sensitivity of instruction-tuned behavior to dataset composition.

**Text-only example prompting (denoted by *):** Across Table 1, text-only example prompts consistently improve Emu's performance, particularly for video tasks. MSVDQA jumps from 18.8% (zero-shot with Multimodal CoT) to 34.3% (with text-only examples)—a +15.5 point improvement. MSRVTTQA jumps from 8.3% to 17.8% (+9.5). Image tasks show more modest gains: OKVQA from 38.2% to 42.8% (+4.6). This large sensitivity suggests that Emu's prompting interface is not yet fully robust—the model's capabilities are partially dependent on the prompt format, and the Multimodal Chain-of-Thought technique used for standard zero-shot evaluation may not be optimal for all task types. Flamingo's evaluation protocol (which uses text-only examples in prompts) appears to better elicit Emu's capabilities for video tasks.

**Classifier-free guidance scale for image generation:** The paper reports that guidance scale 5.0 is used for Emu vs. 3.0 for SDv1.5 because "these settings yield the best performance for both models" (Section 5.1). This is a simple hyperparameter sweep rather than a systematic ablation, but the fact that Emu requires a stronger guidance scale suggests that its visual embeddings provide weaker conditioning signal than CLIP text embeddings (requiring more aggressive interpolation toward the conditioned prediction to achieve comparable fidelity).

**Video frame sampling for few-shot evaluation:** The paper notes that for video few-shot tasks, support video examples use only 2 frames (vs. 8 for the query video) to stay within the LLM's context length limit. No ablation is provided to show whether using more support frames would improve few-shot video QA performance, but the context length constraint is a practical limitation that forces this tradeoff.

**LoRA adaptation scope:** Instruction tuning attaches LoRA only to the self-attention layers of the Multimodal Modeling LLM, with no adaptation to the Vision Encoder. The paper states this is because "the main focus of instruction tuning is to align the model with natural language instructions, which are less relevant to vision features" (Section 4). No ablation is provided comparing full fine-tuning vs. LoRA-only vs. LoRA on additional components, so the claim that vision features don't need adaptation during instruction tuning remains untested.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments in this paper demonstrate that Emu achieves strong zero-shot and few-shot performance on multimodal understanding tasks (image captioning, VQA, video QA) while simultaneously supporting text-to-image generation within a single unified architecture. However, the evidence supports a **narrower claim** than the paper's framing of Emu as a "generalist interface"—the model excels at tasks where its training data and architecture align well with evaluation requirements, but several of the paper's central claims are only partially tested or reveal meaningful limitations.

**Do the experiments support the claim that unified autoregressive pretraining over both text and visual embeddings yields better performance than frozen-encoder text-only approaches?**

The comparison in Table 1 shows Emu outperforming Flamingo-9B on most tasks, with particularly large margins on COCO captioning (CIDEr 112.4 vs. 79.4, a +33.0 point gap) and VizWiz (34.2% vs. 28.8%, a +5.4 point gap). However, the comparison is confounded by several factors. Emu and Flamingo-9B use different vision encoders (EVA-CLIP vs. NFNet), different language models (LLaMA-13B vs. Chinchilla), and different pretraining data mixtures—Emu uses 4-5 data sources including novel video data, while Flamingo's interleaved data (MMC4) overlaps partially but not completely. The performance gap cannot be cleanly attributed to "unified autoregressive training vs. frozen encoders" because the models differ along multiple axes simultaneously. A fair ablation would control for the vision encoder and LLM backbone while varying only whether they're trained end-to-end or frozen, but no such experiment is provided.

The comparison against CM3Leon (Table 11) provides some isolation since both models share the unified generative motivation but differ primarily in image representation (continuous embeddings vs. discrete tokens). Emu's large lead on image-to-text tasks (CIDEr 112.4 vs. 61.6) supports the claim that continuous embeddings preserve more visual information than discretization. But this comparison doesn't isolate the effect of end-to-end training—CM3Leon also trains its visual tokenizer separately, introducing a confound. The frozen-encoder LMMs (Flamingo, BLIP-2, LLaVA) that the paper critiques have a fundamentally different design (they don't attempt visual generation), making direct comparison to Emu about the *benefits of end-to-end training* inherently difficult.

**Do the experiments support the claim that the Causal Transformer enables effective autoregressive visual modeling?**

The Causal Transformer is the core architectural innovation, but there is **no ablation analyzing its contribution** to model performance. The paper does not report results with alternative approaches to visual embedding (e.g., using a simple linear projection from EVA-CLIP features to causal embeddings, or using non-causal visual embeddings in a bidirectional model). The text-to-image FID of 11.66 (Table 2) is better than GILL (12.20) but worse than SDv1.5 (9.93), suggesting the visual embeddings are adequate but not optimal for generation—but we cannot determine whether the Causal Transformer's specific causal structure is responsible for the gap or whether other factors (short decoder training, ℓ₂ loss limitations) dominate. Without an ablation comparing Causal Transformer to alternative visual embedding strategies, the claim that this component is critical remains a design rationale rather than an empirically validated finding.

**Do the experiments support the claim that video data (YT-Storyboard-1B) improves multimodal pretraining?**

The ablation in Table 6 provides direct evidence: adding YT-Storyboard-1B during pretraining improves zero-shot COCO captioning by +2.1 CIDEr and 4-shot MSVDQA by +3.8 percentage points on a 7B model. These are consistent positive effects, supporting the claim that interleaved video-text data benefits multimodal pretraining. However, the effect sizes are modest (2-4% relative improvement), and the ablation is only run on a smaller model with reduced training budget—the full 14B model trained for 10K steps may show different scaling behavior. Additionally, the ablation compares "w/o YT-Storyboard-1B" vs. "w/ YT-Storyboard-1B" but doesn't control for total training data volume—adding 1.8 billion storyboard images substantially increases the total data, and some portion of the gain may be attributable to simply seeing more data rather than the specific *interleaved video* structure.

**Do the experiments support the claim that Emu is a "generalist interface" for diverse multimodal tasks?**

The paper demonstrates Emu performing image captioning, image QA, video QA, text-to-image generation, in-context image generation, image blending, and multimodal dialogue. This is indeed impressive breadth for a single model. However, several capabilities are only demonstrated qualitatively (in-context image generation in Figure 8, image blending in Figure 7, multimodal assistant in Figures 5-6) without quantitative benchmarks. The qualitative examples are compelling but selected—we don't know the failure rate, output diversity, or sensitivity to prompt phrasing. For text-to-image, the quantitative FID result (11.66) confirms viability but falls below dedicated generation models, showing that the "generalist" trade-off comes with a generation quality cost. For video understanding, the NextQA score (19.6% WUPS) is weak, suggesting the generalist interface doesn't extend well to temporal/causal reasoning over video.

**What experiments would strengthen the paper?**

Several missing ablations and comparisons are notable:

1. **Causal Transformer ablation:** Compare the current Causal Transformer design against (a) a simple MLP projection from EVA-CLIP features to causal embeddings with learned positional encodings, (b) non-causal visual embeddings processed by a bidirectional Transformer, and (c) a variant where the Causal Transformer is trained with the same ℓ₂ loss but without cross-attention to EVA-CLIP features. This would isolate the specific contribution of the causal structure and cross-attention conditioning.

2. **Frozen vs. trained vision encoder:** Fine-tune Emu with a frozen EVA-CLIP and compare performance to the end-to-end trained version. This would directly test the paper's claim that training the visual encoder improves multimodal performance.

3. **Training data ablation:** Isolate the effect of each data source (LAION-2B, MMC4, WebVid-10M, YT-Storyboard-1B) on downstream performance to understand which data types drive which capabilities.

4. **Scale analysis:** Evaluate how performance scales with model size (e.g., 7B vs. 14B vs. larger) and with pretraining duration (beyond 10K steps). The paper shows one data point at 14B and 10K steps—we don't know whether the benefits of unified training grow, shrink, or plateau with scale.

5. **Confidence intervals:** The paper reports single-run accuracy numbers for most benchmarks. For close comparisons (e.g., Emu's 52.0% vs. Flamingo-9B's 51.8% on VQAv2), statistical significance is unclear without variance estimates.

6. **Text-to-image quality ablations:** Systematically vary the visual decoder training budget, the number of visual embeddings $N$, and the ℓ₂ loss weight to understand how these design choices affect generation quality.

**Where do the claims hold conditionally?**

The claim that end-to-end unified pretraining yields better performance holds most clearly for image captioning and certain VQA tasks (VizWiz, OKVQA with text-only prompting), where Emu substantially outperforms frozen-encoder models at comparable scale. It holds **less clearly** for video QA without text-only example prompting (MSVDQA 18.8% vs. Flamingo-9B's 30.2%) and for NextQA temporal reasoning (19.6% vs. 23.0%), suggesting that the benefits of end-to-end training may be task-dependent and that video understanding remains a challenge regardless of training methodology. The "generalist interface" claim holds qualitatively but with quantitative caveats: Emu can do many things, but its text-to-image quality lags behind dedicated models, and its video understanding is inconsistent across tasks and prompting formats. The claim about YT-Storyboard-1B's value holds directionally (consistent improvements in the ablation) but the effect sizes are modest and the ablation is on a smaller model.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Causal Transformer—The Core Architectural Innovation—Receives No Ablative Validation

**The assumption or constraint.** The Causal Transformer is introduced as the key mechanism for transforming 2D visual encodings into 1D causal embedding sequences, enabling autoregressive visual modeling without discretization. The paper states that "Auto-regressively modeling images in raster order is counter-intuitive and has not demonstrated satisfactory performance" (Section 2.1) and proposes the Causal Transformer as the solution. However, **the paper provides no ablation or controlled experiment demonstrating that the Causal Transformer specifically—as opposed to any other learnable projection from 2D features to a 1D sequence—is responsible for the model's performance.** There is no comparison against alternative designs: a simple MLP projection with learned positional encodings, a non-causal Transformer decoder, or direct use of EVA-CLIP patch embeddings with causal masking in the LLM.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot determine whether the Causal Transformer is genuinely necessary or whether a simpler component would suffice. The architecture adds 12 Transformer layers (each with causal self-attention, cross-attention, and feed-forward sub-layers) and is randomly initialized, meaning it must be trained from scratch—adding substantial parameters and training cost. If a simpler projection achieved comparable performance, the architectural complexity and training overhead would be unnecessary. Conversely, if the Causal Transformer is genuinely critical, its contribution needs to be isolated so that future work can build on it or improve it. Without ablative evidence, the Causal Transformer remains a **design hypothesis** rather than an empirically validated component—we know the overall Emu architecture works, but we do not know *why* or whether each piece is load-bearing.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The Causal Transformer is described architecturally (Section 2.1), and its training hyperparameters are specified (Table 5: peak learning rate 1e-4, random init), but there is no ablation, no comparison to alternative designs, and no analysis of the causal embeddings it produces (e.g., visualization of what $z_1, z_2, \dots, z_N$ capture, or whether the causal structure is actually meaningful). The only indirect evidence for the continuous-embedding approach comes from the CM3Leon comparison (Table 11, Appendix D), where Emu substantially outperforms CM3Leon's discrete-token approach on image-to-text tasks (CIDEr 112.4 vs. 61.6). But this comparison pits Emu's entire architecture (continuous embeddings + Causal Transformer + end-to-end training) against CM3Leon's entirely different approach (discrete VQ-VAE tokens + separate tokenizer training)—it does not isolate the Causal Transformer's contribution from the continuous-vs-discrete representation choice or the end-to-end-vs-separate training choice.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not attempt to ablate the Causal Transformer, and does not suggest future work on validating or simplifying the component. A practitioner implementing Emu would need to accept the Causal Transformer on faith or run their own ablations at significant computational cost.


### The Pretraining-to-Inference Cost Ratio for Difficulty Estimation Is Not Quantified, and the Cost of Producing Visual Embeddings During Inference Is Not Analyzed

**The assumption or constraint.** All of Emu's headline results assume the model generates outputs autoregressively, producing either text tokens or $N$ visual embeddings (where $N$ is the sequence length of the Causal Transformer output) one element at a time. For text-to-image generation, the model must regress $N$ visual embeddings sequentially before the Visual Decoder can begin the diffusion process (which itself requires 50 denoising steps). The paper does not report inference latency, FLOPs per generated image, or any comparison of the cost of generating an image via Emu versus using a dedicated text-to-image model (e.g., SDv1.5 directly, without the autoregressive embedding step). Section 3.3 notes that the Visual Decoder is trained for only 15,000 iterations and that "our model is inferior to SDv1.5 in terms of FID" (Section 5.1), attributing the gap partly to "the condition space (image embeddings) of our visual decoder deviates a lot from the condition space (text embeddings) of the diffusion model used as initialization."

**The consequence.** A practitioner evaluating whether to deploy Emu for text-to-image generation faces two unquantified costs. First, the **autoregressive generation of $N$ visual embeddings** adds latency and compute that a dedicated diffusion model (which conditions directly on a CLIP text embedding computed in a single forward pass) does not incur. The paper does not report $N$ explicitly, but the Causal Transformer architecture description implies it produces a fixed-length sequence (likely 256 or 257 embeddings to match the EVA-CLIP output dimensions). Generating 256 embeddings autoregressively through a 13B-parameter LLM is substantially more expensive than encoding a text prompt through a CLIP text encoder. Second, the **50-step PNDM diffusion process** that follows is identical in architecture to SDv1.5's but uses a potentially weaker conditioning signal (Emu's regressed embeddings vs. SD's CLIP text embeddings), requiring a higher classifier-free guidance scale (5.0 vs. 3.0) and still producing worse FID (11.66 vs. 9.93). The net result is a model that is both slower and lower-quality at image generation than the dedicated alternative—a tradeoff that may be acceptable if the unified interface is valuable enough, but the paper provides no quantitative framework for making this decision.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The text-to-image results in Table 2 show FID 11.66 for Emu vs. 9.93 for SDv1.5—a clear quality gap. The text notes the guidance scale difference (5.0 vs. 3.0) and the short training budget (15K iterations). However, there is **no measurement of inference cost**: no FLOPs comparison, no wall-clock time on specified hardware, no tokens-per-image or steps-per-image metric. The paper does not even explicitly state the value of $N$ (the number of visual embeddings generated per image), which is a critical parameter for understanding the autoregressive generation cost.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the quality gap (attributing it to insufficient decoder training and condition space mismatch) in Section 5.1 but does not address the inference cost or suggest that the autoregressive embedding generation is a bottleneck. A practitioner would need to measure inference costs empirically on their own hardware to determine whether the unified-interface benefit outweighs the generation cost overhead.


### The 224×224 Visual Input Resolution Creates a Hard Cap on Fine-Grained Visual Understanding

**The assumption or constraint.** Emu processes all images and video frames at 224×224 resolution (Section 3.2). The EVA-CLIP visual encoder uses a patch size of 14×14, producing a 16×16 grid of patch embeddings (256 patches + 1 CLS token). All visual information—text in images, fine spatial relationships, small objects, detailed textures—must be captured within this resolution and patch grid. The paper does not experiment with higher resolutions or dynamic resolution scaling. The Causal Transformer then condenses these 257 embeddings into $N$ causal embeddings, potentially further compressing the information.

**The consequence.** Emu-I achieves a score of only **19.2 on the OCR capability of MM-Vet** (Table 4), dramatically lower than DreamLLM-7B (26.4) and LLaVA-65B (28.2). The Math score is 3.8 versus DreamLLM's 11.5 and LLaVA-65B's 15.0. Both OCR and Math require reading and reasoning about fine-grained visual details (text characters, mathematical symbols, spatial layouts). The 224×224 resolution is almost certainly a bottleneck: text that is legible at 512×512 or higher resolution may become illegible at 224×224, and mathematical expressions with small symbols and precise spatial arrangements may be impossible to parse correctly. The paper's qualitative examples (Figures 5, 10, 11) show the model reading book covers and describing artworks, but these are selected instances where the text is large relative to the image—the failure cases on small or dense text are not shown.

This limitation also affects video understanding, where frames are sampled at 224×224 and temporal information is captured only through the sequence of 8 frames per video. Subtle actions, small objects in motion, or detailed scene changes between frames may be lost at this resolution, contributing to Emu's weak NextQA performance (19.6% WUPS, substantially below Flamingo-9B's 23.0%—Table 1).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The MM-Vet per-capability breakdown (Table 4) provides direct evidence: OCR at 19.2, Spatial at 25.2, Math at 3.8 are all substantially below the model's strengths (Recognition 45.5, Knowledge 36.7). The NextQA score of 19.6% provides corroborating evidence that video understanding with fine temporal and causal structure is a weakness. The paper does not report any analysis of how performance varies with input resolution or whether text-reading accuracy degrades for small-font text.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge the input resolution as a limitation or discuss higher-resolution variants for future work. The 224×224 choice appears motivated by computational constraints (training time, GPU memory, context length in the LLM—each image at higher resolution would produce more patch embeddings, increasing the sequence length). A practitioner deploying Emu for applications requiring text reading or fine-grained visual analysis (document understanding, chart comprehension, medical imaging) would likely find the 224×224 resolution to be a critical bottleneck with no documented mitigation path within the Emu architecture.


### The Generalist Interface Claim Overstates Quantitative Coverage—Video Temporal Reasoning, Image Generation Quality, and Prompting Robustness Remain Weak

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper positions Emu as a "generalist interface for both image-to-text and text-to-image tasks" (Section 1) and a model that "can serve as a multimodal generalist that can perform any types of completion in a multimodal sequence" (Section 2.3). This framing implies broad, robust competence across the claimed task categories. However, the quantitative evidence supports a **narrower capability profile**: Emu is strong at image captioning and certain VQA tasks, adequate at text-to-image generation (but below dedicated models), and weak or inconsistent on video reasoning and temporally-structured tasks.

**The consequence.** The specific failure modes matter for deployment:

- **Video temporal reasoning (NextQA):** Emu achieves 19.6% WUPS (Table 1), which is below Flamingo-9B (23.0%) and represents near-chance performance on a benchmark that explicitly tests the ability to understand temporal and causal relationships in video. For applications involving instructional videos, sports analysis, or surveillance—domains where temporal reasoning is the primary capability requirement—Emu would be an inappropriate choice.

- **Image generation quality:** FID 11.66 (Table 2) is competitive only with the concurrent work GILL (12.20) and substantially worse than dedicated models (SDv1.5: 9.93, DALL-E 2: 10.39). For applications where image quality is paramount (creative tools, stock image generation, design assistance), Emu's generation quality lags behind readily available alternatives.

- **Prompting fragility:** The zero-shot video QA results show extreme sensitivity to prompting format. MSVDQA accuracy jumps from 18.8% (standard zero-shot with Multimodal Chain-of-Thought) to 34.3% (with text-only example prompting, denoted by *)—a +15.5 percentage point swing (Table 1). MSRVTTQA jumps from 8.3% to 17.8%. This magnitude of sensitivity means that Emu's apparent video understanding capability is heavily dependent on how the question is framed, and a user who doesn't know the optimal prompting format may experience dramatically worse performance than the headline numbers suggest.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The evidence is distributed across multiple tables: the NextQA score of 19.6% (Table 1), the text-to-image FID of 11.66 vs. SDv1.5's 9.93 (Table 2), and the large gaps between standard zero-shot and text-only-example-prompted results on video tasks (Table 1, compare Emu vs. Emu* on MSVDQA and MSRVTTQA). The in-the-wild MM-Vet results (Table 4) further show that Math and OCR capabilities are near-absent (3.8 and 19.2 respectively), despite the generalist framing. The qualitative demonstrations (Figures 1, 4-8, 10-12) are selected positive examples and do not provide failure rate or diversity statistics.

**Mitigation status.** The paper presents these results transparently—the weak NextQA and FID numbers are reported alongside stronger results, and the prompting sensitivity is visible in the Emu vs. Emu* rows. However, the paper does not explicitly discuss these as capability boundaries or caution that the "generalist" label should be qualified. Section 7 (Limitations and Future Topics) acknowledges generic LLM/LMM issues (hallucination, slow inference, English-only) but does not engage with the specific capability gaps visible in the quantitative results. A practitioner would need to parse the tables carefully to understand where Emu's performance is competitive versus where it substantially underperforms alternatives.


### The Visual Decoder Is Trained on a Fixed 7:2 Data Mixture for Only 15K Steps, Making Generation Quality Tightly Coupled to This Narrow Recipe

**The assumption or constraint.** The Visual Decoder is fine-tuned from Stable Diffusion v1.5 on a specific data mixture: LAION-COCO and LAION-Aesthetics in a 7:2 ratio, for 15,000 iterations with a batch size of 50×4×8 (effective batch size 1600) on 32 A100-40G GPUs (Section 3.3 and Appendix A.2.2). The Visual Encoder, Causal Transformer, and Multimodal Modeling LLM are **frozen** during this training—the decoder must learn to map from whatever visual embedding space Emu's autoregressive generation produces, without any ability to adapt the embeddings to improve decodability. The learning rate schedule is manually staged (warm up to 1e-4 for 5K steps, decrease to 5e-5 at 10K, 1e-5 at 14K), and text prompts longer than 150 characters are filtered out to prevent GPU memory overflow.

**The consequence.** This training recipe is **underexplored and potentially suboptimal** in several ways. First, the 7:2 mixture ratio is stated without ablation—we do not know whether a different ratio, inclusion of other data sources, or use of higher-quality image-text pairs would improve generation quality. Second, the manual learning rate schedule was likely determined by trial and error on this specific setup; its transferability to other model sizes or data mixtures is unknown. Third, the frozen embedding space means that any mismatch between the embeddings Emu naturally produces during generation and the embeddings the decoder was trained to interpret will result in degraded image quality—and there is no mechanism for the decoder to signal back to the LLM to adjust its regression target. Fourth, the 15K iteration budget is described as "relatively short" (Section 5.1), and the paper explicitly attributes the FID gap versus SDv1.5 partly to this: "our model is trained for a relatively short 15k steps" (Section 5.1). This acknowledgment implies that simply training longer might close some of the gap, but no scaling curve is provided.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The text-to-image FID result (Table 2: 11.66) is the primary evidence of generation quality. The paper's attribution of the SDv1.5 gap to "the condition space... deviates a lot from the condition space of the diffusion model used as initialization" and "our model is trained for a relatively short 15k steps" (Section 5.1) is an explicit acknowledgment that the decoder training is not optimized. The filtering rule (drop text prompts longer than 150 characters) is mentioned in Appendix A.2.1 but its impact on data diversity is not assessed. No ablation over training duration, data mixture, learning rate schedule, or batch size is provided.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the short training duration as a contributor to the FID gap but does not propose specific improvements or report results with longer training. The frozen-embedding issue is not discussed as a fundamental limitation—it is simply noted that the condition space deviates from SDv1.5's original text-embedding space. A practitioner seeking to improve image generation quality would need to explore the decoder training recipe from scratch, with no guidance from the paper on which dimensions (data, duration, learning rate, batch size) matter most.


### Instruction Tuning and Video Pretraining Ablations Are Run Only on a 7B Model, While the Main 14B Results Lack Diagnostic Depth

**The assumption or constraint.** The ablation showing that YT-Storyboard-1B improves multimodal pretraining is conducted on an Emu-7B model with reduced batch sizes and a shorter training schedule (Section A.1.3): "Smaller model size, batch size and shorter training schedule are used compared to the final Emu model to reduce the training cost." The main results in Tables 1-3 are all on the full 14B Emu model, but the only ablation isolating any component's contribution—the video-text data—is on a model half the size with a different (unspecified) training duration. Similarly, instruction tuning (Section 4) is evaluated on the full 14B Emu-I, but the LoRA rank and adaptation scope were chosen without reported ablations (the paper states that LoRA is attached only to self-attention layers because "the main focus of instruction tuning is to align the model with natural language instructions, which are less relevant to vision features"—a plausible but untested claim).

**The consequence.** The internal validity of the ablation is compromised: the 7B model may exhibit different scaling behavior than the 14B model, and the reduced training schedule may not reach the same convergence point. The +2.1 CIDEr improvement from adding YT-Storyboard-1B on the 7B model (Table 6) may not represent the marginal benefit on the full 14B model—it could be larger (if video data's benefits scale with model capacity) or smaller (if the 14B model already saturates the available signal from other data sources). More broadly, the paper makes architectural claims (Causal Transformer's importance, end-to-end training's benefit, video data's value) without isolating these factors via ablations on the full-scale model. This is a common constraint in large-scale ML research—full ablations at the target scale are expensive—but it means the paper's design recommendations are based on plausibility and small-scale evidence rather than rigorous isolation at the deployment scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The Table 6 ablation provides the only controlled experiment isolating a training data component (YT-Storyboard-1B), and it is explicitly on a smaller model. The paper does not report the training duration, batch sizes, or other hyperparameters for this 7B ablation, making replication difficult. No other ablations are provided at any scale: there is no ablation isolating the Causal Transformer, the continuous-embedding approach, the end-to-end training (vs. frozen visual encoder), or the specific data mixture ratios. The 14B results in Tables 1-3 are single-configuration evaluations with no variance estimates, cross-validation, or sensitivity analysis.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the reduced scale of the ablation: "Smaller model size, batch size and shorter training schedule are used compared to the final Emu model to reduce the training cost" (Appendix A.1.3). It does not discuss the transferability of the 7B findings to the 14B model, nor does it suggest that the full model's design choices would benefit from larger-scale ablations in future work. The lack of confidence intervals or multiple evaluation runs for the 14B results means that close comparisons (e.g., Emu's 52.0% vs. Flamingo-9B's 51.8% on VQAv2, a 0.2-point gap) cannot be assessed for statistical significance. A practitioner trying to determine whether Emu's specific design choices (Causal Transformer, continuous embeddings, video data) are worth adopting in their own model must either trust the small-scale ablation's transferability or budget for their own large-scale experiments.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper shifts the conversation around multimodal foundation models by demonstrating that **vision can be a generative output modality trained under the same autoregressive objective as text**, not merely a perceptual input to a language model. Before Emu, the dominant paradigm—exemplified by Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022), BLIP-2 (Li et al., 2023b), and LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023b)—treated vision as a frozen feature extractor whose outputs were fed into an LLM that only generated text. The vision encoder received no gradient signal from the multimodal training objective, and the model had no mechanism to produce visual content. This created a structural ceiling: whatever visual understanding was captured during the vision encoder's separate pretraining was the ceiling on downstream multimodal reasoning, and tasks requiring visual generation required entirely separate models.

Emu's core reframing is that **visual embeddings can be treated as continuous "tokens" in an autoregressive sequence**, placed on equal footing with discrete text tokens. The ℓ₂ regression loss on visual embeddings is not a minor extension of the next-token prediction paradigm—it fundamentally changes what the model is trained to *do*. Rather than learning only $P(\text{text} \mid \text{image})$, Emu learns $P(\text{text}, \text{image}_1, \text{image}_2, \dots)$ as a joint autoregressive factorization over interleaved modalities. This is a **paradigm shift in multimodal pretraining objectives**, moving from conditional generation to joint distribution modeling.

The magnitude of this shift is best understood by what it makes possible that was previously architecturally impossible within a single model:

- **In-context image generation** emerges naturally: because the model learns to complete multimodal sequences, providing an example image-text pair as context and then asking for a new image causes the model to regress visual embeddings conditioned on the example's style and content. This capability (demonstrated qualitatively in Figures 1 and 8) is not something that could be "added on" to a text-only-output model—it requires the model to have learned the joint distribution over interleaved visual and textual content during pretraining.

- **End-to-end visual representation learning for multimodal tasks** becomes possible: because the ℓ₂ regression loss backpropagates through the Causal Transformer into the Visual Encoder, the vision representations are explicitly optimized to be both (a) useful for predicting subsequent text tokens and (b) reconstructible from the LLM's hidden states during autoregressive generation. Prior frozen-encoder approaches could only optimize the adapter layers connecting vision to language; Emu can reshape how the model *sees*.

- **The "generalist interface" concept extends to bidirectional multimodal generation**: rather than deploying separate models for captioning (image → text), VQA (image + text → text), and image generation (text → image), a single Emu model handles all of these as completions in a multimodal sequence. This is not merely an engineering convenience—it enables new forms of interaction (multimodal dialogue with visual responses, in-context style-conditioned generation) that would be difficult to achieve with separate models that don't share representations.

The work also **reconciles a tension in the prior literature** between discretization-based and frozen-encoder approaches to multimodal generation. Models like Unified-IO (Lu et al., 2022) and CM3Leon (Yu et al., 2023a) achieved unified interfaces by discretizing all modalities into tokens, but this introduced a separately-trained VQ-VAE bottleneck that lost fine-grained visual information. Flamingo and BLIP-2 preserved continuous visual features but at the cost of freezing the vision encoder and restricting output to text only. Emu shows that the Causal Transformer provides a third path: continuous visual embeddings that are causally structured for autoregressive generation, avoiding the discretization bottleneck while still enabling the vision pathway to be both generative and end-to-end trainable. The CM3Leon comparison in Table 11 provides empirical weight: COCO CIDEr 112.4 for Emu vs. 61.6 for CM3Leon—a gap that suggests the continuous approach preserves substantially more visual information than VQ-VAE discretization for detailed image understanding tasks.

This work makes certain research directions **more attractive**:

- **Joint multimodal generative pretraining at larger scale**: Emu at 14B parameters and 10K training steps already shows competitive performance. The natural question—unanswered by this paper—is whether these benefits grow, plateau, or invert at larger model sizes and longer training. If the scaling trend holds, a 70B or 175B Emu-style model trained on an order of magnitude more data could substantially close the text-to-image quality gap with dedicated generation models while simultaneously improving understanding capabilities.

- **Verifier-guided or RL-based refinement of generated visual embeddings**: The visual embeddings produced by Emu's regression head are optimized for ℓ₂ distance to the Causal Transformer's target embeddings, which may not correspond to perceptual image quality. A learned reward model (e.g., aesthetics predictor, CLIP score, human preference model) could guide the autoregressive embedding generation to produce embeddings that decode into more visually appealing or semantically faithful images—analogous to how RLHF improved text generation alignment.

- **Expanding the Causal Transformer design space**: The paper proposes the Causal Transformer as a specific architecture (12-layer decoder with causal self-attention and cross-attention), but the design space—different numbers of output embeddings $N$, different architectures for the causal structure, learned vs. fixed positional encodings, whether the causal structure should be image-adaptive—is entirely unexplored. A systematic study of this component could yield substantial improvements or simplifications.

Conversely, this work makes certain directions **less attractive**:

- **Frozen vision encoders as the default architecture for multimodal LLMs**: The paper provides systematic evidence that end-to-end training with a generative vision objective improves performance (Table 1: COCO CIDEr 112.4 vs. Flamingo-9B's 79.4, +33 points; VizWiz 34.2% vs. 28.8%, +5.4 points). While frozen encoders remain computationally cheaper, the performance gap suggests that for applications where accuracy matters, the additional cost of end-to-end training is justified.

- **Discrete VQ-VAE tokenization as the preferred approach for unified multimodal generation**: The CM3Leon comparison (Table 11) shows Emu's continuous embeddings substantially outperform discrete tokens on image understanding tasks. The gap is large enough (CIDEr +50.8 points, VQAv2 +4.4 points) that it likely reflects a fundamental limitation of the discretization bottleneck rather than implementation details. Future work on unified multimodal generation will likely favor continuous embedding approaches.

- **Treating video as a separate, domain-specific problem requiring specialized architectures**: Emu's video results (Table 1: MSVDQA 34.3% with text-only prompts, 4-shot MSVDQA 37.1%) are competitive with dedicated video models, and the YT-Storyboard-1B ablation (Table 6) shows that interleaved video-text data improves image understanding as well. This suggests that video should be integrated into general-purpose multimodal pretraining rather than handled by separate video-specific architectures.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Scaling the visual decoder training budget and data mixture under controlled ablations.** The paper explicitly attributes the FID gap versus SDv1.5 (11.66 vs. 9.93, Table 2) to "relatively short 15k steps" of decoder training and the condition space deviation from SDv1.5's text embedding space. A straightforward follow-up would train the Visual Decoder for 50K, 100K, and 200K iterations while holding the frozen Emu backbone constant, measuring FID on MS-COCO and assessing whether the gap closes, plateaus, or exhibits diminishing returns. Simultaneously, varying the LAION-COCO to LAION-Aesthetics mixing ratio (currently 7:2, chosen without ablation) across values from 1:0 to 0:1 would identify whether aesthetic quality data or high-correlation data matters more for generation quality. If the FID gap does not close even with extended training, this would indicate a fundamental limitation of the ℓ₂-regressed embedding space for conditioning diffusion models—the embeddings simply may not carry enough visual information, and a stronger regression objective or architectural change would be needed.

**Replacing the ℓ₂ regression loss with a perceptual or contrastive objective.** The ℓ₂ loss on visual embeddings penalizes Euclidean distance in the Causal Transformer's output space, which may not correspond to perceptual image similarity. Two embeddings that are close in ℓ₂ could decode into images that look very different to humans, and vice versa. A natural extension is to replace or augment the ℓ₂ loss with a **perceptual loss** computed in the feature space of a pretrained vision model (e.g., CLIP image encoder or a dedicated quality-assessment network), or a **contrastive loss** that pulls the regressed embedding close to the target while pushing it away from embeddings of other images. The specific experiment: fine-tune the full Emu backbone with a combined loss $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(\text{text}) + \alpha \mathcal{L}_2(\text{vision}) + \beta \mathcal{L}_{\text{perceptual}}(\text{vision})$, sweep $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and measure both downstream image understanding accuracy (to ensure the perceptual loss doesn't degrade the utility of visual embeddings for text prediction) and text-to-image FID. If perceptual loss improves generation quality without hurting understanding, it would establish that the representation space matters as much as the autoregressive mechanism.

**Ablating and stress-testing the Causal Transformer.** The Causal Transformer is the most architecturally novel component of Emu, but it receives no ablative validation. A systematic follow-up would compare the current design against: (a) a simple linear projection from EVA-CLIP patch embeddings to $N$ fixed-size embeddings with learned positional encodings (no causal self-attention, no cross-attention), (b) the current Causal Transformer but with **non-causal** self-attention (removing the causal mask, testing whether the causal structure actually matters for downstream performance), (c) using EVA-CLIP patch embeddings directly as input to the LLM (no Causal Transformer at all, with the LLM responsible for processing raw 2D patch features), and (d) varying the number of output embeddings $N$ (e.g., 64, 128, 256, 512) to measure the tradeoff between sequence length and visual fidelity. The evaluation would measure both understanding benchmarks (COCO captioning, VQAv2) and generation quality (FID) on the full 14B model. If variant (a) or (c) matches the full Causal Transformer's performance, the component is unnecessary and can be removed, simplifying the architecture. If the causal mask in (b) proves critical for generation but not understanding, it would clarify that the causal structure exists primarily to enable autoregressive visual decoding, not to improve visual feature quality.

**Difficulty-aware decoding strategies for visual embedding generation.** The paper treats visual embedding generation uniformly: the model always regresses $N$ embeddings for a full image. But visual content varies in complexity—a simple solid-color background requires less representational capacity than a detailed photograph with multiple objects, text, and complex textures. A follow-up could train the model to generate a variable number of visual embeddings depending on image complexity, using a learned "stop" token or an adaptive computation mechanism. The training would modify the Causal Transformer to optionally produce fewer than $N$ embeddings for simple images (with a loss penalty that trades off embedding count against reconstruction quality), and the LLM would learn to generate the appropriate number of embeddings at inference time. The experiment would measure whether variable-length generation reduces average inference cost (fewer autoregressive steps for simple images) without degrading generation quality on complex images. This directly addresses the unquantified inference cost limitation: if 80% of generated images need only 64 of the 256 embeddings, the autoregressive generation bottleneck is substantially reduced.

**Multimodal RLHF or verifier-guided embedding refinement.** Emu's text-to-image generation uses a fixed regression head and frozen LLM—once the embeddings are generated, the only post-processing is classifier-free guidance during diffusion (scale 5.0, Section 5.1). A natural extension is to apply the same kind of preference-based optimization that improved LLM text generation to the visual embedding generation process. Specifically: collect human preference judgments or automated quality scores (CLIP score, aesthetics predictor, ImageReward) for images generated from the same text prompt with different random seeds. Train a reward model on these preferences, then use the reward model to guide the autoregressive embedding generation via best-of-N rejection sampling or RL fine-tuning (PPO) on the LLM's regression head. The key metric would be MS-COCO FID after reward-guided generation vs. baseline Emu, and the diagnostic question is whether reward-guided refinement can close some of the gap to dedicated diffusion models (SDv1.5 FID 9.93) without sacrificing image diversity. If a relatively small reward model can substantially improve generation quality, it would suggest that Emu's embedding space contains sufficient information but the regression head's sampling distribution is not well-aligned with perceptual quality—an alignment problem rather than a capacity problem.

**Scaling Emu to higher input resolution and measuring the OCR/Math capability ceiling.** The MM-Vet results (Table 4) reveal severe weakness in OCR (19.2) and Math (3.8) at 224×224 input resolution. A targeted follow-up would train Emu variants at 336×336 and 448×448 input resolution (with correspondingly larger patch grids from EVA-CLIP and potentially longer Causal Transformer output sequences) and measure the OCR and Math capability scores on MM-Vet. The key question: does the improvement scale linearly with resolution (suggesting the weakness is purely a pixel-count bottleneck) or does it plateau (suggesting the Causal Transformer's fixed-length embedding sequence fundamentally limits fine-grained detail regardless of input resolution)? If resolution scaling substantially closes the gap to DreamLLM-7B (OCR 26.4) and LLaVA-65B (OCR 28.2), it would establish that Emu's architecture is fundamentally capable of fine-grained visual understanding with sufficient input fidelity, making resolution scaling a high-priority optimization. If performance plateaus despite higher resolution, the Causal Transformer's compression from 2D patch grid to 1D causal sequence would be identified as the bottleneck, motivating architectural alternatives (e.g., larger $N$, adaptive $N$, or non-causal visual output with a separate decoder).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Unified multimodal assistants for creative and analytical workflows.** A production deployment of Emu-I could serve as a single assistant that handles image captioning, visual question answering, text-to-image generation, and multimodal dialogue within one model. The quantitative case: Emu-I achieves COCO captioning CIDEr 120.4 and VQAv2 accuracy 62.0% (Table 1) while simultaneously supporting text-to-image generation at FID 11.66 (Table 2). A design team using such an assistant could ask it to describe a reference image (captioning), answer questions about visual details (VQA), generate variations in a specified style (in-context generation, demonstrated in Figure 8), and iterate through multimodal dialogue (Figure 6)—all without switching between separate models. The practical benefit is not just convenience but **shared representations**: the model's understanding of a reference image's style (learned during the captioning or VQA interaction) is directly available as conditioning for subsequent image generation, enabling tighter stylistic consistency than would be possible with separate captioning and generation models that do not share internal representations. The key caveat is that image generation quality (FID 11.66) lags behind dedicated models (SDv1.5: 9.93), so this use case is most appropriate when the unified interface benefit outweighs the generation quality cost—for example, in rapid prototyping or ideation where quick iteration matters more than pixel-perfect output.

**Scalable video understanding with interleaved frame-and-text pretraining.** The YT-Storyboard-1B dataset construction method—pairing storyboard thumbnails with timestamped subtitles to create interleaved video-text sequences at 20× lower storage cost than raw video—is directly adoptable by other research groups and companies building video-capable multimodal models. The ablation evidence (Table 6) shows that adding YT-Storyboard-1B during pretraining improves video QA: 4-shot MSVDQA from 31.1% to 34.9% (+3.8 points). For a production video understanding system (e.g., content moderation, video search, instructional video analysis), this provides a concrete recipe: collect storyboard images and subtitles from a target video domain using the same methodology, integrate them into the multimodal pretraining mixture, and expect improved video understanding from the temporally-aligned cross-modal supervision. The key practical advantage is that the approach works with standard multimodal architectures (no specialized video encoders, 3D convolutions, or temporal attention mechanisms needed) and leverages infrastructure (storyboard thumbnails) that platforms like YouTube already provide, dramatically reducing the engineering barrier to including video data in multimodal pretraining.

**In-context multimodal generation for style-consistent content creation.** Emu's in-context text-to-image generation capability (Figures 1 and 8) enables a workflow where a user provides one or more example images with descriptions, and the model generates a new image that follows the style, composition, or content patterns demonstrated in the context. This is not simply "text-to-image with a style reference"—it's a more general in-context learning ability where the model infers the task structure from examples. A practical application: a marketing team maintains a library of branded visual examples (product photos in a consistent style, with consistent lighting, composition, and color grading). To generate a new product image in the brand style, they provide 2-4 examples as context along with a text description of the new product, and Emu generates an image that follows the stylistic conventions. The quantitative backing: Emu's few-shot VQA performance (Table 3) shows a consistent positive slope with more in-context examples (VizWiz: 37.8% → 41.3% → 43.9% for 2, 4, and 8 shots), demonstrating that the model genuinely leverages additional context rather than plateauing after the first example. While the paper only demonstrates this qualitatively for image generation, the underlying mechanism (autoregressive conditioning on multimodal context) is the same, suggesting the few-shot improvement trend should transfer to generation tasks. The practical limitation is that the quality of in-context generation is not benchmarked quantitatively—users would need to validate style consistency on their specific domain.
