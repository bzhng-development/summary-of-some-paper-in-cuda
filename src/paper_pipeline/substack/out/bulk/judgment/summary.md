Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article (originally from The Peterman Post, distilling the career skill of judgment that powered his Senior and Staff promotions at Meta):

**What Judgment Is**

Ryan defines judgment as "having an accurate sense of what is important for the success of the business." Everything an engineer does should benefit the business, yet he observes that many engineers lack this foundational thinking entirely. The skill isn't taught in school—it's developed on the job through two primary avenues. The first is **understanding the business and your org’s place in its success**. The easiest on-ramp is direct conversation with your manager or tech lead: "they can teach you what metrics & initiatives matter most and why." Another source is your team's strategy docs, which represent "your org’s best writing about what is planned and why." Ryan stresses that different teams create impact in fundamentally different ways, and context matters—an infrastructure team driving faster dev velocity and capacity cost savings is just as critical to the business as an ads team driving direct revenue, even though only one touches the top line. "Just because a team doesn’t directly contribute to revenue doesn’t mean that it isn’t critical to the business’s success." The second avenue is **learning by doing**. Ryan's own judgment sharpened primarily through writing strategy and direction docs and then having to defend them while building alignment. The feedback loop—getting challenged by others who already possess strong business sense—"helped hone my judgment" in a way that passive absorption never could.

**Exercising Judgment**

Possessing good judgment is useless in a vacuum; you have to exercise it to make sure you and your team are going in the right direction. Ryan quotes Steve Jobs: "It doesn't make sense to hire smart people and tell them what to do; we hire smart people so they can tell us what to do." The practical implication is that you don't simply execute assigned tasks—you think critically about the business value of whatever lands on your plate. As you grow more senior, that thinking extends beyond yourself: "you should care that what your team works on is impactful." When something isn't, you speak up and influence the team's direction. Ryan frames this as universally beneficial: "your manager doesn't want you to complete tasks for the sake of doing work. Your manager wants you and the team to have as much impact on the business as possible. The work is just an implementation detail." Since promotions are a byproduct of impact, exercising judgment directly fueled both of his key promos at Meta. For Senior, he **"started an initiative on video ads even though my team was only focused on non-ads video,"** identifying an adjacent space the business needed that fell outside the official roadmap. For Staff, he **"started an initiative optimizing compute capacity even though it wasn't something our team cared about at first,"** again spotting a gap no one was prioritizing. In both cases, he didn't wait for permission—he proved the business case and pulled the work into his orbit. Beyond career growth, Ryan finds the most compelling reason to exercise judgment is autonomy: "if you know what is impactful and can convince others, you can influence your organization to let you prioritize that work," steering yourself toward what you find most exciting and fulfilling.

---

His entire framework rests on a tight loop: **understanding the business** supplies the raw material, **learning by doing** sharpens the instinct, and **exercising judgment** converts both into outsized impact. The two promo stories—video ads and compute optimization—are the same playbook at different altitudes: see a gap the roadmap misses, prove the business case, and pull it into your orbit before anyone asks. That playbook works because it aligns the engineer's autonomy with the org's success, making the work "just an implementation detail" of a larger, self-directed strategy.
