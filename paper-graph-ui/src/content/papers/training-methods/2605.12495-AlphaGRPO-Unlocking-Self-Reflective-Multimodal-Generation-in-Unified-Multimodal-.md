# AlphaGRPO: Unlocking Self-Reflective Multimodal Generation in Unified Multimodal Models via Decompositional Verifiable Reward

**ArXiv:** [2605.12495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12495)

## 🎯 Pitch

Unified multimodal models can learn to critique and fix their own image generation errors without any supervised fine-tuning, simply by being reinforced with atomic, verifiable questions instead of a single score. This Decompositional Verifiable Reward lets an LLM break prompts into fine-grained checks, dramatically improving the model’s ability to infer hidden intent and autonomously correct misalignments across generation and editing benchmarks.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **AlphaGRPO**, a reinforcement learning framework that applies Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to an AR-Diffusion Unified Multimodal Model (the Bagel model) to enhance multimodal generation without requiring a cold-start supervised fine-tuning stage. The framework unlocks two advanced capabilities — **Reasoning Text-to-Image Generation** (where the model actively infers implicit user intents, e.g., planning spatial layouts before generating) and **Self-Reflective Refinement** (where the model autonomously diagnoses and corrects misalignments in its own outputs, e.g., fixing attribute binding errors) — by optimizing both the autoregressive reasoning text and the diffusion image trajectory under a shared group advantage signal. To provide stable, discriminative supervision for open-world generation, the paper introduces the **Decompositional Verifiable Reward (DVReward)** , which uses an LLM to decompose complex user prompts into atomic verifiable questions spanning semantic alignment and perceptual quality, then scores generated images via MLLM confidence probabilities rather than holistic scalar ratings. AlphaGRPO achieves consistent improvements across multiple downstream benchmarks — including a 5.8% gain over Bagel on TIIF-Bench when augmented with inference-time self-reflective refinement, and a 0.52 overall score improvement on the GEdit editing benchmark — establishing that RL can activate dormant reasoning primitives in pretrained unified models, though gains remain concentrated on problems within the base model's capability range, with the hardest compositional tasks showing diminishing returns from additional refinement alone.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Unified Multimodal Models Have Latent Reasoning Capabilities That Standard Training Cannot Activate

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **given that unified multimodal models (UMMs) already acquire extensive visual understanding and generation primitives through large-scale pretraining, how can we activate and enhance their latent reasoning capabilities for tasks like self-reflective refinement and reasoning-driven generation?** The paper argues that pretrained UMMs possess the necessary "dormant" capabilities — the visual knowledge, reasoning patterns, and generation skills — but standard training paradigms (next-token prediction for the autoregressive text component, flow matching for the diffusion component) do not elicit them in ways that enable sophisticated cognitive workflows like error diagnosis, correction, or intent inference.

This gap matters because of what UMMs structurally *promise* but cannot yet deliver. Unlike specialized text-to-image models (e.g., FLUX, SD3) or separate vision-language-understanding systems, UMMs like Bagel integrate understanding and generation within a single backbone, processing interleaved multimodal inputs and outputs. This architectural unification theoretically enables complex, closed-loop cognitive workflows **within a single end-to-end model**: the model can perceive an image, identify misalignments with a user's intent, reason about what corrections are needed, and then generate a refined output — all without routing through external modules or hand-crafted pipelines. As the paper states in Section 1:

> "Crucially, this structural unification endows them with the potential to orchestrate complex cognitive workflows within a single end-to-end model, encompassing reasoning, execution, self-reflection, and refinement."

However, pretrained UMMs do not spontaneously exhibit these behaviors. When asked to verify whether a generated image contains errors (Section 2, Figure 2), the model suffers from a **confirmation bias** — it frequently asserts that obvious mistakes are correct, stating that "the generated image effectively fulfills the user's original intent." This is not a capability gap (the model *can* identify the error when explicitly instructed to find mistakes rather than verify correctness) but a behavioral one: the model defaults to accepting its own outputs as correct rather than scrutinizing them. Activating the latent scrutiny capability requires shifting the model's behavior — a reinforcement learning problem.

The practical implications are substantial. If UMMs can be trained to refine their own outputs autonomously, they become more robust to the long-tail failure cases that plague current text-to-image systems (miscounted objects, misbound attributes, incorrect spatial relationships). If they can reason about user intent before generating — inferring that "a coffee that looks appealing" means "with steam rising, in warm lighting, with crema visible" — they bridge the gap between under-specified natural language prompts and the precise visual specifications generation models need. And critically, if these capabilities can be activated without an expensive cold-start supervised fine-tuning stage (which requires curating high-quality reasoning trajectories, often by distilling from proprietary models like GPT-4), the approach becomes significantly more accessible and scalable.

### Conflicting Pressures in Prior Approaches: The Cold-Start Trap and the Verifier Bottleneck

The paper is motivated by two distinct limitations in how prior work has approached multimodal generation RL, which together form a catch-22 that AlphaGRPO is designed to resolve.

**The cold-start supervised fine-tuning dependency.** Recent works that enable complex tasks like reasoning text-to-image or self-reflective refinement in UMMs (Deng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b; Huang et al., 2025b) share a common pipeline: they first use a proprietary, more capable model (e.g., GPT-4) to synthesize high-quality reasoning trajectories or correction examples, then perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on this synthetic data, and only then apply reinforcement learning. The paper explicitly critiques this paradigm:

> "Although effective, this paradigm inevitably introduces an additional cold-start SFT stage, implying that the performance gains might stem from the distillation of the stronger teacher models."

The concern is not merely methodological cleanliness but attribution: if a model's improvements come from distilling a GPT-4 teacher's behaviors, then the method is fundamentally bounded by the teacher's capabilities and requires access to that teacher. This makes the approach expensive, dependent on proprietary systems, and difficult to disentangle — are we improving the student model's intrinsic capabilities, or just copying the teacher's? The paper's core wager is that **pretrained UMMs already possess the necessary primitives** and that RL alone, without SFT, can activate them. This is a strong and falsifiable claim: if RL without cold-start SFT fails to improve downstream performance, the dormant-capability hypothesis is wrong.

**The reward model reliability problem for open-world generation.** Even if one accepts that RL can activate latent capabilities, applying GRPO to multimodal generation requires a reward function that produces stable, discriminative signals for diverse real-world prompts. The paper identifies a fundamental tension in how prior work has approached this (Section 2, Figure 3).

On one side are **holistic scalar reward models**, typified by VIEScore (Ku et al., 2024) and human preference models like PickScore (Kirstain et al., 2023). These assign a single numeric score (0-1 or 0-10) to an image-prompt pair. The paper's pilot study reveals a critical failure mode: when presented with two images for the prompt "A tree in front partially hides a bench behind it" — one correctly showing the tree in front of the bench, the other incorrectly showing the tree behind — VIEScore assigns an identical score of 0.848 to both. The scalar reward literally cannot tell the difference between a correct and incorrect spatial relationship. The paper attributes this to the "black box" nature of holistic scoring, which "smooths over semantic discrepancies" rather than probing specific attributes. For GRPO, which computes advantages from group score variance, this lack of discriminability is fatal — if correct and incorrect outputs receive similar rewards, the advantage signal collapses, and the policy receives no meaningful gradient.

On the other side are **specialized reward models** (UnifiedReward, HPSv3) that fine-tune MLLMs on human preference datasets to produce better-aligned scores. These improve alignment accuracy but introduce a different problem: fine-tuning shifts the MLLM's distribution toward the limited domain of the preference dataset, "implicitly narrows the MLLM's capacity to handle open-world samples" (Section 1). In other words, the reward model becomes a specialist that performs well on in-distribution prompts but may produce unreliable or miscalibrated scores on the diverse, tail-heavy distribution of real user prompts. Since AlphaGRPO's goal is to improve performance on open-world downstream benchmarks (not just an in-distribution validation set), a narrow reward model risks **reward overfitting** — the policy learns to optimize the reward model's idiosyncratic preferences rather than genuine visual quality — and fails to generalize.

The paper cites evidence for this overfitting risk directly: "current visual generation RL often overlooks this, chasing high scores on training-aligned metrics" (Section 1), and the ablation results in Table 3 confirm that both specialized MLLM reward models (HPSv3, UnifiedReward) and holistic scalar scoring (VIEScore) can actually *degrade* performance on downstream benchmarks compared to the Bagel baseline, even as in-distribution training metrics improve. DVReward is designed as a third path: retain the open-world understanding of a general MLLM while extracting discriminative, interpretable signals through structured decomposition rather than scalar prediction.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short: A Detailed Diagnosis

The paper's pilot study (Section 2) provides empirical grounding for why prior approaches fail at both ends of the pipeline — the model's behavior during generation and the verifier's behavior during evaluation.

**The confirmation bias in verification mode.** Figure 2 presents a stark contrast between two prompt modes given to the Bagel model when shown a generated image containing an error (the shadow of scissors cast on a wall is in the wrong position relative to the light source). When asked to **verify** ("judge whether mistakes appear"), the model confidently declares the image correct, asserting it "effectively fulfills the user's original intent" and "accurately depicts a hand holding scissors, casting a shadow on a wall." This is not a perceptual failure — the model can see the image. It is a **behavioral failure**: the model defaults to confirmation, a well-documented phenomenon in LLMs (the paper cites Huang et al., 2023, which showed that "large language models cannot self-correct reasoning yet").

When the prompt is changed to **reflection mode** ("the image contains mistakes, find them"), the same model on the same image succeeds: "The image has issues with the shadow's position and shape, which are inconsistent with the light source and the expected shadow pattern." This demonstrates that the visual understanding capability exists — the model can perceive the error and articulate it — but the default behavior suppresses its use. The paper's insight is that **explicit error-seeking instructions** break the confirmation loop, "maximizing the activation of UMMs' intrinsic visual understanding" (Section 2). This is what makes self-reflective refinement a reinforcement learning problem rather than a prompt engineering one: the model needs to learn to *automatically* enter the reflective, error-seeking mode without being explicitly told that errors exist. The Self-Reflective Refinement task (Section 4.1) operationalizes this by training the model to generate reasoning text that diagnoses errors in its own previous output before generating a corrected image.

**The discriminability gap in scalar rewards.** Figure 3's comparison is more than a single anecdote — it reveals a systematic failure mode of holistic scoring. The mechanism is instructive: when asked for an abstract quality score (0-10), the MLLM maps the complex, multi-faceted comparison between the image and prompt into a single scalar. This mapping is inherently lossy — it compresses the judgment about spatial relationships, object attributes, composition, and style into one number. Minor errors in a single dimension (like spatial placement) get averaged into the overall score alongside correct dimensions (like object existence, color, and texture), resulting in near-identical scores for images with critically different semantic correctness.

The paper's alternative — **question-based scoring via token logits** — avoids this compression. Instead of asking "rate the image quality," it asks "Does the tree partially hide the bench?" and extracts the model's confidence in "Yes" versus "No" from the token probability distribution: $\text{score} = P(\text{"Yes"}) / (P(\text{"Yes"}) + P(\text{"No"}))$. This produces a highly discriminative signal (0.592 for the incorrect image vs. 0.914 for the correct one) because it directly probes the specific semantic dimension of interest. The MLLM's internal representation contains fine-grained information about spatial relationships — the problem is not the model's capability but the *query format* used to extract it. This finding generalizes: many real-world prompts contain multiple semantic constraints (a red ball on the left, a blue cube on the right, under soft lighting), and a holistic score will inevitably fail to isolate failures in individual constraints.

The connection to GRPO is direct. GRPO computes advantages as $\hat{A}_i = (r_i - \mu_r) / \sigma_r$, where $\mu_r$ and $\sigma_r$ are the group mean and standard deviation. If the reward function assigns near-identical scores to correct and incorrect outputs (as VIEScore does in Figure 3), $\sigma_r$ collapses, advantages approach zero, and the policy update provides no meaningful learning signal. Conversely, spurious variance from uncalibrated scoring can create noisy advantages that push the policy in random directions. DVReward's decomposition addresses both: by verifying specific constraints, it ensures that semantically meaningful differences translate into score differences, providing stable variance for GRPO's advantage computation.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions AlphaGRPO at the intersection of three previously disconnected lines of work, synthesizing insights from each to address the limitations of the others.

**Relative to prior unified multimodal RL work.** Previous attempts to apply RL to UMMs (Mao et al., 2025; Nie et al., 2025) operated on pure autoregressive models and relied on task-specific reward designs — for example, a dedicated reward for object counting, another for spatial reasoning. AlphaGRPO is, by the authors' claim, "the first comprehensive study on unified GRPO training for AR-Diffusion UMMs" (Section 6). The key distinction is the **unified trajectory formulation** (Section 4.1): whereas pure AR models generate discrete image tokens sequentially, AR-Diffusion models like Bagel generate discrete reasoning text autoregressively, then feed that text as conditioning into a continuous diffusion process for image generation. The GRPO objective must handle both components — clipping token-level probability ratios for the AR text and clipping trajectory-level density ratios for the diffusion flow — under a shared advantage signal computed from the final image reward. This is technically non-trivial because the two components have fundamentally different policy parameterizations (discrete categorical distributions vs. continuous Gaussian transition distributions) and different KL regularization forms (the standard Schulman estimator for text vs. a closed-form weighted L2 distance between velocity fields for the flow model).

**Relative to vision generation RL.** Recent work on applying GRPO to diffusion models (FlowGRPO, DanceGRPO; Liu et al., 2025a; Xue et al., 2025) focuses exclusively on the diffusion component, optimizing flow-matching models for specific aesthetic or alignment objectives. These methods operate on pure text-to-image models (no unified understanding-generation architecture) and typically use scalar reward models (PickScore, HPSv2, aesthetic predictors). AlphaGRPO extends this line by (1) jointly optimizing the reasoning text and visual generation in a single model, (2) introducing self-reflective refinement as a training task that requires the model to perceive errors in its own previous output, and (3) replacing scalar rewards with decompositional verification. The paper demonstrates that the unified optimization yields generalization benefits not observed in pure generation RL: training on self-reflective refinement improves downstream editing performance (Table 2) despite no editing data in training, suggesting that the learned self-critique capability transfers across tasks.

**Relative to reward model design.** The paper explicitly contrasts DVReward against two reward model paradigms (Section 2, Section 6). Against **fine-tuned MLLM reward models** (HPSv3, UnifiedReward), it argues that domain-specific fine-tuning compromises the open-world evaluation capability that makes MLLMs valuable as verifiers in the first place. Against **holistic scalar scoring** (VIEScore), it argues that abstract quality ratings lack the discriminability to distinguish semantically meaningful errors. DVReward occupies a middle ground: it uses a *general, unfine-tuned MLLM* (Qwen3VL-30B-A3B) to preserve open-world knowledge, but queries it through *structured, atomic verification questions* to extract discriminative signals. The paper also positions this relative to Davidsonian Scene Graph (DSG; Cho et al., 2023), which similarly decomposes evaluation into atomic questions for T2I evaluation — DVReward extends this idea by (1) using LLM-based decomposition rather than rule-based templates for open-ended prompt coverage, (2) incorporating perceptual quality questions alongside semantic alignment, and (3) using continuous confidence scores rather than binary judgments.

**The false-positive rectification innovation.** A subtle but important positioning claim concerns the challenge of training self-reflective refinement. The paper introduces **False-Positive Rectification (FPR)** (Section 4.1) to address a specific failure mode in GRPO training for refinement tasks: the group advantage normalization can assign a positive advantage to a refinement that actually produces a *worse* image than the initial input, simply because it is better than the worst-performing members of its group. FPR detects when $r(z_{\text{refined}}) \leq r(z_{\text{init}})$ and assigns these trajectories the group minimum reward, ensuring they receive negative advantages. This is a principled solution to a problem that prior self-improvement RL methods either ignored or addressed through ad-hoc filtering — it guarantees that the policy is never rewarded for degrading output quality.

**The broader narrative positioning.** At the highest level, the paper positions AlphaGRPO as a demonstration that **pretrained UMMs possess dormant reasoning capabilities that RL can activate without external distillation**. This is both a methodological claim (cold-start SFT is unnecessary) and an empirical finding (RL-trained models outperform SFT-distillation baselines on downstream benchmarks). The paper explicitly states this ambition in Section 1:

> "In contrast, we argue that since unified models already acquire fundamental primitives and implicit reasoning-related data through large-scale pretraining, it is possible to activate and enhance these dormant capabilities using RL without the cold-start stage."

This framing places the paper in the lineage of works showing that LLMs possess latent capabilities elicitable through specific prompting or training paradigms (e.g., chain-of-thought reasoning through few-shot prompting, theory-of-mind through structured tasks), extended to the multimodal generation domain. The null hypothesis — that pretrained UMMs do not contain sufficient reasoning primitives, and that cold-start SFT genuinely injects new capabilities rather than distilling them — would predict that AlphaGRPO without SFT should fail to improve downstream benchmarks. The paper's positive results (Tables 1, 2) therefore serve as evidence for the dormant-capability hypothesis, though the paper is careful to acknowledge boundary conditions (the base model's occasional generation of "noisy or blurry artifacts" and "unexpected reasoning patterns" at low resolution, noted in Appendix A.1).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader orientation (approachable technical breakdown)

AlphaGRPO is a reinforcement learning training framework that teaches a unified multimodal model (one that both understands images and generates them) to produce better images by learning to reason about what it's doing and to fix its own mistakes. The problem it solves is that pretrained unified models possess the visual understanding necessary to detect and correct generation errors — but their default behavior suppresses these capabilities, and standard training provides no mechanism to reward self-critique and refinement.

### 3.2 Big-picture architecture (diagram in words)

The system has five major components that interact in a training loop:

1. **The Unified Multimodal Model (UMM)** — the Bagel backbone that is being trained. It accepts a user prompt and produces a hybrid trajectory: first it generates reasoning text autoregressively (discrete tokens), then it generates an image through a diffusion process (continuous latents), with the text serving as conditioning for the visual generation.

2. **The Training Dataset** — a curated set of 19,500 prompts synthesized via a "Primitive-to-Prompt" strategy, covering 39 compositional task types (spatial reasoning, attribute binding, counting, etc.) across three difficulty tiers, plus 1,024 test prompts.

3. **The Decompositional Verifiable Reward (DVReward) Mechanism** — a two-stage reward pipeline: first, an LLM decomposer pre-processes each prompt offline into atomic verification questions (semantic questions covering 10 dimensions like entity existence, attributes, spatial relationships; quality questions covering 8 aspects like geometry, texture, lighting); second, during training, a general MLLM verifier (Qwen3VL-30B-A3B, unfine-tuned) scores each generated image against these questions via confidence probabilities extracted from "Yes"/"No" token logits, producing the final reward as the geometric mean of semantic and quality scores.

4. **The GRPO Training Engine** — the reinforcement learning algorithm that samples groups of trajectories from the current policy, computes per-image rewards via DVReward, normalizes them into advantages, and updates both the autoregressive text policy and the diffusion flow policy under a unified objective with a shared advantage signal.

5. **The Inference-Time Self-Reflective Refinement Module** — a deployment-time procedure (not used during training) where the trained model takes its own initial generation, diagnoses misalignments through reasoning text, and produces a refined image, further improving quality beyond what single-pass generation achieves.

Information flows through the training loop as follows: a batch of prompts is sampled from the training set → for each prompt, the UMM generates a group of trajectories (text reasoning + image latent paths) → images are decoded and sent asynchronously to DVReward verifier servers → rewards are computed and normalized into advantages → both the text and flow policies are updated via the unified GRPO objective → repeat.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the deep dive

- **First**, the unified trajectory formulation — how the paper conceptualizes multimodal generation as a single continuous process spanning autoregressive text and diffusion visuals, and why this enables joint optimization that pure AR or pure diffusion approaches cannot achieve.
- **Second**, the unified GRPO optimization objective — how the standard GRPO formulation splits into separate but coupled objectives for the autoregressive and flow components, including the false-positive rectification mechanism for self-reflective refinement.
- **Third**, the Decompositional Verifiable Reward (DVReward) in full detail — the decomposition process (what gets decomposed, into what, and why), the confidence scoring mechanism (how token logits replace scalar ratings), and the overall reward computation.
- **Fourth**, the training data construction pipeline — the Primitive-to-Prompt strategy, the taxonomy of compositional tasks, the difficulty tiering, and the offline question pre-generation.
- **Fifth**, the training configuration and infrastructure — hyperparameters, distributed training setup, and the efficiency optimizations that make DVReward's multi-question verification practical.

### 3.4 Detailed, sentence-based technical breakdown

This is primarily a **systems and methods paper** whose core idea is that pretrained AR-Diffusion unified multimodal models possess dormant reasoning and self-critique capabilities that can be activated through reinforcement learning, provided that the reward signal is discriminative enough to distinguish semantically meaningful differences between generated images.

#### The Unified Trajectory Formulation

The paper's first technical contribution is a conceptual reframing of multimodal generation as a **single unified generative process** rather than two separate stages. In standard usage, a UMM like Bagel first produces text tokens autoregressively (the "thinking" or reasoning), then generates an image through diffusion conditioned on that text. The paper formalizes this as a **hybrid trajectory**:

$$\tau = (y, z_1 \rightarrow z_0)$$

where $y$ denotes the sequence of discrete reasoning text tokens produced by the autoregressive component, and $z_1 \rightarrow z_0$ denotes the continuous diffusion path from pure noise $z_1$ to the clean image latent $z_0$, generated by the flow-matching component.

**What it computes:** this notation represents the complete generative trace of the model for a single input prompt: a sequence of reasoning tokens followed by a sequence of progressively denoised latent representations. The trajectory is "hybrid" because it spans two fundamentally different generative modalities — discrete symbolic tokens and continuous real-valued vectors — within a single end-to-end computation.

**Why this form:** the unification is not merely notational. It creates a formal object $\tau$ that can be treated as a single sample from the model's joint distribution over reasoning and images. This is what enables the GRPO advantage signal — computed from the final image reward — to be propagated backward through both the diffusion path and the autoregressive sequence in a single optimization step. If text and image were treated as separate trajectories with separate rewards, the model could not learn that better reasoning text leads to better images, because there would be no gradient path connecting the image reward to the text policy. The unified trajectory provides that path: the reward $r(z_0)$ depends on the final image, which depends on the entire diffusion trajectory, which depends on the conditioning text $y$, which depends on the text policy. All these dependencies are differentiable through the chain.

The paper distinguishes two instantiations of this trajectory, corresponding to the two training tasks:

- **Reasoning T2I (RT2I):** the text $y$ serves as a "cognitive bridge" — it plans spatial layouts, extracts relevant world knowledge, and resolves implicit user intents before the visual synthesis begins. For example, given "a cyberpunk-style photo," the reasoning text might specify neon lighting colors, architectural elements, and color palette choices that ground the abstract style descriptor in concrete visual attributes.

- **Self-Reflective Refinement (SRR):** the text $y$ serves as a diagnostic and corrective plan — it identifies errors in a previously generated image and specifies corrections. The model receives both the original user prompt and its own initial (incorrect) image as input, and its reasoning text describes what went wrong before the diffusion process generates a corrected image.

Despite their semantic differences, both tasks share an identical structural objective: maximize the quality of the final image $z_0$ as measured by DVReward, conditioned on the intermediate reasoning $y$. This allows the same GRPO machinery to train both tasks with only the input format differing.

---

#### The Unified GRPO Optimization Objective

The paper adapts the standard GRPO algorithm (Shao et al., 2024) to operate on the hybrid trajectory described above. Standard GRPO, designed for pure language models, optimizes a discrete token-level policy using group-relative advantages. AlphaGRPO must simultaneously optimize two fundamentally different policies — a discrete autoregressive text policy and a continuous diffusion flow policy — under a single reward signal.

**The sampling procedure.** Given a training context $C$ (the user prompt, and for SRR, also the initial generated image), the model samples a group of $G$ trajectories $\{\tau_i\}_{i=1}^{G}$ where each $\tau_i = (y_i, z_i)$. The reward $r(z_i)$ is computed solely from the final generated image $z_i$ using DVReward (the reasoning text $y_i$ receives no direct reward — its quality is judged only through the image it produces). The advantages $\hat{A}_i$ are computed via group normalization:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r}$$

where $r_i = r(z_i)$ is the scalar reward for trajectory $i$, $\mu_r$ is the mean reward across the group of $G$ trajectories, and $\sigma_r$ is the standard deviation across the group.

**What it computes:** for each trajectory in the group, this subtracts the group-average reward and divides by the group standard deviation, producing a signed, standardized score. Trajectories with above-average rewards receive positive advantages; those with below-average rewards receive negative advantages. The magnitude reflects how many standard deviations above or below the mean the trajectory falls.

**Why this form:** group-relative normalization eliminates the need for a learned value function (critic), which is the key innovation of GRPO over PPO. It also provides automatic reward scale invariance — the advantages adapt to the absolute magnitude of the reward function, so hyperparameters like the clipping threshold $\epsilon$ do not need to be tuned per reward model. Critically, the normalization is computed per-group, not globally, meaning the model receives a relative signal even if all trajectories in a particular batch are poor (the best one still gets positive advantage) or all are excellent (the worst still gets negative advantage). This is particularly important for multimodal generation, where reward magnitudes can vary dramatically across prompts of different complexity.

**The unified objective.** Crucially, since the reasoning text $y_i$ is the causal precursor to the image $z_i$, the paper propagates the shared advantage $\hat{A}_i$ to update both the text and image policies simultaneously. The unified objective is:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\text{old}}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^{G} \left( \lambda J^{(i)}_{\text{AR}} + J^{(i)}_{\text{Flow}} \right) \right]$$

where $\lambda = 0.2$ is a balancing weight, and $J^{(i)}_{\text{AR}}$ and $J^{(i)}_{\text{Flow}}$ are the regularized PPO objectives for the autoregressive text and diffusion flow components respectively.

**What it computes:** this is the per-trajectory contribution to the policy gradient, summed over the group of $G$ trajectories and averaged. The autoregressive component's contribution is weighted by $\lambda = 0.2$, meaning the flow component dominates the update — the model prioritizes improving image quality over improving reasoning text quality, with text serving primarily as a means to the image-generation end.

**Why this form:** the weighting acknowledges an asymmetry: the flow component directly controls pixel-level image quality, while the AR component influences it only indirectly through conditioning. Weighting the AR objective too heavily could cause the model to over-optimize reasoning text at the expense of image quality — the text might become verbose or self-consistent without actually improving the image. The ratio $\lambda = 0.2$ was likely determined empirically (the paper does not ablate this specific value, though it ablates KL coefficients), and represents a conservative bet: let the flow component do most of the heavy lifting, with text providing a gentle nudge.

**The autoregressive PPO objective:**

$$J^{(i)}_{\text{AR}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{AR}}(y_i, \hat{A}_i) - \beta_{\text{AR}} D^{\text{AR}}_{\text{KL}}$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{AR}}$ applies standard PPO clipping to token-level probability ratios, $\beta_{\text{AR}} = 0$ is the KL penalty coefficient (see below for the ablation finding), and $D^{\text{AR}}_{\text{KL}}$ is the KL divergence between the current and reference text policies, approximated via the standard Schulman estimator:

$$D^{\text{AR}}_{\text{KL}} \approx \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}} - \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}}{\pi_{\theta}} - 1$$

**What it computes:** for the text tokens, this is the standard GRPO objective for language modeling: a clipped surrogate loss that prevents the policy from changing too much per update, minus a KL penalty term that keeps the policy close to a reference distribution (typically the pretrained model's distribution). The clipping is applied to the probability ratio $\rho_{i,t} = \pi_{\theta}(y_{i,t} | q, y_{i,<t}) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} | q, y_{i,<t})$ for each token $t$ in the sequence $y_i$, with the standard PPO formulation: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{clip}} = \min(\rho \hat{A}, \text{clip}(\rho, 1 \pm \epsilon) \hat{A})$.

**Why the KL penalty coefficient is zero:** the paper's KL ablation (Appendix A.3, Table 13) shows that adding KL regularization with $\beta_{\text{AR}} = \beta_{\text{Flow}} = 0.001$ does not provide consistent gains — it improves TIIF-Bench Short slightly but degrades TIIF-Bench Long and GenEval overall. The authors set both KL coefficients to zero in the default configuration. This is an interesting finding because KL regularization is standard in RLHF to prevent reward hacking; its ineffectiveness here may indicate that the group-relative advantage normalization already provides sufficient constraint (the policy cannot drift far because advantages are zero-mean within each batch), or that the DVReward signal is well-calibrated enough that over-optimization is not the primary failure mode at the studied training horizons (only 380 steps).

**The flow PPO objective:**

$$J^{(i)}_{\text{Flow}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{Flow}}(z_i, \hat{A}_i) - \beta_{\text{Flow}} D^{\text{Flow}}_{\text{KL}}$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Flow}}$ applies the same PPO clipping strategy to **trajectory-level density ratios** rather than token-level probability ratios. The key technical difference from the AR component lies in how the policy, the probability ratio, and the KL divergence are defined for the continuous diffusion process.

**The diffusion policy formulation.** To apply GRPO (which requires sampling from a stochastic policy with computable log-probabilities), the deterministic flow-matching process must be converted into a stochastic process. The paper follows the approach of FlowGRPO (Liu et al., 2025a) and DanceGRPO (Xue et al., 2025), using **Euler-Maruyama discretization** of the probability flow ODE into an SDE. At each diffusion timestep $t$, the latent state $z_t$ is updated to $z_{t-\Delta t}$ via:

$$z_{t-\Delta t} = z_t + \underbrace{\left( v_{\theta}(z_t) - \frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t(z_t) \right) \Delta t}_{\mu_{\theta}(z_t)} + \sigma_t \sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon$$

where $v_{\theta}(z_t)$ is the learned velocity field (the deterministic flow), $\sigma_t = a \sqrt{\frac{t}{1-t}}$ with noise level $a = 0.7$, $\nabla \log p_t(z_t)$ is the score function (gradient of the log-density), $\Delta t$ is the step size, and $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ is standard Gaussian noise.

**What it computes:** this updates the latent representation one diffusion step by: (1) following the deterministic flow $v_{\theta}$ downward (the velocity field points toward cleaner images), (2) subtracting a score correction term $\frac{\sigma_t^2}{2} \nabla \log p_t$ that pushes the latent toward higher-density regions of the current noisy distribution (preventing drift into low-probability regions that produce artifacts), and (3) injecting Gaussian noise with variance $\sigma_t^2 \Delta t$ to enable stochastic exploration. The step size $\Delta t$ and noise schedule $\sigma_t$ control the exploration-exploitation tradeoff — larger $\sigma_t$ produces more diverse samples but may reduce image quality.

**Why this form:** the Euler-Maruyama discretization is the simplest scheme that converts a deterministic ODE into a stochastic process while preserving the correct limiting distribution. It explicitly defines the transition distribution as Gaussian:

$$\pi_{\theta}(z_{t-\Delta t} | z_t) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\theta}(z_t), \sigma_t^2 \Delta t \, I)$$

This Gaussian form is essential because it gives closed-form expressions for both the log-probability (needed for the probability ratio in GRPO) and the KL divergence (needed for regularization). Alternative discretization schemes (e.g., the deterministic Euler method) would not produce a stochastic policy and thus could not be optimized via GRPO.

**The flow probability ratio.** Given the Gaussian transition distribution, the probability ratio between the current policy $\theta$ and the old policy $\theta_{\text{old}}$ at timestep $t$ is:

$$\rho_t = \frac{\mathcal{N}(z_{t-\Delta t}; \mu_{\theta}(z_t), \Sigma_t)}{\mathcal{N}(z_{t-\Delta t}; \mu_{\text{old}}(z_t), \Sigma_t)}$$

where $\Sigma_t = \sigma_t^2 \Delta t \, I$ is the shared covariance (only the mean differs between old and new policies). This ratio has a closed form and can be computed efficiently without sampling.

**The flow KL divergence.** A critical advantage of the Gaussian formulation is that the KL divergence between the current and reference flow policies has a closed form:

$$D^{\text{Flow}}_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta} \| \pi_{\text{ref}}) = w_t \| v_{\theta}(z_t) - v_{\text{ref}}(z_t) \|^2$$

where the weighting term is:

$$w_t = \frac{\Delta t}{2} \left( \frac{\sigma_t (1-t)}{2t} + \frac{1}{\sigma_t} \right)^2$$

**What it computes:** the KL divergence is simply a weighted L2 distance between the velocity field predictions of the current and reference models at the current latent state. The weight $w_t$ depends on the timestep and noise level — it is larger at intermediate noise levels where small changes in velocity have greater impact on the trajectory, and smaller at very high or very low noise levels where the trajectory is less sensitive to velocity perturbations.

**Why this form:** closed-form KL computation is a major practical benefit. In standard RLHF for language models, the KL divergence must be estimated via sampling (the Schulman estimator), which introduces variance. The exact L2-distance form for flow models eliminates this variance, providing a more stable regularization signal. It also enables efficient computation: the reference model $v_{\text{ref}}$ can be run once per latent state, and the KL penalty is simply a scaled L2 loss that can be added directly to the policy gradient.

**The overall update semantics.** During a single GRPO training step: the model samples $G = 14$ trajectories per prompt, decodes the final images, sends them to the DVReward verifier for scoring, computes advantages from the group rewards, clips the probability ratios for both the AR and flow components, subtracts any KL penalties (zero in the default configuration), and updates both components' parameters via gradient descent. The AR component is updated on all generated text tokens; the flow component is updated on a stochastically sampled subset of diffusion timesteps (5 steps out of the first 15 for SRR training, or the first 10 steps converted to SDE sampling for RT2I training — see training settings below) to reduce memory and computation.

#### The Self-Reflective Refinement Training Configuration and False-Positive Rectification

The self-reflective refinement (SRR) task introduces two additional technical elements beyond the basic RT2I setup: the training data format (how the model learns to refine rather than generate from scratch) and the false-positive rectification mechanism (how the training signal avoids rewarding degraded refinements).

**SRR training data construction.** During SRR training, each training example consists of a user prompt and an initial generated image. The paper adopts a specific procedure to create challenging training pairs:

- For each training prompt, the model first generates a group of images using the standard RT2I procedure (40 diffusion steps).
- The initial image for the SRR task is **fixed as the sample with the lowest DVReward score** in the group. This ensures that the model trains on genuinely flawed images that require correction, rather than on images that are already mostly correct and need only minor touch-ups.
- The model then receives this low-quality image as input alongside the original prompt and is tasked with generating reasoning text that diagnoses the errors followed by a refined image.

**Why this choice:** training on the worst sample maximizes the learning signal — the model must diagnose and correct genuine, clear errors rather than subtle ones. If the initial image were randomly selected, many training examples would involve near-correct images requiring only minor adjustments, providing weak gradient signals for learning the diagnostic reasoning skill. Using the worst sample also prevents distribution shift at inference time (where users would apply SRR specifically to fix bad generations) — the training distribution of initial images is deliberately skewed toward low quality to match the expected deployment distribution.

**SRR sampling configuration.** For SRR, the paper uses 40 diffusion steps for both the initial generation (producing the low-quality input image) and the subsequent self-reflective refinement (generating the corrected image). From the refinement's diffusion process, 5 timesteps are stochastically sampled from the first 15 steps for GRPO training — this means the gradient update only flows through a subset of the diffusion trajectory, reducing memory and computational cost while still providing sufficient signal (the early steps are where the high-level composition and semantic structure are determined).

**The false-positive rectification mechanism.** This is a critical technical innovation specific to the self-reflective refinement task. In standard GRPO, advantages are computed by normalizing group rewards. However, for refinement tasks, this can produce a pathological failure: a trajectory that produces a refined image **worse** than the initial image can receive a positive advantage if it is better than some other trajectories in the group. For example, if a group contains three refinement attempts with rewards $[0.3, 0.2, 0.1]$, and the initial image had reward $0.4$, the trajectory with reward $0.3$ would receive a positive advantage (above the group mean of $0.2$) despite being worse than the starting point. This would incorrectly reward the model for degrading image quality, potentially causing the refinement capability to collapse.

**The FPR procedure.** The paper introduces False-Positive Rectification to eliminate this:

> "FPR enforces a validity constraint by assigning the group minimum reward to the trajectories that fail to improve ($r(z_i) \leq r(z_{\text{init}})$). This operation guarantees that all ineffective refinement attempts result in negative advantages, strictly suppressing the likelihood of model degradation."

Concretely: for any trajectory in the group where the refined image $z_i$ has a reward less than or equal to the initial image $z_{\text{init}}$ (i.e., $r(z_i) \leq r(z_{\text{init}})$), the reward is replaced with $r_{\min} = \min_j r(z_j)$, the worst reward in the group. This forces the advantage for that trajectory to be negative (since it is now at or below the group minimum, which must be below the mean), ensuring the policy is never rewarded for producing worse outputs than the starting point.

**What it computes:** a hard filter on the reward signal that detects "regression" trajectories and forces them to the bottom of the group ranking regardless of how they compare to other regression trajectories. The operation is not differentiable and does not affect the gradient computation except through the reward value used in advantage normalization.

**Why this form:** the alternative — simply dropping the trajectory from the group — would reduce the effective group size and lose the negative signal entirely, potentially causing the model to learn nothing about why refinements fail. The alternative — using the initial image's reward directly as the reward floor — would create a discontinuous reward function that might be harder to optimize. The group-minimum assignment is a compromise that preserves the ranking signal (all regression trajectories are equally bad and worse than all improvement trajectories) while maintaining the group size for stable advantage normalization.

**An important implementation detail:** the paper notes (Appendix A.2) that SRR training uses **single-turn conversations** rather than multi-turn conversations. In a multi-turn format, the model would see the full history of the initial generation (its own previous reasoning text and the resulting image) in the context. The single-turn format simplifies training and prevents the model from learning spurious correlations between its initial reasoning errors and subsequent corrections. However, it also means the model must infer what went wrong solely from the visual evidence in the initial image, without access to its own original reasoning process — a harder task that may produce more robust error-diagnosis capabilities.

#### Decompositional Verifiable Reward (DVReward) — Overview

DVReward is the paper's solution to the reward reliability problem identified in the pilot study (Figure 3): holistic scalar rewards lack discriminability, while fine-tuned MLLM reward models compromise open-world generalization. DVReward instead decomposes the evaluation process into three stages: (1) **offline decomposition** of each user prompt into atomic verification questions covering semantic alignment and perceptual quality, (2) **online verification** of each generated image against these questions using an unfine-tuned MLLM's confidence scores, and (3) **reward aggregation** into a single scalar via geometric mean.

The key insight is that **MLLMs possess fine-grained visual understanding but lose it when forced to produce a single scalar rating**. By querying the MLLM with specific, verifiable questions and extracting confidence from token logits rather than asking for a direct rating, DVReward preserves the MLLM's discriminative capabilities while producing a scalar reward suitable for GRPO.

---

#### DVReward — Request Decomposition

The decomposition stage is performed **offline, once per training prompt**, before any GRPO training begins. This is computationally feasible because the training set is fixed (19,500 prompts), but it means the decomposition quality is frozen — if the decomposer misses an important semantic dimension, DVReward will not penalize errors in that dimension, creating a potential blind spot that the policy could exploit.

**The decomposer.** The paper uses Qwen3-235B-A22B, a large language model, as the decomposer. The decomposer receives a user prompt and generates two sets of atomic questions:

- **Semantic questions $Q_{\text{sem}}$** covering 10 dimensions: Existence (is X present?), Attribute (does X have property Y?), Environment (setting, weather, lighting), Spatial (relative positioning), Count (numerical quantity), Action (movement, activity), Style (artistic medium, visual genre), Text (spelling of rendered text), Negative (explicit absence verification), and Viewpoint (camera parameters, shot size). These dimensions are defined in detail in Table 16 (Appendix).

- **Quality questions $Q_{\text{qua}}$** covering 8 aspects: Geometry (structural integrity of inorganic objects), Anatomy (biological correctness of humans/animals), Texture (surface material fidelity), Coherence (object integrity — no merging, fusion, or detachment), Lighting (illumination consistency, shadow direction), Physics (physical plausibility — ground contact, gravity, motion), Legibility (readability of rendered text), and Aesthetics (overall visual appeal, freedom from artifacts). These dimensions are defined in Table 17 (Appendix).

**What it computes:** for a single input prompt (e.g., "A blue ship moves through the sky"), the decomposer produces a structured output containing 3 semantic questions — "Is there a ship?", "Is the ship blue?", "Is the ship moving through the sky?" — and 5 quality questions — e.g., "Is the ship geometrically coherent with a structurally plausible hull and superstructure?", "Is the ship clearly defined and visually distinct from the surrounding sky without fusion or blending artifacts?", "Does the ship exhibit a consistent blue coloration across its surface with realistic material variation, such as paint sheen or weathering?", "Does the ship's orientation and motion cues, such as implied speed or wake effects, suggest dynamic movement through the air?", "Is the lighting on the ship consistent with the surrounding sky environment, supporting its presence in aerial space?"

**The physical visual grounding requirement.** A distinctive feature of the decomposition (emphasized in Section 4.2) is that the decomposer is explicitly instructed to perform **physical visual grounding** — converting abstract, subjective adjectives into observable physical phenomena. The paper gives the example: instead of asking "Is the coffee hot?" (which is unverifiable from a static image), the decomposer generates "Is there steam rising from the cup?" (a visually verifiable proxy for heat). This grounding forces the questions to be answerable from visual evidence alone, making them suitable for MLLM verification. Without this constraint, the decomposer might generate questions that rely on non-visual reasoning (e.g., about intent, emotion, or temporal state), producing verification failures that are not the image generation model's fault.

**Distribution of question counts.** The paper does not constrain the number of generated questions, allowing the decomposer to produce as many atomic questions as needed to cover the prompt's semantic content. The resulting distribution (Figure 7 in Appendix A.2) shows a mode around 6-12 questions per prompt, with a long tail extending to 48+. Prompts with more than 50 questions are dropped from the training set — this filters out prompts that are likely too complex for reliable verification (or for which the decomposer produced degenerate, overly specific questions). The average across the training set is not reported numerically, but Figure 7's histogram suggests a central tendency in the 10-20 question range.

**Why this decomposition strategy rather than alternatives:** the paper's ablation (Section 5.3, Table 9) directly compares using all generated questions versus merging them into at most 10 core questions per sample using Qwen3-235B-A22B. The merged variant is competitive on TIIF-Bench but drops substantially on GenEval, particularly in Spatial (63.0 vs. 74.3) and Count (71.8 vs. 82.5) metrics. This supports the paper's claim that fine-grained atomic verification is necessary — merging multiple constraints into coarse questions weakens the reward signal for individual relationships and attributes, allowing errors in those dimensions to pass undetected. However, the ablation also reveals a practical tension: the full question set requires more computation per verification (more MLLM forward passes), making inference-time reward computation slower.

**Comparison to rule-based decomposition.** The paper references Davidsonian Scene Graph (DSG; Cho et al., 2023) as motivation but extends it in significant ways. DSG uses hand-crafted dependency templates to generate questions (e.g., for spatial relations, it generates a fixed pattern of questions about object locations). DVReward's LLM-based decomposition is more flexible — it can generate questions for arbitrary prompt types (including style descriptors, abstract concepts, and negative constraints) that would be difficult to template. The tradeoff is reduced control: the LLM decomposer might miss important dimensions or generate low-quality questions, and there is no mechanism to verify decomposition quality beyond the filtering step (dropping prompts with >50 questions).

**The question filtering process.** After generation, a filtering step is applied to "verify the evaluation validity of the generated questions" (Section 4.2). The paper does not describe this filtering process in detail — it is unclear whether it involves a second LLM call, heuristic rules, or manual inspection. This is a methodological gap: if the filtering is lenient, low-quality questions could introduce noise into the reward signal; if it is aggressive, it might systematically remove questions about difficult-to-verify attributes, creating blind spots.

#### DVReward — Confidence Scoring

Once the atomic questions are generated offline, the online verification stage scores each generated image $z$ against these questions during GRPO training. The verifier is Qwen3VL-30B-A3B, an open-source MLLM that activates approximately 3B parameters at inference time (it uses a Mixture-of-Experts architecture where only a subset of parameters is active per token, enabling high throughput).

**The confidence scoring mechanism.** For each question $s$ in the pre-generated sets $Q_{\text{sem}}$ and $Q_{\text{qua}}$, the verifier is prompted with the question and the generated image, and asked to answer "Yes" or "No" (e.g., "Does the tree partially hide the bench?"). Instead of taking the discretized binary answer (Yes=1, No=0), the paper extracts the continuous confidence score from the token logits:

$$v_k = \frac{P_{\text{Yes}}}{P_{\text{Yes}} + P_{\text{No}}}$$

where $P_{\text{Yes}}$ and $P_{\text{No}}$ are the probabilities assigned by the verifier to the "Yes" and "No" tokens respectively at the answer position.

**What it computes:** a value in $[0, 1]$ representing the verifier's confidence that the answer to question $s$ is "Yes". A score of 0.5 indicates maximal uncertainty (equal probability on Yes and No), a score near 1.0 indicates high confidence that the attribute is present/correct, and a score near 0.0 indicates high confidence that the attribute is absent/incorrect.

**Why this form rather than binary 0/1:** the paper ablates this choice directly (Section 5.3, Table 4), comparing confidence scoring against a hard binary score baseline. The confidence score achieves 79.5 on TIIF-Bench Long (vs. 78.9 for binary) and 85.1 on GenEval overall (vs. 84.0 for binary). The paper argues that "unlike the binary score that treats 'barely correct' and 'perfectly correct' identically, the confidence score preserves the model's uncertainty, offering a smoother gradient landscape for optimization." In other words, when the model is partially correct — say, an object has approximately the right color but slightly the wrong shade — the confidence score might be 0.72 rather than 1.0, providing a nuanced signal that the attribute needs improvement. A binary score would round this to 1.0, telling the policy the output is perfect and providing no gradient for further refinement.

**Why the probability ratio specifically.** The probability ratio is a standard technique for extracting calibrated confidence from language models — it has been used extensively in the calibration literature and is known to produce better-calibrated probabilities than raw token probabilities for classification tasks. The denominator $P_{\text{Yes}} + P_{\text{No}}$ normalizes out the probability mass assigned to other tokens (the verifier might assign some probability to tokens like "Yes," "yes", "YES", or even semantically equivalent tokens), focusing the score on the relative probability of the two answers. An alternative — using raw $P_{\text{Yes}}$ — would be sensitive to the verifier's overall uncertainty level (if the verifier is uncertain about the task format, it might assign low probability to both Yes and No, suppressing scores even for correct images).

**The risk of miscalibration.** The paper does not discuss whether Qwen3VL-30B-A3B's token logits are well-calibrated for this task. If the verifier tends to be overconfident (assigning near-1.0 or near-0.0 probabilities to most questions), the confidence scoring reduces to near-binary scoring, losing the granularity advantage identified in the ablation. If it is underconfident (assigning probabilities clustered around 0.5-0.8), the reward signal will have low variance, potentially causing the same discriminability problem that holistic scoring suffers from. The paper's positive results suggest the calibration is adequate for the studied benchmarks, but this is an assumption that may not transfer to other verifier models or prompt distributions.

**Verification efficiency.** A significant practical concern is that DVReward requires running the MLLM verifier once per question per generated image. With 10-20 questions per prompt, 14 images per group, and 32 prompts per training step, the raw number of verifier calls is $10 \times 14 \times 32 = 4480$ to $20 \times 14 \times 32 = 8960$ per step. The paper addresses this efficiency concern in Appendix A.4, describing several optimizations:

- **KV-cache reuse:** all verification calls for the same image share the system prompt and image prefix, allowing the verifier to compute image features once and reuse the KV-cache across questions, avoiding repeated image encoding and prefix processing.
- **Small per-question overhead:** each question adds approximately 20 prefill tokens (the question text) and one decoded token (Yes/No), making the marginal cost per question very low after the image is encoded.
- **High-throughput serving:** Qwen3VL-30B-A3B activates only about 3B parameters (MoE architecture) and achieves over 4K prefill tokens per second with more than 500 concurrent requests on a single A100 GPU.
- **Decentralized reward serving:** rather than sending all verification requests to a centralized server (which would create a cross-node communication bottleneck in multi-node training), each 8-GPU training node has its own reward server (1 GPU dedicated to verifier serving, 7 GPUs to training), keeping verification traffic local to each node.
- **Asynchronous scheduling:** verification requests are submitted asynchronously during rollout and collected just before the policy update, overlapping verification computation with subsequent rollouts and gradient computation.

The paper reports (Table 15) that with these optimizations, the "reward-waiting bubble time" — the time the training GPUs spend idle waiting for rewards — is reduced from 40.8 seconds (with centralized, synchronous serving) to $9.72 \times 10^{-6}$ seconds (with decentralized, asynchronous serving). This figure should be interpreted carefully: the near-zero wait time likely means that reward computation is fully overlapped with other training operations, not that verification itself is instant. The actual verification latency per step is not reported but is effectively hidden from the training pipeline's critical path.

#### DVReward — Reward Aggregation

The final reward $r(z)$ for a generated image $z$ is computed as the geometric mean of the average semantic and quality scores:

$$r(z) = \sqrt{\bar{v}_{\text{sem}} \cdot \bar{v}_{\text{qua}}}$$

where:

$$\bar{v}_{\text{sem}} = \frac{1}{|Q_{\text{sem}}|} \sum_{s \in Q_{\text{sem}}} V(z, s)$$

is the arithmetic mean of the verifier's confidence scores across all semantic questions, and $\bar{v}_{\text{qua}}$ is defined analogously for quality questions. The function $V(z, s)$ returns the confidence score $v_k \in [0, 1]$ for image $z$ on question $s$ via the probability ratio method.

**What it computes:** a single scalar in $[0, 1]$ that equally weights semantic correctness and visual quality. The geometric mean ensures that if either semantic or quality scores are low, the overall reward is low — an image that perfectly matches the prompt semantically but is visually artifacted will receive a low reward, as will an image that is aesthetically perfect but fails to satisfy the prompt's constraints. This prevents the model from optimizing one dimension at the expense of the other.

**Why the geometric mean rather than the arithmetic mean:** the geometric mean $\sqrt{ab}$ is more punitive to imbalance than the arithmetic mean $(a+b)/2$. For example, if semantic score is 0.9 and quality score is 0.3, the arithmetic mean is 0.6 while the geometric mean is $\sqrt{0.27} \approx 0.52$. The geometric mean therefore creates a stronger penalty for neglecting one dimension entirely, encouraging the model to maintain balance. The paper does not ablate aggregation functions (arithmetic vs. geometric vs. harmonic mean), so the specific choice may be more convention than empirically justified — but the reasoning about imbalance penalization is standard in multi-objective optimization.

**Why separate semantic and quality categories rather than a single unified score:** the decomposition into semantic and quality dimensions is important for interpretability (the paper can inspect which dimension is failing when the reward is low) but also has a structural benefit. Semantic questions are prompt-specific (they ask about elements mentioned in the prompt), while quality questions are more generic (they ask about universally desirable visual properties). By averaging within each category before combining, the reward treats all semantic constraints equally (a missing "blue" attribute penalizes the same as a missing "on the left" spatial relation) and treats all quality aspects equally. If the scores were simply averaged across all questions, prompts with many quality questions would overweight quality relative to semantics, or prompts where the decomposer generated many redundant semantic questions would overweight those constraints.

**The absence of weighting across question types.** The paper does not apply weights to different question types — all semantic questions are equally weighted, and all quality questions are equally weighted. This is a simplification that may not reflect real-world user preferences. For example, most users would consider a miscount of objects (semantic) a more serious error than slightly unrealistic texture (quality), but DVReward weights them equally. The paper does not discuss this limitation, but it could explain some of the residual errors observed in the qualitative results — the model may prioritize easy-to-satisfy quality constraints over difficult semantic ones if both contribute equally to the reward.

#### Training Data Construction

The training data is **synthetically generated** using a "Primitive-to-Prompt" bottom-up strategy designed to ensure comprehensive coverage of compositional visual reasoning tasks while maintaining natural language diversity.

**The visual elements pool.** The paper first constructs a pool of visual primitives — objects (e.g., animals, furniture, vehicles), attributes (colors, materials, sizes, textures), spatial relations (left/right, above/below, behind/in-front), actions (holding, sitting, running), and environmental elements (weather, time of day, setting). The exact size and composition of this pool are not specified in the paper, but it is described as "comprehensive."

**The task taxonomy.** The paper follows the taxonomy of TIIF-Bench (Wei et al., 2025) to define 39 distinct compositional task types. While the full list is not enumerated in the main paper, the categories are implied by the semantic question dimensions in Table 16 and the benchmark categories in Table 19: spatial reasoning (relative positioning, depth relations), attribute binding (color, material, shape, size), counting (numerical quantities), action specification (dynamic movements, physical activities), style specification (artistic medium, visual genre), text rendering (spelled words, signage), negative constraints (explicit absence), viewpoint specification (camera parameters), and environment specification (background, weather, lighting). Each task type presumably corresponds to one or more of these dimensions.

**Prompt synthesis.** For each of the 39 tasks, the paper uses Qwen3-235B-A22B as a prompt generator. The LLM is instructed to synthesize a prompt by stochastically sampling elements from the visual elements pool and composing them into natural language. The generation process considers three difficulty tiers:

- **Easy:** simple prompts with one or two constraints (e.g., "A red ball on a table").
- **Medium:** prompts with multiple interacting constraints (e.g., "A blue cat on the left and a red dog on the right under a cloudy sky").
- **Hard:** prompts with numerous, complex, and potentially counterintuitive constraints (e.g., "A metallic rose that is not fully bloomed is higher than a blooming fabric rose," from Figure 1).

The difficulty ratio is 3:5:2 (Easy:Medium:Hard), meaning 30% easy, 50% medium, and 20% hard prompts. This skew toward medium difficulty is deliberate — easy prompts provide insufficient training signal (the base model already generates them correctly), while hard prompts provide mostly failure cases (the model rarely gets them right even with refinement, so the reward signal has low variance). Medium prompts are where the model is partially correct, creating the most informative gradient for reinforcement learning.

**Scale and splits.** For each of the 39 tasks, 500 prompts are generated, yielding 19,500 training prompts total. An additional 1,024 prompts are generated as a held-out test set. The paper does not specify whether the test set follows the same difficulty distribution as the training set.

**Offline question pre-generation.** After prompts are synthesized, the decomposer (Qwen3-235B-A22B) processes each prompt to generate the semantic and quality question sets $Q_{\text{sem}}$ and $Q_{\text{qua}}$. This step is performed offline once and cached, so the GRPO training loop only needs to run the verifier (Qwen3VL-30B-A3B) on the pre-generated questions — no LLM calls are needed during training for decomposition. Prompts that generate more than 50 verification questions are dropped from the training set (the paper does not specify how many prompts are dropped by this criterion).

**Data processing summary (in order):**
1. Construct visual elements pool (manual curation).
2. Define 39 compositional task types (following TIIF-Bench taxonomy).
3. Use Qwen3-235B-A22B to generate 500 prompts per task (19,500 total) across three difficulty tiers (3:5:2 ratio), plus 1,024 test prompts.
4. For each training prompt, use Qwen3-235B-A22B as decomposer to generate $Q_{\text{sem}}$ (10 dimension types) and $Q_{\text{qua}}$ (8 aspect types).
5. Filter: drop prompts with >50 total questions.
6. Store resulting triplets $(q, Q_{\text{sem}}, Q_{\text{qua}})$ for use during AlphaGRPO training.

**Why this data construction strategy:** the paper argues that synthetic data generation is necessary because naturally occurring prompts from web datasets often lack the diversity and precision of compositional constraints needed to train robust multimodal reasoning. The 39-task taxonomy ensures systematic coverage of reasoning types (spatial, counting, attribute binding, etc.) that might be under-represented in organic data. The difficulty tiering ensures the model trains on a curriculum where it can succeed (easy), struggle productively (medium), and encounter boundary cases (hard). This is a deliberate departure from prior work that used human-written or web-scraped prompts — the cost is that training prompts may differ stylistically from real user prompts (synthetic LLM-generated prompts can be more stilted, verbose, or unnaturally precise), but the benefit is controlled coverage of failure modes.

#### Training Configuration and Infrastructure

**Base model.** AlphaGRPO is implemented on Bagel (Deng et al., 2025), a native unified multimodal model that integrates understanding and generation into a single backbone. Unlike "composite" UMMs that connect specialized LLMs and diffusion transformers via adapter layers, Bagel is trained from scratch on large-scale interleaved multimodal data, meaning its text understanding and image generation capabilities share the same parameters. This makes it an ideal testbed for the paper's unified optimization hypothesis — if the model can learn to improve its images by reasoning textually, the shared parameters allow gradients from the image reward to directly shape the representations used for both understanding and generation.

**Parameter-efficient fine-tuning.** The paper applies Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) with rank $r = 32$ and scaling factor $\alpha = 64$ to all linear layers in the attention and MLP modules of the Bagel backbone. LoRA injects trainable low-rank matrices into the frozen pretrained weights, dramatically reducing the number of parameters that must be updated and stored during training. The paper does not specify the total number of trainable parameters, but for a model of Bagel's scale (likely several billion parameters), a rank-32 LoRA on all linear layers would typically represent 1-5% of the total parameter count.

**Why LoRA:** the paper does not explicitly justify this choice, but the practical benefits are clear: LoRA reduces memory consumption (only adapter weights and their gradients need to be stored, not full model gradients), enables faster training (fewer parameters to update), and prevents catastrophic forgetting of pretrained capabilities (the frozen backbone preserves the model's original knowledge). For a research exploration where the primary claim is about the effectiveness of the training paradigm rather than absolute performance, LoRA is a reasonable choice. However, it does mean the reported gains are from adapting a pretrained model rather than training from scratch, and the full potential of AlphaGRPO on a fully fine-tuned model is not evaluated.

**Diffusion sampling configuration.** The paper uses different diffusion configurations for training and evaluation, and these differ between the RT2I and SRR tasks:

- **RT2I training:** $T = 16$ sampling steps during training, with the first 10 steps converted from deterministic flow to SDE sampling (Euler-Maruyama with noise level $a = 0.7$) for GRPO training. The remaining 6 steps use standard deterministic flow. This means the model explores stochastically during the early, high-level composition phase of generation (where semantic decisions are made) and deterministically during the later refinement phase (where fine details are filled in). For evaluation, $T = 40$ steps are used (all deterministic flow), providing higher image quality at the cost of longer inference time.

- **SRR training:** $T = 40$ steps for both the initial generation (producing the low-quality input image) and the subsequent self-reflective refinement. From the refinement's 40-step trajectory, 5 timesteps are stochastically sampled from the first 15 for GRPO training. This is a more aggressive subsampling than RT2I (5 out of 15 vs. 10 out of 16), presumably because SRR requires more total steps for the refinement to be visually effective, but training on all 40 steps would be computationally prohibitive.

**Why different step counts for training vs. evaluation:** this is a standard technique in diffusion model training — fewer steps during training reduce memory and compute, while more steps during evaluation improve quality. The model learns to produce good images with coarse trajectories, and the additional steps at inference time refine details. However, it introduces a train-test mismatch: the policy is optimized under a 16-step or 40-step generation budget, but evaluated under 40 steps. The paper does not discuss whether this mismatch affects the learned policy's optimality — it is possible that strategies learned under low-step-count budgets (e.g., simplified compositions that are less error-prone) do not generalize optimally to high-step-count evaluation.

**Group size and batch configuration.** Each training step processes 32 prompts simultaneously. For each prompt, the model samples $G = 14$ trajectories, yielding $32 \times 14 = 448$ total trajectories per step. The update minibatch size equals the rollout batch size (the paper notes "We only train 380 steps with update minibatch size equals the rollout batch size"), meaning all 448 trajectories are used in a single gradient update. This is unusual — in standard RL, on-policy data is typically split into multiple minibatches and the policy is updated several times per batch of rollout data. The single-update regime is conservative (less off-policy data reuse, potentially more stable training) but sample-inefficient, which may explain why only 380 total steps are trained.

**Why 380 steps:** the paper does not explain this number directly, but a rough calculation provides context. With 32 prompts per step and 380 steps, the model sees $32 \times 380 = 12{,}160$ unique prompts during training (with prompts potentially repeating if the dataset is cycled). The training set has 19,500 prompts, so the model sees approximately 62% of the training data in a single pass (less than one epoch). This is a very short training horizon by deep learning standards, suggesting that either (a) the model overfits to the reward signal quickly, (b) the computational cost of DVReward verification limits total training steps, or (c) the paper found diminishing returns beyond this point but does not report the finding. The short training horizon is a limitation — it is unclear whether additional training would further improve downstream benchmarks or whether the model has saturated.

**KL regularization.** Both KL coefficients are set to $\beta_{\text{AR}} = \beta_{\text{Flow}} = 0$ in the default configuration, based on the ablation in Appendix A.3, Table 13, which showed that $\beta_{\text{AR}} = \beta_{\text{Flow}} = 0.001$ did not provide consistent gains (TIIF-S: 79.2 vs. 78.9 with KL, TIIF-L: 78.7 vs. 79.5 without, GenEval: 84.1 vs. 85.0 without KL).

**Classifier-free guidance during training.** The paper uses classifier-free guidance (CFG; Ho & Salimans, 2022) during training, which is unusual — CFG is typically used only at inference time to trade off between image fidelity and prompt adherence. For RT2I training, a text CFG of 4.0 is used. For SRR training, both a text CFG of 4.0 and an image CFG of 2.0 are used. The image CFG in SRR training means the model receives both the text prompt and the initial (incorrect) image as conditioning, and the guidance mechanism helps the model attend more strongly to the text prompt's corrections rather than replicating features of the incorrect input image.

**Text generation parameters.** For sampling the autoregressive reasoning text during training, the paper uses a temperature of 1.0 (encouraging diverse exploration of reasoning strategies) and a top-p value of 0.8 (nucleus sampling, filtering out the tail of low-probability tokens). The text sampling uses a format penalty: a reward of -0.5 is assigned if the thinking text contains incorrect XML-style tags (presumably for structuring the reasoning output), and 0 for correctly formatted reasoning. This penalty is added directly to the image reward to compute the advantage for the entire trajectory, meaning poorly formatted reasoning text reduces the advantage signal for both the text and image components.

**Distributed training setup.** Training is conducted on 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs (8 nodes × 8 GPUs). On each 8-GPU node, 7 GPUs are dedicated to model training (parameter updates, forward/backward passes) and 1 GPU to serving the reward model (Qwen3VL-30B-A3B) for DVReward computation. The decentralized reward serving architecture means each node computes rewards for its locally generated images, eliminating cross-node communication overhead for verification. The LoRA adapters (rank 32, alpha 64) are applied to all linear layers in attention and MLP modules.

**Image resolution.** All training is conducted at 512 × 512 resolution. For evaluation at 1024 × 1024 resolution, the model is used directly without high-resolution fine-tuning — the paper argues this tests whether the model has learned generalizable semantic alignment (which should transfer across resolutions) rather than pixel-level memorization of the training resolution.

**Total training duration.** At 380 steps with 32 prompts per step and 14 trajectories per prompt, the model generates $380 \times 32 \times 14 = 170{,}240$ images during training. With 64 A100 GPUs, the paper does not report wall-clock training time, but given the complexity of the DVReward verification pipeline (even with optimizations), training likely takes hours to tens of hours rather than days.

**Training stability.** The paper notes that the base model "occasionally generates noisy or blurry artifacts at a resolution of 512 and outputs unexpected reasoning patterns during self-reflection" (Appendix A.1). This is acknowledged as a limitation and suggests that training stability is sensitive to the initial model quality — AlphaGRPO cannot compensate for fundamental flaws in the base model's generation or reasoning, only amplify existing capabilities. The paper suggests future work using Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) on high-quality self-generated data to improve stability.

**Evaluation configuration.** For benchmark evaluation (Tables 1, 18-21), the model uses $T = 40$ diffusion steps for RT2I and SRR. For inference-time self-reflective refinement, the model first generates an initial image (40 steps), then runs the SRR procedure (40 additional steps) to produce the refined image. Evaluation is performed at both 512 and 1024 resolution to test generalization. The LLM rewriter (mentioned in Table 18, "† refers to the methods using LLM rewriter") is used to preprocess prompts for the GenEval benchmark — this is a standard technique in the field (using an LLM to expand or clarify user prompts before generation) and is applied consistently to both the Bagel baseline and AlphaGRPO to ensure fair comparison.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Cold-Start Independence Hypothesis — RL Can Activate Latent UMM Capabilities Without Distillation from Stronger Models

The paper's most provocative intellectual move is not the use of GRPO for multimodal generation (prior work on FlowGRPO and DanceGRPO already applied GRPO to diffusion models), but the explicit claim that **pretrained unified multimodal models already contain the reasoning primitives needed for self-critique and intent inference, and that RL alone — without a supervised fine-tuning stage on teacher-generated data — can activate them**. This directly challenges the dominant paradigm in the UMM alignment literature, where a "cold-start" SFT phase using data distilled from proprietary models (GPT-4, Gemini) is treated as a necessary prerequisite for complex behaviors like self-reflective refinement (Deng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b; Huang et al., 2025b).

**What makes this intellectually distinctive** is the framing of the problem as one of *behavioral activation* rather than *capability injection*. The paper argues that UMMs suffer from a confirmation bias analogous to the one documented in pure LLMs (Huang et al., 2023) — they default to asserting their own outputs are correct rather than scrutinizing them — and that this bias can be overcome by training the model to adopt an error-seeking stance. The pilot study (Figure 2) provides the diagnostic evidence: the Bagel model fails to identify an obvious shadow misalignment when asked to "verify" but succeeds when explicitly instructed to "find errors," demonstrating that the perceptual and diagnostic capabilities exist but are suppressed by default behavior. The Self-Reflective Refinement training task operationalizes this insight by making the error-seeking behavior the *default* through RL, rather than requiring an explicit prompt ("find errors") at inference time. The paper's evidence that training on SRR improves performance on generation benchmarks (Table 1) and editing tasks (Table 2) — despite no SFT cold-start and no editing data in training — constitutes evidence for the dormant-capability hypothesis: the model is not learning new visual understanding from the RL reward signal (DVReward only scores images, it does not teach the model what errors look like), but rather learning to *access and apply* understanding it already possessed from pretraining.

**The significance beyond performance** is that this reframes the economics and accessibility of UMM alignment. If cold-start SFT is unnecessary, organizations can activate self-refinement in their UMMs without access to a stronger teacher model, without the cost of curating synthetic reasoning trajectories, and without the risk that improvements merely reflect teacher distillation. The boundary conditions are important: the paper acknowledges that the base model must already possess the necessary primitives (it "occasionally generates noisy or blurry artifacts" that training cannot fix, Appendix A.1), and the approach has not been tested on genuinely novel reasoning types that the base model might lack entirely. This is therefore a fundamental but bounded advance — it shows that activation can replace injection for capabilities within the model's existing range, but does not claim to expand that range.

### Innovation 2: Decompositional Verification as a Third Path Between Scalar Collapse and Domain Overfitting in Reward Design

The paper identifies and resolves a fundamental tension in reward model design for open-world multimodal generation RL that prior work had navigated by choosing one of two unsatisfactory options. Holistic scalar reward models (VIEScore, PickScore) preserve the evaluator's general visual understanding but collapse multi-dimensional semantic judgments into a single scalar, losing discriminability — the paper's Figure 3 provides the canonical failure case where a correct and incorrect spatial relationship receive identical scores. Fine-tuned MLLM reward models (HPSv3, UnifiedReward) improve discriminability by specializing on human preference data, but sacrifice the open-world evaluation capacity that made the MLLM valuable in the first place, risking reward overfitting to narrow preference distributions. The paper's Table 3 provides evidence for both failure modes: VIEScore degrades GenEval (81.7 vs. 84.0 baseline), while HPSv3 and UnifiedReward degrade TIIF-Bench Long (77.1, 77.3 vs. 78.6 baseline).

**The conceptual advance** is that the information needed for discriminative evaluation *already exists* in the MLLM's internal representations — the problem is the **query format**, not the model's capability. By decomposing the evaluation into atomic, verifiable questions and extracting confidence from token logits ($P_{\text{Yes}} / (P_{\text{Yes}} + P_{\text{No}})$), DVReward accesses the MLLM's fine-grained visual understanding without fine-tuning the model away from its generalist distribution. This is a diagnostic move with broad implications: it suggests that many failures attributed to "MLLM unreliability as evaluators" are actually failures of *interface design* — asking the model to produce a scalar rating compresses away the discriminative information that the model clearly possesses (as shown by its ability to answer specific questions correctly). The connection to Davidsonian Scene Graph (Cho et al., 2023) is acknowledged but the extension is significant: DSG used template-based decomposition for evaluation, while DVReward uses LLM-based decomposition with explicit physical visual grounding (converting "Is the coffee hot?" to "Is there steam rising from the cup?") to handle open-ended, under-specified user prompts that rule-based templates cannot cover.

**What makes this more than an engineering trick** is the systematic evidence that the specific decomposition matters. The ablation in Table 9 shows that merging atomic questions into at most 10 coarse questions degrades GenEval substantially (81.1 vs. 85.0 overall, with spatial dropping from 74.3 to 63.0 and count from 82.5 to 71.8), confirming that the fine-grained atomic structure — not just the total amount of verification — is what provides the discriminative reward signal. The ablation on confidence scoring (Table 4) shows that continuous confidence (79.5 TIIF-L, 85.1 GenEval) outperforms hard binary scores (78.9, 84.0), validating that preserving the MLLM's uncertainty provides a smoother optimization landscape. Together, these results establish decompositional verification as a principled approach to extracting stable reward signals from general MLLMs — not just a heuristic that happens to work on these benchmarks.

### Innovation 3: The Unified Trajectory as a Mechanism for Cross-Modal Gradient Propagation in AR-Diffusion Models

Prior work on applying GRPO to visual generation (FlowGRPO, DanceGRPO; Liu et al., 2025a; Xue et al., 2025) optimized a pure diffusion model — the policy being updated was the flow-matching velocity field, and the reward was computed on the final image. Prior work on applying RL to UMMs (Mao et al., 2025; Nie et al., 2025) operated on pure autoregressive architectures where images were represented as discrete token sequences, and the RL objective updated a single autoregressive policy. **AlphaGRPO is the first to formulate a joint optimization objective over both an autoregressive text policy and a continuous diffusion flow policy within a single model**, with a shared advantage signal propagated through both components simultaneously.

**The intellectual contribution** is the recognition that the autoregressive reasoning text and the diffusion image trajectory form a **causal chain within a single differentiable model**, and that this chain enables the image reward to provide a training signal for the text policy. The unified trajectory $\tau = (y, z_1 \rightarrow z_0)$ is not merely a notational convenience — it is what makes the entire approach coherent. When the model generates better reasoning text $y$ (more accurate spatial planning, more specific attribute descriptions), the diffusion process produces a better image $z_0$, which receives a higher DVReward score. Because the model is end-to-end differentiable (shared backbone parameters across understanding and generation), the gradient of the reward with respect to the text policy parameters flows through the diffusion path, encouraging the model to produce reasoning text that leads to better images. Without the unified trajectory formulation, the text and image policies would need separate reward signals — but DVReward can only evaluate the final image (it cannot directly score reasoning text quality), so there would be no mechanism to improve the reasoning.

**Why this is non-trivial** is that the two components have fundamentally different policy parameterizations — discrete categorical distributions for text tokens versus continuous Gaussian transition distributions for diffusion latents — requiring separate PPO clipping strategies, separate probability ratio computations, and separate KL regularization forms (the Schulman estimator for AR, closed-form weighted L2 distance for flow). The paper's decision to weight the AR component at $\lambda = 0.2$ (Equation 4) reflects an empirical judgment that the flow component should dominate the update, which makes sense given that the flow directly controls pixel-level image quality while text provides only indirect conditioning. The fact that the KL coefficients were ablated to zero (Table 13) — an unusual finding given that KL regularization is standard in RLHF to prevent reward hacking — raises an interesting implicit claim: when both components share parameters and are optimized jointly, the cross-modal gradient signal may provide a form of implicit regularization that makes explicit KL penalties unnecessary, or at least not beneficial at the 380-step training horizon used in the paper. This is a subtle but potentially important architectural insight: parameter sharing across modalities may constrain policy drift in ways that isolated policies do not experience.

**The evidence for effectiveness** is the generalization result: training on SRR (which involves both text-based error diagnosis and image-based refinement) improves downstream editing performance (Table 2, +0.52 overall on GEdit) despite no editing data in training. This suggests that the jointly learned text policy has internalized a general error-diagnosis capability that transfers to the structurally similar but semantically different editing task, where the model must reason about what to change in an input image based on an editing instruction. A pure diffusion-only GRPO (optimizing only the image generation policy) could not achieve this transfer because there would be no text policy to generalize.

### Innovation 4: False-Positive Rectification as a Principled Solution to Regression in Self-Improvement RL

A widely recognized failure mode in self-improvement RL (where a model attempts to refine its own outputs) is that the group-relative advantage normalization can **reward degradation** — a refinement that produces a worse output than the starting point can receive a positive advantage if it is better than other trajectories in its group. This is not a hypothetical concern: if the model produces several refinement attempts that are all substantially worse than the initial image (but one is less bad than the others), standard GRPO would assign a positive advantage to the least-bad degradation, training the model to prefer slightly-bad outputs over very-bad outputs rather than preferring good outputs.

**The conceptual contribution** is the recognition that this failure mode can be eliminated through a simple validity constraint that is formally guaranteed to prevent the policy from being rewarded for regression. False-Positive Rectification (FPR) detects when $r(z_{\text{refined}}) \leq r(z_{\text{init}})$ and replaces the trajectory's reward with the group minimum $r_{\min}$, forcing its advantage negative. **This is principled rather than heuristic** because it is a direct implementation of the self-reflective refinement task's core assumption: a valid trajectory must strictly improve upon the initial input. The constraint is non-differentiable and operates purely on the reward signal before advantage computation, making it compatible with any GRPO variant without modification to the policy gradient computation.

**What distinguishes this from ad-hoc filtering** (e.g., simply dropping regression trajectories from the batch, or clamping the reward to the initial image's score) is that it preserves the group structure while enforcing the monotonic improvement constraint. Dropping trajectories would reduce the effective group size and eliminate the negative signal entirely, preventing the model from learning *why* refinements fail. Clamping to the initial image's reward would create a discontinuous reward function (all regression trajectories get the same reward regardless of how much they regress), losing information about the severity of failure. The group-minimum assignment is a compromise: it maintains the full group size for stable advantage normalization, ensures all regression trajectories are ranked below all improvement trajectories, and preserves within-regression ranking (though this is largely irrelevant since all regression trajectories get negative advantages — the ranking among them does not affect the update). The ablation in Table 5 shows that FPR provides consistent gains (TIIF-Long improves from 77.8 to 79.5), confirming both that the regression failure mode occurs in practice and that FPR effectively mitigates it.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The primary evaluations are conducted on four standard downstream text-to-image benchmarks: GenEval (Ghosh et al., 2023), TIIF-Bench (Wei et al., 2025), DPG-Bench (Hu et al., 2024), and WISE (Niu et al., 2025b). Additionally, image editing performance is evaluated on GEdit-Bench (Liu et al., 2025b). The paper explicitly prioritizes generalization to these downstream benchmarks rather than relying on an in-distribution test set. For training data, a synthetically generated set of 19,500 prompts is used, constructed via the "Primitive-to-Prompt" strategy covering 39 compositional task types with a difficulty ratio of 3:5:2 (Easy:Medium:Hard), plus 1,024 held-out test prompts (Section 4.3).

- **Base model.** All experiments use Bagel (Deng et al., 2025), a native AR-Diffusion Unified Multimodal Model that integrates visual understanding and continuous image generation within a single backbone trained on large-scale interleaved data. The paper argues Bagel is representative of contemporary UMMs and an "ideal testbed" for joint text-image GRPO optimization because its shared parameters allow gradients from the image reward to directly shape both understanding and generation representations (Section 5.1). Training applies LoRA with rank r=32 and alpha=64 to all linear layers in attention and MLP modules, keeping most pretrained weights frozen.

- **Metrics.** For GenEval, the metric is overall accuracy (%) across six sub-tasks (Single Object, Two Objects, Counting, Colors, Position, Color Attribution). For TIIF-Bench, the metric is average accuracy (%) across Basic Following and Advanced Following dimensions, reported separately for Short and Long prompts, plus overall scores. For DPG-Bench, scores are reported on Global, Entity, Attribute, Relation, and Other dimensions, with an Overall average. For WISE, the metric is a numerical score (0–1 scale) across six world-knowledge categories (Cultural, Time, Space, Biology, Physics, Chemistry) with per-category and overall averages. For GEdit-Bench, three sub-scores are reported: G (General), SC (Semantic Consistency), and PQ (Perceptual Quality), all evaluated by GPT-4.1. All TIIF-Bench and GEdit-Bench evaluations use GPT-4.1 as the judge; other benchmarks use their standard evaluation protocols.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three categories of models. **Generation-only models:** SD3 Medium (Esser et al., 2024), FLUX.1 dev (Labs, 2024), SDXL (Podell et al., 2024), PixArt-α and PixArt-Σ (Chen et al., 2024), Hunyuan-DiT (Li et al., 2024), DALL-E 3 (Betker et al., 2023), OmniGen (Xiao et al., 2025), and OmniGen2 (Wu et al., 2025b). **Unified multimodal models:** Show-o (Xie et al., 2024), Janus and JanusPro (Wu et al., 2025a; Chen et al., 2025b), EMU3 and Emu3-Gen (Wang et al., 2024), TokenFlow-XL (Qu et al., 2025), SEED-X (Ge et al., 2024), ILLUME (Wang et al., 2025a), Transfusion (Zhou et al., 2024), MetaQuery-XL (Pan et al., 2025), BLIP3-o (Chen et al., 2025a), UniWorld-V1 (Lin et al., 2025), and VILA-U (Wu et al., 2024). For the editing benchmark GEdit-Bench, baselines include Instruct-Pix2Pix (Isola et al., 2017), MagicBrush (Zhang et al., 2023), AnyEdit (Yu et al., 2025), OmniGen2, Step1X-Edit (Liu et al., 2025b), Gemini 2.0 (Gemini2, 2025), and GPT-4o (OpenAI, 2025). The primary head-to-head comparison is against the **Bagel baseline** without AlphaGRPO training, with the paper reproducing Bagel's results rather than relying on officially reported numbers (the paper notes that it "could not accurately reproduce the officially reported baseline performance" in Appendix A.5, and reports reproduced values for fair comparison).

- **Generation budget.** The paper does not report a standardized "generation budget" in the sense of FLOPs or sample count for the main benchmark comparisons, as these evaluate fixed generation configurations (40 diffusion steps for evaluation, 16 or 40 for training). For training, the budget is defined implicitly: each GRPO step samples G=14 trajectories per prompt across 32 prompts (448 trajectories per step), with 380 total training steps, yielding 170,240 total image generations during training. The inference-time self-reflective refinement (Inf. SRR) doubles the generation budget at evaluation time since it requires generating both an initial image and a refined image (2× the diffusion steps of single-pass generation).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report confidence intervals, error bars, or statistical significance tests for any of its benchmark results. There is no mention of cross-validation for strategy selection (unlike the referenced example paper, which uses two-fold cross-validation within difficulty bins). The 1,024-prompt test set from the synthetic data is described but its specific use — whether for early stopping, hyperparameter selection, or final evaluation — is not detailed. For human evaluation (Appendix A.3, Table 11), 200 real-user prompts from Pick-a-Pic are evaluated by three annotators comparing Bagel vs. AlphaGRPO pairs; no inter-annotator agreement metrics (e.g., Fleiss' kappa) are reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Text-to-Image Generation Benchmarks (Table 1, Tables 18-21)

**Overall headline result.** AlphaGRPO trained on Self-Reflective Refinement (the default setting) with inference-time self-reflective refinement (Inf. SRR) achieves the strongest results: 83.86 on TIIF-Bench Short, 83.20 on TIIF-Bench Long, 88.2 on GenEval overall, 87.86 on DPG-Bench Overall, and 0.53/0.71 on WISE Overall/Self-CoT at 512 resolution (Table 1, Tables 18-21). Against the Bagel baseline at 512 resolution, this represents gains of +8.65 on TIIF-Bench Short (75.21 → 83.86), +4.64 on TIIF-Bench Long (78.56 → 83.20), +4.2 on GenEval overall (84.0 → 88.2), +2.79 on DPG-Bench Overall (85.07 → 87.86), and +0.01 on WISE with Self-CoT (0.70 → 0.71). The paper notes that AlphaGRPO outperforms Bagel by 5.8% on TIIF-Bench Short with Inf. SRR (Section 1: "reaching 83.9% on TIIF-Bench and outperforming Bagel by 5.8%"), though the table reports the gap as 83.86 - 75.21 = 8.65 percentage points — this discrepancy likely reflects which Bagel baseline value is used (the paper's reproduced value may differ from the officially reported one).

**Training on low resolution generalizes to high resolution.** At 1024×1024 inference resolution, despite training only at 512×512, AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR achieves 89.5 on GenEval overall (vs. 86.6 for Bagel, +2.9) and 82.24/81.39 on TIIF-Bench Short/Long (vs. 76.42/76.15, gains of +5.82/+5.24). The paper interprets this as evidence that the model has learned semantic alignment rather than pixel-level memorization of the training resolution.

**Training on SRR matches or exceeds training on RT2I for generation.** AlphaGRPO trained on SRR and evaluated without Inf. SRR achieves 84.2 on GenEval vs. 85.0 for AlphaGRPO trained on RT2I — a difference of only 0.8 points. On DPG-Bench, SRR training achieves 86.25 vs. 85.98 for RT2I, slightly exceeding it. On TIIF-Bench, the results are mixed: SRR achieves 79.05/79.50 (Short/Long) vs. RT2I's 78.92/79.48. The paper argues this demonstrates that SRR training instills "generalized reasoning regardless of the training format" (Section 5.2), since the SRR task involves fixing flawed images rather than generating from scratch, yet the trained model performs comparably to one directly trained on generation.

**Inference-time SRR provides substantial additional gains.** Applying Inf. SRR on top of AlphaGRPO (SRR) produces the largest improvements: +4.81 on TIIF-Bench Short (79.05 → 83.86), +3.70 on TIIF-Bench Long (79.50 → 83.20), +4.0 on GenEval overall (84.2 → 88.2), and +1.61 on DPG-Bench Overall (86.25 → 87.86). The GenEval sub-metric breakdown (Table 18) reveals where Inf. SRR helps most: Color Attribution improves from 73.3 to 81.5 (+8.2), Counting from 75.6 to 83.4 (+7.8), and Colors from 91.0 to 93.1 (+2.1). Position shows only a modest gain (69.8 → 73.8, +4.0). This pattern suggests Inf. SRR is particularly effective at correcting attribute binding and counting errors (which involve precise local modifications) but less effective at fixing global spatial layout errors (which may require restructuring the entire composition).

**Comparison to generation-only and other unified models.** AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR at 512 resolution (88.2 GenEval) exceeds all open-source unified models: Janus-Pro-7B (80.0), Show-o (68.0), EMU3 (66.0), and MetaQuery-XL (80.0). It also exceeds the generation-only FLUX.1 dev (82.0) and SD3-Medium (74.0). At 1024 resolution, AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR (89.5 GenEval) extends this lead. On DPG-Bench at 512 resolution, AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR (87.86) surpasses FLUX.1 dev (84.00), SD3-Medium (84.08), DALL-E 3 (83.50), Janus Pro (84.19), and OmniGen2 (83.57). The TIIF-Bench results confirm this pattern: at 512 resolution, AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR (83.86/83.20 Short/Long) substantially exceeds FLUX.1 dev (71.09/71.78), SD3-Medium (67.46/66.09), and JanusPro (66.50/65.02).

**Fine-grained TIIF-Bench analysis (Table 19).** The detailed breakdown reveals specific strengths. In the Advanced Following category, AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR achieves 88.23 on Attribute+Relation (Short) vs. 73.66 for Bagel, and 88.73 on Attribute+Reasoning vs. 77.61 for Bagel. Text rendering shows a dramatic improvement: 53.85 for AlphaGRPO (RT2I) vs. 40.72 for Bagel on Short prompts, though Inf. SRR does not further improve this metric (it remains at 45.25 with Inf. SRR). Style consistency reaches a perfect 100.0 with Inf. SRR on both Short and Long prompts. On the Designer subset, which evaluates aesthetic quality, AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR achieves 85.80/85.48 (Short/Long) vs. 78.58/77.86 for Bagel.

**DPG-Bench sub-metric analysis (Table 20).** The largest gains from AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR over Bagel come in the Other category (93.51 vs. 88.67, +4.84) and Relation (91.67 vs. 90.82, +0.85). Attribute shows a modest gain (91.91 vs. 91.29, +0.62). Entity and Global actually decrease slightly compared to Bagel: Entity drops from 90.37 to 90.79 (a small gain, not a decrease — note that Entity actually increases from 90.37 to 90.79), while Global drops from 88.94 to 85.50 (-3.44). The Global metric, which evaluates overall image coherence and quality, is the only metric where AlphaGRPO consistently underperforms the baseline, suggesting that the RL optimization may trade off global composition for improved local attribute accuracy — the model gets better at binding specific attributes but potentially at the cost of holistic image structure.

**WISE world-knowledge analysis (Table 21).** WISE evaluates whether generated images reflect accurate world knowledge (e.g., biological structures, chemical apparatus, cultural artifacts). Bagel with Self-CoT (chain-of-thought reasoning) achieves 0.70 overall. AlphaGRPO with Self-CoT achieves 0.71 overall, a marginal improvement. The per-category breakdown reveals uneven gains: Chemistry shows a substantial improvement (0.58 → 0.64), as does Physics (0.75 → 0.77), while Cultural (0.76 → 0.75), Time (0.69 → 0.70), Space (0.75 → 0.74), and Biology (0.65 → 0.66) are essentially unchanged. This suggests the RL training primarily improves scientific reasoning (where precise structural knowledge matters) rather than cultural or temporal reasoning (which may require broader world knowledge that the base model already handles well).

#### Multimodal Image Editing (Table 2)

**Headline editing result.** AlphaGRPO trained on SRR achieves a GEdit-Bench overall score of 7.08 (evaluated by GPT-4.1), compared to 6.52 for Bagel, a gain of +0.56 or 8.6% relative improvement. The sub-scores are: General 7.67, Semantic Consistency 7.46, Perceptual Quality 7.08. AlphaGRPO trained on RT2I achieves 6.85 overall (General 7.54, Semantic Consistency 7.18, Perceptual Quality 6.85), a gain of +0.33 over Bagel.

**Comparison to specialized editing models and closed-source systems.** AlphaGRPO (7.08 overall) exceeds the open-source editing baselines: Instruct-Pix2Pix (3.68), MagicBrush (4.52), AnyEdit (3.21), Step1X-Edit (6.70), and OmniGen2 (6.41). It approaches but does not match the closed-source GPT-4o (7.53) and substantially trails GPT-4o's General score (7.67 vs. 7.85) and Semantic Consistency (7.46 vs. 7.62). Gemini 2.0 scores lower than AlphaGRPO (6.32 vs. 7.08 overall).

**The key claim about generalization.** The paper emphasizes that "without training on editing data, AlphaGRPO not only maintains comparable gains to AlphaGRPO (RT2I) on image generation benchmarks and secures a 0.52 improvement on editing benchmark" (Section 1). The actual gain over Bagel is 0.56 (7.08 - 6.52), not 0.52 — this minor numerical discrepancy may reflect rounding. The RT2I-trained model's gain over Bagel on editing is +0.33, confirming that SRR training transfers better to editing than RT2I training, which is intuitive since SRR involves analyzing an input image and producing a corrected output — structurally similar to editing tasks.

**Contextualizing the editing results.** It is notable that AlphaGRPO, trained only on synthetic text-to-image prompts with self-reflective refinement, achieves an editing score (7.08) competitive with OmniGen2 (6.41), which was specifically designed for multimodal generation tasks including editing. The qualitative examples in Figure 9 (Appendix A.6) illustrate the transfer: for "Change to a white background," AlphaGRPO preserves the object's photorealistic texture while the Bagel baseline catastrophically degrades it into a line drawing. This suggests the SRR training teaches the model a general "preserve identity while changing specific attributes" capability that transfers to editing instructions.

#### Reward Model Comparison (Table 3)

**Headline finding.** DVReward is compared against PickScore (human preference model, Kirstain et al., 2023), VIEScore (holistic MLLM scalar scoring, Ku et al., 2024), HPSv3 (fine-tuned MLLM reward, Ma et al., 2025), and UnifiedReward (fine-tuned MLLM reward, Wang et al., 2025b). The comparison is performed on two base models: Stable Diffusion 3.5 Medium (SD3.5M) under the FlowGRPO setting (Liu et al., 2025a) and Bagel under the AlphaGRPO setting.

**On SD3.5M**, all reward models improve over the no-RL baseline (TIIF-Short 74.0, TIIF-Long 73.2, GenEval 79.8). DVReward achieves the best results across all three metrics: TIIF-Short 79.1 (vs. 77.6 for PickScore, 76.2 for VIEScore), TIIF-Long 77.7 (vs. 76.3 for PickScore, 72.9 for VIEScore), and GenEval 86.0 (vs. 80.4 for PickScore, 82.9 for VIEScore). Notably, VIEScore degrades TIIF-Long relative to the baseline (73.2 → 72.9), confirming the holistic scoring failure identified in the pilot study (Figure 3).

**On Bagel**, the results are more revealing: the baseline without RL achieves TIIF-Short 75.2, TIIF-Long 78.6, GenEval 84.0. HPSv3 improves TIIF-Short (78.5) but degrades TIIF-Long (77.1) and GenEval (83.4). UnifiedReward improves TIIF-Short (79.2) but degrades TIIF-Long (77.3) and GenEval (83.7). VIEScore degrades GenEval substantially (81.7) while improving TIIF benchmarks. Only DVReward improves all three metrics simultaneously: TIIF-Short 78.9, TIIF-Long 79.5, GenEval 85.1. This pattern — where fine-tuned reward models improve some benchmarks while degrading others — directly supports the paper's claim that "fine-tuning shifts the model's distribution towards a limited domain and implicitly narrows the MLLM's capacity to handle open-world samples" (Section 1). The GenEval degradation is particularly telling: GenEval evaluates compositional reasoning (spatial relations, counting, attribute binding), and the fine-tuned reward models may have been trained on preference data that underweights these dimensions relative to aesthetic quality.

**What this comparison does and does not show.** Table 3 demonstrates DVReward's superiority over the specific baselines tested, but several comparisons are missing. There is no comparison to an ensemble of reward models, to a reward model trained specifically on AlphaGRPO's training distribution, or to DVReward using a fine-tuned MLLM as the verifier (which would test whether decomposition alone, without the generalist MLLM, is sufficient). The comparison is also limited to two base models (SD3.5M and Bagel) and the specific training configurations described, so the finding that DVReward "consistently improves all benchmarks" is constrained to these settings.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablations in Section 5.3 are evaluated on TIIF-Bench and GenEval at 512 resolution, with consistent training settings unless otherwise noted.

**Confidence scoring vs. binary scoring (Table 4):** Replacing DVReward's continuous confidence score ($P_{\text{Yes}} / (P_{\text{Yes}} + P_{\text{No}})$) with hard binary scores (Yes=1, No=0) reduces performance on TIIF-Bench Long from 79.5 to 78.9 and on GenEval overall from 85.1 to 84.0. TIIF-Bench Short is essentially unchanged (78.9 vs. 79.0). The paper attributes this to the confidence score providing a "smoother gradient landscape" by preserving the model's uncertainty, though the gains are modest (0.6 on TIIF-Long, 1.1 on GenEval), suggesting that binary scoring is adequate for easier evaluation dimensions while continuous confidence helps primarily with fine-grained or ambiguous attributes.

**False-Positive Rectification (Table 5):** Removing FPR during SRR training causes consistent degradation: TIIF-Bench Short drops from 79.1 to 77.9 (-1.2), TIIF-Bench Long drops from 79.5 to 77.8 (-1.7), and GenEval overall drops from 84.2 to 83.7 (-0.5). The 1.7-point drop on TIIF-Long is the most substantial, confirming that without FPR, the model occasionally receives positive reinforcement for degrading outputs during refinement, and this noise disproportionately affects performance on longer, more complex prompts where refinement is most needed.

**Composite question types (Table 6):** Training with only semantic alignment questions (excluding perceptual quality questions) in DVReward, evaluated at 1024 resolution on the RT2I task, results in TIIF-Bench Short 76.3, TIIF-Bench Long 77.7, and GenEval 87.3. Adding quality questions improves TIIF-Bench Short to 78.7 (+2.4), TIIF-Bench Long to 79.5 (+1.8), and GenEval to 87.4 (+0.1). The large improvement on TIIF-Bench suggests that quality enforcement prevents the model from generating "semantically correct but visually degraded" samples — without quality questions, the model might satisfy semantic constraints through low-fidelity images that score well on attribute verification but poorly on human-judged benchmarks. The negligible change on GenEval (+0.1) is interesting: GenEval primarily evaluates semantic correctness rather than visual quality, so quality questions provide little additional signal for that benchmark.

**Number of verification questions (Table 9):** Merging the full set of atomic questions into at most 10 core questions per sample (using Qwen3-235B-A22B to consolidate) produces mixed results. On TIIF-Bench, the 10-question variant is competitive: TIIF-Short 78.2 vs. 78.9 for the full set, TIIF-Long 79.7 vs. 79.5. On GenEval, however, it drops substantially: overall 81.1 vs. 85.0, with Spatial collapsing from 74.3 to 63.0 and Count from 82.5 to 71.8. This asymmetric impact is informative: TIIF-Bench evaluates a broad set of alignment dimensions where coarse questions may still capture the relevant constraints, while GenEval's compositional sub-tasks (Spatial, Count) require precise, atomic verification of individual relationships and quantities — merging multiple counting or spatial constraints into a single question dilutes the reward signal for each individual constraint, allowing errors to go undetected.

**KL regularization (Table 13):** Adding KL penalties with βAR = βFlow = 0.001 (vs. the default of 0) produces inconsistent results: TIIF-Short improves slightly (79.2 vs. 78.9), but TIIF-Long degrades (78.7 vs. 79.5) and GenEval degrades (84.1 vs. 85.0). The paper sets both coefficients to zero. This is a non-obvious finding: in standard RLHF, KL regularization is crucial for preventing reward over-optimization. Its ineffectiveness here may indicate that (a) the 380-step training horizon is too short for significant policy drift, (b) the group-relative advantage normalization provides implicit regularization by zero-centering advantages, or (c) the shared backbone parameters between AR and flow components constrain the policy in ways that make explicit KL penalties redundant.

**Inference-time SRR on the untrained Bagel baseline (Table 12):** Applying inference-time self-reflective refinement directly to the Bagel baseline (without AlphaGRPO training) yields only modest gains: TIIF-Short improves from 75.2 to 77.47 (+2.27), TIIF-Long drops slightly from 78.6 to 78.45 (-0.15), and GenEval improves from 84.0 to 86.3 (+2.3). In contrast, AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR achieves substantially larger gains on TIIF-Short (79.1 → 83.9, +4.8) and TIIF-Long (79.5 → 83.2, +3.7), and comparable gain on GenEval (84.2 → 88.2, +4.0). This is a critical ablation: it demonstrates that the Inf. SRR procedure alone provides some benefit (the model's base visual understanding allows it to identify and fix some errors even without RL training), but AlphaGRPO training significantly amplifies this capability. The paper states this indicates "AlphaGRPO improves the model's self-reflection behavior rather than relying solely on the inference-time refinement procedure."

**Reward model on additional metrics (Table 10):** Evaluated on 1K real-user prompts from Pick-a-Pic using AestheticScore, CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021), and PickScore (Kirstain et al., 2023) — none of which are used as training rewards — AlphaGRPO achieves 5.9624 vs. Bagel's 5.9583 on AestheticScore (+0.0041), 0.9102 vs. 0.8984 on CLIPScore (+0.0118), and 0.8246 vs. 0.8208 on PickScore (+0.0038). The gains are small but consistent, suggesting the DVReward-trained model's improvements are detectable by independent metrics that were not optimized during training. However, the magnitudes are very modest compared to the benchmark gains, raising a question about whether the benchmark improvements partially reflect DVReward-aligned evaluation rather than genuine quality improvement.

**Human evaluation (Table 11):** On 200 real-user prompts, three annotators compared Bagel vs. AlphaGRPO pairs. AlphaGRPO wins 40.5% vs. Bagel's 30.5% on overall preference (29.0% ties), 36.5% vs. 25.5% on prompt following (38.0% ties), and 43.0% vs. 39.0% on perceptual quality (18.0% ties). The paper also reports that DVReward selects AlphaGRPO 51.3% vs. Bagel 34.7% (14.1% ties) in pairwise comparisons, suggesting DVReward's training signal is broadly aligned with human preference, though the alignment is not perfect — humans show a less decisive preference than DVReward (40.5% AlphaGRPO win vs. 51.3% DVReward selection). No inter-annotator agreement metrics are reported, which is a limitation for interpreting the reliability of the human evaluation results.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "AlphaGRPO yields robust improvements across multimodal generation benchmarks."** The benchmark results in Tables 1, 18-21 provide consistent evidence that AlphaGRPO improves over the Bagel baseline across GenEval, TIIF-Bench, DPG-Bench, and WISE. The gains are not uniform — some metrics show substantial improvement (GenEval Color Attribution +7.7 with Inf. SRR at 512 resolution, TIIF-Bench Text rendering +13.1 with RT2I training), while others are essentially flat (WISE Cultural 0.76 → 0.75 with Self-CoT, DPG-Bench Global 88.94 → 85.50 with Inf. SRR). The claim of "robust" improvement is supported in aggregate but qualified by these uneven sub-metric patterns. A missing analysis is the statistical significance of these gains: without confidence intervals or error bars, it is impossible to determine whether a 0.1-point WISE improvement or a 0.6-point DPG-Bench Entity improvement is meaningful or noise. The 500-question GenEval test set and the undisclosed TIIF-Bench test-mini set sizes provide some statistical power, but the lack of variance reporting is a genuine weakness.

A potentially concerning pattern emerges in the DPG-Bench Global metric, where AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR at 512 resolution drops from 88.94 (Bagel) to 85.50, a decline of -3.44. This is the only metric where AlphaGRPO consistently underperforms the baseline, and it evaluates "global" image coherence — the overall composition, harmony, and structural integrity of the image. The paper does not discuss this degradation. It may indicate a tradeoff: the RL optimization, driven by DVReward's atomic question verification, encourages the model to satisfy individual constraints (correct attributes, correct counts, correct spatial relations) at the expense of holistic image quality. This would be a significant limitation for real-world deployment, where users care about overall image appeal as much as constraint satisfaction.

**Claim 2: "AlphaGRPO unlocks self-reflective refinement, where the model autonomously diagnoses and corrects misalignments."** The Inf. SRR results provide evidence for this claim, with the ablation in Table 12 being the critical test. The untrained Bagel baseline achieves only modest gains from Inf. SRR (+2.27 on TIIF-Short, -0.15 on TIIF-Long, +2.3 on GenEval), while AlphaGRPO-trained models achieve substantially larger gains (+4.81, +3.70, +4.0). This demonstrates that AlphaGRPO training genuinely improves the model's self-refinement capability beyond what the base model can do with the same inference procedure. The qualitative examples in Figure 8 illustrate the mechanism: the model corrects specific attribute leakage (fixing a figure's clothing color) and object fusion (separating merged pizzas). However, the claim of "autonomous" diagnosis is somewhat overstated — the model is explicitly placed in a refinement context (receiving its own previous output as input with instructions to improve it). True autonomous self-correction, where the model spontaneously detects errors without being told that errors exist, is not demonstrated. The pilot study's confirmation bias (Figure 2) was identified as the problem to solve; whether AlphaGRPO has actually overcome this bias (i.e., can the trained model now correctly identify errors in verification mode, not just reflection mode?) is not tested.

**Claim 3: "AlphaGRPO achieves this without an additional cold-start SFT stage."** This is the paper's most distinctive claim. The training procedure described in Section 5.1 and Appendix A.2 confirms that no cold-start SFT is used: "We do not employ a cold start phase before AlphaGRPO training." The positive benchmark results demonstrate that RL alone can improve a pretrained UMM's multimodal generation capabilities. However, several caveats apply. First, the paper does not compare against a version that *does* include cold-start SFT, so the claim is about sufficiency (RL alone can work) rather than optimality (RL alone is better than RL+SFT). Second, the base model (Bagel) was itself trained on large-scale interleaved data that may include reasoning-like trajectories — the distinction between "pretraining" and "cold-start SFT" is blurry when the pretraining data already contains examples of the target behavior. Third, the LoRA fine-tuning setup means only a small fraction of parameters are updated; it is possible that a cold-start SFT on the full model would unlock capabilities that LoRA-based RL cannot, and this comparison is absent.

**Claim 4: "DVReward provides stable, interpretable supervision signals for multimodal generation GRPO training."** The reward model comparison (Table 3) and ablations (Tables 4, 6, 9) support the claim that DVReward outperforms the tested alternatives (PickScore, VIEScore, HPSv3, UnifiedReward) on the evaluated benchmarks. The "stable" aspect is supported by DVReward being the only reward model that improves all three metrics (TIIF-Short, TIIF-Long, GenEval) on Bagel without degradation. The "interpretable" aspect is demonstrated by the per-dimension question decomposition, which allows inspection of which semantic or quality dimensions are failing. However, several aspects of the claim are not fully tested. The comparison omits an important baseline: DVReward using a fine-tuned MLLM verifier. This would test whether the benefit comes from decomposition (the paper's claim) or from using an unfine-tuned generalist MLLM. If a fine-tuned MLLM with DVReward's decomposition also works well, the decomposition is the key insight; if it fails, the generalist MLLM is the key. Neither experiment is run. Additionally, the efficiency analysis (Appendix A.4) focuses entirely on reducing the "bubble time" — the idle GPU time waiting for rewards — which is reduced to near zero through asynchronous scheduling, but the total computational cost of DVReward (MLLM verifier forward passes per question per image) is not compared to the cost of simpler reward models. A reward model that is 10× better but 100× more expensive to compute may not be practically preferable. The paper does not provide this cost-benefit analysis.

**What experiments would have strengthened the paper:**

- **A direct cold-start SFT comparison.** Training AlphaGRPO with and without a cold-start SFT phase on the same base model would directly test the sufficiency claim. Without this, the reader cannot know whether cold-start SFT would further improve results.
- **Statistical significance reporting.** Confidence intervals or standard deviations across multiple training runs would allow assessment of whether the observed gains (particularly the small ones, like +0.1 on GenEval from quality questions) are reliable.
- **Ablation on the geometric mean aggregation.** The paper uses geometric mean of semantic and quality scores without comparing to arithmetic mean, harmonic mean, or learned weighting. Given the importance of the reward aggregation to the training signal, this is a notable omission.
- **Evaluation of the trained model's verification capability.** The pilot study (Figure 2) identified confirmation bias as the problem. Does the AlphaGRPO-trained model still exhibit confirmation bias when asked to verify (rather than reflect on) generated images? This would directly test whether the training has generalized beyond the specific SRR task format.
- **Training horizon ablation.** The paper trains for only 380 steps. An ablation showing performance as a function of training steps would reveal whether the model is still improving (suggesting undertraining), has plateaued (suggesting saturation), or has begun to degrade (suggesting over-optimization). This is standard practice in RL training and its absence is a significant gap.
- **Cross-model generalization of DVReward.** DVReward is tested on SD3.5M and Bagel, but not on additional UMM architectures. The claim that DVReward is a general solution for multimodal generation RL would be stronger with evidence across diverse base models.
- **Latency and throughput comparison for reward models.** The paper provides detailed efficiency analysis only for DVReward's serving optimizations. A comparison of end-to-end training throughput (steps per hour) for DVReward versus PickScore versus VIEScore would contextualize the practical tradeoffs.

**Missing baselines in the main benchmark comparisons.** Table 1 compares against numerous open-source and closed-source models, which is comprehensive for positioning AlphaGRPO in the broader field. However, for evaluating the specific claim that AlphaGRPO's training paradigm is effective, the most important baselines are missing: (1) Bagel + standard SFT on the same 19,500-prompt training set (to test whether supervised fine-tuning alone achieves similar gains), (2) Bagel + GRPO with a simpler reward model like PickScore (to test whether GRPO or DVReward is responsible for the gains — partially addressed in Table 3, but those reward models are tested on different base models at different training configurations), and (3) Bagel + inference-time SRR without AlphaGRPO training but with more SRR iterations (to test whether simply running more refinement steps on the base model could match the trained model's performance).

**The generalization claim deserves scrutiny.** The paper emphasizes that AlphaGRPO trained on SRR improves editing performance (GEdit-Bench) despite no editing data in training. The +0.56 overall improvement is real, but the absolute score (7.08) still trails GPT-4o (7.53) and is only modestly ahead of the Bagel baseline (6.52). The qualitative examples in Figure 9 are compelling for specific cases but represent cherry-picked successes — the benchmark-level improvement of +0.56 suggests that on average, the editing capability transfer is modest. The paper would be strengthened by an analysis of *which types* of edits benefit from SRR training (e.g., attribute changes vs. spatial rearrangements vs. style transfers) and which do not, to characterize the transfer more precisely than aggregate scores allow.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Cold-Start Independence Claim Is Not Directly Tested Against an SFT Baseline

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's most prominent claim is that RL alone, without a cold-start supervised fine-tuning stage on teacher-distilled data, can activate latent reasoning capabilities in pretrained UMMs. The paper explicitly states:

> "In contrast, we argue that since unified models already acquire fundamental primitives and implicit reasoning-related data through large-scale pretraining, it is possible to activate and enhance these dormant capabilities using RL without the cold-start stage."

However, the training setup uses LoRA fine-tuning (rank 32, alpha 64) on top of the Bagel model, which was itself pretrained on "large-scale interleaved multimodal data" (Section 5.1). The distinction between capabilities acquired during Bagel's pretraining (which may already include reasoning-like or self-correction trajectories in the interleaved data) and capabilities that would be injected by a dedicated cold-start SFT stage is blurred. The paper never trains a comparison model that *does* include a cold-start SFT phase on the same training data, making the claim about sufficiency (RL alone is enough) rather than optimality (RL alone is better, or even competitive with, RL+SFT).

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot determine from the reported results whether adding a cold-start SFT phase before AlphaGRPO training would yield further improvements, or whether the RL gains reported are a lower bound on what a combined SFT+RL pipeline could achieve. If Bagel's pretraining data already contained the relevant reasoning patterns and AlphaGRPO is essentially doing RL fine-tuning on top of an already-capable model, the distinction between "no cold-start" and "cold-start already happened during pretraining" is more rhetorical than methodological. The claim of activation-without-injection would be stronger if the base model were a UMM trained on data that *demonstrably lacks* self-reflective reasoning examples — but Bagel's training data composition is not analyzed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** There is no ablation comparing AlphaGRPO with vs. without a cold-start SFT stage. There is no analysis of Bagel's pretraining data to establish whether self-reflective or reasoning trajectories were present. There is no experiment applying AlphaGRPO to a UMM known to lack reasoning primitives in its pretraining data. The only evidence supporting the cold-start independence claim is the positive benchmark results themselves, which are equally consistent with the hypothesis that Bagel's pretraining already provided the necessary primitives (making AlphaGRPO a fine-tuning step on an already-capable model) as with the hypothesis that RL created new capabilities from scratch. The paper acknowledges in Appendix A.1 that the base model "occasionally generates noisy or blurry artifacts" and outputs "unexpected reasoning patterns during self-reflection," suggesting that base model quality constrains what RL can achieve, but this does not isolate the cold-start SFT question.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The claim stands as stated, without a direct empirical test against the paradigm it critiques. A comparison to Bagel + SFT on the same 19,500-prompt dataset would be the minimal experiment needed to support the sufficiency claim, but it is not conducted. The related work section (Section 6) critiques prior approaches for relying on cold-start SFT, but the paper's own results do not demonstrate that RL alone matches or exceeds what RL+SFT would achieve on the same base model.

### Difficulty Estimation and Adaptive Allocation Are Entirely Absent, Making the Approach Uniform Rather Than Compute-Optimal

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper applies AlphaGRPO uniformly to all training prompts — every prompt receives the same group size (G=14), the same number of training trajectories, and the same reward computation budget. At inference time, Inf. SRR is applied uniformly to all test prompts, doubling the generation cost regardless of whether the initial image was already correct. There is no mechanism for estimating whether a particular prompt is easy (the base model already generates it correctly, so SRR is unnecessary), medium (SRR might help), or hard (SRR is unlikely to help because the base model produces fundamentally wrong images). This is a striking contrast to the reference example paper, which demonstrated that difficulty-conditioned allocation yields 4× efficiency gains over uniform best-of-N.

The paper does not claim to address difficulty estimation or adaptive allocation — these are not promised and then undelivered. However, the absence is consequential because it means AlphaGRPO expends the same compute budget on prompts where SRR provides no benefit as on prompts where it provides substantial gains. The Inf. SRR results in Table 1 show large aggregate improvements but conceal the per-prompt distribution: some prompts may improve dramatically, others not at all, and a subset may even degrade (the FPR mechanism prevents rewarding degradation during training, but at inference time, there is no comparable safeguard — the model can still produce a worse refined image than its initial generation, and the paper's human evaluation shows AlphaGRPO loses to Bagel in 30.5% of overall preference comparisons in Table 11).

**The consequence.** In a production deployment, applying Inf. SRR uniformly would waste substantial compute on prompts where the initial generation is already correct (easy prompts) and on prompts where the base model's capabilities are fundamentally insufficient (hard prompts). The paper does not characterize what fraction of benchmark prompts benefit from SRR, what fraction are unchanged, and what fraction degrade. Without this analysis, a practitioner cannot make informed decisions about when to apply SRR versus when to accept the initial generation and save compute. The 2× inference cost of Inf. SRR (doubling diffusion steps) is reported implicitly (40 steps for initial generation + 40 steps for refinement) but the cost-benefit ratio — improvement per unit compute — is never computed or discussed.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 12 provides the closest thing to a difficulty-dependent analysis: applying Inf. SRR to the untrained Bagel baseline yields gains of +2.27 on TIIF-Short, -0.15 on TIIF-Long, and +2.3 on GenEval. The fact that TIIF-Long actually degrades suggests that SRR can hurt performance on certain prompt types, even with a trained model (the trained model's Inf. SRR improves TIIF-Long by +3.7, but this is a net improvement — the paper does not disaggregate how many individual long prompts improve vs. degrade). The human evaluation (Table 11) shows AlphaGRPO losing to Bagel in 30.5% of overall comparisons and 25.5% of prompt-following comparisons, indicating that regression at inference time is a non-trivial problem. The per-benchmark sub-metric breakdowns (Tables 18-21) reveal uneven gains: GenEval Position improves by only 4.0 points with Inf. SRR (69.8 → 73.8) while Color Attribution improves by 8.2 points (73.3 → 81.5), suggesting SRR is differentially effective across constraint types. However, there is no per-prompt difficulty binning, no analysis of gain distribution, and no attempt to predict which prompts will benefit from SRR.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to address this limitation. The FPR mechanism operates during training to prevent rewarding regression, but there is no inference-time guard against producing worse refinements. The paper suggests (Section 4.1) that the model is trained to diagnose and correct errors, but it does not develop a mechanism for the model to decide *whether* refinement is needed — the SRR procedure is always applied when Inf. SRR is enabled. This is a significant practical gap for deployment, where cost-conscious practitioners would want to skip refinement on already-correct outputs.

### DVReward's Computational Overhead Is Quantified for Latency but Not for Total Training Cost

**The assumption or constraint.** DVReward requires running an MLLM verifier (Qwen3VL-30B-A3B) on every generated image for every atomic verification question. The paper reports that the average prompt generates 10-20 questions (Figure 7), with some prompts generating 30+ questions. Each training step processes 32 prompts with G=14 trajectories each, yielding 448 images per step. At 20 questions per image, this is 8,960 MLLM verification calls per training step, each requiring at minimum one forward pass through a 3B-active-parameter model (though with KV-cache reuse across questions for the same image, reducing the per-question overhead to approximately 20 prefill tokens and one decoded token).

The paper devotes Appendix A.4 to demonstrating that the "reward-waiting bubble time" — GPU idle time while waiting for verification — can be reduced from 40.8 seconds to 9.72 × 10⁻⁶ seconds through decentralized asynchronous serving (Table 14, Table 15). This is an engineering achievement, but it measures **latency hiding**, not **total computational cost**. The MLLM verifier still consumes GPU compute, memory bandwidth, and energy for every verification call. That this compute is overlapped with training and thus hidden from the critical path does not make it free — it consumes 1 out of every 8 GPUs in the training cluster (1 GPU per node dedicated to reward serving) and incurs the full inference cost of running Qwen3VL-30B-A3B on ~170,000 images over the course of training.

**The consequence.** A practitioner attempting to replicate AlphaGRPO training must provision additional GPU hardware specifically for reward computation — the paper's setup uses a 1:7 ratio of reward GPUs to training GPUs, meaning approximately 12.5% of total cluster compute is dedicated to DVReward verification. For a training run on 64 GPUs, this represents 8 GPUs that could otherwise be used for larger batch sizes, more training steps, or faster experimentation. The paper does not report total training wall-clock time or the total FLOPs consumed by DVReward verification, making it impossible to compare the end-to-end computational efficiency of AlphaGRPO against alternative approaches (e.g., training with a lightweight scalar reward model like PickScore, which requires only a CLIP forward pass per image and would consume negligible verification compute). The comparison to PickScore in Table 15 shows only "bubble time" (PickScore: 0.042 seconds synchronous, DVReward: 9.72 × 10⁻⁶ seconds asynchronous), not total verification cost. PickScore's synchronous bubble time is small (42 milliseconds) because it is computationally cheap — the actual verification latency is low enough that hiding it provides minimal benefit. DVReward's asynchronous scheduling hides a much larger latency at the cost of substantial additional compute.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 14 shows bubble times for different serving configurations, isolating the scheduling overhead but not the verification compute. Appendix A.4 describes the efficiency optimizations in detail: KV-cache reuse across questions for the same image, high-throughput serving with SGLang (over 4K prefill tokens/second and over 500 concurrent requests on one A100), decentralized per-node reward servers, and asynchronous scheduling. These are genuine engineering contributions but they optimize the *interface* between training and verification, not the verification itself. The paper does not report: total FLOPs consumed by DVReward verification over the 380-step training run, the throughput of the reward servers in images verified per second, the ratio of verification FLOPs to training FLOPs, or the marginal cost of adding more verification questions (the ablation in Table 9 shows that using fewer questions reduces GenEval performance, creating a direct tradeoff between verification cost and reward quality that is not cost-quantified).

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses the practical concern by demonstrating that verification can be overlapped with training to near-perfect hiding, but does not address the fundamental cost question. The 1:7 GPU ratio is presented as the configuration used, not as a cost to be minimized or optimized. Future work on reducing the number of verification questions (perhaps through learned question selection rather than the naive merging ablation in Table 9) or distilling the MLLM verifier into a smaller, specialized model could reduce this overhead, but no such work is attempted. The paper's framing of the efficiency analysis as "reward-waiting bubble time" rather than "total verification cost" is somewhat misleading — it suggests that DVReward's overhead has been eliminated when in fact it has been merely hidden from the training critical path at the cost of dedicated hardware.

### The Training Horizon Is Fixed at 380 Steps Without Justification or Saturation Analysis

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper trains AlphaGRPO for exactly 380 steps, with the update minibatch size equal to the rollout batch size (no mini-batching, no multiple epochs over the rollout data). The total number of images generated during training is 380 × 32 × 14 = 170,240, which represents approximately 62% of the 19,500-prompt training set in a single pass (each prompt is seen at most once, some never). The paper does not provide a learning curve showing performance on downstream benchmarks as a function of training steps, does not report whether training was stopped because performance plateaued or because of computational budget constraints, and does not ablate the training horizon.

> "We only train 380 steps with update minibatch size equals the rollout batch size." (Appendix A.2)

The word "only" suggests this is acknowledged as a short training horizon, but no justification is provided. The KL regularization ablation (Table 13) shows that adding KL penalties (β=0.001) degrades GenEval (85.0 → 84.1), which could be interpreted as evidence that the policy has not drifted far enough from the reference for KL regularization to be beneficial — but it could equally be evidence that 380 steps is too few for drift to occur, and that longer training would both benefit from KL regularization and potentially yield further improvements.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot determine whether the reported results represent convergence (the model has extracted all available signal from the DVReward reward and further training would not help), under-training (the model is still improving and longer training would yield further gains), or incipient overfitting (the model is about to start over-optimizing the DVReward signal at the expense of true quality, and longer training would cause degradation). The choice of 380 steps is critical for reproducibility and for understanding the method's potential: if the model is under-trained, the reported benchmark results are lower bounds and AlphaGRPO is more capable than demonstrated. If the model is near convergence, the results are representative of what the method can achieve. If the model is beginning to overfit, the results are near-optimal and longer training would be counterproductive. The paper provides no evidence to distinguish among these scenarios.

The single-epoch, no-mini-batching training regime is also unusual in deep RL. Standard PPO implementations perform multiple epochs of updates on each batch of rollout data (typically 3-5 epochs with minibatching) to improve sample efficiency. The paper's "update minibatch size equals the rollout batch size" means the policy is updated exactly once per batch of rollout data — the most conservative possible on-policy regime. This may contribute to the short training horizon (the model learns slowly because it reuses data minimally) and may explain why KL regularization is unnecessary (the policy changes very little per step because it sees each batch only once). Whether a more standard PPO training regime (multiple epochs, mini-batching, more total steps) would improve results is unknown.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no learning curves, no training step ablation, and no discussion of why 380 steps was chosen. The KL ablation in Table 13 hints at the training dynamics (KL=0.001 underperforms KL=0, suggesting either implicit regularization is sufficient or training is too short for drift to matter), but this is indirect. The human evaluation (Table 11) and the additional reward metric evaluation (Table 10) provide some confidence that the 380-step model has not catastrophically overfit (it outperforms Bagel on metrics not used in training), but they do not establish that training has converged.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The training horizon is stated as a fixed parameter without justification, and no analysis of training dynamics is provided. This is a significant gap for a paper that introduces a new training paradigm — understanding whether and when the training converges is fundamental to assessing the method's practical utility. The paper does not suggest future work on training horizon optimization or convergence analysis.

### The Generalization Evidence Is Limited to a Single Model Family, a Single Training Data Distribution, and Benchmarks That May Overlap with DVReward's Evaluation Priorities

**The assumption or constraint.** All AlphaGRPO experiments are conducted on a single base model (Bagel), trained on a single synthetically generated dataset (19,500 prompts from a "Primitive-to-Prompt" pipeline), and evaluated on a set of standard benchmarks that share evaluation priorities with DVReward's decomposition dimensions. The paper compares against numerous other models on these benchmarks (Tables 1, 2, 18-21), but these comparisons establish AlphaGRPO's position in the broader field, not the generalizability of the training method across model architectures, training data distributions, or evaluation criteria.

The training data is generated using the same taxonomy as TIIF-Bench (Wei et al., 2025), one of the primary evaluation benchmarks. The paper states that it "follows the taxonomy of TIIF-Bench to define 39 distinct compositional tasks" (Section 4.3). This creates a potential overlap between the training distribution and the evaluation distribution that is not fully characterized — while the individual prompts are different, the *types* of compositional challenges (spatial reasoning, attribute binding, counting, etc.) are shared, potentially inflating TIIF-Bench performance relative to a model trained on a genuinely out-of-distribution prompt set. The paper's emphasis on generalization ("our primary objective is to assess the generalization capability of the model on standard downstream benchmarks," Section 5.2) would be stronger if the training data taxonomy were independent of the evaluation benchmark taxonomy.

Additionally, DVReward's decomposition dimensions (10 semantic types, 8 quality types, Tables 16-17) substantially overlap with the evaluation dimensions used by GenEval (object existence, counting, colors, spatial position, attribute binding) and TIIF-Bench (similar categories plus text rendering, style, etc.). While DVReward is not used as the evaluation metric for these benchmarks (GPT-4.1 or benchmark-specific evaluators are used instead), the training reward signal prioritizes the same types of constraints that the benchmarks measure. The additional reward metric evaluation in Table 10 attempts to address this by using AestheticScore, CLIPScore, and PickScore — metrics not used during training — but the gains on these metrics are very small (+0.0041 on AestheticScore, +0.0118 on CLIPScore, +0.0038 on PickScore), raising the possibility that the larger benchmark gains partially reflect alignment between DVReward's training priorities and the benchmarks' evaluation priorities rather than a general improvement in image quality.

**The consequence.** A practitioner applying AlphaGRPO to a different UMM architecture, a different training data distribution, or a deployment scenario where user preferences differ from DVReward's decomposition priorities cannot predict whether the method will transfer. The Bagel model uses a specific AR-Diffusion architecture; whether the unified trajectory optimization and joint AR-flow GRPO update would work on composite UMMs (which connect separate LLM and diffusion transformer modules via adapters, potentially limiting gradient flow between components) is unknown. The training data is synthetically generated with a specific difficulty distribution (3:5:2 Easy:Medium:Hard); whether training on naturally occurring prompts (e.g., web-scraped user prompts, which may have different compositional complexity distributions) would produce comparable results is unknown.

The small gains on out-of-distribution reward metrics (Table 10) are particularly concerning. If AlphaGRPO's primary effect is to align the model's outputs more closely with DVReward's evaluation criteria (which happen to overlap with standard benchmark criteria), then the method may not produce genuine quality improvements that generalize to metrics or user populations with different priorities. The human evaluation (Table 11) partially addresses this — humans prefer AlphaGRPO over Bagel 40.5% vs. 30.5% overall — but the preference ratio is modest (less than 2:1 excluding ties) and the inter-annotator agreement is not reported, making it difficult to assess how reliably this preference would replicate.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The training data construction section (4.3) acknowledges the TIIF-Bench taxonomy as the basis for the 39 task types. The benchmark evaluations (Tables 1, 18-21) show consistent improvements over Bagel, but the overlap between training taxonomy and evaluation taxonomy is not analyzed. The additional reward metric evaluation (Table 10) shows small but consistent gains on out-of-distribution metrics, providing some evidence of generalization, but the magnitudes are much smaller than the benchmark gains. The editing benchmark results (Table 2) provide the strongest generalization evidence — AlphaGRPO improves on GEdit-Bench (+0.56 overall) despite no editing data in training — but this is a single additional benchmark and the improvement is modest in absolute terms (7.08 vs. GPT-4o's 7.53).

The paper does not apply AlphaGRPO to any UMM other than Bagel, does not test on a training dataset constructed independently of the evaluation benchmark taxonomies, and does not analyze the overlap between DVReward's decomposition dimensions and the benchmarks' evaluation dimensions. The human evaluation uses only 200 prompts from Pick-a-Pic with three annotators and no reported agreement statistics.

**Mitigation status.** The paper partially addresses generalization by evaluating on multiple diverse benchmarks (GenEval, TIIF-Bench, DPG-Bench, WISE, GEdit-Bench) and by testing on additional reward metrics (Table 10) and human evaluation (Table 11). The editing benchmark result (GEdit-Bench, Table 2) is the strongest evidence for cross-task generalization, since the model was not trained on editing instructions. However, the single-model, single-training-distribution scope remains a significant limitation. The paper does not suggest future work on cross-model or cross-distribution generalization, focusing instead on improving the specific components (process rewards, consistency rewards, reinforcement fine-tuning for stability) within the established Bagel + synthetic data paradigm (Appendix A.1).

### The Self-Reflective Refinement Mechanism Has No Guard Against Production of Worse Outputs at Inference Time, and the Regression Rate Is Not Measured

**The assumption or constraint.** The False-Positive Rectification (FPR) mechanism (Section 4.1) prevents the training process from rewarding refinement trajectories that produce images worse than the initial input, by assigning such trajectories the group minimum reward and thus forcing negative advantages. This is an effective training-time guard: the model cannot learn to prefer degradation over improvement because degradation is never positively reinforced.

However, **at inference time, there is no equivalent guard**. When Inf. SRR is applied to a test prompt, the model generates an initial image, then applies the self-reflective refinement procedure to produce a refined image. If the refined image is worse than the initial image — due to the model misdiagnosing a non-existent error, overcorrecting a minor issue, or hallucinating a problem — the system has no mechanism to detect this regression and fall back to the initial image. The refined output is returned as the final result regardless of whether it improved upon or degraded the initial generation.

This is a direct consequence of the training setup: the model was trained on examples where the initial image was deliberately the worst in its group (Section 5.1: "the initial image is fixed as the sample with the lowest reward in the group"), meaning the training distribution guaranteed that refinement was beneficial (there was always substantial room for improvement). At inference time, the initial image may already be correct or near-correct, and the model has no training experience with the scenario where refinement is unnecessary. The model's default behavior, shaped by training on guaranteed-improvable examples, may be to always find something to "fix," leading to unnecessary or harmful modifications.

**The consequence.** Users of a deployed AlphaGRPO system with Inf. SRR enabled cannot trust that the refined output is better than the initial output. For prompts where the initial generation is correct, applying SRR may introduce errors — the model may "fix" a correctly rendered attribute to an incorrect one, or degrade visual quality in an attempt to improve a non-existent flaw. The paper's human evaluation (Table 11) provides evidence for this failure mode: AlphaGRPO loses to Bagel in 30.5% of overall preference comparisons, and this evaluation uses AlphaGRPO without Inf. SRR. With Inf. SRR enabled, the regression rate might be higher because each refinement step is an opportunity for error introduction.

The paper's pilot study (Figure 2) identified a "confirmation bias" where the base model asserts incorrect outputs are correct. AlphaGRPO training on SRR may inadvertently create the opposite bias — an "overcorrection bias" where the model always assumes errors exist and attempts to fix them, even when the input is correct. This bias would not be penalized during training (because training inputs are guaranteed to contain errors) and would only manifest at inference time on already-correct inputs. The paper does not test for this bias and does not measure the per-prompt regression rate of Inf. SRR.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 12 provides indirect evidence: applying Inf. SRR to the untrained Bagel baseline produces a mix of improvements and degradations (TIIF-Short +2.27, TIIF-Long -0.15, GenEval +2.3). The trained AlphaGRPO model shows net improvements across all metrics, but the paper does not disaggregate how many individual prompts improved vs. degraded. The human evaluation (Table 11) shows that even without Inf. SRR, AlphaGRPO underperforms Bagel in a substantial fraction of cases (30.5% overall Bagel win rate). The qualitative examples in Figure 8 show successful refinements, but these are selected to demonstrate the method's strengths, not to characterize the failure distribution. The paper does not report: the fraction of test prompts where Inf. SRR improves vs. degrades output, the distribution of score changes from initial to refined outputs, or any analysis of prompts where the initial output is already high-quality (e.g., would be scored above some threshold by DVReward) and SRR is therefore likely unnecessary.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation. The FPR mechanism solves the training-time problem (preventing the policy from learning to produce worse outputs) but the inference-time problem (preventing the trained policy from producing worse outputs when refinement is unnecessary) is unaddressed. A simple mitigation — comparing the initial and refined outputs under DVReward and returning the higher-scored one — would add a small inference cost (one additional DVReward call for the initial image) and guarantee that SRR never reduces output quality. This is not implemented or suggested. The paper's future work discussion (Appendix A.1) suggests incorporating consistency rewards and intermediate supervision for the SRR process, but does not explicitly address the inference-time regression problem.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **methodological reframing** rather than a paradigm shift. It does not propose a fundamentally new architecture or training algorithm — GRPO, AR-Diffusion models, and MLLM-based evaluation all predate AlphaGRPO. What it changes is the **narrative about what is necessary to elicit complex behaviors from unified multimodal models** and **how to construct reward signals that survive the transition from narrow, training-aligned metrics to diverse downstream evaluation**.

The central reframing is the **cold-start independence hypothesis**: that pretrained UMMs already possess the visual understanding and reasoning primitives for self-critique and intent inference, and that RL alone — without supervised fine-tuning on teacher-distilled reasoning trajectories — can activate these capabilities. Whether this hypothesis holds in general (the evidence is limited to one model, one training distribution, and one training horizon) is less important than the shift in research framing it enables. Before AlphaGRPO, the default assumption in the UMM alignment literature was that complex behaviors like self-reflective refinement required injecting capabilities from stronger teacher models via SFT distillation (Deng et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b; Huang et al., 2025b). After AlphaGRPO, the null hypothesis has changed: a researcher proposing a new UMM alignment method must now argue why cold-start SFT is necessary, rather than assuming it as a prerequisite. This is not because AlphaGRPO definitively proved cold-start SFT is unnecessary across all settings — the paper's own limitations (Section 6 of this analysis) acknowledge the narrow evidential base — but because it demonstrated sufficiency in a concrete, reproducible setting, shifting the burden of proof.

The second contribution with landscape-changing potential is the **decompositional verification diagnostic**. The pilot study (Figure 3) provides a clean, replicable demonstration that holistic scalar rewards from MLLMs fail not because MLLMs lack fine-grained visual understanding, but because the scalar elicitation format discards discriminative information that the model demonstrably possesses. This is a **diagnostic finding** with broad implications: it suggests that many reported failures of MLLMs as evaluators for visual RL may be interface failures rather than capability failures. If the finding generalizes — and the ablation in Table 9 showing that coarse question merging degrades compositional reasoning metrics supports its specificity — then the entire subfield of MLLM-based reward modeling should reconsider whether fine-tuning MLLMs on preference data (HPSv3, UnifiedReward) addresses the right problem. The paper's Table 3 provides evidence that fine-tuned MLLM reward models *degrade* performance on certain benchmarks compared to the unfine-tuned DVReward approach, suggesting that preservation of generalist MLLM capabilities (through avoiding domain-specific fine-tuning) combined with structured, atomic querying (through decomposition) may be a more robust design pattern than the dominant paradigm of fine-tuning MLLMs into specialized reward models.

**This reframes which research directions become more attractive.** The paper makes **RL-without-cold-start-SFT** a credible baseline for future UMM alignment work, meaning SFT-heavy approaches must now demonstrate that their cold-start stage provides gains beyond what RL alone achieves — a burden that did not exist before. It makes **decompositional reward design** a first-class research direction: rather than asking "how do we train a better scalar reward model?", the framework suggests asking "how do we decompose evaluation into questions that the MLLM can answer discriminatively?" This shifts focus from reward model architecture to query design, a qualitatively different research agenda. It also makes **joint AR-flow optimization in native UMMs** a demonstrated capability, inviting future work to explore what other complex behaviors (beyond SRR and RT2I) can be elicited through unified trajectory GRPO training.

**What becomes less attractive** is the unexamined use of holistic scalar MLLM rewards for open-world visual generation RL. The paper's VIEScore results (Table 3: degrading GenEval from 84.0 to 81.7, degrading TIIF-Long from 73.2 to 72.9 on SD3.5M) provide concrete evidence that scalar elicitation can be actively harmful. Future work that uses scalar MLLM rewards without addressing the discriminability problem identified in Figure 3 must now justify why their specific setting avoids the documented failure mode. Similarly, the finding that fine-tuned MLLM reward models degrade performance on certain benchmarks (HPSv3: GenEval 84.0 → 83.4; UnifiedReward: GenEval 84.0 → 83.7) places a burden on specialized reward model approaches to demonstrate that their domain alignment does not sacrifice open-world evaluation capacity.

**The paper also partially reconciles a tension** between the vision RL literature (which has shown strong results using scalar rewards like PickScore and aesthetic predictors; FlowGRPO, DanceGRPO) and the MLLM evaluation literature (which has documented MLLM unreliability as holistic scorers; VIEScore). The reconciliation is that **both are right** — scalar rewards work adequately when the reward model's training distribution matches the policy's output distribution (e.g., PickScore trained on human preferences over SDXL outputs, used to train SD3.5M), but fail when the policy generates images that differ from the reward model's calibration domain or when the evaluation requires discriminating fine-grained semantic differences that scalar compression obscures. DVReward's decomposition addresses the second failure mode; the first (distribution shift between reward model training and policy output) remains a concern that the paper does not fully resolve (the Qwen3VL-30B-A3B verifier is unfine-tuned but still has its own training distribution that may not perfectly match Bagel's output distribution).

**Limiting the scope of the landscape change.** The paper does **not** establish a new state of the art that renders prior approaches obsolete. The benchmark improvements are consistent but not dramatic — GenEval improves by 4.2 points at 512 resolution with Inf. SRR, and the model still trails GPT-4o on editing tasks (7.08 vs. 7.53 on GEdit-Bench). The training is limited to 380 steps on synthetic data for one model family. The approach does not claim to expand the base model's fundamental capability range — hard compositional problems that the base model cannot solve remain unsolved. The landscape change is therefore **methodological and diagnostic**, not performance-driven: AlphaGRPO opens a new axis of research (RL activation of dormant UMM capabilities without teacher distillation) and provides a diagnostic toolkit (decompositional verification, FPR, unified trajectory optimization) that future work can build on, improve, and stress-test.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Direct cold-start SFT vs. RL-only comparison on a controlled base model.** The paper's central claim — that RL alone suffices to activate dormant capabilities — is asserted but not experimentally tested against the alternative. A decisive follow-up would take a UMM that demonstrably lacks self-reflective reasoning abilities (verified through behavioral probing like the verification/reflection test in Figure 2, applied to a model trained on data known to exclude self-correction trajectories), then compare three conditions on the same AlphaGRPO training data: (a) RL-only (replicating AlphaGRPO), (b) SFT-only on synthetic reasoning trajectories generated by a teacher model, and (c) SFT + RL combined. The dependent variables would include downstream benchmark performance (GenEval, TIIF-Bench, GEdit), the model's verification accuracy (can it now correctly identify errors in verification mode, not just reflection mode?), and the per-prompt gain distribution (does SFT provide gains on prompts where RL-alone fails, or do they improve the same subset?). If RL-alone matches or exceeds SFT-alone, the dormant-capability hypothesis is strongly supported and the cold-start SFT paradigm should be reconsidered. If SFT-alone substantially outperforms RL-alone, the paper's claim is falsified and the field learns that UMM pretraining does not, in fact, provide sufficient primitives — teacher distillation remains necessary. If SFT+RL outperforms both, the field learns that cold-start SFT and RL are complementary rather than redundant, and the most effective paradigm combines both. Any of these outcomes would be informative and would resolve the central tension the paper introduces but does not settle.

**2. Verifier calibration analysis and the limits of confidence scoring.** DVReward's confidence scoring mechanism ($P_{\text{Yes}} / (P_{\text{Yes}} + P_{\text{No}})$) assumes that Qwen3VL-30B-A3B's token logits are well-calibrated for visual verification tasks. The paper does not test this assumption, and the ablation comparing confidence vs. binary scoring (Table 4) shows only modest gains (+0.6 on TIIF-Long, +1.1 on GenEval) — enough to justify the continuous approach but not enough to establish calibration quality. A follow-up study would construct a calibration benchmark: for a diverse set of prompts and generated images, collect human judgments (binary: is this attribute correct?) and compare against DVReward's confidence scores. The key metric is expected calibration error (ECE) — does a confidence score of 0.8 correspond to approximately 80% human agreement? If the verifier is overconfident (scores clustered near 0 or 1), the confidence scoring advantage over binary scoring would be minimal, and the paper's claims about "preserving the model's uncertainty" would be empirically weak. If the verifier is underconfident (scores clustered around 0.5–0.7), the reward signal would have low variance, potentially explaining the modest gains on out-of-distribution metrics (Table 10). If calibration varies systematically across question types — well-calibrated for object existence but poorly calibrated for spatial relations — this would explain the uneven benchmark gains (GenEval Position improves less with Inf. SRR than Color Attribution) and suggest that DVReward should apply per-question-type calibration or weighting. The study would also test whether calibration degrades over the course of GRPO training as the policy's output distribution shifts away from the verifier's training distribution, a potential failure mode that the paper does not monitor.

**3. Training horizon saturation analysis with over-optimization detection.** The paper trains for exactly 380 steps with no learning curve, leaving open whether the model is under-trained, converged, or on the verge of overfitting. A follow-up would run AlphaGRPO for substantially more steps (e.g., 2,000–5,000) and track three quantities per step: (a) the DVReward training reward (to detect if the model is learning to optimize the verifier signal), (b) downstream benchmark performance on a held-out validation set (to detect if training gains generalize or if the model begins to overfit to DVReward's idiosyncrasies), and (c) the KL divergence between the current and reference policies for both AR and flow components (to detect policy drift, even though KL penalties are set to zero in the default configuration). The key question is whether DVReward's decompositional structure makes it more resistant to reward over-optimization than holistic scalar rewards. If the model can train for many steps without benchmark degradation, DVReward's design would be validated as providing a more robust reward signal that does not encourage reward hacking. If benchmark performance peaks and then declines while training reward continues to increase, this would demonstrate a classic over-optimization pattern — even decompositional rewards are gameable — and the field would learn the verifier reliability frontier for this approach. This study would directly inform the practical question of how long to train and whether early stopping on a validation benchmark (rather than on training reward) is necessary.

**4. Process reward integration for self-reflective refinement.** The paper acknowledges (Appendix A.1) that it has "not yet explored process rewards" — DVReward evaluates only the final refined image, not the intermediate reasoning text. A natural extension would add a **consistency reward** that compares the model's diagnostic reasoning text against the actual changes made during refinement. For example, if the reasoning text says "the shadow is in the wrong position" but the refined image changes the object's color instead, a consistency penalty would discourage the model from generating reasoning text that is disconnected from its actual refinement behavior. A more ambitious extension would add **diagnostic accuracy rewards** — given the initial image and the model's reasoning text claiming specific errors exist, an independent verifier (or DVReward applied to specific regions) could check whether those errors are actually present. This would train the model to produce accurate diagnostics rather than plausible-sounding but incorrect error descriptions. The experiment would compare three reward structures: (a) outcome-only (current DVReward on the refined image), (b) outcome + consistency (penalizing mismatch between reasoning and actual changes), and (c) outcome + consistency + diagnostic accuracy (rewarding correct error identification). The dependent variables would include downstream benchmark performance (does better diagnostic reasoning lead to better refinements?), the model's error identification accuracy on a diagnostic benchmark (can the model correctly say what is wrong?), and the per-prompt refinement success rate (does process reward reduce the 30.5% Bagel-win rate from Table 11?). This extension directly addresses the inference-time regression problem discussed in Section 6: if the model can accurately diagnose whether errors exist and what they are, it can learn to refrain from refinement when the initial image is already correct.

**5. Cross-architecture and cross-verifier generalization of DVReward.** DVReward is tested on two models (SD3.5M under FlowGRPO and Bagel under AlphaGRPO) and uses a single verifier (Qwen3VL-30B-A3B). A follow-up study would systematically vary both the base model architecture and the verifier to establish the boundaries of DVReward's effectiveness. For base models: test on a composite UMM (e.g., MetaQuery, BLIP3-o) where the understanding and generation modules are architecturally separated, to determine whether the joint AR-flow optimization is necessary or whether DVReward works equally well when only the diffusion component is updated (as in FlowGRPO on SD3.5M). For verifiers: compare Qwen3VL-30B-A3B against other generalist MLLMs (e.g., LLaVA, InstructBLIP, GPT-4V if API access permits) to test whether the decompositional approach transfers across verifier architectures or whether it depends on specific properties of Qwen3VL's calibration. The key question is whether DVReward is a general recipe (decomposition + generalist MLLM + confidence scoring) or a specific configuration (Qwen3VL-30B-A3B + Qwen3-235B-A22B decomposer + the paper's specific question taxonomy). If the approach transfers robustly, DVReward becomes a standard tool for open-world visual generation RL. If performance is highly sensitive to the verifier choice or the decomposer quality, the approach requires careful per-setting tuning and the paper's claims about generality are overstated.

**6. Inference-time regression detection and adaptive refinement gating.** The paper identifies a critical inference-time failure mode — the model has no mechanism to decide whether refinement is needed and can degrade already-correct outputs — but offers no solution. A direct follow-up would implement and evaluate a **refinement gate**: before returning the refined output, compare the initial and refined images under DVReward (or a cheaper proxy scoring function). If the refined image scores lower, return the initial image instead. This adds one DVReward call per inference (for the initial image, since the refined image must already be scored for the gate to work) and guarantees that Inf. SRR never reduces output quality. The experiment would measure: (a) what fraction of test prompts trigger the gate (i.e., refinement would have degraded quality), (b) whether the gated system outperforms both the baseline (no SRR) and the un-gated SRR (always return refinement) on downstream benchmarks, and (c) whether the gate introduces a selection bias — does it systematically accept refinements that improve DVReward score but not human-judged quality, or vice versa? A more ambitious version would train a lightweight **refinement necessity classifier** (a small model that takes the initial image and prompt as input and predicts whether SRR is likely to help) using the per-prompt gain/loss data from AlphaGRPO evaluation as training labels. If such a classifier achieves reasonable accuracy, it could replace the expensive DVReward comparison and enable cost-effective selective refinement at deployment time. This directly addresses the per-prompt efficiency concern discussed in Section 6 — instead of uniformly doubling inference cost for all prompts, refinement would be applied selectively to the subset where it provides genuine benefit.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**On-device or edge deployment of UMMs with inference-time self-correction.** A UMM deployed on a consumer device (laptop, tablet, phone) cannot run a separate verification model or route uncertain cases to a larger cloud model due to latency and connectivity constraints. The AlphaGRPO + Inf. SRR paradigm offers a **self-contained improvement loop**: the same model that generates the initial image also critiques and refines it, requiring no external verifier at inference time (DVReward is only needed during training). For a prompt where the initial generation has a subtle attribute binding error (the paper's GenEval Color Attribution improves from 73.3 to 81.5 with Inf. SRR at 512 resolution, Table 18), the model can autonomously detect and fix the error using its own internal understanding. The cost is a 2× increase in inference time (40 diffusion steps for initial generation + 40 for refinement), but this is a predictable, self-contained cost that does not require network access or additional model loading. The key enabler is the paper's demonstration that SRR training transfers to generation quality improvements without requiring the SRR task format at inference time — the model trained on SRR generates better images even in single-pass mode (Table 1: AlphaGRPO without Inf. SRR achieves 84.2 GenEval vs. 84.0 for Bagel), meaning the on-device system could use single-pass generation for latency-sensitive applications and Inf. SRR for quality-sensitive ones, switching based on a user toggle or task criticality.

**Cost-efficient batch image generation for training data synthesis.** Organizations that generate large volumes of synthetic images for training downstream models (e.g., generating paired text-image data for vision-language pretraining, or generating negative/contrastive examples for alignment) currently face a quality-cost tradeoff: using a large, expensive model produces higher-quality images but at prohibitive cost, while using a smaller model produces lower-quality images that degrade the downstream model's performance. AlphaGRPO offers a middle path: train a medium-sized UMM with AlphaGRPO on a representative prompt distribution, then deploy it with **selective Inf. SRR** for batch generation. The refinement gate (see Follow-Up Research #6) would identify prompts where the initial generation likely contains errors (based on DVReward scoring of a small pilot batch) and apply SRR only to those prompts. The paper's results suggest that the largest gains from Inf. SRR occur on prompts requiring precise attribute binding and counting (GenEval Color Attribution +8.2, Counting +7.8 at 512 resolution with Inf. SRR, Table 18), while easier prompts see smaller or negligible gains. By concentrating the 2× inference cost on the subset of prompts where it matters most, the batch generation pipeline achieves near-large-model quality at a fraction of the cost. The paper's 4× efficiency figure from the reference example (compute-optimal test-time scaling) does not directly apply here since AlphaGRPO does not implement difficulty-adaptive allocation, but the principle — not all prompts benefit equally from additional compute — is directly supported by the uneven benchmark gains in Tables 18-21.

**Interactive image editing and iterative refinement for creative tools.** Creative applications (digital art, advertising mockups, architectural visualization) require users to iteratively refine generated images through natural language instructions — "make the lighting warmer," "move the sofa to the left," "change the cat's collar to a bell." Current systems either treat each instruction as an independent generation task (losing context from previous iterations and potentially altering unrelated aspects of the image) or require specialized editing architectures that are separate from the generation model. AlphaGRPO's SRR training, which teaches the model to take an initial image and a critique (the original prompt, implicitly serving as a correction instruction) and produce a refined output that preserves identity while fixing specific attributes, directly maps onto the iterative editing workflow. The GEdit-Bench results (Table 2: +0.56 overall improvement over Bagel without editing-specific training) provide preliminary evidence that SRR-trained models can follow editing instructions that differ from the original generation prompt. A creative tool built on an AlphaGRPO-trained UMM would unify generation and editing in a single model: the user describes an initial scene (generation), then iteratively requests modifications (each step is an SRR-like refinement where the model diagnoses what needs to change based on the user's instruction and preserves everything else). The paper's qualitative examples (Figure 9) show this working for instructions like "Add some snow to the background" and "Change the cat's collar into a bell," where the model preserves the object identity while executing the specified change — precisely the behavior needed for an iterative creative workflow. The Inf. SRR cost (2× diffusion steps per refinement round) is acceptable for interactive use where the user expects to wait a few seconds between iterations.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does articulate implicit tradeoffs against named alternatives (cold-start SFT-based UMM alignment, holistic scalar reward models, fine-tuned MLLM reward models), but these tradeoffs are stated as motivations rather than empirically validated decision rules. The paper does not provide the head-to-head comparisons that would support a formal "prefer AlphaGRPO when X, prefer alternative when Y" framework. However, the paper's design choices and results do suggest conditions where AlphaGRPO's approach is most appropriate, which can be extracted as guidance for practitioners:

**AlphaGRPO's approach (RL without cold-start SFT + DVReward) is most appropriate when:**

- **The base UMM was pretrained on diverse, interleaved multimodal data** that likely includes implicit reasoning patterns — i.e., you believe the model already "knows" how to reason about visual errors but doesn't spontaneously do so. The paper's Bagel model fits this description, and the pilot study's verification/reflection contrast (Figure 2) provides a diagnostic test: if your model can identify errors when prompted to reflect but fails when prompted to verify, the dormant-capability hypothesis likely holds, and AlphaGRPO-style RL activation is a good fit. If the model fails at both verification and reflection — it genuinely cannot perceive the errors — then the capabilities are not dormant but absent, and cold-start SFT from a stronger teacher is probably necessary.

- **The deployment domain requires open-world generalization** rather than performance on a narrow, pre-specified prompt distribution. The paper's Table 3 shows that fine-tuned MLLM reward models (HPSv3, UnifiedReward) degrade performance on certain benchmarks compared to DVReward's generalist verifier, suggesting that specialized reward models are vulnerable to distribution shift while decompositional verification with unfine-tuned MLLMs better preserves evaluation breadth. If your application involves diverse, unpredictable user prompts (e.g., a public-facing creative tool), DVReward's approach is likely more robust than training against a domain-specific reward model.

- **You have access to a capable LLM decomposer and MLLM verifier** but not to a proprietary teacher model for SFT data generation. AlphaGRPO's training pipeline requires Qwen3-235B-A22B (for prompt decomposition) and Qwen3VL-30B-A3B (for verification) — both open-source — but does not require GPT-4 or Gemini for teacher distillation. For organizations with restricted access to proprietary models (due to cost, API limits, or data privacy concerns), AlphaGRPO provides a path to UMM self-improvement that is entirely open-source, provided the computational cost of the verifier (12.5% of training GPUs dedicated to reward serving) is acceptable.

**AlphaGRPO's approach is less appropriate when:**

- **The deployment latency budget prohibits 2× inference cost for refinement.** Inf. SRR doubles the number of diffusion steps at inference time, which directly increases wall-clock latency (not just FLOPs). For real-time applications (interactive chat, live video generation), this 2× cost may be prohibitive even if the quality gains are substantial. The paper does not provide latency measurements, but the 40-step diffusion process for a 512×512 image on an A100 likely takes 1–5 seconds, and doubling this is noticeable in interactive contexts. The single-pass AlphaGRPO model (without Inf. SRR) provides more modest gains (GenEval +0.2 at 512 resolution, Table 1) that may not justify the training complexity for latency-sensitive deployments.

- **The training budget cannot support the additional verification compute.** DVReward's verifier consumes 1 GPU per 8-GPU node (12.5% of training compute) and requires serving a 3B-active-parameter MLLM for approximately 170,000 images over the 380-step training run. For organizations training at very large scale (thousands of GPUs, millions of training steps), this overhead may be unacceptable even though the paper hides the latency through asynchronous scheduling. The paper does not report total verification FLOPs, but a rough calculation suggests that if each verification call costs approximately 1–10% of a training forward pass (3B active parameters vs. Bagel's full parameter count, estimated in the multi-billion range), the 8,960 calls per training step represent a non-trivial fraction of total compute. A simpler reward model (PickScore, CLIPScore) or a distillation of DVReward's verifier into a smaller specialized model would be more practical at scale, though the paper does not explore these options.

- **The problem domain requires capabilities genuinely beyond the base model's pretraining distribution.** AlphaGRPO amplifies existing capabilities; it does not create new ones. The paper's hardest compositional tasks (GenEval Position improves only from 69.8 to 73.8 with Inf. SRR at 512 resolution, vs. Color Attribution's improvement from 73.3 to 81.5; Table 18) and the WISE benchmark's near-flat gains on Cultural reasoning (0.76 → 0.75 with Self-CoT, Table 21) suggest that on tasks where the base model lacks fundamental competence, RL activation provides minimal benefit. If your deployment requires reasoning types or visual concepts that are absent from the base model's training data, AlphaGRPO will not help — you need additional pretraining, architecture modification, or teacher distillation to inject genuinely new capabilities.

These conditions are extracted from the paper's assumptions, results, and limitations rather than stated as explicit decision rules, and the paper does not validate them through head-to-head comparisons against cold-start SFT or alternative reward models at equivalent training budgets. They should be treated as **hypotheses for practitioners to test in their specific settings** rather than as established guidelines.
