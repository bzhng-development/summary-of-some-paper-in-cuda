## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Jacobi Forcing**, a progressive distillation paradigm that trains autoregressive models to serve as fast parallel decoders by learning from their own Jacobi decoding trajectories—smoothly shifting AR models into efficient parallel decoders while preserving causal attention, unlike prior diffusion-adaptation approaches that suffer from a pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch (e.g., bidirectional attention conflicting with causal pretraining). Evaluated on Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct across HumanEval and MBPP, Jacobi Forcing achieves 3.8× wall-clock speedup with minimal accuracy loss, and the authors further propose rejection recycling with multi-block decoding—an inference optimization that reuses high-quality n-grams discarded from past Jacobi iterations while maintaining multiple blocks simultaneously—boosting accepted tokens per iteration by up to 4.5× and pushing speedup to nearly 4.0×, establishing that AR models can match or exceed the generation efficiency of diffusion-based parallel decoders only when the pretrained causal prior is preserved throughout post-training rather than overwritten with bidirectional attention.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Autoregressive Decoding Is Inherently Sequential

The fundamental tension this paper addresses is architectural: **modern LLMs are trained to generate text one token at a time, left to right, which limits how fast they can produce output regardless of how much compute is available**. This is the autoregressive (AR) decoding bottleneck. During inference, each token $y_i$ is sampled from the conditional distribution $p_\theta(y_i \mid y_{<i}, x)$, meaning the model cannot begin computing token $y_{i+1}$ until $y_i$ has been fully generated. The result is a strict chain of $n$ sequential forward passes to produce $n$ tokens, where each pass underutilizes the massive parallelism of modern GPUs — most of the accelerator's FLOPs sit idle while waiting for the next token to be computed.

This matters immensely for real-world deployment. When a user interacts with a coding assistant, a math tutor, or an agentic system, **wall-clock latency** — the actual time they spend waiting for a response — determines usability. A model that generates 100 tokens per second (TPS) at batch size 1 is simply too slow for interactive applications, regardless of how high its throughput might be in batched serving scenarios. The paper opens by noting that even state-of-the-art models like GPT-5, Gemini-2.5, and Kimi-K2 "excel at complex and interactive agentic tasks," yet the sequential nature of AR decoding remains a hard ceiling on interactivity.

The problem is not that we lack compute — modern GPUs (A100, H200, B200) have enormous parallel processing capacity. The problem is that **AR decoding fundamentally cannot exploit that capacity** because the dependency chain forces serial execution. This creates a practical mismatch: hardware parallelism grows exponentially with each GPU generation (more streaming multiprocessors, wider memory buses), but the token generation latency of AR models improves only marginally, leaving most of the new compute capacity stranded.

### Why This Is a Hard Problem: The Causal Prior

There is a deeper reason this problem resists easy solutions. AR models are pretrained with a **causal attention mask**, meaning each token can only attend to itself and preceding tokens. This isn't just an implementation detail — it is the defining inductive bias that makes AR pretraining work. The model learns a particular kind of representation: given a clean, coherent left context, predict the next token. This "causal prior" is baked into every parameter during pretraining, which involves billions of tokens. Any attempt to change the generation order at inference time must contend with the fact that **the model has never seen scrambled, noisy, or out-of-order contexts during training**, and its internal representations are specialized to the clean left-to-right regime.

This is the unstated tension beneath all parallel decoding research: you want parallelism to reduce latency, but the model's learned representations assume serial, clean-context generation. The more you deviate from that regime, the more you trigger distribution shift — the model receives inputs unlike anything seen during training, and quality degrades.

### Prior Approaches and Their Shortcomings

The paper situates itself against three families of prior work, each with distinct failure modes:

#### Approach 1: Diffusion Language Models (dLLMs)

Diffusion models replace left-to-right generation entirely. Instead of predicting tokens one at a time, they model sequences jointly: start with pure noise (or fully masked tokens), then iteratively denoise the entire sequence over multiple passes. Each diffusion step updates all token positions simultaneously, enabling massive parallelism. The objective is typically the negative evidence lower bound (NELBO), a variational bound on the AR negative log-likelihood.

**Where they fall short:** The paper identifies two critical weaknesses.

First, the NELBO objective is a **loose bound** on NLL. Cheng et al. (2025), Nie et al. (2024), and Arriola et al. (2025) have shown that training from scratch with NELBO is less statistically efficient than training with the standard AR next-token prediction loss. This manifests as a gap in generation quality: open pretrained dLLMs like LLaDA-7B and Dream-7B underperform AR models of comparable scale on standard benchmarks (e.g., LLaDA-Instruct achieves only 36.0% on HumanEval versus 87.8% for Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct, as shown in Table 1). Quality matters — a faster but substantially worse model has limited practical value.

Second, to ameliorate this quality gap, the community has attempted to **adapt pretrained AR models into dLLMs** via post-training. This adaptation involves (1) constructing masked/perturbed sequences by randomly masking tokens from pretraining data, and (2) modifying the attention mask to enable block-wise **bidirectional attention**, replacing the causal mask. The training data distribution during this post-training phase is fundamentally different from the distribution seen during pretraining — the model suddenly sees tokens with random gaps, masked spans, and bidirectional dependencies it never learned to handle. The paper calls this the **pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch**, and it is the central concept motivating their alternative approach.

The consequence: AR-adapted dLLMs like SDAR (Cheng et al., 2025) suffer "substantial quality drops when large block sizes (e.g., 64 or 128) are adopted," and are "costly to train as shown in Figure 1, and fail to scale speedup reliably with larger block sizes." In other words, you cannot simply dial up parallelism and expect proportional gains — the quality degrades because the model has no causal structure to anchor its predictions. Furthermore, bidirectional attention makes **exact KV cache reuse difficult or impossible**, since the attention dependency graph changes between iterations, harming inference efficiency.

#### Approach 2: Speculative Decoding

Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al., 2022) is a popular AR acceleration technique that uses a small, fast "draft" model to propose several future tokens and has the large target model verify them in one parallel forward pass. If verification succeeds (the target model would have generated the same tokens), multiple tokens are committed at once. If verification fails, the target model regenerates from that point, and the draft model's incorrect proposals are discarded.

**Where it falls short:** The paper explicitly sets speculative decoding outside its scope, noting that "the models themselves don't serve as parallel decoders without supplemental architecture modifications (e.g., via additional heads) or separate draft models." The key distinction is architectural: Jacobi Forcing Model is the parallel decoder itself — there is no draft model, no auxiliary heads. The model natively predicts multiple tokens in a single forward pass. This is an important conceptual difference for deployment scenarios where maintaining and synchronizing a separate draft model is impractical (e.g., on-device inference, memory-constrained settings). However, the paper does provide comparisons with strong speculative decoding baselines (EAGLE-3, HASS) in Appendix B, showing Jacobi Forcing Model achieves competitive or better speedup (3.95× vs. 3.37× for HASS on B200) while maintaining higher accuracy (83.5% vs. 61.6%).

#### Approach 3: Jacobi Decoding and Consistency Distillation

This is the direct intellectual predecessor of Jacobi Forcing. **Jacobi decoding** reframes AR generation as solving a system of equations $f(y_i, y_{<i}, x) = 0$ for all positions simultaneously via parallel fixed-point iteration. Starting from a randomly initialized block of $n$ tokens, the model repeatedly updates all positions in parallel using causal attention (so each token $y_i$ conditions on the *previous* iteration's values of $y_{<i}$). The process continues until the block stabilizes to a fixed point, which is proven equivalent to greedy AR decoding.

The problem: **vanilla Jacobi decoding achieves essentially no speedup** over AR decoding (1.02× on GSM8K, 0.97× on HumanEval; Table 1-2). Why? Because in practice, the fixed-point iteration almost never predicts more than one correct token per pass — the convergence is painfully slow, typically requiring $n$ iterations to resolve an $n$-token block, which defeats the purpose of parallelism. The model was never trained to predict tokens given noisy (unconverged) context, so it simply cannot do so.

**CLLMs** (Kou et al., 2024) attempt to address this through **consistency distillation**: they collect Jacobi trajectories (sequences of intermediate states from the fixed-point iteration), then train the model with a KL-divergence loss that encourages it to jump directly from any point on the trajectory to the converged fixed point:

$$L_c = \mathbb{E}_{i \sim U\{1,\dots,N\}, y_{B_i} \sim J_i}\left[D_{KL}\left(p_{\theta^-}(y^*_{B_i} \mid x, y^*_{B_1}, \dots, y^*_{B_{i-1}}) \;\|\; p_\theta(y_{B_i} \mid x, y^*_{B_1}, \dots, y^*_{B_{i-1}})\right)\right]$$

In other words: given a noisy block, produce the clean block in one shot. This achieves up to 2×-3× speedup (CLLM* achieves 2.50× on HumanEval, 2.08× on GSM8K in the paper's own evaluation; Table 1-2).

**Where CLLMs fall short:** They fail to scale to larger block sizes. The paper explicitly states: "as block size increases, the number of tokens correctly decoded per iteration remains essentially constant." This is the key limitation that Jacobi Forcing addresses. Why does this happen? The consistency loss in CLLM treats each block independently — the model is trained to map a single noisy block to the clean fixed point, conditioned on completely clean preceding blocks. But as the block size $n$ grows, the later tokens in the block ($y_f$ for $f = c+1, \dots, n$) must condition on a long stretch of noisy context ($y'_{c:f-1}$). The model never learned this during pretraining (where context was always clean), so prediction quality degrades rapidly with noise length. CLLMs therefore plateau at moderate block sizes and cannot exploit the abundant FLOPs of modern GPUs that would make larger blocks profitable.

CEED-VLA (Song et al., 2025a) applies a similar consistency loss for vision-language-action models in robotics but faces the same scaling limitation.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

Jacobi Forcing is positioned as a **direct successor to CLLMs** that overcomes the block-size scaling bottleneck. The core insight is that CLLMs fail because they train on lumpy, all-at-once noise — an entire large block of noisy tokens at once. Jacobi Forcing instead introduces two innovations that attack the problem:

1. **Progressive noise schedule**: Rather than presenting the model with one large noisy block, it splits a large block into many small sub-blocks and introduces noise *progressively* — early sub-blocks are mostly clean, later sub-blocks are noisier. This mirrors the natural Jacobi trajectory where tokens converge left-to-right, and ensures the model always predicts tokens conditioned on a *partially clean* prefix, with the longest noisy dependency span reduced from $O(nN)$ to $O(\lceil tn \rceil)$.

2. **Noise-aware causal attention with progressive distillation**: The model is trained to predict the converged block given previous unconverged blocks *using causal attention*, not bidirectional attention. This preserves the causal prior from pretraining — the attention mask still only looks leftward — but the context seen by each token includes unconverged (noisy) earlier blocks, forcing the model to learn robust prediction under noise. This is fundamentally different from dLLM adaptation, which abandons causality and pays the quality penalty.

The paper also introduces **iterative progressive distillation**: after training on trajectories from the original AR model, it collects new trajectories from the partially-trained Jacobi Forcing Model (using larger block sizes) and trains further. This bootstrapping approach squeezes out an additional ~20% speedup.

The positioning relative to dLLMs is explicit and adversarial: the Abstract states that AR-adapted dLLMs "suffer from limited speedup compared to AR models due to a pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch," and the Introduction elaborates that bidirectional attention "conflicts with the causal prior learned during pretraining and hinders the integration of exact KV cache reuse." Jacobi Forcing is presented as the alternative that sidesteps this mismatch entirely by staying within the causal decoding paradigm while still enabling parallel token generation.

In the broader landscape, this paper is arguing that **the causal prior is not something to overcome — it is something to leverage**. dLLMs try to escape causality and pay a quality cost; speculative decoding works around the model without changing it; CLLMs try to teach causal models to handle noise but hit a scaling wall. Jacobi Forcing's position is that the right approach is to teach causal models to handle progressively more noise while respecting causality, which unlocks larger block sizes and correspondingly larger speedups without sacrificing quality.

## 3. Technical Approach

This is primarily a **training methodology and inference optimization paper** whose core idea is that autoregressive models can be progressively trained on their own Jacobi decoding trajectories — with carefully controlled noise levels and causal attention preserved — to become fast parallel decoders that predict multiple correct tokens per forward pass, and that the resulting models exhibit draft-quality characteristics amenable to further inference-time algorithmic speedups.

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **system for converting a standard autoregressive LLM into a parallel decoder** by training it on data generated from its own fixed-point iteration process (Jacobi decoding), where the model learns to predict converged clean tokens given progressively noisier unconverged context — and then exploits the resulting model's improved draft-quality characteristics through a multi-block decoding algorithm that reuses previously discarded high-quality n-grams. The key insight is that prior approaches either abandon causal attention (dLLMs suffer quality degradation from pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch) or train on all-at-once noise (CLLMs plateau at moderate block sizes), while Jacobi Forcing preserves causal attention and structures noise progressively to make learning tractable at large block sizes — thereby converting abundant GPU FLOPs into higher generation speed rather than wasted compute.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components that interact in a cycle:

1. **Base AR Model (Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct)** — the pretrained autoregressive LLM that serves as both the initial trajectory generator and the starting point for training. It produces ground-truth Jacobi trajectories by running Jacobi decoding (Section 3.1: first randomly initialize a block of $n$ tokens, then iteratively apply the model's causal forward pass to update all positions in parallel until convergence). These trajectories provide the training data.

2. **Progressive Distillation Trainer** — the training pipeline that converts the base AR model into a Jacobi Forcing Model. It uses three interacting mechanisms: **(a)** a progressive noise schedule that determines how much noise (unconverged tokens) appears in each sub-block of the training sequence, **(b)** a noise-aware causal attention mask for sequence packing that enables computing both AR loss and progressive consistency loss in a single forward-backward pass, and **(c)** a combined loss function ($L = L_{pc} + wL_{AR}$) that teaches the model to predict clean tokens from noisy context while maintaining standard AR generation capability.

3. **Jacobi Forcing Model** — the trained model, which serves as a fast parallel decoder. It runs Jacobi decoding (or the enhanced multi-block decoding algorithm) at inference time, but now predicts substantially more correct tokens per iteration (higher "fast-forwarded token count" or TPF) because it has been trained to handle noisy context. Critically, it uses standard causal attention — no bidirectional masking — and can leverage exact KV cache reuse across iterations.

4. **Multi-Block Decoder with Rejection Recycling** — the inference-time algorithm (Section 3.2, Algorithm 1) that exploits a behavioral property of Jacobi Forcing Models: they produce high-quality draft n-grams in the tail portions of unconverged blocks (fixed-point segments that match the converged answer appear within noisy blocks, and these segments grow longer across Jacobi iterations). The algorithm maintains up to $K$ simultaneous blocks, reuses discarded high-quality token sequences from previous iterations as candidate proposals (rejection recycling), and verifies them in parallel to skip ahead multiple Jacobi iterations at once.

Information flows as follows: **base AR model** → (generates Jacobi trajectories via Jacobi decoding on training prompts) → **progressive distillation trainer** → (trains the model using progressive noise schedule, noise-aware causal attention, and combined loss) → **Jacobi Forcing Model** → (optionally: regenerates trajectories with larger block sizes for a second distillation round) → **multi-block decoder with rejection recycling** → (at inference time, runs enhanced Jacobi decoding that maintains multiple blocks and reuses discarded n-grams) → **final output tokens with verified quality**.

#### Jacobi Decoding: The Foundation

Before explaining Jacobi Forcing, we must understand Jacobi decoding — the process that generates the training trajectories and is used for inference. Jacobi decoding reframes autoregressive generation as solving a system of nonlinear equations via parallel fixed-point iteration (Song et al., 2021; Santilli et al., 2023).

**The equations.** For an $n$-token block to be generated after prompt $x$, define a function $f$ at each position $i$:

$$f(y_i, y_{<i}, x) := y_i - \arg\max_{y} p_\theta(y \mid y_{<i}, x)$$

where $y_i$ is the token at position $i$, $y_{<i} = \{y_1, \ldots, y_{i-1}\}$ are the preceding tokens, $x$ is the prompt, $p_\theta$ is the pretrained LLM's output distribution, and $\arg\max$ selects the most probable token (greedy decoding).

**What this equation says:** for each position $i$, the token $y_i$ must equal the token the model would predict given the left context $y_{<i}$ and the prompt $x$. If and only if this holds for all $i$ simultaneously, the sequence is the greedy AR decoding result. If any token differs from what the model would predict given its context, $f(y_i, y_{<i}, x) \neq 0$.

**The fixed-point iteration.** Starting from a randomly initialized sequence $y^{(0)} = \{y^{(0)}_1, \ldots, y^{(0)}_n\}$, the Jacobi update at iteration $j$ computes all positions in parallel:

$$y^{(j+1)}_1 = \arg\max_{y} p_\theta(y \mid x)$$
$$y^{(j+1)}_2 = \arg\max_{y} p_\theta(y \mid y^{(j)}_1, x)$$
$$\vdots$$
$$y^{(j+1)}_n = \arg\max_{y} p_\theta(y \mid y^{(j)}_{<n}, x)$$

**Crucial implementation detail:** these $n$ maximization problems (one per position) can be solved in a single forward pass of the LLM. Since each position $i$ only depends on positions $<i$, and those values are taken from the *previous* iteration $y^{(j)}$, the standard causal attention mask naturally implements all $n$ predictions simultaneously — no architectural changes needed. The key is that position $i$ attends to $y^{(j)}_1, \ldots, y^{(j)}_{i-1}$ (from the previous iteration), not to $y^{(j+1)}_1, \ldots, y^{(j+1)}_{i-1}$ (from the current iteration), so there are no circular dependencies within a single forward pass.

**Convergence and equivalence.** The iteration continues until $y^{(k)} = y^{(k-1)}$ (a fixed point), denoted $y^*$. Song et al. (2021) proved that $y^*$ is identical to what greedy AR decoding would produce for the same prompt — Jacobi decoding is exact, not approximate.

**The trajectory.** Define the Jacobi trajectory $J := \{y^{(0)}, y^{(1)}, \ldots, y^{(k)}\}$ as the sequence of intermediate states from the random initialization to convergence. For training Jacobi Forcing Model, these trajectories are the raw data — each trajectory contains examples of what the model's outputs look like when context is noisy (unconverged) versus clean (converged).

**Block-by-block generation.** To generate sequences longer than $n$ tokens, Jacobi decoding operates sequentially over blocks. The first block of size $n$ is decoded to a fixed point $y^*_{B_1}$; then a second block starts its random initialization, but conditioned on the clean, accepted tokens from block 1: $x' = [x, y^*_{B_1}]$. This continues block by block until an end-of-sequence token appears in a fixed point. The full output is the concatenation:

$$l = [y^*_{B_1}, y^*_{B_2}, \ldots, y^*_{B_N}]$$

where $N = \lceil L/n \rceil$ is the number of blocks generated before termination, and $L$ is the total output length.

**Why vanilla Jacobi decoding is slow.** In practice, each Jacobi iteration typically accepts only one or a few tokens. The fixed-point iteration converges like a wave moving left to right — tokens near the left stabilize first, then the next few, etc. Empirically, the number of iterations needed is close to the block size $n$, meaning you still do roughly $n$ forward passes to generate $n$ tokens — the same as AR decoding. The parallelism is wasted because the model was never trained to make accurate predictions when conditioned on noisy (unconverged) context. Most of the parallel predictions at each iteration are wrong and get overwritten in subsequent iterations.

#### CLLM's Consistency Distillation and Its Scaling Failure

CLLMs (Kou et al., 2024) attempt to fix the slow convergence by training the model to jump directly from any point on the Jacobi trajectory to the fixed point. The training data consists of Jacobi trajectories collected by running the base model on a set of prompts. During training, for each block $i$ in each trajectory, a random point $y_{B_i} \sim J_i$ along the trajectory is sampled, and the model is trained to map it directly to the fixed point $y^*_{B_i}$.

**The CLLM loss.** The consistency loss is a KL divergence between the teacher (frozen base model's output distribution on clean context) and the student (model being trained, given noisy context):

$$L_c = \mathbb{E}_{i \sim \mathcal{U}\{1,\dots,N\},\, y_{B_i} \sim J_i} \left[D_{KL}\left(p_{\theta^-}(y^*_{B_i} \mid x, y^*_{B_1}, \ldots, y^*_{B_{i-1}}) \;\|\; p_\theta(y_{B_i} \mid x, y^*_{B_1}, \ldots, y^*_{B_{i-1}})\right)\right]$$

where $\theta^- = \text{stopgrad}(\theta)$ (the frozen teacher, preventing gradient flow through the teacher's output), $i \sim \mathcal{U}\{1,\dots,N\}$ denotes uniformly sampling a block index, $y_{B_i} \sim J_i$ denotes randomly sampling a point from the Jacobi trajectory of the $i$-th block, and $D_{KL}$ is Kullback-Leibler divergence aggregated across the $n$ tokens in a block.

**What this loss computes:** For a randomly selected block and a randomly selected noisy state from that block's Jacobi trajectory, force the student model's output distribution (given the noisy context) to match the teacher model's output distribution (given the clean, converged context). The teacher represents the "correct" output — what the model would predict if it had perfect information — and the student learns to imitate that output despite receiving imperfect, noisy input.

**Why this form:** KL divergence is the natural objective for distribution matching because it penalizes both missing high-probability tokens that the teacher assigns probability mass to, and placing probability mass on tokens that the teacher assigns low probability to. The stop-grad on the teacher prevents the optimization from trivially collapsing both distributions to a degenerate mode; instead, the student is forced to move toward the teacher's already-correct distribution. The random sampling of trajectory points ensures the model sees the full range of noise levels, from nearly clean (late in the trajectory) to highly noisy (early in the trajectory).

**The scaling bottleneck.** The paper identifies precisely why CLLMs plateau as block size increases. Within a single block, consider the prediction of token $y_f$ at position $f$, where the first $c-1$ tokens have already converged (are correct) and positions $c$ through $f-1$ are still noisy (unconverged):

$$y_f = \arg\max_y p(y \mid x_c, y'_{c:f-1})$$

where $x_c = [x, y_{<c}]$ is the clean context up to position $c-1$, and $y'_{c:f-1}$ is the noisy unconverged context from position $c$ to $f-1$.

**The core difficulty:** the model must predict $y_f$ conditioned on $y'_{c:f-1}$ — a stretch of noisy tokens that can be arbitrarily long when $n$ is large. During pretraining, the model only ever saw clean context; it has no experience making predictions when the immediately preceding tokens are wrong. The CLLM training objective tries to teach this skill, but it does so for *entire blocks at once* — the model is asked to map from an arbitrary point in the Jacobi trajectory (which could have $n$ noisy tokens) directly to the clean fixed point. When $n$ is small (e.g., 16), the noisy span is manageable; when $n$ is large (e.g., 64, 128, 256), the noisy span is far beyond what the model can learn to handle in one shot.

**The consequence:** "as block size increases, the number of tokens correctly decoded per iteration remains essentially constant." In other words, increasing parallelism does not increase effective throughput — the model simply cannot use the extra parallel capacity because its per-token prediction accuracy under long noisy contexts is too low.

#### Jacobi Forcing: Progressive Distillation

Jacobi Forcing addresses CLLM's scaling bottleneck by restructuring how noise is presented during training. Instead of presenting one large noisy block, it splits a large block into many small sub-blocks and introduces noise *progressively* according to a schedule, ensuring that each token prediction is conditioned on only a short span of noisy context. **The core idea: teach the model to handle noise in manageable increments, not all at once.**

##### Progressive Noise Schedule

In Jacobi decoding, the noise (unconverged tokens) in a block is not randomly distributed — it is concentrated toward the right, since convergence proceeds left-to-right. Jacobi Forcing's training mimics this structure by dividing a large generation into many small blocks (e.g., $n=16$ tokens each) and applying a noise schedule that determines, for each block, what fraction of its tokens are noisy.

**The schedule definition.** Let $N$ be the total number of blocks in a training sequence. The noise schedule is a cyclic, linearly increasing pattern with window size $w$:

$$W = \{0, \frac{1}{w}, \ldots, \frac{w-1}{w}\}$$
$$t_i = W[j], \quad j = i \bmod w$$

where $t_i \in [0, 1]$ is the noise ratio for the $i$-th block, representing the fraction of tokens in that block that should be noisy (unconverged).

**What this schedule does:** Within each window of $w$ blocks, the noise ratio starts at $0$ (first block is entirely clean — all tokens match the fixed point), increases linearly by $1/w$ per block, and resets to $0$ at the start of the next window. For example, with $w=16$ and $n=16$ (total token block size of $16 \times 16 = 256$), the first block in a window has no noise, the second block has roughly 1 noisy token ($1/16 \times 16 = 1$), the third has 2 noisy tokens, and so on, up to the 16th block which has all 16 tokens noisy. Then the cycle repeats.

**The critical property:** this structure ensures that the longest span of consecutive noisy tokens that any prediction must condition on is $O(\lceil t_i n \rceil)$ — the noise within a single small block — rather than $O(nN)$ (noise across the entire sequence). Specifically, a token in a noisy block conditions on all preceding tokens, but the *clean* blocks that precede it provide clean context, and the *noisy* context only extends back to the start of the current block. With $n=16$, the maximum noisy span is at most 16 tokens, regardless of how many blocks are in the sequence. In contrast, CLLM's random sampling could produce sequences where every token in a large block is noisy, requiring predictions conditioned on up to $n=128$ or $256$ noisy tokens simultaneously.

**Why the linear progressive schedule works (vs. alternatives):** The paper ablates three noise schedules (Table 4): random, linear progressive, and reverse progressive.

- **Random schedule** ($t_i \sim \mathcal{U}(1,\dots,N)$): each block independently gets a random noise ratio. This provides no structure — some blocks may be very noisy, some clean, with no guarantee that noisy blocks are preceded by clean ones. The model sees noisy context of unpredictable length, making consistent learning difficult.

- **Linear progressive**: noise increases gradually within each window, mimicking the natural left-to-right convergence of Jacobi decoding. The model always has a clean(ish) preceding context to anchor its predictions. This schedule achieves the best performance: with window size 8 and N=16, it achieves 84.7% accuracy on HumanEval with 0.48 iterations per token (meaning ~2 tokens accepted per iteration on average).

- **Reverse progressive** (noise decreases within each window): performs significantly worse (only tested at window size 16, achieving 82.9% accuracy with 0.62 iterations per token). The paper hypothesizes that having noisy early blocks followed by clean later blocks contradicts the natural Jacobi convergence pattern, making the training distribution too different from what the model sees at inference.

**Window size ablation** (Table 4, linear progressive column): window sizes of 8 and 16 both work well, with window size 8 achieving 84.7% accuracy and 0.48 iter/token, and window size 16 achieving 81.7% accuracy and 0.46 iter/token. Window size 32 achieves 84.1% accuracy but drops to 0.49 iter/token. Window size 8 provides the best accuracy-speed tradeoff, likely because shorter windows mean noise is introduced more aggressively (two additional noisy tokens per block when $n=16$, vs. one additional noisy token per block when $w=16$), providing more training signal per window.

##### Progressive Consistency Loss

Given the noise schedule, the training objective computes a consistency loss aggregated across *all* blocks in the sequence, not just one randomly sampled block as in CLLM. This aggregation reduces gradient variance and stabilizes training.

$$L_{pc} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} D_{KL}\left(p_{\theta^-}(\cdot \mid x, y^*_{B_1}, \ldots, y^*_{B_{i-1}}) \;\|\; p_\theta(\cdot \mid x, y^{t_1}_{B_1}, \ldots, y^{t_{i-1}}_{B_{i-1}})\right)$$

where $N$ is the total number of blocks, $y^*_{B_1}, \ldots, y^*_{B_{i-1}}$ are clean (converged) blocks preceding the current block, $y^{t_1}_{B_1}, \ldots, y^{t_{i-1}}_{B_{i-1}}$ are preceding blocks with noise levels determined by the schedule (some clean, some noisy), and $p_{\theta^-}$ is the frozen teacher distribution computed on completely clean context (all preceding blocks are converged).

**What this loss computes:** For each block $i$ in the sequence (from 1 to $N$), force the student model $p_\theta$ — which sees preceding blocks with their scheduled noise levels (some clean, some noisy) — to match the teacher model $p_{\theta^-}$, which sees all preceding blocks in clean (converged) form. The key asymmetry: the teacher always conditions on clean context, while the student conditions on a mixture of clean and noisy context determined by the progressive noise schedule. The teacher represents the target: what the model *should* predict if it had perfect information. The student learns to approximate that target using imperfect information.

**Why average over all blocks:** In CLLM, a single block is sampled per training step (i.i.d. uniform), which means the model only gets a gradient signal from one local region of the sequence. Jacobi Forcing averages the loss across all $N$ blocks, which provides a more comprehensive signal — the model learns simultaneously about easy predictions (early blocks, mostly clean context) and hard predictions (later blocks, noisier context). This reduces gradient variance because the loss is less dependent on which specific block happened to be sampled. It also means the model sees the full noise progression in every training step, reinforcing the gradual difficulty structure.

**Why this form (and not, say, MSE or direct token matching):** KL divergence is the natural choice because it operates on probability distributions, not hard tokens. At any position, the student's output is a distribution over the vocabulary — it must learn to concentrate probability mass on the correct token while suppressing mass on incorrect ones *in the same proportion as the teacher would*. Token-level cross-entropy (hard matching) would treat all incorrect tokens as equally wrong, but KL divergence captures the nuance that some incorrect tokens are "more wrong" than others (they have lower teacher probability). This matters because in noisy contexts, the student may be uncertain, and the teacher's distribution provides a soft target that preserves the relative plausibility of alternatives.

##### AR Loss for Quality Safeguarding

Training solely with the consistency loss causes quality degradation, as reported in Kou et al. (2024) and confirmed by the authors' preliminary experiments. The model learns to be a fast parallel decoder but drifts away from the base model's generation quality on clean prompts. To prevent this, a standard autoregressive loss term is added:

$$L(\theta) = L_{pc} + w L_{AR}$$

where $w$ is a tunable weight balancing the two objectives, $L_{pc}$ is the progressive consistency loss defined above, and $L_{AR}$ is the standard next-token prediction loss on clean data (computed over the clean blocks in the packed sequence).

**What $L_{AR}$ does:** It is the conventional cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction on clean, unperturbed data — exactly the objective the model was originally pretrained with. Computing it on the clean blocks in the training sequence ensures the model does not forget how to do standard AR generation. This acts as a regularizer: the progressive consistency loss teaches parallel decoding capability, while the AR loss anchors the model to its original pretrained behavior.

**Why a weighted combination:** The weight $w$ controls the trade-off. If $w$ is too small, the model overfits to the consistency objective and produces worse-quality outputs on clean prompts (since inference still requires clean AR generation for the prompt and for blocks that have fully converged). If $w$ is too large, the model underinvests in learning the consistency skill and the speedup gains diminish. The paper does not report the specific $w$ value used, but the inclusion of the term is motivated by empirical necessity — "using only the consistency objective produces the same effect" as using only the consistency loss in CLLMs, which Kou et al. (2024) found degrades quality.

##### Noise-Aware Causal Attention and Sequence Packing

A major practical innovation in Jacobi Forcing is how training sequences are constructed and how attention masking enables efficient computation of all loss terms in a single forward-backward pass. This is a training efficiency problem: naively computing $L_{pc}$ for $N$ blocks requires $O(2N)$ forward passes (one for the teacher with clean context, one for the student with noisy context, per block) and $O(N)$ backward passes, making training prohibitively expensive.

**The sequence packing trick.** Instead of processing each block separately, the training sequence is constructed by **interleaving** noisy and clean versions of each block. Specifically, for each block $i$ in the original generation:

- A **noisy version** $y^{t_i}_{B_i}$ is included, with noise fraction $t_i$ determined by the schedule (the point along the Jacobi trajectory whose fraction of unconverged tokens is closest to $t_i$).
- A **clean version** $y^*_{B_i}$ (the converged fixed point) is included immediately after.

The packed training sequence therefore has $2N$ blocks: noisy block 1, clean block 1, noisy block 2, clean block 2, ..., noisy block $N$, clean block $N$. Figure 2b illustrates this structure.

**The noise-aware causal attention mask (Figure 2b).** The key insight is that with standard causal attention, tokens in a clean block would attend to the immediately preceding noisy block (which would degrade the AR loss computation, since the AR loss should condition on clean context). Conversely, tokens in a noisy block would attend only to clean preceding blocks (which is what we want for the teacher, but for the student we want it to see noisy context from earlier blocks).

The **noise-conditioned mask** resolves this by applying a custom block-sparse attention pattern:

- **For tokens in clean blocks (computing $L_{AR}$):** Each clean block attends to all preceding *clean* blocks and to the prompt, but **not** to the immediately preceding noisy block. This ensures the AR loss is computed with genuinely clean context, preserving the model's AR capability.

- **For tokens in noisy blocks (computing $L_{pc}$):** Each noisy block attends to **all** preceding blocks — both clean and noisy. This is the student's view: it sees the full noisy context from earlier blocks, exactly as it would during Jacobi decoding inference. The KL divergence is then computed between the student's output distribution (noisy context) and the teacher's output distribution (clean context, obtained from the corresponding clean block's forward pass).

- **Cross-block information flow:** The attention mask respects the overall causal structure — block $i$ (clean or noisy) can attend to blocks $1$ through $i-1$ (as well as the prompt), but not to blocks $i+1$ and beyond. Within each block, standard causal attention applies (token at position $j$ attends to tokens at positions $<j$ within the same block and to all tokens in preceding blocks).

**Why this mask enables $O(1)$ passes:** In a single forward pass with this mask, the model computes logits for every position in every block — both noisy and clean. The output from noisy blocks is used for $L_{pc}$ (student logits vs. teacher logits from the corresponding clean block positions, with the teacher's logits generated from the same forward pass since clean-block tokens attend only to clean context). The output from clean blocks is used for $L_{AR}$ (standard next-token prediction against ground-truth tokens). Backpropagation also requires only a single backward pass since all loss terms are computed from the single forward computation graph. This reduces the computational cost from $O(N)$ to $O(1)$ forward and backward passes per training step.

**Ablation: noise-conditioned vs. intra-window clean context (Table 5).** An alternative mask design conditions each block on clean context from *all* preceding windows — meaning tokens in a noisy block would see only clean blocks from earlier windows, not noisy blocks. Intuitively, this should make predictions easier since the context is cleaner. However, the ablation shows this alternative (NC-IC) achieves only 1.9× speedup, compared to 3.6× for the noise-conditioned mask (NC), both at 82.3% accuracy. Why? Training with cleaner context makes the training distribution too different from what the model sees during inference (where preceding blocks are noisy). The noise-conditioned mask ensures train-test distribution alignment — the model learns to predict under exactly the noise conditions it will encounter when doing Jacobi decoding.

##### Progressive Distillation for Larger Block Sizes

Even after training the Jacobi Forcing Model on trajectories from the original AR model, speedup eventually saturates. The paper identifies the cause: as the model becomes a better parallel decoder, its Jacobi trajectories change — the convergence pattern shifts because the model now predicts more correct tokens per iteration. The training data (collected from the original AR model's trajectories) is now **stale** — it represents a different model's behavior.

**Iterative trajectory regeneration.** To break this ceiling, the authors perform a second round of training:

1. **Round 1:** Collect Jacobi trajectories from the original AR model (Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct) with block size $n=16$, window size $w=16$. Train Jacobi Forcing Model for 10k steps. Learning rate $10^{-6}$, batch size 4, max new sequence length 2048.

2. **Round 2:** Collect **new** Jacobi trajectories from the partially-trained Jacobi Forcing Model (which now has multi-token prediction capability), using a **larger block size** of $n=32$ and a smaller window size of $w=8$. Train for another 10k steps on these self-generated trajectories. Learning rate and batch size remain the same.

**What this achieves:** the second round provides a "further 20% speedup with only minor performance degradation." The larger block size in the second round means the model is trained on trajectories where more tokens are correct per iteration (because the partially-trained model already predicts multiple tokens correctly), exposing it to more challenging noise patterns. The smaller window size ($w=8$ vs. $w=16$) with the same sub-block size ($n=16$ in round 1; sub-blocks are still $n=16$ even though total blocks are larger since they're still split into small sub-blocks) means the noise schedule introduces noise more aggressively — roughly two additional noisy tokens per block — providing stronger training signal.

**Why progressive distillation works (and why it's necessary):** This is a form of curriculum learning applied to parallel decoding. The first round teaches basic noise-handling from the original model's relatively clean trajectories. The second round builds on this foundation, exposing the model to its own more aggressively noisy trajectories — trajectories that didn't exist in the first round because the original model couldn't produce them. Without the second round, the model would never see the distribution of noise patterns that occur when a moderately capable parallel decoder does Jacobi decoding with larger blocks. With the second round, it learns to handle progressively harder noise, which translates to higher accepted token counts at inference.

#### Inference-Time Behavior and Optimization

After training, the Jacobi Forcing Model exhibits a distinctive qualitative behavior under vanilla Jacobi decoding that the paper exploits algorithmically. This behavior is not a separate training objective — it's an emergent property that the progressive distillation training produces.

##### Qualitative Behavior: Fixed-Point Segments and Draft Quality

Figure 4 provides a visualization of a Jacobi Forcing Model's trajectory. The key observations:

1. **Fixed-point segments emerge within noisy blocks:** Among the unconverged (black) tokens, there are stretches where *three or more consecutive tokens match the fixed point*, highlighted in red. These are partial convergences — the model has correctly predicted a subsequence of tokens even though the overall block has not converged. This indicates the model has learned to make accurate predictions in the tail of noisy blocks, consistent with its training pattern where it was taught to predict converged tokens given noisy preceding blocks.

2. **These segments grow across iterations:** Comparing points 1 through 6 in Figure 4, the red segments extend leftward — more tokens become correct as the Jacobi iteration proceeds, even though many preceding tokens remain noisy. This is the "progressive extension" pattern that the training schedule was designed to induce.

3. **High-quality n-grams in the draft:** Fixed-point segments that are 3+ tokens long form candidate n-grams that, if verified, could be accepted in one shot. Prior work on lookahead decoding (Fu et al., 2024) showed that such n-grams from Jacobi iterations can be verified in parallel and reused. The Jacobi Forcing Model produces substantially longer and more frequent fixed-point segments than vanilla AR models, making n-gram-based optimization more profitable.

##### Rejection Recycling

Rejection recycling is an inference-time technique that exploits the high-quality fixed-point segments to skip ahead multiple Jacobi iterations at once. The core idea: tokens that are correct (match the fixed point) but get rejected by the standard Jacobi acceptance criterion (because they appear in a block that hasn't fully converged) can be recycled as candidate proposals in subsequent iterations.

**Mechanism (detailed in Algorithm 1 in Appendix A):**

1. **N-gram pool construction:** During Jacobi decoding, whenever a block is processed and some tokens are accepted (verified against greedy prediction) while others are rejected, the rejected tail of the block — the sequence of unconverged tokens that were predicted — is examined. If it contains consecutive correct tokens (matching what the fixed point would be), these are extracted as n-grams and added to a fixed-size candidate pool $\mathcal{N}$.

2. **Candidate generation:** In subsequent Jacobi iterations, when generating a new block, the system checks whether any n-gram in the pool $\mathcal{N}$ has its first token matching the last accepted token of the current context. If so, that n-gram is concatenated with the current accepted tokens to form a candidate sequence. Multiple candidates (up to a verification budget) can be formed from different n-grams in the pool.

3. **Parallel verification:** All candidates are verified in a single forward pass by appending them along the batch dimension. For each candidate, the model's greedy prediction is compared to the candidate tokens; the candidate with the longest matching prefix (most tokens accepted) is selected. The accepted tokens from that candidate are committed, and the process continues.

4. **Pool management:** The n-gram pool is maintained at a fixed size; new n-grams from rejected tails replace old ones to keep the pool fresh and relevant to the current decoding context.

**Example from Figure 4:** At point 3, there is a fixed-point segment (red) spanning several tokens. If this segment is captured in the n-gram pool and later matched, the decoder can skip directly from point 3 to point 5 — bypassing point 4 entirely — by proposing the fixed-point segment as a candidate and verifying it in parallel. This reduces the number of Jacobi iterations needed.

**Why this is lossless:** The verification step uses greedy rejection sampling (identical to Leviathan et al., 2022's speculative decoding verification): the model's own greedy prediction is compared token-by-token against the candidate. Tokens are accepted only if they exactly match. This guarantees that the final output is identical to what greedy Jacobi decoding would produce — no quality degradation, only speedup.

##### Multi-Block Decoding

Multi-block decoding is an orthogonal optimization that exploits a different property: the Jacobi Forcing Model can maintain coherent predictions for future blocks even when current blocks haven't converged. Instead of waiting for block $i$ to fully converge before starting block $i+1$, multi-block decoding maintains up to $K$ blocks simultaneously, refining all of them in each forward pass.

**Key concepts (Algorithm 1, Figure 3):**

**Real-active vs. pseudo-active blocks.** At any time, exactly one block is designated the **real-active block** (RA) — the block closest to the effective KV cache boundary. All other $K-1$ blocks are **pseudo-active blocks**. The distinction is about which tokens are actually committed to the output:

- **Real-active block:** Tokens in this block that pass verification are **accepted and committed** to the KV cache. They become part of the permanent output context that all future blocks will condition on.

- **Pseudo-active blocks:** Tokens in these blocks are **pseudo-accepted** — they are used as context for the real-active block and for each other during the forward pass, but they are **not committed** to the KV cache. They represent speculative future content that may change as convergence proceeds.

**Why pseudo-acceptance matters:** Even though pseudo-active blocks' tokens aren't finalized, they provide useful context. The Jacobi Forcing Model's training taught it to condition on noisy context, so having plausible (even if imperfect) predictions for future blocks helps the model make better predictions in the current real-active block. The pseudo-active blocks essentially serve as high-quality draft context.

**Spawn mechanism.** When the real-active block reaches an acceptance threshold — the fraction of tokens accepted in the current block exceeds a spawn ratio $r$ (e.g., $r=0.85$ meaning 85% of the block's tokens are accepted) — a new pseudo-active block is spawned. This new block is initialized with random tokens (or patterned initialization) and begins its own Jacobi refinement process. The total number of active blocks never exceeds $K$.

**Threshold definition.** Let $n$ be the block size. The spawn threshold is $s = \lceil r n \rceil$. When $|a_{RA}| \geq s$ (the number of accepted tokens in the real-active block reaches the threshold), and the current number of active blocks is less than $K$, a new block is created. Figure 3 shows an example with $n=4$, $r=0.5$, and $K=2$: when the real-active block reaches 2 accepted tokens (iteration 1, blocks 0-1), a new pseudo-active block is spawned (iteration 2, blocks 1-2).

**Promote mechanism.** When the real-active block fully converges — all $n$ tokens are accepted ($|a_{RA}| \geq n$) — it is "retired" from being real-active. A pseudo-active block that has at least some accepted tokens ($|a_b| > 0$) is selected as the new real-active block. Its draft tokens are rebuilt to length $n$ (filling from its pseudo-accepted tokens), verified once more (now with the benefit of the fully-clean preceding context), and promoted. This promotion step is crucial: the pseudo-active block's tokens were originally drafted under noisy context (the previous real-active block was not fully converged). When promoted and re-verified, they benefit from the now-clean preceding context, which often improves accuracy.

**Verification with rejection recycling for real-active blocks (Algorithm 1, lines 6-12).** For each block in each forward pass:

1. Compute greedy predictions $g$ for the block's span.
2. For the real-active block: verify the longest prefix of the current draft $q_{RA}$ that matches $g$ (the model's own greedy prediction). Additionally, check candidates from the n-gram pool $\mathcal{N}$ (rejection recycling): if any n-gram in the pool, when appended after the committed tokens, produces a longer match with $g$ than the draft alone, use that candidate instead.
3. If an EOS token appears in the accepted region of the real-active block, terminate and return the concatenated output.
4. Update accepted tokens $a_{RA}$ with the newly verified prefix.
5. For the tail (unverified portion): set the new draft $q_{RA}$ to the rejected tail, either continued from the model's prediction or from a recycling candidate.

**Cache trimming (line 13).** After verification, false KV cache entries are deleted: the cache is trimmed to the committed length (prompt length + verified $a_b$ for all accepted blocks + $a_{RA}$ for the real-active block). This prevents memory bloat from speculative tokens that were never committed.

**Stop condition (line 16).** The algorithm terminates when either all blocks have fully converged ($|a_b| \geq n$ for all $b$) or an EOS token is emitted and accepted in the real-active block.

**Why multi-block decoding provides speedup:** Figure 5b demonstrates the benefit. With vanilla Jacobi decoding, the fast-forward count (tokens accepted per iteration) plateaus as block size increases — the model's per-iteration accuracy doesn't scale with parallelism. With multi-block decoding (+ rejection recycling), the fast-forward count continues to grow with block size, reaching 4.5× higher than the baseline at block size 256 on H200. This is because correct tokens in pseudo-active blocks are being decoded *before* the preceding blocks have converged, so when those blocks are eventually promoted, they already contain many correct tokens that can be accepted immediately.

**Wall-clock overhead and hardware utilization (Figures 5a, 7, 8).** The extra FLOPs consumed by multi-block decoding (more tokens decoded per forward pass, parallel verification candidates) would be counterproductive if they increased per-iteration latency enough to offset the token count gains. The paper profiles generation latency as a function of total decoded tokens across A100, H200, and B200 GPUs (Figure 7):

- On H200/B200: latency is essentially flat up to ~256 parallel tokens per iteration, indicating the GPUs have spare compute and memory bandwidth below this threshold.
- On A100: the flat region extends only to ~128 tokens, with noticeable increase beyond that.
- Above these thresholds, additional tokens incur roughly linear latency increase — the GPUs become compute-bound or memory-bound.

This analysis justifies the final inference configuration of block size $n=64$ with verification size 4 on H200/B200 ($64 \times 4 = 256$ tokens, near the flat-region boundary), and block size 128 on A100 (near the 128-token boundary). The paper performs a hyperparameter search (Appendix E, Figure 8) over block sizes and n-gram verification sizes, fitting a smooth surface to empirical TPS measurements, and finds the optimal region near block size 64 and verification size 4 — maximizing hardware utilization without crossing into the linear-latency-increase regime.

**Spawn ratio tuning.** The spawn ratio $r$ (threshold for spawning new blocks) and initialization threshold are tuned via grid search. The paper reports "consistently optimal performance at $r=0.85$ for block size 64 across verification sizes 2 to 8" — meaning a new pseudo-active block is spawned when 85% of the current real-active block's tokens are accepted. Too low a ratio spawns blocks too early, wasting FLOPs on blocks that will be repeatedly revised as the preceding context changes. Too high a ratio provides no speedup benefit since new blocks are spawned too late to contribute meaningful parallel decoding.

**Number of blocks $K$:** "Performance gains from additional blocks saturate at $K=2$ as later drafts degrade quickly." The third and subsequent pseudo-active blocks condition on too much noisy context to produce useful drafts, so maintaining only two blocks (one real-active, one pseudo-active) provides the bulk of the speedup benefit while minimizing wasted FLOPs.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **Progressive (linear) noise schedule over random sampling:** reduces maximum noisy-context span from $O(nN)$ to $O(\lceil t_i n \rceil)$; makes learning tractable by ensuring each prediction only handles a short stretch of noise. Empirically validated by the ablation in Table 4 (linear progressive achieves 84.7% accuracy with 0.48 iter/token vs. 82.9% for random at window size 8).

- **Progressive consistency loss averaged over all blocks (vs. single-block sampling):** reduces gradient variance, exposes model to full difficulty spectrum in every training step, and enables sequence packing for efficient computation.

- **Noise-aware causal attention with sequence packing (vs. bidirectional masking in dLLMs, vs. per-block independent forward passes in CLLM):** preserves pretrained causal prior (avoiding pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch), enables $O(1)$ forward/backward passes, and ensures train-test distribution alignment (the model sees the same noise patterns during training as during inference). Validated by ablation: noise-conditioned mask achieves 3.6× speedup vs. 1.9× for the intra-window clean context variant (Table 5).

- **AR loss as regularizer ($L_{AR}$ term):** prevents quality degradation from overfitting to the consistency objective; this is a known necessity from CLLM work.

- **Iterative progressive distillation (two rounds with increasing block size):** breaks the saturation ceiling by providing fresh, self-generated trajectories that represent the trained model's own noise patterns. First round teaches basic noise handling from base model trajectories ($n=16$, $w=16$); second round scales to larger blocks and more aggressive noise schedules ($n=32$, $w=8$), yielding ~20% additional speedup.

- **Rejection recycling (vs. standard Jacobi or speculative decoding with separate draft model):** exploits emergent fixed-point segments in Jacobi Forcing Model's trajectories; lossless (uses greedy verification); no separate draft model needed; n-gram pool provides cheap, context-relevant candidates without additional forward passes.

- **Multi-block decoding with real/pseudo-active distinction (vs. sequential block-by-block Jacobi):** exploits Jacobi Forcing Model's ability to predict accurately in future blocks despite unconverged preceding blocks; real-active/pseudo-active distinction ensures only verified tokens are committed to output; spawn/promote mechanics maintain a pipeline of draft blocks; $K=2$ provides diminishing returns beyond that.

- **Hardware-aware configuration selection (block size 64, verification size 4, spawn ratio 0.85 on H200/B200):** tuned via roofline profiling (Figure 7) and TPS surface fitting (Figure 8) to saturate GPU FLOPs without entering the compute-bound latency-increase regime.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Causal Prior as a Resource, Not an Obstacle

The dominant assumption in parallel decoding research — particularly in the dLLM community — is that autoregressive causality is the bottleneck to be eliminated. The reasoning is straightforward: left-to-right generation forces sequential dependence, so if you want parallelism, you must abandon causality. Diffusion models do this wholesale, replacing token-by-token generation with full-sequence denoising. AR-adapted dLLMs (SDAR, JetAstra) do it partially, replacing causal attention with block-wise bidirectional attention during post-training so that tokens within a block can attend to each other in both directions.

Jacobi Forcing makes the opposite bet. The paper's core conceptual move is to treat the **causal prior not as a limitation to escape but as a learned structure to exploit**. The model spent billions of pretraining tokens learning to make predictions conditioned on left-to-right context. That inductive bias — that representations are organized around clean, sequentially coherent prefix information — is valuable. Throwing it away (via bidirectional attention) creates a pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch where the model must learn fundamentally new attention patterns from limited post-training data, which is why SDAR suffers "substantial quality drops when large block sizes are adopted" and why dLLM adaptation is "costly to train" (Figure 1).

The innovation is not that Jacobi Forcing uses causal attention — CLLMs also use causal attention. The innovation is the **diagnostic framing**: the paper identifies that the field has been conflating two distinct problems. Problem 1 is *sequential dependency* (you need future tokens to condition on past tokens). Problem 2 is *clean-context dependency* (the model only makes accurate predictions when preceding tokens are correct). AR-adapted dLLMs attack Problem 1 by removing causality, but they inadvertently worsen Problem 2 because the model no longer has the structural regularities it learned during pretraining to handle even partially clean context. Jacobi Forcing keeps causality intact and instead attacks Problem 2 directly: it teaches the model to make accurate predictions when the left context is noisy (unconverged), while preserving the causal attention structure that makes those predictions tractable.

This reframing has a concrete implication beyond this paper: it suggests that future work on parallel decoding should invest in **noise-robust causal prediction** rather than **causality-free sequence modeling**. The causal prior is a source of inductive bias that makes learning from limited post-training data feasible — it provides the scaffolding on which noise-handling capability can be built incrementally. The ablation in Table 5 makes this point empirically: switching from noise-conditioned causal masking (3.6× speedup) to a variant that conditions noisy blocks on cleaner context from previous windows (1.9× speedup) substantially degrades performance, because the training distribution diverges from what the model sees during inference. The causal structure isn't just preserved for ideological reasons — it's preserved because it aligns training and inference distributions, which is the fundamental requirement for effective learning.

---

### Innovation 2: Progressive Noise as a Curriculum for Parallel Decoding

CLLMs demonstrated that consistency distillation can teach AR models to handle noise, but they hit a hard ceiling: as block size grows, the number of correctly decoded tokens per iteration stays constant. The paper's diagnosis of *why* this happens — and the solution it proposes — is a conceptual contribution that generalizes beyond the specific method.

The diagnostic insight is that CLLMs fail because they present noise as an **undifferentiated mass**. The consistency loss (Eq. 5) samples a random point from the Jacobi trajectory and asks the model to map it directly to the fixed point. For a block of size n, this means the model must handle anywhere from 0 to n noisy tokens in a single prediction task. When n is small, this is manageable; when n is large, the longest noisy span can be the entire block, which is far beyond what the model learned during pretraining. The training signal is diluted because the difficulty varies wildly from sample to sample, and the hardest samples (many noisy tokens) dominate the loss but provide little learnable gradient because the prediction problem is essentially unsolvable from that state.

Jacobi Forcing's progressive noise schedule (Section 3.1) is not just a hyperparameter choice — it's a **curriculum learning strategy** applied to the structure of Jacobi trajectories. By splitting a large generation into many small blocks (n=16) and applying a linear progressive schedule with cyclic windows (Eq. 7), the training procedure ensures that:

1. **Every prediction is tractable.** The maximum noisy span any token must condition on is bounded by the sub-block size (O(⌈t_i n⌉), at most 16 tokens when n=16), regardless of the total sequence length. This transforms an impossible learning problem (predict token 200 given 200 noisy preceding tokens) into a feasible one (predict token 200 given at most 16 noisy preceding tokens, with the rest clean).

2. **Difficulty increases gradually.** Within each window of w blocks, noise rises from 0 to 1 linearly. Early blocks in the window are mostly clean — the model can succeed easily on these, reinforcing the correct mapping. Later blocks are noisier — the model applies what it learned from easier cases to harder ones. This progressive structure means the model always has a "scaffold" of easier predictions to build on.

3. **The curriculum matches inference.** During actual Jacobi decoding, noise is not randomly distributed — it's concentrated toward the right of each block, and blocks converge left-to-right. The windowed progressive schedule mimics this structure: each window resets to clean, representing the start of a new converged block, then gradually introduces noise representing the trailing unconverged portion. The train-test alignment is not accidental; it's built into the schedule design.

The ablation in Table 4 validates that this is a real effect, not just a training detail. At window size 8, linear progressive achieves 84.7% accuracy with 0.48 iterations per token. Random schedule — which also bounds noise within small blocks but doesn't structure it progressively — achieves only 82.9% accuracy with 0.53 iter/token. Reverse progressive — which contradicts the natural convergence order — performs worst (82.9% accuracy, 0.62 iter/token). The progressive structure matters independently of the sub-block partition.

This is a **fundamental insight** rather than an incremental refinement because it identifies the structure of the learning problem, not just a better loss function. The failure mode of CLLMs was not insufficient model capacity or wrong objective — it was that the training data distribution did not respect the **intrinsic difficulty gradient** of the task. Progressive noise scheduling makes the difficulty gradient explicit and learnable, which is why Jacobi Forcing scales to larger block sizes where CLLMs plateau. This principle — that parallel decoding should be taught as a curriculum where noise increases gradually along the natural convergence direction — is transferable to any method that trains models to handle noisy context, including potential future dLLM adaptations that might incorporate causal structures.

---

### Innovation 3: Self-Generated Trajectories as a Bootstrap for Breaking the Saturation Ceiling

A less obvious but equally important conceptual contribution is the paper's approach to training data staleness. Consistency distillation methods (CLLMs, CEED-VLA, and the first round of Jacobi Forcing) collect Jacobi trajectories from the original AR model. These trajectories represent how the *untrained* model behaves under Jacobi decoding — convergence patterns, noise distributions, and error characteristics are all specific to a model that has never been taught to handle noise.

As training proceeds, the model changes. It becomes a better parallel decoder — it predicts more correct tokens per iteration, convergence happens faster, and the noise patterns in its own Jacobi trajectories shift. The original training data becomes **off-policy**: it represents a weaker model's behavior, not the current model's. This is the saturation problem: training on stale trajectories eventually stops providing useful gradient signal because the model has already mastered the noise patterns present in the data, and any remaining errors are specific to noise patterns the data doesn't contain.

The paper's solution — iterative trajectory regeneration with progressively larger block sizes — is conceptually analogous to **on-policy reinforcement learning**, applied to the distillation setting. After round 1 training, the Jacobi Forcing Model is used to generate *new* trajectories with a larger block size (n=32 vs. n=16). These trajectories contain noise patterns that didn't exist in the original data: longer noisy spans, more aggressively distributed noise, and the characteristic error modes of a partially-capable parallel decoder. Training on these self-generated trajectories pushes the model into a new capability regime.

The yield is an additional ~20% speedup, but the significance is broader: this demonstrates that parallel decoding capability can be **bootstrapped**. The first round teaches basic noise handling from a weak teacher (the original AR model). The second round teaches advanced noise handling from a stronger teacher (the partially-trained Jacobi Forcing Model itself). There is no obvious reason this process couldn't be repeated — round 3 with block size 64, round 4 with block size 128, each time collecting trajectories from the current best model and training on progressively harder noise distributions.

This is a **fundamental shift** from how consistency distillation was previously conceived. CLLMs treat trajectory collection as a one-time data generation step; Jacobi Forcing treats it as an iterative process where the model's own improving capability generates the curriculum for further improvement. It connects to broader ideas in self-play and iterative self-improvement (e.g., STaR, ReST^EM), but applied specifically to the parallel decoding setting. The ReST^EM ablation in Appendix K — where an RL-based training approach degraded revision model performance — is an instructive contrast: off-policy training on self-generated data works (Jacobi Forcing round 2), but on-policy RL-style training can amplify spurious correlations and fail. The difference is that Jacobi Forcing's distillation objective is supervised (teacher-student KL matching) rather than reward-maximizing, making it more robust to distribution shift in the self-generated data.

---

### Innovation 4: Draft Quality as an Emergent Property, Not an Architectural Feature

The paper's inference-time innovations (rejection recycling and multi-block decoding) emerge from a qualitative observation: Jacobi Forcing Models produce **fixed-point segments** within noisy blocks — stretches of 3+ consecutive tokens that match the converged answer even though the overall block hasn't converged. These segments grow across Jacobi iterations (Figure 4), forming high-quality draft n-grams that can be recycled as speculative candidates.

The innovation here is not the specific recycling or multi-block algorithms — speculative decoding and lookahead decoding (Fu et al., 2024) have explored similar ideas. The innovation is the **diagnostic connection between training methodology and inference behavior**. The paper doesn't just report that Jacobi Forcing Model is faster; it characterizes *what kind of faster* and *why*. The progressive consistency loss with noise-aware causal attention doesn't just improve per-token accuracy under noise — it specifically causes fixed-point segments to emerge in the tails of unconverged blocks, a behavioral signature that can be algorithmically exploited.

This is an instance of a broader principle in ML systems: **training methodology shapes not just aggregate metrics but the structure of model outputs, and that structure can be leveraged at inference time if properly characterized**. Prior work on Jacobi decoding acceleration (CLLMs, lookahead decoding) focused on either training better models or designing better decoding algorithms, but treated these as independent pursuits. Jacobi Forcing connects them: the training is designed to produce a model with specific exploitable behaviors (long fixed-point segments, draft quality in pseudo-active blocks), and the inference algorithm is designed to exploit exactly those behaviors.

The empirical evidence is in Figure 5b. Under vanilla Jacobi decoding, the fast-forward count (tokens accepted per iteration) plateaus with block size — more parallelism doesn't help. Adding multi-block decoding + rejection recycling causes the fast-forward count to continue scaling with block size, reaching 4.5× higher at block size 256. This scaling wouldn't work with a CLLM or a vanilla AR model because those models don't produce the long fixed-point segments (for rejection recycling) or coherent pseudo-active block drafts (for multi-block decoding) that Jacobi Forcing Model produces. The algorithmic optimizations are **coupled to the model's training**: they amplify a capability that the training specifically cultivated, rather than being generic accelerations that work equally well on any model.

This is a **conceptual reframing** of how to approach inference optimization. Rather than asking "how do we design a faster decoding algorithm for a given model," the paper asks "what model behaviors would enable faster decoding, and how do we train to produce those behaviors?" The answer — train for noise-robust causal prediction with progressive difficulty, measure the resulting trajectory structure, design algorithms that exploit that structure — is a template that could apply beyond Jacobi decoding to any setting where training shapes the geometry of model outputs in algorithmically useful ways.

---

### Innovation 5: A Unified Perspective on the Pretrain-to-Posttrain Mismatch as the Limiting Factor in AR Adaptation

The paper's most significant negative contribution — and arguably its sharpest conceptual claim — is the identification and empirical characterization of the **pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch** as the fundamental bottleneck in adapting AR models for parallel decoding. This is not presented as a theoretical result but as an empirical pattern that explains the failure modes of multiple competing approaches.

The pattern is: whenever post-training modifies the core architectural assumptions that held during pretraining, quality degrades, and the degradation scales with the magnitude of the modification. For dLLMs, the modification is replacing causal attention with bidirectional attention — a structural change to the attention mechanism. The consequence is that dLLMs trained from scratch with NELBO underperform AR models (LLaDA-Instruct: 36.0% on HumanEval vs. 87.8% for Qwen2.5-Coder), and AR-adapted dLLMs that impose bidirectional attention during post-training suffer quality drops at large block sizes (SDAR). For CLLMs, the modification is subtler — they preserve causal attention but train on undifferentiated noise — and the consequence is a scaling ceiling rather than outright quality collapse (CLLM* achieves 2.50× speedup but cannot go higher with larger blocks). For Jacobi Forcing, the modification is minimized: causal attention is preserved, noise is structured progressively, and training and inference distributions are aligned via the noise-aware mask. The result is the best scaling behavior — 3.8× speedup on the base model, further boosted to 4.0× with inference optimizations, while largely preserving accuracy.

The claim is not that any deviation from pretraining is harmful — Jacobi Forcing itself deviates by introducing noisy context. The claim is that **the type of deviation matters**: modifications that respect the pretrained structural prior (causality, left-to-right information flow, clean prefixes) are much easier to learn from limited post-training data than modifications that override it. This is why Jacobi Forcing's design is not just a different loss function but a different philosophy: work *with* the causal prior, not against it.

This framing has implications beyond this paper. It provides a principled criterion for evaluating parallel decoding approaches: **how much does the method's training distribution and architectural constraint deviate from the pretraining regime?** Methods with smaller deviation (Jacobi Forcing, speculative decoding with draft models) should be expected to scale better with limited post-training data than methods with larger deviation (dLLM adaptation). It also suggests that the field's pursuit of "escape causality" approaches may be fundamentally limited by the pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch, and that the alternative path — embrace causality, teach noise handling — has more headroom.

The evidence for this claim is distributed across the paper's comparisons: Jacobi Forcing Model achieves 83.5% on HumanEval with 3.86× speedup, while Fast-dLLM (bidirectional adaptation) achieves only 53.0% with 1.45× speedup, and D2F (another bidirectional adaptation) achieves 54.3% with 1.77× speedup (Table 1). The accuracy-speedup Pareto frontier strongly favors methods that preserve the causal structure. This isn't an incidental result — it's the paper's central empirical thesis, and it's supported across two model families (Qwen2.5-Coder, Qwen2.5-Math) and four benchmarks (HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K, MATH).

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper evaluates on coding and math benchmarks. **Coding**: HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021), using Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct as base. Training data for coding is OpenCodeInstruct (Ahmad et al., 2025), from which the authors curate 450k instances with generations that pass all unit tests for trajectory generation. **Math**: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and MATH (Hendrycks et al., 2021), using Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024) as base, trained on the math split of Openthought2 (Guha et al., 2025) with the same trajectory generation pipeline.

- **Base model(s).** Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct for coding tasks (HumanEval, MBPP) and Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct for math tasks (GSM8K, MATH). Both are fine-tuned instruction variants of the Qwen2.5 7B-parameter family. The authors argue these represent the capabilities of contemporary LLMs at a scale where inference acceleration is practically valuable, and where the base model's AR quality is high enough to serve as a meaningful target for preservation.

- **Metrics.** **Tokens Per Forward pass (TPF)** — the average number of correct (relative to greedy AR output) tokens decoded per model forward pass. **Tokens Per Second (TPS)** — the wall-clock throughput, measured as total generated tokens divided by total inference time. **Speedup** — the TPS ratio relative to the AR baseline on the same hardware (A100 or B200). **Accuracy / Solve Rate** — pass@1 under greedy decoding, reported as strict accuracy (%) on HumanEval, MBPP (Table 1), and solve rate on GSM8K, MATH (Table 2). The paper additionally reports **iterations per token** in ablations (Table 4), a normalized measure of convergence speed.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against multiple families:
  - **AR-based parallel decoders**: Vanilla Jacobi decoding (Santilli et al., 2023), which produces identical outputs to greedy AR but achieves essentially no speedup (0.97–1.02×). CLLM* (Kou et al., 2024) — the authors' own reproduction of CLLM training with the same sequence packing technique as Jacobi Forcing, but without progressive training or larger block sizes. CLLM* achieves 2.50× speedup on HumanEval at 87.8% accuracy, 2.08× on GSM8K at 92.2% solve rate (Tables 1–2).
  - **Diffusion-based parallel decoders**: LLaDA-Instruct (Nie et al., 2025a), Dream-Base (Ye et al., 2025), Fast-dLLM + dual cache (Wu et al., 2025c), and D2F (Wang et al., 2025). These baselines are tested with generation configurations comparable to Jacobi Forcing Model (block size 128 where applicable). Dream-7B is chosen over LLaDA-7B for the Fast-dLLM and D2F baselines as it is "significantly faster with similar or better performance" (Table 1).
  - **Speculative decoding** (Appendix B): EAGLE-3 (Li et al., 2025) and HASS (Zhang et al., 2025), representing state-of-the-art draft-model approaches. Measured on B200 using the strongest released checkpoints.
  - **Distilled dLLM** (Appendix B): dParallel (Chen et al., 2025), trajectory-level consistency distillation applied to discrete diffusion models.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** For AR-based methods, compute is measured in model forward passes. Each Jacobi iteration — whether vanilla, CLLM*, or Jacobi Forcing Model — consumes one forward pass that simultaneously predicts tokens at all positions within the active block(s). For diffusion-based methods, each denoising step is one forward pass; speedup is measured as TPS relative to the AR baseline, which normalizes for differences in per-pass cost. For multi-block decoding and rejection recycling, extra forward pass cost is incurred from parallel verification (additional batch dimension entries for candidate verification), and this cost is included in the reported TPS. The paper does not use a FLOPs-based budget — wall-clock time on specific hardware (A100, H200, B200) is the primary cost metric, reflecting the practical concern of interactive latency. For ablation studies where only convergence speed matters (Table 4), iterations per token is the unit.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the inference configuration search (Appendix E), the authors perform a grid search over block sizes and verification sizes, fit a smooth polynomial surface (total degree up to 6, best degree chosen by MSE) over measured TPS values, and interpolate onto a dense grid using scipy with Gaussian-like smoothing. This produces the contour maps in Figure 8 used to select optimal inference hyperparameters. The paper does not report cross-validation for the main accuracy/speedup results; these are single-run evaluations on the standard test sets. No confidence intervals or error bars are reported for any metric.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Coding Benchmarks: Jacobi Forcing Model vs. AR and Diffusion Baselines

Table 1 reports results on HumanEval and MBPP using Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct, with all methods run on A100 GPUs.

**On HumanEval**, Jacobi Forcing Model achieves **4.01 TPF (159.5 TPS)** for a **3.86× speedup** over the AR baseline (1.00 TPF, 41.3 TPS), with accuracy of 83.5% — a drop of 4.3 percentage points from the AR baseline's 87.8%. Jacobi Forcing Model with multi-block decoding and rejection recycling (MR) pushes this to **4.09 TPF (163.9 TPS)** for **3.97× speedup** at the same 83.5% accuracy, since MR is a lossless inference optimization over the same model checkpoint.

For comparison: CLLM* achieves 2.68 TPF (103.3 TPS) for 2.50× speedup at 87.8% accuracy — fully preserving the AR baseline's quality, but with substantially lower speedup. Vanilla Jacobi decoding achieves 1.03 TPF (39.9 TPS) for 0.97× — actually slightly slower in wall-clock time than AR decoding despite identical output quality, because the per-iteration overhead of processing full blocks exceeds any token-level parallelism gains when only 1 token per iteration is accepted.

The best diffusion-based parallel decoder on HumanEval is D2F at 2.50 TPF (73.2 TPS) for 1.77× speedup, but at only 54.3% accuracy — meaning Jacobi Forcing Model is simultaneously 1.5× faster and 29.2 percentage points more accurate. Fast-dLLM with dual cache achieves 1.80 TPF (60.0 TPS) for 1.45× speedup at 53.0% accuracy. The raw dLLM baselines without optimization (LLaDA-Instruct, Dream-Base) are much slower: LLaDA-Instruct achieves 0.07× speedup (2.8 TPS) at 36.0% accuracy; Dream-Base achieves 0.49× speedup (20.2 TPS) at 54.3% accuracy.

**On MBPP**, the pattern is consistent but with compressed speedups: Jacobi Forcing Model achieves 2.74 TPF (110.7 TPS) for 2.57× speedup at 70.4% accuracy (vs. AR baseline 1.00 TPF, 43.1 TPS, 74.3% accuracy — a drop of 3.9 percentage points). With MR: 2.84 TPF (113.0 TPS) for 2.62× speedup. CLLM*: 2.10 TPF (80.1 TPS) for 1.94× speedup at 71.4% accuracy. D2F: 2.30 TPF (105.0 TPS) for 2.44× speedup at 55.2% accuracy. Jacobi Forcing Model is 1.07× faster than D2F (2.62× vs. 2.44×) with 15.2 percentage points higher accuracy.

**Key observation: the accuracy-speedup trade-off is not monotonic across methods.** CLLM* preserves full AR accuracy (87.8% on HumanEval, 71.4% on MBPP) but only achieves moderate speedup. Jacobi Forcing Model sacrifices ~4 percentage points of accuracy to nearly double the speedup (from 2.50× to 3.86× on HumanEval). The dLLM baselines preserve neither accuracy nor achieve comparable speedup. This suggests a fundamental quality-efficiency frontier where methods that stay closer to the AR causal prior (CLLM) preserve quality better, while methods that more aggressively optimize for parallelism (Jacobi Forcing) trade some quality for substantially higher speedup.

#### Math Benchmarks: Generalization to a Different Domain and Model Family

Table 2 reports results on GSM8K and MATH using Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, on A100 GPUs.

**On GSM8K**, Jacobi Forcing Model achieves **3.72 TPF (146.1 TPS)** for **3.50× speedup** at 91.4% solve rate (vs. AR baseline 1.00 TPF, 41.8 TPS, 92.4% solve rate — a drop of 1.0 percentage point). With MR: 4.04 TPF (154.9 TPS) for 3.71× speedup at the same 91.4%. CLLM*: 2.25 TPF (86.8 TPS) for 2.08× speedup at 92.2% solve rate. The best diffusion baseline, D2F, achieves 3.10 TPF (91.2 TPS) for 2.18× speedup at 77.6% solve rate — Jacobi Forcing Model is 1.6× faster with 13.8 percentage points higher accuracy.

**On MATH**, Jacobi Forcing Model achieves **3.82 TPF (150.7 TPS)** for **3.65× speedup** — and notably, this comes with a **slight accuracy improvement**: 77.4% solve rate vs. 77.0% for the AR baseline (a gain of 0.4 percentage points). With MR: 3.98 TPF (152.0 TPS) for 3.68× speedup at 77.4%. CLLM*: 2.23 TPF (84.4 TPS) for 2.04× speedup at 77.2% solve rate — also a slight improvement. D2F: 2.60 TPF (98.8 TPS) for 2.39× speedup at 35.4% solve rate — less than half the accuracy.

The MATH result is significant because it demonstrates that Jacobi Forcing can sometimes **improve** accuracy while accelerating, which goes beyond the typical trade-off expectation. This may be because the progressive distillation training acts as a form of self-distillation, where the model learns more robust representations by being forced to predict tokens under noisy context — akin to a denoising objective that can regularize and improve generalization.

#### Speedup on Newer Hardware (B200)

Table 3 reports HumanEval speedup on B200 GPUs using the same Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct backbone. The AR baseline achieves higher absolute TPS on B200 (83.0 vs. 41.3 on A100), and the speedup ratios remain stable: Jacobi Forcing Model achieves 4.01 TPF (301.7 TPS) for 3.63× speedup, and with MR achieves 4.21 TPF (328.0 TPS) for 3.95× speedup. This stability across hardware generations (A100 → B200) indicates that the method's efficiency gains are not bottlenecked by specific GPU characteristics — the TPF improvement translates to wall-clock speedup on both architectures.

#### Inference Optimization Gains: Rejection Recycling and Multi-Block Decoding

The inference optimizations (MR) provide consistent but modest additional speedup beyond the base Jacobi Forcing Model across all benchmarks:
- HumanEval: 3.86× → 3.97× (+0.11×, +2.8% relative)
- MBPP: 2.57× → 2.62× (+0.05×, +1.9% relative)
- GSM8K: 3.50× → 3.71× (+0.21×, +6.0% relative)
- MATH: 3.65× → 3.68× (+0.03×, +0.8% relative)
- HumanEval on B200: 3.63× → 3.95× (+0.32×, +8.8% relative)

The largest relative gains come on B200 for HumanEval, likely because the B200's higher compute capacity allows more aggressive parallel verification without entering the linear-latency regime (cf. Figure 7b vs. Figure 7c). The gains are small on MATH, where the base Jacobi Forcing Model already achieves high TPF.

#### Block Size Scaling and Fast-Forward Behavior

Figure 5b shows how the fast-forward token count (tokens accepted per iteration) scales with block size on HumanEval (H200 GPU) for three strategies: running window (optimized vanilla Jacobi), multi-block decoding + rejection recycling, and (presumably) vanilla Jacobi Forcing Model without optimizations. The key finding: multi-block decoding + rejection recycling causes the fast-forward count to continue scaling with block size — reaching approximately 4.5× higher at block size 256 compared to smaller blocks — while the running window method plateaus. This empirically validates the claim that the Jacobi Forcing Model produces high-quality future tokens (fixed-point segments) that the inference algorithm can exploit, and that this exploitability scales with block size once the progressive distillation training has enabled it.

#### Speedup vs. Block Size at Fixed Fast-Forward Counts

Figure 5a shows wall-clock speedup as a function of (log-scaled) block size for different fixed fast-forwarding counts per iteration (TPF = 2, 3, 4, 5) on H200. The curves are flat up to block size ~64, then begin to decline slowly at 128 and more steeply beyond. This analysis informs the choice of block size 128 as the default for speedup evaluation (Table 1 caption: "we run all evaluations with a block size of 128 except for Jacobi Forcing Model (MR) since MR takes extra FLOPs") — it's the largest block size before wall-clock latency penalties become significant, maximizing the TPF potential without incurring excessive per-iteration cost.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

- **Training noise schedule type (Table 4):** The linear progressive schedule (the one used in Jacobi Forcing) achieves 84.7% accuracy with 0.48 iterations per token on HumanEval at block size 256 with N=16 sub-blocks, window size 8. The random schedule achieves 82.9% accuracy with 0.53 iter/token at the same window size — more iterations needed per token (worse convergence speed) and lower accuracy. The reverse progressive schedule (noise decreasing within each window) performs worst: 82.9% accuracy with 0.62 iter/token at window size 16 (the only tested configuration for reverse progressive). This confirms that the progressive structure is not merely a convenience — it provides a measurable improvement in both speed (fewer iterations per token) and quality over unstructured noise scheduling.

- **Training noise schedule window size (Table 4):** Under the linear progressive schedule, window sizes 8, 16, and 32 are compared. Window size 8 achieves 84.7% accuracy with 0.48 iter/token; window size 16 achieves 81.7% accuracy with 0.46 iter/token (faster convergence but lower accuracy); window size 32 achieves 84.1% accuracy with 0.53 iter/token. Window size 8 provides the best accuracy-speed trade-off, likely because shorter windows introduce noise more aggressively (two additional noisy tokens per block when n=16), providing stronger training signal per window at the cost of slightly more difficult predictions.

> **Important caveat on Table 4 ablations:** The checkpoints used for this ablation are trained on only 10k randomly sampled instances from the OpenCodeInstruct trajectory dataset, not the full 450k training set used for the main results in Table 1. The authors note this explicitly: "for ablation purpose, the checkpoints are not trained with full datascale as in Table 1." This means the absolute accuracy numbers in Table 4 (81.7–84.7%) are lower than the main Jacobi Forcing Model result (83.5% after full training), and the relative comparisons between schedule variants should be interpreted within this reduced-data regime. It is unclear whether the ranking of schedule types would hold at full data scale, though the authors presumably selected the progressive schedule based on these ablations and then scaled up.

- **Training mask type (Table 5):** The noise-conditioned mask (NC), which conditions each block on both clean and noisy preceding blocks as the student would see during inference, achieves 3.6× speedup at 82.3% accuracy. An alternative mask that conditions blocks on clean context from all preceding windows (NC-IC, noise-conditioned with intra-window clean context) achieves only 1.9× speedup at the same 82.3% accuracy. The 1.7× speedup gap is substantial and validates a key design principle: the training distribution must match the inference distribution. Providing artificially clean context during training makes the task easier during training (the model learns to predict given clean context) but fails to prepare the model for the noisy context it will encounter during actual Jacobi decoding, resulting in far fewer tokens accepted per iteration during inference.

- **Inference configuration (block size and verification size) (Figure 8, Appendix E):** A grid search over block sizes {8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256} and n-gram verification sizes {1, 2, 4, 8, 12} with K=2 and r=0.85, smoothed via polynomial surface fitting, identifies the optimal region near block size 64 and verification size 4 on B200. Larger block sizes push toward the hardware roofline (~256 parallel tokens on B200; Figure 7b) and begin degrading wall-clock latency. Larger verification sizes similarly increase parallel decoding overhead beyond the FLOPs budget. The surface in Figure 8b shows a clear peak rather than a plateau, indicating that the configuration is genuinely optimal rather than being insensitive within a broad range.

- **Spawn ratio (Section 4.3):** The spawn ratio $r$ (threshold for spawning new pseudo-active blocks, expressed as fraction of tokens accepted in the real-active block) is tuned via grid search. The paper reports that "performance gains from additional blocks saturate at block size = 2 as later drafts degrade quickly," meaning K=2 pseudo-active blocks is optimal — the third block's drafts are too noisy to provide useful parallelism. The spawn ratio "shows consistently optimal performance at r=0.85 for block size 64 across verification sizes 2 to 8."

- **Hardware roofline characterization (Figure 7, Appendix D):** On H200 and B200, generation-only latency is approximately flat up to ~256 parallel decoded tokens, then increases roughly linearly beyond that. On A100, the flat region extends only to ~128 tokens, with steeper increase beyond. This hardware characterization justifies the per-GPU configuration choices: block size 64 with verification size 4 (256 parallel tokens) on H200/B200, and likely smaller configurations on A100 (the paper uses block size 128 for A100 evaluations but may be operating near the roofline boundary).

- **Second-round progressive distillation (Section 3.1, end of section):** The authors train for 10k steps in round 1 (block size 16, window size 16), then collect new trajectories from the trained model with block size 32, window size 8, and train for another 10k steps. This provides "a further 20% speedup with only minor performance degradation." The paper does not provide a direct ablation showing the speedup and accuracy of the round-1-only checkpoint vs. the round-1+2 checkpoint on the main benchmarks — this would have been a clean demonstration of the bootstrap effect. The 20% figure appears to be an aggregate claim rather than a table entry.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments demonstrate that Jacobi Forcing achieves substantially higher speedup than prior AR-based parallel decoders (CLLM*: 2.50× → 3.86× on HumanEval) and dramatically outperforms dLLM adaptations in the accuracy-speedup Pareto sense. However, several aspects of the experimental design warrant scrutiny.

**Handling the accuracy-speedup trade-off: what does "minimal loss in performance" actually mean?** The paper's Abstract claims Jacobi Forcing achieves 3.8× speedup "with minimal loss in performance." On HumanEval, the loss is 87.8% → 83.5%, a drop of 4.3 percentage points. On MBPP, it's 74.3% → 70.4%, a drop of 3.9 percentage points. These are non-trivial accuracy degradations — a 4-point drop on HumanEval represents roughly 7 out of 164 problems now being solved incorrectly that were previously correct. Whether this qualifies as "minimal" depends on the use case; for applications where correctness is critical (e.g., safety-critical code generation), a 4% error rate increase may be unacceptable. The paper does not provide a detailed error analysis showing whether the lost accuracy is concentrated on particular problem types or distributed uniformly. It is noteworthy, however, that on MATH the accuracy actually *improves* slightly (77.0% → 77.4%), and on GSM8K the drop is only 1.0 percentage point (92.4% → 91.4%), suggesting the trade-off may be domain-dependent.

**Incomplete ablation of the progressive distillation rounds.** The paper claims a ~20% speedup from the second distillation round, but this is stated narratively in Section 3.1 rather than demonstrated in a table comparing round-1-only vs. round-1+2 checkpoints on the full benchmarks. Without this ablation, it is unclear how much of the final speedup is attributable to the progressive distillation idea vs. simply training for more steps. The 20% figure could be confounded with extended training time (20k total steps vs. 10k steps).

**Limited data scale for ablations.** As noted above, the noise schedule and window size ablations in Table 4 use only 10k training instances (vs. 450k for main results). This weakens the evidential value of these ablations for the final model configuration — the ranking of noise schedules might shift at full data scale, and the interaction between data scale and noise schedule is unexplored.

**Missing comparisons with other self-speculative / parallel decoding approaches.** The paper compares against CLLM*, dLLMs, and speculative decoding baselines (EAGLE-3, HASS in Appendix B), but does not compare against lookahead decoding (Fu et al., 2024) or other Jacobi-based acceleration methods (e.g., Fu et al.'s n-gram caching from Jacobi trajectories, which rejection recycling builds upon). Including a comparison with lookahead decoding would strengthen the claim that Jacobi Forcing's training is necessary for the high-quality fixed-point segments, since lookahead decoding also uses Jacobi trajectories but without model retraining.

**Single-shot evaluations only.** The paper reports single-run accuracy numbers without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple random seeds. Given that the test sets are moderate-sized (HumanEval: 164 problems; MBPP: ~500 problems; MATH: 500 problems), the accuracy differences between Jacobi Forcing Model (83.5%) and CLLM* (87.8%) on HumanEval could be partially attributable to sampling noise — a 4.3-point swing on 164 problems is approximately 7 problems, which is not obviously beyond the range of random variation. The paper would benefit from bootstrap confidence intervals or multiple training runs.

**Wall-clock speedup measurement details are thin.** The TPS numbers are the critical metric, but the paper provides limited detail on measurement methodology. Are TPS numbers averaged over the full test set, or over individual generations? Is prompt processing time (encoding the input) included or excluded? Are the measurements taken in a controlled environment with fixed GPU clocks and no other processes? These details matter for reproducibility, given that TPS can vary substantially based on measurement protocol.

**No sensitivity analysis for the AR loss weight w.** The combined loss $L(\theta) = L_{pc} + wL_{AR}$ introduces a hyperparameter w that controls the accuracy-speedup trade-off. The paper states that "using only the consistency objective produces the same effect" as quality degradation in CLLMs, confirming that w > 0 is necessary. But the actual w value is never reported, and no sweep over w is presented. This is a significant omission, because w directly controls the central trade-off the paper optimizes — a larger w would likely preserve more accuracy but reduce speedup, and the paper's claim of "minimal loss in performance" depends on this implicit choice.

**The multi-block decoding speedup is modest compared to the training speedup.** The training innovations (progressive schedule, noise-aware causal attention, iterative distillation) provide the bulk of the speedup (e.g., 3.86× on HumanEval vs. 2.50× for CLLM*). The inference optimizations (MR) add only 0.11–0.32× additional speedup depending on benchmark and hardware. While the paper presents MR as a contribution, its practical impact is limited relative to the training gains, and the complexity it adds (n-gram pool management, multi-block state tracking, spawn/promote logic) may not justify the marginal improvement for many deployments.

**Hardware-specific optimization limits generality.** The optimal inference configuration (block size 64, verification size 4 on B200) is derived from roofline profiling specific to A100, H200, and B200 GPUs. Different hardware (e.g., consumer GPUs, TPUs, edge accelerators) will have different rooflines, requiring re-tuning of these hyperparameters. The paper's hardware-aware methodology is sound, but the specific numbers should not be interpreted as universal.

**Limited evidence for the "causal prior preservation" claim at scale.** The central conceptual argument — that preserving causal attention avoids the pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch that limits dLLM adaptation — is supported by the overall accuracy-speedup comparison (Jacobi Forcing Model far outperforms dLLM adaptations). However, the paper does not provide a direct ablation where Jacobi Forcing is trained with the same progressive noise schedule but using bidirectional attention within blocks. Such an ablation would cleanly test whether causality preservation is the differentiating factor, or whether the progressive schedule alone would suffice even with bidirectional attention. The ablation in Table 5 (NC vs. NC-IC) addresses a related but different question — it shows that conditioning on noisy context (matching inference distribution) is better than conditioning on clean context, but both variants use causal attention. The missing ablation is *causal vs. bidirectional* attention under the progressive schedule.

Overall, the experiments establish that Jacobi Forcing meaningfully advances the state of the art in AR-based parallel decoding, achieving speedups that prior consistency distillation methods could not match, while confirming that the pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch is a real barrier for dLLM adaptation approaches. The primary limitations are the moderate accuracy degradation (~4 points on coding benchmarks), the lack of statistical rigor (no confidence intervals, limited seed diversity), and missing ablations that would strengthen the causal claims about *why* the method works.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Accuracy Degradation on Coding Benchmarks Is Non-Trivial

**The assumption or constraint.** Jacobi Forcing Model sacrifices some generation quality in exchange for speedup. The Abstract claims the method achieves 3.8× wall-clock speedup "with minimal loss in performance," but the empirical results paint a more nuanced picture.

**The consequence.** On HumanEval, accuracy drops from 87.8% (AR baseline and CLLM*) to 83.5% for Jacobi Forcing Model — a decline of 4.3 percentage points (Table 1). On MBPP, the drop is from 74.3% to 70.4%, a decline of 3.9 points. These represent roughly 7 additional incorrect solutions on HumanEval (out of 164 problems) and approximately 20 additional incorrect solutions on MBPP (out of ~500 problems). For applications where correctness is critical — safety-critical code generation, educational tools where wrong answers are harmful, or agentic systems where downstream actions depend on code correctness — a 4-point accuracy regression may be unacceptable, particularly when CLLM* preserves full accuracy (87.8% on HumanEval, 71.4% on MBPP) while still achieving 2.50× speedup. A practitioner choosing between Jacobi Forcing Model (3.86× speedup, 83.5% accuracy) and CLLM* (2.50× speedup, 87.8% accuracy) faces a non-trivial accuracy-speed trade-off that the paper's framing as "minimal loss" understates.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The accuracy numbers in Table 1 and Table 2 are the primary evidence. On math benchmarks, the degradation is smaller: GSM8K drops from 92.4% to 91.4% (1.0 point), and MATH actually *improves* from 77.0% to 77.4% (+0.4 points, Table 2). This domain dependence — worse degradation on code, better on math — is noted but not explained. The paper provides no error analysis showing which types of coding problems become incorrect, whether failures concentrate on particular problem categories, or whether the errors are systematic (e.g., the model produces plausible-looking but subtly wrong code) versus random.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not directly address this trade-off beyond including the AR loss term ($L_{AR}$) as a safeguard (Section 3.1). The weight $w$ controlling the balance between progressive consistency loss and AR loss is never reported, and no sweep over $w$ is presented to characterize the accuracy-speedup Pareto frontier. The paper also does not explore whether the accuracy can be recovered by accepting a lower speedup (e.g., by reducing the block size or adjusting the noise schedule). This leaves practitioners without guidance on how to tune the method for their specific accuracy requirements.

---

### The Accuracy-Speedup Trade-off Is Controlled by an Unreported Hyperparameter

**The assumption or constraint.** The combined training objective $L(\theta) = L_{pc} + w L_{AR}$ (Eq. 9) introduces a weight $w$ that balances the progressive consistency loss (which teaches parallel decoding) against the standard autoregressive loss (which preserves generation quality). The paper states that omitting $L_{AR}$ entirely causes quality degradation, consistent with CLLM findings, confirming $w > 0$ is necessary. But the actual value of $w$ used in the main experiments is never disclosed.

**The consequence.** The central trade-off of the paper — how much accuracy to sacrifice for speedup — is governed by this unexamined hyperparameter. A larger $w$ would anchor the model more strongly to its pretrained AR behavior, likely preserving more accuracy but reducing the speedup because the model would invest less in learning noise-robust parallel decoding. A smaller $w$ would push the model toward faster decoding at the cost of more accuracy loss. The reported results (3.86× speedup at 83.5% accuracy on HumanEval) represent a single point on an unknown Pareto frontier. Practitioners who need a different point — say, 2.8× speedup at 86% accuracy, or 4.5× speedup accepting 80% accuracy — have no way to determine whether Jacobi Forcing can achieve it without running their own sweep. Moreover, the optimal $w$ likely depends on the base model, training data, and target domain, meaning the paper's implicit choice may not transfer.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The existence of the AR loss term and its necessity are discussed in Section 3.1: "Kou et al. (2024) notes that using only the consistency loss (Eq. 5) must be supplemented with an AR loss to maintain generation quality. Our preliminary experiments show that using only the consistency objective (Eq. 8) produces the same effect." Beyond this, the value of $w$, any ablation over $w$ values, or the sensitivity of the speedup-accuracy trade-off to $w$ are absent from the paper and appendices.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. This is a significant gap for a method whose headline result is a quantitative speed-quality trade-off. A sweep over $w \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0\}$ showing how TPF and accuracy vary, or at minimum reporting the $w$ used for the main results, would substantially improve the paper's practical utility.

### Training Data Scale and Quality Requirements Are Substantial and Under-Discussed

**The assumption or constraint.** Jacobi Forcing requires access to a large corpus of high-quality task-specific prompts *with verified correct completions* to generate Jacobi trajectories. For the coding experiments, the authors curate 450k instances from OpenCodeInstruct specifically filtering for "question instances that come with generations that pass all unit tests" (Section 4.1). For math, they use the math split of Openthought2 with "the same training settings for trajectory generation and training." The trajectory generation process itself requires running the base model (with Jacobi decoding) on each of these prompts, collecting the full Jacobi trajectories (multiple intermediate states per block, each requiring a forward pass), and storing them.

**The consequence.** This data pipeline imposes requirements that may not be met in many practical settings: (1) access to a large corpus of task-specific prompts with verified correct answers (unit tests for code, ground-truth answers for math), (2) sufficient compute budget to run Jacobi decoding on hundreds of thousands of prompts for trajectory collection (each trajectory requires multiple forward passes per block, and the paper's block-by-block Jacobi decoding multiplies this by the number of blocks in each generation), and (3) the base model must already be capable enough on the task to produce correct solutions at a non-trivial rate, since trajectories that never converge to correct answers provide no useful training signal. For domains where verified correct answers are scarce, expensive to obtain, or ambiguous (open-ended generation, creative writing, dialogue, multi-step reasoning without unique correct answers), the trajectory generation pipeline breaks down. Even within coding, the filtering step ("generations that pass all unit tests") discards all prompts where the base model cannot reliably produce correct solutions, which biases the training distribution toward problems the model already handles well — potentially limiting the method's ability to improve on harder problems.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 4.1 specifies the data curation: 450k instances from OpenCodeInstruct filtered for unit-test-passing generations, and the math split of Openthought2. The paper does not report what fraction of the original datasets passed the filtering criteria, how many GPU-hours the trajectory collection consumed, or what storage footprint the collected trajectories required. The ablation checkpoints in Table 4 use only 10k instances (vs. the full 450k), and the results show substantially lower accuracy (81.7–84.7% vs. 83.5% for the full model) — confirming that data scale matters, but without characterizing the scaling curve.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not discuss data efficiency, the cost of trajectory collection, or alternative training paradigms that might reduce data requirements (e.g., online trajectory generation during training rather than offline pre-collection). The iterative distillation procedure (round 2 with self-generated trajectories) partially addresses the requirement for high-quality base model outputs by bootstrapping from the partially-trained model, but it still requires the initial round 1 data. For practitioners with smaller task-specific datasets or domains without clean verification signals, the paper offers no guidance.

### Hardware-Specific Inference Optimization Limits Generality and Reproducibility

**The assumption or constraint.** The inference hyperparameters for Jacobi Forcing Model (MR) — block size, verification size, spawn ratio, number of blocks — are tuned via hardware-specific roofline profiling on A100, H200, and B200 GPUs (Section 4.3, Appendix D, Appendix E). The optimal configuration (block size 64, verification size 4, K=2, r=0.85 on B200) is derived from empirical measurements showing that generation latency is flat up to ~256 parallel tokens on H200/B200 and ~128 tokens on A100 (Figure 7). The paper's speedup claims are measured on these specific GPUs.

**The consequence.** Different hardware — consumer GPUs (RTX 3090, 4090), AMD GPUs, TPUs, Apple Silicon, edge accelerators, or even future NVIDIA GPU generations — will have different rooflines, memory bandwidth, and compute characteristics. The optimal block size and verification size may shift substantially. A practitioner deploying Jacobi Forcing Model on different hardware cannot simply adopt the paper's configuration; they would need to repeat the roofline profiling and hyperparameter grid search (Appendix E) for their specific deployment target. This limits the out-of-the-box applicability of the reported speedup numbers. Furthermore, the hardware-specific tuning makes it difficult to compare Jacobi Forcing Model's efficiency to other methods fairly — a method that achieves 3.5× speedup on A100 with no hardware-specific tuning may be more practically valuable than Jacobi Forcing's 3.86× with tuned hyperparameters, even if the raw number is lower.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 7 shows the generation latency vs. parallel token count curves for A100, H200, and B200 — and they differ substantially. The A100 curve begins rising noticeably after ~128 tokens, while H200 and B200 remain flat to ~256. Figure 8 shows the TPS surface over block size and verification size specifically for B200, with the optimum at block size 64 and verification size 4. Table 3 reports B200 speedup numbers that differ from A100 numbers (Tables 1-2), confirming hardware sensitivity. The paper does not provide analogous TPS surfaces for A100 or H200, nor does it report speedup numbers on any hardware beyond these three datacenter GPU models.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the hardware dependence implicitly by providing the roofline profiling methodology (Appendix D) and the inference configuration search methodology (Appendix E). These serve as recipes for practitioners to replicate the tuning on their own hardware. However, no guidance is offered on how to estimate the roofline without running the full profiling (e.g., whether the parallel-token capacity correlates with GPU FLOPs, memory bandwidth, or SM count in a predictable way), and no "default" configuration is recommended for hardware-agnostic use. The paper also does not discuss whether the speedup gains are robust to less precise tuning — if a practitioner uses block size 64 and verification size 4 on any GPU, how far from optimal are they likely to be?

### No Direct Evidence That Causal Attention Preservation Is the Differentiating Factor

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's central conceptual claim is that preserving causal attention during post-training avoids the pretrain-to-posttrain mismatch that limits dLLM adaptation approaches (which impose bidirectional attention). This claim is supported by the aggregate comparison: Jacobi Forcing Model (causal) achieves 3.86× speedup at 83.5% accuracy, while Fast-dLLM (bidirectional) achieves 1.45× at 53.0%, and D2F (bidirectional) achieves 1.77× at 54.3% on HumanEval (Table 1).

**The consequence.** The aggregate comparison confounds multiple differences simultaneously: attention type (causal vs. bidirectional), training objective (progressive consistency loss vs. NELBO), noise structure (progressive schedule vs. random masking), training data (Jacobi trajectories vs. masked pretraining data), and base architecture (AR model adapted vs. dLLM trained from scratch or adapted). Jacobi Forcing Model outperforms dLLMs, but the paper cannot attribute this to causal attention specifically — it could be primarily due to the progressive noise schedule, the quality of Jacobi trajectory training data, the KL-divergence objective, or the iterative distillation procedure, with the attention type being incidental. The paper's central thesis — that causal prior preservation is "the key" to avoiding the mismatch — requires an ablation where the *same* progressive distillation training is applied with *bidirectional* attention within blocks, and this ablation shows substantially worse results. Without it, the causal claim remains an interpretation, not an empirical finding.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The ablation in Table 5 compares two *causal* attention masking variants: noise-conditioned (NC: each block attends to both clean and noisy preceding blocks, matching inference) versus noise-conditioned with intra-window clean context (NC-IC: noisy blocks attend only to clean blocks from earlier windows, making training easier but mismatching inference). NC achieves 3.6× speedup vs. 1.9× for NC-IC. This demonstrates the importance of train-inference distribution alignment, but it does not test the causal-vs-bidirectional distinction. There is no experiment where bidirectional attention is substituted into the Jacobi Forcing training pipeline. The comparisons with Fast-dLLM and D2F are across entirely different training paradigms, not controlled ablations.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's framing treats the causal-vs-bidirectional distinction as settled by the aggregate comparisons, but a practitioner trying to understand *why* the method works — and whether further gains might be achieved by relaxing causality in controlled ways — gets no direct evidence. The paper would benefit from even a small-scale ablation: train Jacobi Forcing Model with causal masking vs. block-wise bidirectional masking, same progressive schedule, same loss, same data, and compare. This would cleanly isolate the contribution of attention type to the speedup-quality trade-off.

### Inference Optimizations Provide Only Modest Gains Relative to Their Complexity

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper introduces rejection recycling and multi-block decoding (Section 3.2, Algorithm 1) as inference-time optimizations that exploit the Jacobi Forcing Model's emergent fixed-point segments and draft quality in future blocks. These are presented as an orthogonal contribution that further improves speedup beyond the training gains.

**The consequence.** The marginal speedup from these inference optimizations is limited: on A100, Jacobi Forcing Model without MR achieves 3.86× speedup on HumanEval; with MR, this improves to 3.97× — a gain of only 0.11× (2.8% relative improvement, Table 1). On MBPP, the gain is 2.57× → 2.62× (+0.05×, 1.9% relative). On MATH, it is 3.65× → 3.68× (+0.03×, 0.8% relative). The largest gain is on B200 for HumanEval: 3.63× → 3.95× (+0.32×, 8.8% relative, Table 3), suggesting the benefits are hardware-dependent and largest when the GPU has spare compute to absorb the extra parallel verification FLOPs without latency penalty. Given the implementation complexity of multi-block decoding — maintaining up to K blocks simultaneously with real-active/pseudo-active state, managing an n-gram pool with insertion and eviction, handling spawn/promote logic with threshold parameters, performing parallel verification with batch-dimension candidate expansion, and trimming KV caches — a practitioner must weigh whether a 2-9% incremental speedup justifies the engineering effort and the additional hyperparameters (spawn ratio r, number of blocks K, verification size, pool size) that require hardware-specific tuning.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The MR-vs-no-MR comparison is available across all four benchmarks and two hardware platforms (Tables 1-3). Figure 5b shows that MR enables continued scaling of fast-forward count with block size beyond where the running window method plateaus, but the absolute fast-forward count gains do not translate proportionally to wall-clock speedup because the extra FLOPs consume some of the parallelism benefit. Appendix E and Figure 8 detail the hyperparameter tuning required to realize the gains, underscoring the configuration complexity.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the extra FLOPs consumed by MR (Section 4.3: "each technique consumes extra FLOPs for parallel drafting and parallel verification, respectively") and accounts for this cost in the reported TPS numbers. However, the paper does not provide an ablation showing which component (rejection recycling alone, multi-block decoding alone, or both together) contributes how much of the gain, making it unclear whether one of the two techniques dominates or both are necessary. The paper also does not report the engineering effort required to implement MR — the algorithm description (Algorithm 1) is 17 lines with multiple nested conditions and state-management bookkeeping, and a practitioner implementing it from the paper alone would face non-trivial integration complexity. A simpler variant (e.g., rejection recycling without multi-block, or multi-block without recycling) might achieve most of the gain with less complexity, but this is not explored.
