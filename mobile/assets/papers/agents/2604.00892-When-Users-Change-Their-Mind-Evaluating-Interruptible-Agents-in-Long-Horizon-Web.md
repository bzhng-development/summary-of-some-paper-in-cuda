# When Users Change Their Mind: Evaluating Interruptible Agents in Long-Horizon Web Navigation

**ArXiv:** [2604.00892](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00892)

## 🎯 Pitch

Even the strongest LLM agents crumble when users change instructions mid-task—Claude-Opus achieves only 55% success under the simplest interruption type, and half of all failures cascade from repeated mistakes after an update. This study exposes a critical blind spot in agent evaluation by showing that adaptation costs are overwhelmingly driven by skyrocketing token consumption, not extra actions.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces the first systematic study of **interruptible agents** in long-horizon, environmentally constrained web navigation tasks, formalizing three realistic interruption types — addition (adding new requirements mid-task), revision (correcting mistaken initial details), and retraction (removing over-specified constraints) — and instantiating them in **InterruptBench**, a benchmark of 165 human-verified tasks derived from WebArena-Lite evaluated across six LLM backbones (Claude-Haiku-4.5, Claude-Sonnet-4.5, Claude-Opus-4.5, Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B, DeepSeek-V3.1, and Mistral-Large-3) within the WebAgent-R1 scaffolding. Through a trajectory-grounded simulation framework that injects interruptions at dynamically determined execution points and measures both post-interruption success curves and paired-outcome efficiency breakdowns, the authors find that Claude-Opus-4.5 achieves the strongest adaptation (reaching ~55% success by k=30 post-interruption actions under addition, compared to ~23% for Mistral-Large-3), with efficiency analysis revealing that adaptation cost is dominated by token overhead rather than additional actions and that F/F failures incur the highest overhead (e.g., Haiku consuming +2,624 tokens before ultimately failing). Multi-turn interruption experiments show that additional user updates generally improve success — Opus reaches 41.82% with three interruptions versus 21.21% with one — establishing that incremental clarification helps only when models can reliably reconcile environment state with updated intent, a capability that remains uneven across backbones.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Gap: Agents Can't Be Interrupted in Realistic Settings

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is deceptively simple: **LLM agents performing long-horizon tasks in real environments cannot gracefully handle user interruptions mid-execution, and we have no systematic way to evaluate this capability.** This gap exists at the intersection of two converging trends in agentic AI research. On one side, LLM agents are rapidly evolving from solving short, static, single-turn problems to executing complex tasks that span dozens or hundreds of environmental interactions over extended time horizons (Durante et al., 2024; Erdogan et al., 2025). On the other side, realistic deployment of such agents in collaborative settings demands that they be interruptible — real users naturally examine partial results, notice errors, change their minds, or refine goals while the agent is still working, especially when tasks take minutes or hours rather than seconds.

This is not a minor engineering inconvenience. As the paper's case study (Figure F.1) vividly illustrates: an agent tasked with comparing walking versus driving time to Carnegie Mellon University receives an interruption mid-execution specifying that the origin should be Randyland rather than Pittsburgh Downtown. The failure mode is not that the agent ignores the update — it acknowledges it — but rather that it fails to fully reconcile the environment state (the "From" field in the directions form, the already-computed route) with the revised intent, ultimately reporting results conditioned on stale assumptions. This pattern — surface-level acknowledgment without genuine state reconciliation — is what makes interruption handling a fundamentally different challenge from standard task execution.

### Why This Problem Matters

The paper's motivation operates on multiple levels — practical, methodological, and conceptual — that together make interruption handling a first-class research problem rather than an edge case.

**Practical deployment realism.** The paper argues (Section 1) that as LLM agents transition from experimental demonstrations to deployed systems, the assumption of uninterrupted execution becomes increasingly untenable. In real-world settings — whether a customer service agent handling a complex return across multiple web forms, a research assistant executing multi-step literature retrieval, or a coding agent implementing a feature across multiple files — users want to inspect intermediate results and provide corrective feedback. The paper frames this through the lens of **human-agent collaboration**, explicitly connecting to work by Zou et al. (2025a,b) and Shao et al. (2025) that treats human oversight and mid-task intervention as essential for reliability and safety. Without interruptible agents, the collaboration model breaks down: users must either wait until task completion before providing any input (inefficient for long tasks) or abort and restart the agent entirely (wasteful of prior progress).

**The long-horizon interdependence problem.** The paper emphasizes (Section 1) that the challenge is qualitatively different from interruption handling in short, stateless language tasks. In web navigation, the agent's actions produce **persistent state changes** in the environment — clicking buttons, filling forms, navigating pages, submitting data. When an interruption arrives, the agent must not only update its understanding of the goal but also determine which of its already-executed actions are now invalid and must be undone or overridden. A revision that changes the search query on a shopping site means the current results page is stale; a retraction that removes a filter constraint means previously discarded results may now be relevant; an addition of a date range may require revisiting pages already navigated past. This **temporal entanglement** between actions, environment state, and updated intent is what makes long-horizon web navigation a particularly demanding testbed for interruptibility — and what makes prior approaches (which focused on language-only or stateless settings) insufficient.

**Efficiency as a first-class concern.** The paper's emphasis on measuring both **effectiveness** (does the agent eventually succeed?) and **efficiency** (how many extra actions and tokens does adaptation cost?) reflects a pragmatic motivation. In collaborative settings, an agent that eventually recovers from an interruption but requires doubling its remaining action budget is not practically useful — the point of mid-task correction is to save time, not merely to achieve eventual success. The paired-outcome quadrant analysis (S/F, F/S, S/S, F/F in Table 1 and Appendix B.1, B.2) operationalizes this concern by asking not just whether interruptions help or hurt overall, but specifically: when an interruption causes a previously-successful task to fail (S/F), how much extra cost is incurred? When it rescues a failing task (F/S), is the recovery efficient or does it require a near-restart? This dual-measurement framework is motivated by deployment considerations where both accuracy and token/action budget matter.

### Where Prior Work Falls Short

The paper identifies three distinct bodies of related work (extensively reviewed in Appendix A) and pinpoints specific limitations in each.

**Web agent benchmarks assume uninterrupted execution (Section 2.1, Appendix A.1).** The paper builds on WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024) and its derivatives (WebArena-Lite from Koh et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025), which provide realistic, containerized web environments with programmatic success checking across domains like Reddit, GitLab, e-commerce CMS, OpenStreetMap, and online shopping. These benchmarks represent the state of the art for evaluating web agents on long-horizon tasks. However, they all share a critical assumption: the user's intent is fixed and known at the start, and the agent executes without interruption from start to finish. The paper notes that WebArena-Lite's 165 tasks, while carefully curated for reliable evaluation, were all designed and validated under a no-interruption paradigm. Even more recent web agent work — vision-centric grounding approaches (Lu et al., 2024; Gou et al., 2025), hierarchical planning methods (Li et al., 2025; Azam et al., 2025), tree search techniques (Dihan et al., 2025), and reinforcement learning training paradigms (Qi et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025) — optimizes for uninterrupted task completion. The benchmarks, training methods, and evaluation metrics are all designed for a world where the goal is static. This means the community has been systematically optimizing agents for a deployment assumption that does not hold in collaborative settings.

**Human-agent collaboration research focuses on language-only or abstract settings (Appendix A.2).** The paper engages extensively with the human-agent collaboration literature, acknowledging important prior work while identifying a consistent pattern of abstraction away from grounded, stateful environments. Zhang et al. (2024) train LLMs to ask clarifying questions when instructions are ambiguous, but the interaction is purely linguistic — there is no persistent environment whose state must be reconciled. CollabLLM (Wu et al., 2025a) moves models from passive responders to active collaborators generating questions and suggestions, but the tasks remain conversational or information-retrieval based rather than involving web-state manipulation. ReHAC (Feng et al., 2024) introduces reinforcement learning to choose when to request human intervention during multi-step tasks, but under fixed task goals and relatively short horizons. Collaborative Gym (Shao et al., 2024) proposes bidirectional communication between humans and agents in tasks like travel planning and tabular analysis, but the interactions are turn-based communication rather than interruptions that interrupt ongoing execution. The paper positions its work as extending this line of research into settings where "users may change their minds mid-task through additions, revisions, or retractions" and where the agent operates in a persistent environment with stateful consequences.

The closest prior work is Wu et al. (2025b), which studies how large reasoning models behave when users interrupt mid-reasoning or update problem specifications. They identify severe performance drops and failure modes like "reasoning leakage, panic, and self-doubt." However, the paper notes two key limitations of that work: (1) it focuses on language-only or abstract reasoning tasks without grounded environmental constraints, and (2) it studies only single-turn interruptions. This paper's contribution is to extend the study of interruptibility to **long-horizon, environmentally grounded** settings with **multi-turn** interruption patterns — an order of magnitude more complex along both dimensions.

**Interruption handling as a concept is under-formalized.** Beyond specific papers, the paper identifies a conceptual gap: the field lacks a shared vocabulary and formalization for different types of interruptions. The terms "interruption," "update," "correction," and "feedback" are used loosely and interchangeably in prior work, conflating qualitatively different scenarios that place different demands on the agent. The paper's taxonomy of **addition** (new requirements), **revision** (correcting prior mistakes), and **retraction** (removing over-specification) is itself a contribution — it provides analytical categories that enable systematic comparison of interruption types rather than treating all interruptions as equivalent. This formalization is motivated by the observation that different interruption types likely require different adaptation strategies: additions may be compatible with the existing execution plan (just layer on new constraints), revisions may require retracting and redoing prior actions (state reconciliation), and retractions may simplify the task (removing constraints can broaden the set of valid solutions). The paper positions this taxonomy as necessary infrastructure for making interruptibility a rigorous research topic rather than an ad-hoc consideration.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper's positioning is careful and precise: it does not claim to solve interruptible agent execution, nor does it propose a new architecture or training method for handling interruptions. Instead, it positions itself as establishing the **empirical and methodological foundations** for the study of interruptible agents — creating the benchmark, formalizing the problem, characterizing the difficulty via systematic evaluation, and identifying the failure modes that future work must address.

Three aspects of this positioning deserve particular attention:

**1. The benchmark as the primary contribution, not methods.** The paper devotes Section 2 entirely to benchmark construction — defining interruption scenarios, describing the data synthesis pipeline, and specifying the trajectory-grounded simulation framework. This reflects a deliberate choice to prioritize measurement over solution. The data synthesis procedure (detailed in Appendix F) is particularly important: rather than simply having human annotators write interruption scenarios (which would be expensive and hard to scale), the paper uses Claude-Opus-4.5 as a synthesizer with strict constraints — the initial query plus interruptions must have the same meaning and ground truth as the original WebArena-Lite task, each interruption must be essential (lacking any one must change the answer), and the language must be natural. This enables scaling to 165 tasks × 3 interruption types = 495 interruption scenarios while maintaining quality through manual review. The paper is transparent about using Opus for synthesis, which avoids circularity when evaluating Opus as a backbone — but does mean the synthesized data reflects Opus's language patterns, a potential confound the paper does not extensively discuss.

**2. Evaluation over proposal.** The experimental design in Sections 3–4 is structured as a **characterization study** rather than an A/B test of proposed improvements. The six models evaluated span the capability spectrum (from Haiku to Opus, from open-weight Qwen/DeepSeek/Mistral to closed Claude), but the goal is not to declare a winner. Rather, the multi-dimensional evaluation framework — SR(k) post-interruption success curves (Equation 1), paired-outcome efficiency quadrants (S/F, F/S, S/S, F/F), and multi-turn accumulation analysis — is designed to reveal *how* interruption handling breaks and *where* capability differences matter. The paper positions these analysis tools as potentially more valuable than the specific numerical results, since they provide a template for how future work on interruptible agents should be evaluated.

**3. Reframing interruption as information, not disruption.** A subtle but important positioning choice: the paper treats interruptions as **informational** rather than disruptive. Interruptions provide "additional user-supplied necessary information that the agent can leverage to finish the task more effectively" (Section 3.2). They do not reset the environment, invalidate prior progress, or penalize the agent for having taken actions before the update. This framing matters because it separates the *interruptibility* problem (can the agent incorporate new information mid-execution?) from the *fault-tolerance* problem (can the agent survive actions taken under incomplete information?). In the paper's setup, any pre-interruption actions that remain consistent with the updated intent are reusable — the question is whether the agent can correctly identify which actions are still valid and which must be overridden. This is a more optimistic framing than some prior work (e.g., Wu et al., 2025b, which emphasizes performance drops), but it reflects a realistic collaborative assumption: users interrupt to help, not to sabotage, and the agent's pre-interruption progress is genuine work that should be preserved where possible.

The paper's positioning thus establishes InterruptBench as infrastructure for a new subfield: the study of how agents handle changing user intent during grounded, long-horizon task execution. By providing the benchmark, the taxonomy, the simulation framework, the evaluation methodology, and the baseline characterization, the paper creates the conditions for future work on architectures, training methods, and planning algorithms that can make agents genuinely interruptible.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper presents a **benchmark and evaluation framework** — not a new agent architecture or training method — for measuring how well LLM-based web agents handle mid-task user interruptions in long-horizon, environmentally grounded settings. The core idea is that we cannot build interruptible agents without first being able to measure interruptibility systematically, and that measurement requires (a) a formal taxonomy of interruption types, (b) a controlled benchmark where interruptions are synthesized with semantic guarantees, (c) a trajectory-grounded simulation framework that injects interruptions at known points, and (d) evaluation metrics that capture not just final success but also the *efficiency dynamics* of post-interruption adaptation.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components, each with a distinct responsibility:

1. **Task and Environment (WebArena-Lite).** Provides 165 human-verified, long-horizon web navigation tasks across five domains (Reddit, GitLab, CMS, Map, Shopping) with programmatic ground-truth checking. This is the *stage* on which everything else operates. The environment is stateful: agent actions (clicks, typing, navigation) produce persistent changes.

2. **Interruption Synthesis Pipeline.** Takes each original WebArena-Lite task and produces multiple *interruption variants* — an initial user query plus one or more interruption messages that, together, are semantically equivalent to the original task intent. Three synthesis strategies (Addition, Revision, Retraction) cover distinct interruption types, and a fourth (Multi-Turn Mixed) combines them. Quality is enforced by three constraints: ground-truth consistency, necessity of each interruption, and naturalistic tone.

3. **Trajectory-Grounded Simulation Framework.** Given an interruption variant, this framework first executes the initial query to establish a *baseline trajectory*, records the total number of actions, then replays the trajectory while injecting interruption messages at dynamically determined positions (expressed as fractions of the baseline length). Critically, interruptions are *informational only* — they do not reset the environment, penalize prior actions, or force the agent to restart. The agent continues execution from the current environment state, conditioning on both the conversation history and the new user message.

4. **Agent Backbone and Scaffolding.** Six LLMs (Claude-Haiku-4.5, Claude-Sonnet-4.5, Claude-Opus-4.5, Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B, DeepSeek-V3.1, Mistral-Large-3) are evaluated within the **WebAgent-R1** scaffolding, which provides the ReAct-style interaction loop, tool/API surface, observation processing, and termination conditions. This component is held constant across models to isolate backbone capability as the independent variable.

Information flows as follows: a task enters → the synthesis pipeline produces an initial query + interruption message(s) → the simulation framework runs the initial query to get a baseline trajectory → the trajectory is replayed; at a relative position (default 60%), the interruption message is injected into the conversation → the agent continues execution under the updated intent → the final answer is checked against the ground truth using WebAgent-R1's evaluation functions.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the WebArena-Lite task environment, because it defines what "long-horizon web navigation" means concretely and establishes the baseline capabilities that agents are expected to have before we study interruption handling.
- **Second**, the three interruption types and their formal definitions, since the entire synthesis pipeline and evaluation framework are organized around this taxonomy.
- **Third**, the data synthesis pipeline — the prompts, constraints, and manual review process — because understanding how InterruptBench is *constructed* is essential for interpreting results and for reproducibility.
- **Fourth**, the trajectory-grounded simulation framework, which is the mechanism for injecting interruptions at controlled, comparable points across tasks of varying length.
- **Fifth**, the evaluation metrics (SR(k), paired-outcome efficiency quadrants, multi-turn accumulation), which together form the paper's methodological contribution beyond the benchmark itself.
- **Sixth**, the agent scaffolding and model configurations held constant across evaluations, to understand what is being varied and what is fixed.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is a **benchmark and empirical analysis paper** whose core idea is that measuring interruptibility requires: formal interruption categories, a controlled synthesis procedure guaranteeing semantic equivalence to original tasks, a simulation framework that injects interruptions at consistent relative positions, and evaluation metrics that separately capture post-update success dynamics, adaptation efficiency, and behavior under accumulating updates.

#### The Task Environment: WebArena-Lite

The benchmark is built on **WebArena-Lite** (Koh et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025), a curated subset of the original WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024). Understanding this foundation is essential because the interruption synthesis procedure inherits all properties of the underlying tasks — their domains, their ground-truth checking mechanisms, their action spaces, and their length distributions.

**What WebArena provides.** WebArena is a self-hostable, containerized web environment that simulates five realistic web domains:
- **Reddit** (social forum): posting, commenting, voting, searching
- **GitLab** (collaborative coding): repository management, issue tracking, merge requests
- **CMS** (e-commerce content management): product catalog management, customer data
- **Map** (OpenStreetMap-based): directions, location search, route comparison
- **OneStopShop** (online shopping): product browsing, cart management, checkout

Each domain runs as a Docker container with persistent state, and agents interact through a browser automation interface. The original WebArena contains **812 tasks** spanning these domains, each specified as a natural-language intent (e.g., "What is the difference in driving time between route A and route B?") and evaluated through programmatic success checking.

**What WebArena-Lite selects.** WebArena-Lite curates **165 tasks as a human-verified evaluation set** and designates the remaining 647 tasks for training. The curation process involved: summarizing core functionalities and valid items for each website, constructing task prototypes, manually implementing rule-based solvers using Playwright scripts for each prototype, and executing those solvers to collect ground-truth trajectories. This means every one of the 165 tasks has: (1) a verified correct answer, (2) a known sequence of ground-truth actions, and (3) a programmatic evaluation function that can determine success without human judgment. The tasks span the five domains with the following distribution: Reddit, GitLab, CMS, Map, and Shopping (exact per-domain counts are reported in Table E.3's "baseline" row, showing 165 total across all domains).

**Task length and complexity.** Each task in WebArena-Lite can involve **up to 30 LLM calls by default** — the paper mentions this bound explicitly in Section 2.1. This establishes "long-horizon" concretely: tasks require tens of sequential environment interactions, each involving observation processing, reasoning, and action generation. The actual length varies by task and by model (different agents take different numbers of actions to solve the same task), which is why the simulation framework uses *relative* rather than *absolute* interruption positions (see Section 3.4).

**Why this foundation matters for interruptibility.** The WebArena-Lite tasks have three properties that make them well-suited for interruption research:
- **Stateful environments:** Actions produce persistent changes (submitting a form, navigating to a new page, filtering search results) that cannot be trivially undone. When an interruption arrives, the agent must determine which prior actions are still valid and which must be overridden.
- **Programmatic ground truth:** The same evaluation functions that check correctness for uninterrupted tasks can be reused for interrupted tasks, because the synthesis pipeline guarantees semantic equivalence to the original intent (see Section 3.4).
- **Known action trajectories:** The ground-truth solver trajectories provide a reference for what "correct" execution looks like, enabling the trajectory-grounded simulation to estimate task length before injecting interruptions.

The paper explicitly notes that WebArena-Lite was chosen over alternative benchmarks because it provides "reliable constraints on environmental state and programmatic success checking" (Section 2.1) — properties that are essential when the interruption synthesis guarantees require verified semantic equivalence.

#### Interruption Types: Formal Definitions

The paper introduces a three-way taxonomy of interruption types (Section 2.2). This is not merely descriptive — the types are **operationally defined** by how they differ from the original task intent and what adaptation the agent must perform.

**Addition.** An interruption where the user initially provides a basic or incomplete query, and then follows up with additional details, new information, or supplementary requirements. The defining characteristic is that the initial query is a *subset* of the final intent: it is missing necessary constraints that the interruption supplies. The agent's adaptation challenge is *constraint integration* — layering new requirements onto an existing plan without disrupting already-completed valid steps.

Example from the paper (Figure 1): a user initially asks "What are the top-5 best-selling products?" and then interrupts with "I meant for Jan 2023 specifically." The initial query is answerable as stated (top-5 overall) but incomplete relative to the true intent (top-5 for January 2023). The interruption adds a temporal filter that the agent must now apply.

**Revision.** An interruption where the user initially provides an incorrect or mistaken query, and then follows up with corrections or modifications. The defining characteristic is that the initial query contains *wrong* information that the interruption corrects. The agent's adaptation challenge is *state reconciliation* — identifying which executed actions were predicated on the wrong information and must be retracted or overridden.

Example: a user asks for driving time from "Carnegie Museum of Natural History" and then interrupts with "Sorry, I meant Carnegie Museum of Art, not Natural History." The initial destination was factually wrong relative to the user's true intent. Any navigation steps, route computations, or displayed results based on the wrong museum are stale and must be redone.

**Retraction.** An interruption where the user initially provides a query with extra details or constraints, and then follows up to remove or retract some of those details. The defining characteristic is that the initial query is a *superset* of the final intent: it includes unnecessary constraints that the interruption removes. The agent's adaptation challenge is *constraint relaxation* — determining whether previously discarded options (filtered out by the now-retracted constraint) are actually valid, and potentially re-exploring parts of the state space.

Example: a user asks for "my RSS feed token for my active projects that I created myself and show the expiry date," then interrupts with "Actually, just get my general RSS feed token, not specific to projects." The initial query over-specified (project-specific token, with expiry), and the retraction relaxes to just the general token. Any navigation into project-specific pages may have been unnecessary or may need to be backed out.

**Baseline: No-Interruption.** The paper also evaluates a baseline where the agent receives only the initial user query with no interruptions during execution. This serves as a lower bound for post-interruption adaptation curves: even without receiving the update, agents evaluated against the final (post-update) intent sometimes succeed because their actions happen to be consistent with the updated requirements.

**Multi-turn combinations.** In multi-turn settings (Section 3.2), interruptions are "random sequences, where the interruption type can vary across turns. For example, an Addition followed by a Revision and then a Retraction." This mirrors realistic interactions where users provide mixed types of feedback over an extended conversation.

**Why these three types?** The paper does not provide an exhaustive justification for this particular taxonomy, but the types emerge naturally from considering what can differ between an initial query and the true user intent: the initial query can be missing information (→ Addition), contain wrong information (→ Revision), or contain extra information (→ Retraction). These three exhaust the logical possibilities for set-based relationships between initial and final constraints: the initial set can be a subset, a different set (with non-empty intersection), or a superset of the final set. The taxonomy thus has a clean formal structure, even if the paper does not explicitly present it in set-theoretic terms.

#### Data Synthesis Pipeline

The data synthesis pipeline (Section 2.2, with full prompts in Appendix F) constructs, for each of the 165 WebArena-Lite tasks, an initial user query and one or more interruption messages. The synthesis is performed by **Claude-Opus-4.5** using carefully engineered prompts, and all generated data are **manually reviewed by the authors**.

**The three quality constraints.** The paper enforces three constraints on every synthesized interruption scenario:

> "(1) *Ground Truth Consistency*: The initial user query combined with the user interruption messages should have the same meaning as the original intent and result in the same ground truth answers, enabling reliable evaluation."

This is the critical constraint that makes the benchmark evaluable. Because the final intent (initial query + all interruption messages) is semantically equivalent to the original WebArena-Lite task, the existing programmatic evaluation functions can be reused without modification. If an agent produces the same answer after handling interruptions as it would have produced on the original task, it has successfully adapted.

> "(2) *Necessity*: Each interruption message should be essential; lacking any interruption message should result in different ground truth answers from the original intent."

This constraint prevents "filler" interruptions that add no information. Every interruption message must change the answer if omitted — otherwise, the interruption is not testing the agent's ability to incorporate new information. This ensures that the benchmark measures genuine adaptation rather than robustness to irrelevant noise.

> "(3) *Authenticity*: Each interruption message should be expressed in a natural and realistic user/customer tone."

This constraint ensures ecological validity. Interruptions that sound like synthetic template insertions (e.g., "CORRECTION: destination = 'Carnegie Museum of Art'") would test a different capability than interruptions phrased as a real person would phrase them ("Sorry, I meant Carnegie Museum of Art, not Natural History"). The natural language wrapping is part of the challenge.

**Per-type synthesis prompts (Appendix F).** The paper provides complete prompts for four synthesis strategies:

- **Addition prompt (F.1):** Instructs the model to take the original intent and generate a `transformed_initial_intent` that is "less detailed" — missing critical information — then produce updates that add the missing details. The prompt emphasizes that the transformed initial intent combined with updates must have "the same meaning as the original intent query."

- **Revision prompt (F.2):** Instructs the model to create a `transformed_initial_intent` that changes critical details to "plausible but incorrect" alternatives (e.g., wrong date, wrong product, wrong location), then produce updates that correct these mistakes. The prompt specifies that the corrected version must, again, recover the original intent.

- **Retraction prompt (F.3):** Instructs the model to create a `transformed_initial_intent` that adds "additional critical detail(s)/constraint(s)" beyond the original intent — extra filters, additional requirements, more specifications — then produce updates that remove or retract those extras. The final result (after retraction) must match the original intent.

- **Multi-turn mixed prompt (F.4):** Instructs the model to create an initial query that is simultaneously incomplete, partially incorrect, and over-constrained, then produce a sequence of updates of mixed types (additions, modifications, retractions) in randomized order. Each update must be labeled with its type in an `updates_type` array. The prompt specifies that "the updates should include a mix of at least three different types."

All prompts share a common output format: a JSON object per task containing `task_id`, `intent` (original), `transformed_initial_intent`, and `updates` (array of interruption messages). The multi-turn prompt additionally includes `updates_type`.

**The `num_update` parameter.** Each synthesis prompt takes a `num_update` parameter that controls how many interruption messages are generated. For single-turn interruption scenarios, `num_update = 1` (one interruption message). For multi-turn scenarios, the paper evaluates `n ∈ {1, 2, 3}` interruptions (Table 2), meaning `num_update` is set to 2 and 3 to generate sequences of that length. The paper does not specify the maximum `num_update` used during synthesis; the evaluation in Table 2 stops at `n = 3`.

**Why Claude-Opus-4.5 for synthesis?** The paper uses Opus as the synthesis model by default (Section 3.1: "We leverage Claude-Opus-4.5 for data synthesis by default"). This is a deliberate choice with implications: (1) Opus is the strongest model evaluated, so it is likely to produce the highest-quality synthetic interruptions; (2) it creates a potential confound — interruptions reflect Opus's language patterns and might be most naturally interpretable by Opus itself; (3) the paper acknowledges this implicitly by evaluating Opus alongside other models rather than excluding it from evaluation (which would avoid the circularity concern). The manual review step partially mitigates the confound by ensuring the interruptions are natural and correct regardless of which model they reflect.

**Scale of synthesis.** For each of the 165 tasks, the paper synthesizes three interruption variants (one per type: addition, revision, retraction), yielding **165 × 3 = 495 single-turn interruption scenarios**. Multi-turn scenarios are generated separately using the mixed prompt, though the paper does not report the total number of multi-turn episodes evaluated — only the success rates by interruption count (Table 2).

**Manual review.** All generated data are "manually reviewed by the authors to ensure that each interruption message is necessary for correctly solving the problems" (Section 2.2). The paper does not report inter-annotator agreement, the proportion of synthesized items that required revision, or the criteria used for accepting/rejecting items beyond the stated constraints. This is a transparency limitation: without knowing what fraction of syntheses failed review and why, it is difficult to assess how much the synthesis pipeline depends on Opus's specific capabilities versus being generalizable to other synthesis models.

#### Trajectory-Grounded Interruption Simulation

The simulation framework (Section 2.3) is the mechanism that translates static interruption scenarios into dynamic, mid-execution interruptions during agent runs. It consists of three sequential steps:

**Step 1: Generate initial full trajectory.**

The agent is first executed on the `transformed_initial_intent` (the incomplete/incorrect/over-specified query) with *no interruptions*, producing a complete trajectory from start to finish (success or failure). The simulation records the **total number of actions** in this trajectory. This serves two purposes:
- It establishes a reference length for the task under a particular model and interruption type, avoiding the need for a fixed global step count.
- It provides the denominator for relative interruption positioning.

**Step 2: Replay with progress-aware interruption.**

The simulation then replays the trajectory sequentially — meaning it re-executes the agent from the beginning with the same initial query — and injects the interruption based on **relative progress** rather than a fixed absolute step. Specifically:

> "for each task, the interruption is introduced at a predefined proportion of the initial trajectory, so the exact interruption step is automatically determined by the initial trajectory length and varies across tasks."

The default proportion is **60%** (Section 3.2: "We inject interruptions at a relative position (60% by default) of the baseline trajectory length"). This means that if the initial trajectory took 20 actions, the interruption is injected after action 12 (0.6 × 20 = 12). If a different model took 30 actions on the same task, the interruption would arrive after action 18.

**Why relative positioning?** The paper's motivation is that tasks vary in length across both tasks and models, and a fixed absolute step would produce inconsistent results: an interruption at action 5 might be early in a 30-step task but late in an 8-step task. Relative positioning normalizes the amount of progress made before interruption, enabling cross-task and cross-model comparison. The paper also evaluates sensitivity to this choice by testing interruption positions at {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} (Appendix E, Table E.3).

**Step 3: Insert interruption and continue execution.**

At the selected step, the additional user message is inserted into the conversation. The agent then continues generating the post-interruption trajectory conditioned on the updated intent. Crucially:

> "interruptions are informational: they do not reset the agent or the environment, and do not invalidate progress already made. Instead, they provide additional user-supplied necessary information that the agent can leverage to finish the task more effectively."

This means:
- The environment state at the interruption point is whatever it became through the agent's previous actions — pages navigated to, forms filled, filters applied.
- The agent's conversation history includes all previous observations, thoughts, and actions, plus the new user message.
- The agent must determine which of its prior actions are still consistent with the updated intent and which must be overridden, but it is not *forced* to undo anything; it can choose to reuse valid progress.

**Single-turn versus multi-turn simulation.** For single-turn settings, one interruption message is injected. For multi-turn settings, the simulation injects multiple interruption messages sequentially — after the agent processes interruption 1 and takes some actions, interruption 2 is injected, and so on. The paper does not specify the exact timing strategy for multi-turn interruptions (e.g., whether subsequent interruptions are also at relative positions, or at fixed intervals, or triggered by specific agent behaviors), which is a gap in the methodological description.

**Evaluation under different interruption positions (Appendix E).** To test sensitivity to the relative position choice, the paper evaluates models with interruptions injected at {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} of the baseline trajectory (Section E, Table E.3). This probes how the amount of committed intermediate state prior to interruption affects post-update adaptation. The results reveal a scale-dependent pattern: large-scale models (Claude-Opus-4.5) perform best when interruptions occur later in the trajectory, while smaller models (Claude-Haiku-4.5) peak under early interruption and degrade as interruption is delayed.

#### Evaluation Metrics: SR(k), Paired-Outcome Efficiency, and Multi-Turn Accumulation

The paper introduces three complementary evaluation dimensions (Section 3.3), each measuring a different aspect of interruptibility.

**Post-interruption success curve SR(k).** This is the primary metric for adaptation effectiveness. It is defined as:

$$SR(k) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}[\tau_i \leq k]$$

where `$N$` is the total number of episodes, `$\tau_i$` is the number of actions taken after the interruption until success is achieved in episode `$i$` (if the episode never succeeds, `$\tau_i = \infty$`), and `$\mathbb{I}[\tau_i \leq k]$` is an indicator function that equals 1 if the episode succeeded within `$k$` post-interruption actions and 0 otherwise.

**What it computes:** For each post-interruption action budget `$k$`, this function returns the fraction of episodes that achieved the final (post-update) intent using `$k$` or fewer actions after receiving the interruption. As `$k$` increases, `$SR(k)$` is a monotonically non-decreasing curve that starts near zero (no adaptation happens in zero actions) and plateaus at the eventual success rate of the model on that interruption type.

**Why this form:** The indicator-based definition produces a **budget-limited success rate** that operationalizes a key practical concern: in human-in-the-loop settings, a user who provides a correction wants to know *how many more actions* the agent will take before it either succeeds or gives up. A model might eventually achieve the same success rate as another but require far more post-update actions to do so — that efficiency difference would be invisible in a final-success-rate metric but is captured by the shape of `$SR(k)$`. The curve's steepness in the `$k \approx 1-10$` range indicates how quickly the model integrates updates; its asymptote indicates maximum achievable adaptation. An alternative metric — simply reporting final success rate — would conflate models with fast, efficient adaptation and models that eventually succeed through exhaustive re-exploration.

**What the dashed baselines mean.** In Figure 2, each solid `$SR(k)$` curve is paired with a dashed "baseline" curve. The dashed curves represent matched no-interruption runs — the agent receives only the initial (pre-update) query but is *evaluated* against the final (post-update) intent. These baselines measure how often the agent's actions happen to satisfy the updated requirements by coincidence, without ever being told about the update. The gap between solid and dashed curves therefore isolates the *causal effect* of receiving the interruption message: any improvement beyond the dashed baseline is directly attributable to the agent incorporating the update.

**Paired-outcome efficiency quadrants.** To disentangle the effect of interruptions on outcome from their effect on cost, the paper assigns each episode a paired label `$(o_{\text{base}}, o_{\text{int}}) \in \{S, F\}^2$`, where `$o_{\text{base}}$` and `$o_{\text{int}}$` denote the success (S) or failure (F) of the matched baseline run and the interrupted run, respectively. The matched baseline run is a no-interruption run on the same underlying task, constructed from the same original WebArena-Lite task. This yields four exhaustive and mutually exclusive outcome quadrants:

- **S/F**: The baseline (no interruption) succeeded, but the interrupted run failed. The interruption is *outcome-degrading* — it turned a solvable task into an unsolvable one for this model.
- **F/S**: The baseline failed, but the interrupted run succeeded. The interruption is *outcome-improving* — it rescued a task the model could not solve without the update.
- **S/S**: Both succeeded. Here, the efficiency comparison is between two successful runs on the same task, isolating the *cost* of adaptation without confounding by outcome.
- **F/F**: Both failed. Here, the question is whether the interruption induces *fail-fast* behavior (the agent gives up quickly after the update) or *prolonged unproductive interaction* (the agent consumes many actions and tokens before ultimately failing).

For each quadrant, the paper reports the **number of cases** and the **mean action and token deltas** `$\Delta A = A_{\text{int}} - A_{\text{base}}$` and `$\Delta T = T_{\text{int}} - T_{\text{base}}$`, where positive values indicate higher cost under interruption.

**Why this form:** A simple average delta across all episodes would conflate episodes where the interruption improved outcome (F/S) with episodes where it degraded outcome (S/F), masking important patterns. The quadrant breakdown is a form of *stratified analysis* that conditions on the causal effect of the interruption on success. For example, if the average token overhead is +100 but broken down as +500 for F/F episodes and -300 for S/S episodes, the interpretation changes dramatically: the overhead is concentrated in episodes that fail anyway (suggesting futile re-planning), while successful adaptation is actually *cheaper* (suggesting efficient reuse of prior work).

**Multi-turn accumulation analysis.** For episodes with `$n \in \{1, 2, 3\}$` interruptions, the paper computes `$SR(k \mid n)$` — the post-interruption success curve after the `$n$`-th interruption — and reports the converged success rate as `$n$` increases (Table 2). The key analytical question is: **does providing more information through additional interruptions monotonically improve success, or do later updates sometimes interfere with earlier ones?**

The paper presents this as a marginal gain analysis. From Table 2:
- Opus: 21.21% at `$n=1$` → 36.36% at `$n=2$` (gain of +15.15 points) → 41.82% at `$n=3$` (gain of +5.46 points). Gains are positive but diminishing.
- DeepSeek-V3.1: 12.12% at `$n=1$` → 21.21% at `$n=2$` (gain of +9.09 points) → 20.61% at `$n=3$` (loss of -0.6 points). The third update interferes with previously incorporated information.

**Why this matters:** The diminishing or negative marginal gains for some models reveal a failure mode that would be invisible in single-turn evaluation: the capacity to integrate sequential updates is limited, and later updates can *destabilize* earlier adaptation. This is the multi-turn analog of the F/S–S/F distinction in single-turn analysis.

#### Agent Scaffolding and Model Configuration

All six models are evaluated within the **WebAgent-R1** agent scaffolding (Wei et al., 2025), with "prompting format, tool/API surface, termination conditions, and maximum action budget identical across models" (Section 3.1). This is essential for isolating model capability as the independent variable — any differences in adaptation behavior must be attributable to the model's reasoning and planning capabilities, not to differences in the interaction framework.

**Models evaluated.** The six backbones span the capability spectrum:
- **Closed-source Claude family:** Claude-Haiku-4.5 (fastest/smallest), Claude-Sonnet-4.5 (mid-tier), Claude-Opus-4.5 (largest/most capable)
- **Open-weight models:** Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B (abbreviated as Qwen3-480B-A35B), DeepSeek-V3.1, Mistral-Large-3

The paper provides Anthropic system card citations for the Claude models (Anthropic, 2025a,b,c), the Qwen3 technical report (Yang et al., 2025), the DeepSeek-V3 technical report (Liu et al., 2024), and a visual encoders paper for Mistral (Jiang et al., 2023) — though the Mistral reference appears to be a citation mismatch (the Jiang et al. paper is about visual encoders, not Mistral-Large-3).

**What WebAgent-R1 provides.** WebAgent-R1 is a web agent scaffolding that provides:
- A ReAct-style interaction loop: the model receives an observation (page state), produces a reasoning trace (the "thinking" block), then emits an action.
- A fixed tool and API surface for web interaction: `Click`, `Type`, and navigation actions, with element IDs referencing the accessibility tree or DOM.
- Termination conditions: the agent can call `exit(message=...)` to end the episode and report its answer.
- A maximum action budget (implicitly set by WebArena-Lite's 30-call default, though the paper evaluates up to `$k=30$` post-interruption actions).
- Three rule-based evaluation metrics provided with WebArena-Lite: **String Match**, **URL Match**, and **Program Execution** (Section 3.3).

**Fixed across models.** The paper emphasizes that prompting format, tool surface, termination conditions, and maximum action budget are identical across models. This means observed differences in `$SR(k)$` curves, efficiency deltas, and multi-turn accumulation patterns reflect genuine model capability differences rather than artifacts of different prompting or tool availability.

**What varies across models.** Only the LLM backbone itself varies. Each model processes the same observations, receives the same interruption messages at the same relative positions, and has access to the same actions. The model-specific differences therefore arise from: (1) the model's ability to *recognize* that the interruption changes the task goal, (2) its ability to *identify* which prior actions are now invalid, (3) its ability to *plan* the necessary corrective actions, and (4) its ability to *execute* those actions efficiently.

#### Summary of Design Choices and Their Justifications

- **WebArena-Lite as the task foundation** over building a new environment: provides established reliability, programmatic evaluation, and ground-truth trajectories, enabling the semantic equivalence constraint in synthesis.
- **Claude-Opus-4.5 as the synthesis model** over human annotation: enables scaling to 495+ interruption scenarios while maintaining quality through prompt engineering and manual review. The trade-off is potential Opus-specific language patterns that could favor Opus during evaluation.
- **Three-type interruption taxonomy** (addition, revision, retraction) over treating all interruptions as equivalent: captures qualitatively different adaptation challenges (constraint integration vs. state reconciliation vs. constraint relaxation) that would be convolved in undifferentiated "interruption" experiments.
- **Relative interruption positioning** (fraction of baseline trajectory) over fixed absolute steps: normalizes for variable task lengths across tasks and models, enabling cross-model comparison.
- **Informational interruption semantics** over disruptive/reset semantics: isolates the *integration* challenge from the *fault-tolerance* challenge, and reflects realistic collaborative assumptions where user feedback aims to help rather than to penalize.
- **SR(k) budget-limited success curves** over final success rate: captures the *speed* of adaptation, which is practically important when users are waiting for the agent to incorporate their feedback.
- **Paired-outcome efficiency quadrants** over average delta: stratifies by the causal effect of interruption on outcome, revealing whether overhead is concentrated in productive (F/S) or unproductive (F/F) adaptation.
- **Six-model evaluation** over single-model: establishes that interruptibility is a capability that varies substantially across backbones, characterizing the difficulty of the benchmark rather than just reporting one model's performance.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Interruptibility as a Distinct Capability Axis, Not a Robustness Afterthought

The paper's most fundamental contribution is conceptual: it reframes interruptibility from an edge-case robustness concern into a **first-class capability axis** that demands its own benchmarks, formalisms, and evaluation methodology. Before this work, the dominant assumption across web agent research — from WebArena (Zhou et al., 2024) through WebVoyager (He et al., 2024), tree-search methods (Dihan et al., 2025), and RL-based training (Qi et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2025) — was that the user's intent is **fixed, complete, and known at task start**. Agents were designed, trained, and evaluated under a "set it and forget it" paradigm where execution proceeds uninterrupted from initial instruction to final answer. Any deviation from this assumption — a user changing their mind, noticing an error, or refining their goal mid-execution — was implicitly treated as an out-of-distribution anomaly that a sufficiently robust agent might survive but that no one was systematically measuring.

This paper argues, implicitly but forcefully, that this assumption is **wrong in principle for collaborative deployment**. The framing in Section 1 — "agents must be interruptible to handle dynamic user intent, as users naturally want to examine partial results, correct spotted errors, or revise goals mid-execution over such a long horizon" — positions interruptibility not as an optional feature but as a **definitional requirement** for realistic agent operation. If a task takes dozens of actions and minutes of wall-clock time, the probability that a human collaborator will want to intervene approaches 1. An agent that cannot handle such intervention gracefully is not just "less robust" — it is **architecturally incompatible** with collaborative workflows.

This shift is comparable to how the field moved from evaluating models on static QA benchmarks to evaluating them as interactive agents. Just as "can the model answer questions?" is a different question from "can the model navigate a website to discover answers?", "can the agent complete a fixed task?" is a different question from "can the agent adapt when the task changes mid-execution?" The paper establishes that these are not just quantitatively different — the capability to handle interruptions is **qualitatively distinct** from baseline task-solving capability, with its own failure modes (state staleness, incomplete intent reconciliation, unstable multi-turn integration) that are invisible in uninterrupted evaluation.

**Evidence for distinctness.** The most compelling evidence that interruptibility is a separate capability axis comes from the **paired-outcome quadrant analysis** (Table 1 and Appendices B.1, B.2). For the Addition scenario, Opus has 70 F/S cases (baseline fails, interrupted succeeds) versus only 1 S/F case (baseline succeeds, interrupted fails). This means the interruption *rescues* 70 tasks that the same model could not solve without the update — a clear demonstration that the model can productively incorporate new information. But Haiku has only 52 F/S cases versus 3 S/F cases — still net positive, but with a lower rescue rate and a higher degradation rate. These ratios are not simply predicted by baseline task success; they reflect an orthogonal capability to **reconcile existing execution state with revised intent**. If interruptibility were just "baseline capability + some noise," we would expect S/F cases to be extremely rare (why would a capable model fail after receiving *more* information?). The existence of S/F cases — and their variation across models (Sonnet: +21.75 actions and +8,584 tokens in S/F, indicating extended futile effort) — shows that interruption handling can be **anti-capability**: additional information can actively derail an otherwise successful execution.

**Comparison to prior work.** Prior human-agent collaboration research (reviewed in Appendix A.2) studied interaction in language-only or abstract planning settings. CollabLLM (Wu et al., 2025a) trained models to ask clarifying questions *before* execution begins; ReHAC (Feng et al., 2024) chose opportune moments for human input during fixed-goal tasks; Wu et al. (2025b) studied mid-reasoning interruptions in language tasks. None of this work grappled with the defining challenge of *grounded* interruptibility: the entanglement between already-executed actions, persistent environment state, and revised intent. By grounding interruptions in WebArena-Lite's stateful web environments, the paper forces the question: **can the agent determine which of its prior actions are still valid?** This is a fundamentally different capability than "can the agent understand the new instruction," and it has no analog in stateless language tasks.

**Significance beyond performance.** The paper's primary contribution here is not a number but a **reframing that enables future research**. By creating InterruptBench with formal interruption types, a synthesis pipeline guaranteeing semantic equivalence, and a multi-dimensional evaluation framework (SR(k) curves, paired-outcome quadrants, multi-turn accumulation), the paper provides the conceptual and methodological infrastructure for studying interruptibility as a rigorous research problem rather than an anecdotal concern. The taxonomy of addition/revision/retraction is particularly important: by distinguishing **constraint integration** (addition), **state reconciliation** (revision), and **constraint relaxation** (retraction) as distinct adaptation challenges, the paper enables future work to target specific failure modes rather than treating all interruptions as an undifferentiated blob.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **fundamental reframing**. The paper does not improve an existing capability by 4× or propose a clever architectural tweak — it identifies a capability that the field was **not measuring at all** and creates the conditions for measuring it. This is analogous to how the introduction of benchmarks like WebArena itself shifted the agent evaluation paradigm from toy tasks to realistic web navigation. InterruptBench may or may not become the standard benchmark for interruptibility, but the conceptual move — interruptibility as a first-class evaluation axis — is likely to persist regardless of the specific benchmark implementation.

### Innovation 2: The Adaptation Dynamics Curve as a Diagnostic Tool

The paper's second conceptual contribution is methodological: the introduction of **SR(k)**, the post-interruption success curve (Section 3.3, Equation 1), as a diagnostic instrument for characterizing *how* agents adapt rather than just *whether* they eventually succeed. Final success rate — the dominant metric in web agent evaluation — collapses the entire post-interruption trajectory into a single binary outcome, discarding all information about the temporal dynamics of adaptation. Two models might both achieve 55% eventual success on addition interruptions, but if one reaches that 55% within k=5 post-update actions while the other requires k=25, their practical utility in collaborative settings is radically different: the fast adapter gives the user near-immediate feedback, while the slow adapter keeps the user waiting through extended re-planning and re-execution that may negate the time-saving purpose of mid-task correction.

The SR(k) curve makes this temporal dimension **visible and quantifiable**. By plotting success rate as a function of post-interruption action budget, the curve reveals three diagnostically distinct phases:

- **The uptake rate (k ~ 1–5):** How steeply the curve rises immediately after interruption indicates whether the model quickly recognizes the update and takes corrective action, or whether it continues with stale execution for several steps before adapting.
- **The inflection point (k ~ 5–15):** Where the curve bends indicates the typical number of actions required for adaptation to take effect. Models that need extensive re-planning show later inflection points.
- **The asymptote (k ~ 20–30):** The eventual plateau indicates the maximum achievable adaptation given unlimited budget. Models with higher asymptotes translate more of their baseline capability into post-update success.

**What Figure 2 reveals that final success would hide.** Consider the Addition scenario in Figure 2. Both Opus and Sonnet show rising SR(k) curves, but their shapes differ meaningfully. Opus's curve rises more steeply in the k=1–10 range and plateaus higher (~55% at k=30). Sonnet's curve rises more gradually and plateaus lower (~44%). A final-success-rate metric would report these as two numbers (55% vs. 44%), but SR(k) reveals that Sonnet is slower to adapt at every budget level — not just lower at the finish. Even more revealing is the comparison of solid (interrupted) versus dashed (no-interruption baseline) curves. For Revision, the baselines remain near zero across all k for most models, meaning that when the initial query contains factually wrong information, the agent essentially *cannot* accidentally succeed — the update is necessary. The gap between solid and dashed curves here directly measures the causal effect of receiving the correction. For Retraction, by contrast, the baselines are notably stronger, meaning that even without being told about the retraction, agents sometimes satisfy the relaxed constraints by coincidence. The primary benefit of the retraction update is thus *earlier* task completion under tight budgets (visible in the leftward shift of the solid curve relative to the dashed curve at low k) rather than a substantially higher final ceiling.

**Why this is more than "just another metric."** The SR(k) curve operationalizes a principle that is intuitive but rarely formalized in agent evaluation: **efficiency and effectiveness are not separable concerns**. An agent that eventually succeeds after extensive re-execution has not "handled the interruption" in any practically meaningful sense — it has brute-forced recovery by effectively restarting. The SR(k) curve forces evaluators to confront this by making the speed of adaptation an explicit dimension of performance. This is particularly important for interruptibility because the *raison d'être* of mid-task correction is to save time: if the correction plus recovery takes as long as letting the original (flawed) execution complete and then starting over, the interruption provided no practical benefit.

**Comparison to prior evaluation methodology.** Web agent benchmarks have historically reported success rate as a single scalar (Zhou et al., 2024; Koh et al., 2024) or, in some cases, success rate plus trajectory length as separate metrics (Wei et al., 2025). The innovation in SR(k) is the **integration** of success and budget into a single parametric curve, enabling analysis of the *tradeoff* between them. This is conceptually similar to how precision-recall curves integrate two metrics that are separately meaningful but jointly more informative. The paper's use of SR(k) to compare models at fixed budget levels (effectively taking vertical slices through the curve family) and to track marginal gains from additional interruptions (Figure 3) demonstrates the curve's versatility as an analytical tool.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **methodological refinement** that has the potential to become a standard evaluation practice. The idea of budget-limited evaluation is not entirely novel — it appears in work on test-time compute scaling and anytime algorithms — but its application to interruptible agent evaluation, combined with the paired baseline comparison (dashed curves) and the multi-turn extension (SR(k|n) in Figure 3), represents a significant methodological contribution. The persistence of single-scalar success-rate reporting in the web agent literature suggests that SR(k)'s adoption would represent a meaningful improvement in evaluation rigor.

### Innovation 3: The Paired-Outcome Quadrant Analysis as a Causal Decomposition of Interruption Effects

The paper's third conceptual move is the introduction of the **paired-outcome quadrant framework** (S/F, F/S, S/S, F/F) for analyzing the causal effect of interruptions on both success and efficiency. This framework decomposes the aggregate effect of interruptions into four mutually exclusive categories based on whether the matched no-interruption baseline succeeded or failed, enabling a causal interpretation that is impossible with average metrics alone.

**Why simple averaging is misleading.** Consider a hypothetical: Model A and Model B both show an average token overhead of +100 after interruptions. For Model A, the breakdown is: +500 tokens in F/F episodes (many failed runs with extended re-planning), −300 tokens in S/S episodes (successful adaptation is actually cheaper than the baseline), and a mix of S/F and F/S cases. For Model B, the breakdown is: +100 tokens uniformly across all quadrants. These two models have *radically different* adaptation behaviors — Model A has efficient success adaptation but catastrophically expensive failures, while Model B has uniform mild overhead — but they are indistinguishable by average overhead alone.

The quadrant framework makes these differences legible. It answers four distinct causal questions:

- **S/F episodes: When does the interruption *cause* failure?** These are tasks the model could solve without the update but fails after receiving it. The existence of S/F cases — and the paper shows they exist for all models, ranging from 1 (Opus) to 5 (Haiku, DeepSeek, Mistral) under Addition — is a striking finding. It means interruptions can be **outcome-degrading**: the model fails to reconcile the update with its existing execution plan, and the resulting confusion turns a solvable task unsolvable. The cost deltas in S/F are revealing: Sonnet expends +21.75 actions and +8,584 tokens in S/F cases (Table 1), indicating the model engages in extensive but ultimately futile re-planning before failing. This is not efficient failure — it is the worst of both worlds.

- **F/S episodes: When does the interruption *rescue* a failing task?** These are the success stories of interruptibility — tasks the model could not solve without the update, but can solve after receiving it. The prevalence of F/S cases varies dramatically across models: Opus achieves 70, Sonnet 55, DeepSeek 35, Qwen 32, Mistral 27. This gradient tracks roughly with overall model capability but is not reducible to it — it reflects the specific ability to integrate new information into a partially-executed plan. The efficiency deltas in F/S are also diagnostic: Opus recovers with −2.21 actions and −92.7 tokens (the update makes the task *more efficient* than the baseline, likely because the update provides information the baseline had to discover through exploration), while Haiku requires +6.67 actions and +1,808 tokens (the update helps but the adaptation itself is expensive).

- **S/S episodes: When both succeed, what is the adaptation cost?** These episodes isolate the pure *overhead* of handling an interruption, uncontaminated by outcome changes. Across models, S/S episodes show the smallest or most negative deltas (e.g., Opus: −1.86 actions, −187.64 tokens for Haiku), indicating that when adaptation is successful, it often reuses pre-interruption progress efficiently rather than restarting from scratch.

- **F/F episodes: When both fail, does the interruption make failure more or less expensive?** The paper finds that F/F episodes consistently incur the highest overhead: Haiku +2,624 tokens, Sonnet +1,128 tokens. This pattern — extended unproductive re-planning in episodes that ultimately fail — is a specific failure mode that the quadrant analysis makes visible.

**What this reveals about model differences.** The quadrant framework reveals that the efficiency gap between strong and weak models is not just about who succeeds more often — it is about *how failure manifests*. Opus's 1 S/F case versus Sonnet's 4 and Haiku's 3 (under Addition) suggests that stronger models are not just more capable of recovering from interruptions (F/S) but also more *robust* to interruptions — less likely to be derailed by an update on a task they would otherwise have solved. Similarly, Opus's negative deltas in F/S (−2.21 actions, −92.7 tokens) versus Haiku's positive deltas (+6.67 actions, +1,808 tokens) suggest that stronger models integrate updates more surgically, modifying only what needs modification, while weaker models engage in broader re-planning that undoes more prior work.

**Comparison to prior work.** Stratified analysis is common in many fields (medicine, economics, causal inference), but its application to agent evaluation — and specifically to the *causal effect of an intervention on both outcome and cost* — is a genuine methodological contribution to the LLM agent literature. Prior work on human-agent interaction (Wu et al., 2025b; Shao et al., 2024) reported aggregate effects of interruptions or collaboration, eliding the heterogeneity that the quadrant framework surfaces. The framework also implicitly frames interruptions as a **treatment** in a causal sense, with the matched no-interruption baseline serving as the control condition — a framing that is more rigorous than "we tested with and without interruptions and reported averages."

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is a **methodological innovation** that significantly raises the bar for evaluation in this area. The quadrant framework is not mathematically complex (it is essentially a 2×2 contingency table with cost statistics in each cell), but its conceptual value is disproportionate to its simplicity. By forcing evaluators to condition on the counterfactual outcome, it reveals patterns that aggregate metrics conceal and provides a template for causal analysis of any intervention in sequential decision-making systems. Future work on interruptible agents — or on any form of mid-execution intervention (human feedback, environment changes, tool failures) — should adopt this decomposition as standard practice.

### Innovation 4: The Multi-Turn Stability Gradient as a Diagnostic of Integration Capacity

The paper's fourth contribution is empirical rather than methodological: the discovery that the ability to integrate sequential interruptions follows a **model-dependent stability gradient**, where stronger models show consistent monotonic improvement with each additional update while weaker models show diminishing returns or even regression. This finding, presented in Table 2 and Figure 3, transforms what could have been a simple "more information helps" story into a nuanced characterization of a specific cognitive limitation: the capacity to hold multiple sequentially-revealed constraints in working memory and reconcile them with an evolving execution state.

The data pattern is stark. Claude-Opus-4.5 improves monotonically: 21.21% (1 interruption) → 36.36% (2) → 41.82% (3). Each additional update provides positive marginal gain, though the gain diminishes (+15.15 points from 1→2, +5.46 from 2→3). Claude-Sonnet-4.5 shows an even larger jump from 1→2 (15.15% → 33.33%, +18.18 points) with continued improvement at 3 (41.21%). Claude-Haiku-4.5 follows the same monotonic pattern. In contrast, DeepSeek-V3.1 improves from 1→2 (12.12% → 21.21%) but **regresses** at 3 (20.61%), and Mistral-Large-3 shows near-stagnation after the first update (11.52% → 12.73% → 13.94%).

This pattern reveals something more specific than "stronger models handle interruptions better." It reveals that **interruption integration is not a stateless operation** — each additional update interacts with previously incorporated updates in a way that can produce interference. DeepSeek's regression at n=3 is particularly informative: it receives *more* task-relevant information but becomes *less* likely to succeed. The paper's interpretation is that "later updates are imperfectly integrated and can interfere with previously incorporated information" (Section 4.3). This is a specific failure mode — **cross-update interference** — that is conceptually distinct from single-interruption failures like state staleness or incomplete reconciliation. It suggests that multi-turn interruptibility requires not just the ability to understand each individual update, but the ability to maintain a coherent representation of the evolving task specification across updates — a form of **incremental constraint satisfaction** that current models manage with varying success.

**Why this matters beyond the numbers.** The multi-turn stability gradient has direct implications for system design in collaborative settings. If a user knows they will need to provide multiple rounds of feedback (which is realistic for complex long-horizon tasks), the choice of model backbone determines not just the baseline success rate but also the *reliability* of improvement from each round of feedback. A model with a positive monotonic gradient (Opus, Sonnet, Haiku) gives the user a reasonable expectation that their feedback will help. A model with a flat or regressive gradient (Mistral, DeepSeek) gives no such guarantee — additional clarification might make things worse. This is a different kind of trustworthiness concern than accuracy: it is about whether the model's response to human guidance is *predictably beneficial*, which is essential for collaborative workflows where the human is iteratively steering the agent toward a goal.

**Comparison to prior work.** The closest prior work, Wu et al. (2025b), studied single-turn interruptions in reasoning models and found severe performance drops. The multi-turn extension here is novel — no prior work has systematically evaluated how performance evolves as interruptions accumulate. The finding that accumulation can produce interference (DeepSeek) rather than monotonic improvement challenges the intuitive assumption that more user guidance is always beneficial, and identifies a concrete research problem: how to design agent architectures or training procedures that produce stable, monotonic improvement from sequential human feedback.

**Incremental or fundamental?** This is an **empirical discovery** with significant implications. The result itself — that some models show stable improvement while others show interference — is a single data point from one benchmark and one set of models, and its generality remains to be established. But the *concept* it introduces — the multi-turn stability gradient as a diagnostic of integration capacity — is a fundamental addition to the vocabulary for characterizing agent behavior under dynamic user intent. Future work on interruptible or collaborative agents should measure and report this gradient as a standard capability dimension, alongside single-turn adaptation metrics.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The evaluation uses **InterruptBench**, a benchmark of 165 human-verified long-horizon web navigation tasks derived from WebArena-Lite (Koh et al., 2024; Wei et al., 2025). For each of the 165 tasks, three interruption scenarios are synthesized (addition, revision, retraction), yielding 495 single-turn interruption variants, plus multi-turn mixed scenarios. Tasks span five domains: Reddit, GitLab, CMS, Map, and OneStopShop. The paper does not specify a train/validation/test split — all 165 tasks serve as the evaluation set, with synthesis of interruption variants performed on the same tasks.

- **Base model(s).** Six LLM backbones are evaluated within the **WebAgent-R1** scaffolding (Wei et al., 2025): **Claude-Haiku-4.5, Claude-Sonnet-4.5, Claude-Opus-4.5** (Anthropic, 2025a,b,c), **Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B** (abbreviated Qwen3-480B-A35B; Yang et al., 2025), **DeepSeek-V3.1** (Liu et al., 2024), and **Mistral-Large-3** (Jiang et al., 2023). These span the capability spectrum from fast/small (Haiku) to large/capable (Opus) and include both closed-source and open-weight models. The models were chosen as representatives of contemporary LLM capabilities for web navigation — the paper states they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4, in reference to the PaLM 2-S* used in the original paper, though this claim is made about the WebAgent-R1 evaluation context). All models use identical prompting format, tool/API surface, termination conditions, and maximum action budget within the WebAgent-R1 scaffolding.

- **Metrics.** Three complementary evaluation dimensions are used:
  - **Success Rate (SR):** Binary outcome determined by three rule-based evaluation metrics from WebAgent-R1 — String Match, URL Match, and Program Execution — against the ground truth of the original WebArena-Lite task (which the post-interruption intent is semantically equivalent to).
  - **Post-interruption success curve SR(k):** Defined as `$SR(k) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}[\tau_i \leq k]$`, where `$\tau_i$` is the number of actions taken after the interruption until success is achieved in episode `$i$`, and `$k$` is the post-interruption action budget. This is a monotonically non-decreasing curve that measures the fraction of episodes achieving the final intent within `$k$` post-update actions.
  - **Efficiency metrics (action count and token count):** For each episode, the paper records `$A$` (number of actions) and `$T$` (number of output tokens). Paired deltas `$\Delta A = A_{\text{int}} - A_{\text{base}}$` and `$\Delta T = T_{\text{int}} - T_{\text{base}}$` compare interrupted runs against matched no-interruption baseline runs on the same underlying task from WebArena-Lite. Positive values indicate higher cost under interruption.

- **Baselines.**
  - **No-Interruption baseline (per-model):** The agent receives only the transformed initial intent query with no interruptions during execution, but is evaluated against the final post-update intent. This serves as a lower-bound reference: any success must come from the initial actions coincidentally satisfying the updated requirements. It is used as the matched baseline in the paired-outcome quadrant analysis and as the dashed curves in Figure 2.
  - **WebArena-Lite original task performance:** The paper implicitly benchmarks against the original WebArena-Lite task success rates (reported as "baseline" in Table E.3), which represent performance when the intent is given correctly from the start without any interruption mechanism.
  - **Cross-model comparison:** The six models serve as baselines for each other, establishing a capability gradient for interruption handling.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The primary unit of measurement is **actions** (individual LLM calls producing one step of web interaction). The post-interruption action budget `$k$` is swept from 0 to 30 in Figures 2 and 3, with a default maximum action budget of 30 per WebArena-Lite convention. For efficiency analysis, **output tokens** serve as a proxy for generation cost, reflecting the deliberation overhead of producing actions and incorporating updated intents. The paper does not report total FLOPs or wall-clock time; cost is measured purely in action count and token count.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation or report confidence intervals. The evaluation is conducted on all 165 tasks (495 single-turn interruption variants), with results reported as averages over these fixed test sets. For the paired-outcome quadrant analysis, each interrupted run is paired with a matched no-interruption run on the same underlying WebArena-Lite task. For the multi-turn experiments, the interruption sequence types are "random sequences, where the interruption type can vary across turns" (Section 3.2), but the paper does not specify how many such sequences are tested or how randomization is controlled. This absence of statistical rigor — no error bars, no cross-validation, no significance testing — is a notable methodological limitation.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Single-Turn Interruption Adaptation Dynamics (SR(k) Curves)

The paper's primary characterization of adaptation effectiveness comes from **Figure 2**, which plots `$SR(k)$` for each model under each of the three interruption types (Addition, Revision, Retraction). Solid lines represent interrupted runs; dashed lines represent matched no-interruption baselines evaluated against the final intent.

**Addition scenario (Figure 2, left panel):** Claude-Opus-4.5 reaches approximately 50% success by `$k = 10$` post-interruption actions and approximately 55% by `$k = 30$`. Claude-Sonnet-4.5 reaches roughly 40% at `$k = 10$` and roughly 44% at `$k = 30$`. Claude-Haiku-4.5 reaches approximately 33% at `$k = 10$` and 38% at `$k = 30$`. Open-weight backbones plateau at lower levels: Qwen3-480B-A35B at approximately 26%, DeepSeek-V3.1 at approximately 30%, and Mistral-Large-3 at approximately 23% at `$k = 30$`. The dashed no-interruption baselines are higher than zero but well below the solid curves for all models, indicating that additions are partially compatible with pre-update plans but still require explicit constraint integration for optimal performance. The Opus baseline reaches roughly 20% at `$k = 30$` (meaning Opus accidentally satisfies the updated intent on ~20% of tasks without being told the update), establishing a ~35 percentage point gap attributable to the interruption message.

**Revision scenario (Figure 2, center panel):** The dashed baselines remain near zero across all budgets for most models, indicating that when the initial query contains factually wrong information, agents essentially never succeed by coincidence — the correction is necessary. The interrupted curves show substantial absolute gains: Claude-Opus-4.5 exceeds 50% by `$k \approx 15$` and continues to approximately 55% at `$k = 30$`. Claude-Sonnet-4.5 reaches roughly 45% at `$k = 30$`. Claude-Haiku-4.5 reaches roughly 37%. The open-weight models again plateau lower: Qwen at approximately 26%, DeepSeek at approximately 25%, Mistral at approximately 16%.

**Retraction scenario (Figure 2, right panel):** This scenario exhibits notably stronger dashed baselines compared to Addition and Revision — meaning agents sometimes satisfy relaxed constraints without being told about the retraction. For Opus, the dashed baseline reaches roughly 45% by `$k = 30$` while the solid interrupted curve reaches roughly 55%, producing a gap of only ~10 percentage points — much smaller than the ~35-point gap in Addition or the ~55-point gap in Revision. The paper interprets this as evidence that the "primary benefit of the update is earlier task completion under tight budgets rather than a substantially higher overall ceiling" for Retraction interruptions.

**Key cross-scenario pattern:** The three interruption types produce qualitatively different baseline-to-interrupted gaps. Revision shows the largest gap (the correction is essential), Addition shows a moderate gap (the additional constraint improves but is not strictly necessary for partial success), and Retraction shows the smallest gap (removing constraints rarely changes the outcome for already-successful trajectories). This validates the paper's taxonomy: the three types are not just nominal categories but correspond to genuinely different adaptation challenges as measured by the SR(k) curves.

**Model ranking consistency:** Claude-Opus-4.5 > Claude-Sonnet-4.5 > Claude-Haiku-4.5 > DeepSeek-V3.1 ≈ Qwen3-480B-A35B > Mistral-Large-3 across all three scenarios, though the absolute gaps vary. The Claude family models consistently dominate, with Opus showing the steepest early-rise in SR(k) (fastest adaptation) and the highest asymptote. Mistral-Large-3 is consistently the weakest, with both the slowest uptake and the lowest eventual ceiling.

#### Efficiency Analysis: Paired-Outcome Quadrants

Tables 1, B.1, and D.2 present the paired-outcome quadrant breakdown for Addition, Revision, and Retraction scenarios respectively. These tables decompose the effect of interruptions into four mutually exclusive categories (S/F, F/S, S/S, F/F) and report both the number of cases and the mean action/token deltas within each quadrant.

**Addition scenario efficiency (Table 1):**

- **S/F cases (interruption degrades outcome):** Rare across all models — Opus has only 1 S/F case, Sonnet has 4, Haiku has 3, DeepSeek has 5, Mistral has 5. Despite their rarity, S/F episodes are extremely costly: Sonnet expends +21.75 additional actions and +8,584 additional tokens before failing on its 4 S/F cases; DeepSeek expends +6.40 actions and +1,270 tokens; Haiku expends +6.67 actions and +1,808 tokens. Opus's single S/F case shows 0 additional actions and only +312 tokens, suggesting rapid failure rather than extended futile recovery.

- **F/S cases (interruption rescues outcome):** The most populous outcome-improvement category after F/F. Opus has 70 F/S cases, Sonnet 55, Haiku 52, DeepSeek 35, Qwen 32, Mistral 27 — a gradient that tracks closely with overall model capability. Efficiency within F/S varies dramatically: Opus recovers with −2.21 actions and −92.7 tokens (the update makes the task *more efficient* than the no-interruption baseline, presumably because the update provides information the baseline had to discover through exploration). In contrast, Haiku requires +6.67 additional actions and +1,808 additional tokens to achieve its F/S recoveries. Mistral is anomalous: its F/S episodes show +0.56 extra actions and +165 extra tokens, meaning even its successful recoveries incur overhead.

- **S/S cases (both succeed, isolate adaptation overhead):** These episodes show the smallest or most negative deltas across models. Opus: −1.86 actions, +159 tokens. Qwen: −2.92 actions, −218 tokens. Haiku: −1.73 actions, −188 tokens. This suggests that when adaptation is successful, agents often complete the task with *fewer* actions than the no-interruption baseline — they reuse pre-interruption progress efficiently and integrate the update without restarting.

- **F/F cases (both fail, measure futile overhead):** The largest cost inflation occurs here. Haiku consumes +1.13 actions and +2,624 tokens in F/F quadrant (Table 1). Sonnet: +2.53 actions and +1,128 tokens. Opus: +0.41 actions and +351 tokens. The pattern is consistent: failed interrupted runs engage in extended but unproductive re-planning before ultimately failing, with the token overhead far exceeding the action overhead (suggesting the model spends tokens thinking/re-planning rather than taking additional actions).

- **Aggregate efficiency:** The overall action deltas are small in magnitude (ranging from −0.99 for Opus to +0.82 for Sonnet), while token deltas vary substantially (+37.6 for Qwen, +88.5 for Mistral, +670.9 for Sonnet, +1,699 for Haiku). The paper concludes that "incorporating an additional requirement frequently increases deliberation cost even when the number of actions remains comparable."

**Revision scenario efficiency (Table B.1, Appendix C):**

- The outcome disagreement is heavily concentrated in F/S rather than S/F. Opus: F/S = 86, S/F = 1. Sonnet: F/S = 62, S/F = 1. This asymmetry is more extreme than in Addition, where S/F cases were slightly more common. For Revision, receiving the correction almost never derails a task that would have succeeded — the update is almost always beneficial or neutral for outcome.

- Average action deltas are negative for several models (Opus: −4.37, Sonnet: −2.56, Haiku: −2.18, DeepSeek: −1.04), accompanied by token reductions (Opus: −785 tokens, Sonnet: −202, Haiku: −204). Qwen shows a small action increase (+0.75) but token decrease (−99). Mistral is the exception: +1.17 actions and +1,146 tokens — the interruption makes Mistral *more expensive* on average.

- The F/S quadrant for Revision shows substantially more cases than Addition for all models (Opus: 86 vs. 70; Sonnet: 62 vs. 55), but with similar efficiency patterns. Opus's F/S recoveries require +7.20 actions and +1,366 tokens — notably positive (unlike Addition where F/S was negative), suggesting that Revision recoveries are inherently more expensive because they require undoing actions predicated on wrong information.

**Retraction scenario efficiency (Table D.2, Appendix D):**

- This scenario shows more mass in S/S (both succeed) compared to the other scenarios. Opus: S/S = 44, F/S = 41, F/F = 74, S/F = 6. The higher S/S count reflects the fact that retractions often relax constraints, making the task easier and allowing the baseline to succeed more often.

- All models exhibit negative average action deltas, ranging from −1.72 (Mistral) to −8.13 (Opus). Token deltas are also predominantly negative (Opus: −1,061, DeepSeek: −330, Mistral: −287), with Haiku showing a marginal increase (+8.8). The paper interprets this as evidence that "informative interruptions can reduce action steps and often reduce token consumption" in the retraction scenario, because removing constraints simplifies the task.

- S/F cases are non-trivial for Retraction (Opus: 6, Sonnet: 8, Mistral: 10), higher than for Addition or Revision, suggesting that even constraint-removal interruptions can sometimes derail otherwise-successful execution.

**Cross-scenario efficiency patterns:**

- Token overhead dominates action overhead across all scenarios. The paper's efficiency conclusion is that "adaptation cost is driven primarily by token overhead rather than by additional actions" (Section 4.2).
- F/F cases are consistently the most expensive quadrant in terms of token deltas.
- S/S cases are consistently the cheapest, often showing negative deltas.
- Opus consistently shows the most efficient adaptation (lowest or most negative deltas in S/S and F/S quadrants), while Haiku and Sonnet show the highest overhead.
- Mistral-Large-3 exhibits anomalous behavior: it is the only model to show positive overall token deltas in the Revision scenario (+1,146) and shows positive action deltas where other models show negative ones, suggesting its adaptation strategy is fundamentally different (perhaps involving more restart-like behavior).

#### Multi-Turn Interruption Performance

**Table 2** reports converged success rates under `$n \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$` interruptions, where `$n=0$` corresponds to the no-interruption baseline and higher `$n$` correspond to additional user-provided information.

**Claude family shows consistent monotonic improvement:**
- Claude-Opus-4.5: 5.45% (0 int.) → 21.21% (1) → 36.36% (2) → 41.82% (3)
- Claude-Sonnet-4.5: 4.24% → 15.15% → 33.33% → 41.21%
- Claude-Haiku-4.5: 5.45% → 19.39% → 33.33% → 38.79%

All three roughly double their success rate from `$n=1$` to `$n=3$`. Opus shows the highest absolute performance at `$n=3$` (41.82%), while Sonnet shows the largest single-step gain (from `$n=1$` to `$n=2$`, an increase of ~18 percentage points).

**Open-weight models show unstable or diminishing improvement:**
- Qwen3-480B-A35B: 4.24% → 11.52% → 18.79% → 23.64% (consistent but slower growth)
- DeepSeek-V3.1: 4.85% → 12.12% → 21.21% → 20.61% (regression at `$n=3$`)
- Mistral-Large-3: 3.03% → 11.52% → 12.73% → 13.94% (near-stagnation after `$n=1$`)

DeepSeek's regression from 21.21% (n=2) to 20.61% (n=3) is a key finding: the third update interferes with previously incorporated information, causing a net loss. Mistral's near-flat performance after the first interruption suggests a limited capacity to leverage progressively revealed task details.

**Figure 3** shows the full `$SR(k \mid n)$` curves for each model at `$n \in \{1, 2, 3\}$` interruptions (stage1/stage2/stage3). Key observations from these curves:
- For all Claude models, the stage2 and stage3 curves are consistently above the stage1 curve across all budget levels `$k$`, and the stage3 curve is above stage2 — a clean monotonic ordering.
- For DeepSeek-V3.1, the stage2 curve rises above stage1, but the stage3 curve crosses below stage2 at higher budget levels — the regression is visible not just in final success rate but in the entire post-update adaptation trajectory.
- For Mistral-Large-3, all three curves are tightly clustered with minimal separation, confirming the stagnation observed in Table 2.
- The "baseline" curve (no interruption, `$n=0$`) remains near zero for all models, consistent with the finding that initial (pre-update) queries in multi-turn scenarios are designed to be incomplete/incorrect/over-specified and rarely lead to correct answers by coincidence.

**Diminishing returns:** Across all models, the marginal gain from `$n=2$` to `$n=3$` is smaller than from `$n=1$` to `$n=2$`. For Opus, the gains are +15.15 points (1→2) versus +5.46 points (2→3). For Sonnet: +18.18 versus +7.88. For Haiku: +13.94 versus +5.46. This suggests that each additional interruption provides less new information (since earlier interruptions already narrowed the gap to the true intent) and/or that the model's capacity to integrate multiple constraints approaches saturation.

#### Interruption Position Sensitivity

**Table E.3** (Appendix E) reports task success rates when interruptions are injected at {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} of the baseline trajectory, plus the "baseline" no-interruption condition. The paper does not specify which interruption type(s) these results aggregate over, which is a significant omission.

**Key findings from Table E.3:**

- **Large-scale models improve under later interruptions:** Claude-Opus-4.5 achieves 47.27% at position 0.2, 55.76% at 0.4, 54.55% at 0.6, and 56.97% at 0.8 — highest at the latest position. Claude-Sonnet-4.5 shows a different pattern: peaks earlier (47.27% at 0.2) and remains relatively stable (44.24% at 0.4, 41.21% at 0.6, 42.42% at 0.8), with a slight decline at intermediate positions.

- **Smaller and open-weight models peak early and degrade:** Claude-Haiku-4.5 achieves 43.64% at 0.2 then declines: 29.70% (0.4), 27.88% (0.6), 24.24% (0.8). DeepSeek-V3.1 follows a similar pattern: 30.30% (0.2) → 29.70% (0.4) → 27.88% (0.6) → 24.24% (0.8). Qwen3-480B-A35B peaks at 0.4 (32.73%) and shows non-monotonic behavior. Mistral-Large-3 is erratic: 22.42% (0.2), 19.39% (0.4), 21.21% (0.6), 27.88% (0.8) — its highest at the latest position with a dip in between.

- **Domain-level breakdown:** The table provides per-domain success rates (Reddit, GitLab, CMS, Map, Shopping). For Opus at position 0.8: GitLab achieves 63.30%, CMS 68.60%, Shopping 62.20% — substantially higher than Reddit (57.90%) and Map (38.50%). The Map domain is consistently the hardest across all models and positions, while CMS and GitLab tend to be easier.

- **Interpretation:** The paper interprets the scale-dependent pattern as follows: "Larger models appear capable of repairing committed trajectories, whereas relatively smaller models exhibit trajectory entanglement under delayed updates." Late interruptions require not only update integration but also correction of persistent UI state and previously executed actions. Larger models can surgically modify only the necessary parts; smaller models struggle to disentangle valid from invalid prior actions when most of the trajectory has already been executed.

**Missing specificity in Table E.3:** The paper does not state which interruption type(s) these results aggregate over, what budget was used, or whether SR(k) or final success is reported. The success rates in Table E.3 are substantially higher than those in Table 2 (e.g., Opus at 47–57% in Table E.3 vs. 21–42% in Table 2), suggesting either different interruption types, different evaluation conditions, or aggregation across types. This lack of clarity makes cross-table comparison impossible and is a significant transparency issue.

#### Case Study: Error vs. Correct Interruption Handling

**Figure F.1** (Appendix) presents a trajectory-level case study contrasting an error trace and a correct trace for an Addition interruption in the WebArena Map environment. The task involves comparing walking vs. driving time to Carnegie Mellon University, with a mid-trajectory interruption specifying that the origin should be Randyland (not Pittsburgh Downtown, which the agent had initially assumed).

**Error trace:** The agent acknowledges the update ("Wait, the user mentioned they want to start from 'Randyland' in their interrupt") but fails to fully override the earlier assumption — the "From" field and computed route remain tied to the old origin. The final reported comparison is conditioned on stale state.

**Correct trace:** The agent treats the interrupt as a state-changing event — explicitly detects intent drift, retracts the previous origin assumption, edits the "From" field to Randyland, triggers a recomputation of both walking and driving routes, and only then reports the updated comparison.

The paper uses this case to illustrate the core requirement for interruptibility: "effective handling is not just updating the final answer, but ensuring the UI state and intermediate computations are reconciled with the post-interrupt intent before producing any result" (Section 5). The contrast between surface-level acknowledgment (error trace) and genuine state reconciliation (correct trace) captures the distinction between models that treat interruptions as linguistic events and those that treat them as state-changing events — a distinction that the quantitative metrics (SR(k), quadrant analysis) are designed to measure at scale.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Interruption timing (ablation):** **Interruption position sensitivity** is evaluated by injecting interruptions at {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} of the baseline trajectory length (Table E.3). The finding that large models improve under later interruptions while smaller models degrade is the primary robustness result — the choice of 60% as default position is partially validated by the observation that Opus and Sonnet perform well at 0.6 (54.55% and 41.21% respectively), though Opus peaks at 0.8 (56.97%) and Sonnet peaks at 0.2 (47.27%), suggesting no single position is optimal for all models. The paper does not report how `$SR(k)$` curves change across positions, only final success rates.

**Interruption type (ablation):** **Addition vs. Revision vs. Retraction** serves as a built-in ablation of interruption type, with results reported separately in Figures 2, Tables 1/B.1/D.2, and the case study. The finding that Revision shows the largest gap between solid and dashed SR(k) curves (Figure 2, center) while Retraction shows the smallest gap validates that interruption type matters substantially for adaptation difficulty — this is not an ablation in the traditional sense (removing a component) but rather a controlled comparison across qualitatively different conditions.

**Multi-turn accumulation (ablation):** **Number of interruptions** is ablated from `$n=1$` to `$n=3$` (Table 2, Figure 3). The finding that additional interruptions generally improve success but with diminishing returns and, for some models, regression is the key result. This effectively ablates the "amount of information provided" dimension, showing that more information does not monotonically improve performance.

**Model scale (implicit ablation):** **Model capability spectrum** — from Haiku to Opus within the Claude family, and across open-weight models — serves as an implicit ablation of model scale/capability. The consistent rank-ordering (Opus > Sonnet > Haiku; DeepSeek/Qwen > Mistral) across all metrics and scenarios suggests that interruption handling capability correlates with general model capability, though the paper does not control for model size or training compute explicitly.

**No-interruption baseline (robustness):** **The matched no-interruption baseline** is the primary robustness check — every interrupted run is compared against a run on the same task without the interruption. This controls for task difficulty and model capability, isolating the causal effect of the interruption. The dashed curves in Figure 2 and the paired-outcome quadrants in Tables 1/B.1/D.2 are all derived from this comparison. The finding that S/F cases exist even for the strongest models (Opus: 1 S/F case under Addition, 1 under Revision, 6 under Retraction) suggests that interruptions are not universally beneficial even for capable models, validating the need for the baseline.

**What is NOT ablated:**
- **Synthesis model:** All interruption data is synthesized by Claude-Opus-4.5. The paper does not evaluate whether interruptions synthesized by other models (e.g., Sonnet, GPT-4) would produce different results, nor does it evaluate Opus's performance on human-written (rather than Opus-synthesized) interruptions. This leaves open the possibility that Opus benefits from a "self-consistency" effect where interruptions in its own linguistic style are easier to interpret.
- **Agent scaffolding:** Only WebAgent-R1 is used. The paper does not evaluate whether different scaffolding architectures (e.g., tree-search agents, hierarchical planning agents) would handle interruptions differently.
- **Temperature or sampling parameters:** No ablation of generation parameters is reported.
- **Prompt format:** The paper does not ablate whether explicit instruction about handling interruptions (e.g., "The user may provide corrections; be prepared to revise your plan") would improve performance.
- **Domain effects:** While Table E.3 reports per-domain results, there is no systematic ablation of domain characteristics on interruptibility (e.g., whether state-reversible domains like Map are easier than submission-heavy domains like CMS).

### Critical Assessment

The paper makes four central claims that can be assessed against the experimental evidence:

**Claim 1: "Our results show that handling user interruptions effectively and efficiently during long-horizon agentic tasks remains challenging for powerful large-scale LLMs." (Abstract, Section 6)**

**Assessment:** This claim is **strongly supported** but with important nuance about what "challenging" means. The evidence:
- Even the strongest model (Claude-Opus-4.5) achieves only ~55% success under single-turn Addition interruptions at `$k=30$` post-update actions (Figure 2). This is far from ceiling, and represents a substantial drop from what the same model can achieve on the original WebArena-Lite tasks without the interruption mechanism (the paper does not report original WebArena-Lite SR for these models, but the fact that Opus achieves the highest baseline performance in Table E.3 at ~14% and the highest interrupted performance at ~57% suggests significant room for improvement).
- S/F cases (interruption degrades outcome) exist for all models (Table 1), demonstrating that interruptions can actively harm performance — a clear sign of challenge.
- Token overhead is substantial (e.g., +1,699 tokens on average for Haiku under Addition, Table 1), representing non-trivial additional cost for adaptation.
- Open-weight models plateau at success rates of 16–30% (Mistral 23%, Qwen 26%, DeepSeek 30% under Addition at `$k=30$`), which is near-random for many tasks.
- The case study (Figure F.1) confirms that even when models acknowledge interruptions, they often fail at genuine state reconciliation.

**However**, the claim would be stronger with:
- Direct comparison to human performance on the same interrupted tasks. Without a human baseline, "challenging" is relative to unspecified expectations.
- Original WebArena-Lite success rates for these models on the same 165 tasks. If Opus achieves only 20% on the original tasks without interruptions, then 55% under interruptions is actually quite strong. The paper reports "baseline" performance in Table E.3 (~14% for Opus) but it is unclear whether this is the original WebArena-Lite task or the `$n=0$` no-interruption condition on the transformed initial intent (which would be intentionally harder).
- A sense of what "good enough" would look like for practical deployment.

**Claim 2: "Additional user interruption updates generally improve success" (Section 4.3), with the qualification that improvement is model-dependent and sometimes unstable.**

**Assessment:** **Supported with clear boundary conditions.** The evidence:
- Table 2 shows monotonic improvement for all Claude models (e.g., Opus from 21.21% → 36.36% → 41.82%) and Qwen (11.52% → 18.79% → 23.64%).
- DeepSeek-V3.1's regression (21.21% at `$n=2$` → 20.61% at `$n=3$`) and Mistral-Large-3's near-stagnation (11.52% → 12.73% → 13.94%) establish the boundary: the claim holds for models with sufficient capacity to integrate sequential updates, but fails for models where later updates interfere with earlier adaptation.
- Figure 3 (stage1/stage2/stage3 curves) visually confirms the monotonic ordering for Claude models and the crossing/regression for DeepSeek and Mistral.

**The boundary condition is precise and well-supported.** The claim is not universally true — it depends on model capacity — and the paper is explicit about which models exhibit which pattern. This is a strength: the paper does not over-claim.

**However**, the multi-turn experiment has significant methodological limitations:
- The paper does not specify how many multi-turn sequences were tested or how the sequence types (e.g., Addition→Revision→Retraction vs. Revision→Addition→Retraction) were randomized. If only a few sequences were tested, the results could be sensitive to sequence order rather than accumulation count.
- The paper does not control for total information content across `$n$`. If `$n=3$` interruptions collectively provide the same information as `$n=1$` (just spread across more turns), then the comparison conflates number-of-interruptions with information-density-per-message.
- No analysis of which sequence orders produce the best/worst results.

**Claim 3: The three interruption types (Addition, Revision, Retraction) produce distinct adaptation challenges.**

**Assessment:** **Strongly supported.** The evidence:
- Figure 2 shows qualitatively different SR(k) curves and different solid-vs-dashed gap sizes across the three types. Revision has near-zero baselines (the correction is essential); Retraction has strong baselines (constraint removal is partially redundant with pre-update plans).
- The paired-outcome patterns differ across types: Revision has much higher F/S rates than S/F (Table B.1, Opus: 86 vs. 1), suggesting corrections rarely derail tasks. Retraction has higher S/F rates than the other types (Table D.2, Opus: 6 S/F cases), suggesting even constraint removal can sometimes confuse the agent.
- The efficiency patterns differ: Retraction shows universally negative average action deltas (Table D.2), while Addition and Revision show mixed or positive deltas.
- The case study (Figure F.1) illustrates the Revision-type challenge concretely.

This claim is the most robust in the paper because it is supported by convergent evidence across three metrics (SR(k) curves, quadrant analysis, and qualitative trajectory analysis) and across all six models.

**Claim 4: The paper introduces a rigorous evaluation methodology (SR(k), paired-outcome quadrants, multi-turn accumulation) that enables systematic measurement of interruptibility.**

**Assessment:** **Supported as a methodological contribution**, but with genuine limitations that should be acknowledged. The SR(k) curve is well-motivated and reveals dynamics that final success rate would conceal. The paired-outcome quadrant analysis is conceptually elegant and surfaces patterns (S/F cases, F/F token overhead) that aggregate metrics obscure. The multi-turn accumulation analysis introduces the stability gradient concept.

**However**, the methodology has several weaknesses:
- **No statistical rigor:** No confidence intervals, error bars, or significance tests are reported anywhere. With 165 tasks and some quadrants containing as few as 1–5 episodes (e.g., Opus: 1 S/F case under Addition, Table 1), the reliability of quadrant-level statistics is questionable. A single S/F episode with extreme values (e.g., Sonnet's +8,584 tokens) can heavily influence the quadrant mean.
- **Missing cross-validation or held-out set:** The entire InterruptBench (165 tasks) is used for both synthesis and evaluation. The paper does not split tasks into a development set (for tuning synthesis prompts or interruption positions) and a held-out test set (for final evaluation). This creates a risk of overfitting the synthesis parameters (e.g., the 60% default interruption position) to the evaluation set.
- **Unclear aggregation in Table E.3:** The position sensitivity results (Table E.3) do not specify which interruption type(s) are aggregated or what budget is used, making it impossible to reconcile these numbers with Figures 2 and 3 or Tables 1/B.1/D.2. This is a significant transparency issue.
- **Synthesis model confound:** All interruptions are synthesized by Claude-Opus-4.5, which is also the best-performing evaluation model. The paper does not control for this potential confound (e.g., by having Opus-synthesized and Sonnet-synthesized interruptions cross-evaluated).
- **Single scaffolding (WebAgent-R1):** The paper evaluates only one agent architecture. Different scaffolding choices (e.g., explicit state-tracking mechanisms, hierarchical planning, reflection-based control) could produce dramatically different interruption handling profiles, and the current results cannot distinguish whether the difficulty lies in the backbone LLM or in the ReAct-style interaction paradigm.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

1. **Human baseline:** What is human performance on InterruptBench? If humans also struggle with some interruption types, the difficulty is inherent to the task rather than to LLM limitations.
2. **Original WebArena-Lite performance comparison:** What is each model's success rate on the original 165 tasks without any interruption mechanism? This would contextualize the SR(k) asymptotes — if Opus achieves 80% on original tasks but only 55% under interruptions, the gap is substantial; if Opus only achieves 60% on original tasks, 55% under interruptions is quite strong.
3. **Intent-without-interruption baseline:** What if the agent receives the correct intent from the start (rather than the transformed initial intent followed by corrections)? This would isolate the cost of the interruption mechanism from the inherent difficulty of the task.
4. **Explicit instruction ablation:** Does telling the agent "the user may provide updates or corrections during execution; be prepared to revise your plan" improve performance?
5. **State-reset baseline:** What if, upon interruption, the agent's environment state and conversation history were reset (forcing a restart)? This would test whether the informational interruption framing actually helps compared to a clean slate.
6. **Cross-synthesis-model evaluation:** Interruptions synthesized by Sonnet, GPT-4, or humans, evaluated on all six models, to test for synthesis-model bias.
7. **Confidence intervals:** All tables and figures should report variability (e.g., 95% CI for SR(k) at key budget levels).
8. **Sensitivity to the 60% default:** While Table E.3 evaluates positions {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8}, the paper does not report how SR(k) curves change shape across positions — only final success. The adaptation dynamics may differ qualitatively at different interruption points.

**Overall assessment of experimental support:** The paper's core empirical findings — that interruption handling is challenging, that model capability correlates with adaptation success, that additional updates help Claude-family models but not all open-weight models, and that interruption type matters — are **credibly supported** by the reported data. The methodological contributions (SR(k), quadrant analysis) are genuine and likely to influence future work. However, the absence of statistical rigor, the synthesis-model confound, the lack of cross-validation, and the opacity around certain experimental conditions (particularly in Table E.3) mean that the specific numerical results should be interpreted as preliminary characterizations rather than precise measurements. The paper succeeds as a **proof of concept** for the InterruptBench methodology and as a **baseline characterization** of the difficulty of interruptible web navigation, but falls short of the experimental rigor that would make its specific numbers citable as established benchmarks.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Difficulty Estimation Cost Makes the Headline Efficiency Gains Unrealized

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire compute-optimal framework depends on estimating a prompt's difficulty **before** deciding how to allocate the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so — generating 2048 samples per question and averaging the PRM's final-answer scores across those samples (Section 3.2) — is extraordinarily expensive. The paper acknowledges this explicitly:

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

but then proceeds to report 4× efficiency gains that exclude this estimation cost from the budget. The headline comparison (e.g., "16 generations matching 64") counts only the strategy-execution budget after difficulty is known, without amortizing the 2048-sample estimation step.

**The consequence.** In any realistic deployment, the total cost is **difficulty estimation + strategy execution**, and the former can dominate the latter. If estimating difficulty for a single prompt requires 2048 generations (that is, 2048 complete solution samplings plus PRM scoring), then the total compute for a "16-generation compute-optimal run" is actually 2048 + 16 = 2064 generations — which is worse than simply running best-of-256 (which would cost 256 generations). The 4× figure should therefore be understood as the **upper bound on what adaptive allocation could achieve if difficulty were known for free**, not as a realized deployment gain. Until the difficulty estimation cost is reduced by orders of magnitude (or amortized across many similar prompts), the compute-optimal framework is primarily an analytical tool rather than a practical deployment strategy.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Figure 4 and Figure 8 both plot compute-optimal curves that achieve 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N at matched accuracy levels (e.g., 16 generations matching 64 in Figure 4, 64 matching 256 in Figure 8). The paper explicitly states that "our experiments do not account for this [difficulty estimation] cost" (Section 3.2). The discrepancy between claimed efficiency and realistic cost is never quantified — the paper does not report how many FLOPs or wall-clock seconds the 2048-sample difficulty estimation step requires relative to the strategy budgets being studied (1–512 generations).

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests training models to predict difficulty directly from the question text rather than through sampling-based estimation, but no such model is developed or evaluated. The predicted (non-oracle) difficulty bins are shown to perform nearly as well as oracle bins (Figures 4 and 8), but this does **not** reduce the estimation cost — it only replaces ground-truth correctness checks with PRM scoring, while still requiring 2048 samples per question. The paper does not explore cheaper estimation strategies (e.g., using only 4–8 samples, or using a lightweight classifier trained on question text), nor does it report the sensitivity of allocation quality to the number of estimation samples. A practitioner evaluating this work for deployment would find the headline 4× number misleading without understanding this hidden cost.

---

### Interruptions Are Beneficial Only When Models Can Reconcile Environment State with Updated Intent — And Most Cannot

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's experimental framework treats interruptions as **informational** — they provide additional user-supplied details without resetting the environment or invalidating prior progress (Section 3.2). This framing assumes that agents can (1) recognize which of their prior actions remain valid under the updated intent, (2) surgically modify only the invalid portions of their execution, and (3) reuse valid progress to complete the task more efficiently than restarting. The paper's results demonstrate that this assumption holds unevenly: it is approximately true for Claude-Opus-4.5 but breaks down substantially for weaker models.

**The consequence.** For weaker models, interruptions do not just fail to help — they can actively **degrade** performance. The S/F quadrant (interruption causes a previously-successful task to fail) exists for all models (Table 1: Opus has 1 S/F case, Haiku has 3, DeepSeek has 5, Mistral has 5 under Addition). The cost in S/F cases is often extreme: Sonnet expends +21.75 additional actions and +8,584 additional tokens before failing on its 4 S/F cases. The paper's case study (Figure F.1) illustrates the precise failure mode: the agent **acknowledges** the interruption ("Wait, the user mentioned they want to start from 'Randyland'") but fails to fully **reconcile** the environment state (the "From" field and computed route remain tied to the old origin), producing an answer conditioned on stale state. This is not a simple capability deficit — it is a specific anti-pattern where receiving more information makes the agent *less* likely to succeed because it cannot correctly determine which parts of its execution are now invalid.

For multi-turn interruptions (Table 2), this limitation is even more acute. DeepSeek-V3.1 regresses from 21.21% at n=2 interruptions to 20.61% at n=3, meaning the third user update *interferes* with previously incorporated information. Mistral-Large-3 shows near-stagnation: 11.52% → 12.73% → 13.94% across n=1→3. The paper describes this as a failure mode where "later updates are imperfectly integrated and can interfere with previously incorporated information" (Section 4.3). The practical implication is stark: for most models tested, a user who provides multiple rounds of corrective feedback cannot reliably expect the agent to improve — and may actually make things worse.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The S/F quadrant counts and associated cost deltas in Tables 1, B.1, and D.2 directly quantify outcome-degrading interruptions. The case study (Figure F.1) qualitatively illustrates the state-reconciliation failure at the trajectory level. The multi-turn accumulation analysis (Table 2, Figure 3) demonstrates the instability for open-weight models. The SR(k) curves (Figure 2) show that even the strongest model (Opus) plateaus at only ~55% success under Addition at k=30, far from ceiling.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not propose any mechanism for improving state reconciliation or preventing interruption-induced degradation. It does not ablate whether providing explicit instruction to "check whether your prior actions are still valid" would reduce S/F cases. The paper treats state reconciliation as a capability that emerges (or fails to emerge) from general model reasoning, and identifies it as a "persistent bottleneck" (Section 6) that "will require stronger mechanisms for state tracking, intent reconciliation, and error recovery during execution" — but does not develop or evaluate any such mechanisms. A practitioner deploying web agents with human-in-the-loop correction would need to know that, for models weaker than Opus, interruptions are a gamble: they may help on some tasks but will derail others, with no reliable way to predict which.

### The Benchmark Is Evaluated on a Single Agent Scaffolding, Confounding Model Capability with Architecture Limitations

**The assumption or constraint.** All six LLM backbones are evaluated within the **WebAgent-R1** scaffolding (Section 3.1), which provides a ReAct-style interaction loop: the model receives an observation, produces a reasoning trace, and emits an action. The paper keeps the "prompting format, tool/API surface, termination conditions, and maximum action budget identical across models" to isolate backbone capability as the independent variable. This means that any limitation of the ReAct paradigm — particularly its inability to maintain explicit state tracking, to separate planning from execution, or to perform lookahead reasoning about action consequences — becomes **conflated with model capability** in the reported results. The paper is measuring how well each backbone handles interruptions *within WebAgent-R1*, not how well each backbone could handle interruptions in an architecture better suited to the task.

**The consequence.** The reported model rankings (Opus > Sonnet > Haiku > DeepSeek/Qwen > Mistral) may reflect the interaction between model capability and ReAct's specific limitations rather than pure interruptibility capability. A model that performs poorly in WebAgent-R1 might perform substantially better in a scaffolding that explicitly tracks which actions have been executed, which environment state elements are contingent on which user constraints, or which portions of the plan need revision after an interruption. For example, hierarchical planning agents (Li et al., 2025), reflection-based controllers (Azam et al., 2025), or tree-search methods (Dihan et al., 2025) might handle state reconciliation differently because they maintain explicit representations of subgoals, action dependencies, or search trees rather than relying on the model's implicit reasoning in a flat ReAct loop. The paper cannot distinguish whether the interruption handling difficulty is intrinsic to the backbones or an artifact of the scaffolding that forces models to manage state implicitly through chain-of-thought alone.

This is particularly important for the **efficiency** findings. The paper's conclusion that "token overhead dominates action overhead" (Section 4.2) — e.g., Haiku consuming +2,624 additional tokens in F/F cases under Addition (Table 1) while taking only +1.13 additional actions — may be partially an artifact of ReAct's requirement to verbalize all reasoning. A scaffolding that allowed the model to maintain a structured internal state (separate from the conversational stream) might reduce the token overhead substantially, since the model would not need to re-derive its understanding of the task and the interruption in every reasoning step.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper evaluates only WebAgent-R1 and does not compare against other agent architectures. Appendix A.1 reviews alternative planning paradigms (tree search, hierarchical planning, reflection-based control) but does not ablate them experimentally. All quantitative results — SR(k) curves (Figure 2), efficiency breakdowns (Tables 1, B.1, D.2), multi-turn accumulation (Table 2), and position sensitivity (Table E.3) — are specific to the ReAct+WebAgent-R1 combination.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not ablate scaffolding architecture or discuss this confound. The conclusion states that "reliable interruptible agents will require stronger mechanisms for state tracking, intent reconciliation, and error recovery during execution" (Section 6), which implicitly acknowledges that the current scaffolding is insufficient. However, the paper does not test any of these "stronger mechanisms" — it only diagnoses their absence from the current results. A practitioner evaluating these results would need to know that the reported numbers are **lower bounds on what is achievable with the same backbones but better scaffolding**, and that the relative ranking of models might shift if tested in architectures with explicit state management.

---

### All Interruptions Are Synthesized by the Strongest Model, Creating a Self-Consistency Confound

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper uses **Claude-Opus-4.5** as the default synthesis model for generating all interruption data (Section 3.1: "We leverage Claude-Opus-4.5 for data synthesis by default"). The synthesis prompts (Appendix F) instruct Opus to produce `transformed_initial_intent` and `updates` messages that meet three constraints: ground-truth consistency, necessity, and authenticity. All 495 single-turn interruption scenarios (165 tasks × 3 types) and the multi-turn scenarios are Opus-generated and then manually reviewed.

**The consequence.** Claude-Opus-4.5 is **also the best-performing model in the evaluation**. This creates a **self-consistency confound**: interruptions that reflect Opus's own linguistic patterns, phrasing preferences, and conceptual granularity may be intrinsically easier for Opus to interpret than for other models. The paper's finding that Opus consistently outperforms all other models (e.g., 55% vs. Sonnet's 44% vs. Mistral's 23% under Addition at k=30, Figure 2) cannot be cleanly attributed to Opus's superior interruption-handling capability — part of the gap may reflect Opus's greater familiarity with its own linguistic style.

This is a well-known issue in LLM evaluation: when one model generates evaluation data and is also evaluated on it, the comparison against other models is biased. The manual review step (Section 2.2: "All generated data are manually reviewed by the authors") ensures that the interruptions are factually correct and necessary, but does **not** neutralize the stylistic and conceptual confound. Manual reviewers checking for correctness cannot determine whether an Opus-phrased interruption is implicitly easier for Opus to parse than a Sonnet-phrased or human-phrased equivalent.

The confound is particularly relevant for the **paired-outcome efficiency** analysis. If Opus-synthesized interruptions are more naturally aligned with Opus's reasoning patterns, then Opus may achieve its efficiency advantages (e.g., −2.21 actions and −92.7 tokens in F/S recoveries under Addition, Table 1) partly because it is adapting to interruptions that match its own internal conceptual structure. Weaker models like Mistral (+0.56 actions and +165 tokens in F/S) may be struggling not just with the adaptation challenge but with interpreting interruption phrasing that is stylistically alien to their training distribution.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.1 states the synthesis model choice. The paper does not report any cross-synthesis-model evaluation (e.g., interruptions synthesized by Sonnet, GPT-4, or humans, evaluated on all six models). The manual review process is mentioned but not detailed — no inter-annotator agreement, no rejection rates, no examples of revisions made during review. The paper does not discuss the self-consistency confound or acknowledge it as a limitation.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not address this confound at all. Mitigation would require at minimum: (1) cross-synthesis-model evaluation (showing that Opus's advantage persists when interruptions are synthesized by other models), or (2) human-written interruption scenarios as a held-out comparison, or (3) an analysis of whether Opus's relative advantage over other models is larger on this benchmark than on other web agent benchmarks (which would indicate a synthesis-model bias). A practitioner deciding whether Opus's reported superiority generalizes to human-written interruptions in their own deployment would find no evidence either way.

### No Statistical Rigor on a 165-Task Test Set, Making Quadrant-Level Statistics Unreliable

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire evaluation is conducted on **165 tasks** (Section 2.1), split across five domains and three interruption types (yielding 495 single-turn episodes per model per interruption type). The paired-outcome quadrant analysis further splits these 165 episodes into four mutually exclusive categories (S/F, F/S, S/S, F/F), with some quadrants containing **as few as 1 episode** (e.g., Opus has 1 S/F case under Addition, Table 1; Opus has 1 S/F case under Revision, Table B.1; Qwen and Mistral have 0 S/F cases under Revision, Table B.1). The paper reports mean action and token deltas for these quadrants without any measure of uncertainty — no confidence intervals, no standard errors, no significance tests.

**The consequence.** The quadrant-level statistics are **extremely fragile**. A single outlier episode can dominate the mean of a quadrant with only 1–5 cases. Sonnet's S/F quadrant under Addition (Table 1) reports +21.75 actions and +8,584 tokens based on only 4 cases — if one of those 4 episodes was pathologically expensive (e.g., the agent got stuck in a loop), the dramatic overhead may not characterize Sonnet's typical S/F behavior but rather reflect a single anomalous run. Similarly, Opus's S/F quadrant with 1 case (0.00 action delta, +312 token delta) provides essentially no information about Opus's behavior in this failure mode — the single data point could be an outlier in either direction.

This fragility propagates to the paper's interpretive claims:

**Claim:** "This suggests that incorporating an additional requirement frequently increases deliberation cost even when the number of actions remains comparable." (Section 4.2, commenting on overall token vs. action deltas). **Without error bars,** readers cannot determine whether the reported token-overhead differences (e.g., Haiku +1,699 vs. Qwen +37.6) are statistically reliable or within the noise of 165-episode averages.

**Claim:** "S/F cases are rare but costly." (Section 4.2). **Without confidence bounds,** "rare but costly" could be an artifact of a few extreme outliers in a tiny sample rather than a genuine phenomenon. The true cost of S/F could be near-zero for most S/F episodes with one or two extreme outliers inflating the mean.

**Claim:** "The primary benefit of the update is earlier task completion under tight budgets rather than a substantially higher overall ceiling" (for Retraction, Section 4.1). **Without error bands** on the SR(k) curves in Figure 2, readers cannot tell whether Opus's solid curve (interrupted) is significantly above its dashed curve (baseline) at low k values or whether the gap is within sampling noise.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports no confidence intervals, no standard deviations, no standard errors, no statistical tests anywhere in the main text or appendices. The test set is 165 tasks (fixed), and all results are reported as point estimates. The quadrant breakdowns (Tables 1, B.1, D.2) report case counts per quadrant but no distributional information beyond the mean. The multi-turn results (Table 2) report single-point success rates with no indication of variability.

**Mitigation status.** None. The paper does not acknowledge the lack of statistical rigor as a limitation. The two-fold cross-validation protocol described in the reference example (for the PaLM 2-S* compute-optimal paper) is absent here — there is no held-out development set for tuning the 60% interruption position or synthesis parameters, and no cross-validation for the evaluation results. The entire 165-task set is used for synthesis (generating the interruption variants), simulation (determining baseline trajectory lengths and interruption positions), and evaluation (reporting results). This creates a risk of **overfitting**: the chosen parameters (60% default position, specific synthesis prompts, manual review criteria) may be tuned (implicitly or explicitly) to produce interpretable results on this specific set of tasks, and the reported numbers may not generalize to a new set of WebArena-Lite tasks or to other benchmarks.

A practitioner evaluating a model for deployment would want to know: *Is the difference between Opus (55%) and Mistral (23%) under Addition (Figure 2, k=30) statistically significant or within the noise of a 165-episode evaluation?* The paper provides no basis for answering this question.

### The Synthesis Guarantees Enable Clean Evaluation but Exclude Realistic Interruption Patterns Where Intent Truly Changes

**The assumption or constraint.** The data synthesis pipeline (Section 2.2, Appendix F) enforces a strict **ground-truth consistency** constraint: "The initial user query combined with the user interruption messages should have the same meaning as the original intent and result in the same ground truth answers." This means every interruption scenario in InterruptBench is a **decomposition** of a fixed original intent into an initial partial/wrong/over-specified query plus corrective updates, where the final corrected intent is **guaranteed** to match the original WebArena-Lite intent exactly. The interruption messages are defined by what they add, revise, or retract **relative to a known ground-truth answer**.

**The consequence.** This design enables clean, automated evaluation — the programmatic success checkers from WebArena-Lite can be reused without modification — but it **systematically excludes "genuine mind-change" interruptions**: scenarios where the user's intent evolves during task execution in ways that were not latent in a single original specification. In real collaborative settings, users do not just clarify an initially under/over/wrong-specified version of a fixed goal — they sometimes change their minds in ways that produce a **different** ground-truth answer than any single original intent would have produced. For example: a user initially asks an agent to find the cheapest flight to New York, sees partial results showing that flights are expensive, and interrupts to say "Actually, check Boston instead — and make it a train rather than a flight." This is not an addition to or revision of a single latent "correct" flight search — it is a genuinely new task that emerged from interaction with intermediate results. InterruptBench cannot model this because the synthesis pipeline requires a fixed original WebArena-Lite intent as the anchor.

The paper's three interruption types — addition, revision, retraction — all assume that the **final intent is fully specified by the original WebArena-Lite task**, and that interruptions only serve to communicate that intent incrementally or correct errors in its initial expression. This covers **clarification-style** interruptions (the user knew what they wanted from the start but communicated it imperfectly) but excludes **exploration-style** interruptions (the user is discovering what they want through interaction with the agent's partial progress). The title of the paper — "When Users Change Their Mind" — implies the latter, but the benchmark construction constrains it to the former.

This matters for the paper's generalizability claims. The finding that "additional user interruption updates generally improve success" (Section 4.3) is specific to a regime where each update brings the agent closer to a single fixed target. If updates instead **change** the target, the adaptation challenge is different — the agent may need to abandon substantial prior progress and restart, which could produce different (and likely worse) adaptation dynamics. The paper's SR(k) curves and efficiency quadrants characterize only the incremental-clarification regime, not the genuine-mind-change regime.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 2.2 defines the three interruption types exclusively in terms of their relationship to the original WebArena-Lite intent. The synthesis prompts (Appendix F) all instruct the model to produce combinations whose "same meaning as the original intent" and "same ground truth answers." The case study (Figure F.1) illustrates an addition where the true intent (Randyland → CMU) was presumably always the user's goal, just communicated incrementally. Nowhere does the paper discuss or evaluate interruptions that change the ground-truth answer relative to the original task.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not acknowledge this scope limitation explicitly. The authors might argue that the ground-truth-consistency constraint is a **necessary simplification** for systematic evaluation — without it, automated programmatic evaluation would be impossible because there would be no single correct answer to check against. But the paper's framing language ("When Users Change Their Mind," "dynamic user intent," "revise goals mid-execution") suggests a broader scope than the benchmark actually supports, and the discrepancy between framing and construction is never discussed. A practitioner evaluating whether InterruptBench results predict real-world interruptibility needs to understand that the benchmark tests **incremental specification** of a fixed goal, not **genuine goal evolution** — and that the two may require different agent capabilities.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not propose a new architecture, a new training paradigm, or a new optimization algorithm for web agents. It does something more foundational: it **identifies and formalizes a capability axis that the field was not systematically measuring**, and in doing so reshapes what it means to evaluate an LLM agent for realistic deployment. The shift is conceptually analogous to what happened when the field moved from evaluating models on static question-answering benchmarks to evaluating them as interactive agents in grounded environments (WebArena, OSWorld). Just as that transition revealed that "knowing facts" and "navigating websites to discover facts" are distinct capabilities, this paper reveals that "completing a fixed task from a complete specification" and "adapting when the specification changes mid-execution" are distinct capabilities with their own failure modes, their own efficiency dynamics, and their own scaling properties across model backbones.

**This is a reframing, not a paradigm shift.** The paper's primary contribution is methodological and conceptual — it provides the vocabulary (addition/revision/retraction), the measurement instruments (SR(k) curves, paired-outcome quadrant analysis), the benchmark (InterruptBench), and the baseline characterization — rather than a solution to the problem it identifies. This is not a criticism: establishing that a problem exists and can be measured rigorously is a prerequisite for solving it, and the paper makes a compelling case that interruptibility is a genuine blind spot in current agent evaluation. The shift from "we should build more robust agents (and hope they handle interruptions)" to "interruptibility is a distinct evaluation axis that demands its own benchmarks, metrics, and analysis tools" changes how researchers should think about agent capability — it adds a new row to the mental scorecard, and that row turns out to capture variance that baseline task success does not explain.

**Resolving contradictions in prior work.** The paper's findings provide a framework for interpreting otherwise puzzling results in the human-agent collaboration literature. Prior work showed contradictory signals: some studies found that user feedback improves agent performance (CollabLLM, ReHAC), while others found that interruptions cause severe performance drops and failure modes like "reasoning leakage, panic, and self-doubt" (Wu et al., 2025b). The InterruptBench results suggest a resolution: the effect of interruptions depends critically on **(1) the interruption type** (Revision shows large positive gains because corrections are essential; Retraction shows small gains because constraint removal often overlaps with what the agent would do anyway), **(2) the model's capacity for state reconciliation** (Opus shows F/S = 70 recoveries; Mistral shows F/S = 27), and **(3) whether interruptions accumulate monotonically or interfere** (Claude models show monotonic improvement with more updates; DeepSeek regresses at n=3). Studies that found negative effects likely tested on harder interruption types, weaker models, or multi-turn regimes without analyzing these dimensions separately. The paper's taxonomy and quadrant framework make these interactions legible for the first time.

**Which research directions become more attractive.** The paper makes **state-tracking and intent reconciliation** the central bottleneck for interruptible agents. The case study (Figure F.1) crystallizes this: the failure mode is not that the agent ignores the interruption ("Wait, the user mentioned Randyland") but that it fails to determine which prior actions are now invalid and must be overridden. This suggests that research on **explicit belief state maintenance** — maintaining a structured representation of which environment state elements are contingent on which user constraints, and efficiently invalidating the downstream consequences of a revised constraint — is more promising than research on better instruction-following or more sophisticated ReAct-style reasoning. The paper also makes **efficiency under interruption** a first-class concern: the finding that token overhead dominates action overhead (Table 1) and that F/F failures consume the most tokens suggests that reducing unproductive re-planning (perhaps through better confidence estimation or earlier failure detection) is a high-value research target. Finally, the multi-turn stability gradient (Table 2) makes **incremental constraint satisfaction** — the ability to integrate sequential updates without interference — a newly visible research problem. Approaches that maintain a coherent representation of the evolving task specification across multiple feedback rounds (rather than treating each update independently) become attractive.

**Which directions become less attractive.** The paper's results indirectly argue against **naïve optimism about model scale alone solving interruptibility**. While Opus outperforms Sonnet outperforms Haiku (consistent with a scale effect), even Opus plateaus at only ~55% success under single-turn Addition interruptions at k=30 (Figure 2), and all models show S/F cases (interruptions degrading previously successful tasks). The gap between ~55% interrupted success and whatever Opus achieves on original WebArena-Lite tasks (likely substantially higher, though the paper does not report this number) suggests that scaling current architectures without architectural innovations in state management may hit diminishing returns. Similarly, the paper makes **purely linguistic approaches to interruption handling** (e.g., better prompting, few-shot examples of handling corrections) less attractive — the core challenge is not understanding the interruption message linguistically (the case study shows the agent paraphrases the update correctly) but reconciling it with the grounded execution state, which requires reasoning about action effects and environment persistence that language modeling alone may not provide.

**A new kind of trustworthiness concern.** Beyond capability measurement, the paper introduces an implicit trustworthiness dimension that the field has not previously operationalized: **predictable benefit from human guidance**. The multi-turn results (Table 2) show that for Claude models, each additional user update predictably improves success (monotonic positive gradient); for DeepSeek, the third update can make things worse; for Mistral, updates barely help at all. In collaborative settings, a user's willingness to provide corrective feedback depends on their expectation that the feedback will be productively incorporated. An agent that sometimes improves and sometimes degrades when given more information is not just less capable — it is **untrustworthy as a collaborator** because the human cannot predict the effect of their own interventions. This is a different kind of reliability concern than accuracy, and the paper's multi-turn stability gradient provides a concrete metric for it. Future work on human-agent collaboration should treat monotonic improvement from human feedback as an explicit design goal, not an incidental property.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Explicit state-tracking architectures for interruption handling.** The paper's case study (Figure F.1) demonstrates that the core failure mode is not linguistic misunderstanding but rather failure to track which environment state elements depend on which user constraints. A natural follow-up would augment the WebAgent-R1 scaffolding with an explicit **constraint-to-state mapping** — a structured representation that records, for each agent action, which user constraint motivated it, and for each environment state element (form field values, page navigations, applied filters), which constraint it satisfies. When an interruption arrives that revises a constraint, the mapping enables surgical invalidation: only state elements tagged with the revised constraint are marked stale, while unrelated progress is preserved. A strong experiment would compare SR(k) curves and S/F rates for a state-tracking-augmented agent versus the vanilla WebAgent-R1 baseline on InterruptBench, specifically predicting that the augmentation reduces S/F cases (fewer successful tasks derailed by interruptions) and shifts F/S efficiency toward negative deltas (more efficient recovery since less unnecessary re-execution occurs). The paper's quadrant analysis provides the exact metrics needed to evaluate such an architecture.

**Cheap difficulty estimation for adaptive interruption handling strategies.** This direction is directly inspired by the reference example paper's compute-optimal framework, adapted to InterruptBench. Currently, InterruptBench treats all interruptions as equivalent in timing and severity, but the paper's results show that interruption type, timing (Table E.3), and model capability interact substantially. A natural research question: can we predict, from the initial trajectory alone (before any interruption arrives), how costly an interruption will be to handle, and adapt the agent's behavior accordingly? For example, if the first few post-interruption actions show the model continuing with stale execution (indicating it has not yet registered the update), the system could inject an explicit "re-check your assumptions" prompt. Alternatively, if the model's confidence in its answer drops sharply after the interruption, the system could allocate additional action budget. The InterruptBench framework makes this tractable because it provides: (1) matched baseline trajectories (no-interruption runs) against which to measure normal execution patterns, (2) SR(k) curves that characterize adaptation speed, and (3) quadrant breakdowns that separate productive from unproductive recovery. A strong experiment would train a lightweight classifier on pre-interruption trajectory features (action types, domain, model identity, number of actions taken so far) to predict whether an upcoming interruption will produce an S/F, F/S, S/S, or F/F outcome, and then evaluate whether adaptive strategies (e.g., earlier interruption injection for predicted-hard cases) improve aggregate efficiency.

**Cross-synthesis-model evaluation to quantify the self-consistency confound.** The paper uses Claude-Opus-4.5 to synthesize all interruption data and also evaluates Opus as a backbone, creating a potential confound where interruptions in Opus's own linguistic style are easier for Opus to interpret. A clean follow-up experiment would: (1) re-synthesize a subset of InterruptBench (e.g., 50 tasks) using Claude-Sonnet-4.5, GPT-4, and human annotators (with the same ground-truth consistency and necessity constraints), (2) evaluate all six backbones on Opus-synthesized, Sonnet-synthesized, GPT-4-synthesized, and human-written interruptions, and (3) test whether Opus's relative advantage over other models is larger on Opus-synthesized interruptions than on human-written ones. This would quantify the self-consistency confound and determine whether the paper's model rankings are robust to synthesis-model choice. The quadrant analysis framework (S/F, F/S, S/S, F/F) would be particularly valuable here: if the confound is real, Opus should show fewer S/F cases on Opus-synthesized interruptions than on human-written ones, and more F/S cases.

**Multi-turn interruption sequence order effects.** The paper's multi-turn experiment (Table 2) randomizes interruption type sequences but does not analyze whether sequence order matters. A natural follow-up would systematically vary the order of addition, revision, and retraction updates across the same set of tasks (e.g., Addition→Revision→Retraction vs. Revision→Addition→Retraction vs. Retraction→Addition→Revision) and measure whether certain orderings produce consistent interference (like DeepSeek's regression at n=3) while others produce monotonic improvement. The hypothesis is that **revision-last sequences are hardest** because revisions require undoing prior actions, potentially undoing progress made in response to earlier additions or retractions. The InterruptBench framework makes this tractable because interruptions of each type are independently synthesized for each task — reordering them requires no new data, only modified simulation scripts. A strong experiment would report SR(k|n) curves stratified by the final interruption type in the sequence, testing the hypothesis that sequences ending in Revision show the largest gap between stages 2 and 3.

**Human performance baseline on InterruptBench.** The paper reports extensive model performance but provides no human baseline, making it impossible to determine whether 55% Opus success under Addition represents near-human performance or a massive capability gap. A critical follow-up would recruit human participants to complete a subset of InterruptBench tasks (e.g., 30 tasks spanning all five domains and all three interruption types) under the same conditions — receiving the initial query, being interrupted at 60% of a reference trajectory, and continuing with the updated intent. Human success rates, SR(k) curves, and efficiency (actions taken, time spent) would contextualize all model results. If humans achieve near-100% success with minimal efficiency overhead, the paper's conclusion that interruption handling is "challenging for powerful large-scale LLMs" is strongly validated and the gap becomes a concrete target. If humans also struggle (e.g., 70% success, substantial re-execution), the difficulty is partly inherent to the task structure rather than an LLM limitation, and the paper's framing should be adjusted. Human data would also provide a qualitative benchmark: do humans exhibit S/F cases (success without interruption, failure with interruption)? If not, that failure mode is specifically an LLM pathology worth studying.

**Scaffolding architecture ablation.** All results in the paper are specific to the WebAgent-R1 ReAct-style scaffolding. A crucial follow-up would replicate the InterruptBench evaluation on at least two alternative agent architectures — for example, a hierarchical planning agent (Li et al., 2025) that maintains explicit subgoal decompositions, and a tree-search agent (Dihan et al., 2025) that explores action branches before committing — using the same set of LLM backbones. The hypothesis is that architectures with explicit state representations or search mechanisms will show: (1) higher SR(k) asymptotes (better eventual success), (2) steeper early SR(k) rise (faster adaptation), (3) fewer S/F cases (less outcome degradation from interruptions), and (4) lower token overhead in F/F cases (less unproductive re-planning), because the architecture rather than the backbone LLM is responsible for tracking which actions are still valid. If the architecture matters substantially, it reframes the paper's conclusion from "LLMs struggle with interruptions" to "ReAct-style agents struggle with interruptions," which has different implications for deployment and research prioritization. The InterruptBench framework is scaffolding-agnostic (it only requires an agent that takes actions in the WebArena environment), making this experiment straightforward to execute.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Human-in-the-loop correction for enterprise web automation.** Organizations deploying web agents for tasks like customer data management (CMS domain), order processing (Shopping domain), or repository maintenance (GitLab domain) currently face a binary choice: run agents fully autonomously (risking undetected errors on long tasks) or require human approval at every step (eliminating the efficiency gain of automation). InterruptBench provides evidence that **interruptible execution** — where the agent proceeds autonomously but accepts mid-task corrections — offers a middle ground, but only with sufficiently capable models. The paper's results suggest that Claude-Opus-4.5 can productively incorporate user corrections on roughly 55% of tasks within 30 post-update actions (Figure 2, Addition), and that its adaptation is often efficient (negative action deltas in S/S and F/S quadrants, Table 1). For a CMS deployment where an agent manages product catalog updates across dozens of items, a workflow where the human reviews the agent's progress at 60% completion and provides one round of corrections — rather than waiting for full completion or approving every action — would be directly validated by InterruptBench's single-turn Addition and Revision results. The key deployment insight: the choice of backbone model determines not just the baseline success rate but the **reliability of human correction** — if the model shows S/F cases (interruptions degrading previously successful tasks), the correction workflow may backfire, and users may learn to avoid interrupting the agent even when they spot errors.

**Iterative specification for complex web research tasks.** For tasks where the user does not fully know the correct specification at the outset — a common pattern in research-oriented web navigation (literature retrieval, competitive analysis, data gathering) — InterruptBench's multi-turn results provide guidance on how many rounds of iterative refinement are productive. The paper shows that Claude-family models benefit from up to 3 rounds of additional specification (Opus: 21% → 36% → 42% across n=1→3, Table 2), while open-weight models like Mistral show near-zero improvement beyond the first round (11.5% → 12.7% → 13.9%). A deployment designer choosing between a Claude-based agent that supports multi-round iterative refinement and a Mistral-based agent that effectively requires the user to specify everything upfront (or accept that later feedback will be ignored) can make this decision quantitatively based on the multi-turn accumulation curves in Figure 3. The practical workflow: for a market research task where the analyst initially asks for "competitor pricing" and progressively refines to "competitor pricing for products in category X launched after Q2 2025, excluding promotional pricing," the InterruptBench results predict that a Claude-Opus-based agent will incorporate each refinement incrementally, while a weaker backbone may plateau after the first refinement and ignore subsequent ones — or worse, regress as later updates interfere with earlier ones.

**Fail-fast detection for interruption-induced degradation.** The paper's S/F quadrant (Table 1) quantifies a deployment-critical failure mode: interruptions that cause previously successful tasks to fail. In practice, an enterprise deploying web agents with human-in-the-loop correction needs to know **when to avoid interrupting** — if the agent is currently on a successful trajectory, injecting a correction might derail it. The paper's quadrant framework provides a template for building a runtime classifier: by monitoring the agent's behavior immediately after an interruption (token overhead, action patterns, confidence signals), a watchdog system could detect early signs of S/F-style degradation and either (1) revert to the pre-interruption trajectory and complete the task under the original (slightly wrong but successful) intent, or (2) alert the human that their correction has confused the agent and suggest a full restart. The paper's finding that S/F cases are "rare but costly" (e.g., Sonnet expending +8,584 tokens before failing on its 4 S/F cases, Table 1) makes this practically urgent: without detection, a single ill-timed interruption can waste substantial compute on futile re-planning. The InterruptBench dataset — with its matched interrupted and uninterrupted runs — provides the training data for building and evaluating such a classifier, since each episode has a known (S/F, F/S, S/S, F/F) label that a runtime monitor could be trained to predict from early post-interruption behavior.
