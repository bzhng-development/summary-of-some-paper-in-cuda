Here are the takeaways from this James Stanier interview (engineering director at Shopify, author of two books on engineering management, sharing the path to managing managers):

**The catch-22 of moving up the management track**

James frames the core dilemma facing any engineering manager who wants to take the next step: external Director, Head of, and VP roles almost always ask for people who have already done the job. "Often there's a Catch-22 situation that you'll face: they're typically looking for people who have had experience doing it before." Even when job descriptions claim otherwise, you'll likely be up against candidates who have already held that title. You might get lucky with a cold application, but luck isn't a strategy — and James believes "there are ways of preparing to make yourself even more lucky."

**The union of four characteristics that make the leap possible**

Rather than chasing a silver bullet, James argues you want to find yourself at the intersection of several conditions simultaneously. First, a solid foundation: two to five years as an EM with "a provable track record of shipping and people management, with references that can back you up." If you don't know how to run one team inside out, you won't be ready to run several. Second, the right environment: "a company with a strong management culture with clear career progression tracks for managers and individual contributors," so the path is legible and you're learning the right skills. Third, organizational flux: companies that are "changing fast and tends to be unable to keep up with the chaos" — periods of rapid growth, downsizing, or mergers and acquisitions that "begin to question the current shape of the org chart being correct." Fourth, external visibility: "a good presence online via the usual social channels: LinkedIn, Twitter, Github, and so on," so people can find you.

**Why internal candidates dominate senior management hires**

James is blunt about the math: "Quite often more senior engineering management positions are either headhunted or promoted from within." The logic is straightforward. A bad senior hire is disproportionately risky — internal candidates are "a known entity that likely already has domain knowledge," and companies are far more willing to take a chance on them. Smart organizations, he notes, will even set up a 30-60-90 plan that "allows the promoted member of staff to revert to their old role amicably if it's not working out." Meanwhile, "good companies will look out for their staff first and seize opportunities to offer career progression to retain existing talent." James estimates that "maybe in the 70%+ region" of manager-of-managers roles are filled without ever being advertised externally. If you hire well consistently, "you'll always have someone great progressing up the ranks."

**The unplanned nature of the role itself**

A key insight James offers: the need for a manager of managers "is rarely something that undergoes extensive planning." The role emerges reactively. During rapid growth, headcount stacks up until existing teams need splitting, "thus creating opportunities for a node in the org chart above those teams." Sometimes an existing manager of managers quits and "filling that void internally, on a tight timeline, is much easier than reaching outside the company." Mergers can create "a bunch of empty nodes in the org chart needing urgently to be filled." In each case, the people already inside the company — visible, trusted, available — have the upper hand. The lesson isn't to wait passively; it's to position yourself in an environment where these unplanned moments are likely to arise.

**The high-growth startup playbook**

James advocates strongly for "ambitious, high growth companies" as the most fertile ground for these leaps, "especially ones that have just received a new venture capital investment. An injection of money means an injection of people, which creates more opportunities for leading them as the org chart adapts." He points to a historical pattern: "After all, how do you reckon many CTOs became CTOs for the first time?" His advice echoes Wayne Gretzky — "**skate where the puck is going**." Keeping an eye on external ads and applying to everything is one path, but "depending on where you currently work, it may be more fruitful to change your role sooner at your current level — which is often easier — to stand more of a chance of upward growth over time." He notes this is no small feat in itself; doing a stellar job running a team is the prerequisite that makes everything else possible, and he advocated this startup path strongly in the penultimate chapter of his book.

**The parallel with early IC careers**

James draws a deliberate analogy to how individual contributors break into the industry. "It's often hard to land that first job because the applicant has yet to prove themselves. However, once they've stuck around for a few years and have gained experience by shipping lots of code, they find themselves having to bat the recruiters away." Management progression works the same way. "Getting your first management role is tough, but you only have to do it once to prove you can do it again. The same is true about the next step of managing managers. People often grow from within by being excellent performers and working their way upwards."

**Seven diagnostic questions for your own readiness**

James closes with a self-assessment framework for EMs considering the next step. How likely is progression at your current company within a reasonable timeframe? If not, "you might need to look elsewhere sooner rather than later." Is your current EM role defined similarly to how other notable companies define it? "The more your current EM role fits that of the best employers in the industry, the better chance you have of moving sideways and upwards." How many years of experience do you have? "I would expect a manager of managers to be a high growth EM with at least a few years of tenure." What notable infrastructure, features, or products have you shipped as an EM? Are you seen as influential — "do people outside your team seek your advice?" Have you coached anyone outside your team? "If not, I'd advise you to start giving it a go." And finally, have you found anyone already doing the role and talked to them about what changed? "What happened to their time to contribute technically?" — a pointed question that hints at a fundamental shift in the nature of the work. The implicit closing challenge: "What's your current story? Does it feel like you're ready for the next step? Have you already been working at the level of your next promotion?"

---

His **skate where the puck is going** stance, his emphasis on the **union of four characteristics**, and his observation that the role itself is **rarely planned** all converge on the same bet: that career luck is largely about placing yourself in a high-churn environment, earning a "known entity" trust discount, and being the obvious internal choice when an unplanned node opens up on the org chart. The seven diagnostic questions then serve as a practical check on whether you've accumulated enough of that internal credibility — shipped products, cross-team influence, coaching outside your own team — to be the candidate the company reaches for when the chaotic moment arrives. His framing that "maybe 70%+" of these roles are never advertised turns the conventional job-search advice on its head: the most important application is the one you never formally submit.
