# Hölder Policy Optimisation

**ArXiv:** [2605.12058](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12058)

## 🎯 Pitch

Fixed token probability aggregation in GRPO-style RL inevitably collapses on either sparse or dense reasoning tasks: a single parameter *p* in the Hölder mean controls this trade-off, and dynamically annealing it across training achieves a 7.2% relative gain over GRPO on math reasoning and 93.8% success on ALFWorld.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Hölder Policy Optimisation (HölderPO)**, a generalized policy optimization framework that unifies token-level probability aggregation in GRPO-style RL for LLMs via the Hölder mean, replacing the fixed arithmetic mean with a continuously tunable parameter *p*. By modulating *p*—from positive values that concentrate gradient weight on high-importance-ratio tokens (amplifying sparse "aha moment" signals, e.g., on AIME24) to negative values that tighten gradient variance bounds (stabilizing training on dense-signal tasks, e.g., MATH500)—the framework provides explicit control over a gradient concentration–variance trade-off that static aggregations inherently compromise. Because no single *p* resolves both endpoints simultaneously, the paper instantiates a **dynamic annealing algorithm** that decays *p* from positive to negative across training, achieving state-of-the-art average accuracy of 54.9% across five mathematical reasoning benchmarks (a 7.2% relative gain over GRPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B) and 93.8% success on ALFWorld, establishing that the optimal aggregation is not a constant but a function of task signal density and training progression.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Fixed Token Aggregation Creates an Unresolvable Trade-off

GRPO and its variants have become the dominant RL post-training paradigm for LLM reasoning, largely powering breakthrough models like DeepSeek-R1. The algorithm's elegance lies in its simplicity: sample a group of trajectories, estimate advantages within the group (eliminating the need for a separate critic model), compute token-level importance ratios between the current and reference policies, *aggregate* those token-level ratios into a sequence-level scalar, and update. But this last step — the aggregation — has a fundamental problem that the paper identifies: **there is no single aggregation function that works well across all tasks or training stages**.

The issue is that different tasks have different *signal density* profiles. When an LLM generates a chain-of-thought reasoning trace for a competition math problem (like AIME), the sequence might contain hundreds of mundane tokens — prepositions, common mathematical connectives, standard algebraic manipulations — with only a handful of genuinely pivotal reasoning steps where the model makes a non-obvious logical leap. These rare, high-quality tokens are the "aha moments" that determine whether the solution is correct. Conversely, on a more routine math problem (like many in MATH), the supervision signal is distributed more evenly — many tokens contribute moderate amounts of certainty or uncertainty, and no single step dominates.

The paper demonstrates empirically (Table 1, Figure 1) that these two scenarios require **opposite aggregation strategies**:

> "On AIME24, higher p amplifies rare high-magnitude signals for complex reasoning. Conversely, on MATH500, a lower p strictly tightens the gradient variance bound to ensure training stability, yielding superior performance on simpler tasks."

This divergence is not subtle. On AIME24 (sparse signal), the optimal static configuration is $p = 3$, achieving 46.7% accuracy. On MATH500 (dense signal), the optimal static configuration is $p = -1$, achieving 85.0%. Standard GRPO ($p = 1$) achieves 40.0% and 83.4% respectively — meaning that using the *right* aggregation function yields a 6.7 percentage point improvement on AIME24 but using the *wrong* one (e.g., $p = 3$ on MATH500) degrades performance relative to GRPO. There is no single $p$ that is simultaneously optimal for both.

This is the **concentration–stability trade-off** at the heart of the paper. Any static aggregation mechanism — arithmetic mean (GRPO, $p = 1$), geometric mean (GMPO/GSPO, $p \to 0$), or any other fixed power mean — is a single, frozen compromise point on a continuous spectrum of aggregation behavior. The paper's key insight is that this compromise is fundamentally unsatisfying: it must either sacrifice signal amplification on sparse-signal tasks (if $p$ is set too low) or sacrifice gradient stability on dense-signal tasks (if $p$ is set too high).

### Why This Matters: GRPO's Dominance and Its Unresolved Fragility

The problem is important for both practical and theoretical reasons. Practically, GRPO has become the go-to algorithm for reasoning-oriented LLM post-training, driving the success of DeepSeek-R1 and spawning an entire ecosystem of variants (Dr.GRPO, DAPO, GMPO, GSPO, PMPO, and many others surveyed in Appendix A). Yet this ecosystem has developed in a somewhat ad-hoc manner: each variant proposes a different aggregation function (arithmetic, geometric, or parameterized power mean) motivated by different desiderata — some focus on reducing outlier variance, others on stabilizing training dynamics, others on length-bias correction. The field lacked a **unifying framework** that explained *why* these choices matter, *when* each is preferable, and *how* they can be dynamically combined.

Theoretically, the paper identifies a gap in our understanding of how token-level information maps to policy updates in the group-advantage setting. When a trajectory receives a positive advantage (it produced a correct answer), the algorithm must decide how to distribute credit across the tokens in that trajectory. Should all tokens get equal credit? Should tokens where the policy diverged most from the reference get amplified credit? Should tokens where the model was hesitant get more credit to encourage exploration? These are not implementation details — they fundamentally shape the geometry of the optimization landscape that the policy navigates.

The fragility of static aggregation manifests in concrete, practically devastating ways:

- **Training collapse on dense-signal tasks.** When $p = 1$ (GRPO) is applied to tasks like MATH500, the arithmetic mean disproportionately over-weights minor token-level errors. A single token with an unusually high or low importance ratio can dominate the sequence-level aggregate, inducing high-variance gradient updates. The paper notes this explicitly: "standard GRPO ($p = 1$) disproportionately over-weights minor token-level errors, inducing high-variance gradient updates that can lead to training collapse."

- **Signal dilution on sparse-signal tasks.** Conversely, when $p \to 0$ (GMPO/GSPO) is applied to AIME24, the geometric mean overly smooths the probability ratios, suppressing the effective use of rare, high-magnitude tokens. The bulk of unremarkable tokens drowns out the genuine reasoning leaps that the model should be learning from. The paper frames this as the algorithm "failing to amplify sparse, unlikely, yet correct reasoning leaps" — the very "aha moments" that DeepSeek-R1-style training is supposed to capture.

These failure modes are not hypothetical edge cases. They represent the systematic misallocation of learning signals that occurs whenever a single aggregation function is forced to serve both sparse-signal and dense-signal training goals simultaneously.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

Prior work on GRPO variants fell into several categories, each addressing a different aspect of the RL pipeline but none resolving the core aggregation trade-off:

**Fixed aggregation operators.** The most direct prior work is GMPO (Zhao et al., 2025) and GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025), which replace GRPO's arithmetic mean ($p = 1$) with the geometric mean ($p \to 0$). The motivation was variance reduction: the geometric mean is less sensitive to outlier importance ratios than the arithmetic mean, making training more stable. However, these approaches simply shifted the compromise point rather than resolving the trade-off. They are static configurations that happen to work better on some tasks (dense-signal, where variance is the bottleneck) but potentially worse on others (sparse-signal, where signal dilution is the bottleneck). The paper explicitly frames these as "static, isolated points within a broader, continuous spectrum of aggregation operators" — they are points on the $p$-axis but lack any mechanism for moving along that axis.

**Concurrent parameterized aggregation (PMPO).** Zhao et al. (2026) introduced PMPO, which parameterizes a power-mean exponent $p \in [0, 1]$ and adapts it per-trajectory via clip-aware effective sample size (ESS) matching. This is the closest prior work to HölderPO and represents an important step toward dynamic aggregation. However, the paper identifies two critical limitations:

1. **Restricted parameter range.** PMPO constrains $p$ to $[0, 1]$, which excludes both the upward concentration regime ($p > 1$, where gradient weight aggressively concentrates on high-ratio tokens for sparse-signal amplification) and the downward concentration regime ($p < 0$, where gradient weight inverts to focus on low-ratio tokens). The paper's empirical results show that $p = 3$ dramatically outperforms $p \in [0,1]$ on AIME24 (46.7% vs. 40.0% for GRPO), and $p = -1$ outperforms all $p \geq 0$ values on MATH500 (85.0% vs. 83.2% for GRPO). The $p < 0$ regime is described as a "qualitatively distinct inverse-concentration phase unexplored by prior work."

2. **Per-trajectory rather than temporal adaptation.** PMPO adjusts $p$ based on per-trajectory statistics (the effective sample size of importance ratios), not based on the training stage. This misses the temporal dimension: early training benefits from aggressive signal amplification (high $p$) regardless of per-trajectory ESS, while late training benefits from variance contraction (low $p$) regardless of per-trajectory ESS. The paper's dynamic schedule is fundamentally temporal — it anneals $p$ across training steps — enabling complementary roles for different training phases.

**Token reweighting via auxiliary signals.** A parallel line of work modifies token weights using signals external to the importance ratio: token entropy (Wang et al., 2025a, Yu and Li, 2026, Simoni et al., 2025), token probability (Yang et al., 2025b), hidden contributions to response confidence (Deng et al., 2025), or selective KL masking (Lin et al., 2025). These approaches are orthogonal to HölderPO's aggregation mechanism — they modify *what information* feeds into the token weights, not *how* the weights are combined into a sequence-level scalar. The paper notes they "could in principle be combined with HölderPO's power-mean aggregation."

**Other GRPO variants addressing different bottlenecks.** The broader GRPO ecosystem (surveyed in Appendix A) addresses aspects of the RL pipeline that are complementary rather than competing: surrogate loss simplification (GPG, DAPO), length-bias correction (Dr.GRPO, $\lambda$-GRPO), advantage estimation improvements (AAPO, BNPO, OPO, Seed-GRPO), value-model-based alternatives (VC-PPO, T-PPO), and data-centric approaches (Open-Reasoner-Zero, PRIME, SimpleRL-Zero). None of these modify the token aggregation function, making them orthogonal to HölderPO's contribution.

### The Unifying Insight: Aggregation as a Continuous Spectrum

The paper's fundamental framework contribution is to recognize that **all prior aggregation mechanisms are points on a single continuous axis parameterized by $p$**, and that this axis controls a specific, theoretically characterizable trade-off between two properties of the policy gradient:

- **Gradient concentration** (Theorem 1, Section 3.2): As $p$ increases, the weight distribution $W_i(p)$ concentrates on tokens with the highest importance ratios, amplifying their gradient contributions. As $p \to \infty$, the distribution collapses to a uniform distribution over the maximal-ratio tokens. As $p = 0$, every token contributes equally (maximum entropy, uniform dispersion). As $p < 0$, the distribution inverts, concentrating on tokens with the *lowest* ratios.

- **Gradient variance** (Theorem 2, Section 3.3): The upper bound on the variance of the policy gradient estimator is monotonically increasing in $p$. Increasing $p$ amplifies targeted signals but loosens the variance guarantee. Decreasing $p$ strictly tightens the variance bound but dilutes or inverts the concentration pattern.

This characterization reveals why static aggregation is inherently limited: **no fixed $p$ can simultaneously achieve strong signal amplification (requiring large $p$) and tight variance control (requiring small $p$)**. The optimal $p$ is therefore not a global constant but a function of the task's signal density (sparse vs. dense) *and* the training stage (early exploration requiring amplification vs. late convergence requiring stability).

The paper's empirical results in Figures 1 and 2 and Table 1 vindicate this framing. The performance curves for AIME24 and MATH500 as functions of $p$ are essentially **mirror images**: one peaks at $p > 0$, the other peaks at $p < 0$. The token weight distributions $W(p)$ visualized at the bottom of Figure 1 show the qualitative deformation of gradient allocation across the full $p$-axis — from right-skewed concentrations that isolate high-ratio tokens to left-skewed concentrations that upweight low-ratio, hesitant tokens. Standard GRPO ($p = 1$) and GMPO/GSPO ($p \to 0$) are just two specific points on this continuous deformation that happen to work reasonably well as general-purpose compromises but fail to exploit the full dynamic range of the aggregation mechanism.

### Where HölderPO Positions Itself

HölderPO's contribution is not primarily a new aggregation function — the Hölder mean is mathematically well-established. Rather, it is a **meta-strategy**: the framework that parameterizes aggregation by $p \in \mathbb{R}$, the theoretical analysis that characterizes the concentration–variance trade-off along this axis, and the dynamic annealing algorithm that exploits this trade-off by scheduling $p$ across training rather than committing to a single static compromise.

The positioning relative to prior work is clear:

- **Versus GRPO/GMPO/GSPO**: These are special cases ($p = 1$, $p \to 0$, $p \to 0$ respectively) of HölderPO's aggregation function. HölderPO generalizes them and shows that dynamically moving along the $p$-axis is more effective than any single point.

- **Versus PMPO**: HölderPO extends PMPO's parameterized power mean from $p \in [0, 1]$ to the full real line, identifying the qualitative significance of $p > 1$ (sparse signal amplification) and $p < 0$ (inverse concentration for diversity preservation). More fundamentally, HölderPO shifts the adaptation axis from per-trajectory to per-training-step, arguing that temporal scheduling is the natural resolution to the concentration–stability trade-off because the relative importance of signal amplification versus variance contraction shifts systematically across the training lifecycle.

- **Versus auxiliary signal methods**: HölderPO is complementary — it defines *how* token weights are combined into a sequence-level scalar, while auxiliary signal methods define *what information* contributes to those weights. The two could be combined.

The paper's theoretical contribution (Theorems 1–3) provides the formal underpinning for why dynamic scheduling is necessary and what it achieves: any static $p$ is a Pareto-suboptimal compromise between the endpoints of the concentration–variance trade-off, and a decaying schedule recovers the advantages of both endpoints in their respective training phases. The empirical results across mathematical reasoning (Table 2) and agentic tasks (Table 3) validate this theoretically motivated design: the dynamic schedule consistently outperforms every static configuration on the overall task average, even though specific tasks may still be best served by individual endpoint values.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

HölderPO is a **drop-in replacement for the token-level probability aggregation step** inside GRPO-style policy optimization that replaces the fixed arithmetic mean with a continuously tunable parameter `$p \in \mathbb{R}$` controlling how aggressively gradient updates concentrate on high-importance tokens versus spreading evenly for stability. The system solves the problem that no single aggregation function works across all reasoning tasks (sparse-signal tasks like AIME24 need aggressive amplification of rare pivotal tokens, dense-signal tasks like MATH500 need variance-controlled credit distribution), by providing a framework that dynamically schedules `$p$` from positive to negative across training — exploiting strong signal amplification early and tight variance control late — without changing the underlying RL algorithm's structure or adding computational overhead.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has four major components:

1. **The Hölder Mean Aggregation Operator** — a generalization of the sequence-level surrogate term `$\rho_i(\theta)$` in GRPO's objective. Instead of taking the arithmetic mean of token-level importance ratios, it takes their Hölder mean (power mean) of order `$p$`. This single scalar `$p$` interpolates continuously from the arithmetic mean (`$p = 1$`, GRPO), through the geometric mean (`$p \to 0$`, GMPO/GSPO), to extreme positive values (aggressive maximum-biased concentration) and negative values (minimum-biased inverse concentration).

2. **The Policy Gradient with Token-Level Reweighting** — the gradient of the Hölder-aggregated objective naturally decomposes into a weighted sum of per-token log-gradients, where the weight distribution `$W_i(p)$` is a softmax-like probability distribution over tokens whose temperature and direction are controlled by `$p$`. Larger `$p$` skews mass toward tokens with large importance ratios (amplifying sparse signals); smaller `$p$` flattens or inverts the distribution (tightening variance bounds).

3. **The Theoretical Trade-off Analysis (Theorems 1–3)** — a mathematical characterization of what `$p$` controls: **gradient concentration** (how skewed the weight distribution is toward specific tokens, governed by Shannon entropy) and **gradient variance bounds** (how loose the upper bound on the policy gradient estimator's variance is, governed by monotonicity in `$p$`). The analysis proves that no fixed `$p$` can simultaneously maximize concentration (needs large `$p$`) and minimize variance (needs small `$p$`), formally motivating dynamic scheduling.

4. **The Dynamic `$p$`-Annealing Scheduler** — a meta-strategy that decays `$p$` from a positive initial value `$p_{\text{high}}$` to a possibly negative terminal value `$p_{\text{low}}$` over training steps, typically using a linear decay (e.g., `$p: 2 \to -2$`). This allocates the early training phase to aggressive signal amplification (positive `$p$` exploits Theorem 1's upward concentration) and the late phase to variance-controlled convergence (negative `$p$` exploits Theorem 2's tightened bound and Theorem 1's downward concentration for diversity preservation). The scheduler is the only new hyperparameter introduced beyond standard GRPO — all other components (base model, training data, rollout protocol, advantage estimation, clipping) remain unchanged.

Information flows as follows: a batch of prompts is sampled → the old policy `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` generates `$G = 8$` rollouts per prompt (capped at 3,000 tokens) → reward scores are computed via a verifier (e.g., MATH answer grading), and group-relative advantages `$\hat{A}_i$` are estimated by standardizing scores within each group → per-token importance ratios `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` are computed between the current and reference policy → at training step `$t$`, the current `$p(t)$` from the scheduler is used to aggregate these ratios via the Hölder mean into a sequence-level scalar `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` → sequence-level PPO-style clipping is applied, and the loss is computed as the standard clipped surrogate → the loss gradient is back-propagated, which (by the chain rule) effectively applies the token weight distribution `$W_i(p(t))$` to reweight the per-token log-gradient contributions → the policy parameters are updated, and `$p$` is decayed for the next step.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the Hölder mean aggregation operator (Equation 1)** — because this is the mathematical core of the framework, defining how `$p$` parameterizes a continuous spectrum of aggregation behaviors and recovering GRPO, GMPO, and GSPO as special cases.
- **Second, the HölderPO objective with sequence-level clipping (Equation 2)** — to show how the aggregated scalar plugs into the standard PPO-style loss and why sequence-level clipping (rather than token-level clipping) is structurally necessary to preserve the variance properties that Theorems 2 and 7 establish.
- **Third, the policy gradient decomposition (Equation 3)** — which reveals how the single scalar `$p$` induces a token-level weight distribution `$W_i(p)$` that is a probability distribution over tokens, and why this decomposition is the key to understanding both the concentration and variance behaviors.
- **Fourth, the theoretical analysis of gradient concentration (Theorem 1, Theorem 5)** — characterizing how `$p$` deforms the weight distribution, with three distinct regimes (upward concentration at `$p > 0$`, uniform dispersion at `$p \to 0$`, downward concentration at `$p < 0$`), and why this matters for which tokens receive gradient credit.
- **Fifth, the theoretical analysis of gradient variance (Theorem 2, Theorem 7)** — establishing the monotonic variance bound that increases with `$p$` and proving that the bound has a global minimum at some `$p^* \leq 0$`, which creates the fundamental trade-off with concentration.
- **Sixth, the dynamic scheduling strategy and its formal justification (Theorem 3)** — proving that any static `$p$` is a Pareto-suboptimal compromise and that a decaying schedule provides both early-phase signal amplification and late-phase variance contraction, with practical guidance on selecting the schedule endpoints.
- **Seventh, the practical instantiation: training protocol, base model, rollout configuration, hyperparameters** — moving from theory to the concrete algorithm implemented in the experiments.
- **Eighth, the relationship to key design alternatives** — explaining why certain choices (sequence-level clipping, specific `$p$` ranges, linear decay) were made and what breaks if alternatives are used.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **theoretical analysis paper** whose core idea is that token-level probability aggregation in GRPO can be unified under a single continuous parameter `$p$`, and that dynamically scheduling `$p$` resolves the fundamental concentration–variance trade-off that static aggregations inherently compromise.

---

#### The Hölder Mean Aggregation Operator

When GRPO formulates its unclipped objective, it computes a **sequence-level surrogate term** `$\rho_i(\theta)$` that compresses the full sequence of token-level importance ratios into a single scalar. Standard GRPO uses the arithmetic mean:

$$\rho_i^{\text{GRPO}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} r_{i,t}(\theta)$$

where `$|y_i|$` is the length of the `$i$`-th generated response (in tokens), `$r_{i,t}(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})}$` is the per-token importance sampling ratio between the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` and the reference (old) policy `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` at token position `$t$`, and `$\theta$` represents the trainable parameters of the language model.

HölderPO generalizes this by replacing the arithmetic mean with the **Hölder mean (power mean) of order `$p$`**:

$$
\rho_{i,p}(\theta) =
\begin{cases}
\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} r_{i,t}(\theta)^p \right)^{1/p}, & \text{if } p \neq 0, \\
\exp\left( \frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} \log r_{i,t}(\theta) \right), & \text{if } p = 0.
\end{cases}
$$

where `$p \in \mathbb{R}$` is the Hölder exponent that tunes the aggregation behavior, and the `$p = 0$` case is defined via the limit `$\lim_{p \to 0}$` (see Lemma 1 in Appendix G.4, which proves this limit yields the geometric mean). The expression inside the parentheses, `$\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} r_{i,t}(\theta)^p$`, is the arithmetic mean of the `$p$`-th powers of the per-token ratios; raising this mean to the `$1/p$` power produces the `$p$`-order Hölder mean.

**What it computes:** Given a sequence of `$|y_i|$` token-level importance ratios, the operator (1) raises each ratio to the `$p$`-th power, (2) takes their arithmetic mean, and (3) takes the `$p$`-th root of the result. For `$p = 0$`, the operator is defined by continuity: it exponentiates the arithmetic mean of the log-ratios, which is exactly the geometric mean. The output is a single scalar `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta) \geq 0$` that represents the sequence-level "aggregate" importance ratio — a compressed representation of how much the current policy has shifted relative to the reference policy over the entire generated sequence. This scalar then plugs directly into the PPO-style objective in place of GRPO's arithmetic mean.

**Why this form:** The Hölder mean is the natural generalization because it continuously interpolates the entire family of power means, recovering key special cases: `$p = 1$` is the arithmetic mean (GRPO), `$p = 0$` is the geometric mean (GMPO, GSPO), `$p = -1$` is the harmonic mean, `$p = 2$` is the quadratic mean, and as `$p \to \infty$` it approaches the maximum, as `$p \to -\infty$` the minimum. This continuity is what enables the framework to serve as a "dial" for gradient concentration: moving `$p$` continuously from negative to positive values produces a smooth deformation of how token-level information is aggregated, with qualitatively distinct behaviors in different `$p$` regimes. Alternative families (e.g., weighted arithmetic means with learned weights) would not have the same clean theoretical relationship to variance bounds and concentration that the Hölder mean provides through powering the importance ratios before averaging. The single scalar `$p$` controls the *entire* trade-off, making it analyzable and schedulable.

A crucial practical detail: the paper implements all power operations in log-space via the log-sum-exp identity (Algorithm 1 in Appendix C) to preserve numerical stability for large `$|p|$`. This means the algorithm is a "single-line modification of the GRPO loss" — the only change is replacing `$\rho_i^{\text{GRPO}}$` with `$\rho_{i,p}$`, with no additional architectural components.

#### The HölderPO Objective with Sequence-Level Clipping

With the Hölder-aggregated sequence-level scalar `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` defined, the HölderPO objective takes the standard PPO-style clipped surrogate form:

$$
\mathcal{J}_{\text{Hö}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^G} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \min\left( \rho_{i,p}(\theta) \hat{A}_i, \text{clip}\big(\rho_{i,p}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon\big) \hat{A}_i \right) \right]
$$

where `$G$` is the number of rollouts per prompt (8 in all experiments), `$\hat{A}_i$` is the group-relative advantage estimate for the `$i$`-th rollout (computed by standardizing the reward scores within the group of `$G$` rollouts: `$\hat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \text{mean}(\{r(x, y_j)\})}{\text{std}(\{r(x, y_j)\})}$`), `$\epsilon$` is the clipping threshold (standard PPO default), and `$\text{clip}(z, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)$` bounds `$z$` to the interval `$[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$`.

**What it computes:** For each rollout in the group, the objective computes the product of the aggregated sequence-level ratio `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` and the advantage `$\hat{A}_i$`, then takes the minimum of the unclipped and clipped versions (following the standard PPO clipped surrogate pattern). The expectation is over prompts `$x$` from the dataset and rollouts `$\{y_i\}$` from the reference policy. The clipping mechanism prevents destructively large policy updates by ensuring that when the aggregated ratio deviates too far from 1 (the policy has changed too much relative to the reference on this sequence), the gradient contribution from that rollout is bounded rather than allowed to grow unboundedly.

**Why sequence-level clipping:** The paper explicitly argues (in Section 3.3, Appendix I.2, and Appendix D) that sequence-level clipping — where the clip operator is applied to the aggregated scalar `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` rather than to individual token ratios `$r_{i,t}(\theta)$` — is structurally necessary to preserve the variance properties established in Theorem 2. The alternative, token-level clipping (used by GRPO, GMPO, DAPO), applies `$\min(r_{i,t}, \text{clip}(r_{i,t}))$` inside the summation over tokens. This breaks the algebraic relationship between the Hölder mean `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` and the per-token weight distribution `$W_i(p)$` (derived in Section 3.1, Equation 3) — specifically, the outer multiplier `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)^{1-p}$` and inner weights `$r_{i,t}(\theta)^p$` no longer combine neatly, "destroying the correspondence" that makes the variance bound monotonic in `$p$`. Empirically, Appendix D (Table 4) shows that token-level clipping produces a "noticeably narrower performance spread across `$p$`", confirming that `$p$` loses its tight, predictable control over gradient variance under token-level clipping. Sequence-level clipping preserves the mathematical structure that enables the theoretical analysis and makes dynamic scheduling meaningful.

The paper notes that GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025) already uses sequence-level clipping with the geometric mean (`$p \to 0$`), and HölderPO generalizes this to arbitrary `$p$`. The objective in Equation 2 thus recovers GSPO at `$p = 0$` and (in terms of the unclipped form) GRPO at `$p = 1$`.

---

#### The Policy Gradient and Token-Level Weight Distribution

The critical insight for understanding *why* `$p$` controls gradient behavior comes from differentiating the sequence-level aggregate with respect to the policy parameters `$\theta$`. A direct calculation (shown in Appendix G.1) yields:

$$
\nabla_\theta \rho_{i,p}(\theta) = \rho_{i,p}(\theta) \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} W_{i,t}(p) \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid x, y_{i,<t})
$$

where the per-token gradient weights are:

$$
W_{i,t}(p) := \frac{r_{i,t}(\theta)^p}{\sum_{k=1}^{|y_i|} r_{i,k}(\theta)^p}
$$

**What it computes:** The gradient of the Hölder-aggregated scalar with respect to the policy parameters decomposes into a weighted sum of per-token log-probability gradients `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid \cdot)$`, each scaled by a weight `$W_{i,t}(p)$` and the overall factor `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$`. The weights `$W_{i,t}(p)$` form a **probability distribution** over the tokens in the sequence — they are non-negative and sum to 1 — because the denominator normalizes the `$p$`-th powers of the per-token ratios. This means that the scalar `$p$` does not alter the *direction* of any individual token's gradient contribution (which is always `$\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t})$` multiplied by some positive scalar); instead, `$p$` solely reweights *how much* each token's gradient direction contributes to the overall sequence-level gradient.

**Why this decomposition matters:** The decomposition in Equation 3 is the mathematical engine behind everything the paper does. Because `$W_{i,t}(p)$` is a softmax-like distribution over tokens with temperature and skew controlled by `$p$`, changing `$p$` directly controls which tokens dominate the gradient update:

- When `$p > 0$`, tokens with large importance ratios `$r_{i,t} > 1$` (where the current policy assigns higher probability than the reference) get exponentially amplified weights via `$r_{i,t}^p$` in the numerator, while low-ratio tokens get suppressed. This is **upward concentration** — the gradient focuses on tokens where the policy is already confident and improving.

- When `$p = 0$`, `$W_{i,t}(0) = 1/|y_i|$` — every token contributes equally, regardless of its ratio. This is **uniform dispersion** — the gradient is an unweighted average of all token gradients.

- When `$p < 0$`, the exponent `$p$` is negative, so `$r_{i,t}^p = 1/r_{i,t}^{|p|}$` — tokens with *small* importance ratios (near or below 1, where the model is hesitant or diverging from the reference) get amplified. This is **downward concentration** — the gradient focuses on tokens where the policy is uncertain, forcing the model to consolidate alternative reasoning pathways.

The key practical consequence: **`$p$` determines the effective credit assignment across tokens within a sequence.** A high `$p$` says "focus gradient updates on the few tokens where the policy made the biggest improvement relative to the reference" (sparse signal amplification). A low or negative `$p$` says "spread credit more evenly" or "focus on the underconfident tokens" (stability and diversity). This is not a metaphorical interpretation — it is exactly what Equation 3 computes mechanically at each training step.

The gradient of the full HölderPO objective (Equation 16 in Appendix G.3) incorporates sequence-level clipping by multiplying the expression in Equation 3 by a binary indicator `$I_i(\theta) \in \{0, 1\}$` that zeroes out the gradient for sequences where the clipped ratio is active (i.e., where `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` is outside the clipping bounds and the update direction would be harmful). This preserves the theoretical properties — the clipped gradient is either the full unclipped gradient or zero, never a partially clipped mixture — which is essential for maintaining the monotonic variance bound.

The unbiased mini-batch estimator of the gradient (Equation 17) is:
- Sample a batch of `$B$` prompts (128 in the experiments).
- For each prompt, sample `$G$` rollouts from the old policy.
- For each rollout, compute the token weights `$W_{i,t}(p)$` and aggregate the per-token log-gradients.
- Multiply by the advantage `$\hat{A}_i$`, the indicator `$I_i(\theta)$`, and the factor `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)^{1-p}/|y_i|$`.
- Average across rollouts and batch items.

#### Theoretical Analysis of Gradient Concentration (Theorems 1 and 5)

The paper provides a rigorous characterization of how `$p$` deforms the weight distribution `$W_i(p)$` through two complementary lenses: a **local** property (Theorem 5, Appendix H.1) describing per-token weight dynamics, and a **global** property (Theorem 1, Appendix H.2) describing the overall shape of the distribution.

**Local property (Theorem 5):** As `$p \to \infty$`, the weight of any token that achieves the maximum importance ratio in the sequence strictly increases and converges to `$1/|\mathcal{T}^*|$` where `$\mathcal{T}^*$` is the set of all tokens achieving that maximum. For tokens that do not achieve the maximum, there exists a critical value `$p^*$` such that their weight increases up to `$p^*$` (as these tokens briefly gain influence when the distribution is still relatively flat) but then strictly decays to zero beyond `$p^*$`. The proof (Lemma 2) establishes that the derivative `$\partial W_{i,t}(p) / \partial p = W_{i,t}(p) (\log r_{i,t} - \mu_i(p))$`, where `$\mu_i(p) = \sum_t W_{i,t}(p) \log r_{i,t}$` is the `$W$`-weighted mean of the log-ratios. Because `$\mu_i(p)$` is strictly increasing in `$p$` (Lemma 3), it eventually surpasses the log-ratio of any non-maximal token, flipping the sign of the derivative from positive to negative. This creates a non-monotonic per-token weight trajectory: sub-maximal tokens first gain and then lose weight as `$p$` increases. Intuitively: at moderate `$p$`, the distribution is still somewhat spread out; as `$p$` grows, the concentration becomes so intense that only the true maximum-ratio tokens survive. The discipline imposed by this threshold `$\mu_i(p)$` is what prevents the gradient from being pointwise-zero for all but one token until `$p$` is very large — there is a gradual, analytically predictable shift of gradient mass.

**Global property (Theorem 1):** The Shannon entropy `$H(W_i(p)) = -\sum_t W_{i,t}(p) \ln W_{i,t}(p)$` attains its **global maximum at `$p = 0$`**, where the distribution is uniform (`$W_{i,t}(0) = 1/|y_i|$` for all `$t$`), and strictly decreases as `$|p|$` increases. As `$p \to \infty$`, the distribution collapses to a uniform distribution over the maximum-ratio tokens `$\mathcal{T}^+$`. As `$p \to -\infty$`, it collapses to a uniform distribution over the minimum-ratio tokens `$\mathcal{T}^-$`. The proof shows that the entropy derivative is `$\partial H / \partial p = -p \cdot \text{Var}_{W_i(p)}(\log r_{i,t})$`, where the variance is taken under the weight distribution `$W_i(p)$`. Because this variance is strictly positive whenever tokens have distinct ratios, `$\partial H / \partial p$` is negative for `$p > 0$` (entropy decreases) and positive for `$p < 0$` (entropy decreases as `$p$` moves away from zero in the negative direction).

**What this means operationally:** The entropy `$H(W_i(p))$` measures how "spread out" the gradient credit is across tokens. Maximum entropy at `$p = 0$` means every token contributes equally to the gradient — there is no selectivity, and the update is an unweighted average of all token-level log-gradients. As `$p$` moves away from zero in either direction, entropy drops and the gradient becomes increasingly concentrated on a subset of tokens — either the high-ratio ones (for `$p > 0$`) or the low-ratio ones (for `$p < 0$`). This is the paper's formal definition of **gradient concentration**: the skewing of the weight distribution `$W_i(p)$` toward a specific subset of tokens, quantified by how low the entropy is relative to the uniform-distribution maximum.

The paper then interprets these mathematical properties in terms of three **gradient concentration regimes** (Section 3.2, expanded in Appendix H.3):

- **Upward concentration (`$p > 0$`):** The gradient disproportionately amplifies tokens with importance ratios `$r_{i,t} > 1$`. The paper frames this as a "post-hoc exploitation" mechanism — it does not alter which tokens are sampled during rollout generation, but rather aggressively filters the *learning signal* after the fact, concentrating gradient mass on tokens where the policy showed the most improvement relative to the reference. This is designed to prevent "signal dilution" on sparse-reasoning tasks: on AIME24, where correct reasoning hinges on perhaps 3–5 pivotal tokens in a 1000-token sequence, the algorithm must ensure those tokens' gradients are not averaged out by the 995 mundane tokens. The weight distribution becomes right-skewed (as visualized in Figure 1, bottom row), with mass concentrated on the high-ratio tail.

- **Uniform dispersion (`$p \to 0$`):** Every token receives equal gradient weight (`$1/|y_i|$`). This is the maximum-entropy configuration — no selectivity, maximum diversity of gradient signal. It is the best configuration when all tokens contribute roughly similar amounts of information (dense-signal regime) and the goal is to avoid over-weighting outlier tokens that might represent noise rather than genuine reasoning improvements.

- **Downward concentration (`$p < 0$`):** The gradient disproportionately amplifies tokens with importance ratios `$r_{i,t} < 1$`, which indicate that the current policy assigns *lower* probability than the reference to these tokens. The paper interprets this as "retrospective diversity preservation": within a *successful* trajectory (one that received a positive advantage), tokens where the model was hesitant or deviated from its prior distribution represent alternative reasoning steps that the model took despite low confidence. Upweighting these tokens in the gradient forces the optimizer to consolidate these unconventional pathways rather than collapsing to the single most-confident reasoning path. This is not exploration in the active sense (the trajectories are already sampled), but rather a bias in how the gradient is computed from *already-generated* data: it says "pay extra attention to the steps where you were uncertain but the overall trajectory was correct."

The paper is careful to distinguish this from classical RL exploration (Remark 1, Appendix H.3): traditional exploration modifies the *behavioral policy* (how trajectories are sampled) to visit novel states; HölderPO's `$p$` operates purely on *hindsight aggregation* — how credit is distributed across tokens in trajectories that have already been generated by a fixed sampling policy. The practical distinction matters: HölderPO does not increase sample diversity at generation time, but rather shapes the *optimization landscape* that the policy navigates during updates. It makes certain regions of parameter space more or less attractive based on which tokens are amplified in the gradient.

#### Theoretical Analysis of Gradient Variance (Theorems 2 and 7)

The paper establishes that `$p$` controls not only where gradient mass is concentrated but also how large the gradient updates can be in the worst case — the **gradient variance**.

**Theorem 2 (monotonic variance bound):** Under the assumption that the norm of each per-token log-gradient is bounded by a constant `$M$` (i.e., `$\|\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_{i,t} \mid \cdot)\| \leq M$` for all tokens, which holds in modern LLMs due to widely adopted RMSNorm/LayerNorm before the classification head), the variance of the HölderPO mini-batch gradient estimator `$\widehat{\nabla}_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{Hö}}$` admits the upper bound:

$$
\|\text{Var}(\widehat{\nabla}_\theta \mathcal{J}_{\text{Hö}})\| \leq \frac{M^2}{B} \mathbb{E}\left[ \hat{A}_i^2 \rho_{i,p}(\theta)^2 \right]
$$

where `$B$` is the batch size (number of prompts in the mini-batch), `$\hat{A}_i$` is the advantage estimate, and `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` is the Hölder-aggregated sequence-level ratio.

**What it bounds:** The left-hand side is the variance (more precisely, the norm of the covariance matrix) of the stochastic gradient estimator — a measure of how much the gradient estimate fluctuates from mini-batch to mini-batch due to sampling noise. High variance means the gradient direction is unreliable; low variance means the updates are stable and the optimization converges smoothly. The right-hand side is an upper bound: the actual variance may be lower, but it is guaranteed not to exceed this quantity. The bound decreases with larger batch size `$B$` (as expected from statistical averaging) and increases with larger advantages and aggregated ratios.

**Why it's monotonic in `$p$`:** The term `$\mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_i^2 \rho_{i,p}(\theta)^2]$` is strictly increasing in `$p$` because the Hölder mean `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` is strictly increasing in `$p$` (Generalized Mean Inequality) whenever the token ratios are not all identical. Intuitively: as `$p$` increases, the aggregate ratio `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` grows (since a larger `$p$` gives more weight to large individual ratios), and this inflated aggregate ratio gets squared and multiplied by the advantage in the variance bound. So larger `$p$` means potentially much larger gradient norms — which can be good for making progress (amplifying weak signals) but bad for stability (the gradient estimates become noisier).

The proof (Appendix I.2) proceeds in two steps: first bounding the unclipped estimator's variance by its second moment via the standard variance decomposition `$\text{Var}(g) = \mathbb{E}[\|g\|^2] - \|\mathbb{E}[g]\|^2 \leq \mathbb{E}[\|g\|^2]$`, then bounding the norm of each gradient contribution by `$M \cdot |\hat{A}_i| \cdot \rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` using the triangle inequality and the boundedness assumption. The sequence-level clipping makes the bound hold exactly: because the clip operator either preserves or zeroes out the entire gradient contribution, the clipped second moment is pointwise bounded by the unclipped one.

**Theorem 7 (refined variance expression under orthogonality):** Under the additional assumption of approximate token-level gradient orthogonality (Assumption 1, Appendix I.3) — that `$\mathbb{E}[g_t^\top g_k] \approx 0$` for `$t \neq k$`, justified by the high dimensionality of LLM parameter spaces and the functional specialization of neurons to specific linguistic patterns — the variance simplifies to an exact expression (not just a bound):

$$
\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{g}_i(p)\|^2] = \hat{A}_i^2 M^2 \rho_{i,p}(\theta)^2 \sum_{t=1}^{|y_i|} W_{i,t}(p)^2
$$

where `$\sum_t W_{i,t}(p)^2$` is the **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)** of the weight distribution — a measure of concentration that equals `$1/|y_i|$` for uniform weights and `$1$` for a one-hot distribution.

**What this reveals:** The variance decomposes into the product of two factors that respond to `$p$` in opposite ways:
- `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)^2$` is strictly increasing in `$p$` (Generalized Mean Inequality) — it pushes variance up as `$p$` increases.
- `$\sum_t W_{i,t}(p)^2$` (the HHI) is strictly decreasing as `$p$` decreases from `$\infty$` to `$0$` — as the weight distribution flattens from one-hot toward uniform, concentration drops and variance drops.
- As `$p \to -\infty$`, the HHI grows back toward 1 as the distribution collapses to a one-hot on the minimum-ratio tokens, counteracting the continued decrease of `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)^2$`.

The consequence (Theorem 7, property 3): **there exists a variance-minimizing `$p^* \leq 0$`**. The variance is strictly increasing for all `$p > 0$` (property 1), so the minimum cannot be in the positive regime. It must lie somewhere in the non-positive range, where the decreasing Hölder mean eventually gets counterbalanced by the increasing HHI from the negative concentration regime. This `$p^*$` is the "most stable" aggregation point — where gradient estimates have the minimum expected squared norm. The paper does not claim to compute `$p^*$` analytically for every sequence, but the existence proof is sufficient to establish that **moving `$p$` to small or negative values is the principled way to control variance**.

The combined message of Theorems 1 and 2/7 is the paper's central theoretical contribution: **the concentration–variance trade-off**. `$p$` controls two things simultaneously:
- Gradient concentration (entropy of `$W_i(p)$`, Theorem 1): larger `$p$` means more selective gradient credit on high-ratio tokens (sparse signal amplification); smaller `$p$` means more uniform or inversely concentrated credit (stability and diversity).
- Gradient variance (bound in Theorem 2, exact expression in Theorem 7): larger `$p$` means looser variance guarantees (potentially unstable updates); smaller `$p$` means tighter variance bounds (stable convergence).

These two properties pull in opposite directions: to amplify sparse signals, we want large `$p$` (high concentration); to keep training stable, we want small `$p$` (low variance). A static `$p$` must pick a compromise point on this trade-off curve, sacrificing either signal amplification or stability. A dynamic schedule can have both — in sequence, not simultaneously — by exploiting the fact that amplification matters most early in training and stability matters most late in training.

#### Dynamic Scheduling: Formal Justification and Practical Design

**Theorem 3 (advantage of dynamic scheduling):** Let `$p_{\text{stat}} \in [p_{\text{low}}, p_{\text{high}}]$` be any fixed parameter, and consider a dynamic schedule that starts at `$p_{\text{high}} > 0$` and decays monotonically to `$p_{\text{low}} \leq 0$`. Then, for a sequence `$y_i$` of length `$n$`:

1. **Early-phase signal amplification:** If the sequence contains a high-ratio token `$t^*$` with `$r_{i,t^*} \gg 1$` while other tokens have constant-bounded ratios, and provided the pre-saturation condition `$r_{i,t^*}^{p_{\text{high}}} \ll n - 1$` holds (meaning the high-ratio token's `$p_{\text{high}}$`-th power is not yet so large that it saturates the weight distribution), then shifting from `$p_{\text{stat}}$` to `$p_{\text{high}}$` exponentially amplifies the gradient weight of `$t^*$`:

$$
\frac{W_{i,t^*}(p_{\text{high}})}{W_{i,t^*}(p_{\text{stat}})} \geq C \cdot r_{i,t^*}^{p_{\text{high}} - p_{\text{stat}}}
$$

where `$C > 0$` is a constant. The gradient weight of the pivotal token grows exponentially in the gap `$p_{\text{high}} - p_{\text{stat}}$`, multiplied by `$\log r_{i,t^*}$`.

2. **Late-phase variance contraction:** The terminal variance bound is strictly contracted relative to any static parameter:

$$
V(p_{\text{low}}) < V(p_{\text{stat}})
$$

where `$V(p) := \mathbb{E}[\hat{A}_i^2 \rho_{i,p}(\theta)^2]$` is the term in the variance bound from Theorem 2.

**What this theorem establishes:** A static `$p_{\text{stat}}$` is Pareto-suboptimal — it must either (if `$p_{\text{stat}}$` is low) sacrifice the exponential signal amplification that the early phase could deliver, or (if `$p_{\text{stat}}$` is high) sacrifice the variance contraction that the late phase needs. The dynamic schedule achieves both advantages sequentially: it starts at `$p_{\text{high}}$` to exploit the exponential weight amplification (part 1), providing orders-of-magnitude larger gradient contributions for the rare pivotal tokens than any static low `$p$` could provide. As training progresses and the model's confidence in those once-sparse signals grows (the pre-saturation condition eventually breaks), the schedule decays to `$p_{\text{low}}$` to achieve strictly tighter variance control (part 2) than any static `$p_{\text{stat}} > p_{\text{low}}$` could maintain throughout training.

The pre-saturation condition `$r_{i,t^*}^{p_{\text{high}}} \ll n - 1$` is not a restrictive mathematical artifact — the paper argues (Remark 3, Appendix J) that it is "exceptionally easy to satisfy in modern LLM reasoning tasks" due to two structural factors: (a) chain-of-thought sequences are long (`$n \sim 10^3$`), so the background mass `$n-1$` provides a large buffer; and (b) in early training, the model has only just discovered the correct reasoning path, so its probability `$\pi_\theta$` is only marginally higher than the reference `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`, making `$r_{i,t^*}$` moderately above 1 — not large enough that its `$p_{\text{high}}$`-th power immediately saturates the weight distribution. The condition formalizes the "window of opportunity" where aggressive amplification by high `$p$` provides the maximum benefit. Once the model fits the correct trajectory and `$r_{i,t^*}$` grows large enough to violate the condition, the amplification benefit saturates, and it becomes optimal to decay `$p$` toward the variance-contracting regime — exactly what the dynamic schedule does.

**Practical schedule design:** The paper selects the schedule endpoints `$[p_{\text{high}}, p_{\text{low}}]$` and the decay shape (linear, square, cube, sinusoidal) based on three considerations (Section 4.4):

1. **Empirical performance is concentrated in a moderate range:** The static sweep in Section 4.2 shows that the strongest configurations across all benchmarks fall within `$p \in [-2, 2]$`, with performance degrading smoothly outside this interval. Extreme values (e.g., `$p = 5$` or `$p = -5$`) are not explored or reported. The default endpoints are therefore `$[2, -2]$` for mathematical reasoning — aggressive enough at the high end to amplify sparse signals, low enough at the low end to achieve meaningful variance contraction and inverse concentration.

2. **The lower bound is constrained by optimization stability:** Corollary 7 (from Theorem 7) establishes that the variance-minimizing `$p^*$` is `$\leq 0$` but not `$-\infty$`, because as `$p \to -\infty$` the weight distribution collapses to a one-hot on minimum-ratio tokens, causing the HHI to grow back to 1 and counteracting the continued decrease of `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)^2$`. Very negative `$p$` would thus be counterproductive for variance control, as the exploding HHI term offsets the Hölder mean reduction. The paper empirically finds `$p = -2$` to be a reasonable practical lower bound without exploring more negative values.

3. **The optimal range is task-dependent and model-dependent:** For Qwen2.5-Math-7B, the default `$[2, -2]$` works well because "Qwen2.5-Math's strong pre-training tolerates the aggressive upper bound for early-phase signal amplification." For Qwen2.5-Instruct-1.5B on ALFWorld, where the base model lacks domain-specific pre-training and may not produce reliable high-ratio tokens early on, a more conservative `$[1, -1]$` schedule outperforms `$[2, -2]$` (93.8% vs. 87.5% success rate, Table 3). The endpoints should be calibrated to "the base model's reasoning maturity and the task's signal-density profile."

**Decay shape:** The paper sweeps four monotonic interpolation families — linear, square (`$p(t) \propto t^2$`), cube (`$p(t) \propto t^3$`), and sinusoidal — in both ascending (`$-2 \to 2$`) and descending (`$2 \to -2$`) directions (Appendix E, Table 5). Among all variants tested, the **descending linear schedule** achieves the best overall performance (54.9% on the 7B model). This is the default across all main experiments. The ascending variants (`$-2 \to 2$`) perform significantly worse, confirming that the order matters: amplification must come *first*, variance contraction *last*. The paper does not provide a theoretical characterization of the optimal decay shape, flagging this as a limitation (Section 5).

#### Practical Instantiation: Training Protocol, Models, and Hyperparameters

All the theoretical machinery operates within a standard GRPO training pipeline. The paper's experiments follow two established protocols:

**For mathematical reasoning (all models in Table 2):**
- **Training data:** 8,523 problems from MATH (Levels 3–5, Hendrycks et al., 2021), following the recipe of Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025). This subset provides a diverse spread of difficulty appropriate for RL post-training.
- **Rollout configuration:** Each prompt is paired with `$G = 8$` sampled rollouts from the reference (old) policy `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$`, capped at 3,000 tokens per rollout. Within each RL round, `$\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$` produces 1,024 trajectories total (128 prompts × 8 rollouts), after which the current policy `$\pi_\theta$` is refreshed 8 times (8 gradient updates per round) using a mini-batch size of `$B = 128$`.
- **Advantage estimation:** For each group of `$G = 8$` rollouts per prompt, raw reward scores `$r(x, y_i)$` are standardized: `$\hat{A}_i = \frac{r(x, y_i) - \text{mean}(\{r(x, y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}{\text{std}(\{r(x, y_j)\}_{j=1}^G)}$`. This is the standard GRPO group-relative advantage.
- **Clipping:** PPO-style clipping with `$\epsilon$` (standard value, not separately tuned), applied at the **sequence level** as in Equation 2.
- **Hölder aggregation:** Computed in log-space via Algorithm 1 (Appendix C) for numerical stability. For `$|p| < 10^{-6}$`, the geometric mean branch is used. For all other `$p$`, the power-mean formula is evaluated with the log-sum-exp identity.
- **Schedule:** Linear decay from `$p = 2$` to `$p = -2$`, applied per training step: `$p(t) = 2 - \frac{t}{T} \cdot 4$`, where `$t$` is the current step and `$T$` is the total number of training steps.
- **Models evaluated:** Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, Qwen3-4B-Base, Qwen3-8B-Base. All models are trained on 4×H100 GPUs.
- **Baselines:** GRPO (`$p = 1$`), Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025), GMPO (Zhao et al., 2025), GSPO (Zheng et al., 2025), PMPO (Zhao et al., 2026), and multiple static HölderPO configurations (`$p \in \{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\}$`), all under matched configurations (same data, same rollouts, same number of updates).
- **Evaluation:** Pass@1 accuracy, greedy decoding with temperature 0.0, one sample per question. Benchmarks: AIME24 (30 problems), AMC (83), MATH500 (500), Minerva (272), OlympiadBench (675). All answers graded using standard reference implementations.

**For agentic reasoning (ALFWorld, Table 3):**
- **Protocol:** Follows GiGPO (Feng et al., 2025) for both training and evaluation.
- **Base model:** Qwen2.5-Instruct-1.5B — an instruction-tuned model without domain-specific mathematical pre-training.
- **Schedule:** Evaluated with both `$[2, -2]$` (87.5% success) and `$[1, -1]$` (93.8% success), with the more conservative range proving superior due to the base model's weaker reasoning priors (as argued in Section 4.4, consideration 3).
- **Evaluation:** Success rate across six ALFWorld sub-task categories: Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, Pick Two. Standard evaluation protocol for the environment.

**What makes this "drop-in":** The algorithm is a single-line modification to the GRPO loss function (Algorithm 1 in Appendix C): replace `$\frac{1}{|y_i|} \sum_t r_{i,t}$` with `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` using the current `$p(t)$` from the schedule. No additional models (critic, verifier, auxiliary networks), no architectural changes to the policy network, no changes to the rollout sampling procedure, no changes to advantage estimation. The only new hyperparameters introduced are `$p_{\text{high}}$`, `$p_{\text{low}}$`, and the decay shape — everything else (learning rate, optimizer, number of rollouts, clipping `$\epsilon$`, etc.) follows standard GRPO defaults. This minimalism is deliberate: it isolates the effect of aggregation to `$p$` alone, avoiding confounding factors from auxiliary mechanisms.

#### Relationship to Key Design Alternatives

**Why not token-level clipping?** As discussed in the Objective section and explicitly ablated in Appendix D (Table 4), token-level clipping breaks the algebraic correspondence between `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)$` and the weight distribution `$W_i(p)$` that makes the variance bound monotonic in `$p$`. Under token-level clipping, the per-token ratios `$r_{i,t}$` are individually clipped before aggregation, which means the Hölder mean is computed over a modified set of ratios that are not simply the `$p$`-th powers of the original ratios. The gradient then involves a mix of clipped and unclipped terms inside the summation (Equation 13), and the outer multiplier `$\rho_{i,p}(\theta)^{1-p}/|y_i|$` no longer cleanly factors. The paper's empirical confirmation: token-level clipping produces "a noticeably narrower performance spread across `$p$`" (Table 4 vs. Table 2) — meaning `$p$` loses its ability to sharply differentiate between sparse-signal and dense-signal tasks, which is precisely the control that dynamic scheduling exploits. Sequence-level clipping preserves this differentiability while still preventing unbounded ratio explosion (proved in Appendix I.2 via the ODE argument: without clipping, `$\rho_{i,p}(\tau)$` follows the exponential growth law `$d\rho/d\tau = k(\tau)\rho$` as the policy improves, so clipping is necessary but must be applied at the sequence level to maintain the variance structure).

**Why not per-trajectory adaptation (like PMPO)?** PMPO adapts `$p$` per trajectory based on the effective sample size of importance ratios within that trajectory. The paper argues (Section 2) that temporal adaptation — scheduling `$p$` across training steps — is more principled for the concentration–variance trade-off because the relative importance of signal amplification versus variance contraction shifts systematically across training phases, not across trajectories within a phase. Early training universally benefits from high `$p$` regardless of per-trajectory statistics: the model has not yet learned the reasoning patterns, so every trajectory where a correct signal appears needs maximum amplification. Late training universally benefits from low `$p$` regardless of per-trajectory statistics: the model has internalized the reasoning, and the goal shifts to stable convergence and diversity preservation. Per-trajectory adaptation would produce high variance in `$p$` across rollouts at the same training step, potentially mixing amplification-seeking and stability-seeking updates simultaneously, which defeats the purpose of the trade-off. The paper does not ablate per-trajectory vs. temporal adaptation directly, but the theoretical framing (Theorem 3's proof structure, which relies on early-phase vs. late-phase distinction) strongly favors the temporal axis.

**Why linear decay?** The paper sweeps four monotonic interpolation families in Appendix E (Table 5): linear, square, cube, and sinusoidal, in both ascending and descending directions. Among all seven variants, the descending linear schedule achieves the highest overall average (54.9% on the 7B model). The paper does not provide a theoretical reason for linearity being optimal, but the experimental sweep shows that more aggressive early decay (square, cube) underperforms linear, while sinusoidal (which spends more time near the endpoints) also underperforms. The ascending variants all underperform, confirming that the order matters more than the shape. The paper flags the lack of theoretical characterization of optimal decay shape as a limitation (Section 5).

**Why not add auxiliary signals to the weights?** The paper's framework focuses purely on the importance ratio `$r_{i,t}$` as the sole input to token weighting. Concurrent work modifies token weights using external signals (entropy, confidence, KL divergence from reference) that could provide additional information about which tokens are "important" beyond what the ratio alone captures. The paper explicitly positions these approaches as orthogonal: HölderPO defines *how* token-level information (whatever that information is) gets aggregated into a sequence-level scalar; auxiliary signal methods define *what information* feeds into the per-token weights. The two could be combined — e.g., use an entropy-based token importance score raised to the `$p$`-th power before Hölder aggregation. The paper leaves this combination to future work.

**Why not use a learned/adaptive `$p$`?** The dynamic schedule is a fixed, hand-designed annealing function — it does not adapt to real-time training metrics (gradient variance, ratio dispersion, validation performance). The paper identifies adaptive scheduling as a primary future direction (Section 5): "an adaptive scheduler that adjusts `$p$` from real-time metrics (e.g., batch-level gradient variance or token-ratio dispersion), removing the need for manual tuning." The current work establishes the theoretical foundation and empirical efficacy of dynamic scheduling; the adaptive extension is deferred. The limitation is explicitly acknowledged: "The schedule introduces hyperparameters (`$p_{\text{high}}$`, `$p_{\text{low}}$`, decay shape) that require empirical tuning per task."

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Aggregation as a Continuous Spectrum Rather Than a Fixed Architectural Choice

The paper's most fundamental conceptual move is **reframing token-level probability aggregation in GRPO from a discrete design decision (arithmetic mean vs. geometric mean vs. some other fixed operator) to a continuous, tunable axis parameterized by a single scalar `p`**. Before HölderPO, the field treated aggregation as an architectural choice: GRPO used the arithmetic mean (`p = 1`), GMPO and GSPO argued for the geometric mean (`p → 0`) on grounds of variance reduction, and PMPO explored a restricted power-mean range (`p ∈ [0, 1]`) adapted per-trajectory. Each new variant proposed a *different fixed operator*, motivated by different desiderata, without a framework for understanding how these operators relate to one another or when one should be preferred over another.

This fragmented landscape is symptomatic of a deeper problem: different aggregation functions produce qualitatively different optimization dynamics, but the field lacked the vocabulary to describe *why* or *how* they differ. The arithmetic mean amplifies outlier importance ratios (making it sensitive to rare high-confidence tokens but vulnerable to noise-driven variance spikes). The geometric mean dampens outliers (making it stable but potentially diluting sparse reasoning signals). These are not arbitrary properties — they are points on a continuum where moving `p` up or down trades off concentration against variance in a mathematically precise way.

HölderPO's reframing does two things that no prior work did. First, it **identifies the full real line `p ∈ ℝ` as the relevant parameter space**, discovering that `p < 0` is a qualitatively distinct **inverse-concentration regime** where gradient weight concentrates on low-ratio tokens rather than high-ratio ones. This regime was unexplored by prior work — PMPO constrained `p` to `[0, 1]`, missing both the positive-amplification regime (`p > 1`) and the negative-diversity regime (`p < 0`). The paper's empirical results show that the optimal `p` lies outside `[0, 1]` on both sides: `p = 3` achieves 46.7% on AIME24 (vs. 40.0% for `p = 1`) and `p = -1` achieves 85.0% on MATH500 (vs. 83.4% for `p = 1`). These are not minor performance differences — they represent **qualitative improvements accessible only by leaving the conventional `p ∈ [0, 1]` range**.

Second, it **provides a rigorous theoretical characterization of what `p` controls** — gradient concentration (Theorem 1, quantified by the Shannon entropy of the token weight distribution) and gradient variance bounds (Theorems 2 and 7, quantified by a monotonic upper bound with a proven minimum at `p* ≤ 0`). This transforms `p` from a hyperparameter to be tuned into a **theoretically meaningful control knob** with provable properties. One can now reason about aggregation in terms of trade-offs — "increasing `p` amplifies sparse signals at the cost of looser variance guarantees" — rather than in terms of heuristics. This is a significant upgrade in conceptual clarity: it explains *why* GMPO/GSPO's geometric mean sometimes outperforms GRPO's arithmetic mean (variance-sensitive dense-signal tasks) and sometimes underperforms (signal-dilution on sparse-signal tasks), reconciling conflicting results from prior work within a single framework.

The significance of this reframing extends beyond HölderPO's specific empirical results. It establishes a **new axis for algorithmic design** in the GRPO family: rather than proposing yet another fixed aggregation operator, future work can explore dynamic `p` schedules, adaptive `p` controllers, or task-conditioned `p` predictors. The fact that `p` is continuous and monotonic in its effects makes it amenable to optimization (gradient-based or Bayesian), unlike the discrete choice between arithmetic and geometric means that prior work treated as a binary architectural decision. This is a fundamental shift, not an incremental refinement — it changes the *type of question* researchers ask about aggregation, from "which operator is best?" to "what is the optimal `p(t)` trajectory?"

---

### Innovation 2: Dynamic Temporal Scheduling as the Resolution to a Structural Trade-off That No Static Configuration Can Resolve

The paper's second major insight is that the concentration–variance trade-off is not just an empirical observation but a **structural necessity** — no fixed `p` can simultaneously achieve strong signal amplification (requiring high `p`) and tight variance control (requiring low `p`) — and that the correct resolution is **temporal scheduling**, not per-trajectory adaptation. This is a distinctive meta-strategy contribution: it does not propose a new `p` value that is universally better, but rather a new *way of using `p`* that dynamically allocates different aggregation behaviors to different training phases.

The idea that hyperparameters should be scheduled rather than fixed is not itself novel in deep learning (learning rate annealing, exploration schedules in RL, curriculum learning). What makes this contribution distinctive is the **tight coupling between the theoretical characterization of the trade-off (Theorems 1–3) and the design of the schedule**. The paper proves formally that a decaying schedule achieves what no static parameter can: early-phase exponential signal amplification (Theorem 3, part 1) and late-phase strict variance contraction (Theorem 3, part 2). The proof is not merely an existence argument — it quantifies the *exponential* amplification gain (`r_{i,t*}^{p_high - p_stat}`) and establishes the *strict inequality* `V(p_low) < V(p_stat)` for any static intermediate `p`. This means the schedule is **provably non-dominated**: there exists no single `p` that matches the schedule's early-phase amplification *and* late-phase variance contraction simultaneously.

This is fundamentally different from PMPO's per-trajectory adaptation (Zhao et al., 2026), which adjusts `p` based on per-sequence statistics (effective sample size of importance ratios). Per-trajectory adaptation operates on the assumption that the optimal `p` varies *across trajectories* within a training step — some trajectories need more amplification, others need more stability. HölderPO's temporal scheduling operates on the assumption that the optimal `p` varies *across training steps* — early training universally needs amplification, late training universally needs stability. The paper does not ablate these two adaptation axes against each other directly, but the theoretical framework (Theorem 3's phase-based analysis) and empirical results (the descending schedule dramatically outperforms ascending `-2 → 2`, Table 5) both strongly support the temporal axis as the more fundamental one. The fact that *reversing* the schedule direction degrades performance (ascending `-2 → 2` achieves 52.6% average vs. 54.9% for descending `2 → -2`, Table 5) is telling: it confirms that the training lifecycle has an inherent directionality — early amplification, late contraction — that a static compromise cannot respect.

The broader significance of this insight is that it **shifts the optimization problem from finding a single best `p` to designing an optimal `p(t)` trajectory**. This is a higher-dimensional problem (schedule shape, endpoints, decay rate vs. a single scalar), but also a richer one: the search space admits solutions that are strictly better than any static configuration, as Theorem 3 proves. The paper's ablation of schedule shapes (Appendix E) and endpoint ranges (Section 4.4) provides initial empirical guidance for this design space, but the theoretical framework suggests that even more sophisticated schedules — adaptive, data-dependent, or multi-phase — could yield further gains. This is an incremental contribution in implementation (it's "just" adding a schedule to an existing parameter) but a fundamental one in conception: it redefines what it means to "choose `p`" from a single-point decision to a trajectory design problem. The paper acknowledges that the schedule hyperparameters require empirical tuning (Section 5, Limitations), which is a practical weakness but does not diminish the conceptual advance.

---

### Innovation 3: Empirical Discovery of the Inverse-Concentration Regime (`p < 0`) and Its Role in Diversity Preservation

Prior to this work, the `p < 0` regime for power-mean aggregation was essentially unexplored in the LLM reasoning literature. GMPO and GSPO set `p → 0` (geometric mean) motivated by variance reduction — they moved `p` *toward* zero from above but did not cross into negative territory. PMPO constrained `p` to `[0, 1]`, explicitly excluding negative values. The default assumption in the field was that the useful aggregation spectrum lay between the arithmetic and geometric means, with the geometric mean representing the limit of variance reduction.

HölderPO's empirical results overturn this assumption. On MATH500, a dense-signal benchmark where variance control is the primary bottleneck, **`p = -1` achieves 85.0% accuracy, outperforming all non-negative `p` values**: `p = 0` (84.6%), `p = 1` (83.2%), `p = 2` (82.0%), `p = 3` (81.8%). The performance monotonically improves as `p` moves from positive to negative, crossing the geometric mean and continuing upward — the optimal configuration lies in a region that prior work did not explore. This is not a marginal gain: the 85.0% at `p = -1` vs. 83.2% at `p = 1` represents a meaningful improvement on a saturated benchmark.

The theoretical significance of this discovery goes beyond the empirical numbers. Theorem 1 characterizes the `p < 0` regime as **downward concentration** — the gradient weight distribution concentrates on tokens with the *smallest* importance ratios, which represent steps where the current policy assigns lower probability than the reference (the model was "hesitant" or "uncertain" about these tokens). The paper interprets this as **retrospective diversity preservation**: within successful trajectories, the tokens where the model deviated from its prior distribution represent alternative reasoning pathways that it explored despite low confidence. By upweighting these tokens in the gradient, negative `p` forces the optimizer to consolidate these unconventional pathways rather than collapsing to the single most-confident reasoning path.

This interpretation connects to a broader challenge in RL for reasoning: the **distribution sharpening trap** identified by He et al. (2025). Standard GRPO predominantly rewards tokens that are already likely under the current policy, creating a positive feedback loop where the model becomes increasingly confident in its existing reasoning patterns and fails to explore alternative approaches. The negative-`p` regime provides a **gradient-level mechanism for resisting this collapse**: it explicitly redirects credit toward low-confidence tokens, forcing the model to strengthen the reasoning steps it was uncertain about rather than just amplifying the ones it was already confident in. This is a conceptually clean solution to the sharpening problem — it does not require changing the sampling procedure or adding entropy bonuses to the objective, but rather modifies how credit is allocated within already-generated trajectories.

The combination of positive-`p` early training (aggressive signal amplification on sparse pivotal tokens) and negative-`p` late training (diversity-preserving credit redistribution to hesitant tokens) creates a **two-phase optimization dynamic** that is qualitatively richer than any static aggregation: first identify and amplify the critical reasoning steps, then consolidate and diversify the surrounding reasoning patterns. The dynamic schedule is not merely a compromise between two endpoints but a **structured curriculum** that exploits the distinct functional roles of positive and negative `p` at different training stages. This is a non-obvious empirical discovery — it was not predicted by theory (the concentration analysis in Theorem 1 treats positive and negative `p` as symmetric entropy-reducing regimes) but rather emerged from the experimental sweep in Table 1 and was subsequently retrofitted with an interpretation. The fact that the optimal schedule endpoint is `p = -2` for mathematics (where diversity preservation matters in late training to avoid mode collapse on standard solution templates) but `p = -1` for ALFWorld (where the base model lacks strong priors, making negative-`p` diversity preservation more urgent) further supports the functional interpretation: the optimal `p_low` depends on how much the task benefits from resisting distributional collapse.

---

### Innovation 4: A Formal Proof That the Variance-Minimizing Aggregation Point Lies at `p* ≤ 0`, Not at `p = 0`

The field's prior intuition — reflected in GMPO and GSPO adopting `p → 0` — was that the geometric mean represents the "safest" aggregation in terms of gradient variance because it dampens outlier importance ratios most effectively among non-negative power means. This intuition is partly correct (variance is strictly decreasing as `p` decreases from `∞` to `0`, Theorem 2) but **incomplete**: it assumes that decreasing `p` below zero continues to decrease variance, which Theorem 7 proves is false.

The paper's refined variance analysis (Theorem 7, Appendix I.4) decomposes the gradient variance into the product of two terms: `ρ_{i,p}(θ)^2` (the squared Hölder mean, which decreases as `p` decreases) and `∑_t W_{i,t}(p)^2` (the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index of the weight distribution, which reaches its minimum at `p = 0` where weights are uniform and increases as `|p|` grows). As `p` moves from `0` toward `-∞`, the Hölder mean continues to decrease (reducing variance) but the HHI grows toward 1 as the weight distribution collapses to a one-hot on the minimum-ratio tokens (increasing variance). These two effects oppose each other, and the existence of a minimum at some `p* ≤ 0` is guaranteed by the Extreme Value Theorem — the function is continuous, bounded below by 0, and strictly increasing for `p > 0`.

The significance of this result is that it **corrects the field's implicit assumption that lower `p` is always better for variance**. The geometric mean (`p = 0`) is not the variance-minimizing aggregation — it is simply the point of maximum weight entropy. Further decreases in `p` can reduce variance if the Hölder-mean reduction dominates, but at some point the HHI growth overtakes and variance begins to increase again. The minimum lies somewhere in the negative regime, but not at `-∞`. This explains why `p = -2` works well empirically while extreme negative values (e.g., `p = -10`) would likely be unstable: the HHI collapse would dominate, producing high-variance, near-one-hot gradients on minimum-ratio tokens.

This is a **theoretical diagnostic contribution** rather than an empirical one — it does not directly produce a performance gain but clarifies the structure of the optimization landscape, preventing future work from making the mistake of assuming that arbitrarily negative `p` is beneficial for stability. It also provides theoretical justification for the dynamic schedule's endpoint selection: `p_low` should be chosen in the negative regime (to exploit the variance minimum) but not arbitrarily negative (to avoid the HHI rebound). The paper's empirical choice of `p_low = -2` is consistent with this analysis, though the exact location of `p*` depends on the sequence-level ratio distribution and is not computed analytically.

Combined with Theorem 2's monotonic variance bound for `p > 0`, Theorem 7 completes the picture: **variance is monotonically increasing for all `p > 0`, has a global minimum at some `p* ≤ 0`, and increases again as `p → -∞`**. This V-shaped variance curve (monotonically decreasing from `+∞` to `p*`, then increasing from `p*` to `-∞`) is a precise structural result that no prior work had established. It transforms the qualitative intuition "low `p` is more stable" into a quantitative characterization with a proven minimum, enabling principled selection of the low-`p` endpoint in dynamic schedules.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All mathematical reasoning experiments use **MATH** (Hendrycks et al., 2021), specifically the split from Lightman et al. (2022) containing 12,000 training questions and 500 test questions. The paper explicitly selects MATH because "test-time compute is expected to help most when the model already possesses the necessary knowledge and the challenge is drawing complex inferences — mathematical reasoning fits this profile." For agentic evaluation, the paper uses **ALFWorld** (Shridhar et al., 2020) with its six standard sub-task categories (Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, Pick Two).

- **Base model(s).** The paper evaluates across multiple model families and scales. For the primary mathematical reasoning experiments (Section 4.3, Table 2), the main model is **Qwen2.5-Math-7B** (Yang et al., 2024), with additional experiments on Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (Guo et al., 2025), Qwen3-4B-Base, and Qwen3-8B-Base (Yang et al., 2025a). The Qwen2.5-Math series is chosen because it represents "a broad spectrum of base models" with strong but not saturated mathematical pre-training — the 7B base model achieves only 26.5% average accuracy across five benchmarks before RL post-training (Table 2), leaving substantial room for improvement. For agentic tasks, the paper uses **Qwen2.5-Instruct-1.5B** (Qwen et al., 2025), an instruction-tuned model without domain-specific mathematical pre-training.

- **Metrics.** For mathematical reasoning, the primary metric is **Pass@1 accuracy** — the fraction of questions for which a single greedily decoded answer (temperature 0.0, one sample per question) matches the ground truth. All answers are graded using reference implementations from the respective benchmarks. The paper reports individual benchmark scores (AIME24, AMC, MATH500, Minerva, OlympiadBench) and an unweighted average across all five. For ALFWorld, the metric is **task success rate** — the fraction of episodes where the agent successfully completes the specified task — broken down by sub-task category and averaged.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not frame comparison around a generation budget in the sense of "number of rollouts per prompt at inference time," since all models are evaluated with Pass@1 (single greedy sample). Instead, **training compute is standardized** through matched configurations: all compared methods use the same training data (8,523 MATH problems), the same number of rollouts per prompt (`G = 8`), the same number of trajectories per RL round (1,024), and the same number of policy updates per round (8 updates with mini-batch size 128). All models are trained on **4×H100 GPUs**. The paper reports no wall-clock time or total FLOPs, making compute comparison purely about matched training protocol rather than quantitative resource accounting.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against a comprehensive set of recent methods. For the main 7B experiments (Table 2): **GRPO** (`p = 1`) (Shao et al., 2024), **Dr.GRPO** (Liu et al., 2025), **GMPO** (`p → 0`) (Zhao et al., 2025), **GSPO** (Zheng et al., 2025), **PMPO** (Zhao et al., 2026), and several static HölderPO configurations (`p ∈ {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3}`). For the 1.5B experiments: the same baselines plus **Oat-Zero-1.5B** (Liu et al., 2025). For the R1-Distill-Qwen-7B experiments: GRPO, Dr.GRPO, GMPO, PMPO. For the Qwen3 experiments (Tables 6–7): GRPO, **GSPO**, and **DAPO** (Yu et al., 2025). Additional 7B baselines from prior work are included: **SimpleRL-Zero-7B** (Zeng et al., 2025), **PRIME-Zero-7B** (Cui et al., 2025), **OpenReasoner-Zero-7B** (Hu et al., 2025), **Eurus-7B** (Yuan et al., 2024), **GPG-7B** (Chu et al., 2025), and **Oat-Zero-7B** (Liu et al., 2025). For agentic tasks (Table 3): **GRPO**, **GMPO**, and **GiGPO** (Feng et al., 2025). All baselines are run under matched training configurations (same data, rollout budget, optimizer settings, and number of updates) to ensure fair comparison — the only varying factor is the RL algorithm and, for HölderPO, the value of `p`.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation for strategy selection, since `p` is either fixed (static experiments) or follows a predetermined schedule (dynamic experiments). For the schedule-shape ablation (Appendix E, Table 5), the paper runs each variant once and reports the resulting benchmark scores — there is no averaging across random seeds or folds. The test sets are fixed: AIME24 (30 problems), AMC (83), MATH500 (500), Minerva (272), OlympiadBench (675), ALFWorld (six sub-tasks with standard evaluation protocol). The paper reports no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or statistical significance tests for any result.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Task-Specific Sensitivity of `p` (Section 4.2, Table 1, Figure 1)

The paper's foundational empirical claim is that the optimal aggregation parameter `p` differs dramatically depending on the signal-density profile of the task. Table 1 reports Pass@1 accuracy for Qwen2.5-Math-7B under eight different objective configurations:

| Training Objective | AIME24 (Sparse) | MATH500 (Dense) |
|---|---|---|
| GRPO (`p = 1`) | 40.0 | 83.4 |
| GMPO (`p → 0`) | 43.3 | 82.0 |
| HölderPO (`p = -2`) | 36.7 | 84.6 |
| HölderPO (`p = -1`) | 36.7 | **85.0** |
| HölderPO (`p → 0`) | 43.3 | 84.6 |
| HölderPO (`p = 1`) | 40.0 | 83.2 |
| HölderPO (`p = 2`) | 43.3 | 82.0 |
| HölderPO (`p = 3`) | **46.7** | 81.8 |

On AIME24, a sparse-signal benchmark where "correct reasoning is concentrated in a small number of rare, high-magnitude tokens," performance peaks at `p = 3` (46.7%), representing a **6.7 percentage point absolute improvement** over GRPO (`p = 1`, 40.0%) and a **3.4 point improvement** over GMPO (`p → 0`, 43.3%). The paper notes this "breaks the existing performance ceiling" on AIME24 — prior work had achieved 43.3% (GMPO), and HölderPO at `p = 3` pushes to 46.7%. The performance curve is monotonically increasing over `p ∈ {1, 2, 3}`, consistent with the theory that larger `p` concentrates gradient weight on the rare high-importance-ratio tokens that characterize successful AIME24 solutions.

On MATH500, a dense-signal benchmark where "supervision is more densely distributed across many tokens," the pattern is essentially reversed. Performance peaks at `p = -1` (85.0%), with a **1.8 point improvement** over GRPO (`p = 1`, 83.2%) and a **3.0 point improvement** over GMPO (`p → 0`, 82.0%). The performance curve is not perfectly monotonic — `p = -1` slightly outperforms `p = -2` (85.0% vs. 84.6%) — but the trend is clear: **all negative-`p` configurations (`p = -2, -1`) outperform all non-negative-`p` configurations (`p = 0, 1, 2, 3`)** on this benchmark. The best negative `p` (-1) achieves 85.0% vs. 83.2% for the best non-negative `p` (0 or 1), a 1.8 percentage point gap.

The paper highlights that these optimal `p` values lie outside the range explored by prior work. PMPO (Zhao et al., 2026) constrains `p ∈ [0, 1]`, which would peak at 83.4% on MATH500 (`p = 0` or `p = 1` identical to GRPO within rounding) and at 43.3% on AIME24 (`p = 1` in that range). By extending to `p = 3` and `p = -1`, HölderPO recovers substantial gains that a restricted-`p` method cannot access.

Figure 1 visually encodes this divergence: the bar chart shows AIME24 accuracy monotonically increasing from `p = -1` to `p = 3`, while MATH500 accuracy monotonically decreases over the same range — the two curves are essentially mirror images. The dashed horizontal lines mark GRPO baselines, showing that GRPO (`p = 1`) sits roughly in the middle for both, achieving neither the peak on AIME24 (which requires `p > 1`) nor the peak on MATH500 (which requires `p < 1`). The bottom row of Figure 1 visualizes the token weight distribution `W(p)` for representative sequences, showing how the distribution deforms from right-skewed (at positive `p`, concentrating on high-ratio tokens) to flat (at `p = 0`) to left-skewed (at negative `p`, concentrating on low-ratio tokens).

**Significance assessment:** These results directly support the paper's central claim that "no single static `p` yields optimal performance universally." The optimal `p` on AIME24 (3) is not merely different from the optimal `p` on MATH500 (-1) — they are on opposite sides of zero, producing qualitatively opposite gradient concentration behaviors. This empirical divergence is the foundation for dynamic scheduling: if the optimal static `p` differs this dramatically across tasks, then a single task can be viewed as transitioning from sparse-signal (early training) to dense-signal (late training), motivating a decaying schedule.

However, the result is demonstrated on only two benchmarks (AIME24 and MATH500) for the static sweep. The paper does not report static `p` sweeps on the other three mathematical benchmarks (AMC, Minerva, OlympiadBench), so it is unclear whether the optimal static `p` on those benchmarks lies in the positive, negative, or zero range. The sweeping conclusion that "there is no silver bullet among static mean functions" is drawn from two data points.

#### Aggregate Performance and Dynamic Scheduling (Section 4.3, Table 2)

Table 2 is the paper's central results table, reporting Pass@1 accuracy across five mathematical benchmarks for Qwen2.5-Math-7B (with extensions to 1.5B and R1-Distill-7B shown in separate sections). The headline result for the 7B model: **HölderPO with linear decay `p: 2 → -2` achieves an average accuracy of 54.9%** across the five benchmarks, compared to:

- GRPO (`p = 1`): 51.2% (a **7.2% relative gain**: `(54.9 - 51.2) / 51.2 ≈ 0.072`)
- GMPO (`p → 0`): 52.7% (a 4.2% relative gain)
- PMPO: 54.2% (a 1.3% relative gain)
- Best static HölderPO configuration: 52.6% (achieved by both `p → 0` and `p = 3`, a 4.4% relative gain)

The dynamic schedule's 54.9% is the highest average in the table, surpassing all prior work and all static configurations. Breaking down by benchmark:

- **AIME24**: 43.3% (ties with `p → 0` and `p = 2`, below `p = 3` at 46.7%). The paper acknowledges the schedule does not beat the best single-benchmark static configuration here — AIME24's optimal static `p = 3` achieves 46.7%.
- **AMC**: 68.7% (ties with PMPO as the highest in the table, substantially above GRPO at 59.0% and GMPO at 61.4%). This represents a **9.7 percentage point improvement over GRPO**.
- **MATH500**: 82.2% (below the best static `p = -1` at 85.0% and PMPO at 83.8%, but comparable to GRPO at 83.4%). The schedule sacrifices some MATH500 performance relative to the dense-signal-optimal `p = -1`.
- **Minerva**: 34.9% (ties with PMPO as the highest among methods trained from Qwen2.5-Math-7B, above GRPO's 32.4%).
- **OlympiadBench**: 45.3% (below `p = 2` at 46.5% and PMPO at 46.7%, but above GRPO's 41.3% and GMPO's 43.6%).

The dynamic schedule's advantage is **distributional rather than pointwise**: on no single benchmark does it achieve the absolute highest score (AIME24 favors `p = 3`, MATH500 favors `p = -1`, OlympiadBench favors PMPO and `p = 2`). But on the **unweighted average across all five benchmarks**, the schedule strictly outperforms every static configuration and every prior method. This is the key empirical insight: the schedule efficiently allocates the benefits of high-`p` and low-`p` aggregation to the benchmarks where each is most valuable, producing a better overall portfolio performance even though it may lose on any single task to a task-specific static optimum. The paper does not report per-benchmark statistical significance, so the magnitude of differences (e.g., 54.9% vs. 54.2% for PMPO, a difference of 0.7 percentage points on the average) should be interpreted as point estimates without confidence bounds.

**1.5B model results (Table 2, top section):** HölderPO-1.5B with dynamic scheduling (decay details not explicitly stated, inferred to be `2 → -2` linear) achieves 44.5% average accuracy, compared to:
- Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B base: 33.1%
- Oat-Zero-1.5B: 42.1%
- GMPO-1.5B: 43.9%
- HölderPO-1.5B: 44.5%

On AIME24 specifically, HölderPO-1.5B achieves 30.0%, a **10-point gain** over the next-best 1.5B method (Oat-Zero at 20.0%). This is a striking result — the 1.5B model reaches AIME24 accuracy comparable to some 7B baselines (GRPO-7B: 40.0%, GMPO-7B: 43.3%) despite being ~4.7× smaller, though the paper does not perform a FLOPs-matched comparison to quantify this efficiency.

**R1-Distill-Qwen-7B results (Table 2, bottom section):** Starting from a stronger base model (already fine-tuned on DeepSeek-R1 distillation data), HölderPO with dynamic scheduling achieves **66.4% average accuracy**, compared to:
- GRPO: 59.3%
- Dr.GRPO: 61.5%
- GMPO: 63.4%
- PMPO: 64.6%
- HölderPO: 66.4%

The gain over PMPO is 1.8 percentage points on the average; over GRPO it is 7.1 points. On AIME24 specifically, the dynamic schedule achieves 53.3%, a **10-point gain over GRPO** (43.3%) and a 6.6-point gain over PMPO (46.7%). This demonstrates that HölderPO's benefits compound with stronger base models — the relative gain over GRPO is larger on the R1-Distill model (12.0% relative: `(66.4 - 59.3) / 59.3`) than on the Qwen2.5-Math-7B model (7.2% relative).

**Generalization to Qwen3 base models (Appendix F, Tables 6–7):** On Qwen3-4B-Base, HölderPO achieves 50.9% average accuracy vs. GRPO's 43.9% (a 7.0-point gain), GSPO's 44.5%, and DAPO's 46.4%. On Qwen3-8B-Base, HölderPO achieves 56.9% vs. GRPO's 48.0% (an 8.9-point gain), GSPO's 48.7%, and DAPO's 52.6%. The paper notes: "The advantage of HölderPO grows with model scale." On the 8B model, Minerva shows a particularly large gain — 37.5% vs. DAPO's 27.9% — a 9.6-point absolute improvement on a benchmark requiring graduate-level multi-step derivations. This suggests the dynamic schedule's benefits are not limited to specific model architectures or pre-training distributions.

#### Agentic Reasoning Generalization (Section 4.5, Table 3)

Table 3 reports task success rates on ALFWorld using Qwen2.5-Instruct-1.5B as the base model. The paper evaluates two dynamic schedules:

- **Linear decay `2 → -2`**: 87.5% average success rate
- **Linear decay `1 → -1`**: **93.8% average success rate**

Baseline comparisons:
- GRPO (`p = 1`): 72.8%
- GMPO (`p → 0`): 85.9%
- GiGPO: 86.7%

The `1 → -1` schedule achieves the highest average, representing a **28.8% relative gain** over GRPO (`(93.8 - 72.8) / 72.8 ≈ 0.288`) and a 7.1-point absolute gain over GiGPO (86.7%). Per sub-task (for the `1 → -1` schedule):

- Pick: 96.9% (vs. GRPO 85.3%, GMPO 93.1%)
- Look: **100.0%** (vs. GRPO 53.7%, GMPO 78.6%)
- Clean: **100.0%** (vs. GRPO 84.5%, GMPO 81.0%)
- Heat: **100.0%** (vs. GRPO 78.2%, GMPO 88.2%)
- Cool: 85.7% (vs. GRPO 59.7%, GMPO 82.1%)
- Pick Two: 84.5% (vs. GRPO 53.5%, GMPO 89.5%)

The paper highlights that Look, Clean, and Heat achieve perfect 100% success rates under the conservative schedule — a remarkable result for an embodied agentic environment with multi-step textual decision-making. The performance on Pick Two (84.5%) is slightly below GMPO (89.5%), consistent with the observation that no single `p` dominates every sub-task.

The key finding is that the **conservative schedule (`1 → -1`) dramatically outperforms the aggressive schedule (`2 → -1`)** — a 6.3 percentage point difference (93.8% vs. 87.5%). The paper attributes this to the base model's characteristics: "Qwen2.5-Instruct-1.5B lacks the extensive domain-specific pre-training seen in the mathematical models, it does not initially possess strong, reliable intuitions for embodied environments." An aggressive initial `p = 2` risks "over-amplifying early, noisy exploration" because the model does not yet produce reliably high-importance-ratio tokens in successful trajectories — the positive-concentration regime amplifies noise rather than signal when the base model's reasoning is weak. A more moderate `p = 1` provides "a steadier phase of signal amplification before transitioning into variance contraction."

This result is significant for two reasons. First, it demonstrates that HölderPO transfers beyond pure mathematical reasoning to open-ended sequential decision-making — the same dynamic scheduling principle (early amplification, late contraction) works in a qualitatively different domain. Second, it establishes that the schedule endpoints are **task- and model-dependent**, not universal constants. The paper's guidance (Section 4.4) — to calibrate endpoints based on "the base model's reasoning maturity and the task's signal-density profile" — is empirically validated here rather than merely asserted.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Schedule-shape ablation (Appendix E, Table 5):** The paper sweeps four monotonic interpolation families (linear, square, cube, sinusoidal) in both ascending (`-2 → 2`) and descending (`2 → -2`) directions on Qwen2.5-Math-7B. The key findings:
- **Descending `2 → -2` linear**: 54.9% average accuracy (the default, best overall).
- **Descending square**: 51.3% — substantially worse than linear, suggesting that spending too little time at high `p` (square decay drops rapidly early) sacrifices signal amplification.
- **Descending cube**: 51.1% — even more aggressive early decay, even worse performance.
- **Descending sinusoidal**: 52.4% — intermediate.
- **All ascending variants**: `-2 → 2` linear (52.6%), square (51.4%), cube (51.4%), sinusoidal (53.1%) — all underperform the corresponding descending variants. The best ascending variant (sinusoidal, 53.1%) still trails the best descending variant (linear, 54.9%) by 1.8 percentage points.

The clear conclusion: **(1) descending is better than ascending** — the order of `p` (high first, low later) is essential; reversing it harms performance. **(2) Linear decay is the best among tested shapes** — more aggressive early decay (square, cube) underperforms, as does sinusoidal (which spends more time near the endpoints). The paper provides no theoretical explanation for why linearity is optimal, only the empirical sweep.

**Token-level vs. sequence-level clipping (Appendix D, Table 4):** HölderPO with token-level clipping (the standard in GRPO, GMPO, DAPO) is evaluated on Qwen2.5-Math-7B across five benchmarks. The key finding: **token-level clipping produces a noticeably narrower performance spread across `p`**. The range from best (`p → 0`: 52.7%) to worst (among reported: `p = -2`: 51.8%) is only 0.9 percentage points, compared to a spread of ~4.4 points under sequence-level clipping (Table 2: `p = -1` at 52.0% to `p = 0` and `p = 3` at 52.6%, with `p = -2` at 50.5%). The paper interprets this as confirmation that token-level clipping "breaks the algebraic structure underlying the variance bound's monotonicity in `p`, weakening the controlled trade-off that motivates dynamic scheduling." That is, token-level clipping makes `p` less able to differentiate between signal-density regimes — the very differentiation that the dynamic schedule exploits.

**Generalization to Qwen3 Base Models (Appendix F, Tables 6–7):** This is both an ablation (varying the base model) and a robustness check. HölderPO's advantage over GRPO generalizes: +7.0 points on Qwen3-4B (50.9% vs. 43.9%) and +8.9 points on Qwen3-8B (56.9% vs. 48.0%). The advantage over DAPO (the strongest prior token-aggregation baseline for these models) is +4.5 points on 4B and +4.3 points on 8B. The paper highlights that the relative improvement on Minerva is particularly large (+9.6 over DAPO on 8B: 37.5% vs. 27.9%), confirming gains extend to the hardest benchmark in the suite. Note: Table 6 reports AIME25 (a different year's exam) rather than AIME24, and uses Pass@8 rather than Pass@1 — this is an inconsistency in evaluation protocol relative to the main experiments, though the relative comparisons within Table 6/7 are internally consistent.

**Training dynamics: entropy and gradient-norm (Appendix B, Figure 3):** The paper visualizes per-step policy entropy and gradient norm ∥∇L∥ (log scale on Math, linear on ALFWorld) for Qwen2.5-Math-7B and Qwen2.5-1.5B under constant `p ∈ {+2, 0, -2}` and the linear decay schedule `2 → -2`. Key observations:
- **Entropy:** Positive `p` drives entropy downward rapidly (mass concentrates on high-likelihood tokens — the "sharpening" effect). Negative `p` sustains higher entropy throughout training, actively resisting mode collapse. The dynamic schedule inherits both regimes: early entropy drops sharply (like `p = +2`), then stabilizes at a higher level (like `p = -2`) in later training.
- **Gradient norm:** The dynamic schedule keeps the gradient norm in a tighter band than any constant `p`. Constant `p = +2` produces large early gradients (high variance), constant `p = -2` produces small gradients throughout. The schedule starts high (aggressive updates) and converges to moderate, avoiding both the explosive early variance of `p = +2` and the anemic updates of `p = -2`.

This figure provides qualitative validation of the mechanism: the schedule does what theory predicts — early amplification, late contraction — and the resulting training dynamics are visually more stable than any static configuration.

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the experiments support the paper's central claims?

**Claim: "HölderPO unifies token-level aggregation via the Hölder mean, and `p` provides continuous control over the gradient concentration–variance trade-off."**

The experiments in Table 1, Figure 1, and the sweep across `p ∈ {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3}` provide robust support for this claim *on the two benchmarks tested* (AIME24 and MATH500). The performance curves are cleanly opposite: AIME24 peaks at `p = 3`, MATH500 peaks at `p = -1`. The theoretical characterization of what `p` controls (concentration vs. variance) is consistent with these results — sparse-signal AIME24 benefits from concentration, dense-signal MATH500 benefits from variance reduction — but the paper does not provide *direct* empirical evidence for the theoretical mechanisms. There are no measurements of actual gradient variance, no quantification of weight distribution entropy during training, and no causal manipulation showing that the performance effects are *due to* the claimed mechanisms rather than some other consequence of changing `p`. The training dynamics plot (Figure 3) shows entropy and gradient norm, which is suggestive but not definitive. The paper's theoretical claims about concentration and variance are supported mathematically (Theorems 1–3, 5–7), but the empirical connection is correlational — the performance curves look like what the theory would predict — not causal.

**Claim: "No static configuration can universally resolve the concentration–stability trade-off, motivating dynamic scheduling."**

This is the paper's most important empirical claim, and it is supported but with important scope limitations. The evidence that no single `p` is optimal across AIME24 and MATH500 is clear (Table 1). The evidence that the dynamic schedule (54.9%) outperforms every static configuration (best: 52.6%) on the *overall average* across five benchmarks (Table 2) is also clear. However, the claim is about *universality* — "no static configuration can universally resolve" the trade-off — but the empirical demonstration covers only five mathematical benchmarks from the MATH family plus one agentic benchmark. There are entire categories of reasoning tasks (code generation, scientific reasoning, logical deduction, multi-hop QA) for which the trade-off might look different or not exist at all. The paper does not claim universality across all tasks, but the strong theoretical framing (Theorem 3's proof that any static `p` is dominated) implies a generality that the empirical evaluation does not fully match.

A more specific concern: **the dynamic schedule does not dominate every static configuration on every benchmark**. On AIME24, `p = 3` (46.7%) clearly outperforms the schedule (43.3%). On MATH500, `p = -1` (85.0%) outperforms the schedule (82.2%). On OlympiadBench, `p = 2` (46.5%) and PMPO (46.7%) outperform the schedule (45.3%). The schedule wins on the average because its losses on individual benchmarks are smaller than its gains on others — it sacrifices some peak performance on extreme-signal-density benchmarks to achieve broad competence across diverse signal densities. This is a legitimate optimization strategy, but it means the schedule is a **portfolio-level improvement, not a pointwise-dominant one**. A practitioner who only cares about AIME24 performance would be better served by static `p = 3` than by the dynamic schedule. The paper's framing of the schedule as "resolving" the trade-off could be read as claiming universal dominance, which is not supported.

**Claim: "Dynamic scheduling achieves state-of-the-art average accuracy of 54.9% across five mathematical benchmarks."**

This claim is supported, with qualifications about the comparison pool. The 54.9% is the highest reported number among the methods evaluated in Table 2 on Qwen2.5-Math-7B. The comparison includes GRPO, Dr.GRPO, GMPO, and PMPO under matched configurations. However, several important qualifications:

1. **Not all prior work is compared under matched configurations.** The paper includes numbers for SimpleRL-Zero-7B (46.6%), PRIME-Zero-7B (48.0%), OpenReasoner-Zero-7B @ 8k (45.9%), Eurus-7B (48.0%), GPG-7B (51.0%), and Oat-Zero-7B (51.4%) in Table 2, but these are taken from prior publications and were not re-run under HölderPO's training protocol. They may have used different training data, different numbers of RL rounds, different base model checkpoints, or different evaluation procedures. The paper does not describe how these numbers were obtained or verify their comparability. Given that training data quantity, quality, and RL training duration can substantially affect final performance, the comparison against these numbers should be treated as approximate rather than rigorously controlled.

2. **The "state-of-the-art" claim is relative to the specific model scale (7B).** Larger models and proprietary systems (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 at 671B parameters, OpenAI o1) achieve substantially higher absolute scores on these benchmarks, but they are not in the comparison class.

3. **The 54.9% is a 0.7-point improvement over PMPO (54.2%).** Without confidence intervals or significance tests, it is unclear whether this difference is statistically meaningful or within the range of run-to-run variance. The paper provides no uncertainty quantification for any result.

**Claim: "HölderPO achieves 93.8% success rate on ALFWorld, a 28.8% relative gain over GRPO."**

This claim is supported (Table 3) but the comparison deserves scrutiny. HölderPO with `1 → -1` schedule achieves 93.8% vs. GRPO's 72.8%. However, the baselines are not all equally tuned: GMPO achieves 85.9% and GiGPO achieves 86.7%, meaning the improvement over the *next-best* method is 7.1 percentage points, not 21 points (the GRPO-to-HölderPO gap). The 28.8% relative gain is over the *weakest* baseline (GRPO). The improvement over the strongest prior method (GiGPO) is a more modest 8.2% relative gain. (Note: the paper reports GiGPO at 86.7% and the `1 → -1` schedule at 93.8%. The raw difference is 7.1 points. Relative gain: `(93.8 - 86.7) / 86.7 ≈ 0.082`.)

#### Genuine weaknesses

**No run-to-run variance or statistical testing.** Every number in Tables 1–7 is a point estimate from a single training run. There are no error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests anywhere in the paper. Given that RL training is notoriously high-variance (random seed effects, batch ordering, initialization), a 0.7-point difference between HölderPO's 54.9% and PMPO's 54.2% could easily be within the noise floor of single-run comparisons. This is the most significant weakness in the experimental methodology — it prevents any claim of statistical superiority over the closest baselines. The paper implicitly acknowledges this by not making strong claims about PMPO being "significantly" beaten, but the "state-of-the-art" language implies a level of confidence that the methodological rigor does not support.

**Small test sets for key benchmarks.** AIME24 has 30 problems. A 3.3 percentage point difference on AIME24 (e.g., `p = 3` at 46.7% vs. `p = 0` at 43.3%) corresponds to a single additional correctly solved problem. With 30 total problems, the granularity is extremely coarse — the minimum measurable difference is ~3.3 percentage points, meaning that performance distinctions between several `p` values (e.g., `p = 0, 2, 3` at 43.3%, 43.3%, and 46.7% respectively) may hinge on one or two problems. The AMC benchmark has 83 problems, somewhat better. MATH500 (500 problems) and OlympiadBench (675 problems) provide more reliable estimates. The paper does not report per-benchmark sample sizes in the main text or discuss the implications of these small test sets for the reliability of cross-benchmark comparisons.

**No ablation of the number of RL rounds or the total training budget.** All experiments use a fixed training protocol (8,523 MATH problems, 1,024 trajectories per round, 8 updates per round), but the paper does not report how many rounds were run or perform learning curve analyses. It is possible that different methods have different convergence speeds — e.g., high-`p` methods might make faster early progress but plateau sooner, while low-`p` methods might learn more slowly but reach higher asymptotic performance. A fixed training budget might advantage whichever method happens to converge fastest, not whichever is truly better given unlimited training. The dynamic schedule could be benefiting from a "best of both worlds" effect where early amplification accelerates learning and late contraction stabilizes convergence — but without learning curves showing performance as a function of training budget, this interpretation is speculative.

**The static sweep is incomplete for characterizing the `p` axis.** The paper reports results for `p ∈ {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3}` on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, but only on AIME24 and MATH500. On the other three benchmarks (AMC, Minerva, OlympiadBench), static `p` results are only reported for the full 7B model, not for 1.5B or R1-Distill models. This makes it impossible to characterize how signal density interacts with model scale — the paper's claim that optimal `p` depends on task signal density is demonstrated at one model scale, not shown to generalize across scales. The 1.5B and R1-Distill models are evaluated primarily with the dynamic schedule, without comprehensive static `p` baselines at those scales to confirm that the schedule outperforms the best static configuration (rather than just the particular static configurations tested).

**No direct measurement of the proposed mechanism.** The paper's theoretical framework claims that `p` controls gradient concentration (entropy of `W_i(p)`) and gradient variance (bound in Theorem 2). But the experiments only measure downstream task accuracy — there are no measurements of gradient variance, weight distribution entropy, or any other mechanistic quantity during actual training runs (except the qualitative training dynamics in Figure 3, which shows aggregate entropy and gradient norm, not the token-level weight distribution entropy that Theorem 1 analyzes). The empirical connection between the theory and the results is therefore indirect: performance patterns are *consistent* with the theory, but the theory is not *verified* by the experiments. A study that actively manipulated concentration and variance and measured the predicted effects would be stronger.

**Limited exploration of endpoint selection.** The paper provides heuristics for choosing `[p_high, p_low]` (Section 4.4) but does not systematically sweep endpoints. The default `[2, -2]` is motivated by moderate range and empirical performance, but alternatives like `[3, -1]`, `[3, -2]`, `[2, -3]`, or `[1, -2]` are not explored. The finding that `[1, -1]` outperforms `[2, -2]` on ALFWorld suggests endpoints matter significantly, but the paper provides no systematic study of how endpoint choices affect performance across different base models or task types. This is a practical limitation: a practitioner wanting to apply HölderPO to a new task has little guidance beyond "try `[2, -2]` first, then try `[1, -1]` if the base model is weak."

#### Missing experiments

- **Multi-seed runs with error bars** for the dynamic schedule and the best static configurations, to establish whether the observed differences are statistically significant.
- **Learning curves** (accuracy vs. training steps or RL rounds) for different `p` values and the dynamic schedule, to characterize convergence speed vs. asymptotic performance.
- **Direct measurement of gradient variance and weight distribution entropy** during training under different `p`, to validate the theoretical mechanisms.
- **Sweep of static `p` on all five mathematical benchmarks** for all model scales (not just 7B on two benchmarks), to confirm the relationship between optimal `p` and signal density generalizes.
- **Endpoint ablation** — systematic sweep of `[p_high, p_low]` pairs to provide practical guidance for endpoint selection.
- **Comparison against a learned/adaptive `p` controller** (the paper's stated future work) — even a simple baseline like selecting `p` based on gradient variance would help establish whether the hand-designed schedule is near-optimal or significantly improvable.
- **Evaluation on non-mathematical reasoning benchmarks** beyond ALFWorld — code generation (HumanEval, MBPP), logical reasoning (ARC, ProofWriter), or multi-hop QA — to test the generality of the signal-density hypothesis.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 No Run-to-Run Variance or Statistical Significance is Reported

The paper's experimental methodology reports every performance number as a single point estimate from a single training run. There are no error bars, no confidence intervals, no standard deviations, and no multiple-seed results anywhere in Tables 1–7 or Figures 1–3.

**The consequence.** RL training runs are notoriously stochastic — random seed, batch ordering, and initialization can all induce substantial performance variation. The paper's headline result (54.9% average for the dynamic schedule vs. 54.2% for PMPO on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Table 2) represents a 0.7 percentage point difference on an unweighted average across five benchmarks. Without any estimate of run-to-run variance, a practitioner cannot determine whether this difference reflects a genuine improvement or is within the noise floor of single-run comparisons. The same issue affects the static `p` sweep in Table 1, where the optimal `p = -1` on MATH500 achieves 85.0% vs. 83.2% for `p = 1` — a 1.8-point gap that could be within single-run sampling error on a 500-problem test set.

The AIME24 benchmark compounds this issue through its tiny test size: with only 30 problems, a single additional correctly solved problem shifts accuracy by ~3.3 percentage points. The paper reports `p = 3` at 46.7%, `p = 0` and `p = 2` both at 43.3%, and the dynamic schedule at 43.3% — differences that could reflect random variation across two or three problems. The AMC benchmark (83 problems) provides somewhat better granularity, and MATH500 (500 problems) and OlympiadBench (675 problems) provide the most reliable estimates. The paper never discusses how benchmark size interacts with the reliability of reported comparisons.

**What evidence exists.** The paper acknowledges this limitation only implicitly — no variance estimates appear, and the authors make no statistical claims. The phrasing around results is descriptive ("achieves state-of-the-art average accuracy of 54.9%") rather than inferential. The training dynamics figures (Figure 3, Appendix B) show per-step entropy and gradient norm trajectories for single runs, not averages across seeds.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper provides no multi-seed results and does not discuss this limitation. This is the most significant methodological weakness because it undermines confidence in every quantitative comparison where the performance gap is smaller than plausible single-run noise levels — which includes the comparison to PMPO and several static-`p` variants.

---

### 6.2 The Schedule Hyperparameters Require Per-Task Empirical Tuning

Section 5 (Limitations) acknowledges:

> "The schedule introduces hyperparameters (phigh, plow, decay shape) that require empirical tuning per task; while linear decay performed best in our setup, we provide no theoretical characterisation of the optimal shape."

The paper selects the default schedule endpoints `[2, -2]` for mathematical reasoning based on the static sweep showing strong performance within `p ∈ [-2, 2]` and on the empirical observation that Qwen2.5-Math-7B's strong mathematical pre-training "tolerates the aggressive upper bound for early-phase signal amplification" (Section 4.4). However, this calibration is post-hoc: the authors ran the static sweep, observed the useful range, and set the endpoints accordingly. A practitioner applying HölderPO to a new task or model family cannot rely on this same range without conducting a comparable sweep.

**The consequence.** HölderPO's practical adoption requires significant hyperparameter search. The endpoints matter considerably: on ALFWorld, `[1, -1]` achieves 93.8% success while `[2, -2]` achieves only 87.5% (Table 3) — a 6.3 percentage point gap attributable to endpoint selection. The paper provides heuristics (base model reasoning maturity, task signal density) but no systematic endpoint ablation to guide selection. A practitioner cannot simply use the paper's defaults and expect them to transfer to novel domains.

The decay shape introduces additional degrees of freedom. The linear decay performs best among tested shapes (Appendix E, Table 5), but the choice is empirical rather than theoretically justified. Square and cube decays (which spend less time at high `p` and drop more rapidly) underperform substantially (~3.5 percentage points below linear), suggesting the schedule is sensitive to *how* `p` transitions, not just the endpoints. The paper does not characterize this sensitivity beyond the single sweep in Table 5.

**What evidence exists.** The schedule-shape ablation (Table 5) demonstrates that shape matters: linear decay achieves 54.9%, square achieves 51.3%, cube achieves 51.1%. The endpoint sensitivity is demonstrated concretely in ALFWorld results (Table 3) and implied by the static-sweep divergence between AIME24 (optimal `p = 3`) and MATH500 (optimal `p = -1`). The ALFWorld result directly validates that `[1, -1]` can substantially outperform `[2, -2]` in a domain where the base model lacks strong pre-training.

**Mitigation status.** The paper identifies adaptive scheduling as the primary future direction to remove manual tuning (Section 5):

> "A primary direction is an adaptive scheduler that adjusts `p` from real-time metrics (e.g., batch-level gradient variance or token-ratio dispersion), removing the need for manual tuning."

This is the correct long-term solution, but it is not implemented or evaluated in the current work. Until adaptive scheduling is available, practitioners face a hyperparameter tuning burden that the paper does not quantify — the cost of sweeping endpoints and shapes to find a near-optimal configuration for each new task/model combination.

### 6.3 The Positive-Concentration Regime Amplifies Vulnerability to Reward Hacking

Section 5 (Limitations) states:

> "The positive-concentration regime amplifies tokens with high importance ratios, making HölderPO more susceptible to reward hacking when the verifier provides false-positive signals."

This is a fundamental tension: the same mechanism that provides HölderPO's main advantage on sparse-signal tasks — aggressive amplification of high-ratio tokens via large positive `p` — is structurally more vulnerable to verifier errors. When a trajectory receives a positive advantage due to a *spurious* correct answer (the model used flawed intermediate reasoning that coincidentally produced the correct final result, a phenomenon documented by Shao et al. (2025)), the algorithm at high `p` will concentrate gradient mass on the tokens with the largest importance ratios. If those high-ratio tokens correspond to flawed reasoning that the model was confident about (and that happened to look correct to the verifier), the gradient update will reinforce the error rather than penalize it.

**The consequence.** On sparse-signal benchmarks like AIME24, where the paper advocates for high `p` (e.g., `p = 3` achieves the peak 46.7%), the risk of reward hacking is structurally elevated. The algorithm's strength — concentrating updates on rare pivotal tokens — becomes its vulnerability: if the verifier misidentifies *which* pivotal tokens matter, the concentrated gradient amplifies the wrong signal. The paper does not measure or characterize this vulnerability empirically. There are no experiments with deliberately corrupted reward signals, no analysis of how often the verifier's false-positive rate interacts with `p` to produce incorrect reinforcement, and no comparison of `p = 3` vs. `p = -1` under varying verifier quality.

The dynamic schedule partially mitigates the risk by decaying `p` in late training — when the model may have already internalized some spurious patterns, the schedule shifts to negative `p`, which concentrates gradient weight on low-ratio (hesitant) tokens rather than high-ratio (confident, potentially hacked) tokens. But this mitigation operates on the assumption that by late training, the model's most confident tokens are legitimate reasoning improvements rather than verifier-exploiting shortcuts. If reward hacking occurs *during* the early high-`p` phase, the damage may be baked in before the schedule decays.

**What evidence exists.** The paper acknowledges the limitation (Section 5) but provides no measurement of its severity. The static-`p` sweep on MATH500 (Table 1) shows that `p = 3` performs worse than `p = -1` (81.8% vs. 85.0%) — consistent with high-`p` amplifying noise on dense-signal tasks where many tokens matter — but this is about signal density, not verifier quality. The paper does not analyze whether `p = 3` on AIME24 ever reinforces incorrect reasoning patterns or produces higher-variance performance (beyond what is reported as aggregate accuracy).

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed empirically. The paper flags the issue as a known limitation and suggests it as a topic for future investigation, but provides no measurement, no mitigation strategy beyond the dynamic schedule's implicit reduction of `p` in late training, and no guidance for practitioners to assess their risk of reward hacking when deploying high `p`.

---

### 6.4 The Mechanism is Validated Only on a Single Task Family (Mathematical Reasoning) with One Non-Math Extension

All core experiments demonstrating HölderPO's theoretical mechanisms and systematic static-`p` sweeps are conducted on mathematical reasoning benchmarks from the MATH family (MATH500, AIME24, AMC, Minerva, OlympiadBench). The paper's central claims — optimal `p` depends on signal density, dynamic scheduling outperforms any static `p`, and the concentration–variance trade-off drives performance — are demonstrated exclusively on math.

**The consequence.** The paper's theoretical framework (Theorems 1–3) is mathematically general — it does not depend on math-specific properties — but the empirical validation is narrow. Several aspects of mathematical reasoning may make it unusually amenable to HölderPO's mechanisms:

- **Clean verifiability:** Math answers are deterministically gradable, providing noise-free reward signals. In domains with noisy or learned reward models (dialogue, summarization, creative generation), the advantage estimates `Â_i` would be noisier, potentially changing the optimal `p` (since positive-`p` amplifies all signals, including noise).
- **Sparse pivotal tokens:** Competition math problems genuinely contain rare, high-magnitude reasoning tokens (e.g., non-obvious substitutions, theorem applications), making them a clear case for positive-`p` amplification. Domains where reasoning is more evenly distributed (code generation, where every line contributes roughly equally) may not exhibit the same strong divergence between optimal `p` on different tasks.
- **Strong base model pre-training:** All evaluated models are pre-trained on substantial math corpora, providing reasonable initial reasoning capability that GRPO can sharpen. A domain where the base model has essentially random performance might not generate sufficiently discriminative importance ratios for `p` to meaningfully reweight gradients.

The ALFWorld result (Table 3) provides some evidence of generalization: the dynamic schedule works in an embodied agentic environment with multi-step textual decision-making, achieving 93.8% success with `[1, -1]`. However, ALFWorld is a single benchmark with a specific interaction structure, and the paper does not report a static-`p` sweep on ALFWorld to characterize whether the concentration–variance trade-off structure (opposite optimal `p` for different sub-tasks) replicates the math findings. The ALFWorld sub-tasks (Pick, Look, Clean, Heat, Cool, Pick Two) are qualitatively different from math in their reasoning demands, but the paper only reports aggregate and per-sub-task results under the schedule, not a full `p`-sweep to confirm that task-specific signal density explains the performance patterns.

**What evidence exists.** The generalization evidence consists of: (1) ALFWorld results (Table 3) showing schedule outperformance over baselines; (2) Qwen3 base model results (Appendix F, Tables 6–7) showing the method transfers across model families within math; (3) the qualitative claim about "signal density" being the relevant property rather than "mathematics." None of these directly test whether the theoretical mechanisms operate identically in non-math domains or whether the schedule endpoints `[2, -2]` transfer to, e.g., code generation or logical reasoning.

**Mitigation status.** The paper provides no mitigation beyond demonstrating one non-math benchmark (ALFWorld). Generalization to diverse reasoning domains — code generation, scientific reasoning, multi-hop QA, logical deduction — is left entirely to future work. A practitioner considering HölderPO for a non-math application has no direct evidence that the dynamic-scheduling principle will transfer, nor guidance on adapting the schedule endpoints.

---

### 6.5 The Method is Studied in Isolation from Complementary Token-Reweighting Approaches

The paper positions auxiliary-signal token-reweighting methods (Section 2) as "orthogonal to ours and could in principle be combined with HölderPO's power-mean aggregation." These methods — which weight tokens based on entropy (Wang et al., 2025a; Yu and Li, 2026), confidence (Deng et al., 2025), or selective KL masking (Lin et al., 2025) — modify *what information* feeds into token weights, while HölderPO modifies *how* those weights are aggregated. The paper never tests such combinations.

**The consequence.** The paper cannot distinguish between two interpretations of its results: (1) HölderPO's power-mean aggregation is a sufficient mechanism that captures most of the benefit achievable from better token-level credit assignment, making auxiliary signals redundant; or (2) HölderPO and auxiliary-signal methods provide complementary benefits, and the combination would outperform either alone. If interpretation (2) is correct, the paper's reported performance numbers represent a lower bound on what the approach could achieve — but the paper provides no evidence either way.

This matters for practitioners because auxiliary-signal methods are being actively developed and deployed. Without combination experiments, a practitioner cannot determine whether to invest in (a) implementing HölderPO's dynamic scheduling only, (b) implementing an auxiliary signal method only, or (c) implementing both. The paper's claim that the methods are "orthogonal" is theoretically reasonable but empirically unsubstantiated — potential interactions (e.g., entropy-based weighting might interact with `p`'s concentration behavior in non-obvious ways) are not explored.

**What evidence exists.** None within the paper. The related work section (Section 2) identifies the orthogonality but provides no empirical investigation. The paper's experiments compare HölderPO only against methods that modify the aggregation function (GRPO, GMPO, GSPO, PMPO) or address entirely different RL pipeline components (Dr.GRPO, DAPO, GPG). No experiment combines HölderPO's power-mean aggregation with any auxiliary token-weighting scheme.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not identify this as a limitation or suggest combination experiments as future work — the orthogonality is mentioned descriptively without acknowledging that the untested interaction is a gap in the evaluation. A practitioner seeking to maximize performance has no guidance on how to combine HölderPO with concurrent token-reweighting innovations.

---

### 6.6 No Evidence on Whether the Training-Time Benefits Transfer to Inference-Time Savings

The paper's evaluation measures **Pass@1 accuracy with greedy decoding** — a single sample per question at temperature 0.0. This tests the quality of the learned policy but does not characterize whether HölderPO's training benefits translate into advantages at inference time when additional compute (multiple samples, best-of-N, majority voting) is available. The dynamic schedule improves the *model's parameters* during training; it is not an inference-time method.

**The consequence.** A practitioner might reasonably ask: does training with HölderPO produce a model that (a) has higher Pass@1 accuracy (demonstrated), (b) also benefits more from inference-time compute scaling, or (c) reaches a given accuracy threshold with fewer inference samples? The paper cannot answer (b) or (c). If HölderPO's dynamic schedule produces a policy that is better calibrated but less amenable to further improvement via best-of-N sampling (e.g., because negative-`p` late training encourages diversity that reduces the consensus quality needed for majority voting to help), then the Pass@1 advantage could be offset by worse scaling behavior at inference time. Conversely, if HölderPO produces a more robust policy that reaches high accuracy with fewer inference samples, the method would have even larger practical impact than Pass@1 numbers alone indicate.

This distinction matters because modern LLM deployments routinely use inference-time compute augmentation — best-of-N, majority voting, verifier-guided search — to boost final accuracy beyond single-sample performance. A method that improves Pass@1 by 7.2% relative over GRPO (Table 2) but underperforms at Pass@64 (or equivalent inference budgets) would have limited practical relevance for high-stakes applications where inference-time compute is abundant. The paper's evaluation protocol precludes assessing this.

**What evidence exists.** The paper provides no inference-time scaling experiments — no majority voting, no best-of-N, no temperature sweeps at inference. All evaluation uses greedy decoding with a single sample. The training dynamics analysis (Appendix B, Figure 3) shows that negative-`p` late training sustains higher policy entropy than positive-`p` training, which could theoretically produce a more diverse policy that benefits more from majority voting (diverse correct solutions can be aggregated) or could produce a noisier policy where single-sample accuracy is lower. The direction of this effect is uncertain and not tested.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the training–inference compute relationship or acknowledge the lack of inference-time scaling experiments as a limitation. The method is evaluated purely as a training-time improvement, which is a legitimate scope but leaves open the question of how the gains interact with standard deployment practices.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper does not introduce a new algorithm in the sense of a novel objective function or architectural component. Instead, it performs a more foundational operation: it **extracts a single latent parameter `p` from what was previously treated as a fixed, implicit design choice** — the token-level probability aggregation operator inside GRPO — and demonstrates that this parameter controls a mathematically precise, empirically consequential trade-off between gradient concentration and variance. This is less a paradigm shift than a **conceptual reframing with immediate practical leverage**: it reveals that several independently proposed GRPO variants (GRPO, GMPO, GSPO) are not competing algorithms but **special cases (`p = 1`, `p → 0`, `p → 0`) of a unified family**, and that the previously unexplored regions of this family (`p > 1`, `p < 0`) contain configurations that strictly dominate the known special cases on specific task profiles.

The magnitude of this contribution should be understood as a **diagnostic and a design space expansion**, not a one-shot performance win. The paper's 54.9% average accuracy on Qwen2.5-Math-7B (7.2% relative gain over GRPO) is a concrete result, but the deeper impact is that it changes how researchers should think about token-level credit assignment in group-advantage RL for LLMs. Before HölderPO, the question was "which aggregation operator is best?" — a discrete choice among a handful of candidates. After HölderPO, the question becomes "what is the optimal `p(t)` trajectory for my task and model?" — a continuous design problem with a theoretically characterized trade-off surface. This shift is analogous to how the Chinchilla scaling laws (Hoffmann et al., 2022) reframed pretraining from "how big a model should I train?" to "what is the optimal allocation of compute between model size and data quantity?" In both cases, the contribution is not a new answer but a **new vocabulary for asking the question** — one that reveals previously invisible degrees of freedom and enables principled optimization over them.

The paper also performs a valuable **reconciliation function** for the GRPO variant literature. Prior work presented conflicting narratives: GMPO and GSPO argued that the geometric mean (`p → 0`) is superior on grounds of variance reduction; standard GRPO (`p = 1`) remained the default due to simplicity and broad empirical success; PMPO explored a restricted power-mean range (`p ∈ [0, 1]`). Each paper's claims were locally valid on the benchmarks and models it tested, creating an apparent contradiction. HölderPO resolves this by showing that **all these claims are simultaneously true — on different parts of the `p`-axis, for different signal-density profiles**. GMPO's geometric mean genuinely outperforms GRPO's arithmetic mean on variance-sensitive dense-signal tasks (MATH500: GMPO 82.0% vs. GRPO 83.4%? — wait, GRPO actually outperforms GMPO on MATH500 in Table 2: 83.4% vs. 82.0%. The reconciliation is more nuanced: GMPO outperforms GRPO on some benchmarks, but not all). The point is that the contradiction was an artifact of each method being evaluated as a global claim rather than as a point on a spectrum whose optimal location is context-dependent. This is a genuinely useful service to the field: it converts a fragmented landscape of competing point-solutions into a unified framework with predictable, theoretically grounded trade-offs.

The most significant **research direction deprioritization** implied by this work is the search for "better" fixed aggregation operators. If the optimal `p` depends on task signal density, model pre-training maturity, and training stage, then proposing yet another static operator (e.g., harmonic mean, trimmed mean) is unlikely to be productive — it simply adds another point on the `p`-axis that will be optimal for some specific configuration but suboptimal in general. The paper's framework absorbs these as special cases and shows that dynamic movement along the axis dominates any static point. Future GRPO research should focus on **how to move `p`**, not on **which fixed `p` to use**.

Conversely, the work **elevates the importance of verifier quality and reward signal structure** as research priorities. The paper's positive-concentration regime (`p > 0`) amplifies tokens with high importance ratios, which are computed from the policy's own probability shifts — not from external reward signals. But the advantage `Â_i` that scales the entire gradient contribution is derived from the verifier's reward. If the verifier produces false positives, high `p` will amplify the gradient updates from trajectories that were *incorrectly* rewarded, potentially reinforcing flawed reasoning. The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly (Section 5), but the implication is broader: **as we gain finer-grained control over credit assignment via `p`, the fidelity of the reward signal becomes the binding constraint**. Research on robust verifiers, reward model calibration, and spurious reward detection (the problem space opened by Shao et al., 2025) becomes more urgent, not less, when we can precisely target which tokens receive gradient mass.

Finally, the work establishes **temporal scheduling of hyperparameters as a principled resolution to structural trade-offs**, not an ad-hoc trick. The proof that any static `p` is Pareto-suboptimal (Theorem 3) — and that a decaying schedule achieves both exponential signal amplification in early training and strict variance contraction in late training — provides a template for thinking about other RL hyperparameters that may exhibit similar phase-dependent optimal values. Learning rates, clipping thresholds, KL penalties, and entropy bonuses all have plausible "explore early, stabilize late" signatures; HölderPO's theoretical framework (continuous parameterization + formal trade-off characterization + scheduling proof) offers a methodology for analyzing whether scheduling is warranted and what it achieves, rather than treating it as a heuristic to be tuned.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Adaptive `p` scheduling from real-time gradient statistics.** The paper's dynamic schedule is a fixed, hand-designed linear decay, and the authors explicitly identify this as the primary limitation: "an adaptive scheduler that adjusts `p` from real-time metrics (e.g., batch-level gradient variance or token-ratio dispersion), removing the need for manual tuning" (Section 5). This is the most direct and actionable follow-up. A strong implementation would: (1) track the empirical gradient variance (or the effective sample size of the weight distribution `W_i(p)`, which Theorem 7 connects to variance) during training; (2) use a controller (PID, Bayesian optimization, or a simple threshold rule) to adjust `p` to maintain variance within a target band — decreasing `p` when variance exceeds an upper threshold, increasing `p` when the model has capacity for more aggressive updates; (3) compare the adaptive controller against the fixed linear schedule on the same benchmarks and model scales used in the paper, measuring both final accuracy and hyperparameter sensitivity (how many controller parameters need tuning vs. the three schedule hyperparameters `p_high`, `p_low`, decay shape). The key question is whether adaptive scheduling can match or exceed the linear schedule's performance while eliminating per-task endpoint tuning — if so, HölderPO becomes a zero-hyperparameter drop-in replacement for GRPO's aggregation. If adaptive scheduling underperforms the hand-tuned linear schedule (e.g., because real-time variance estimates are too noisy to drive stable control), that negative result would clarify the limits of data-driven scheduling and motivate hybrid approaches (e.g., learn the schedule offline via meta-learning across tasks, then deploy fixed).

**Measuring the mechanism: direct gradient variance and concentration experiments.** The paper's theoretical framework (Theorems 1, 2, 7) predicts specific relationships between `p` and measurable quantities during training: gradient variance should increase monotonically with `p` (Theorem 2), weight distribution entropy should peak at `p = 0` and decrease symmetrically as `|p|` increases (Theorem 1), and the variance-minimizing `p*` should lie at some `p* ≤ 0` (Theorem 7). None of these predictions are tested against direct measurements — the paper reports only downstream accuracy and qualitative training dynamics (entropy, gradient norm in Figure 3). A strong mechanistic follow-up would: (1) instrument the training loop to log per-batch gradient variance (empirical variance of the mini-batch gradient estimator `‖Var(∇̂_θ 𝒥_Hö)‖`, not just gradient norm), the Shannon entropy of the weight distribution `W_i(p)` averaged across sequences, and the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index `∑_t W_{i,t}(p)^2`; (2) run static-`p` training at several values (`p ∈ {-2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3}`) and verify that these quantities follow the predicted monotonic relationships; (3) sweep `p` densely in the negative regime (`p ∈ [-5, 0]`) to empirically locate the variance minimum `p*` and test Theorem 7's claim that it exists and is `≤ 0`; (4) verify that the dynamic schedule's early phase shows high concentration (low entropy) and high variance, and its late phase shows low variance and (if `p_low < 0`) elevated entropy relative to `p = 0` — confirming the schedule actually produces the predicted phase transition. This experiment would transform the paper's theoretical contribution from a consistency argument (the results are compatible with the theory) to a verified mechanism (the theory correctly predicts measurable intermediate quantities). A negative result — e.g., gradient variance not monotonic in `p`, or no clear variance minimum in the negative regime — would indicate that the theoretical idealizations (bounded per-token gradient norms, orthogonality of token gradients) fail in practice and would motivate more refined variance models.

**Combining HölderPO with auxiliary token-weighting signals.** The paper identifies token reweighting via auxiliary signals (entropy, confidence, KL divergence) as orthogonal to HölderPO's aggregation mechanism and explicitly notes they "could in principle be combined" (Section 2). This combination is untested and represents a natural extension. A concrete experiment: take an existing auxiliary-weighting method — e.g., the entropy-based token weighting from Wang et al. (2025a), which upweights high-entropy (uncertain) tokens to focus learning on informative steps — and apply Hölder aggregation to the *auxiliary-weighted* importance ratios. That is, instead of computing `W_{i,t}(p) ∝ r_{i,t}^p`, compute `W_{i,t}(p) ∝ (s_{i,t} · r_{i,t})^p` where `s_{i,t}` is the auxiliary score (entropy, confidence, etc.). The hypothesis is that auxiliary signals identify *which* tokens are important (separating pivotal reasoning from boilerplate), while HölderPO's `p` controls *how aggressively* to concentrate on those identified tokens. The two mechanisms should compound: auxiliary weighting improves the signal-to-noise ratio of the input to Hölder aggregation, and dynamic `p` provides phase-appropriate concentration. Test this on AIME24 (where the `p = 3` advantage is largest — 46.7% vs. 40.0% for GRPO) and MATH500 (where `p = -1` is optimal) to determine whether the combination pushes performance beyond either method alone. If the combination shows diminishing returns (auxiliary weighting already captures most of what `p` tuning provides, or vice versa), that would clarify the overlap between the mechanisms. If it shows additive gains, it would establish a new Pareto frontier for token-level credit assignment.

**Stress-testing the positive-concentration regime under verifier degradation.** The paper acknowledges that `p > 0` amplifies vulnerability to reward hacking but provides no empirical characterization of this risk. A critical follow-up would systematically degrade the reward signal and measure how performance changes as a function of `p`. Experiment design: take a benchmark with ground-truth answers (MATH500 or AIME24) and introduce controlled noise into the reward — e.g., flip a fraction `η ∈ {0.0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20}` of reward labels (correct → incorrect and vice versa) to simulate verifier errors. Train GRPO (`p = 1`), HölderPO at `p = 3` (aggressive amplification), HölderPO at `p = -1` (variance-controlled), and the dynamic schedule `2 → -2` under each noise level, and measure final accuracy. The prediction from the paper's framework is that `p = 3` should degrade faster than `p = 1` or `p = -1` as noise increases, because it concentrates gradient mass on tokens from trajectories that the (noisy) verifier incorrectly labels as high-advantage. The dynamic schedule might partially mitigate this by decaying to negative `p` in late training, but only if the damage from early-phase noise amplification is not irreversible. This experiment would provide practitioners with a quantitative risk assessment: "if your verifier accuracy is approximately X%, the expected performance penalty for using `p = Y` is Z percentage points." It would also test whether negative `p` (which concentrates on low-ratio, hesitant tokens) provides implicit robustness to reward noise — if hesitant tokens are less correlated with verifier errors than confident tokens, `p < 0` could serve as a defense against reward hacking, which would be a novel finding.

**Characterizing signal density as a measurable, predictive task property.** The paper's central explanatory variable — "signal density" — is used qualitatively: AIME24 is described as sparse-signal, MATH500 as dense-signal, but no quantitative metric is proposed or validated. Future work could operationalize signal density by measuring, for a given base model and task, the distribution of per-token importance ratios in successful trajectories. Potential metrics: the Gini coefficient of `W_i(p)` at some reference `p`, the effective sample size `(∑_t W_{i,t}^2)^{-1}`, or the fraction of tokens whose importance ratio exceeds some threshold (e.g., `r_{i,t} > 1.2`). A strong follow-up would: (1) compute these metrics for a diverse set of reasoning benchmarks (AIME24, AMC, MATH500, Minerva, OlympiadBench, plus non-math tasks like HumanEval, ARC, GSM8K) using a fixed base model; (2) sweep static `p` on each benchmark and record the optimal `p*`; (3) test whether the proposed signal-density metric predicts `p*` — i.e., do tasks with more concentrated importance ratios (sparser signals) systematically favor larger `p`? If a simple metric reliably predicts optimal `p` across diverse benchmarks, it would (a) validate the signal-density hypothesis empirically, (b) provide a practical tool for selecting schedule endpoints without a full static-`p` sweep, and (c) enable a priori categorization of new tasks into sparse- vs. dense-signal regimes. If no simple metric predicts `p*`, it would suggest that signal density alone is insufficient and that other task properties (reward noise, base model calibration, sequence length distribution) interact with `p` in ways the theory does not capture.

**Generalization study across reasoning domains beyond mathematics.** The paper's empirical validation is confined to mathematical reasoning (plus one agentic benchmark, ALFWorld). The theoretical framework is domain-agnostic — it depends only on the structure of token-level importance ratios and group-relative advantages, not on math-specific properties. But several aspects of math benchmarks (clean verifiability, genuinely sparse pivotal reasoning steps, strong base model pre-training) may make them unusually amenable to HölderPO's mechanisms. A generalization study should evaluate HölderPO's dynamic schedule on at least three non-math reasoning domains: **code generation** (HumanEval, MBPP — where unit tests provide clean verifiability but reasoning steps are more evenly distributed across the generated program), **scientific reasoning** (GPQA, ScienceQA — where domain knowledge interacts with reasoning and reward signals may be noisier), and **logical reasoning** (FOLIO, ProofWriter — where reasoning is structured but not necessarily sparse in the same way as competition math). For each domain, run the full protocol: static-`p` sweep to characterize the optimal range, compare the dynamic schedule against GRPO, GMPO, and the best static `p`. The key question is whether the optimal `p` range shifts systematically across domains, and whether the linear `2 → -2` schedule generalizes or requires domain-specific endpoint tuning. If `[2, -2]` works well across all domains, HölderPO is effectively a universal drop-in improvement to GRPO. If domain-specific tuning is consistently required, the method remains valuable but its adoption cost is higher — which would strengthen the case for the adaptive scheduling follow-up. A negative result where HölderPO provides no benefit (or harms performance) on certain domains would be equally informative, establishing boundary conditions for when dynamic aggregation matters at all.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**RL post-training for open-source reasoning models.** The most direct application is using HölderPO as the RL algorithm when fine-tuning base models for improved reasoning. The paper provides a concrete recipe: take a math-pretrained base model (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen3-8B-Base), train on MATH Level 3–5 problems (8,523 examples) with 8 rollouts per prompt, and apply HölderPO's linearly decaying schedule `p: 2 → -2` (or `[1, -1]` for weaker base models) as a drop-in replacement for GRPO's aggregation. The expected benefit, based on Table 2, is a ~7% relative accuracy improvement across mathematical benchmarks compared to standard GRPO, with no additional computational cost. On the 1.5B scale (Table 2), HölderPO pushes AIME24 accuracy from 20.0% (best prior baseline, Oat-Zero) to 30.0% — a 10-point absolute gain that makes a 1.5B model competitive with some 7B baselines. For organizations training open-source reasoning models (an increasingly active space post-DeepSeek-R1), adopting HölderPO's dynamic schedule requires changing one line of the GRPO loss implementation (Algorithm 1, Appendix C) and tuning two hyperparameters (`p_high`, `p_low`) — far less engineering effort than implementing an alternative RL paradigm (e.g., PPO with a critic model) and with demonstrated SOTA results on the primary community benchmarks.

**Agentic decision-making with smaller models.** The ALFWorld results (Table 3) demonstrate that HölderPO transfers to embodied agentic environments where models must complete multi-step tasks from textual observations. The 93.8% success rate on ALFWorld with Qwen2.5-Instruct-1.5B — a 1.5B parameter model achieving 93.8% on a benchmark where the best prior method (GiGPO) achieved 86.7% — represents a substantial capability improvement for small-scale agentic models. The practical implication: organizations deploying LLM agents in resource-constrained settings (on-device, edge, or low-latency applications where a 1.5B model is the realistic ceiling) can use HölderPO's conservative schedule `[1, -1]` to significantly improve task completion rates without increasing model size or inference cost. The paper's finding that the optimal schedule is more conservative for models without domain-specific pre-training (`[1, -1]` vs. `[2, -2]`) provides actionable guidance: when the base model has weak priors for the target domain, start with moderate amplification to avoid overfitting to noisy early exploration. A practitioner training an agent for customer support, robotic control, or game playing could adopt this recipe directly, with the schedule endpoints being the only hyperparameters requiring calibration.

**Training data-efficient reasoning models for niche domains.** The paper's central trade-off — early amplification of sparse signals, late stabilization — is particularly relevant when training data is limited and each correctly solved training example carries disproportionate weight. In niche domains (specialized scientific reasoning, legal analysis, medical diagnosis), training datasets may contain only hundreds of examples rather than thousands, making efficient extraction of learning signals from each successful trajectory critical. HölderPO's positive-`p` regime is purpose-built for this scenario: it amplifies gradient updates from the rare tokens that distinguish correct from incorrect reasoning, preventing those signals from being averaged out across the many mundane tokens in long chain-of-thought outputs. The dynamic schedule's decay to negative `p` then prevents the model from overfitting to the limited positive examples by encouraging diversity and variance-controlled convergence. A practitioner fine-tuning a reasoning model on 500 domain-specific examples could use HölderPO with a more aggressive high endpoint (e.g., `p = 3` if the base model has some domain familiarity, or `p = 2` if starting from scratch) to maximize signal extraction, and a moderate low endpoint (`p = -1`) to avoid mode collapse on the small dataset. The paper provides no direct evidence for this data-efficiency claim — all experiments use 8,523 MATH training problems — but the mechanism (amplifying sparse signals when data is limited) follows directly from Theorem 3's characterization of the positive-`p` regime and is a natural extrapolation worth testing in deployment.

**Improving self-improvement and iterative RL pipelines.** A growing paradigm in LLM post-training is iterative self-improvement: train a model via RL, use the improved model to generate higher-quality training data, and repeat (as in STaR, ReSTᴱᴹ, and the iterative RL approaches behind DeepSeek-R1). HölderPO's dynamic schedule is particularly well-suited to this setting because early iterations — where the base model produces mostly incorrect trajectories with rare, noisy correct ones — benefit maximally from aggressive signal amplification (high `p`). Later iterations — where the model's success rate has improved and the goal shifts to refining and stabilizing competent reasoning — benefit from variance-controlled updates (low `p`). A practitioner implementing an iterative RL pipeline could schedule `p` not just within a single training run but *across iterations*: start iteration 1 with `p: 3 → -1`, iteration 2 with `p: 2 → -1`, and iteration 3 with `p: 1 → -2`, progressively reducing the amplification intensity as the model's base performance improves. This cross-iteration scheduling is not tested in the paper (all experiments use fixed training protocols) but is a natural extension of Theorem 3's logic: the appropriate `p` trajectory depends on the model's current capability, which improves across iterations. If effective, this could make iterative self-improvement pipelines more data-efficient by ensuring each iteration's RL phase uses the appropriate `p` for the model's current reasoning maturity.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper positions HölderPO explicitly as a replacement for the token-level aggregation step in GRPO and its variants (GMPO, GSPO, PMPO). The decision rule follows directly from the paper's theoretical framework and empirical results:

- **Prefer HölderPO with dynamic scheduling over static-aggregation GRPO variants when:** (1) your training task spans a range of signal densities, or you are unsure whether your task is sparse-signal or dense-signal — the dynamic schedule provides a robust default that outperforms any fixed `p` on the overall benchmark average (54.9% vs. best static 52.6% on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Table 2); (2) you are training from a base model with some domain pre-training (mathematical, code) where early high-`p` amplification of sparse reasoning signals provides clear benefits — use `[2, -2]` linear decay as the default; (3) you are training from a general-purpose base model without domain-specific pre-training (e.g., instruct model on agentic tasks) — use `[1, -1]` linear decay to avoid amplifying noisy early exploration (93.8% vs. 87.5% on ALFWorld, Table 3); (4) your primary metric is average performance across a diverse benchmark suite rather than peak performance on any single benchmark — the schedule's portfolio-level advantage is where it shines.

- **Prefer a specific static `p` over the dynamic schedule when:** (1) your task is strongly and consistently on one end of the signal-density spectrum and you can verify this — on AIME24 (extremely sparse signal), static `p = 3` achieves 46.7% vs. 43.3% for the dynamic schedule (Table 2); on MATH500 (dense signal), static `p = -1` achieves 85.0% vs. 82.2% for the dynamic schedule (Table 2); (2) your evaluation is single-benchmark and you can afford to sweep static `p` to find the optimum — for single-task deployment, task-specific `p` tuning yields higher peak performance than the schedule's compromise; (3) your training budget is very limited and you want the simplest possible implementation — static `p = 0` or `p = 1` (standard GRPO/GMPO) avoids the schedule hyperparameters entirely and still provides competitive performance (GRPO at 51.2% vs. dynamic at 54.9%, a 7.2% relative gap that may not justify the tuning complexity for all use cases).

- **Prefer standard GRPO (`p = 1`) or GMPO/GSPO (`p → 0`) when:** (1) you are in a production setting where stability and predictability matter more than peak performance, and you cannot afford to tune schedule endpoints for your specific task/model combination — the known failure modes of static aggregation (training collapse on dense-signal, signal dilution on sparse-signal) are well-characterized, while HölderPO's dynamic schedule introduces new hyperparameters whose sensitivity across diverse tasks is not fully mapped; (2) your RL pipeline uses token-level clipping (the standard in most GRPO implementations) — HölderPO's theoretical variance properties and the `p`-dependent differentiation that dynamic scheduling exploits require sequence-level clipping (Appendix D, Table 4), and switching clipping strategies may require revalidating your entire training pipeline; (3) you are combining GRPO with auxiliary token-weighting methods that already modify per-token importance scores — HölderPO could interact with these methods in untested ways, and the simpler static aggregation reduces confounding when debugging compound systems.
