Here are the takeaways from this Joakim Recht interview (grew from Senior to Distinguished Engineer at Uber, originally built the platform that managed all stateful workloads at Uber):

**Building Odin: the platform that managed all of Uber’s stateful workloads**

Not long after Joakim started at Uber in the Denmark office, he was tasked with building a new object store called Schemaless to replace the company’s single PostgreSQL instance before it ran out of headroom. His job was managing the MySQL shards: monitoring them, scaling them, operating them. In those early days it was all bare metal—Puppet and MySQL—and every master promotion or host replacement meant logging in, editing Puppet configs, and hoping for the best. It “got harder because the system scaled more.”

Containerization was still a new concept (Kubernetes “was just released in the first alpha version around that time”), but the team started asking: why not run databases in Docker to colocate multiple MySQL instances on a single host and improve utilization? At that point, running a database required at least nine physical servers just for redundancy. The bigger idea was to move from a procedural model—manually promote a master, manually replace a host—to a declarative one: define what you want as a goal state and have a system that takes you there. “You just say we want a database with nine individual clusters, three nodes each, and make that work.”

They built this first for Schemaless, and it included an entire ecosystem—monitoring, management, version control, and an orchestration layer. Joakim has “a somewhat aversion to GitHub” because it’s a one-way thing: push code and hope for the best, with no feedback on whether it’s working. Their orchestration layer solved that. Once it worked for Schemaless, the question became: why not do this for *all* the databases at Uber?

That became the platform called **Odin**. Previously, every database technology had its own team doing bare-metal host management, monitoring, provisioning, decommissioning, and database operations—each with their own toolchain. The vision was a single platform that could operate the entire fleet. It took a long time, partly because of organizational resistance (“from a local point of view, it doesn’t make any sense”) and partly because some technologies like HDFS and Kafka were “notoriously bad at moving stuff around dynamically”—host names encoded everywhere, pod numbers hardcoded, no good way to shuffle data. But they rolled it out incrementally: first their own team’s databases, then databases owned by friendly teams, then gradually wider. When Joakim left Uber, Odin was running all stateful workloads: around 120,000 physical servers and roughly half a million databases, all co-located and operated by a team of about 20 people. “The ability to operate a cluster with a single click, or rotate, decommission data centers, or commission new data centers” was the end state.

**The lazy engineer philosophy and force multiplication**

Joakim’s driving motivation is simply that he hates doing things too many times: “I have this personal thing, I just don’t like doing things too many times.” He calls it the **lazy engineer** framing. If he’s manually replaced a database host a couple times, the next step is to automate or abstract it away. Over time, that instinct expands from your own waste to other people’s waste—and that’s how scope grows. But he warns there’s a danger: a “depth-first approach to everything” where you rabbit-hole. A tool is annoying to build, so you improve the build system, but the build system is also annoying, so you build *that* tool, and so on. It’s how you end up “completely sidetracking what you should actually be doing.”

On scaling yourself: rather than just mentoring or doing design work, his preferred model is to become a force multiplier by **removing problems entirely** from other engineers’ plates. “Think about how do we actually take the problem that you have out of the equation? Because I think that’s the most fundamental thing you can do.” If they don’t have to do that thing anymore, what could they be doing instead?

**How promos actually work (and don’t)**

Joakim has a straightforward view of promotions: they’re tied to the scope of your work and the number of people you influence. His own trajectory tracked Odin’s natural expansion perfectly—from inside the team, to the local org, to the wider platform org, to the entire company—and the promotions “just followed the work.” He admits that riding a single expanding project is easier than being shuffled between canceled or failed ones, though possible in both cases.

But not all promos are fair. He describes the early Uber promo committees as “one of the scariest experiences of my life”—every manager, VP, senior director, and senior engineer in a room for an entire day, going through 200–500 engineers with no preparation and no materials. Managers just stood up and talked about their reports, and “obviously, they all thought everybody should be promoted.” Eventually someone suggested a point system. In that setup, “it gets super unfair because it all depends on how good your manager is at presenting your case.” Even with more structure later, inherent unfairness remains: you might have built something that got canceled by management through no fault of your own, or a dependency team ghosted you. Are you promoted or not? On what basis?

Some people feel it’s unfair “because they think they did really well compared to maybe the team,” but the team itself isn’t strong, or they’ve been operating on an island. When Uber switched from Python to Go, he saw groups working in isolation who “had been under their own influence”—their output didn’t compare to what was happening elsewhere, and getting that news in a promo committee room felt brutal. This is why he’s evangelized what it means to **“beat the promo committee”**—really a framework for what it means to be a software engineer at Uber specifically, since each company’s version of that role is different. Without that clarity, “people work towards something that was not the thing it should have been.”

**Write code. Every day. No exceptions.**

The core advice in that framework: “a software engineer needs to write code.” If your title includes “engineer,” you should be writing code regularly—ideally every day. He’s heard this is controversial but doesn’t understand why. It applies at every level, including Distinguished. The second piece: commit messages and code reviews matter. Real-life software engineering is a craft you don’t learn in college or alone; “you have to see somebody doing it in order to improve.” And the third principle: **running code beats perfect code any day**. Some engineers get paralyzed chasing edge cases, refactoring for a month without a single commit. That’s useless—no feedback, no peer eyes, potential to be completely off-direction. “Just do something.”

**Take the boring work; delegate the hard stuff**

The standard advice for senior engineers is to stop coding and delegate everything. Joakim “doesn’t subscribe to that idea at all.” If you stop writing code, you lose touch with the system. Designs become decoupled from reality—idealistic whiteboard drawings that actual engineers see and think, “who came up with this?” Over time, you lose trust and people won’t tell you openly, but they’ll think, “who’s that guy to come and say whatever?”

His counterintuitive model: if you’re senior, you don’t need to prove anything anymore, so **push the hard, interesting, growth-creating work down** to engineers who can barely manage it—then support them. Take the boring, unglamorous work for yourself. “We had this expression of shoveling shit.” The boring work often turns out to be non-trivial when you actually look at it—bug backlogs reveal systemic testing gaps, repetitive ops reveal tooling gaps. “It usually turns out to expand into something that is not trivial any longer.” This also earns trust: if you’re in the code doing the unpleasant parts, people will follow you. If you’re just issuing top-down mandates with no skin in the game, “how are you supposed to successfully implement the project?”

**The seed-planting model of influence**

Joakim says the best outcome is “when the other person thinks the idea is theirs.” It doesn’t happen often, but it’s the best feeling: overhearing someone explain an idea you planted months ago, with full conviction, as if it were their own. No force involved. You planted a seed, the environment nurtured it, and they got there. “I’ve seen it a couple of times. It’s just the best feeling—those guys, they learned, they’re on the right track now, and it was without force.”

He takes the same approach with mentoring. He prefers **mentees outside his org** rather than within it, for two reasons: it’s more efficient for building a broad network and planting influence-seeds across the company, and it’s a two-way learning street—you learn about parts of the codebase and problems you’d never encounter otherwise. His advice to people seeking a mentor: don’t lead with “can you mentor me?”—that feels like a big commitment. Instead, ask for help on something specific, or just start a conversation about a design. Show up, ask good questions. And once you’re mentoring, keep it informal; “I don’t want a plan. I don’t want a schedule.”

**Why he left Uber and why it was sad**

Joakim joined in 2014, which was Uber’s “very hot place to be”—people came from Google, Netflix, Amazon, Apple. Coming from small tech, he had imposter syndrome, then realized “all those people who came from other places were not that much better than me after all.” The Denmark office punched far above its weight: 70 people, out of 4,500 engineers company-wide, produced multiple distinguished engineers. The projects were challenging and satisfying—the kind you’d read about in blog posts and wish you had.

Then management changed, and engineering culture shifted. His local manager and site lead left. The first distinguished engineer in the office left. Others followed, in Denmark and the US. He describes a “full-on drain of especially senior engineers.” The inflection point for him was a top-down decision to move to cloud, justified on cost grounds: “no engineer believed that. It also turned out not to be true.” The only way it penciled out was by cutting platform costs nearly 50%, which they could have done on-prem even cheaper. For Joakim, it became an environment where he couldn’t stand behind the decisions being made. A peer manager told him, “why not just stick around and do your own thing and just take the money?” He couldn’t: “I don’t think I can witness what other people are going through and just do my own thing.” He calls it “a pretty sad thing—a self-inflicted thing that Uber did for no good reason.”

He joined a startup instead because he wanted a *real* change, not just doing the same infrastructure work again at Databricks (where many of his colleagues landed). “If you’re doing something, we might as well do something drastic.” The startup—an AI enterprise automation company—has been “a lot of fun, harder than I thought it would be.” He enjoys building the whole product rather than just infrastructure, though fundraising stress is real.

**Politics, control, and the illusion of control**

The more senior you get, the more you’re exposed to the reality that “nobody’s in control.” As a junior, you assume there’s a grand plan. “Then you realize at some point nobody’s in control. There’s an illusion of control, but there’s just people randomly getting ideas and pushing them out.” That’s scary for a rational engineer. His survival strategy: he’s never been a career planner; he just has “a very strong aversion to projects that I can just see as stupid or dead ends.” He stays away from them, says no a lot. As long as his own project has enough scope, he doesn’t need to engage with the noise. Physical distance from headquarters—the Denmark office’s nine-hour time difference—was a huge asset: days were uninterrupted heads-down work, with meetings only in the evening. “You only get a couple of hours every day to get sucked into it.”

On the scandals of the 2017 era: being far from the center was protective. They could keep executing while “every day you just think, what’s going to happen now?” He was baffled by the individualistic, “all about you” performance culture—negotiation-driven, team-ignoring. “I don’t get it.” The Denmark team never fully adopted that mindset, so the cultural reset in the wake of Travis Kalanick’s departure felt less jarring to them. Still, visiting US offices pre-2017 was a culture shock: “fist bump and then do you want a whiskey? It’s like Tuesday at 2:00 PM.”

**Engineering mistakes: the cost of full freedom**

He hesitates to call any single early decision a clear “mistake,” because many choices that didn’t scale were right at the time. The real problem was Uber’s inability to transition from “let builders build” to necessary structure. “Once you’re 10 years into it, that’s an insane strategy that’s going to kill you. But in the early days, you don’t want a lot of structure because you don’t know what you’re doing.” The Jaeger tracing mandate is the canonical example: repeated emails from 2015 through 2018 demanding all teams implement tracing, and it never got done. “That inability to affect global change just made so many projects so hard.”

If he has to name a concrete bad call from the 2016 era: someone built a software-defined networking stack—meant to route service-to-service communication through an ingress—in Node. “I was not that senior yet. So I thought, okay, but those people are pretty senior, they must know what they’re talking about. Turned out they did not know what they were talking about and that caused a lot of pain and many years of untangling.”

**Advice to his younger self**

“Just try stuff. Don’t be afraid just because it looks harder, because those other people look much better than you. It’s not rocket science. It is just computers. And if you like computers, you’re pretty well off if you just keep going with that.” Let curiosity take you wherever. Don’t assume you’re in the best spot—there’s probably something better out there, or soon will be. Stay open to it.
