## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Demonstration ITerated Task Optimization (DITTO)**, a method that aligns language models to individual user preferences using only a small number (< 10) of demonstrated examples rather than large pairwise preference datasets. Evaluated on author-specific writing benchmarks—news articles and blog posts from the CMCC and CCAT datasets—and a user study with 16 participants on email-writing tasks, DITTO treats user demonstrations as preferred over model-generated outputs, iteratively constructing pairwise comparisons between demonstrations and samples from the current model and its training checkpoints, then applying direct preference optimization. DITTO achieves an average 19 percentage-point win rate improvement over baselines including few-shot prompting, supervised fine-tuning, and self-play methods on Mistral 7B, with gains holding even against few-shot GPT-4 (an 18 percentage-point advantage), establishing that task-specific alignment via demonstrations is an order of magnitude more sample-efficient than collecting pairwise preferences, though the method is bounded to problems where the base model already possesses relevant capabilities within its pretraining distribution.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Are Aligned to the Collective, Not the Individual

The fundamental tension this paper addresses is one that anyone who has used a large language model for a specific, personal task has likely encountered: **the model produces outputs that feel generic, sanitized, and impersonally "one-size-fits-all."** This is not a bug in the traditional sense—it is a direct consequence of how modern LLMs are trained. Instruction tuning and RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) optimize models to satisfy the *average* preference across thousands of annotators (Ouyang et al., 2022). When you prompt an LLM to write an email to your advisor, what you get is a composite of how thousands of people think an email to an advisor *should* sound. It is polite, well-structured, and utterly devoid of your personal voice. As the paper puts it (Section 1):

> "LLM outputs feel unopinionated and generic because of this mismatch."

This is not merely an aesthetic complaint. The mismatch between general-purpose alignment and individual user needs has concrete negative consequences. A user who wants an email that reflects their casual, exclamation-point-heavy communication style must engage in tedious prompt engineering—continually refining instructions like "make it more casual," "no, more casual than that," "use emojis," "no, different emojis." This trial-and-error process, documented extensively by Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. (2023), places an unreasonable burden on end-users to translate their stylistic instincts into effective text instructions, a skill that even experienced users find brittle and frustrating. The paper argues that what users *really* want is a model that aligns to their specific, fine-grained preferences—not population-level averages.

### Why This Problem Matters: The Gap Between Personalization and Practicality

The paper's framing of this problem as one of **practical personalization** has two dimensions: a real-world impact dimension and a technical feasibility dimension.

**Real-world impact.** The domains where this matters are ubiquitous. Consider the paper's running example of email writing (Section 1): the same user writes differently to a friend, a colleague, a professor, or a customer. A user might want a model that adopts their characteristic sentence fragments when writing to close collaborators but switches to a more formal register when drafting a departmental announcement. Beyond email, the paper's benchmarks span blog posts, news editorials, recipes, and opinion pieces—all tasks where individual style is not just decorative but constitutive of the communication's effectiveness. A generic email to a friend that begins "I hope this message finds you well" signals distance rather than closeness. The stakes are not hypothetical: if LLMs are to serve as writing assistants, personal style alignment is a core usability requirement, not a nice-to-have.

**Technical feasibility.** The paper identifies a genuine bottleneck in existing alignment pipelines: **the prohibitive data cost of personalization.** Standard RLHF requires thousands to hundreds of thousands of pairwise comparisons to learn a reliable reward function (Ouyang et al., 2022; Stiennon et al., 2020). Even supervised fine-tuning, which is simpler, typically demands on the order of ~1,000 examples for effective style transfer or task adaptation (Zhou et al., 2024). For a single individual, providing even 100 labeled examples—let alone thousands—is an unreasonable ask. The paper frames this quantification directly (Section 2):

> "While existing approaches such as supervised or preference finetuning are effective, they can require a large corpus of (un)acceptable behavior (on the order of ≈1K samples), which in turn requires unreasonably high effort from an individual."

This is the crux of the practical problem: **the alignment methods that work at population scale break down at the individual scale** because the data collection cost per user is intractable. A user might be willing to provide a handful of examples—say, 4–7 demonstrations of how they would write specific emails—but not hundreds. The paper's core bet is that this handful of demonstrations contains sufficient information for effective alignment, if only we knew how to extract it.

### Where Existing Approaches Fall Short

The paper situates its contribution by systematically cataloging the limitations of prior approaches along three axes: prompting, static fine-tuning, and population-level preference learning.

**Prompting is tedious and brittle.** The most accessible method for an end-user to steer an LLM is through prompt engineering—either zero-shot instructions ("write a casual email") or few-shot demonstrations included directly in the context window. The paper acknowledges that prompting is data-efficient (no training required) but argues it is fundamentally insufficient for deep personalization (Section 2). The reason, which the paper demonstrates empirically (Table 1, Table 2), is that **the RLHF priors baked into instruction-tuned models are surprisingly strong.** Even when shown 7 examples of an author's distinctive style in a few-shot prompt, the model still produces outputs that "sound GPT-generated" relative to the author's actual writing. Qualitatively, the paper shows through a Fightin'-Words analysis (Monroe et al., 2008) that GPT-4 outputs contain statistically significant overuse of cliché phrases like "greatly appreciate your time and understanding" or "hope this message finds you well" (Table 6, Appendix) compared to DITTO-aligned outputs. Few-shot prompting can nudge the model toward a desired style but cannot *overwrite* the deep stylistic tendencies imprinted during RLHF. The paper also notes that iterative prompt refinement—asking users to engineer their own prompts through trial and error (the "self-prompt" baseline in the user study, Section 4.2)—actually underperforms simply providing demonstrations in a few-shot prompt (46.9% vs. 51.6% win rate, Table 2), reinforcing the difficulty users face in verbalizing stylistic preferences.

**Supervised fine-tuning overfits in low-data regimes.** SFT on a small set of demonstrations is the most direct approach: simply fine-tune the model to maximize the likelihood of the expert demonstrations. This works reasonably well—the paper reports 56.78% win rate on CMCC and 73.89% on CCAT for SFT (Table 1)—but the paper argues it leaves substantial performance on the table. The core limitation is that SFT only provides a **positive signal**: it teaches the model what correct outputs look like, but provides no **negative signal** about what incorrect outputs look like. In a data-rich setting, this is not problematic because the diversity of positive examples implicitly defines the boundaries of acceptable behavior. But with only 5–7 demonstrations, the model lacks the contrastive information needed to distinguish the target style from nearby-but-incorrect stylistic variants. The paper analogizes this to overfitting: SFT increases the probability of the demonstrated completions but does not decrease the probability of other off-style completions that the model might generate on novel prompts, leading to generations that are stylistically inconsistent or that "feel longwinded" (Table 7, Appendix). DITTO's core mechanism—generating negative examples from the model itself and training against them—directly addresses this gap.

**Population-level preference learning ignores the individual.** RLAIF methods like Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) automate pairwise preference collection by having an LLM judge outputs against general principles (e.g., "is this output helpful and harmless?"). While this eliminates the human annotation bottleneck, it aligns models to universal principles, not individual idiosyncrasies. A constitutional principle like "be polite and professional" would actively *misalign* a model for a user whose personal style is informal, irreverent, or sarcastic. The paper cites Group Preference Optimization (GPO, Zhao et al., 2023) as a step toward few-shot alignment, but notes that GPO requires pre-defined preference groups for meta-learning, which itself demands a large dataset for training the meta-learner—it does not solve the *personalization from scratch* problem that DITTO targets. Separately, the paper discusses work by Gao et al. (2024) that distills direct user edits into prompt-based principles, acknowledging this as a parallel direction but arguing that demonstrations carry richer information than the derived principles can capture.

**Self-play methods are designed for SFT-scale datasets, not few-shot personalization.** The paper compares directly against SPIN (Chen et al., 2024), a self-play fine-tuning method where the model generates its own training data by treating current-model outputs as inferior to previous-model outputs. SPIN is the closest prior work to DITTO algorithmically, and the paper explicitly grounds its differences relative to SPIN (Section 2). SPIN was designed to replace SFT at scale, using *thousands* of demonstrations. DITTO makes three key departures that the paper claims are critical for the few-shot regime: (1) DITTO does not update the reference policy $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ during training (SPIN does, which the paper shows leads to overfitting—win rates drop from 70.1% to 45.8% in the ablation, Table 3); (2) DITTO uses inter-model comparisons (comparing checkpoints from different training iterations to each other) in addition to expert-model comparisons, creating a richer, smoother preference ranking; (3) DITTO incorporates replay comparisons (expert vs. earlier checkpoints) to prevent catastrophic forgetting of negative regions. The paper's empirical results on CMCC (Table 1) show SPIN at 51.56% win rate vs. DITTO at 71.67%, confirming that the design decisions that work at scale do not transfer to the few-shot regime.

**Online imitation learning for LLMs is unexplored.** The paper identifies a gap at the intersection of two literatures: online imitation learning (OIL) from robotics and control, and preference-based fine-tuning for LLMs. In OIL, methods like T-REX (Brown et al., 2019) and D-REX (Brown et al., 2020a) use ranked demonstrations or automatically generated rankings to learn reward functions that can produce policies outperforming the demonstrator. The paper explicitly builds on this lineage (Section 3.3), particularly the insight from Brown et al. (2020a) that pairwise comparisons can be automatically generated by treating higher-ranked demonstrations as preferred over lower-ranked ones. However, OIL methods were developed for continuous control tasks with small policy networks—they assume explicit reward function learning and are not designed for the scale or autoregressive structure of LLMs. DITTO bridges this gap by combining the OIL insight (generate comparisons from demonstrations automatically) with the direct preference optimization framework (DPO, Rafailov et al., 2023), which avoids explicit reward modeling and operates directly on the language model's policy. The paper formalizes this connection in Section 3.3, deriving DITTO as an online imitation learning algorithm where the inner policy maximization (Eq. 1) and outer reward minimization are collapsed into a single DPO loss through the standard reparameterization trick.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contribution not as proposing a fundamentally new learning algorithm, but as repurposing and recombining existing algorithmic components—DPO, online sampling, demonstrations as preference anchors—into a configuration specifically designed for **few-shot personalization from demonstrations.** The authors articulate this positioning clearly (Section 1):

> "Rather than using prompts, principles, or pairwise preferences, we show that we can achieve strong alignment with individuals by leveraging a small number of user-provided examples of desired behavior."

Several conceptual moves distinguish this positioning:

**Demonstrations are repositioned as implicit preference signals.** Instead of treating demonstrations as targets for likelihood maximization (SFT), DITTO treats them as **always preferred** over any model-generated output. This reframing converts a small set of positive-only examples into an unbounded preference dataset: for each demonstration, the model can generate arbitrarily many negative samples (its own completions) that are definitionally worse, creating the contrastive signal that SFT lacks. The paper formalizes this as the ranking $D_E \succ D_t \succ D_{t-1} \succ ... \succ D_0$, where $D_E$ is the expert demonstration dataset and $D_i$ are samples from policy $\pi_i$ at iteration $i$ (Eq. 2, Section 3.2).

**The method is explicitly positioned for "local" alignment, not general-purpose improvement.** The paper is careful to scope its contribution: DITTO is designed for aligning a model to a specific user or task, not for improving the model's general capabilities. This is a key distinction from self-improvement methods like STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) or self-rewarding LMs (Yuan et al., 2024), which aim to bootstrap the model's overall reasoning or instruction-following abilities. DITTO presumes the base model already possesses the necessary knowledge and skills; the problem is one of stylistic and preference alignment, not capability acquisition. This scoping explains why DITTO works with < 10 demonstrations—it is not teaching the model to do something new, but rather refining *how* it does something it already knows how to do.

**The paper connects to HCI traditions of programming by demonstration.** In a notable interdisciplinary move, the paper invokes the programming-by-demonstration literature from human-computer interaction (Cypher, 1991; Cypher & Halbert, 1993) as inspiration for its feedback modality. The core idea—that users can specify complex behaviors by *showing* rather than *describing*—translates directly to language model alignment: a demonstration communicates stylistic preferences more richly and with less cognitive overhead than a natural language description of those preferences. This connection is more than decorative; it anchors the paper's argument that demonstrations as a feedback mode are fundamentally more efficient for individuals than pairwise comparisons or principles, a claim the paper substantiates empirically in Section 5.3 (Figure 3), showing that 4 demonstrations with DITTO match or exceed the performance achievable with 500+ pairwise preference annotations.

**The theoretical framing provides a guarantee that matters.** The paper derives DITTO as online imitation learning (Section 3.3, Appendix B) not just for intellectual coherence but because it enables Lemma 3.1, which states a formal condition under which DITTO can **extrapolate beyond the demonstrator's performance**—i.e., produce outputs that are *more* aligned to the user's implicit reward function than the demonstrations themselves. This is a strong claim that distinguishes DITTO from SFT, which can at best mimic the demonstrations. The condition involves two KL-divergence terms: extrapolation is guaranteed if $J_{KL}(\pi^*) - \mathbb{E}_{D_E}[r(x,y)] > \alpha D_{KL}(\hat{\pi}||\pi^*) - \alpha D_{KL}(\hat{\pi}||\pi_{\text{ref}})$. In plain language: if the optimal policy (which perfectly maximizes the user's reward subject to a KL constraint from the base model) is substantially better than the average reward of the demonstrations, *and* DITTO's learned policy is closer to the optimal policy than to the reference policy, then DITTO will outperform the demonstrator. This theoretical result, adapted from Brown et al. (2020a), provides a principled explanation for why generating negative samples and contrasting against them can yield behavior better than the examples provided.

## 3. Technical Approach

This is primarily a **methods paper** proposing a novel algorithm for few-shot personalization of language models, where the core idea is to convert a small set of user demonstrations into an unbounded preference dataset by treating those demonstrations as always preferred over model-generated outputs, then iteratively applying direct preference optimization.

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

DITTO is a training procedure that takes a handful of user-provided writing examples (e.g., 4–7 emails or blog posts written in the user's own style) and fine-tunes a language model to produce outputs that match that user's specific preferences, voice, and stylistic conventions. The problem it solves is the mismatch between population-level alignment (what RLHF produces—generic, "one-size-fits-all" outputs) and individual-level alignment (what a specific user actually wants). The "shape" of the solution is an iterative bootstrapping loop: the model generates its own negative examples by producing completions that are definitionally worse than the user's demonstrations, constructs a ranking over these comparisons, and uses that ranking to push the model's behavior toward the demonstrated style while pushing it away from its own generic outputs.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The DITTO system has four major components connected in a cyclical data flow:

1. **Expert Demonstration Dataset (`$D_E$`):** A small set of `$N$` prompt-completion pairs `$\{(x_i, y_i^E)\}_{i=1}^N$` provided by the user, where each `$y_i^E$` represents the user's desired output for prompt `$x_i$`. This is the only ground-truth signal—typically 4–7 examples, collected either from a user's existing interaction logs, direct edits to model outputs, or writing examples authored from scratch.

2. **Initial Policy (`$\pi_0$`):** The starting point for iterative training. DITTO first runs supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on `$D_E$` for a limited number of gradient steps—just enough to bias the model toward the demonstrations without fully overfitting. This SFT checkpoint becomes `$\pi_0$`, the first policy in the iterative loop.

3. **Comparison Data Generator:** At regular intervals during training (every `$K$` gradient steps), DITTO samples `$M$` completions from the current policy `$\pi_t$` for each prompt in `$D_E$`, producing a batch of model-generated outputs `$D_t = \{(x_i, y_j \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x_i))\}_{j=1}^M$` for each demonstration `$i$`. These samples are treated as inferior to the expert demonstrations and are fed into a ranking structure that also includes samples from previous training checkpoints.

4. **DPO Optimizer:** The pairwise comparisons extracted from the ranking are used to update the policy via the standard DPO loss, which increases the log-ratio of the preferred completion relative to the reference model while decreasing the log-ratio of the dispreferred completion. The reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is **frozen** at the initial SFT checkpoint throughout training—it is never updated.

**Information flow:** User provides demonstrations → SFT produces `$\pi_0$` → loop begins: `$\pi_t$` generates `$M$` completions per prompt → these are combined with prior checkpoints' samples into a ranked comparison dataset → a batch sampled from this ranking is used to compute the DPO loss → gradient update produces `$\pi_{t+1}$` → repeat until convergence. The cycle produces a progressively more diverse set of negative examples, as each new checkpoint generates slightly different (and ideally better) completions, which in turn provide tighter contrastive signal.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First: the RLHF objective and KL-constrained policy optimization** (Eq. 1), because this is the mathematical substrate on which DITTO operates—understanding what `$J_{KL}(\pi)$` maximizes and why the KL constraint matters is prerequisite to understanding why DITTO's design choices work.
- **Second: the core insight—generating comparisons from demonstrations** (the "naïve" version of DITTO), establishing how a few expert examples can be inflated into an unbounded preference dataset by sampling from the current policy.
- **Third: the temporal extension—from pairwise comparisons to ranked policies** (Eq. 2), explaining the replay and inter-model comparison mechanisms that distinguish DITTO from simpler approaches and prevent overfitting.
- **Fourth: the practical algorithm** (Algorithm 1 and its three components), translating the conceptual framework into a concrete training loop with specific hyperparameters, batch composition ratios, and sampling schedules.
- **Fifth: the theoretical derivation as online imitation learning** (Eq. 3 and the DPO reparameterization), placing DITTO in the broader context of inverse reinforcement learning and explaining Lemma 3.1's extrapolation guarantee.
- **Sixth: design choices, hyperparameters, and their justifications**, covering the frozen reference policy, the 70/20/10 batch composition split, the initial SFT warm-start, and the choice of DPO over alternative preference optimization algorithms.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

#### The KL-Constrained RLHF Objective

DITTO inherits the standard RLHF objective that has become canonical in language model alignment (Ziegler et al., 2019; Ouyang et al., 2022; Rafailov et al., 2023). A language model is viewed as a policy `$\pi(y|x)$`—a probability distribution over completions `$y$` given a prompt `$x$`. The goal is to train this policy to maximize a reward function `$r(x, y)$` that captures the user's preferences while not straying too far from a reference policy `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` (typically the base instruction-tuned model before personalization).

The objective is:

$$J_{KL}(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x), x \sim p}\left[ r(x, y) - \alpha \log \frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} \right]$$

where `$r(x, y)$` is the (unknown) reward function encoding the user's preferences, `$\alpha > 0$` is a hyperparameter controlling the strength of the KL penalty, `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is the reference policy (the initial model before personalization), and `$p$` is the distribution over prompts. The expectation is taken over prompts sampled from `$p$` and completions sampled from the current policy `$\pi$`.

**What it computes:** This expression calculates an expected "net benefit" for policy `$\pi$`. For each prompt-completion pair, it adds the reward `$r(x, y)$` (how much the user likes this output) and subtracts a penalty term `$\alpha \log(\pi(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x))$` that grows when `$\pi$` assigns much higher probability to completion `$y$` than `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` did. The penalty discourages the policy from drifting into regions where the reference model assigned negligible probability, effectively constraining the personalized model to remain in the "vicinity" of the base model's distribution. The result is a scalar objective value; maximizing it over `$\pi$` yields a policy that balances reward-seeking against staying close to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`.

**Why this form:** The KL penalty serves two critical functions. First, it prevents **reward hacking**—without it, an optimizer would find pathological completions that fool a learned reward model into assigning high scores (e.g., repetitive text that exploits a quirk of the reward function) but are not actually high-quality. By penalizing divergence from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`, which was trained on a vast corpus to produce coherent text, the objective ensures outputs remain linguistically well-formed. Second, it provides **coverage**: the KL term ensures `$\pi$` assigns non-zero probability wherever `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` did, preventing catastrophic forgetting of general language capabilities. The parameter `$\alpha$` is deliberately chosen to be small in DITTO's experiments (the paper states `$\beta = 0.05$` for the equivalent DPO parameter, where `$\beta$` is the inverse of `$\alpha$` in the DPO formulation), reflecting a setting where the user's personal preferences can override the reference distribution relatively easily—appropriate for a personalization task where the base model's "average" style is precisely what needs changing.

Critically, in standard RLHF, `$r(x, y)$` is a **learned** function trained on thousands of pairwise human preferences, representing the collective preferences of a population. DITTO reinterprets `$r(x, y)$` as the **individual reward function of a single user**—the latent function that determines whether that specific user would prefer one completion over another. This reframing is what makes the data problem different: you cannot collect thousands of preferences from one person, so you cannot learn this reward function through standard reward modeling.

A property of this objective that becomes important later (in the theoretical derivation, Section 3.3) is that it has a **closed-form solution**:

$$\pi^*(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) e^{r(x,y)/\alpha}$$

where `$Z(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)}[e^{r(x,y)/\alpha}]$` is the partition function that normalizes the distribution. This solution tells us that the optimal policy is simply the reference policy reweighted by exponentiated reward—high-reward completions get their probability increased; low-reward completions get it decreased. The existence of this closed form enables the DPO reparameterization that DITTO relies on.

#### The Core Insight: Generating Comparisons from Demonstrations

The central technical idea of DITTO is deceptively simple: **user demonstrations can serve as implicit preference signals by always being treated as preferred over model-generated outputs.** This insight converts the positive-only demonstration dataset into an unbounded comparative dataset.

**Why SFT is insufficient.** Standard supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations optimizes:

$$\mathcal{L}_{SFT} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^E) \sim D_E}\left[ \log \pi(y^E|x) \right]$$

This loss only increases the probability of the expert completions `$y^E$`. It provides no signal about what the model should *avoid* generating. With thousands of demonstrations spanning diverse prompts, the positive signal alone implicitly defines the target distribution—if the model sees enough examples of "casual email style," its probability mass concentrates on casual-style text. But with only 4–7 demonstrations, the positive signal is too sparse: the model learns to produce high-probability expert outputs on the training prompts but has no constraint on its behavior for unseen prompts, leading to inconsistent style transfer and outputs that drift back toward the model's generic RLHF priors.

**The comparison generation trick.** DITTO exploits a simple observation: if `$\pi_E$` is the user's expert policy (the distribution that produced the demonstrations), then by definition, `$\pi_E$` maximizes the user's expected reward. Therefore, for any other policy `$\pi$`:

$$\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_E(\cdot|x)}[r(x, y)] \geq \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[r(x, y)]$$

In expectation, expert completions have higher reward than model-generated completions. This justifies constructing pairwise comparisons `$(x, y^E, y^\pi)$` where `$y^E \succ y^\pi$`—the expert completion is preferred to the model completion. For each demonstration `$(x_i, y_i^E)$` in `$D_E$`, DITTO samples `$M$` completions from the current policy `$\pi_t(\cdot|x_i)$` and automatically labels all of them as dispreferred relative to the expert output. The number of comparisons is `$N \times M$`, and since `$M$` can be set arbitrarily (the paper uses `$M = 10$` in most experiments), this produces an effectively unbounded preference dataset from a fixed handful of demonstrations.

**The naïve approach.** The simplest version of DITTO would stop here: at each training step, generate comparisons `$D_E \succ D_t$` (expert vs. current policy), and apply standard DPO:

$$\mathcal{L}_{DPO}(\pi) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^E, y^\pi) \sim D}\left[ \log \sigma\left( \alpha \log \frac{\pi(y^E|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^E|x)} - \alpha \log \frac{\pi(y^\pi|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^\pi|x)} \right) \right]$$

where `$\sigma$` is the logistic (sigmoid) function. This loss increases the log-ratio of the expert completion (making it more probable under `$\pi$` relative to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`) while decreasing the log-ratio of the model completion (making it less probable). The contrastive signal directly addresses SFT's limitation: the model is explicitly told *not* to produce outputs like the ones it currently generates, pushing it away from its generic style and toward the demonstrated style.

However, the paper identifies a problem with this naïve formulation: **it decreases the likelihood of the model only at the specific policy `$\pi_t$` used to generate the negative samples.** Since the model is being updated, `$\pi_t$` represents only one point in policy space. The contrastive signal pushes probability mass away from that point but does not guarantee the mass moves where we want it to—it could simply redistribute to other regions that are equally undesirable, or oscillate as the policy shifts. This is the overfitting problem that plagues SFT in low-data regimes, just dressed in contrastive clothing.

#### The Temporal Extension: From Pairwise Comparisons to Ranked Policies

DITTO's key algorithmic innovation is extending the comparison structure from a single expert-vs-policy pair to a **ranking over multiple policies**, incorporating both past checkpoints and intermediate comparisons between non-expert policies.

**Replay comparisons.** The first extension is straightforward. Since `$\pi_E$` is the reward-maximizing policy, we have `$\mathbb{E}_{\pi_E}[r] \geq \mathbb{E}_{\pi_t}[r]$` for all `$t$`, but also `$\mathbb{E}_{\pi_E}[r] \geq \mathbb{E}_{\pi_i}[r]$` for all earlier checkpoints `$i < t$`. This means we can construct comparisons not just with the current policy but with **all previously generated policies**:

$$D_E \succ D_t, \quad D_E \succ D_{t-1}, \quad D_E \succ D_{t-2}, \quad \ldots, \quad D_E \succ D_0$$

These are termed "replay" comparisons, analogous to experience replay in deep reinforcement learning (Mnih et al., 2015). Their function is to maintain a diverse set of negative examples: earlier checkpoints produced completions with different characteristics than the current policy, so training against them prevents the model from only learning to avoid the most recent type of undesired output. The paper allocates 20% of each training batch to these replay comparisons.

**Inter-model comparisons.** The second extension is more subtle and powerful. If we make the assumption that the policy improves monotonically—that is, `$\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{t+1}}[r(x, y)] \geq \mathbb{E}_{\pi_t}[r(x, y)]$`—then we can construct a **total ordering** over all policies encountered during training:

$$D_E \succ D_t \succ D_{t-1} \succ D_{t-2} \succ \ldots \succ D_1 \succ D_0$$

The paper explicitly acknowledges that this monotonic improvement assumption is not theoretically guaranteed. However, in practice, they found that models tended to improve with each iteration, "perhaps owing to the convexity of both reward modeling and Eq. (1)." This means that comparisons between, say, `$\pi_3$` and `$\pi_1$` are empirically valid most of the time—samples from `$\pi_3$` tend to be better aligned to the user's style than samples from `$\pi_1$`, so treating `$\pi_3$` outputs as preferred over `$\pi_1$` outputs provides useful training signal.

The inter-model comparisons (allocated 10% of each batch) serve a distinct and critical function: **smoothing the implicit reward landscape.** When the model is trained only against the expert (70% of the batch), the contrastive signal is binary and sharp—expert good, everything else bad. This can cause the model to collapse probability mass onto the exact expert demonstrations (overfitting) rather than learning the underlying stylistic manifold. The inter-model comparisons add a gradient: samples from later checkpoints (which are closer to the target style) are preferred over samples from earlier checkpoints (which are further away). This teaches the model *which directions of variation are improvements*, creating a smoother optimization landscape where small deviations from the demonstrations that still trend toward the user's style are rewarded rather than penalized.

**Effect of the combined ranking.** The likelihoods of earlier samples (e.g., those in `$D_1$` from the first iteration) are pushed down more aggressively than those of later samples (e.g., those in `$D_t$` from the current iteration). This is because earlier samples appear as the dispreferred element in more comparisons: a sample from `$D_1$` is dispreferred relative to the expert, relative to `$D_t$`, relative to `$D_{t-1}$`, and so on up to `$D_2$`—it occupies the bottom of the ranking and receives downward pressure from all directions. A sample from `$D_t$` is only dispreferred relative to the expert, and may even appear as the preferred element in inter-model comparisons with earlier checkpoints.

#### The Practical Algorithm

DITTO is implemented as a concrete iterative procedure with three phases (Algorithm 1 in the paper):

**Phase 1: SFT Warm-Start.** The base instruction-tuned model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` is fine-tuned on the expert demonstrations `$D_E$` using standard supervised fine-tuning. The paper specifies that this is done "for a limited number of gradient steps" and that training is stopped when the binary cross-entropy loss on a batch approaches 1.00—early stopping to prevent full overfitting. The resulting model is designated `$\pi_0$`. This warm-start is important: without it, the initial policy would generate outputs that are so far from the demonstrated style that the contrastive signal between expert and model would be almost trivially learnable but not particularly informative (the model would learn "don't be as generic as the base model" rather than "be more like this specific style"). The SFT step brings the model into the right ballpark so that subsequent contrastive training can refine the details.

**Phase 2: Iterative Sampling and Policy Update.** The main loop proceeds as follows:

1. A new dataset `$D_t$` is constructed by sampling `$M$` completions from the current policy `$\pi_t$` for each of the `$N$` demonstrations in `$D_E$`. The paper's primary experiments use `$M = 10$` negative samples per demonstration, with sampling temperature set to `$1.0$`. This sampling step is performed every `$K = 10$` gradient steps (the "resample step-rate"), meaning the model generates fresh negatives periodically rather than at every single step—a practical compromise between the ideal of fully online training and computational efficiency.

2. The sampled dataset `$D_t$` is added to the cumulative collection of policy-generated datasets `$\{D_0, D_1, \ldots, D_t\}$` ranked as in Eq. 2.

3. A batch `$B$` of pairwise comparisons is sampled from this ranking. The batch composition is: **70% online comparisons** (`$D_E \succ D_t$`, the current policy vs. expert), **20% replay comparisons** (`$D_E \succ D_{i<t}$`, expert vs. earlier checkpoints), and **10% inter-model comparisons** (`$D_{i \leq t} \succ D_{j<i}$`, comparing checkpoints against each other). These ratios were tuned via hyperparameter sweep on the validation set.

4. The policy is updated using the DPO loss on batch `$B$`. Crucially, the reference model `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` in the DPO loss is **always the initial SFT checkpoint**, never updated during training. This is one of the key departures from SPIN (Chen et al., 2024), which updates `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` to be the previous iteration's policy. The paper ablates this choice explicitly (Table 3): updating `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` causes win rates to drop from 70.1% to 45.8%, which they attribute to overfitting—as `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` tracks the policy, the KL constraint becomes vacuous, and the model can drift arbitrarily far from the initial instruction-tuned behavior, losing general capabilities.

**Phase 3: Convergence Check.** The loop continues until a convergence criterion is met (the paper does not specify a formal criterion but notes that added iterations beyond 6 eventually result in performance degradation due to overfitting). The final policy `$\pi_T$` is the DITTO-aligned model.

**Key hyperparameters.** The paper's main experiments (Table 5 in Appendix D) fix the following configuration, determined by random hyperparameter sweep on a single randomly selected author from each benchmark corpus (CMCC and CCAT) and validated on held-out data:

- **LoRA rank:** 16, alpha: 32 (Hu et al., 2021). All training uses Low-Rank Adaptation rather than full fine-tuning; the authors state that "full finetuning for a handful of authors...observed no significant difference."
- **SFT phase:** learning rate `$3 \times 10^{-5}$`, batch size 4, cosine scheduler with warmup ratio 0.1, AdamW optimizer, early stopping when BCE train loss approaches 1.00.
- **DPO phase:** learning rate `$1 \times 10^{-6}$`, effective batch size approximately 24 (constructed from the comparison sampling), `$\beta = 0.05$` (the DPO parameter equivalent to `$1/\alpha$` in the KL objective), 40 gradient steps per resample interval, constant learning rate with warmup ratio 0.25.
- **DITTO-specific:** `$M = 10$` negatives per demonstration, `$K = 10$` steps between resampling, sampling temperature 1.0, batch composition 70/20/10 (online/replay/inter-model), typically 4 DITTO iterations.

**Why DPO and not another preference optimization algorithm?** The paper states that DITTO can support any direct preference optimization method (KTO, SimPO, etc.) and that "the exact choice of the preference optimization algorithm had limited downstream effect," so DPO was used as the default. This is an important design note: DITTO's value comes from the comparison generation and ranking structure, not from the specific loss function used in the final step.

#### Deriving DITTO as Online Imitation Learning

The paper provides a theoretical derivation that places DITTO in the online imitation learning framework, which is useful for understanding *why* it should work and what guarantees it provides.

**The two-player game formulation.** Online imitation learning can be framed as a min-max game (Ziebart et al., 2008; Ho & Ermon, 2016; Sikchi et al., 2022) between a **policy player** and a **reward player**:

$$\min_r \left\{ -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim D^\pi}\left[ \log \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)) \right] \right\} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \pi = \arg\max_\pi J_{KL}(\pi, r)$$

where `$r$` is the reward function being learned, `$D^\pi$` is an online dataset of comparisons generated by the policy, and `$J_{KL}$` is the KL-constrained RL objective from Eq. 1. The reward player `$\min_r$` minimizes the Bradley-Terry loss—it tries to assign higher rewards to preferred completions and lower rewards to dispreferred ones. The policy player `$\max_\pi$` maximizes the expected reward minus KL penalty—it tries to produce completions that the current reward function rates highly. The constraint links the two: the policy must be optimal for the current reward function before the reward function is updated.

**The DPO reparameterization.** The key technical move is eliminating the inner policy optimization using the closed-form solution for `$J_{KL}$`. From the optimal policy expression `$\pi^*(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)e^{r(x,y)/\alpha}$`, we can algebraically rearrange to express the reward in terms of the policy:

$$r(x, y) = \alpha \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \alpha \log Z(x)$$

where `$Z(x)$` is the partition function. Substituting this expression for `$r$` into the Bradley-Terry reward modeling loss yields the DITTO objective:

$$\min_\pi -\mathbb{E}_{D^\pi}\left[ \log \sigma\left( \alpha \log \frac{\pi(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \alpha \log \frac{\pi(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]$$

The partition functions `$\log Z(x)$` cancel out in the difference `$r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)$` because they depend only on the prompt `$x$` and not the specific completion. This is the same cancellation that makes DPO work without an explicit reward model.

**What the derivation establishes.** This formulation shows that DITTO is not an ad hoc combination of heuristics but an instance of a principled framework. The distinction from standard DPO is in the dataset `$D^\pi$`: standard DPO uses a fixed, offline dataset of human-annotated preferences, while DITTO's dataset is **online**—it is generated by the evolving policy itself and continuously updated. This online property is what allows DITTO to function with only a handful of expert demonstrations, because the comparisons are generated automatically rather than requiring human annotation.

**Lemma 3.1: The extrapolation guarantee.** The paper proves a condition under which DITTO can outperform the demonstrator—i.e., produce outputs that the user would prefer *more* than the demonstrations themselves:

> Let `$\pi^*$` be the optimal policy for Eq. (1) and `$\hat{\pi}$` be the policy estimated by DITTO using expert demonstrations `$D_E$`. Extrapolation beyond the demonstrator, i.e. `$\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\pi}}[r(x, y)] > \mathbb{E}_{D_E}[r(x, y)]$`, is guaranteed if `$J_{KL}(\pi^*) - \mathbb{E}_{D_E}[r(x, y)] > \alpha D_{KL}(\hat{\pi} || \pi^*) - \alpha D_{KL}(\hat{\pi} || \pi_{\text{ref}})$`.

Breaking this down into operational terms: the left-hand side `$J_{KL}(\pi^*) - \mathbb{E}_{D_E}[r(x, y)]$` is the **headroom**—how much better the optimal KL-constrained policy could be compared to the average reward of the demonstrations. If the demonstrations are noisy or suboptimal (e.g., the user was rushed when writing them), this headroom is large. The right-hand side is the **net KL gap**: how much closer `$\hat{\pi}$` is to `$\pi^*$` than to `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`, weighted by `$\alpha$`. If DITTO's learned policy is closer to optimal than to the base model (which it should be, since it trained toward the expert), and the headroom exceeds this gap, then the learned policy will outperform the demonstrations.

**Why this matters practically.** This lemma provides theoretical backing for the empirical finding that DITTO often produces outputs that are *better stylized* than the demonstrations—the model can clean up inconsistencies, amplify stylistic features that the user only partially expressed, and produce novel outputs in the user's voice that the user would judge as better than their own training examples. SFT provides no such guarantee; it can at best mimic the demonstrations, including their imperfections. The condition also explains when DITTO might fail: if the demonstrations are already near-optimal (small headroom), or if `$\alpha$` is too large (heavy KL penalty prevents the policy from moving away from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` enough to reach `$\pi^*$`), then DITTO may not extrapolate.

#### Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Frozen reference policy.** Unlike SPIN, which sets `$\pi_{\text{ref}} \leftarrow \pi_t$` at each iteration, DITTO keeps `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` fixed at the initial SFT checkpoint. The justification is empirical (Table 3: 70.1% → 45.8% win rate when updating `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`) and principled: a moving reference policy means the KL constraint evaluates divergence from the *previous* policy rather than from the base instruction-tuned model. In the data-scarce few-shot setting, this allows the policy to accumulate drift over iterations—each step's KL penalty is small, but the total divergence from the base model becomes unbounded. This leads to overfitting: the model loses general language capabilities and collapses onto the narrow style of the demonstrations. A frozen `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$` anchors the KL constraint to a high-entropy, broadly capable distribution, preventing this collapse.

**70/20/10 batch composition split.** The batch sampling ratios were tuned via hyperparameter sweep: 70% online (`$D_E \succ D_t$`), 20% replay (`$D_E \succ D_{i<t}$`), 10% inter-model (`$D_{i \leq t} \succ D_{j<i}$`). The dominance of online comparisons (70%) ensures that the primary training signal comes from the most relevant contrast: the current policy's outputs vs. the expert. The replay component (20%) prevents forgetting of negative regions: earlier checkpoints may have produced different types of errors that the current policy has already overcome, but training against those errors ensures those regions of output space remain low-probability. The inter-model component (10%) provides the smoothing effect described earlier. The paper tested alternative splits (0.25/0.5/0.25 and 0.1/0.7/0.2, mentioned in Appendix D) and found this configuration optimal on the validation set.

**Initial SFT warm-start.** Training DPO from the raw instruction-tuned model without the SFT phase would mean the contrastive signal compares the expert against extremely different model outputs (generic, formal RLHF-style text). While this provides a strong gradient, it is also a coarse one—the model learns to move away from "generic formal" but may not land precisely on the user's style. The SFT warm-start brings the model into the neighborhood of the target style first, so that DPO's contrastive signal can perform fine-grained refinement rather than coarse relocation. The early stopping criterion (BCE loss approaching 1.00) prevents the SFT phase from fully memorizing the demonstrations, leaving room for the DPO phase to generalize beyond them.

**Sampling frequency `$K = 10$` and number of negatives `$M = 10$`.** Resampling every 10 gradient steps (rather than every step) is a practical compromise. Generating `$M = 10$` completions for each of `$N$` demonstrations at every gradient step would be computationally expensive, and the policy does not change dramatically between single steps—resampling too frequently produces largely redundant negatives. Resampling too infrequently would mean the model trains extensively against stale negatives that no longer represent its current behavior, weakening the online learning property. The paper's ablation in Figure 2 shows that increasing `$M$` from 2 to 10 improves win rates by approximately 21% relative (monotonically increasing) and reduces variance across users, but notes that further increases yield plateauing performance and increased runtime.

**Number of DITTO iterations.** The paper typically uses 4 iterations (Figure 2, left), finding that performance improves from iteration 1 to 4 (31.5% relative increase), though the improvement is non-monotonic—iteration 2 shows a slight dip (-3.4%). Beyond 6 iterations, performance degrades, attributed to overfitting on the small demonstration set. The non-monotonicity at iteration 2 is hypothesized to result from noisier samples in early iterations: `$\pi_1$` may still be partially generic in some dimensions and overly fitted to the demonstrations in others, producing negatives that are not cleanly "worse" than `$\pi_0$` outputs in all respects, which injects noise into the inter-model comparisons.

**LoRA rather than full fine-tuning.** The paper reports that full fine-tuning was attempted on a handful of authors and "observed no significant difference" from LoRA. Using LoRA (rank 16, alpha 32) provides practical benefits: smaller checkpoints, faster training, and the ability to route between the base model and the personalized adapter by simply dropping the LoRA weights. This last property is exploited in Appendix H.1 to mitigate forgetting: on coding tasks (HumanEval), the DITTO-ed model's pass@1 drops from 0.31 to 0.13, but selectively applying the LoRA adapter only to style-relevant queries (as determined by a prompted router) recovers the original 0.31 performance.

**Choice of `$\beta = 0.05$` in DPO.** The DPO parameter `$\beta$` (equivalent to `$1/\alpha$` in Eq. 1) controls the strength of the KL penalty. A small `$\beta$` means a weak KL penalty, allowing the policy to deviate more from `$\pi_{\text{ref}}$`. The paper's value of 0.05 is relatively small, consistent with the personalization setting where the desired behavior may differ substantially from the base model's generic style. Too large a `$\beta$` would prevent DITTO from moving away from the instruction-tuned priors, defeating the purpose of personalization; too small a `$\beta$` (effectively removing the KL constraint) would risk overfitting and language degradation. The value was tuned via hyperparameter sweep alongside other parameters.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Demonstrations as Implicit, Auto-Generating Preference Signals

The most fundamental conceptual move in this paper is the reframing of what a user demonstration *is* from an optimization perspective. Before DITTO, the dominant paradigm—inherited from supervised learning and standard fine-tuning—treated demonstrations as **target outputs**: the model should learn to produce outputs that match these examples. This framing leads naturally to maximum-likelihood training (SFT), which provides only a positive signal and struggles when data is scarce. The paper instead treats demonstrations as **implicit preference axioms**: each demonstration is definitionally preferred over any output the model could generate, which converts a handful of positive-only examples into an unbounded, self-generating contrastive dataset.

This is not merely a clever data augmentation trick. It is a **fundamental reframing of the information content of a demonstration.** In the SFT framing, a demonstration says "produce text like this." In DITTO's framing, a demonstration says "produce text like this *rather than like that*," where "that" is dynamically defined by the model's own current behavior. The contrastive signal provides information about the *boundary* of the user's preferences—where acceptable style ends and generic style begins—that a purely positive signal cannot convey with few examples. This reframing is what enables DITTO to function with fewer than 10 demonstrations where SFT requires orders of magnitude more data (Zhou et al., 2024).

The distinction from prior contrastive approaches is equally important. Methods like RLHF and DPO use pairwise preferences that require human annotators to explicitly compare two outputs and select the better one. Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) automates this by having an LLM judge outputs against general principles. Both approaches treat the preference signal as something that must be **externally provided**—either by humans or by a principled critic. DITTO's insight is that for the specific case of individual-level alignment, the preference signal is **already latent in the demonstrations** and only needs to be mechanically extracted by sampling from the model. The user does not need to label which model outputs are bad; by the very fact that they are model outputs and not user demonstrations, they are definitionally worse under the user's implicit reward function.

This reframing has implications beyond the specific DITTO algorithm. It suggests a broader design principle for human-AI interaction: **demonstrations should be treated as preference-carrying rather than output-targeting.** When a user edits an AI-generated draft, the edit is not just a corrected output—it is a statement that the edit is preferred over the original. DITTO operationalizes this principle algorithmically, but the conceptual move (demonstrations as preference evidence rather than supervised targets) is independent of the specific optimization procedure.

Evidence for the significance of this reframing comes from the paper's sample efficiency analysis (Section 5.3, Figure 3): 4 demonstrations with DITTO match or exceed the performance achievable with over 500 explicitly collected pairwise preferences, and pairwise preferences collected by sampling from the base model's distribution alone (without using demonstrations to guide sampling) yield essentially no improvement regardless of quantity. This result demonstrates that the information density of demonstrations-as-preferences far exceeds that of explicit pairwise labels, validating the reframing empirically.

### Innovation 2: Temporal Policy Ranking as a Smoothing Mechanism for Few-Shot Contrastive Learning

The paper's second conceptual contribution is the insight that **contrastive learning with only a handful of positive examples creates a brittle, discontinuous reward landscape, and that ranking policies temporally—treating later checkpoints as preferred over earlier ones—acts as a regularizer that smooths this landscape without requiring additional human data.**

Standard contrastive fine-tuning against a small set of positive examples creates a binary optimization landscape: the expert outputs occupy a small region of high probability, and *everything else* is pushed to low probability. In a data-rich setting, this binary signal is sufficient because the positive examples are diverse enough to define the target manifold. But with fewer than 10 demonstrations, the expert region is a set of isolated points, not a connected manifold. Training with only expert-vs-model comparisons would cause the model to collapse probability mass onto the exact expert completions—overfitting to the specific prompts and wording of the demonstrations rather than learning the underlying style. This is the well-known failure mode of SFT in low-data regimes, and DITTO's naïve expert-only contrastive version would suffer similarly.

The inter-model and replay comparisons solve this by introducing a **gradient of preference quality** rather than a binary good/bad distinction. Rather than learning "expert good, everything else equally bad," the model learns "expert > late-checkpoint outputs > mid-checkpoint outputs > early-checkpoint outputs." This teaches the model the *direction* of improvement—which stylistic changes constitute progress toward the target and which constitute regressions—even for regions of output space that are far from the expert demonstrations. The paper explicitly states this (Section 3.2): "The effect of adding these intermodel and replay comparisons is that the likelihoods of earlier samples are pushed down more than those of later samples, smoothing the implicit reward landscape."

This is a fundamentally different approach to regularization than standard techniques like weight decay or early stopping. Those techniques constrain the *parameters* of the model; DITTO's temporal ranking constrains the *learned reward function* by imposing monotonicity: the reward function must respect the temporal ordering `π_t > π_{t-1} > ... > π_0`. This is a form of structured prior that encodes the assumption that training progress is (on average) monotonic, and it costs no additional human data—the comparisons are generated automatically from the sequence of checkpoints that training already produces.

The ablation results in Table 3 quantify the importance of this innovation. Removing inter-model comparisons (keeping only expert-vs-model) reduces win rates from 70.1% to 68.1%—a modest but consistent 2-percentage-point drop. Removing replay comparisons (keeping only current-model vs. expert, without comparisons involving earlier checkpoints) causes a larger 6.5-percentage-point drop to 63.6%. Removing both—which reduces DITTO to the naïve approach of sampling all negatives at the start and training only expert-vs-model—causes win rates to plummet from 70.1% to 57.3%. Each component of the temporal ranking contributes additively to performance, confirming that the smoothing effect is real and practically significant.

This innovation is incremental in mechanism (it extends an existing idea—experience replay from deep RL, Mnih et al., 2015—to the preference-based fine-tuning setting) but **fundamental in its diagnosis of the problem.** The paper identifies *why* contrastive learning fails in few-shot settings (discontinuous reward landscape from binary comparisons) and provides a principled solution (imposing temporal monotonicity as a structural prior) that generalizes beyond the specific DITTO implementation. Any future method for few-shot preference alignment could incorporate this insight.

### Innovation 3: Empirical Proof That Demonstration-Based Feedback Is Radically More Sample-Efficient Than Pairwise Preferences for Individual Alignment

While the reframing of demonstrations as preference signals is a conceptual contribution, the paper's third innovation is the **empirical quantification of just how large the efficiency gap is** between different feedback modalities for individual-level alignment. This is not merely a "DITTO works better" result—it is a finding about the relative information content of different types of human feedback, with direct implications for how alignment interfaces should be designed.

The dominant assumption in the RLHF literature is that pairwise comparisons are the natural unit of preference feedback. This assumption is so entrenched that the standard pipeline (collect preferences → train reward model → optimize policy) is named after it. Alternative feedback modalities—demonstrations, natural language critiques, scalar ratings—have been explored but are typically treated as lossy approximations to pairwise preferences that must be converted into comparisons before they can be used. DITTO's results challenge this hierarchy.

The paper's sample efficiency experiment (Section 5.3, Figure 3) is designed as a direct head-to-head comparison: for the same user and the same task, how many pairwise preferences are needed to match the performance of 4 demonstrations processed through DITTO? The answer is striking in two ways. First, **pairwise preferences sampled from the base model's output distribution provide zero benefit**, regardless of quantity—500 labeled pairs from π_ref achieve no improvement, because the base model's outputs are so far from the user's target style that the preference comparisons provide no relevant information. This is a damning finding for approaches like Constitutional AI that generate preference data from a fixed model without first moving the sampling distribution toward the user's preferences. Second, even when the sampling distribution is improved by first fine-tuning on the demonstrations (the "demo-finetuned policy" condition), **over 500 pairwise preferences are needed to match DITTO's performance with 4 demonstrations.** Given that labeling 500 pairwise comparisons is a substantially more burdensome task for a user than providing 4 demonstrations, the efficiency advantage of demonstrations is not marginal—it is transformative.

This finding has implications that extend beyond the DITTO algorithm. It suggests that the field's focus on pairwise preferences as the gold-standard feedback modality may be misplaced for personalization applications. Pairwise preferences are efficient when the goal is to learn a population-level reward function from many annotators, because each annotator can contribute a few comparisons and the aggregate signal is rich. But for an individual user, the cost of labeling comparisons is borne entirely by that user, and the paper shows that the per-unit information content of a demonstration far exceeds that of a pairwise judgment. This is consistent with the HCI literature the paper cites (Cypher, 1991; Cypher & Halbert, 1993): showing is more efficient than comparing, because a demonstration communicates a complete positive example while a pairwise judgment communicates only a relative ordering between two specific points.

The practical implication is a call to redesign alignment interfaces. If a user is willing to spend 15 minutes providing feedback to personalize an LLM, the evidence suggests that time should be spent writing or editing 4–7 examples rather than labeling dozens of pairwise comparisons. DITTO provides the algorithmic machinery to extract maximal value from those demonstrations; the empirical finding is that this value far exceeds what can be extracted from an equivalent effort spent on pairwise labeling.

This innovation is primarily **empirical and diagnostic** rather than algorithmic. The efficiency advantage of demonstrations was hypothesized but not quantified before this work. By providing concrete numbers (4 demos ≈ 500+ pairwise preferences), the paper establishes a benchmark against which future feedback modalities can be measured.

### Innovation 4: Formalizing and Exploiting the Extrapolation Property of Online Imitation Learning for Language Model Personalization

The paper's fourth contribution is bringing the theoretical result from online imitation learning—that policies trained with automatically generated comparisons can **outperform the demonstrator** under specifiable conditions—into the language model alignment literature and demonstrating it empirically. This is a conceptual bridge between two previously disconnected fields, and it provides a principled explanation for a phenomenon that might otherwise seem like magic: how can training on a few user examples produce outputs that are *better* than those examples?

The prior state of the alignment literature had no language for this. SFT is fundamentally bound by demonstrator performance: it can at best reproduce the distribution of the training data, imperfections and all. RLHF with pairwise preferences can theoretically exceed annotator performance because the reward model can generalize, but this property is not formalized as a guarantee. DITTO, by deriving itself as online imitation learning (Section 3.3, Appendix B), inherits the extrapolation result from Brown et al. (2020a) and makes it a central feature of the method. Lemma 3.1 states the precise condition: extrapolation occurs when the gap between the optimal policy's objective and the average reward of the demonstrations exceeds a weighted difference of two KL divergences.

What makes this more than a theoretical curiosity is that **the condition is empirically satisfiable in the personalization setting.** The demonstrations provided by a user are rarely optimal under the user's own preferences—they may be hastily written, contain minor errors or inconsistencies, or represent only a narrow slice of the user's stylistic range. The optimal policy π^* (which perfectly balances reward maximization against staying close to the base model) could achieve substantially higher expected reward than the average demonstration, creating the headroom that Lemma 3.1 requires. Meanwhile, DITTO's KL penalty (controlled by β = 0.05) is weak enough that the learned policy can move meaningfully away from π_ref toward π^*, making the right-hand side of the inequality small. The qualitative evidence in Table 7 (Appendix) supports this: DITTO outputs often amplify and clean up the user's style relative to the demonstrations, producing text that is stylistically *more consistent* than the training examples.

This is a **theoretical advance** that distinguishes DITTO from prior few-shot alignment methods. SPIN (Chen et al., 2024), the closest algorithmic predecessor, does not provide an extrapolation guarantee and was designed for data scales where SFT already works well. DITTO's derivation makes explicit *why* the self-play dynamic can produce super-demonstrator performance, and the ablation showing that updating π_ref destroys this property (Table 3: 70.1% → 45.8%) confirms that the theoretical conditions matter in practice—a moving reference policy allows the KL gap to vanish, eliminating the condition that makes extrapolation possible.

The significance of this innovation is that it **repositions alignment from a mimicking problem to an optimization problem.** Demonstrations are not a target to be imitated; they are evidence of a latent reward function that the model should optimize. This framing opens the door to methods that explicitly reason about the uncertainty in the user's preferences given limited demonstrations, potentially active learning approaches that query the user for demonstrations that maximize information gain about the reward function, or hybrid methods that combine demonstrations with other feedback modalities in a principled way.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper uses three data sources. First, two author attribution (AA) datasets reframed for alignment evaluation: (1) **CMCC** (Goldstein et al., 2008), consisting of emails and blog posts from 21 students across six genres and six controversial topics, filtered to include only single-author emails and blog posts, yielding 10 authors with 12–50+ writing samples each; and (2) **CCAT** (Lewis et al., 2004), consisting of news articles from the Reuters RCV1-v2 corpus, from which 10 authors were sampled. These datasets provide pre-existing, author-specific writing that can test whether an aligned model produces text attributable to the same author. Second, a **user study** (N=16) where participants defined four email-writing tasks, provided two demonstrations each for two training prompts (4 total demonstrations), and evaluated outputs on two held-out test prompts. CMCC and CCAT test generalization across diverse writing tasks (opinion pieces, recipes, editorial articles) within each author; the user study tests naturalistic personalization with real user effort constraints.

- **Base model(s).** The primary base model is **Mistral Instruct v0.2 7B** (Jiang et al., 2023), chosen as a representative instruction-following LLM with strong general capabilities that has undergone RLHF training—precisely the type of model whose generic style DITTO aims to override for specific users. All training uses LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) with rank 16 and alpha 32 rather than full fine-tuning; the paper reports that "full finetuning for a handful of authors...observed no significant difference." For prompting baselines, the paper also evaluates **GPT-4** (specifically gpt-4-0613) as a stronger closed-source comparison point, testing whether few-shot prompting of a more capable model can match DITTO's alignment on a smaller fine-tuned model. The user study additionally uses GPT-4 for zero-shot, few-shot, and self-prompt baselines.

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation metric is **head-to-head win rate (%)**, computed via pairwise comparison between model outputs. Given a reference text (an author's held-out writing sample or a user's preference judgment), two generated outputs from different conditions are compared, and the evaluator selects which output better matches the reference. Win rates are averaged across all pairwise comparisons, with order swapped to account for positional bias. For the static benchmarks (CMCC, CCAT), evaluation is automated using **GPT-4 as a judge**—the model is prompted to determine which of two generations is more likely written by the target author, given a sample of that author's writing. The paper benchmarks this automated evaluation against human judgments on CMCC: when pairing an author's original text with another author's text (both written on the same topic), GPT-4 achieves 81.79 ± 2.42% accuracy at identifying the correct author. For the user study, evaluation is performed by the **participants themselves** through blinded pairwise preference elicitation (20 comparisons per user, 320 total). The paper also reports standard error of the mean across authors for aggregate win rates. Notably, the paper does not report standard generation metrics (perplexity, BLEU, ROUGE), arguing that style alignment is inherently subjective and better captured by comparative judgment.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against five distinct approaches:
  1. **Zero-shot prompting**: the base model (Mistral 7B or GPT-4) is prompted with only the task description and no examples.
  2. **Few-shot prompting**: all training demonstrations are included directly in the model's context window (7 demonstrations for static benchmarks, 4 for user study) as input-output pairs, with instructions to respond in the same style.
  3. **Self-prompting** (user study only): participants iteratively author their own prompts to steer model outputs, representing the trial-and-error prompt engineering that users typically employ today. This baseline tests whether users can effectively verbalize their stylistic preferences.
  4. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)**: standard maximum-likelihood training on the demonstration dataset for a longer duration than DITTO's SFT warm-start phase, testing whether more SFT alone can achieve DITTO's performance.
  5. **SPIN** (Chen et al., 2024): an iterative self-play method where the model generates its own training data by treating current-model outputs as inferior to previous-model outputs. SPIN is the closest algorithmic predecessor to DITTO and tests whether design decisions specific to DITTO (frozen reference policy, replay comparisons, inter-model comparisons) matter in the few-shot regime.
  The paper also tested zero-shot prompting with explicit constraints (e.g., "do not generate output that is GPT-like") but found this reduced few-shot win rates by approximately 4 percentage points (Appendix E), so it was not included as a primary baseline.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not measure compute in FLOPs or wall-clock time for the main alignment comparison, as the primary axis of comparison is **sample efficiency** (number of demonstrations or preference pairs required) rather than training compute. For DITTO, the key resource metric is the number of expert demonstrations N (typically 4–7) and the number of negative samples M generated per demonstration (typically 10). The paper acknowledges that DITTO is slower than SFT—"15 minutes with DITTO vs. 2 minutes with SFT on 7 demonstrations" (Section 6, Limitations)—but treats this as an acceptable tradeoff given the large performance improvements. All training was conducted on a single A100 80GB GPU. For the sample efficiency comparison against pairwise preferences (Section 5.3), the budget is measured in number of pairwise labels (0–500) vs. number of demonstrations (4).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** For the static benchmarks, the paper randomly selects 10 authors from each dataset (CMCC, CCAT), uses 7 randomly selected demonstrations per author for training, and splits the remaining samples into validation and test sets (2–3 per author for CMCC, 3 per author for CCAT; see Table 4 in Appendix C). Hyperparameters are tuned via random sweep on a single randomly selected author from each corpus, with selection based on validation set performance, then fixed for all other authors. This protocol is important: it means hyperparameters are not tuned per-author, avoiding inflated results from author-specific optimization. For the main results (Table 1), each model is trained with 3 different random seeds, and 3 samples are generated from each model at temperature 1.0 for evaluation, with win rates averaged across all comparisons. Statistical significance is assessed via ANOVA followed by Tukey test: DITTO's improvements are significant (p < 0.05) compared to all other conditions in Table 1, except few-shot GPT on CMCC. For the user study, 320 total pairwise preferences are collected (16 users × 20 comparisons each), and significance is also assessed via ANOVA + Tukey test (p < 0.05 for DITTO vs. all other methods, Table 2). The paper does not report confidence intervals on individual author-level win rates, only standard error of the mean across authors.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Static Benchmark Results: Author Attribution Alignment

The central quantitative claim is that **DITTO achieves an average 77.09% win rate across all 20 authors and both datasets, outperforming all baselines** (Table 1). This is the aggregate number, but the per-dataset breakdown and baseline comparisons reveal the structure of DITTO's advantage.

**On CMCC** (emails and blog posts, 10 authors), DITTO achieves a 71.67% average win rate (SEM ± 2.30%). The next best method is SFT at 56.78% (± 7.04%), representing an 11.7 percentage-point improvement for DITTO over SFT. The gap is substantially larger against prompted baselines: few-shot GPT-4 achieves 63.89% (± 3.18%), few-shot Mistral 7B achieves 46.89% (± 4.76%), and zero-shot Mistral achieves only 27.33% (± 2.24%). SPIN, the closest algorithmic baseline, achieves 51.56% (± 3.85%), trailing DITTO by 20.1 percentage points. DITTO outperforms all baselines for 5 out of 10 individual authors on CMCC, with few-shot GPT winning on 3 authors and SFT winning on the remaining 2. The per-author variation is substantial: SFT win rates range from 18.06% (author 2) to 86.11% (author 4), while DITTO's range is tighter (58.33% to 81.94%), suggesting DITTO is more robust to variation in demonstration quality or author style complexity.

**On CCAT** (news articles, 10 authors), DITTO achieves an 82.50% average win rate (± 1.93%). SFT is closer on this dataset (73.89% ± 2.50%), with DITTO providing an 8.6 percentage-point improvement. SPIN performs better on CCAT (62.13% ± 3.11%) than on CMCC but still trails DITTO by 20.4 percentage points. Few-shot GPT-4 achieves 53.70% (± 2.19%), and few-shot Mistral achieves only 40.37% (± 2.33%). DITTO outperforms all baselines for 9 out of 10 individual authors on CCAT—the most dominant per-author result across both datasets. The CCAT results are notable because news articles may have more standardized stylistic conventions than personal emails and blogs, potentially making SFT more competitive (smaller 8.6-point gap vs. 11.7 on CMCC) but also making DITTO's contrastive signal more effective at distinguishing fine-grained authorial differences within those conventions.

**The prompted baseline degradation is dramatic and consistent.** Zero-shot Mistral achieves only 27.33% (CMCC) and 18.06% (CCAT)—essentially indistinguishable from random chance (50% would be parity with the comparison target; these numbers indicate the evaluator strongly prefers the author's original text over zero-shot model outputs). Zero-shot GPT-4 performs somewhat better (31.89% CMCC, 19.35% CCAT) but remains far below DITTO. This confirms the paper's central premise: instruction-tuned models produce outputs that are stylistically distant from individual authors, and prompting alone cannot bridge this gap. Adding all 7 training demonstrations as few-shot context helps substantially—few-shot Mistral improves to 46.89% (CMCC) and 40.37% (CCAT), gains of 19.6 and 22.3 percentage points respectively—but still falls well short of DITTO. The paper's qualitative analysis (Table 7) and Fightin'-Words results (Table 6) attribute this residual gap to the persistence of GPT-like clichés ("greatly appreciate," "hope this message finds you well") even in few-shot prompted outputs, suggesting that the RLHF priors encoded in the model's weights are not easily overridden by in-context examples alone.

**The GPT-4 vs. DITTO (Mistral 7B) comparison is particularly informative.** On CMCC, few-shot GPT-4 achieves 63.89% vs. DITTO's 71.67%—DITTO on a 7B model outperforms few-shot prompting of GPT-4 by 7.8 percentage points. On CCAT, the gap widens to 28.8 percentage points (82.50% vs. 53.70%). This is a striking result: it implies that for personalization tasks, **a small fine-tuned model with demonstration-based alignment can substantially outperform a much larger, more capable model used only through prompting.** The paper explicitly notes this implication in the context of "inference-only alignment methods for black box LLMs that do not require fine-tuning" (Section 6, Future Work) as a motivation for why fine-tuning access matters.

**Per-author variance reveals DITTO's consistency advantage.** Across both datasets, DITTO's win rates have substantially lower variance than SFT. On CMCC, SFT's standard deviation across authors is approximately 22.3 percentage points (estimated from the per-author range of 18.06–86.11), while DITTO's is approximately 7.3 percentage points (range 58.33–81.94). This suggests that **DITTO's contrastive mechanism provides a form of robustness to demonstration quality**: even when demonstrations are noisy or inconsistent (leading to poor SFT performance, as with author 2 on CMCC where SFT achieves only 18.06%), the negative sampling and preference ranking in DITTO can partially recover. The paper does not explicitly analyze this robustness property, but it is visible in the per-author breakdown.

#### User Study Results: Naturalistic Task Generalization

**DITTO achieves a 68.8% win rate in the user study, significantly outperforming all baselines** (Table 2, p < 0.05 via ANOVA + Tukey test). The ranking of methods is: DITTO (68.8%) > SFT (55.5%) > few-shot GPT-4 (51.6%) > self-prompt (46.9%) > zero-shot GPT-4 (27.3%). This ordering mirrors the static benchmark results but with two notable differences.

First, **the SFT advantage over few-shot prompting is smaller in the user study** (55.5% vs. 51.6%, a 3.9-point gap) than in the static benchmarks (11.3-point gap on CMCC, 33.5-point gap on CCAT). The paper does not explain this directly, but it is consistent with the user study having only 4 demonstrations per user (vs. 7 in the static benchmarks) and with users potentially providing demonstrations that are closer to GPT-4's default style (since participants were shown GPT-4 outputs as starting points for their edits, as described in Section 4.2). The average normalized Levenshtein edit distance between GPT-4 defaults and final user demonstrations was 0.43, indicating that users edited nearly half the text—substantial changes, but the remaining structural similarity may have made few-shot GPT-4 more competitive.

Second, **the self-prompt baseline (46.9%) underperforms few-shot prompting (51.6%).** This is a notable finding: users who iteratively refined their own prompts to steer model outputs achieved worse alignment than simply including their demonstrations as few-shot examples. The paper interprets this as evidence that "users generally struggle with verbalizing preferences into prompts" (Section 4.2), consistent with the HCI literature on prompt engineering difficulty (Zamfirescu-Pereira et al., 2023). The fact that self-prompting underperforms even static few-shot prompting—where the demonstrations were written once and not iteratively refined—suggests that the bottleneck is not effort but **the fundamental difficulty of translating stylistic instincts into explicit natural language instructions.**

The Fightin'-Words analysis (Monroe et al., 2008) comparing DITTO outputs to GPT-4 outputs in the user study (Table 6) identifies specific lexical patterns: GPT-4 outputs contain statistically significantly higher frequencies of cliché phrases associated with formal RLHF training ("greatly appreciate your time and understanding," "hope this message finds you well," "trust," "initiatives"). Words more associated with GPT include "appreciate" (log odds 1.40, z = 2.82, p < 0.000), "greatly" (log odds 1.07, z = 2.44, p = 0.015), and "understanding" (log odds 1.35, z = 2.42, p = 0.016). This provides quantitative evidence for the paper's claim that RLHF priors are deeply embedded and resistant to surface-level prompting interventions.

#### Sample Efficiency and Hyperparameter Scaling

The paper includes a set of scaling analyses (Figure 2) that characterize how DITTO's performance varies with its key hyperparameters. These are presented as within-author normalized win rates (% improvement from a baseline configuration) to control for inter-author variance.

**Number of DITTO iterations (Figure 2, left):** Performance improves substantially from iteration 1 to iteration 4, with a 31.5% relative increase in win rate. The improvement is non-monotonic: iteration 2 shows a slight decrease (−3.4% relative) before recovering at iterations 3 and 4. The paper hypothesizes that early iterations produce noisier samples, which may temporarily reduce performance before the contrastive signal stabilizes. Beyond 6 iterations, performance degrades (not shown quantitatively in Figure 2 but stated in the text), attributed to overfitting on the limited demonstration set. This non-monotonicity is an important practical finding: users should not assume that more iterations always help, and early performance dips should not trigger premature stopping.

**Number of negative samples M (Figure 2, middle):** Increasing M from 2 to 10 produces a monotonic 21.09% relative improvement in win rate. Critically, variance across users also decreases as M increases—the error bars shrink visibly from M=2 to M=10. This suggests that sampling more negatives provides a more robust estimate of the model's current behavior, reducing the impact of outlier samples that might provide misleading contrastive signal. The paper notes that increasing M beyond 10 yields plateauing performance, and that runtime increases linearly with M, establishing M=10 as a practical sweet spot.

**Number of demonstrations N (Figure 2, right):** Performance improves rapidly from N=1 to N=3, with normalized win rates roughly doubling for each additional demonstration (0% → 5% → 11.9% relative). From N=4 to N=7, gains diminish (11.9% → 15.39%), suggesting that DITTO extracts most of the available signal from the first 3–4 demonstrations and that additional demonstrations provide marginal returns. This has direct practical implications: a user need only provide 3–4 high-quality demonstrations to achieve near-maximal performance, and effort spent on additional demonstrations would be better invested elsewhere (e.g., ensuring the initial demonstrations are diverse and representative).

#### Pairwise Preferences vs. Demonstrations: A Sample Efficiency Comparison

**The paper's most striking efficiency result is that 4 demonstrations processed through DITTO match or exceed the performance of over 500 explicitly labeled pairwise preferences** (Figure 3, Section 5.3). This experiment was conducted by a single author (one of the paper's authors) who provided demonstrations and annotated preference pairs for the user study tasks.

Two conditions are compared. In the first condition ("Base policy," blue line in Figure 3), pairwise preferences are generated by sampling outputs from the base instruction-following Mistral 7B and having the user label which is preferred. Even with 500 labeled pairs, this approach yields **no improvement**—the win rate against DITTO (trained on 4 demonstrations) remains near zero. The paper explains this starkly: "preferences generated over OOD samples (relative to the user's true reward) are essentially irrelevant." Because the base model's outputs are so far from the user's target style, pairwise comparisons between them provide no information about the direction of improvement toward the demonstrated style.

In the second condition ("Demo-finetuned policy," orange line in Figure 3), the model is first fine-tuned on the 4 demonstrations (SFT) before sampling outputs for pairwise labeling. This brings the sampling distribution closer to the user's style, making the pairwise comparisons more informative. Even so, **over 500 labeled pairs are needed to approach DITTO's performance** with the same 4 demonstrations. The paper does not report exact win rates at each budget level, but the trend is clear: the DITTO line (horizontal, since it is fixed at 4 demos) intersects the orange curve at approximately the 500-pair mark.

This result is the empirical foundation for the paper's central claim about demonstration efficiency. If we take the user effort required as the relevant cost metric, then: providing 4 demonstrations (which the user study showed took the form of editing GPT-4 outputs, with average edit distance 0.43) is substantially less effort than labeling 500 pairwise comparisons (which requires reading two outputs and making a comparative judgment for each pair). The paper does not directly measure time or cognitive load for each feedback mode, but the magnitude of the difference (4 vs. 500+) makes the efficiency advantage unambiguous.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

All ablations in this section are reported in Table 3 (Section 5.1), conducted on the CMCC dataset, with win rates computed as head-to-head comparisons against the full DITTO configuration.

**Sampling all negatives at the start vs. iterative resampling:** When DITTO samples all negative examples once at the beginning of training (from π_0 only) and does not resample during the iterative process, win rates drop from 70.1% to 57.3%. This 12.8 percentage-point decrease confirms that the online nature of DITTO—generating fresh negatives from the evolving policy—is critical. Static negatives from the initial policy become increasingly irrelevant as the model improves, providing no contrastive signal about the model's *current* errors. This ablation essentially reduces DITTO to standard DPO on a fixed dataset of expert-vs-π_0 comparisons, losing the benefit of temporal diversity in the negative examples.

**Removing inter-model comparisons (D_{i≤t} ≻ D_{j<i}):** When inter-model comparisons are removed from the batch composition (keeping only expert-vs-policy comparisons, both online and replay), win rates drop from 70.1% to 68.1%—a modest but consistent 2.0 percentage-point decrease. This confirms that inter-model comparisons provide a small but real benefit, consistent with their role as a smoothing mechanism that teaches the model the direction of improvement. The relatively small magnitude of this drop—compared to the replay ablation—suggests that inter-model comparisons are a refinement rather than a core driver of DITTO's performance.

**Removing replay comparisons (D_E ≻ D_{i<t}):** When replay comparisons are removed (keeping only comparisons between the expert and the *current* policy, plus inter-model comparisons), win rates drop from 70.1% to 63.6%—a 6.5 percentage-point decrease. This is a substantially larger drop than removing inter-model comparisons, indicating that maintaining a diverse set of negative examples from earlier checkpoints is more important than the relative ranking between those checkpoints. Replay comparisons prevent the model from forgetting the types of errors it made in earlier iterations; without them, the model may re-introduce previously eliminated errors as it explores new regions of output space.

**Updating the reference policy π_ref:** When π_ref is updated to be the previous iteration's policy (matching SPIN's approach, Chen et al., 2024), win rates plummet from 70.1% to 45.8%—a 24.3 percentage-point decrease. This is the largest single-ablation effect and confirms that the frozen reference policy is the most critical design decision distinguishing DITTO from SPIN. The paper attributes this to overfitting: as π_ref tracks the policy, the KL constraint becomes progressively weaker, allowing the model to drift arbitrarily far from the initial instruction-tuned distribution. In the few-shot regime, this unbounded drift leads to catastrophic forgetting of general language capabilities and collapse onto the narrow style of the demonstrations. This ablation provides the clearest evidence that **SPIN's design, while effective at SFT-scale data regimes, is fundamentally unsuited to few-shot personalization.**

**Testing on a base (non-instruction-tuned) model:** To rule out the possibility that DITTO's advantage comes solely from the instruction-following model's strong priors, the paper also trained DITTO on a base variant of Mistral (not instruction-tuned). When comparing DITTO (instruction-tuned Mistral) against SFT and few-shot prompting on the base model, win rates against DITTO were 9.4% and 10.4% respectively—even lower than the instruction-tuned baselines. The paper concludes that "general instruction-following capabilities are required as a 'starting point'" and that "jointly learning instruction-following and demonstrated feedback is too difficult a task to learn from a handful of demonstrations." This is an important boundary condition: DITTO assumes a model that already knows how to follow instructions and produce coherent text; it refines the style of that capability, but cannot create it from scratch.

**Mitigating forgetting via LoRA routing (Appendix H.1):** A concern with any fine-tuning method is that personalization may degrade general capabilities. The paper evaluated a DITTO-trained model on HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), a coding benchmark, and found that pass@1 dropped from 0.31 (base Mistral 7B) to 0.13 (DITTO-ed model)—a substantial degradation. However, by using a prompted router to selectively apply the LoRA adapter only to style-relevant queries (determined by showing the router the demonstrations and asking whether the new task is similar), pass@1 is fully recovered to 0.31. This mitigation demonstrates that the forgetting problem is addressable through simple routing, though it requires knowing when to use the personalized adapter vs. the base model—a practical deployment consideration the paper acknowledges but does not fully solve.

**Demonstration cohesiveness and performance (Appendix H.2):** The paper conducted a preliminary analysis of whether the internal consistency of the provided demonstrations affects DITTO's performance. Using an LLM-based scoring prompt from Lam et al. (2024) to rate demonstration cohesiveness on a 1–5 Likert scale, the paper found a moderate positive correlation (Pearson's R = 0.42) between cohesiveness scores and performance improvement over the few-shot baseline. This suggests that **providing demonstrations that are stylistically consistent with each other improves DITTO's ability to extract the target style**, while heterogeneous demonstrations (e.g., mixing formal and casual emails) may provide conflicting signal. This is a practically important finding for guiding users on how to select demonstrations, though the paper treats it as preliminary and leaves systematic study to future work.

### Critical Assessment

#### Do the Experiments Support the Central Claims?

**Claim 1: DITTO achieves an average 19 percentage-point win rate improvement over baselines.** This claim is supported by Table 1, but requires careful interpretation. The "average 19% points" in the abstract refers to the average across all baselines and datasets—SFT (11.7-point gap on CMCC, 8.6 on CCAT), SPIN (20.1 and 20.4), few-shot Mistral (24.8 and 42.1), zero-shot Mistral (44.3 and 64.4), few-shot GPT-4 (7.8 and 28.8), and zero-shot GPT-4 (39.8 and 63.2). Averaging all these gives approximately 19% points, but this obscures enormous variance: the improvement over few-shot GPT-4 ranges from 7.8 (CMCC) to 28.8 (CCAT) percentage points. The claim is an accurate aggregate but masks the fact that DITTO's advantage over prompting baselines is much larger on CCAT (news articles) than on CMCC (emails/blogs), and that zero-shot models are so far below parity that any fine-tuning method would show large improvements. The more meaningful comparison is against SFT and SPIN, where the improvements are more modest but still substantial (8.6–20.4 points) and represent gains over training-based methods that use the same data. These comparisons are the ones that isolate DITTO's algorithmic contribution from the simple benefit of fine-tuning vs. prompting.

**Claim 2: DITTO enables few-shot alignment with <10 demonstrations.** This claim is directly supported by the experiments, which use 7 demonstrations for static benchmarks and 4 for the user study. DITTO consistently outperforms SFT trained on the same demonstrations, confirming that the method extracts more value per demonstration than standard fine-tuning. However, the paper does not establish a precise lower bound on the number of demonstrations needed—Figure 2 (right) shows that performance with 1 demonstration is near baseline, with rapid improvement from 1 to 3 and diminishing returns thereafter. The practical minimum appears to be 3–4 demonstrations to achieve meaningful alignment, which is still remarkably low but not quite the extreme few-shot regime (1–2 examples) that the abstract's phrasing might imply.

**Claim 3: Demonstrations are an order of magnitude more sample-efficient than pairwise preferences.** The evidence for this claim (Figure 3, Section 5.3) is dramatic but methodologically limited. The experiment was conducted by a single author (one of the paper's authors)—this is not a user study with multiple participants—so the generalizability of the specific ratio (4 demos ≈ 500+ pairs) is unknown. Different users, tasks, or demonstration quality could change this ratio substantially. The experiment also does not control for total user time or effort: providing 4 demonstrations and labeling 500 pairwise preferences are fundamentally different activities with different cognitive demands, and the paper does not measure which is actually "cheaper" for users. The claim of "sample efficiency" is about the number of feedback instances, not about user experience. Additionally, the finding that pairwise preferences from the base model distribution provide zero benefit is an important negative result, but it is also somewhat circular: if the base model's outputs are completely off-target, then no amount of comparisons between off-target outputs will identify the target. The more informative condition—pairwise preferences from the demo-finetuned distribution—still requires 500+ pairs, but this number may be inflated by the specific demo-finetuned model used; a more aggressively fine-tuned model might produce a distribution where pairwise preferences are more informative, reducing the required count. The qualitative conclusion (demonstrations are substantially more efficient) is well-supported, but the specific quantitative claim (two orders of magnitude) should be treated as a point estimate from a single user rather than a general law.

**Claim 4: DITTO can extrapolate beyond demonstrator performance (Lemma 3.1).** This is a theoretical claim, and the paper provides no direct empirical test of it. There is no experiment where DITTO outputs are compared to the demonstrations themselves and judged to be better. The win rate metric compares DITTO to other methods, not to the demonstrations. The qualitative examples in Table 7 are suggestive—some DITTO outputs appear to be more stylistically consistent than the demonstrations—but this is anecdotal. Proving extrapolation would require collecting user preference judgments comparing DITTO outputs directly against the user's own demonstrations on held-out prompts, which was not done. The theoretical result is elegant, but its empirical status in this paper is unverified.

#### Genuine Weaknesses in the Experimental Design

**Single model family (Mistral 7B) for all training experiments.** DITTO is evaluated only on Mistral Instruct v0.2 7B. The paper compares against GPT-4 only through prompting, never through fine-tuning (which would be impossible for a closed-source model). The claim that DITTO is a general method for few-shot personalization is thus supported only for one model architecture and scale. Different base models have different RLHF priors (some may be less "generic" than others), different in-context learning capabilities, and different fine-tuning dynamics. The paper acknowledges this limitation explicitly: "because of evaluation and computational constraints, we do not test across model families or sizes. Exploring how DITTO scales is an avenue for future work" (Section 6, Limitations). This is a significant gap—without multi-model evaluation, we cannot know whether DITTO's advantage over SFT is a general property of the algorithm or specific to Mistral 7B's particular RLHF training.

**GPT-4 evaluation introduces potential self-enhancement bias.** The static benchmark results rely entirely on GPT-4 as an automatic evaluator. While the paper benchmarks this evaluation against human judgments on CMCC (81.79% accuracy for author identification), this benchmark tests whether GPT-4 can distinguish *different authors* from each other, not whether it can accurately judge which model better matches a specific author's style. A known issue in LLM-as-judge evaluation is self-enhancement bias: LLMs tend to prefer outputs that resemble their own generation style (Panickssery et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). The paper acknowledges this (Section 4.1): "GPT-4 eval exhibits a self-enhancement bias, likely inflating performance for LLM-like generations." If GPT-4 evaluator prefers its own "GPT-like" style, it might actually *penalize* DITTO outputs that successfully deviate from that style toward the human author's voice, making the reported win rates conservative estimates. However, the benchmarking result—where GPT-4 correctly identifies human text as more similar to the target author than its own zero-shot outputs (98.8% accuracy)—partially mitigates this concern. The user study, which uses human evaluators and also finds DITTO outperforming baselines, provides convergent validation that the GPT-4 eval results are not purely artifacts of evaluator bias. Still, the magnitudes of win rates in the static benchmarks vs. the user study differ (DITTO at 71.67–82.50% vs. 68.8%), and some of this difference could be evaluator-related.

**Small test set sizes per author.** The static benchmarks use 2–3 test samples per author for CMCC and 3 per author for CCAT. With 10 authors, this means DITTO's performance is evaluated on approximately 25–30 held-out prompts total per dataset. Win rates computed over such small test sets have high variance—a single lucky or unlucky generation can swing an author's win rate by 33–50 percentage points. The paper partially addresses this by generating 3 samples per prompt (increasing total comparisons) and averaging across 3 training seeds, but the fundamental limitation is the small number of distinct test prompts. This is an inherent constraint of author-specific datasets (individual authors simply haven't written that many texts), but it means that the reported win rates should be interpreted as approximate rankings rather than precise performance estimates. The user study partially compensates for this with 20 comparisons per user, though it introduces its own limitations (N=16 participants, all recruited from social media with many being PhD students—a narrow demographic).

**Missing baseline: DPO on expert demonstrations with synthetic negatives from a different policy.** DITTO's core mechanism is generating negative samples from the current policy. An alternative approach would be to generate negatives from a *different* policy—for example, the original instruction-tuned model, or a deliberately "anti-stylized" model. This would provide a fixed set of negatives without requiring iterative resampling. The paper's "sample all at start" ablation partially tests this (using π_0 negatives only), but does not test whether negatives from π_ref (the original instruction-tuned model, never fine-tuned) would work better or worse than DITTO's online approach. This is a reasonable baseline to expect: if the primary benefit is simply having *some* negative examples, then a fixed set of π_ref negatives might suffice and would be computationally cheaper. The paper's results suggest this would underperform (since online resampling helps), but the specific comparison is absent.

**No comparison against parameter-efficient prompting methods.** The paper compares against few-shot prompting (examples in context) but not against more sophisticated prompt-based personalization methods like prompt tuning (learning soft prompts) or retrieval-augmented generation where demonstrations are dynamically retrieved. Given that DITTO uses LoRA fine-tuning, a comparison against prompt tuning with a similar parameter budget would help isolate whether the benefit comes from the training algorithm or simply from having trainable parameters.

**The user study's self-prompt baseline may not represent best-practice prompt engineering.** Participants were asked to iteratively author prompts to steer GPT-4's outputs. This simulates what an unaided user would do, but it does not compare against state-of-the-art prompt engineering techniques (e.g., using an LLM to automatically generate and refine prompts, as in Zhou et al., 2022). A user armed with an automatic prompt optimizer might achieve better results than the self-prompt baseline, potentially narrowing the gap with DITTO. The paper's focus is on what users can achieve *without* specialized tools or expertise, which is a valid and important use case, but the self-prompt baseline should be understood as representing unaided human prompt engineering, not the upper bound of what prompting can achieve.

#### Missing Experiments That Would Strengthen the Paper

**Cross-model generalization of DITTO adapters.** If a user trains a DITTO LoRA adapter on Mistral 7B, does that adapter transfer to a different base model (e.g., Llama 2, or a different size of Mistral)? This would test whether DITTO learns style representations that are model-independent or model-specific. The paper's LoRA-based implementation makes this experiment straightforward but it is not conducted.

**Sensitivity to demonstration ordering and selection.** The paper briefly explores demonstration cohesiveness (Appendix H.2), finding a moderate correlation with performance, but does not systematically vary which demonstrations are selected from a larger pool. An experiment where different subsets of an author's available demonstrations are used (varying diversity, quality, and topic coverage) would provide practical guidance on how users should choose which examples to provide.

**Ablation of the SFT warm-start phase.** DITTO begins with SFT on the demonstrations before starting the iterative DPO loop. What happens if this phase is skipped entirely—if DPO training begins immediately from the instruction-tuned model? The paper does not report this ablation. This is important for understanding whether the SFT phase is merely an optimization convenience (getting the model into the right region of parameter space) or whether it provides essential initialization without which DPO would fail.

**Evaluation on non-stylistic, task-oriented personalization.** All experiments focus on writing style (emails, blog posts, news articles). Does DITTO work for personalizing *behavior* rather than *style*—for example, teaching a model to always include specific disclaimers, to format answers in a particular way, or to follow domain-specific rules? This would test whether DITTO's mechanism generalizes beyond stylistic features to more explicit preference constraints. The paper's focus on author attribution datasets, while methodologically convenient (they provide ground-truth individual writing), limits the scope of demonstrated applicability to style transfer.

**Direct measurement of extrapolation beyond the demonstrator.** As noted, Lemma 3.1's extrapolation claim is not empirically tested. An experiment where users rate DITTO outputs as better or worse than their own demonstrations on held-out prompts would directly test this. Without it, the extrapolation property remains a theoretical possibility rather than an empirically validated feature of the method.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: The Method Assumes a Base Model Already Possesses Relevant Capabilities, Providing No Path for Fundamentally Out-of-Distribution Tasks

DITTO is explicitly designed and evaluated for stylistic and preference alignment—refining *how* a model expresses content it already knows how to produce. The paper is transparent about this scope, positioning DITTO as a method for "local" alignment rather than capability acquisition (Section 3.3):

> "DITTO is designed for aligning a model to a specific user or task, not for improving the model's general capabilities."

This assumption manifests concretely in the paper's choice of base model (Mistral Instruct v0.2 7B, an instruction-tuned model that has undergone RLHF) and in the finding that DITTO fails entirely when applied to a base (non-instruction-tuned) variant of Mistral. The paper reports that training DITTO on the base model yields win rates against instruction-tuned DITTO of only 9.4% (few-shot) and 10.4% (SFT), concluding that "general instruction-following capabilities are required as a 'starting point'" and that "jointly learning instruction-following and demonstrated feedback is too difficult a task to learn from a handful of demonstrations" (Section 5.1).

The consequence of this limitation is that DITTO cannot teach a model to perform tasks or adopt styles that are fundamentally outside its pretraining or instruction-tuning distribution. If a user wants to align a model to write in a highly specialized domain (legal briefs, medical reports, poetry in a specific meter) where the base model has no relevant knowledge, DITTO's contrastive mechanism has nothing to refine—there are no approximately-correct outputs for the negative sampling to push away from. This is analogous to the finding in the test-time compute paper (Snell et al., 2024) that test-time strategies provide zero benefit on the hardest problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero: both methods amplify existing capability but cannot create it. The paper's evaluation domains (personal emails, blog posts, news articles) all fall within the general writing capabilities of an instruction-tuned LLM, so this limitation is not empirically tested, but it follows directly from the method's design.

The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation or propose methods for extending DITTO to capability-poor domains. This is not a flaw—the method is scoped to a specific problem—but it establishes a hard boundary condition that practitioners must respect: before applying DITTO, verify that the base model can produce approximately correct outputs for the target task, even if those outputs are stylistically misaligned. If the base model cannot produce any correct output, DITTO will not help.

### Limitation 2: The Cost of Generating and Training on Negative Samples Makes DITTO Slower Than SFT, with Training Time Scaling Linearly with the Number of Demonstrations and Negatives

DITTO's core mechanism—iteratively sampling negative examples from the current policy and training against them—comes with a computational cost that the paper acknowledges but does not fully account for in its efficiency claims. The authors state (Section 6, Limitations):

> "One limitation involves DITTO speed: DITTO is slower than training-free approaches (prompting) and SFT (15 minutes with DITTO vs. 2 minutes with SFT on 7 demonstrations). A bottleneck lies in sampling, though we suspect a mix of prior (e.g., vLLM Kwon et al. (2023)) and future work in LLM inference optimization can improve DITTO's speed."

The training time scales with three factors: the number of iterations (typically 4), the number of negative samples per demonstration (M = 10), and the resampling frequency (every K = 10 gradient steps). Each negative sample requires a full forward pass through the model to generate a completion, which for autoregressive LLMs is substantially more expensive than the forward-backward pass of a single gradient step. The paper's configuration of 4 iterations × 10 negatives × 7 demonstrations × (number of resampling events) produces hundreds of generated completions over the course of training, each of which adds latency.

For a user who needs quick personalization, this latency matters. The 15-minute figure is for a specific hardware configuration (single A100 80GB GPU, 7 demonstrations, Mistral 7B with LoRA). Users with less powerful hardware or larger models would face longer training times. More critically, the latency scales linearly with the number of demonstrations (N) and negatively sampled completions (M). The paper's ablation in Figure 2 (middle) shows that increasing M from 2 to 10 yields a 21% relative improvement in win rate—but the training time for M=10 is 5× that for M=2, creating a direct accuracy-latency tradeoff that users must navigate.

The paper partially mitigates this by using LoRA rather than full fine-tuning, which reduces the per-step gradient computation cost, and by resampling only every K=10 steps rather than every step. However, the generation bottleneck remains and is inherent to the online imitation learning framework: negative samples must be drawn from the current policy to provide relevant contrastive signal, as demonstrated by the ablation where sampling all negatives at the start (static negatives) causes win rates to drop from 70.1% to 57.3% (Table 3). The paper suggests future work on inference optimization as a mitigation but does not implement or evaluate any speed improvements. For practitioners, the practical question is whether the 7.5× slowdown relative to SFT (15 min vs. 2 min) is justified by the 11.7–20.4 percentage point improvement in win rate—likely yes for most applications, but the tradeoff is real and must be weighed against time-sensitive deployment constraints.

### Limitation 3: DITTO's Evaluation Is Confined to a Single Model Family and Scale, with No Evidence That the Method Transfers to Larger or Architecturally Different LLMs

All DITTO training experiments use Mistral Instruct v0.2 7B as the base model. The paper does not train DITTO on any other model family (e.g., Llama, Qwen, Gemma) or on larger-scale variants (e.g., Mistral 8×7B, Llama-70B). The only non-Mistral model evaluated is GPT-4, which is used exclusively in a prompting capacity (zero-shot and few-shot), not fine-tuned with DITTO. The authors acknowledge this explicitly (Section 6, Limitations):

> "because of evaluation and computational constraints, we do not test across model families or sizes. Exploring how DITTO scales is an avenue for future work."

This single-model evaluation creates several uncertainties for practitioners. First, the effectiveness of DITTO depends critically on the strength and nature of the base model's RLHF priors—the "generic" style that DITTO is designed to overwrite. Mistral Instruct v0.2 underwent a specific RLHF procedure; other models, particularly those trained with different alignment methodologies (e.g., Constitutional AI, direct human feedback from different demographics), may have different stylistic biases that are either easier or harder to override with a small number of demonstrations. A model with weaker or different RLHF priors might show a smaller gap between DITTO and SFT, while a model with stronger priors might require more demonstrations or negative samples than the paper's recommended configuration.

Second, the paper's LoRA configuration (rank 16, alpha 32) was tuned for Mistral 7B. Whether these hyperparameters transfer to larger models—where the ratio of LoRA parameters to total parameters is smaller—is unknown. A larger model might require a higher LoRA rank to express the personalized style transformation, or might exhibit different overfitting dynamics that change the optimal number of iterations or negative samples.

Third, the DITTO hyperparameters (β = 0.05, M = 10, K = 10, batch composition 70/20/10) were tuned via random sweep on a single randomly selected author from each benchmark corpus. The paper then fixes these hyperparameters for all other authors and models. This tuning methodology means the reported DITTO performance is optimized for Mistral 7B on these specific datasets; applying the same hyperparameters to a different model family without re-tuning could yield suboptimal results. The paper provides no sensitivity analysis showing how DITTO's performance varies if these hyperparameters are used off-the-shelf on a different model.

The paper compares DITTO-trained Mistral 7B against few-shot GPT-4 and shows DITTO wins (71.67% vs. 63.89% on CMCC, 82.50% vs. 53.70% on CCAT), demonstrating that a 7B model with DITTO can outperform a much larger prompted model. But the reverse comparison—DITTO applied to the larger model—is absent. It is plausible that DITTO applied to a larger, more capable base model would yield even stronger personalization, or that the gap between DITTO and SFT might narrow at larger scales (since larger models are better at few-shot generalization from SFT alone). Without this evidence, practitioners cannot estimate the marginal benefit of applying DITTO to their specific model.

### Limitation 4: DITTO-Trained Models Exhibit Catastrophic Forgetting of General Capabilities, Requiring an External Routing Mechanism to Preserve Performance on Non-Personalized Tasks

Personalization through fine-tuning, by design, moves the model away from its general-purpose behavior toward a specific user's preferences. DITTO is no exception, and the paper provides direct evidence of capability degradation. When evaluated on HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021), a standard coding benchmark, a DITTO-trained model's pass@1 drops from 0.31 (base Mistral 7B) to 0.13—a 58% relative decrease (Appendix H.1). This is not surprising—the model was fine-tuned on email and blog post demonstrations, and its probability mass has shifted toward stylistic writing at the expense of code generation—but the magnitude is substantial.

The paper proposes a mitigation: using a prompted router to selectively drop the DITTO LoRA adapter for queries that are not style-relevant. When the router determines that a query (e.g., a coding task) should be handled by the base model, the LoRA weights are removed, and pass@1 is fully recovered to 0.31. The routing prompt asks the base model to compare the new task against the demonstrations and decide whether the specialized or general model is appropriate (Appendix H.1).

This mitigation, while effective in the paper's demonstration, has several unresolved practical issues. First, the router itself is a prompted LLM judgment, which introduces latency and potential errors—a misrouted query either loses personalization (if a style-relevant query is routed to the base model) or loses capability (if a capability-intensive query is routed to the personalized model). The paper provides no quantitative evaluation of router accuracy. Second, the routing approach requires maintaining both the base model and the LoRA adapter, and making a routing decision for every query, which complicates deployment relative to a single unified model. Third, the paper only evaluates forgetting on coding (HumanEval); other capabilities (reasoning, factual knowledge, multilingual generation) are not tested, so the full scope of capability degradation is unknown.

The fundamental tension here is between **specialization depth** and **capability preservation**. DITTO's strength—its ability to substantially reshape the model's output distribution toward a specific style—is also the source of the forgetting problem. The KL penalty in the DPO objective (controlled by β = 0.05) provides some constraint against drifting too far from the reference model, but the paper's own results show that this constraint is not sufficient to preserve capabilities in domains far from the personalization data. The routing mitigation is a pragmatic workaround, but it effectively concedes that DITTO produces a specialized model that cannot simultaneously serve as a general-purpose assistant—a meaningful deployment constraint for applications where users expect a single model to handle both personalized communication and general knowledge tasks.

### Limitation 5: The Sample Efficiency Claim Rests on a Single-User Experiment, and the Cost Model Ignores the Cognitive Difficulty of Authoring High-Quality Demonstrations

The paper's most striking claim—that 4 demonstrations processed through DITTO match the performance of over 500 explicitly labeled pairwise preferences (Section 5.3, Figure 3)—is based on an experiment conducted by a single author (one of the paper's co-authors). The paper states this explicitly: "one author provided demonstrations for the user study and also annotated 500 preference pairs" (Section 5.3). While the result is directionally compelling, it has limited generalizability for several reasons.

First, the demonstration-to-preference efficiency ratio likely depends on the quality of the demonstrations and the specific user's ability to articulate their preferences. A user who provides carefully crafted, internally consistent demonstrations (which the paper shows in Appendix H.2 correlates moderately with DITTO performance, Pearson's R = 0.42) will extract more value per demonstration than a user who provides rushed or inconsistent examples. The single-author experiment cannot capture this variance—the result may reflect the specific author's demonstration quality rather than a general property of demonstrations as a feedback modality.

Second, the pairwise preference condition that samples from the base model distribution ("Base policy," blue line in Figure 3) shows zero improvement even at 500 pairs. The paper correctly identifies the reason: preferences over off-distribution samples provide no signal about the target style. However, this condition is somewhat artificially weak—it assumes a user is labeling comparisons between outputs that are both far from their desired style, which is an inefficient use of labeling effort that a well-designed preference collection interface would avoid. The more realistic condition is the "Demo-finetuned policy" (orange line), where the sampling distribution is first moved toward the user's style via SFT on the 4 demonstrations. Even here, 500+ pairs are needed to match DITTO, but the specific number depends on how much the SFT step improved the sampling distribution. A more aggressively fine-tuned model might produce a distribution where pairwise preferences are substantially more informative, potentially reducing the required number of pairs below 500. The paper does not explore this sensitivity.

Third, the paper's sample efficiency framing compares the *number* of feedback instances (4 demonstrations vs. 500+ pairwise preferences) without accounting for the *difficulty* of producing each type of feedback. Authoring a high-quality demonstration requires generating an entire email or article in the target style—a cognitively demanding task that involves composition, editing, and stylistic consistency. Labeling a pairwise preference requires reading two outputs and selecting the better one—a comparative judgment that is typically faster and less cognitively demanding than generation. The paper does not measure time-on-task or cognitive load for either feedback mode, so the claim that demonstrations are "more efficient" is specifically about instance count, not about user effort. A user might prefer to label 100 pairwise comparisons (each taking 30 seconds) rather than author 4 demonstrations from scratch (each taking 5–10 minutes), even if the resulting alignment quality is lower. The user study partially addresses this by having users edit GPT-4 outputs rather than write from scratch (Section 4.2), but this introduces its own confound: the demonstrations are partially GPT-4-generated, which may constrain the stylistic range users can express.

The paper's response to this limitation is implicit in its design choices: the user study specifically asks participants to edit GPT-4 outputs rather than write from scratch, explicitly to reduce the burden of demonstration authoring (Section 4.2). The average edit distance of 0.43 confirms users made substantial changes, but the cognitive effort of editing vs. pairwise comparison remains unmeasured. A rigorous efficiency comparison would require a controlled user study measuring both alignment quality and user effort (time, cognitive load, satisfaction) across feedback modalities, which the paper does not conduct.

### Limitation 6: DITTO's Advantage Over SFT Is Inconsistently Demonstrated and Depends on Prompt-Demonstration Similarity That Is Not Controlled For

In the user study, the gap between DITTO (68.8%) and SFT (55.5%) is 13.3 percentage points—substantial but notably smaller than the average 11.7-point gap on CMCC and the 8.6-point gap on CCAT reported in the static benchmarks. The paper does not explain this variation, but it points to an important uncontrolled variable: the relationship between training prompts and test prompts.

The static benchmarks (CMCC, CCAT) use pre-existing author writing samples where the training and test prompts are drawn from genuinely different tasks. For example, an author might have written about "the legalization of marijuana" for training and "whether the Catholic Church needs to adapt" for testing—prompts that differ in topic, argument structure, and required background knowledge. The user study, by contrast, uses email-writing tasks where all prompts fall within the same general domain. If the training prompts were "write an email to your advisor asking for feedback" and "write an email to a collaborator scheduling a meeting," and the test prompts were "write an email requesting mentorship opportunities" and "write an email sharing a recipe with a friend," the stylistic demands overlap substantially. DITTO's contrastive mechanism may provide more benefit when the test prompts require non-trivial generalization beyond the training prompts' topics, while SFT may be sufficient when train and test prompts are stylistically similar.

The paper does not analyze this variable systematically. There is no measurement of prompt similarity (e.g., semantic embedding distance) between training and test prompts, no stratification of results by generalization difficulty, and no experiment that explicitly varies the train-test prompt gap. The per-author results in Table 1 show DITTO outperforming SFT on 5/10 CMCC authors and 9/10 CCAT authors, but the paper does not investigate why SFT wins on the remaining authors. It is possible that for authors whose training demonstrations happen to cover topics structurally similar to the test prompts, SFT already captures most of the available stylistic signal, and DITTO's additional contrastive mechanism provides diminishing returns. Conversely, for authors where test prompts require stylistic generalization to new topics, DITTO's ability to learn the *boundary* of the style (not just its center) provides larger gains.

This limitation has practical implications for deployment: if a user's test-time prompts are likely to be similar to their training demonstrations, the additional cost and complexity of DITTO over SFT may not be justified. The paper provides no guidance on how to make this determination prospectively. A practitioner would want to know: given my specific set of demonstrations and anticipated test prompts, should I use DITTO or SFT? The paper's aggregate results favor DITTO, but the per-author variance suggests the answer is not uniform, and the paper provides no predictive model or heuristic for making this decision.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper introduces a **fundamental reframing of what a user demonstration means** in the context of language model alignment, and that reframing has consequences that extend well beyond the specific DITTO algorithm. Before this work, the dominant paradigm—inherited from supervised learning—treated demonstrations as target outputs: examples of correct behavior that the model should learn to reproduce through likelihood maximization. This framing works when data is abundant but creates an intractable efficiency problem at the individual level, because a handful of positive-only examples cannot define the boundary between acceptable and unacceptable behavior. DITTO's core conceptual move is to treat demonstrations not as targets but as **implicit preference axioms**: each demonstration is definitionally preferred over any output the model could generate, which converts a small set of positive-only examples into an unbounded, self-generating contrastive dataset. This is not merely a data augmentation trick—it is a different theory of what information a demonstration carries.

The practical significance of this reframing is established by the paper's sample efficiency result (Section 5.3, Figure 3): 4 demonstrations processed through DITTO match or exceed the performance of over 500 explicitly collected pairwise preferences. If we take this result at face value—and the user study provides convergent validation, albeit with a different comparison structure—then the implication is that **demonstrations are not just an alternative feedback modality to pairwise preferences; they are a fundamentally denser carrier of preference information for individual-level alignment.** This finding should redirect research attention away from optimizing pairwise preference collection interfaces (how to elicit more comparisons per unit time, how to select the most informative pairs) and toward optimizing demonstration collection interfaces (how to help users author diverse, high-quality demonstrations, how to select which prompts users should demonstrate on). The paper's finding that pairwise preferences sampled from the base model's distribution alone provide zero benefit regardless of quantity (the flat blue line in Figure 3) is particularly damning for approaches that assume the base model's output space is a reasonable sampling distribution for preference elicitation. This result implies that **Constitutional AI-style methods (Bai et al., 2022) that generate preference data from a fixed model are fundamentally unsuited to personalization** unless the base model's outputs already fall within the user's preference region—which, for stylistic personalization, they demonstrably do not.

The paper also establishes **verifier over-optimization's analog in the preference-learning domain**: training exclusively against expert-vs-model comparisons with a small expert dataset creates a brittle, discontinuous reward landscape where the model collapses probability mass onto the exact demonstration points rather than learning the underlying style manifold. DITTO's solution—introducing a temporal ranking over policies to smooth this landscape—is elegant and principled, and the ablation results (Table 3) quantify its contribution: removing replay comparisons costs 6.5 percentage points, removing inter-model comparisons costs 2.0 points, and removing both (reducing DITTO to a static, expert-only contrastive method) costs 12.8 points. This establishes that **contrastive learning in the few-shot regime requires structural priors about the direction of improvement**, and that the sequence of policies produced during training provides those priors for free. Future few-shot alignment methods, regardless of whether they use demonstrations or pairwise preferences, should incorporate this insight—imposing monotonicity or smoothness constraints on the learned reward function is not merely a regularization heuristic but a requirement for avoiding collapse when positive examples are scarce.

On a methodological level, the paper resolves a latent tension in the personalization literature between two conflicting intuitions. The first intuition, drawn from the few-shot prompting literature, is that language models can adapt to new styles from a handful of in-context examples—just put the demonstrations in the prompt and the model will "get it." The second intuition, drawn from the fine-tuning literature, is that deep stylistic change requires updating model weights—surface-level prompting cannot override the RLHF priors baked into billions of parameters. DITTO's results synthesize these positions: few-shot prompting does provide meaningful improvement over zero-shot (CMCC: 27.33% → 46.89% for Mistral; user study: 27.3% → 51.6% for GPT-4), confirming that in-context learning captures *some* stylistic signal, but fine-tuning is necessary to close the remaining gap (DITTO at 71.67% on CMCC, 68.8% in user study), confirming that in-context learning cannot fully *overwrite* deep model priors. The resolution is that **in-context examples provide a stylistic nudge, weight updates provide a stylistic transformation**, and the two are complementary rather than competing—a finding that has direct implications for deployment architectures, where a base model might use few-shot prompting for quick, coarse adaptation and reserve DITTO-style fine-tuning for persistent, high-quality personalization.

The paper also provides one of the first systematic quantifications of **how much "GPT-ness" persists after prompting interventions.** The Fightin'-Words analysis (Table 6) showing statistically significant overuse of phrases like "greatly appreciate your time and understanding" and "hope this message finds you well" in GPT-4 outputs—even after few-shot prompting with user demonstrations—puts numbers to a phenomenon that users experience qualitatively. The fact that these clichés survive few-shot prompting but are substantially reduced in DITTO outputs (as demonstrated by the qualitative examples in Table 7 and the user study win rates) suggests that **certain stylistic features are "deep" in the sense that they are encoded in the model's weights through RLHF and are resistant to in-context suppression**. This has implications for the broader debate about whether prompting can substitute for fine-tuning: for tasks where the desired behavior directly conflicts with the model's RLHF training (e.g., making outputs *less* helpful, *less* polite, *less* formal), prompting alone may be fundamentally insufficient regardless of how cleverly the prompt is engineered.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**DITTO with active demonstration selection: closing the loop between what the user demonstrates and what the model needs to learn.** The paper's analysis of demonstration cohesiveness (Appendix H.2) found a moderate correlation (Pearson's R = 0.42) between how internally consistent the provided demonstrations are and DITTO's downstream performance. This suggests that not all demonstration sets are equally informative, but the paper provides no mechanism for guiding users toward better demonstration selection. A natural extension is an active learning loop: after an initial round of DITTO training on 2–3 seed demonstrations, the model identifies the prompts on which its outputs are most stylistically uncertain (e.g., where the PRM-style verifier score has highest variance, or where inter-policy ranking is least consistent), and asks the user to provide additional demonstrations specifically for those prompts. This would replace the paper's ad hoc demonstration collection protocol (users pick their own tasks and provide demonstrations) with a targeted, information-theoretically motivated protocol. The key metric would be alignment quality per demonstration, compared against random demonstration selection, to determine whether active selection can reduce the number of demonstrations needed to reach a target win rate.

**Cross-model transfer of DITTO style adapters: measuring whether learned personalization is model-specific or model-agnostic.** The paper trains all DITTO adapters on Mistral 7B using LoRA. A straightforward but high-impact experiment would train a DITTO LoRA adapter on Mistral 7B for a specific author, then apply that same adapter (or a transformed version of it) to a different base model—e.g., Mistral 8×7B, Llama-2 7B, or Llama-2 13B—and measure whether the stylistic alignment transfers. The paper's LoRA-based implementation makes this experimentally cheap: the adapter is a low-rank matrix that can be applied to any model with compatible architecture. If adapters transfer within the same model family (Mistral 7B → Mistral 8×7B) but not across families, that would indicate DITTO learns style representations that are entangled with the base model's specific RLHF priors. If adapters transfer across families, that would suggest DITTO extracts a more abstract, model-agnostic style representation. The result would determine whether a user's DITTO investment is tied to a specific model or can follow them as they upgrade to newer or larger models—a practically crucial question for deployment.

**DITTO for non-stylistic preference alignment: testing whether the method generalizes beyond writing style to behavioral and factual preferences.** All of DITTO's evaluation domains (emails, blog posts, news articles) concern writing style—prose rhythm, formality level, lexical choice, cliché avoidance. An important stress test is whether DITTO works for preferences that are not primarily stylistic: a user who wants the model to always include specific disclaimers ("This is not financial advice"), to format outputs in a particular structured template, to consistently prefer one factual framing over another, or to adopt a specific problem-solving methodology. These are preference dimensions where the "right" and "wrong" outputs differ in content rather than style, and where the base model's RLHF priors may be less uniformly misaligned (the base model has no strong opinion on whether a user prefers bullet points or paragraphs, but it may have strong opinions on whether to include legal disclaimers). A concrete experiment would construct demonstration datasets where user preferences conflict with generally desirable behavior—e.g., a user who wants the model to always express high confidence even when uncertain—and measure whether DITTO can learn this preference without degrading the model's overall accuracy or safety. The paper's HumanEval forgetting result (pass@1 drops from 0.31 to 0.13) suggests DITTO can substantially reshape model behavior, but whether this reshaping can target specific behavioral policies without collateral capability damage is an open question.

**Combining DITTO with inference-time compute allocation: difficulty-conditioned personalization depth.** The paper characterizes DITTO's performance as a function of the number of iterations, negative samples, and demonstrations (Figure 2), finding diminishing returns beyond 3–4 demonstrations and 4 iterations. This suggests that different prompts within a user's task distribution may benefit from different amounts of personalization effort—easy prompts (stylistically close to the demonstrations) might need only 1 iteration, while hard prompts (requiring stylistic generalization to new genres or audiences) might benefit from the full 4-iteration treatment. A natural extension is a compute-adaptive version of DITTO: during inference, estimate the "stylistic distance" between the current prompt and the training demonstrations (e.g., via embedding similarity or the PRM score distribution of initial samples), and route the prompt to an earlier or later DITTO checkpoint accordingly. This would parallel the compute-optimal test-time scaling framework from Snell et al. (2024), but applied to personalization depth rather than reasoning depth. The key experiment would measure whether such routing can achieve equivalent alignment quality with reduced average inference cost, and would require collecting a dataset of prompts with human-annotated "personalization difficulty" to serve as ground truth for the difficulty estimator.

**Demonstration quality metrics: building a predictive model of DITTO success from demonstration properties.** The paper's cohesiveness analysis (Appendix H.2) is preliminary but points toward a practically important research direction: can we predict, from properties of the demonstrations alone, how well DITTO will perform? A systematic study would collect demonstrations from many users (or generate synthetic demonstrations with controlled properties), train DITTO on each set, and regress DITTO's win rate against measurable features of the demonstration set: lexical diversity, syntactic complexity, topic diversity (semantic embedding spread), length consistency, formality variance, presence of distinctive stylistic markers (emoji usage, sentence fragments, signature phrases), and the distance between the demonstrations and the base model's default outputs for the same prompts. The goal would be a lightweight "demonstration quality score" that a user-facing interface could compute in real-time and surface as guidance ("adding a more casual example would increase your style coverage by an estimated 15%"). This would transform DITTO from a black-box training procedure into an interactive system where users receive feedback on the informativeness of their demonstrations before training begins.

**DITTO with principled multi-user aggregation: learning shared vs. individual preference structure.** The paper positions DITTO for single-user personalization, but many practical deployments involve small groups with partially shared preferences (a lab group that wants a shared formal style for grant proposals but individual styles for personal emails, or a customer support team with a shared helpfulness policy but individual voice). A multi-user extension of DITTO could learn a hierarchical preference structure: a shared base adapter captures group-level preferences (trained on the union of all members' demonstrations, with member ID as metadata), and per-user residual adapters capture individual deviations. The training procedure would need to disentangle shared from individual variation—potentially through a regularization term that encourages the shared adapter to explain as much stylistic variance as possible, with per-user adapters penalized for complexity. The experiment would compare this hierarchical approach against independent DITTO models per user (the paper's current approach) and against a single pooled model, measuring both personalization quality and total parameter cost. This would determine whether DITTO can be extended from a purely individual alignment method to one that exploits shared structure across related users—a crucial capability for organizational deployments where training one adapter per user is parameter-inefficient.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Personalized writing assistants with per-recipient style adaptation.** The most direct application of DITTO is a writing assistant that learns a user's voice from a small number of examples and applies it to new writing tasks. The paper's user study (Section 4.2) demonstrates this for email: participants provided 4 demonstrations (spanning requests to advisors, scheduling with collaborators, sharing recipes with friends) and DITTO produced outputs that users preferred over GPT-4's own generations by a 68.8% to 27.3% margin. A deployed system could maintain multiple DITTO LoRA adapters per user—one for professional correspondence, one for casual messages, one for specific frequent recipients—and route prompts to the appropriate adapter based on the detected audience. The per-adapter training cost (15 minutes on a single A100 for 7 demonstrations, per the paper's timing) is low enough that adapters could be trained on-demand as users provide new demonstrations, and the LoRA format means adapters are small (a few megabytes) and can be swapped at inference time with negligible overhead. The paper's demonstration that the DITTO adapter can be selectively dropped for non-stylistic tasks (Appendix H.1, recovering full HumanEval pass@1) suggests a practical deployment where a general-purpose model handles most queries and DITTO adapters are applied only when writing tasks are detected.

**Rapid style transfer for content creation across platforms and audiences.** The paper's static benchmarks (Table 1) show DITTO outperforming SFT and few-shot prompting across diverse authorial styles, from financial editorials to opinion pieces to personal blog posts. For content creators who write across multiple platforms (Twitter threads vs. LinkedIn posts vs. Substack newsletters vs. internal memos), DITTO offers a way to maintain platform-appropriate voice without manually restyling every piece of content. A creator could provide 3–4 examples of their ideal output for each platform, train separate DITTO adapters, and then draft content in a single voice while having the adapters automatically translate it to platform-appropriate styles. The paper's finding that DITTO can extrapolate beyond the demonstrations (Lemma 3.1, and the qualitative observation that DITTO outputs sometimes clean up inconsistencies in the training examples) is particularly valuable here: the model can amplify the creator's intended style even when the training examples are imperfect first drafts. The 23–28 percentage-point win rate advantage over few-shot GPT-4 on CCAT (82.50% vs. 53.70%) and the user study (68.8% vs. 51.6%) suggests that for professional writing where stylistic consistency directly impacts audience engagement, the gap between prompting-based personalization and DITTO-level personalization is commercially meaningful.

**Demonstration-based alignment for domain-specific chatbots and assistants.** While the paper focuses on open-ended writing, DITTO's mechanism applies to any setting where a user can provide examples of desired behavior and the base model already possesses the relevant capabilities. For a customer support team, a handful of example responses to common queries (showing the team's preferred tone, disclaimer inclusion, escalation patterns) could train a DITTO adapter that captures the team's collective style. For an educator, example feedback on student essays could train an adapter that provides feedback in the educator's specific voice and with their specific pedagogical priorities. For a researcher, example code reviews or paper summaries could train an adapter that matches their preferred level of detail and critical stance. In each case, the paper's 4-demonstration efficiency threshold (Figure 2, right: diminishing returns after N=3–4) means the initial training data can be collected in a single sitting of less than 30 minutes—a realistic ask for professional users who would otherwise spend that time repeatedly prompt-engineering or manually editing outputs. The LoRA adapter format means these personalized models can be distributed as lightweight add-ons to a shared base model, avoiding the cost of serving separate full-model instances per user.

### When to Prefer This Method

DITTO is explicitly positioned against three alternatives for individual-level alignment: few-shot prompting, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and pairwise preference collection (RLHF/DPO with explicit labels). The paper's results provide clear decision criteria for choosing between them, grounded in the empirical comparisons:

- **Prefer DITTO over few-shot prompting when:** (1) the user's style conflicts with the base model's RLHF priors—the paper's Fightin'-Words analysis (Table 6) shows that prompts cannot eliminate deep stylistic tendencies like cliché usage, while DITTO's weight updates can; (2) the user needs consistent style transfer across a diverse range of prompts, not just prompts similar to the few-shot examples—DITTO's contrastive mechanism learns the boundary of the style, not just its center; (3) latency at inference time is more important than latency at training time—DITTO's cost is a one-time 15-minute training phase (for 7 demos on an A100), after which inference is as fast as the base model, whereas few-shot prompting adds context length (and thus inference cost) to every query; and (4) the user has access to fine-tune the model—few-shot prompting is the fallback for black-box API-only access.

- **Prefer DITTO over SFT when:** (1) the user has very few demonstrations (fewer than ~10)—SFT performance degrades more rapidly than DITTO's as demonstrations decrease, with DITTO showing 31.5% relative improvement from 1 to 4 iterations that SFT cannot replicate by training longer; (2) the test prompts require non-trivial generalization beyond the training prompts—the paper's per-author results show DITTO's advantage is largest when train and test domains differ (CCAT: 82.50% vs. 73.89% SFT) and smaller when they are similar, suggesting DITTO's contrastive signal helps most with generalization; and (3) the user can tolerate the additional training time (15 min vs. 2 min for SFT) and has access to the necessary GPU resources for negative sampling.

- **Prefer DITTO over pairwise preference collection when:** (1) the user is providing feedback for their own personalization, not contributing to a population-level reward model—the paper's Section 5.3 experiment shows that 4 demonstrations with DITTO match 500+ pairwise labels for an individual user; (2) the base model's output distribution is far from the target style—pairwise comparisons between off-target outputs are uninformative (the flat blue line in Figure 3), and collecting enough comparisons to move the sampling distribution into the target region is prohibitively expensive; and (3) the user finds it easier to *demonstrate* correct behavior (authoring or editing examples) than to *judge* comparative quality (selecting between two generated outputs)—the paper does not measure this directly, but the user study's demonstration collection protocol (editing GPT-4 outputs, average edit distance 0.43) was successfully completed by all 16 participants, while labeling 500 pairwise preferences was only completed by one author for experimental purposes.

- **The boundary conditions are equally important.** DITTO should not be preferred when: (1) the base model lacks the fundamental capability to perform the target task—the paper's base-model experiment shows DITTO cannot substitute for missing instruction-following or domain knowledge; (2) the user needs a model that simultaneously preserves full general-purpose capabilities without a routing mechanism—DITTO causes measurable forgetting (HumanEval pass@1 drops from 0.31 to 0.13); (3) the deployment environment cannot support the inference cost of negative sampling during training; or (4) the user can achieve adequate alignment through prompting alone—if the base model's default style is already close to the target (a situation the paper does not test, as its authors were deliberately chosen for stylistic distinctiveness), the marginal benefit of DITTO over few-shot prompting may not justify the training cost.
