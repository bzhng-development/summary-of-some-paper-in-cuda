# Self-Play Preference Optimization for Language Model Alignment

**ArXiv:** [2405.00675](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00675)

## 🎯 Pitch

Human preferences are often intransitive and irrational, yet standard alignment methods force them into a single reward hierarchy. SPPO treats alignment as a two-player game and provably converges to the Nash equilibrium—without needing a reward model—pushing Mistral-7B to a 28.5% win rate against GPT-4-Turbo using only a tiny 0.4B preference model.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **Self-Play Preference Optimization (SPPO)**, an iterative fine-tuning method that treats language model alignment as a constant-sum two-player game and provably approximates the Nash equilibrium policy via multiplicative weight updates. Using only 60k prompts from UltraFeedback and a 0.4B-parameter preference model (PairRM), SPPO fine-tunes Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 to achieve a length-controlled win rate of **28.53%** against GPT-4-Turbo on AlpacaEval 2.0 — surpassing iterative DPO and IPO — while starting from Llama-3-8B-Instruct reaches **38.77%**. Unlike prior pairwise losses (DPO, IPO) that only drive apart the relative likelihoods of winner and loser responses, SPPO's regression objective directly fits each response's win-rate against the current policy (e.g., pushing the winner's log-ratio toward +1/2 and the loser's toward −1/2), establishing that self-play preference optimization improves alignment without external supervision from stronger models, though gains on knowledge benchmarks degrade after the first iteration.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Human Preferences Are Not Transitive, But Our Models Assume They Are

The fundamental problem this paper tackles is a mismatch between how human preferences actually work and how current alignment methods model them. **Standard RLHF** (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) — the technique behind ChatGPT and other aligned models — operates on a critical simplifying assumption: that human preferences are **transitive, rational, and expressible as a single scalar reward**. If you prefer A over B, and B over C, then you must prefer A over C. Under the Bradley-Terry (BT) model (Bradley and Terry, 1952), this transitivity is baked in mathematically: every response gets a single "goodness score," and the probability of preferring response $y$ over $y'$ is simply:

$$P(y \succ y' | x) = \frac{\exp(r(y; x))}{\exp(r(y; x)) + \exp(r(y'; x))} = \sigma(r(y; x) - r(y'; x))$$

The reward function $r(y; x)$ collapses the entire preference landscape into a one-dimensional value hierarchy. This makes optimization straightforward — just maximize expected reward — but it imposes a structure that reality may not support.

**The evidence against transitive preferences is both classic and recent.** Tversky (1969) demonstrated decades ago that human decisions can exhibit **intransitive loops**: people prefer A over B, B over C, yet C over A, depending on which attributes they attend to at the moment of choice. These are not measurement errors — they reflect genuine psychological processes where different features dominate in different pairwise comparisons. For LLMs specifically, Munos et al. (2023) provided direct empirical evidence: **predicting pairwise preferences directly achieves higher accuracy than predicting them through a BT-based reward model**. If preferences were truly transitive and reward-based, the BT model would capture all the signal. The fact that it doesn't — that there's information in the preference pairs that a single scalar reward can't encode — is the smoking gun that human preferences contain non-transitive structure.

This gap matters enormously in practice. When an RLHF-trained model produces a response, it's optimizing against a reward model that assumes preferences are one-dimensional. If real human evaluators make context-dependent, attribute-weight-shifting judgments, the reward model is systematically misrepresenting what humans want. The model might learn to exploit features that correlate with high reward scores under a specific comparison framing but don't generalize across the full diversity of human preference expression.

---

### Why This Problem Is Important: Alignment Quality and the Limits of Current Methods

The practical stakes extend well beyond academic curiosity about preference structure. The entire enterprise of LLM alignment — making models helpful, honest, and harmless — depends on accurately capturing human values. If the mathematical framework is misspecified, the alignment is necessarily compromised.

**1. Reward hacking becomes more dangerous under a misspecified preference model.** The well-documented phenomenon of reward overoptimization (Gao et al., 2023) — where models learn to score highly on the reward model without actually satisfying human preferences — is exacerbated when the reward model itself can't represent the full preference structure. If preferences are non-transitive but the model forces them into a single dimension, the optimization process will find degenerate solutions that exploit whatever dimension the reward model latches onto, including spurious features like response length (a notorious confound in LLM evaluation). Table 1 in the paper shows this concretely: iterative DPO drives average response length from 1676 to 2189–2736 characters depending on the variant, while SPPO's length increase is more moderate (2163), suggesting the scalar-reward framework systematically rewards verbosity.

**2. Multi-dimensional value tradeoffs cannot be expressed.** Human preferences over LLM outputs involve competing considerations: accuracy vs. conciseness, helpfulness vs. safety, specificity vs. appropriate caution. A single scalar reward forces these tradeoffs into a fixed weighting that may not reflect how humans actually evaluate responses in context. The non-transitive preference framework — formalized as a two-player game where each player is a policy maximizing its win probability against the opponent — naturally accommodates multi-dimensional evaluations because the "preference oracle" $P(y \succ y' | x)$ can encode any pattern of pairwise judgments without assuming an underlying scalar structure.

**3. The open-source alignment pipeline remains bottlenecked by access to strong supervision.** Most high-performing aligned models (GPT-4, Claude) rely on proprietary human feedback data and/or distillation from yet-stronger models. Methods like iterative DPO (Xu et al., 2023) and self-rewarding language models (Yuan et al., 2024) attempt to bootstrap alignment from weaker signals, but as the paper shows (Table 1), iterative DPO and IPO hit ceilings — their length-controlled win rates plateau or decline while raw win rates increase, indicating they're exploiting evaluator biases rather than genuinely improving response quality. A method that can provably converge to the Nash equilibrium of the preference game using only a lightweight preference model (PairRM-0.4B) offers a path to strong alignment without dependence on GPT-4-level supervision.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper identifies three categories of prior work, each with specific shortcomings that SPPO addresses:

#### 1. Explicit and Implicit Reward Models (RLHF with PPO, DPO, SLiC, KTO)

These methods — whether they learn an explicit reward model (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) or implicitly derive one from pairwise preferences (Rafailov et al., 2024b; Zhao et al., 2023; Ethayarajh et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023) — all **presuppose the Bradley-Terry model or some variant that assigns a single score to each response**. The DPO loss, for example, is derived directly from the closed-form solution of the KL-regularized reward maximization problem under the BT model:

$$\ell_{\text{DPO}}(x, y_w, y_l; \theta; \pi_{\text{ref}}) := -\log \sigma\left( \eta^{-1} \left[ \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} \right] \right)$$

The key limitation is not implementation efficiency — DPO is elegant and stable — but rather that **the entire derivation assumes preferences are transitive and reward-based**. When this assumption fails, DPO optimizes a proxy that may not correspond to actual human preferences.

Moreover, as Pal et al. (2024) demonstrated empirically, **DPO often only drives down the likelihood of the loser response without increasing the likelihood of the winner**. The loss cares about the *gap* between $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}$ and $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}$, not the absolute values. This means DPO can achieve low loss by making the model *less likely* to produce the loser — which is useful — without making it *more likely* to produce the winner — which is the actual goal. The paper formalizes this in Section 4.5: letting $a = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}$ and $b = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}$, the DPO loss is $-\log\sigma(a-b)$, which only constrains the difference $a-b$, not the individual values. SPPO's loss $(a - 1/2)^2 + (b + 1/2)^2$ explicitly pushes $a$ toward $+1/2$ (increasing winner likelihood) and $b$ toward $-1/2$ (decreasing loser likelihood).

**KTO** (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) addresses a related issue by pushing winner scores to be high and loser scores low independently, but it still derives from a utility function that implies a scalar value per response — it doesn't escape the transitive preference assumption.

#### 2. General Preference Models Without Reward (IPO, Nash-MD, NLHF)

A more recent line of work (Azar et al., 2023; Munos et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Swamy et al., 2024) explicitly abandons the reward model and works directly with the **preference probability** $P(y \succ y' | x)$. This is exactly the right move for handling non-transitive preferences. The problem is reformulated as finding the **Nash equilibrium** (or von Neumann winner) of a two-player constant-sum game:

$$(\pi^*, \pi^*) = \arg\max_\pi \min_{\pi'} \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x), y' \sim \pi'(\cdot|x)} \left[ P(y \succ y' | x) \right] \right]$$

The Nash equilibrium policy $\pi^*$ has the property that it wins against any other policy on average — a definition of "optimal" that makes no reference to transitive reward scores.

**IPO (Identity Preference Optimization)** (Azar et al., 2023) derives a loss from this framework. However, the paper identifies a critical weakness: the IPO loss, when written in the same $a$, $b$ notation, is:

$$\ell_{\text{IPO}}(y_w, y_l, x) = [(a - b) - 1]^2$$

Like DPO, this only constrains the *difference* $a - b$, not the individual values. Pal et al. (2024) showed empirically that IPO suffers from the same failure mode as DPO — the gap widens, but the winner's likelihood doesn't necessarily increase. Section 4.5 explains this clearly: IPO attempts to fit $\beta\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y_w|x)}{\pi_t(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y_l|x)}{\pi_t(y_l|x)}$ to $P(y_w \succ \pi_t|x) - P(y_l \succ \pi_t|x)$, which is a difference-of-differences objective. SPPO instead directly fits $\beta\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}$ to $P(y \succ \pi_t|x) - 1/2$ for each response individually.

**Nash-MD** (Munos et al., 2023) proposed a mirror-descent algorithm with last-iterate convergence guarantees, but **only for tabular representations**. Computing the normalizing factor $Z_{\pi_t}(x) = \sum_y \pi_t(y|x) \exp(\eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x))$ requires enumerating the entire response space, which is impossible for LLMs. Their approximate execution samples one token at a time rather than full responses — a fundamentally different and less practical approach for large-scale fine-tuning.

**SPO (Self-play Preference Optimization)** (Swamy et al., 2024) independently proposed the same game-theoretic framework and showed that no-regret online learning reduces the preference optimization problem to the **exponential weight update rule**:

$$\pi_{t+1}(y|x) \propto \pi_t(y|x) \exp(\eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x))$$

This is exactly the update rule SPPO is built on. However, Swamy et al. (2024) focused on multi-step Markov Decision Processes in **robotic and game tasks**, and proposed using PPO or SAC to approximate the exponential update — methods that are notoriously difficult to apply stably at the scale of 7B+ parameter language models. Their work doesn't address the practical challenges of efficient LLM fine-tuning: how to estimate the win rate $P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ from finite samples, how to handle the partition function $Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ without expensive computation, and how to design a loss function that is both theoretically grounded and amenable to standard autoregressive training pipelines.

#### 3. Iterative / Self-Play Fine-Tuning

Several methods apply preference optimization repeatedly in a self-play loop (Xu et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Rosset et al., 2024). The idea is sensible: generate new responses from the current policy, annotate preferences, and train a new policy to beat the old one. However, the paper identifies that these methods **still use DPO or DPO-like pairwise losses inside each iteration**. This means they inherit the fundamental limitation — optimizing a difference rather than absolute likelihoods — and lack provable guarantees of converging to the Nash equilibrium. They are "iterative" in execution but not in their theoretical foundation.

Additionally, some of these approaches (Rosset et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024) rely on **external strong supervision** — GPT-4-generated responses as "gold samples" or GPT-4 as a judge — which defeats the purpose of self-play: the goal is to improve without access to a stronger model. SPPO deliberately avoids this, using only PairRM-0.4B.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper positions SPPO as filling a specific and previously unfilled gap: **an algorithm for large-scale LLM alignment that (1) provably converges to the Nash equilibrium of the general preference game, (2) can be implemented as a simple regression loss suitable for efficient fine-tuning, and (3) achieves strong empirical results without external supervision from stronger models.**

**The theoretical positioning** (Section 4.1) is explicit: start from the exponential weight update rule (4.1), which is known to converge to the Nash equilibrium on average (Freund and Schapire, 1999). Rather than approximating this update with complex policy optimization (PPO, SAC) as Swamy et al. (2024) did, the paper takes the log of the update rule:

$$\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} = \eta \cdot P(y \succ \pi_t|x) - \log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$$

and directly fits this relationship with an $L_2$ regression loss (4.4). This is a fundamentally different design choice from DPO/IPO: **instead of canceling the partition function $Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ by differencing two responses, SPPO approximates it with a constant ($\eta/2$)**. The justification (Appendix A) is clever: under the assumption that pairwise preferences are equally likely to go either way when aggregated over many responses, the partition function converges to $e^{\eta/2}$. Even if this assumption doesn't hold exactly, the constant serves as a variance-reducing baseline in the policy gradient interpretation (Section 4.3) — it doesn't need to be exact, just close enough to stabilize training.

**The practical positioning** (Section 5) is to compare against iterative DPO and iterative IPO as the closest baselines, using exactly the same experimental setup as Snorkel (the state-of-the-art iterative DPO model at the time). All methods use the same base model (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2), the same prompt dataset (UltraFeedback, 60k prompts), the same preference model (PairRM-0.4B), and similar generation budgets (K=5 responses per prompt, with pairwise selection for fairness). The comparison isolates the effect of the optimization objective — DPO loss vs. IPO loss vs. SPPO loss — while holding everything else constant.

**The connection to policy gradient** (Section 4.3) provides a unifying interpretation that wasn't present in prior work. The SPPO loss is shown to be equivalent to a policy gradient step with a specific baseline, connecting game-theoretic self-play to the standard RL optimization toolkit in a way that explains why the simple regression loss works. This is not just exposition — it's a genuine insight that the square loss form $(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} - \eta[P(y \succ \pi_t|x) - 1/2])^2$ is the same as what policy gradient would optimize, with $1/2$ acting as the (soft) value function baseline for variance reduction.

**The token-level interpretation** (Section 4.4) goes even further, showing that the SPPO objective implicitly trains the model to match token-level optimal value functions under the Max-Entropy RL framework. This explains why SPPO works better than pairwise losses: it provides a **token-level training signal** that guides each individual token's probability adjustment, rather than just a sequence-level preference gap.

In summary, the paper positions itself as the **first method to bring provable Nash equilibrium convergence to practical, efficient LLM fine-tuning**, bridging the gap between game-theoretically sound algorithms (Munos et al., 2023; Swamy et al., 2024) that weren't designed for LLM scale, and empirically successful iterative methods (iterative DPO, Snorkel) that lack theoretical guarantees and suffer from the likelihood-gap problem identified by Pal et al. (2024).

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper is primarily a methodology paper that derives a new optimization objective for aligning language models from general (potentially non-transitive) preferences, and then embeds that objective into an iterative self-play training loop. The system being built is a **fine-tuned language model that approximates the Nash equilibrium of a two-player preference game** — meaning it produces responses that are preferred over responses from any other policy on average, without assuming preferences can be reduced to scalar reward scores. The solution takes the form of an **iterative algorithm**: in each round, the current policy generates responses, a preference oracle compares them to estimate each response's "win rate" against the current policy, and then a simple regression loss trains a new policy to match each response's log-probability ratio to its estimated win rate.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The SPPO system has five major components that interact in a repeated loop:

1. **Base policy ($\pi_1$)** — An instruction-tuned language model (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 or Llama-3-8B-Instruct) that serves as the starting point and generates all responses throughout training. In each iteration, the previous iteration's policy becomes the new "reference policy" against which improvements are measured.

2. **Preference oracle ($P$)** — A pre-trained pairwise preference model (PairRM, 0.4B parameters based on DeBERTa-V3) that takes a prompt and two responses, and outputs a scalar $s(y, y'; x)$ reflecting the relative strength of $y$ over $y'$. This is converted to a preference probability via a logistic function: $P(y \succ y'|x) = \frac{\exp(s(y, y'; x))}{1 + \exp(s(y, y'; x))}$. Unlike Bradley-Terry reward models, PairRM does not guarantee transitive comparisons — it directly models the pairwise preference, making it suitable for non-transitive preference structures.

3. **Response generation and win-rate estimation module** — For each prompt $x$, the current policy $\pi_t$ generates $K$ responses $y_1, \ldots, y_K$. The preference oracle is queried for all $O(K^2)$ pairwise comparisons. Each response's empirical win rate $\hat{P}(y_i \succ \pi_t|x)$ is computed as the average of its pairwise win probabilities against all $K$ responses: $\hat{P}(y_i \succ \pi_t|x) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K P(y_i \succ y_k|x)$. This win rate is the key learning signal — it measures how well response $y_i$ performs against the current policy's typical outputs.

4. **SPPO loss function** — A regression objective that trains the new policy $\pi_{t+1}$ to match each response's log-probability ratio $\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}$ to the scaled, centered win rate $\eta(\hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x) - 1/2)$, where $\eta$ is a learning rate hyperparameter. The loss is the squared error between these two quantities, averaged over prompts and sampled responses.

5. **Iterative training loop** — The above process repeats for $T = 3$ iterations. In each iteration, the dataset of prompts is split into three portions, with one portion used per iteration to prevent overfitting. The policy $\pi_{t+1}$ becomes the reference policy for the next round's generation. The final policy $\bar{\pi}_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \pi_t$ (the uniform mixture of iterates) is guaranteed to approach the Nash equilibrium as $T$ grows.

Information flows as follows:

- **Iteration t begins** → The current policy $\pi_t$ generates $K=5$ responses per prompt → PairRM scores all pairs → Win rates are computed for each response → The winner and loser (highest and lowest PairRM score, using a summary score $s_{\text{PairRM}}(y_i; x) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K s(y_i, y_k; x)$) are selected to form training pairs → The SPPO loss trains $\pi_{t+1}$ to adjust response likelihoods according to their win rates → $\pi_{t+1}$ becomes the policy for iteration $t+1$.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First, the exponential weight update framework** (Equation 4.1) — the game-theoretic foundation that defines what the algorithm is trying to approximate and why it converges to a Nash equilibrium.
- **Second, the SPPO loss derivation** (Equations 4.2–4.6) — how the exponential update is transformed into a tractable regression objective, including the critical decision to approximate the partition function $Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ with the constant $\eta/2$ rather than differencing it away as DPO/IPO do.
- **Third, the policy gradient connection** (Section 4.3) — why the SPPO loss form is equivalent to a policy gradient step with the win rate as reward and $\eta/2$ as a variance-reducing baseline, providing a second theoretical justification independent of the game-theoretic derivation.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **theoretical-method paper** whose core idea is that solving the general preference alignment problem as a two-player constant-sum game, and implementing the solution via a regression loss that directly fits response-level log-ratios to win rates, yields stronger and more principled alignment than pairwise preference losses. The method is backed by two independent theoretical frameworks (game-theoretic multiplicative weights and policy gradient) that converge to the same practical algorithm.

---

#### 3.4.0 Foundational Framework: The Two-Player Constant-Sum Game

The paper formulates language model alignment with general preferences as identifying the **Nash equilibrium** of a symmetric two-player constant-sum game. This is the formal foundation on which everything else is built.

The game is defined as follows: two players, both language models parameterized as policies $\pi$ and $\pi'$, each receive a prompt $x$ drawn from some distribution $\mathcal{X}$ and produce a response. A preference oracle $P(y \succ y'|x)$ — which can represent any pattern of pairwise judgments, including non-transitive ones — determines the probability that $\pi$'s response $y$ is preferred over $\pi'$'s response $y'$. Player $\pi$'s payoff is its expected win probability against $\pi'$:

$$P(\pi \succ \pi'|x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)} \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \pi'(\cdot|x)} [P(y \succ y'|x)]$$

where $P(\pi \succ \pi'|x)$ is the probability that a response from $\pi$ is preferred over a response from $\pi'$ on prompt $x$, $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}$ denotes sampling a response from policy $\pi$ given prompt $x$, and $\mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \pi'(\cdot|x)}$ denotes sampling a response from the opponent policy $\pi'$.

**What it computes:** the expected win rate of policy $\pi$ against policy $\pi'$ on a specific prompt. It averages the pairwise preference probability $P(y \succ y'|x)$ over all possible response pairs that the two policies could generate. The game is constant-sum because $P(\pi \succ \pi'|x) + P(\pi' \succ \pi|x) = 1$ (assuming no ties, or with ties broken by assigning 1/2 to each).

**Why this form:** this formulation makes no reference to reward functions, Bradley-Terry models, or transitivity assumptions. The preference oracle $P(y \succ y'|x)$ is treated as a black box that can represent any pairwise comparison structure. The Nash equilibrium policy $\pi^*$ satisfies:

$$(\pi^*, \pi^*) = \arg\max_\pi \min_{\pi'} P(\pi \succ \pi')$$

where $P(\pi \succ \pi') = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}}[P(\pi \succ \pi'|x)]$ is the expected win rate averaged over prompts. The Nash equilibrium has the property that it achieves at least a 50% win rate against any opponent — including itself — making it the optimal policy in a minimax sense without requiring a scalar reward.

The paper also defines two intermediate quantities used throughout the derivation. The first is the **winning probability of a single response $y$ against a distribution of responses $\pi$**:

$$P(y \succ \pi|x) = \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}[P(y \succ y'|x)]$$

where $P(y \succ \pi|x)$ is the probability that response $y$ is preferred over a random response drawn from policy $\pi$ on prompt $x$. This is the key quantity that SPPO estimates from finite samples — it measures how good a specific response is relative to what the current policy typically produces.

The second is the **winning probability of one policy against another, averaged over prompts**:

$$P(\pi \succ \pi') = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}}[P(\pi \succ \pi'|x)]$$

where $\mathcal{X}$ is the prompt distribution. This is the objective that the game-theoretic framework aims to maximize (specifically, to find $\pi$ that maximizes the minimum over $\pi'$).

---

#### 3.4.1 The Exponential Weight Update and Its Derivation

The theoretical engine of SPPO is the **multiplicative weight update rule** from online learning and game theory (Freund and Schapire, 1999). The paper adopts this as the high-level algorithmic framework that provably solves the two-player game:

$$\pi_{t+1}(y|x) \propto \pi_t(y|x) \exp(\eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x)), \quad \text{for } t = 1, 2, \ldots$$

where $\pi_t$ is the policy at iteration $t$, $\pi_{t+1}$ is the updated policy, $\eta > 0$ is a learning rate (step size) that controls how aggressively the policy updates based on win rates, and $P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ is the win rate of response $y$ against the current policy $\pi_t$ on prompt $x$.

**What it computes:** a weight update for each response $y$: responses that win against the current policy $\pi_t$ on average (i.e., $P(y \succ \pi_t|x) > 1/2$) get their probability increased relative to $\pi_t$, while responses that lose on average get their probability decreased. The strength of the update is proportional to the win rate — a response that wins 80% of the time against the current policy gets a larger probability boost than one that wins 55% of the time.

**Why this form:** Freund and Schapire (1999) proved that applying this update repeatedly, with the opponent at each round being the average of all previous policies, guarantees that the time-averaged policy $\bar{\pi}_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \pi_t$ converges to the Nash equilibrium of any constant-sum game at a rate of $O(1/\sqrt{T})$. The proof (restated as Lemma B.1 in the paper's Appendix B) shows that the duality gap — the difference between the best possible win rate and the worst-case loss rate of the averaged policy — shrinks to zero as $T$ increases. This provides the provable guarantee that SPPO's iterative procedure, if executed exactly, converges to the optimal policy under the general preference model.

To make this update rule implementable as a training objective for neural networks, the paper rewrites it in log-ratio form by normalizing:

$$\pi_{t+1}(y|x) = \frac{\pi_t(y|x) \exp(\eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x))}{Z_{\pi_t}(x)}$$

and then taking the logarithm:

$$\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} = \eta \cdot P(y \succ \pi_t|x) - \log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$$

where $Z_{\pi_t}(x) = \sum_y \pi_t(y|x) \exp(\eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x))$ is the normalizing factor (partition function) that ensures $\pi_{t+1}$ sums to 1, $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ is its logarithm, $\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}$ is the log-ratio of the new policy's probability to the old policy's probability for response $y$, and everything else is as defined above.

**What it computes:** this equation says that, for an ideal exact update, the log-probability ratio between the new and old policy for any response $y$ should equal $\eta$ times the response's win rate against the old policy, minus a constant $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ that depends only on the prompt $x$ (not the specific response $y$). The term $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ acts as a normalization constant that makes the log-ratios zero-mean in expectation under $\pi_{t+1}$.

**Why this form:** expressing the update as a relationship between the log-ratio and the win rate transforms the problem from "sample from the exponentially reweighted distribution" (which is intractable for autoregressive LLMs) to "find a policy whose log-likelihood ratios match the scaled, shifted win rates." This is now a regression problem — train $\pi_\theta$ to minimize the squared error between $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}$ and the target $\eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x) - \log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$. The alternative — directly sampling from $\pi_{t+1}$ — would require computing the partition function $Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ explicitly, which sums over the exponentially large space of all possible responses and is computationally impossible.

#### 3.4.2 The SPPO Loss Function: From Exponential Weights to Regression

The paper proposes to approximate the ideal log-ratio relationship (4.3) by minimizing a **squared L2 loss** over the policy parameters:

$$\pi_{t+1} = \arg\min_\pi \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x)} \left[ \left( \log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} - \left( \eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x) - \log Z_{\pi_t}(x) \right) \right)^2 \right]$$

where the outer expectation $\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x)}$ means: sample a prompt $x$ from the prompt distribution, then sample a response $y$ from the current policy $\pi_t$ given that prompt, $\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}$ is the model's predicted log-ratio for the sampled response, $\eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ is the scaled win rate of the sampled response, and $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ is the log-partition function for that prompt.

**What it computes:** for each sampled (prompt, response) pair, the loss computes the squared difference between what the model's log-ratio actually is and what the exponential-update theory says it should be. Minimizing this loss encourages $\pi$ to satisfy equation (4.3) across the distribution of prompts and responses generated by $\pi_t$.

**Why this form:** this is a straightforward regression approach to approximating the exponential update. Unlike DPO and IPO — which cancel $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ by subtracting the log-ratio equations for two responses $y_w$ and $y_l$ — SPPO keeps the partition function explicit. The advantage is that SPPO directly constrains the **absolute** log-ratio for each response, rather than only the **relative difference** between winner and loser log-ratios. This means SPPO pushes the winner's probability up (toward positive log-ratios) and the loser's probability down (toward negative log-ratios) independently, rather than only ensuring the gap between them is correct. The disadvantage is that $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ must be estimated or approximated.

In practice, the expectations are approximated with finite samples. For each prompt $x$, the policy $\pi_t$ generates $K$ responses $y_1, \ldots, y_K$. The empirical distribution of these $K$ responses is denoted $\hat{\pi}_t^K$. The finite-sample optimization problem becomes:

$$\pi_{t+1} = \arg\min_\pi \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x)} \left[ \left( \log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} - \left( \eta P(y \succ \hat{\pi}_t^K|x) - \log Z_{\hat{\pi}_t^K}(x) \right) \right)^2 \right]$$

where $\hat{\pi}_t^K$ denotes the empirical distribution formed by the $K$ sampled responses, $P(y \succ \hat{\pi}_t^K|x) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K P(y \succ y_k|x)$ is the average win rate of response $y$ against all $K$ sampled responses, and $Z_{\hat{\pi}_t^K}(x) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x)}[\exp(\eta P(y \succ \hat{\pi}_t^K|x))]$ is the partition function estimated from the $K$ samples.

**What changes from the ideal objective:** the true win rate $P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ against the full continuous policy distribution is replaced by the empirical average against $K$ specific samples, and the partition function is computed over the empirical distribution. As $K \to \infty$, this recovers the ideal objective. For finite $K$, the estimation introduces variance but the approach remains tractable — unlike computing exact expectations over the infinite response space.

#### 3.4.3 The Critical Simplification: Replacing $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ with $\eta/2$

The partition function $Z_{\hat{\pi}_t^K}(x)$ requires an expectation over responses sampled from $\pi_t$, which itself requires additional computation. To avoid this, the paper makes a crucial simplification: **replace $\log Z_{\hat{\pi}_t^K}(x)$ with the constant $\eta/2$.** This yields the final practical SPPO objective:

$$\pi_{t+1} = \arg\min_\pi \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x)} \left[ \left( \log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} - \eta \left( \hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x) - \frac{1}{2} \right) \right)^2 \right]$$

where $\hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ is shorthand for $P(y \succ \hat{\pi}_t^K|x)$ (the empirical win rate), $\eta/2$ is the constant replacing $\log Z_{\hat{\pi}_t^K}(x)$, and everything inside the squared error is now: (predicted log-ratio) minus $\eta \times$ (win rate minus 1/2).

**What it computes:** each response's target log-ratio is $\eta(\hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x) - 1/2)$. If response $y$ wins exactly half the time against the current policy ($\hat{P} = 0.5$), the target log-ratio is zero — the model should neither increase nor decrease $y$'s probability relative to $\pi_t$. If $y$ wins more than half the time ($\hat{P} > 0.5$), the target is positive — increase probability. If $y$ wins less than half ($\hat{P} < 0.5$), the target is negative — decrease probability. The magnitude of the adjustment is proportional to how far the win rate is from 0.5, scaled by $\eta$.

**Why $\eta/2$ is a valid approximation:** Appendix A provides two theoretical justifications. First, consider the **most "disordered" case** where all pairwise preferences are independent fair coin flips: $P(y_i \succ y_j|x)$ is 1 with probability 1/2 and 0 with probability 1/2. Under this assumption, as $K \to \infty$, the partition function converges to $e^{\eta/2}$, so $\log Z_{\hat{\pi}_t^K}(x) \to \eta/2$. The proof uses properties of Rademacher random variables and Chebyshev's inequality to show that the variance of the partition function estimate vanishes as $K$ grows.

Second, consider the **most "ordered" case** where there is a strict total ordering among all responses: $y_1 \prec y_2 \prec \cdots \prec y_K$. In this extreme, as $K \to \infty$, $\log Z_{\hat{\pi}_t^K}(x) \to \log\frac{e^\eta - 1}{\eta}$. For $\eta \approx 1$, this is approximately $0.54\eta$; for large $\eta \approx 10^3$ (used in the paper's experiments), this approaches $\eta$. The constant $\eta/2$ sits between these two extremes ($\eta/2$ for maximum disorder, $\approx \eta$ for maximum order). The paper chooses $\eta/2$ because "this paper is partially motivated by human intransitive and irrational preference behavior" — that is, they expect real preferences to be closer to the disordered case than the perfectly ordered case.

**Why a constant approximation works at all:** Section 4.3 reveals that $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ is actually the **soft value function** — the expected future reward from prompt $x$ under the optimal policy. In policy gradient methods, subtracting a baseline (any function of state only) reduces variance without biasing the gradient. The constant $\eta/2$ serves as a crude but functional baseline: it doesn't need to be exact, just close enough to keep the gradient variance manageable. The key insight is that approximating the partition function is equivalent to choosing a variance-reducing baseline in a policy gradient algorithm.

#### 3.4.4 The Algorithm: Putting It All Together (Algorithm 1)

The paper presents the complete SPPO procedure as Algorithm 1. Here's the step-by-step execution:

**Inputs:**
- A base policy $\pi_{\theta_1}$ (initialized from Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 or Llama-3-8B-Instruct).
- A preference oracle $P$ (PairRM, 0.4B parameters).
- A learning rate $\eta = 1000$.
- The number of generated responses per prompt $K = 5$.

**For each iteration $t = 1, 2, \ldots, T$ (where $T = 3$):**

**Line 3 — Generate synthetic responses:** For each prompt $x$ in the training split for iteration $t$, sample $K = 5$ responses $y_1, \ldots, y_K \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x)$. The generation uses `top_p = 1.0` and `temperature = 1.0` with different random seeds to encourage diversity. The training dataset UltraFeedback contains approximately 60k prompts split into three portions of ~20k each, with one portion used per iteration to prevent overfitting.

**Line 4 — Annotate win rates:** For every pair of responses $(y_k, y_{k'})$, query the preference oracle PairRM to obtain the relative reward $s(y_k, y_{k'}; x)$. Convert this to a probability via:

$$P(y_k \succ y_{k'}|x) = \frac{\exp(s(y_k, y_{k'}; x))}{1 + \exp(s(y_k, y_{k'}; x))}$$

This requires $K^2 = 25$ oracle queries per prompt (including self-comparisons, which give $P(y_k \succ y_k|x) = 0.5$). PairRM is based on DeBERTa-V3 (He et al., 2021) and was trained on high-quality human preference datasets. It outputs only relative rewards — unlike Bradley-Terry reward models, $s(y_1, y_2; x) + s(y_2, y_3; x) \neq s(y_1, y_3; x)$ in general, meaning the preference model genuinely captures non-transitive structure.

**Line 5 — Select responses and form dataset:** Rather than including all $K=5$ responses in the training data, the paper follows Snorkel's protocol and selects only two responses per prompt (the winner $y_w$ and loser $y_l$) for fair comparison with DPO/IPO baselines. The selection criterion is the summary PairRM score:

$$s_{\text{PairRM}}(y_i; x) := \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K s(y_i, y_k; x)$$

where $s_{\text{PairRM}}(y_i; x)$ is the average relative reward of response $y_i$ against all $K$ responses. The response with the highest summary score is designated as the winner $y_w$, and the response with the lowest summary score is the loser $y_l$. The dataset $\mathcal{D}_t$ consists of tuples $(x, y_w, y_l, \hat{P}(y_w \succ \pi_t|x), \hat{P}(y_l \succ \pi_t|x))$, where the empirical win rates are computed as:

$$\hat{P}(y_w \succ \pi_t|x) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K P(y_w \succ y_k|x)$$

and similarly for $y_l$. Even though only the winner and loser are used for training, the win rates are estimated using all $K=5$ responses for lower variance.

**Line 6 — Optimize according to the SPPO loss:**

$$\theta_{t+1} \leftarrow \arg\min_\theta \ \mathbb{E}_{(x, y, \hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x)) \sim \mathcal{D}_t} \left[ \left( \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} - \eta \left( \hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x) - \frac{1}{2} \right) \right)^2 \right]$$

where $\pi_t$ is the reference policy (frozen — the policy from the previous iteration), $\pi_\theta$ is the policy being trained (initialized from $\pi_t$), $\eta = 1000$ is the learning rate controlling the strength of the update, $\hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ is the estimated win rate for the specific response $y$ (computed from all $K=5$ samples), and the target value for each response is $\eta(\hat{P} - 0.5)$.

**What happens during optimization:** the model $\pi_\theta$ is fine-tuned using standard autoregressive training. For each (prompt, response, win-rate) triplet, the model computes the log-probability of the response under $\pi_\theta$, subtracts the log-probability under the frozen reference $\pi_t$ to get $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}$, and computes the squared error against the target $\eta(\hat{P} - 0.5)$. Backpropagation adjusts $\theta$ to minimize this error.

The training hyperparameters are:
- Global batch size: 64
- Learning rate: 5.0e-7
- Number of total training epochs: 18
- Warmup ratio: 0.1 (linear warmup over the first 1.8 epochs)
- Learning rate schedule: linear decay after warmup
- Hardware: 8 × Nvidia A100 GPUs

The best hyperparameters are selected by evaluating the average win rate (judged by PairRM-0.4B) on a hold-out subset of Ultrafeedback as the validation metric. In each iteration, only the model trained on the first epoch of the 20k prompts is selected to proceed to the next iteration — this early stopping prevents overfitting to the synthetic preference data.

**Why these hyperparameter choices:**
- $\eta = 1000$ is surprisingly large, but necessary because the log-ratios $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}$ are typically very small values (log-probabilities are negative numbers with large magnitude, and the difference between two similar policies is a small adjustment). Multiplying a win-rate difference $(0.6 - 0.5) = 0.1$ by $\eta = 1000$ gives a target of 100, which is on a comparable scale to the log-ratios after normalization. Without a large $\eta$, the update would be too weak to meaningfully change the policy.
- `learning rate = 5.0e-7` is very small, which is standard for fine-tuning large language models where the pretrained weights should not be moved too far too quickly.
- $K = 5$ balances estimation quality (more samples reduce win-rate variance) with computational cost (each sample requires generation and $O(K^2)$ preference queries).
- Only selecting the winner and loser (not all $K=5$ responses) maintains comparability with DPO baselines that use a single preference pair per prompt.
- Three-fold data splitting (20k prompts per iteration, rotated) prevents the model from overfitting to specific prompts while still allowing each iteration to see all 60k prompts over the course of training.

#### 3.4.5 The Policy Gradient Interpretation (Section 4.3)

SPPO has a second, independent theoretical justification that connects it to standard reinforcement learning. Consider the standard KL-regularized reward maximization objective:

$$\max_\theta \ J(\theta) := \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ r(y; x) - \eta^{-1} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} \right]$$

where $r(y; x)$ is some reward function, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a reference policy, and $\eta^{-1}$ controls the KL penalty strength.

**What this computes:** the standard RLHF objective: maximize expected reward while penalizing deviation from the reference policy via reverse KL divergence. The term $-\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ subtracts a penalty proportional to the log-ratio — if $\pi_\theta$ puts much more probability on $y$ than $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ does, the penalty is larger.

The policy gradient of this objective (by the policy gradient theorem, Sutton et al., 1999) is:

$$\nabla J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ \left( r(y; x) - \eta^{-1} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} - b(x) \right) \nabla \log \pi_\theta(y|x) \right]$$

$$= \eta \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ -\nabla \left( r(y; x) - \eta^{-1} \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} - b(x) \right)^2 \right]$$

where $b(x)$ is an arbitrary baseline function depending only on the prompt $x$ (used for variance reduction and does not bias the gradient), $\nabla \log \pi_\theta(y|x)$ is the score function gradient, and the second line follows from the chain rule: $\nabla(f^2) = 2f\nabla f$.

**What this shows:** the policy gradient of the KL-regularized reward objective is proportional to the gradient of a **squared error loss** where the target is the reward minus the KL penalty minus a baseline. In other words, minimizing a squared error between $\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}$ and $(r(y; x) - b(x))$ is equivalent to following the policy gradient.

Now set $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_t$ (the current policy), $r(y; x) = P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ (the win rate as the reward), and $b(x) = \eta^{-1}\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ (the soft value function as the baseline). The SPPO objective (4.4) becomes:

$$\pi_{t+1} = \arg\min_\pi \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x)} \left[ \left( \eta^{-1} \log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} - \left( P(y \succ \pi_t|x) - \eta^{-1} \log Z_{\pi_t}(x) \right) \right)^2 \right]$$

which is exactly the policy gradient squared-loss form with the specific reward $P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ and baseline $\eta^{-1}\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ — rescaled by $\eta^2$.

**Why this connection matters:**
1. It provides an independent justification: SPPO is not only derivable from game-theoretic multiplicative weights but also from standard policy gradient with a particular reward function (the win rate) and baseline (the value function).
2. It explains why the constant $\eta/2$ approximation for $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ works: in policy gradient, any constant baseline reduces variance, and $\eta/2$ is a reasonable guess for the average value across prompts. The baseline doesn't need to be exact — an imperfect baseline still reduces variance compared to no baseline.
3. It reveals that SPPO is a **semi-online** variant of policy gradient: it collects samples from $\pi_t$ at the start of each iteration (like online RL), but then performs multiple gradient steps on those fixed samples (like offline RL). This is more efficient than pure online policy gradient (which would require new samples after each gradient step) while being more adaptive than pure offline methods (which use a fixed dataset).

**Comparison with Swamy et al. (2024)'s SPO:** Swamy et al. proposed using PPO or SAC to approximate the exponential update, which requires maintaining value function critics, using gradient clipping, and handling the instability of policy optimization at scale. SPPO's policy gradient interpretation shows that the simple regression loss achieves the same goal without any of this complexity — the squared loss **is** the policy gradient, so optimizing it directly is equivalent to taking a policy gradient step, without needing the elaborate machinery of actor-critic methods.

#### 3.4.6 The Token-Level Interpretation (Section 4.4)

The most theoretically rich part of the paper's analysis shows that SPPO's loss implicitly trains the model to match **token-level optimal value functions** under the Max-Entropy RL framework. This explains why SPPO provides a stronger training signal than pairwise losses that only operate at the sequence level.

The derivation builds on Rafailov et al. (2024a), who showed that under the Max-Entropy RL formulation, the token-level log-ratio $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(a_h|s_h)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_h|s_h)}$ can be interpreted as an implicit token-level reward or advantage function. The setup is as follows:

The token-level MDP defines:
- **State $s_h$** = $(x, y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_{h-1})$: the prompt plus all previously generated tokens.
- **Action $a_h$** = $y_h$: the next token to generate.
- **Transition**: deterministic concatenation — $s_{h+1} = (x, y_1, \ldots, y_h)$.
- **Horizon $H$**: the length of the complete response (variable in practice, but assumed fixed for the derivation).
- **Sequence-level reward $r(y; x)$** assigned at the final state $s_{H+1}$ (the EOS token).

The Max-Entropy RL setting considers the same KL-regularized objective as before (3.2), but now views it through the lens of entropy-regularized MDPs. The (soft) optimal value functions are defined recursively:

$$V^*(s_{H+1}) = r(s_{H+1}) := r(y; x) \quad \text{(reward at EOS)}$$

$$Q^*(s_h, a_h) = \eta^{-1} \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(a_h|s_h) + V^*(s_{h+1})$$

$$V^*(s_h) = \eta^{-1} \log \sum_a \exp(\eta Q^*(s_h, a)), \quad \text{when } h \leq H$$

where $V^*(s_{H+1})$ is the value of the terminal state — just the sequence-level reward, $V^*(s_h)$ is the (soft) value of a prefix state $s_h$ — the expected future reward under the optimal policy starting from that state, $Q^*(s_h, a_h)$ is the (soft) Q-value of taking action $a_h$ in state $s_h$, which equals the log-probability the reference model assigns to that token plus the value of the resulting next state, and $\eta^{-1}$ controls the entropy bonus scale.

**What these equations compute:** the soft value functions recursively decompose the sequence-level reward into token-level contributions. The Q-value $Q^*(s_h, a_h)$ encodes how good token $a_h$ is in context $s_h$, taking into account both the immediate reference model preference ($\eta^{-1}\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_h|s_h)$) and the expected future reward ($V^*(s_{h+1})$). The state value $V^*(s_h)$ is the softmax over Q-values — it's the log-sum-exp of $\eta Q^*$, scaled by $\eta^{-1}$, which acts as a smooth maximum: tokens with higher Q-values dominate the sum.

Rafailov et al. (2024a) showed that the optimal policy $\pi^*$ satisfies:

$$\eta^{-1} \log \pi^*(a_h|s_h) = Q^*(s_h, a_h) - V^*(s_h)$$

$$= \eta^{-1} \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(a_h|s_h) + V^*(s_{h+1}) - V^*(s_h)$$

**What this shows:** the optimal policy's log-probability for a token is the token's advantage: the Q-value (how good the token is) minus the state value (how good the current situation is on average). This is the standard advantage function decomposition from RL, applied at the token level.

For the initial state $s_1 = (x)$ (just the prompt, no tokens generated yet), unraveling the recursion gives:

$$\exp(\eta V^*(s_1)) = \sum_{a_1} \exp(\eta Q^*(s_1, a_1))$$

$$= \sum_{a_1} \pi_{\text{ref}}(a_1|s_1) \exp(\eta V^*(s_2))$$

$$= \cdots = \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp(\eta r(y; x))$$

where the summation telescopes through all token sequences $y$ that form complete responses.

**What this computes:** the partition function $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp(\eta r(y; x))$ that appears in the closed-form solution of the KL-regularized RLHF problem (Rafailov et al., 2024b). Its log is $\eta V^*(s_1)$, the soft value of the initial prompt state.

**Now the critical connection to SPPO:** In SPPO, at iteration $t$, we set:
- $\pi_{\text{ref}} = \pi_t$ (the current policy becomes the reference)
- $r(y; x) = P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ (the win rate becomes the reward)

Then $V^*(s_1) = \eta^{-1} \log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ — the log-partition function is exactly $\eta$ times the initial state value. Substituting this back into the SPPO loss (4.4):

$$\pi_{t+1} = \arg\min_\pi \ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{X}, y \sim \pi_t(\cdot|x)} \left[ \left( \log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} - \left( \eta P(y \succ \pi_t|x) - \log Z_{\pi_t}(x) \right) \right)^2 \right]$$

and using the token-level decomposition $\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)} = \sum_{h=1}^H \log\frac{\pi(a_h|s_h)}{\pi_t(a_h|s_h)}$, along with the property that the optimal policy would satisfy $\eta^{-1}\log\frac{\pi^*(a_h|s_h)}{\pi_t(a_h|s_h)} = V^*(s_{h+1}) - V^*(s_h)$ (the token-level advantage), the SPPO objective becomes:

$$\pi_{t+1} = \arg\min_\pi \ \mathbb{E}_{s_1 \sim \mathcal{X}, a_h \sim \pi_t(\cdot|s_h)} \left[ \left( \sum_{h=1}^H \log\frac{\pi(a_h|s_h)}{\pi^*(a_h|s_h)} \right)^2 \right]$$

where $s_1 = (x)$, $a_h \sim \pi_t(\cdot|s_h)$ means sampling tokens from the current policy to form a trajectory, and $\pi^*$ is the optimal policy under the Max-Entropy formulation with reward $P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$.

**What this reveals:** the SPPO loss at the sequence level is secretly minimizing the squared sum of token-level log-ratio errors between the learned policy $\pi$ and the optimal policy $\pi^*$. This means:
1. **Every token gets a training signal.** The loss decomposes (non-trivially, because of the square outside the sum) into cross-terms that couple token-level decisions. This is a richer signal than sequence-level pairwise losses like DPO, which only provide a single scalar feedback per response pair.
2. **The model learns token-level value functions implicitly.** By matching the token-level probabilities to those of $\pi^*$, the model internalizes the (soft) value function $V^*$ and advantage function $Q^* - V^*$ that define optimal behavior under the win-rate reward.
3. **The gradient has a nice form.** The paper notes that minimizing the squared loss in (4.11) is related to minimizing the KL divergence $\text{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi^*)$ via policy gradient:

$$\nabla_\theta \text{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi^*) = \mathbb{E}_{s_1 \sim \mathcal{X}, a_h \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s_h)} \left[ \left( \sum_h \log\frac{\pi_\theta(a_h|s_h)}{\pi^*(a_h|s_h)} \right) \sum_h \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_h|s_h) \right]$$

$$= \mathbb{E}_{s_1 \sim \mathcal{X}, a_h \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|s_h)} \left[ \nabla_\theta \left( \sum_h \log\frac{\pi_\theta(a_h|s_h)}{\pi^*(a_h|s_h)} \right)^2 \right]$$

where the gradient of the squared error equals the KL gradient. This closes the loop: SPPO's regression loss, the exponential weight update, the policy gradient with baseline, and the token-level KL minimization are all different perspectives on the same fundamental optimization.

**Why this matters compared to DPO:** DPO's loss, derived from the same Max-Entropy framework, also implicitly involves token-level value functions (Rafailov et al., 2024a). However, DPO only constrains the difference between winner and loser log-ratios, which means it only cares about the relative quality of two full responses. SPPO constrains each response's absolute log-ratio independently against the policy it was sampled from, which — through the token-level decomposition — provides a more granular training signal that adjusts probabilities at each token position based on how that token contributes to winning against the current policy.

#### 3.4.7 Algebraic Comparison with DPO, IPO, and KTO (Section 4.5)

The paper provides a clean algebraic comparison that crystallizes why SPPO's loss form leads to different behavior than prior methods. The derivation assumes we have a single preference pair $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $y_w$ is the winner and $y_l$ is the loser, and a hard label $P(y_w \succ y_l|x) = 1$. Define:

$$a = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}, \quad b = \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}, \quad c = \beta \ \text{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$$

where $\beta = \eta^{-1}$ is the inverse learning rate (a temperature parameter), $a$ is the scaled log-ratio for the winner — positive means $\pi_\theta$ assigns higher probability to $y_w$ than $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ does, $b$ is the scaled log-ratio for the loser — negative means $\pi_\theta$ assigns lower probability to $y_l$ than $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ does, and $c$ is the scaled KL divergence from $\pi_\theta$ to $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ (a measure of how much the policy has changed overall).

Then the four losses compared are:

**DPO loss:**
$$\ell_{\text{DPO}}(y_w, y_l, x) = -\log \sigma(a - b)$$

where $\sigma(x) = e^x/(e^x + 1)$ is the logistic sigmoid.

**What it computes:** the negative log-likelihood of the preference under the Bradley-Terry model. Minimizing this loss pushes the sigmoid of $(a - b)$ toward 1, which means pushing $(a - b)$ toward large positive values — the winner's log-ratio should be much larger than the loser's.

**Why this form is limited:** the loss only constrains the **difference** $a - b$. It doesn't care whether $a$ is positive or negative, as long as $a$ exceeds $b$ by a sufficient margin. This means DPO can achieve low loss by making $b$ very negative (aggressively reducing the loser's likelihood) while leaving $a$ near zero (not increasing the winner's likelihood) — exactly the failure mode Pal et al. (2024) observed.

**IPO loss:**
$$\ell_{\text{IPO}}(y_w, y_l, x) = (a - b - 1)^2$$

**What it computes:** the squared error between the log-ratio difference $(a - b)$ and the target value 1. Under IPO's theoretical framework, the optimal policy should satisfy $a - b = 1$, meaning the winner's log-ratio should exceed the loser's by exactly $\beta^{-1}$.

**Why this form is limited:** like DPO, it still only constrains the **difference** $a - b$. Setting $a = 0$ and $b = -1$ achieves perfect loss but hasn't increased the winner's likelihood at all. The paper notes: "IPO attempts to fit $\beta\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y_w|x)}{\pi_t(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_{t+1}(y_l|x)}{\pi_t(y_l|x)}$ to $P(y_w \succ \pi_t|x) - P(y_l \succ \pi_t|x)$" — it's a difference-of-differences objective that leaves individual response likelihoods underdetermined.

**KTO loss (simplified):**
$$\ell_{\text{KTO}}(y_w, y_l, x) = \sigma(-a + c) + \sigma(b - c)$$

**What it computes:** the first term $\sigma(-a + c)$ penalizes when the winner's log-ratio $a$ is too small relative to the KL divergence $c$ — since $\sigma(-a + c)$ is small when $-a + c$ is negative (i.e., $a > c$), the loss encourages $a$ to be large. The second term $\sigma(b - c)$ penalizes when the loser's log-ratio $b$ is too large relative to $c$ — encouraging $b$ to be negative. KTO does constrain individual values, not just the difference.

**Why it's still limited:** KTO derives from a utility function that models human decision-making under prospect theory, but it still fundamentally assumes preferences are expressible through scalar utilities. The dependence on $c = \beta \text{KL}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$ couples the winner and loser updates through the overall policy change, which may not be the right coupling for non-transitive preferences.

**SPPO loss:**
$$\ell_{\text{SPPO}}(y_w, y_l, x) = (a - 1/2)^2 + (b + 1/2)^2$$

**What it computes:** the first term pushes the winner's log-ratio $a$ toward $+1/2$ — meaning $\pi_\theta$ should assign higher probability to $y_w$ than $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ by a factor of $\exp(1/(2\beta))$. The second term pushes the loser's log-ratio $b$ toward $-1/2$ — meaning $\pi_\theta$ should assign lower probability to $y_l$ by a factor of $\exp(1/(2\beta))$. Combined, the gap $a - b$ is pushed toward 1 (matching IPO's target), but each term is individually constrained.

**Why this is better:**
1. **Individual absolute constraints:** SPPO explicitly pushes $a > 0$ (winner more likely) and $b < 0$ (loser less likely) rather than only caring about $a - b$. This directly addresses the failure mode where DPO/IPO leave the winner's likelihood unchanged.
2. **No reliance on the Bradley-Terry model:** the targets $+1/2$ and $-1/2$ come from the exponential weight update and the $\eta/2$ partition function approximation, not from assuming a scalar reward structure.
3. **Symmetry:** the winner and loser are pushed symmetrically around zero, which is natural for a constant-sum game where the win probabilities sum to 1. The targets reflect that if $y_w$ always beats $y_l$, then $\hat{P}(y_w \succ \pi_t|x) \approx 1$ and $\hat{P}(y_l \succ \pi_t|x) \approx 0$, so $\eta(\hat{P} - 1/2) = \eta/2$ for the winner and $\eta(0 - 1/2) = -\eta/2$ for the loser — exactly the SPPO targets when scaled by $\beta = \eta^{-1}$.

**The key insight:** when preference pairs are scarce (e.g., one pair per prompt), DPO and IPO can only widen the gap between winner and loser log-ratios — they can't independently push the winner up and the loser down. SPPO, by fitting each response's log-ratio to its own win rate (estimated from multiple comparisons, even if only the winner/loser are used for training), explicitly encourages both: the winner should be more likely, and the loser should be less likely, relative to the reference policy.

#### 3.4.8 Practical Implementation Details and Design Choices (Section 5.1)

The experimental setup is carefully designed to enable fair comparison with prior work while isolating the effect of the SPPO loss function. The key design choices and their justifications:

**Base model selection: Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2.** This is an instruction-tuned 7B-parameter model. The paper also experiments with Llama-3-8B-Instruct as a stronger starting point. Both models are "well-aligned" — they've already undergone instruction tuning and some preference alignment — so the task is to improve an already-reasonable policy, not to align from scratch. This tests whether SPPO can push beyond the standard RLHF alignment ceiling.

**Preference model selection: PairRM-0.4B.** PairRM (Jiang et al., 2023b) is based on DeBERTa-V3 (He et al., 2021) with 0.4B parameters. Crucially, it outputs a **relative reward** $s(y, y'; x)$ that is not guaranteed to be transitive: $s(y_1, y_2; x) + s(y_2, y_3; x) \neq s(y_1, y_3; x)$ in general. This makes it a true general preference oracle, not a Bradley-Terry reward model in disguise. The preference probability is obtained via a logistic function:

$$P(y \succ y'|x) = \frac{\exp(s(y, y'; x))}{1 + \exp(s(y, y'; x))}$$

On benchmarks like Auto-J Pairwise (Li et al., 2023a), PairRM-0.4B outperforms most language-model-based reward models and performs comparably with larger reward models like UltraRM-13B (Cui et al., 2023). The paper uses it as a lightweight but effective judge that enables the self-play loop without requiring GPT-4-level external supervision.

**Training data: UltraFeedback prompts only, no responses.** UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023) contains approximately 60k prompts from diverse sources. The paper uses **only the prompts** — no pre-existing response pairs, no human preference labels, no GPT-4 generated "gold" responses. All responses are generated by the current policy during the self-play loop, and all preferences are annotated by PairRM. This is a key differentiator from methods like Rosset et al. (2024) and Chen et al. (2024) which incorporate external strong supervision.

**Data splitting: three portions, one per iteration.** To prevent overfitting during iterative training, the 60k prompts are split into three portions of ~20k each. In iteration $t$, only prompts from portion $t$ are used for generation and training. This ensures the model sees fresh prompts in each iteration, preventing it from memorizing specific prompts while still allowing improvement on the overall prompt distribution. This follows the Snorkel protocol exactly for fair comparison.

**Response generation: $K=5$, temperature=1.0, top_p=1.0.** For each prompt, the current policy generates 5 responses with temperature 1.0 (maximum diversity within the standard sampling framework) and top_p=1.0 (no nucleus filtering). Different random seeds are used to encourage diversity. The choice of $K=5$ balances: too few samples (e.g., $K=2$) gives noisy win rate estimates; too many samples increases computational cost quadratically due to $O(K^2)$ preference queries.

**Winner/loser selection: summary PairRM score, not win rate.** Rather than using the estimated win rate $\hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ to select the winner and loser, the paper uses the average relative reward:

$$s_{\text{PairRM}}(y_i; x) := \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K s(y_i, y_k; x)$$

The response with the highest summary score is the winner $y_w$; the lowest is $y_l$. This follows the Snorkel protocol and ensures that the constructed preference pairs are high-quality — the winner genuinely dominates the loser in the pairwise reward signal, even if the win rate estimate is noisy. Only $y_w$ and $y_l$ are included in the training data $\mathcal{D}_t$ (matching DPO/IPO which use one pair per prompt), but their win rates $\hat{P}$ are still estimated using all $K=5$ comparisons for lower variance.

**Why not include all $K=5$ responses in training?** For fair comparison with DPO baselines that use one preference pair per prompt, SPPO also uses only the winner and loser. However, the method naturally supports using all $K$ responses — each with its own win rate target — which could provide a stronger training signal. The ablation study (Section 5.3, Figure 4) shows that using $K=5$ vs. $K=2$ for win rate estimation gives similar performance, suggesting the method is robust to the number of comparisons used.

**Model selection: first-epoch checkpoint per iteration.** After each iteration, the model trained for only 1 epoch (out of the 18 total epochs scheduled) on the 20k-prompt portion is selected to proceed to the next iteration. The selection criterion is the average win rate (judged by PairRM) on a hold-out subset of Ultrafeedback. This early stopping is crucial: fine-tuning for too many epochs on synthetic preference data can lead to overfitting to the specific preference patterns of PairRM rather than genuine improvement.

**Hyperparameter $\eta = 1000$:** As discussed, this large value is necessary to scale the win-rate differences (which are in $[0,1]$, centered at 0.5) to a magnitude comparable to the log-ratio values. With $\eta = 1000$, a win rate of 0.6 gives a target of $\eta(0.6 - 0.5) = 100$, while the log-ratio $\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_t(y|x)}$ for similar policies is also on the order of tens to hundreds. Without a large $\eta$, the target would be too small to drive meaningful policy changes.

**Global batch size 64:** Standard for 7B-8B models on 8×A100 GPUs. This is the total batch size; with 8 GPUs, each processes a micro-batch that sums to 64.

**Three iterations total:** $T = 3$. Why 3? The paper shows (Figure 1, Table 1) that SPPO improves steadily from Iter1 → Iter2 → Iter3, with diminishing gains (+8.81% → +3.42% → +3.62% for Llama-3-8B-SPPO), suggesting that additional iterations might provide further gains but with diminishing returns. Computational constraints are also a factor: each iteration requires generating responses for 20k prompts, scoring $O(K^2)$ pairs per prompt, and fine-tuning for one epoch, which is expensive even on 8×A100s.

**Best-of-16 re-ranking at test time:** Not part of training, but used at evaluation. For each prompt, the final SPPO model generates 16 responses (using the same sampling parameters: temperature=1.0, top_p=1.0). PairRM scores all responses, and the one with the highest summary score $s_{\text{PairRM}}(y_i; x)$ is selected as the final output. This is a standard technique that provides a modest additional gain (~3.6% win rate improvement for SPPO Iter3, Table 1) by leveraging the preference model at inference time without additional training. Crucially, the paper shows that SPPO does not over-optimize against PairRM — the improvement from best-of-16 is similar for the base model (+5.34%), DPO/Snorkel (+3.57%), and SPPO (+3.6%), suggesting SPPO hasn't gamed the preference model.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Reframing Alignment as Nash Equilibrium Search with a Response-Level Regression Target

The dominant paradigm in LLM alignment — spanning PPO-based RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022), DPO (Rafailov et al., 2024b), and even the more general IPO (Azar et al., 2023) — inherits a fundamental structural commitment: the loss function is defined on *pairs* of responses. Whether you're maximizing the log-likelihood of a Bradley-Terry preference or fitting a squared gap between log-ratios, the optimization signal is a single scalar per (winner, loser) pair. This pair-wise construction is not an accident. In DPO, it arises because taking the difference of the closed-form optimality conditions cancels the intractable partition function $Z(x)$. In IPO, the difference is the object of interest directly — the loss fits $\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}$ to a target gap. In both cases, the pair is the minimal unit of learning.

SPPO makes a fundamentally different choice. By taking the log of the exponential weight update (4.1) and treating it as a regression target *per response*, not *per pair*, the method decouples the learning signal from the differencing operation. Each response $y$ gets its own target: $\eta(\hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x) - 1/2)$. The winner $y_w$ is pushed toward a log-ratio of $+1/2$, and the loser $y_l$ toward $-1/2$, **independently**. This is not a small architectural tweak — it's a conceptual shift from "the gap matters" to "the absolute likelihood matters."

Why does this matter? Because the failure mode documented by Pal et al. (2024) — that DPO and IPO drive down the loser's likelihood without increasing the winner's — is a direct consequence of the pair-wise loss structure. When the loss only sees $(a - b)$, any combination of $a$ and $b$ achieving the right gap works. The model can satisfy the objective by making the loser very unlikely while leaving the winner unchanged, or even making both less likely as long as the gap is maintained. The SPPO loss $(a - 1/2)^2 + (b + 1/2)^2$ eliminates this underdetermination: $a$ must go to $+1/2$ specifically, and $b$ must go to $-1/2$ specifically. The targets pin down both values independently.

This is a genuine theoretical advance over IPO, which also derives from the general preference framework and also aims to solve the Nash equilibrium iteratively. IPO's loss $[(a - b) - 1]^2$ is the natural pairwise reduction of SPPO's per-response regression — in fact, subtracting the SPPO targets for $y_w$ and $y_l$ gives $(a - 1/2) - (b + 1/2) = a - b - 1$, exactly the IPO target. But the reduction loses information: it collapses two independent constraints into one. SPPO retains both constraints, and the empirical results (Table 1) show this matters: SPPO Iter3 reaches 28.53% LC win rate vs. IPO's 25.45% (Iter1), and IPO's average response length balloons to 2760 characters while SPPO's stays at 2163 — consistent with IPO exploiting length bias in the preference model by widening the gap without genuinely improving winner quality.

The regression formulation also enables a second conceptual move that pair-wise losses cannot easily accommodate: estimating the win rate $\hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ from **multiple comparisons** ($K=5$ responses, 25 pairwise queries) rather than a single pair. Each response's target is the average of its win probability against all $K$ responses — a more robust estimate than a single head-to-head result. In DPO/IPO, the target is implicitly $P(y_w \succ y_l|x)$, a single binary or soft comparison. SPPO's per-response target naturally aggregates over a mini-batch of the policy's output distribution, providing lower-variance training signal without requiring additional infrastructure. The ablation in Figure 4 confirms that even $K=2$ works, but $K=5$ provides slightly better early-iteration performance, suggesting the multi-comparison estimation helps when the policy is changing most rapidly.

### Innovation 2: The Partition Function as a Baseline — Approximating Rather Than Canceling

All preference optimization methods that derive from the closed-form solution of the KL-regularized RLHF objective must confront the partition function $Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp(\eta r(y; x))$. This sum over the exponentially large space of all possible responses is intractable. The field has developed exactly one strategy for dealing with it: **cancel it by differencing**.

DPO does this explicitly: the derivation takes the closed-form optimal policy $\pi^*(y|x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp(\eta r(y; x))$, solves for $r(y; x)$ in terms of log-ratios, plugs into the Bradley-Terry likelihood, and the partition functions from the winner and loser terms cancel. IPO does the same: it works with the difference of log-ratios, so $\log Z(x)$ cancels. This cancellation trick is so central to the derivation that it's easy to mistake it for a mathematical inevitability — of course you have to difference, because you can't compute $Z(x)$.

SPPO's most counterintuitive conceptual move is rejecting this perceived inevitability. Instead of canceling $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ by subtracting equations for two responses, SPPO **approximates it with a constant**: $\eta/2$. The justification in Appendix A is elegant and surprising. Under the assumption that pairwise preferences are maximally disordered — independent fair coin flips — the expected partition function converges to $e^{\eta/2}$ as the number of responses grows. Under the opposite extreme of perfect ordering, it converges to $(e^\eta - 1)/\eta \approx e^\eta$ for large $\eta$. The constant $\eta/2$ sits at the disordered end of this spectrum, which the authors argue is the more realistic model for human preferences that exhibit intransitivity and context-dependent evaluation.

But the deeper insight, which the paper only partially develops, is that **the approximation doesn't need to be exact**. Section 4.3 reveals that $\eta^{-1}\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ is the (soft) value function $V^*(s_1)$ — the expected future reward from the prompt under the optimal policy. In policy gradient methods, subtracting any function of state (a baseline) reduces variance without biasing the gradient. The constant $\eta/2$ is simply a crude but functional baseline guess. If the true value function varies across prompts (which it certainly does — some prompts are inherently harder to win at than others), a constant baseline will leave some variance on the table, but the policy gradient is still unbiased. The fact that SPPO works with this crude approximation suggests that **the baseline doesn't need to be good, it just needs to not be terrible** — and the default of having no baseline (implicit in differencing-based methods) may be worse.

This reframes the entire design space for preference optimization losses. DPO and IPO are locked into the differencing pattern because they treat $\log Z(x)$ as a nuisance to eliminate. SPPO shows you can treat it as a statistical quantity to estimate (however roughly) and gain the ability to constrain absolute log-ratios rather than just relative gaps. This opens up a new axis of method design: rather than asking "how can we cancel the partition function?", future work can ask "how can we better estimate the partition function or its proxy baseline?" — potentially using learned value functions, per-prompt estimates from a few samples, or adaptive baselines that improve as training progresses.

The empirical evidence that this conceptual move is valuable is indirect but persuasive. SPPO's length-controlled win rate (28.53%) beats IPO (25.45%) and DPO (24.23%) while producing more moderately-lengthed responses. The length explosion in IPO (2660 chars) and DPO (2028-2736) is consistent with these methods exploiting the differencing structure: they can make the loser look worse by making the winner longer (length bias in the preference model), widening the gap without genuinely improving content quality. SPPO's per-response absolute targets make this strategy less effective because the winner's log-ratio is directly constrained to a specific positive value, not just "bigger than the loser's."

### Innovation 3: Synthesizing Game-Theoretic Convergence Guarantees with Practical LLM Fine-Tuning

The theoretical literature on preference-based RL without reward models had established that the exponential weight update (4.1) provably converges to the Nash equilibrium of the two-player constant-sum game (Freund and Schapire, 1999; Munos et al., 2023; Swamy et al., 2024). But these guarantees came with a significant practical limitation: they were either proven only for tabular representations (Munos et al., 2023), required complex policy optimization machinery like PPO or SAC to approximate the exponential update (Swamy et al., 2024), or sampled token-by-token rather than at the response level (Munos et al., 2023). None had been demonstrated at the scale of 7B+ parameter language models with the standard autoregressive fine-tuning stack.

SPPO bridges this gap not by developing new theory — the convergence proof (Theorem 4.1, restating Freund and Schapire, 1999) is included for completeness but is not the paper's theoretical contribution — but by recognizing that the **exponential update can be expressed as a regression target**. The move from "sample from the exponentially reweighted distribution" to "train a model to match log-ratios to win rates via squared loss" is a reduction from sampling to supervised learning. The former is intractable for LLMs (requires enumerating responses or running MCMC in text space); the latter is standard autoregressive fine-tuning with a straightforward loss function.

What makes this intellectually significant beyond the specific algorithm is the **validation that game-theoretic alignment objectives can be made practical through regression-based approximation**. Prior to this work, there was a perceived tradeoff: you could have theoretical soundness (general preferences, Nash equilibrium convergence) or practical scalability (simple losses, standard training pipelines), but not both. DPO and its variants chose practicality by retaining the Bradley-Terry assumption. IPO chose generality but retained the differencing pattern. Munos et al. (2023) chose full generality but accepted tabular execution. Swamy et al. (2024) chose generality and function approximation but required RL infrastructure. SPPO shows that with a single insight — approximate the partition function instead of canceling it, then regress — you can have the theoretical guarantees of the exponential weight framework, the generality of non-transitive preferences, AND the simplicity of a supervised loss.

The theoretical convergence guarantee (Theorem 4.1) is not vacuous window dressing. It states that the uniform mixture of SPPO iterates $\bar{\pi}_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \pi_t$ has a duality gap of $O(1/\sqrt{T})$, meaning it approaches the Nash equilibrium at a known rate. This is a stronger claim than DPO or IPO can make: DPO has no iterative convergence theory at all (it's a single-shot optimization of the Bradley-Terry likelihood), and while IPO has been shown by Calandriello et al. (2024) to solve the Nash equilibrium when applied iteratively (IPO-MD), it lacks the per-response constraint that SPPO provides. The guarantee gives practitioners reason to believe that running more iterations will continue to improve the policy (in a minimax sense), not just overfit to the preference model.

The empirical evidence in Figure 1 supports this: SPPO shows steady improvement across three iterations (+7.69% → +2.10% → +1.64% for Mistral-7B on LC win rate; +8.81% → +3.42% → +3.62% for Llama-3-8B), with no sign of collapse or over-optimization against PairRM. In contrast, iterative DPO and IPO show unstable or declining performance across iterations (Table 1), consistent with methods that lack convergence guarantees and may drift toward exploiting evaluator biases.

### Innovation 4: Revealing That DPO/IPO's Pairwise Loss Structure Is the Root Cause of the "Winner Likelihood Stagnation" Failure Mode

Pal et al. (2024) empirically documented a troubling pattern: DPO often improves alignment scores by making the model produce the *loser* response less often, without making it produce the *winner* response more often. The model learns to avoid bad outputs but not to actively generate good ones. This was presented as an empirical observation — a failure mode to be aware of and possibly mitigate.

SPPO provides the **theoretical diagnosis** for why this happens, and the diagnosis is structural: it's a direct consequence of the pairwise loss formulation. The algebraic analysis in Section 4.5 crystallizes this with exceptional clarity. When the loss function depends only on $a - b$ (the difference between winner and loser log-ratios), the optimization landscape has a flat direction: any pair $(a, b)$ where $a - b = \text{target}$ achieves the same loss. The model can improve by making $b$ more negative (pushing down loser likelihood) without touching $a$ (leaving winner likelihood unchanged). This isn't an implementation bug or a hyperparameter issue — it's the geometry of the loss function itself.

The innovation is not just identifying this failure mode (Pal et al., 2024 did that) but **tracing it to the cancellation of the partition function and providing a principled fix that preserves the theoretical framework**. IPO shares this vulnerability because its loss is $[(a - b) - 1]^2$ — still only a function of the difference. DPO's loss is $-\log\sigma(a - b)$ — also only a function of the difference. The entire family of "difference-based" preference losses, regardless of whether they assume Bradley-Terry or general preferences, is susceptible to this pathology.

SPPO's fix is structural, not heuristic: by fitting each response's log-ratio to its own target $(a - 1/2)^2 + (b + 1/2)^2$, the loss landscape has a unique minimum at $a = 1/2, b = -1/2$. There's no flat direction to exploit. The winner's likelihood must increase; the loser's must decrease. Both are independently constrained.

The significance of this diagnosis extends beyond SPPO. It suggests that future preference optimization methods should be evaluated not just on their aggregate win rates but on **whether they increase the absolute likelihood of preferred responses**. A method that achieves a +5% win rate improvement entirely through loser suppression is fundamentally different from one that achieves it through winner enhancement — even if the aggregate metric looks identical. SPPO gives the field a lens for making this distinction and a concrete algorithmic pattern (per-response targets instead of pairwise gaps) for achieving the better kind of improvement.

The KTO loss (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) independently recognized the importance of constraining individual response scores, and SPPO can be seen as providing the game-theoretic justification for why this matters: in a constant-sum game, the Nash equilibrium policy should satisfy $\pi^*(y|x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp(\eta P(y \succ \pi^*|x))$, which constrains each response's probability individually based on its win rate, not just pairwise gaps. KTO arrived at a similar loss form from prospect theory; SPPO arrives at it from game theory. The convergence of these independent derivations on the same structural insight — individual constraints matter — strengthens the case that it's a genuine advance rather than an incidental tweak.

The empirical evidence in Table 1 supports the diagnosis: iterative DPO's win rate increases from 14.72% (base) to 24.46% (Iter2) but then plateaus at 23.39% (Iter3) while average length continues growing to 2189 characters. This is consistent with a method that's widening the gap through length exploitation rather than genuinely improving winner quality. SPPO's win rate continues improving through Iter3 (31.02%) with more moderate length growth (2163 characters), suggesting winner quality is genuinely increasing.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** All experiments use the **UltraFeedback** dataset (Cui et al., 2023), which contains approximately 60k prompts from diverse sources covering general human instructions. The paper uses **only the prompts** — no pre-existing responses, human preference labels, or GPT-4-generated "gold" responses are incorporated. All responses during training are generated by the current policy in the self-play loop, and all preferences are annotated by PairRM. The prompts are split into three portions (~20k each), with one portion used per iteration to prevent overfitting, following the same protocol as Snorkel (the state-of-the-art iterative DPO model) for fair comparison.

- **Base model(s).** Two instruction-tuned models are used: **Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2** (Jiang et al., 2023a) as the primary base model, and **Llama-3-8B-Instruct** as a stronger starting point for additional experiments. Both are 7–8B parameter models that have already undergone instruction tuning and some preference alignment, testing whether SPPO can push beyond the standard RLHF alignment ceiling. Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 starts at a length-controlled win rate of 17.11% against GPT-4-Turbo on AlpacaEval 2.0; Llama-3-8B-Instruct starts at 22.92%.

- **Metrics.** Five evaluation benchmarks are used:
  - **AlpacaEval 2.0** (Dubois et al., 2024a): 805 prompts from AlpacaFarm, where model responses are compared to GPT-4-Turbo reference responses by a GPT-4-Turbo-based annotator. Reports both raw win rate (%) and **length-controlled (LC) win rate** to account for the known bias of LLM judges toward longer outputs. The LC win rate is the primary metric since it adjusts for verbosity exploitation.
  - **Arena-Hard** (Li et al., 2024): 500 challenging user queries evaluated with the same GPT-4-Turbo judge, designed to have higher correlation with human Chatbot Arena rankings than AlpacaEval 2.0.
  - **MT-Bench** (Zheng et al., 2024): 80 multi-turn open-ended questions covering writing, role-playing, math, coding, etc., scored directly by GPT-4 on a 1–10 scale (no pairwise comparison).
  - **Open LLM Leaderboard** (Beeching et al., 2023a): Six datasets — ARC, HellaSwag, Winogrande, MMLU, TruthfulQA, and GSM8k — measuring reasoning, knowledge, and truthfulness. Average score across all six is reported.
  - **PairRM win rate**: During training and for model selection, the average win rate judged by PairRM-0.4B on a hold-out subset of Ultrafeedback serves as the validation metric.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against:
  - **Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2**: The unmodified base model, serving as the starting point and lower bound.
  - **Snorkel (Mistral-PairRM-DPO)** (Snorkel AI, 2024): A state-of-the-art iterative DPO model obtained by three rounds of DPO fine-tuning from Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, using the same prompts, same preference model (PairRM), and same data splitting. This is the primary competitive baseline.
  - **(Iterative) DPO**: The authors' own reimplementation of iterative DPO, using the same experimental setup and hyperparameter optimization as SPPO but with the DPO loss function (4.13). This ensures the comparison isolates the loss function rather than confounds from data or compute differences.
  - **(Iterative) IPO**: The authors' own reimplementation of iterative IPO (Azar et al., 2023), with the same experimental setup and hyperparameter optimization as SPPO, using the IPO loss (4.14). This is the closest theoretical baseline since IPO also derives from the general preference framework.
  - **Self-Rewarding Language Models** (Yuan et al., 2024): Trained from Llama 2 70B using the LLM itself as a preference judge. Results from the original paper are cited for AlpacaEval 2.0 comparison. Note the substantial model size difference (70B vs. 7B).
  - **Proprietary and larger models**: GPT-4 Turbo, GPT-4 0314, GPT-4 0613, Claude 3 Opus, Claude 2, Gemini Pro, Llama 3 70B Instruct, Mistral Medium, and Mixtral 8×7B are included in the AlpacaEval 2.0 leaderboard comparison (Table 2).

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not report FLOPs directly. Instead, compute is tracked implicitly through:
  - **Number of responses generated per iteration**: For each of the ~20k prompts in an iteration, the current policy generates $K=5$ responses (totaling ~100k generated responses per iteration). Each response is a full autoregressive generation at temperature 1.0.
  - **Number of PairRM queries**: For each prompt, all $K^2 = 25$ pairwise comparisons among the 5 responses are scored by PairRM (0.4B parameters), totaling ~500k preference queries per iteration.
  - **Training compute**: Fine-tuning is performed for 1 epoch on the selected 20k prompt-response pairs (winner + loser per prompt, so ~40k training examples), with batch size 64 on 8×A100 GPUs for 18 total epochs scheduled (though only the first-epoch checkpoint is used).
  - **Total iterations**: $T = 3$, so the above is repeated three times, with different prompt splits each time.
  - **Fairness control**: All baseline methods (iterative DPO, iterative IPO, Snorkel) use the same number of generated responses ($K=5$, with winner/loser selected identically), the same number of training examples per iteration, and the same number of total iterations (3 for DPO/IPO/SPPO). So while FLOPs are not equalized at the token level, the high-level compute budget (prompts × responses × iterations) is matched across methods.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not use k-fold cross-validation for evaluation. Instead:
  - **Model selection**: In each iteration, the best model checkpoint is selected based on the **average win rate judged by PairRM-0.4B** on a hold-out subset of Ultrafeedback prompts (distinct from the training portion for that iteration). This is a form of validation-based early stopping.
  - **Data rotation**: The three-fold prompt split (different 20k prompts per iteration) prevents the model from being evaluated on prompts it was trained on in previous iterations, though the same hold-out validation set may be reused across iterations.
  - **No test-set cross-validation**: The reported results on AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard, MT-Bench, and Open LLM Leaderboard are on fixed standard test sets — there is no cross-validation over test splits. Results are single-point estimates without confidence intervals.
  - **Ablation study**: The effect of mini-batch size $K$ (Section 5.3, Figure 4) is reported as a comparison of two SPPO runs with different $K$, not as an average over multiple seeds.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### AlpacaEval 2.0: SPPO Achieves State-of-the-Art Win Rates with Moderate Length Growth

The headline result (Table 1) is that **Mistral-7B-SPPO Iter3 achieves a length-controlled win rate of 28.53% and a raw win rate of 31.02% against GPT-4-Turbo**, substantially outperforming all baselines trained from the same base model with the same preference model:

- **vs. Base model (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2)**: LC win rate improves from 17.11% to 28.53% (+11.42 percentage points). Raw win rate improves from 14.72% to 31.02% (+16.30 points).
- **vs. Snorkel (iterative DPO, the best publicly available baseline)**: LC win rate 28.53% vs. 26.39% (+2.14 points). Raw win rate 31.02% vs. 30.22% (+0.80 points). While Snorkel achieves a comparable raw win rate, its average response length is 2736 characters vs. SPPO's 2163 — a 26% increase — suggesting Snorkel's raw win rate is inflated by length exploitation.
- **vs. Iterative DPO (authors' reimplementation)**: SPPO Iter3 LC win rate 28.53% vs. DPO Iter2's best LC win rate of 24.23% (+4.30 points). DPO's LC win rate actually declines from Iter2 (24.23%) to Iter3 (22.30%), while SPPO improves steadily across all three iterations.
- **vs. Iterative IPO (authors' reimplementation)**: SPPO Iter3 LC win rate 28.53% vs. IPO Iter1's best LC win rate of 23.78% (+4.75 points). IPO's LC win rate declines sharply from Iter1 (23.78%) to Iter3 (20.06%), and its average length explodes to 2760 characters at Iter3 — the longest of any method.
- **vs. Self-Rewarding 70B**: SPPO's raw win rate of 31.02% exceeds Self-Rewarding 70B Iter3's 20.44% by 10.58 points, despite SPPO using a 7B model vs. Self-Rewarding's 70B model.

The **per-iteration improvement pattern** (Figure 1) shows SPPO gaining steadily: +7.69% LC win rate from base to Iter1, +2.10% from Iter1 to Iter2, and +1.64% from Iter2 to Iter3. The diminishing returns are consistent with convergence toward the Nash equilibrium (Theorem 4.1 guarantees $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ convergence of the duality gap). In contrast, iterative DPO shows 23.81% → 24.23% → 22.30%, and iterative IPO shows 23.78% → 21.08% → 20.06%, indicating these methods do not monotonically improve with additional iterations.

**Best-of-16 re-ranking** at test time (using PairRM to select the best among 16 generated responses) provides consistent additional gains: SPPO Iter3 improves from 28.53% to 32.13% LC win rate (+3.60 points). This incremental improvement is comparable to the gains seen for the base model (+5.34 points, from 17.11% to 22.45%) and Snorkel (+3.57 points, from 26.39% to 29.97%), suggesting SPPO has **not over-optimized against PairRM** — the preference model still provides a useful signal for re-ranking at test time, rather than being exploited during training.

**Starting from a stronger base model (Llama-3-8B-Instruct)** amplifies the gains (Table 1, bottom): **Llama-3-8B-SPPO Iter3 achieves 38.77% LC win rate and 39.85% raw win rate**, an improvement of +15.85 LC points over the base Llama-3-8B-Instruct (22.92%). The per-iteration gains are larger in absolute terms: +8.81% (Iter1), +3.42% (Iter2), +3.62% (Iter3). This suggests SPPO's effectiveness scales with the base model's capability — a stronger starting policy generates higher-quality responses for the self-play loop, enabling larger improvements.

**Leaderboard comparison (Table 2)** positions SPPO among much larger and proprietary models:
- Mistral-7B-SPPO Iter3 (28.53% LC) outperforms Claude 2 (28.2%), Gemini Pro (24.4%), Mistral 8×7B v0.1 (23.7%), and Llama 3 8B Instruct (22.9%).
- Mistral-7B-SPPO Iter3 with best-of-16 (32.13% LC) is competitive with GPT-4 0613 (30.2%), Claude 3 Opus (40.5%), and Llama 3 70B Instruct (34.4%).
- Llama-3-8B-SPPO Iter3 (38.77% LC) surpasses GPT-4 0314 (35.3%) and approaches Claude 3 Opus (40.5%).
- All SPPO models are 7–8B parameters, while competing models at similar performance levels are 70B+ or proprietary models of unknown size.

**Response length analysis (Table 1, "Avg. Len" column):**
- Base Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2: 1676 characters
- SPPO Iter3: 2163 characters (+29% vs. base)
- DPO Iter3: 2189 characters (+31% vs. base)
- IPO Iter3: 2760 characters (+65% vs. base)
- Snorkel: 2736 characters (+63% vs. base)

SPPO's length increase is moderate compared to DPO/IPO variants, yet its win rate improvement is larger. This is consistent with the theoretical argument (Section 4.5) that pairwise losses achieve their gap targets through any combination of winner/loser likelihood changes — including making winners longer (exploiting length bias) without improving content. SPPO's per-response absolute targets constrain this exploitation path.

- **Within each method, newer iterations beat older iterations**: SPPO Iter3 beats SPPO Iter2 (0.631), IPO Iter3 beats IPO Iter2 (0.552), etc.
- **SPPO and IPO consistently outperform DPO**: SPPO Iter1 achieves a 0.656 win rate against DPO Iter1; IPO Iter1 achieves 0.719 against DPO Iter1. This pattern holds across all iteration comparisons.
- **IPO Iter3 achieves the highest overall PairRM win rates**, slightly edging out SPPO Iter3 in direct comparison (IPO Iter3 vs. SPPO Iter3: the heatmap cell is not explicitly stated, but IPO Iter3's row shows generally higher win rates against other models than SPPO Iter3's row).

However, the paper identifies a critical confound: **PairRM may favor models with longer outputs**, and IPO Iter3 has the longest average response length of any model (2760 characters, Table 1). On benchmarks with more powerful judge models like GPT-4-Turbo (AlpacaEval 2.0 LC, MT-Bench, Arena-Hard), SPPO significantly outperforms IPO. This suggests IPO's superior PairRM win rates are partially or largely attributable to exploiting length bias in the smaller preference model, while SPPO's regularization (through the multiplicative weight update framework and per-response absolute targets) prevents this exploitation. This is a **central piece of evidence** that SPPO's robustness to reward hacking (verifier over-optimization) is not just theoretical but empirically consequential.

#### Summary of Quantitative Patterns

1. **SPPO consistently outperforms iterative DPO and IPO on all GPT-4-judged benchmarks** (AlpacaEval 2.0 LC, MT-Bench, Arena-Hard), with the margin being largest on length-controlled metrics that penalize verbosity exploitation.
2. **SPPO shows monotonic or near-monotonic improvement across iterations** on alignment benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard), while DPO and IPO show plateauing or declining performance.
3. **SPPO's gains are robust to base model quality**: the method improves both Mistral-7B (+11.42 LC points) and Llama-3-8B (+15.85 LC points), with larger absolute gains from the stronger base model.
4. **On knowledge/reasoning benchmarks** (Open LLM Leaderboard), SPPO shows modest improvements initially but experiences the alignment tax — performance peaks at Iter2 for Mistral-7B and declines slightly at Iter3, though the decline is less severe than for DPO/IPO.
5. **PairRM judging alone overstates IPO's performance** relative to SPPO, likely due to length bias in the smaller preference model — a finding that validates SPPO's theoretical robustness to verifier exploitation.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Mini-batch size for win rate estimation ($K$)**: Figure 4 compares SPPO with $K=2$ vs. $K=5$ responses for estimating $\hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ on AlpacaEval 2.0. With $K=2$, the win rate is estimated from only the winner and loser: $\hat{P}(y_w \succ \pi_t|x) = 1/2 + P(y_w \succ y_l|x)/2$. The results show:
- $K=5$ achieves slightly higher Iter1 LC win rate (24.79% vs. 23.85%), but the gap narrows in subsequent iterations (Iter3: 28.53% for $K=5$ vs. 28.26% for $K=2$).
- $K=2$ produces more moderate length growth (1961 chars at Iter3 vs. 2163 for $K=5$).
- The finding is that SPPO is **robust to noise in win rate estimation** — even coarse estimates from a single preference pair (with the win rate = 0.5 + half the pairwise probability) provide sufficient signal for effective training. This robustness is important for practical deployment where $O(K^2)$ preference queries may be expensive.

**Method comparison at fixed generation budget**: While not a formal ablation, the experimental design controls for compute by using identical generation budgets (K=5 responses per prompt, one winner/loser pair selected for training, three iterations, same data splits) across SPPO, DPO, and IPO. This ensures the performance differences are attributable to the loss function rather than differences in data quantity or generation budget.

**Best-of-16 re-ranking as over-optimization check**: The consistent ~3.6% improvement from best-of-16 re-ranking across SPPO iterations (Table 1: Iter1 +3.92%, Iter2 +4.34%, Iter3 +3.60%) and the similar gains for the base model (+5.34%) and Snorkel (+3.57%) indicate that SPPO has not overfit to PairRM during training. If SPPO had gamed the preference model, re-ranking with the same model would provide diminishing or zero benefit. The persistent re-ranking gain suggests PairRM still captures genuine preference signal not fully exploited by the policy.

**Llama-3-8B-Instruct as a robustness check**: Replicating the full SPPO procedure on a different base model family (Llama-3 vs. Mistral) with different training data and instruction-tuning procedure and obtaining consistent gains (+15.85 LC points) provides evidence that SPPO's effectiveness is not specific to a particular base model.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: SPPO achieves state-of-the-art length-controlled win rates against GPT-4-Turbo, outperforming iterative DPO and IPO.** The evidence strongly supports this for the specific experimental configuration tested. Mistral-7B-SPPO Iter3 achieves 28.53% LC win rate vs. 24.23% for the best iterative DPO and 23.78% for the best iterative IPO (Table 1). The improvements are consistent across iterations (Figure 1), hold on multiple GPT-4-judged benchmarks (MT-Bench: 7.59 vs. 7.09 DPO; Arena-Hard: 23.3 vs. 20.7 Snorkel), and replicate on a different base model (Llama-3-8B-SPPO: 38.77% LC). The evidence is robust across the specific benchmarks and models tested.

**However**, the claim is demonstrated only on models fine-tuned from two specific base models (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 and Llama-3-8B-Instruct), using a single preference model (PairRM-0.4B), and evaluated on a fixed set of benchmarks. The generalizability to other model families (e.g., Qwen, Gemma, Yi), other preference models (e.g., UltraRM-13B, GPT-4-as-judge during training), other prompt distributions, or production deployment settings is untested. The paper's title and abstract present SPPO as a general method for "Language Model Alignment," but the experimental validation covers a narrower scope: improving instruction-tuned 7–8B models on general chat benchmarks using a specific 0.4B preference model. Whether SPPO works as well with larger preference models (which might have different bias structures) or on domain-specific alignment tasks is unknown.

**Claim 2: SPPO provably converges to the Nash equilibrium of the two-player game.** Theorem 4.1 provides a theoretical guarantee for the idealized exponential weight update, but the practical SPPO algorithm introduces three layers of approximation: (1) replacing the exact win rate $P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ with an empirical estimate from $K=5$ samples, (2) replacing the partition function $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ with the constant $\eta/2$, and (3) minimizing the regression loss (4.6) using finite gradient steps rather than exactly solving the optimization. None of these approximations are accounted for in the convergence proof. The experimental evidence of monotonic improvement across three iterations is consistent with convergence, but three iterations is far from the asymptotic regime ($T \to \infty$) where the $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ rate becomes meaningful. Moreover, the deviation between PairRM judging (where IPO Iter3 looks best) and GPT-4 judging (where SPPO Iter3 looks best) suggests that different approximations to the Nash equilibrium can rank differently depending on the judge — a phenomenon not captured by the theory, which assumes a fixed, ground-truth preference oracle.

**Claim 3: SPPO's per-response absolute constraints (pushing winner log-ratio to +1/2 and loser to −1/2) avoid the failure mode where DPO/IPO only widen the gap between winner and loser without increasing winner likelihood.** This claim rests on the algebraic analysis in Section 4.5 and the empirical observation that SPPO achieves higher win rates with less length exploitation than DPO/IPO. However, the paper does not directly measure whether the absolute likelihood of winning responses increases under SPPO vs. DPO/IPO — it infers this from final win rates and response lengths. A direct measurement would require tracking the log-probability of specific winning responses across iterations under different methods, which the paper does not do. The length-control analysis provides circumstantial evidence (SPPO's moderate length growth vs. IPO's explosion suggests less exploitation of length bias), but length is only one confound among many potential preference model biases. Without direct likelihood measurements, the claim that SPPO fixes the "winner likelihood stagnation" problem is a plausible interpretation supported by consistent patterns, but not directly verified.

**A missing experiment that would have substantially strengthened the paper:** Training SPPO for more than three iterations to observe whether (a) performance continues to improve, plateaus, or eventually degrades, and (b) whether the convergence pattern matches the $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ theoretical prediction. Three iterations showing diminishing but positive gains (+7.69% → +2.10% → +1.64% for Mistral-7B) is suggestive but insufficient to distinguish convergence from temporary plateau. Similarly, running the iterative DPO and IPO baselines under identical conditions but with the same number of gradient steps and data budget would more cleanly isolate the loss function effect — currently, the authors re-tune hyperparameters for DPO/IPO using PairRM win rate as the objective, which could advantage SPPO if PairRM win rate is a better validation metric for SPPO-like objectives.

**Another missing experiment:** Evaluating whether SPPO genuinely increases winner likelihood by measuring the log-probability assigned to held-out winning responses before and after each iteration, compared to DPO and IPO baselines. This would directly test the core theoretical claim and provide mechanistic insight beyond win rate metrics.

**A caveat on the best-of-16 results:** Table 2 places Mistral-7B-SPPO Iter3 (best-of-16) at 32.13% LC win rate, ahead of GPT-4 0613 (30.2%). However, GPT-4 0613 is presumably evaluated with a single response (greedy or single sample), not best-of-16. The inference-time compute budgets are not equalized — SPPO uses 16× more generation and extensive re-ranking. This is noted in the table but occasionally overlooked in discussion: the SPPO model itself (single response) achieves 28.53%, below GPT-4 0613's 30.2%. The "competitive with GPT-4" framing applies only when augmenting SPPO with additional test-time compute that GPT-4 is not given.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 Convergence Theory Assumes Exact Exponential Updates; the Practical Algorithm Introduces Three Layers of Unquantified Approximation Error

**The assumption or constraint.** Theorem 4.1 proves that the idealized exponential weight update (4.1), applied exactly, produces a mixture policy whose duality gap converges to zero at rate $O(1/\sqrt{T})$. This proof assumes (a) the exact win rate $P(y \succ \pi_t|x)$ is available, (b) the partition function $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ is known, and (c) the policy update is solved to optimality each round. The practical SPPO algorithm (Algorithm 1) replaces all three with approximations: win rates are estimated from $K=5$ samples per prompt, $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ is replaced by the constant $\eta/2$, and the optimization (4.6) is solved approximately via gradient descent with early stopping after 1 epoch. The paper is explicit about the first approximation (Section 4.1: "Informally speaking, when $K \to \infty$, (4.5) will recover (4.4)"), and justifies the second via Appendix A, but **none of these approximation errors appear in the convergence bound**. Theorem 4.1 is a statement about an idealized algorithm, not about Algorithm 1 as actually run.

**The consequence.** A practitioner cannot use Theorem 4.1 to predict how many iterations of real SPPO are needed to reach a desired performance level, nor to guarantee that running more iterations will continue to improve the policy rather than overfit or oscillate. The three approximation errors interact in unknown ways: finite-sample win rate estimation introduces variance that could cause the policy to chase noise in the preference signal; the constant partition function approximation could bias the update direction if the true soft value varies substantially across prompts (which it certainly does — some prompts are inherently harder to win at than others); and approximate optimization could leave slack that compounds across iterations. The empirical observation that SPPO improves monotonically across three iterations (Figure 1, Table 1) is consistent with convergence but does not validate the rate or guarantee continued improvement. At $T=3$ iterations, the $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ bound is loose — the asymptotic regime hasn't been reached — and the practical algorithm may diverge or plateau at larger $T$.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The only evidence is the per-iteration trajectory on AlpacaEval 2.0 (Table 1, Figure 1): for Mistral-7B, LC win rate improves +7.69% (Iter1) → +2.10% (Iter2) → +1.64% (Iter3). For Llama-3-8B, the pattern is +8.81% → +3.42% → +3.62%. The diminishing gains are consistent with convergence, but three data points are insufficient to distinguish genuine convergence from temporary plateau or to estimate a convergence rate. The paper does not report results beyond 3 iterations, nor does it evaluate how the duality gap (the quantity bounded by Theorem 4.1) evolves across iterations. The Open LLM Leaderboard results (Table 3) show that for Mistral-7B, SPPO Iter2 (66.75) slightly outperforms Iter3 (66.40), suggesting that on some metrics, additional iterations can degrade performance — a phenomenon not captured by the theory which guarantees monotonic improvement of the mixture policy in game-theoretic terms, not monotonic improvement on all downstream benchmarks.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this gap between theory and practice beyond noting that the approximations become exact as $K \to \infty$ (Section 4.1) and providing the heuristic justification for $\eta/2$ in Appendix A. No error analysis, finite-sample bound, or empirical convergence diagnostic is provided. The limitation is partially acknowledged implicitly by the empirical focus — the paper's main claims are supported by benchmark results, not by invoking Theorem 4.1 — but the theoretical guarantee is presented prominently (Section 1: "provably approximate the Nash equilibrium"; Section 4.1: Theorem 4.1) without caveats about the gap between the analyzed and implemented algorithms. A practitioner who reads the theorem and expects provable convergence from the code as written would be misled.

---

### 6.2 All Gains Are Measured Against a Single Preference Model; There Is No Evidence of Transfer to Human Preferences or Alternative Judges

**The assumption or constraint.** The entire SPPO training loop — response generation, win rate estimation, model selection, and hyperparameter tuning — depends on a single preference oracle: **PairRM-0.4B** (Jiang et al., 2023b). The paper explicitly states: "SPPO can obtain a model from fine-tuning Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 that achieves the state-of-the-art length-controlled win-rate of 28.53% against GPT-4-Turbo on AlpacaEval 2.0" (Abstract), where the evaluation judge is GPT-4-Turbo, but all training signal comes from PairRM. The alignment target is therefore PairRM's approximation of human preferences, not human preferences directly.

**The consequence.** This creates a **transfer gap**: the policy is optimized to win against PairRM's judgments, and its performance on GPT-4-Turbo or human evaluation depends entirely on how well PairRM's preferences correlate with those of the downstream judge. The paper provides evidence of a specific failure mode of this transfer: Figure 3 shows that under PairRM judging, **IPO Iter3 appears superior to SPPO Iter3**, but on GPT-4-Turbo-judged benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0 LC, MT-Bench, Arena-Hard), **SPPO significantly outperforms IPO**. The paper's own interpretation (Section 5.2) is that IPO exploits length bias in PairRM — IPO Iter3 produces responses averaging 2760 characters, the longest of any model — and that PairRM over-rewards this verbosity relative to GPT-4-Turbo. This demonstrates that **optimizing against one preference model can produce policies that look good to that model but not to others**. The converse risk is equally plausible: SPPO may be under-optimizing certain qualities that PairRM undervalues relative to human preferences, and we would not detect this from the reported benchmarks alone — we would need human evaluation or a diverse panel of preference judges.

More subtly, the model selection procedure (choosing the best checkpoint per iteration based on PairRM win rate on a hold-out set) means that every design choice in SPPO — the number of iterations, the learning rate $\eta$, the early stopping epoch — is selected to maximize PairRM's approval. If PairRM has systematic biases (e.g., favoring certain response styles, penalizing appropriate caution, over-rewarding confident tone), SPPO will amplify these biases iteratively. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by noting the alignment tax on Open LLM Leaderboard (Table 3) and attributing it to "aligning with human preferences (simulated by PairRM preference in our study) might not improve or even hurt the general performance." But the deeper issue is that we don't know which aspects of SPPO's improvements are genuine alignment (would transfer to human judges) and which are PairRM-specific exploitation (would evaporate under a different judge). The best-of-16 re-ranking analysis (Table 1) provides partial evidence against severe over-optimization — re-ranking with PairRM at test time still provides ~3.6% improvement, suggesting the model hasn't fully gamed PairRM — but this only checks for one failure mode (reward hacking in the narrow sense). It doesn't validate that the improvements are human-meaningful.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports results on multiple GPT-4-Turbo-judged benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-Bench, Arena-Hard), which provides some cross-judge validation. The finding that SPPO outperforms IPO on GPT-4 benchmarks despite underperforming on PairRM benchmarks (Figure 3 vs. Table 1) is evidence that SPPO transfers better, at least relative to IPO. However, there is **no human evaluation** — no study with human raters comparing SPPO outputs to baselines — and no evaluation using preference models other than PairRM (during training) or GPT-4-Turbo (during evaluation). All claims about "alignment" rest on the assumption that GPT-4-Turbo's judgments are a good proxy for human preferences, which is the standard assumption in the LLM evaluation literature but is itself contested (e.g., Dubois et al., 2024a developed length-controlled evaluation specifically because GPT-4 judges exhibit systematic biases).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation directly. There is no human evaluation, no evaluation with alternative training preference models (e.g., UltraRM-13B, a GPT-4-based judge), and no analysis of how SPPO's performance varies with preference model quality. The choice to use PairRM is justified on efficiency grounds (Section 5.1: "a balance between accuracy and efficiency"), but the paper does not investigate whether the SPPO method is robust to the choice of preference model or whether better preference models would yield better or different alignment outcomes. The gap between PairRM and GPT-4 judging (Figure 3 vs. Table 1) is noted as an empirical observation — the authors "believe this is due to IPO exploiting the length bias in PairRM" — but is not framed as evidence of a fundamental limitation of single-judge alignment.

---

### 6.3 The Alignment Tax: Knowledge, Reasoning, and Truthfulness Degrade Under Preference Optimization

**The assumption or constraint.** SPPO, like all preference optimization methods tested in the paper, **improves alignment metrics at the cost of degrading performance on factual knowledge, reasoning, and truthfulness benchmarks**. The paper explicitly acknowledges this (Section 5.2):

> "This limitation may be attributed to the 'alignment tax' phenomenon (Askell et al., 2021), which suggests that aligning with human preferences (simulated by PairRM preference in our study) might not improve or even hurt the general performance."

The implicit assumption is that this tradeoff is acceptable or that the alignment gains on chat/instruction-following benchmarks outweigh the capability losses on knowledge benchmarks.

**The consequence.** For practitioners deploying aligned models in applications that require both helpfulness AND factual accuracy (e.g., medical QA, legal advice, educational tutoring), Table 3 reveals a concrete risk: SPPO fine-tuning of Mistral-7B increases the Open LLM Leaderboard average from 65.74 (base) to 66.75 (Iter2) but then declines to 66.40 (Iter3). While this is a net improvement for SPPO, the trajectory is concerning: further iterations might continue the decline. More importantly, **all methods eventually degrade**: DPO Iter3 drops to 64.52 (below base), IPO Iter3 drops to 64.72 (below base). SPPO's degradation is milder than DPO/IPO's, but the pattern is the same — preference optimization eventually hurts capabilities.

The specific capabilities affected vary by base model. For Mistral-7B, SPPO Iter3 vs. base shows: ARC +1.71, TruthfulQA +3.12, Winogrande -1.18, GSM8k +0.75, HellaSwag +0.27, MMLU -0.70. The losses on Winogrande (commonsense reasoning) and MMLU (massive multitask knowledge) are modest but real. For Llama-3-8B, the pattern is different: ARC +2.90, TruthfulQA +6.39, Winogrande +1.02, GSM8k -0.98, HellaSwag +2.13, MMLU +0.01. Here GSM8k (grade-school math) uniquely degrades. This inconsistency — different capabilities degrade for different base models — means a practitioner cannot predict *which* capabilities will be sacrificed without running the experiment on their specific model and task distribution.

The underlying mechanism is straightforward: PairRM is trained to judge response quality along dimensions like helpfulness, coherence, and harmlessness, but not factual accuracy per se. A response that is confidently wrong but well-written and helpful-sounding may receive a higher PairRM score than a response that is accurate but terse or uncertain. By optimizing against this signal, SPPO amplifies the qualities PairRM rewards and may suppress qualities PairRM is indifferent to — including truthfulness. The improvement on TruthfulQA (+3.12 for Mistral-7B) suggests SPPO doesn't universally hurt truthfulness, but the decline on MMLU and Winogrande suggests knowledge-intensive reasoning suffers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides comprehensive evidence across six diverse benchmarks for both Mistral-7B and Llama-3-8B. The key numbers:

- **Mistral-7B-SPPO**: Iter1 66.67, Iter2 66.75 (peak), Iter3 66.40. The peak at Iter2 means the alignment tax begins between iterations 2 and 3.
- **Mistral-7B-DPO**: Iter1 65.62, Iter2 65.38, Iter3 64.52. Monotonic decline; Iter3 below base (65.74).
- **Mistral-7B-IPO**: Iter1 66.52, Iter2 64.66, Iter3 64.72. Sharp decline after Iter1; Iter3 below base.
- **Llama-3-8B-SPPO**: Iter1 69.35, Iter2 69.91, Iter3 70.29. Monotonic improvement. The alignment tax has not yet appeared by Iter3 for this base model.

The contrast between Mistral-7B (tax appears at Iter3) and Llama-3-8B (no tax through Iter3) is informative but unexplained. It could be that Llama-3-8B-Instruct's stronger initial capabilities provide a larger buffer before preference optimization starts degrading knowledge, or that PairRM's preferences are better aligned with Llama-3's output distribution, or simply that three iterations is not enough to observe the tax for Llama-3.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly proposes one mitigation:

> "Improving language model capabilities through alignment iterations remains a topic for future research, and we posit that incorporating high-quality SFT annotations (Chen et al., 2024) could play a significant role in this endeavor."

This is a forward-looking suggestion, not an implemented solution. The paper does not experiment with mixing SFT data into the SPPO training, nor with any form of capability-preserving regularization (e.g., KL penalty to the original base model rather than to the previous iteration's policy, or multi-objective training that combines PairRM win rate with downstream task performance). The three-fold data splitting (different prompts per iteration) likely helps by ensuring the model sees diverse prompts, but it does not directly address catastrophic forgetting of factual knowledge. A practitioner who needs both alignment gains and knowledge retention would need to develop their own mitigation strategy, as the paper provides no recipe beyond the suggestion to incorporate SFT data.

### 6.4 The Partition Function Approximation $\eta/2$ Is a Heuristic Whose Failure Mode Is Uncharacterized

**The assumption or constraint.** The SPPO loss (4.6) replaces the intractable log-partition function $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ with the constant $\eta/2$. Appendix A justifies this by analyzing two extreme cases: (1) maximum disorder, where all pairwise preferences are independent fair coin flips, yielding $Z \to e^{\eta/2}$; and (2) perfect ordering, where responses are strictly ranked, yielding $Z \to (e^\eta - 1)/\eta \approx e^\eta$ for large $\eta$. The authors choose $\eta/2$ because "this paper is partially motivated by human intransitive and irrational preference behavior," implying real preferences are closer to the disordered case. However, this is a **qualitative argument, not a quantitative bound**. The paper does not measure how close $\eta/2$ is to the true $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ in practice, how this gap varies across prompts or iterations, or whether errors in this approximation cause systematic biases in the learned policy.

**The consequence.** If the true $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ varies substantially across prompts — which it should, since some prompts admit responses that consistently win (high $Z$) while others have responses with uniformly near-0.5 win rates (low $Z$) — then using a constant approximation introduces **prompt-dependent bias** in the learning targets. For a prompt where $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x) \gg \eta/2$ (the true partition function is large, meaning the current policy already covers some high-win-rate responses), the SPPO target $\eta(\hat{P} - 1/2)$ is **too large** relative to the correct target $\eta\hat{P} - \log Z$. This would cause SPPO to push up the probabilities of winning responses more aggressively than the exponential update prescribes, potentially leading to overfitting or mode collapse on those prompts. Conversely, for prompts where $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x) \ll \eta/2$, the target is too small, causing under-updating.

The policy gradient interpretation (Section 4.3) partially mitigates this concern — in policy gradient, a constant baseline introduces variance but not bias in the gradient expectation — but only if the expectation is taken over the correct sampling distribution. SPPO's semi-online procedure (sampling from $\pi_t$, then taking multiple gradient steps) breaks the standard policy gradient assumptions, and the bias properties are unclear.

More practically, the choice between $\eta/2$ and alternative constants (e.g., $\eta$, corresponding to the perfectly ordered case) could significantly affect training dynamics. The paper acknowledges this (Appendix A): "Fine-tuning the coefficient of this constant as a hyperparameter is also an option and can help improve performance on given dataset." But making this a tuned hyperparameter would mean the partition function approximation is effectively absorbing unknown properties of the preference model and prompt distribution, making the method less principled than it appears.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** No experiment measures $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ or compares the constant $\eta/2$ approximation to alternative approximations (e.g., estimating $Z$ from samples, learning a value function, or using a per-prompt baseline). The ablation study (Figure 4) compares $K=2$ vs. $K=5$ for win rate estimation but does not vary the partition function treatment. The strong empirical results suggest that $\eta/2$ works adequately in practice, but they don't tell us whether a better approximation would yield further gains or whether the constant approximation is a hidden source of variance that limits scaling to more iterations or larger models. The divergence between PairRM and GPT-4 judging (Figure 3 vs. Table 1) could partially reflect prompt-dependent biases introduced by the constant approximation, but this is speculative without direct measurement.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to validate or improve the partition function approximation beyond the theoretical analysis in Appendix A. The suggestion to treat the coefficient as a tunable hyperparameter acknowledges the limitation but does not resolve it — it effectively concedes that $\eta/2$ is a heuristic starting point. A more principled approach (e.g., estimating $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ from the same $K$ samples used for win rate estimation, or training a lightweight value function alongside the policy) is not explored.

### 6.5 Single Preference Model, Single Prompt Distribution, Two Base Models: Limited Evidence for Generalization

**The assumption or constraint.** The experimental validation of SPPO covers a narrow slice of the possible design space: **one preference model** (PairRM-0.4B), **one prompt dataset** (UltraFeedback, 60k prompts), **two base models** from related size/architecture families (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 and Llama-3-8B-Instruct), and **one task domain** (general instruction-following/chat). The paper's title — "Self-Play Preference Optimization for Language Model Alignment" — presents SPPO as a general alignment method, but the evidence for generality is limited. The most direct statement of scope appears in Section 6 (Conclusions):

> "The experiments are run on one dataset UltraFeedback and the models are tested on a few benchmarks due to limited computational resources, but the proposed methods can be further validated on more models, datasets, and benchmarks to have a holistic evaluation given more resources."

**The consequence.** Several important generalization questions are unanswered:

1. **How does SPPO perform with different preference models?** PairRM is relatively small (0.4B) and based on DeBERTa-V3. Would SPPO work better or worse with larger preference models (UltraRM-13B, GPT-4-as-judge), with ensemble judges, or with actual human feedback? The paper's choice of PairRM is motivated by efficiency, but if SPPO's effectiveness depends on PairRM-specific properties (its particular bias structure, its calibration, its non-transitivity characteristics), then the method may not transfer to other preference oracles.

2. **How does SPPO scale with model size?** All experiments use 7–8B parameter models. The method's computational cost — generating $K=5$ responses per prompt, scoring $O(K^2)$ pairs with a 0.4B judge, and fine-tuning for one epoch — is manageable at this scale. For 70B+ models, generation cost dominates, and the self-play loop (which requires generation from the *current* policy, not offline data) becomes substantially more expensive. Whether the gains are worth the cost at larger scales, and whether the hyperparameters ($\eta=1000$, learning rate, number of iterations) transfer, is unknown.

3. **Does SPPO work for domain-specific alignment?** UltraFeedback contains general human instructions. For specialized domains (medical, legal, code, math), the preference structure may differ — e.g., factual accuracy may dominate over stylistic qualities that PairRM rewards. The alignment tax observed on Open LLM Leaderboard (Table 3) might be more severe in domains where PairRM's preferences are poorly aligned with task success.

4. **How sensitive is SPPO to prompt distribution?** If the deployment prompt distribution differs from UltraFeedback's distribution (which skews toward general chat), the difficulty estimation and win-rate targets learned during training may not transfer. The three-fold splitting within UltraFeedback provides some distribution shift within the same dataset, but does not test out-of-distribution generalization.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The replication across two base models (Mistral-7B and Llama-3-8B) is the primary evidence for generalization. The fact that SPPO improves both models, with consistent per-iteration gains (Table 1), suggests the method is not brittle to the choice of base model — at least within the 7–8B instruction-tuned regime. The evaluation across five diverse benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-Bench, Arena-Hard, Open LLM Leaderboard, PairRM win rates) provides evidence that improvements transfer across evaluation protocols, though all GPT-4-based evaluations share the same judge model family. However, all training uses PairRM, so the transfer from PairRM training to GPT-4 evaluation is tested only for this specific (PairRM, GPT-4-Turbo) pair. There is no evidence for transfer from PairRM to human judges, or from other preference models during training.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation in Section 6 (quoted above) and frames it as a resource constraint rather than a fundamental limitation. This is fair — running SPPO on multiple datasets, with multiple preference models, at multiple scales, with human evaluation, would be a multi-paper research program, not a single study. But the acknowledgment does not change the fact that a practitioner considering SPPO for a specific application with a different preference model, different base model size, or different domain cannot predict performance from the reported results alone. They would need to run their own validation.

### 6.6 Generation and Preference Annotation Costs Are Substantial and Not Amortized Over the Reported Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** Each SPPO iteration requires: (1) generating $K=5$ responses from the current policy for each of ~20k prompts (100k total generations), (2) scoring all $O(K^2) = 25$ pairwise comparisons per prompt with PairRM (500k preference queries), and (3) fine-tuning the 7B model for 1 epoch (18 epochs scheduled, early stopping at epoch 1). This is repeated for $T=3$ iterations, totaling ~300k policy generations and ~1.5M PairRM queries. The paper reports performance improvements relative to the base model and baselines, but **does not compare methods at equalized total compute cost**. The baselines (iterative DPO, iterative IPO) use the same generation budget by design (same $K$, same number of iterations, same data splits), so the comparison across methods is fair. However, the absolute cost is not compared to alternatives that might achieve similar gains with less compute — e.g., training on more offline preference data, using a larger base model with fewer iterations, or using a single round of DPO with more/better preference pairs.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deciding whether to adopt SPPO needs to weigh the alignment gains against the computational cost. The headline improvement — Mistral-7B-SPPO Iter3 achieves 28.53% LC win rate vs. 17.11% for the base model (+11.42 points) — comes at the cost of three full self-play iterations. Could similar gains be achieved by simply using a larger base model (e.g., Mixtral 8×7B with 46.7B parameters, which scores 23.7% LC win rate according to Table 2) and doing less (or no) preference optimization? Could a single round of DPO on more carefully curated preference data match SPPO's performance? The paper's FLOPs-matched analysis is limited to comparing SPPO against iterative DPO/IPO under matched generation budgets — it does not provide the kind of cost-benefit analysis that would guide practical deployment decisions.

The cost structure has specific practical implications:

- **Online generation requirement:** SPPO requires generating responses from the *current* policy $\pi_t$, not from a fixed dataset. This means the full model must be loaded and run for inference between each training iteration. For very large models (70B+), this inference cost may dominate the total compute budget, making iterative self-play substantially more expensive than methods that can reuse previously generated responses or train purely offline.
- **Pairwise annotation scaling:** The $O(K^2)$ preference queries per prompt is manageable for $K=5$ (25 queries) and PairRM-0.4B (fast inference), but would be expensive for larger $K$ or more expensive preference models (e.g., using GPT-4 as a judge during training, as some concurrent work does). The ablation (Figure 4) showing that $K=2$ works almost as well as $K=5$ mitigates this somewhat, but $K=2$ still requires generating responses and running pairwise comparisons.
- **Iteration count:** Three iterations were chosen, but it's unclear whether this is optimal or simply what was computationally feasible. Running 5 or 10 iterations might yield further gains (consistent with the convergence theory) or might hit diminishing returns or degradation (consistent with the alignment tax on Open LLM Leaderboard). The paper provides no cost-vs-iteration analysis.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None directly. The paper does not report total FLOPs, GPU-hours, or wall-clock time for SPPO training. It does not include comparisons at equalized total compute (e.g., giving DPO/IPO more training data or more iterations to match SPPO's total compute budget). The experiments control for generation budget per iteration (same $K$, same number of prompts) but do not account for the possibility that different methods might need different amounts of compute to reach their best performance. The paper also does not evaluate whether the gains from SPPO justify the cost relative to simpler alternatives like "use a larger base model and do less fine-tuning" or "collect more offline preference data and run one round of DPO."

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper's goal is to demonstrate that SPPO *works* and *outperforms comparable methods under matched conditions* — it achieves both. Whether SPPO is *cost-effective* in an absolute sense is a separate question that the paper leaves to practitioners and future work. This is a reasonable scope limitation for a methods paper, but it is a consequential unknown for deployment decisions.

## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

SPPO delivers a precise, surgically-targeted intervention in the preference optimization landscape, not a paradigm shift. The field's conceptual framework — general preferences require game-theoretic solutions, not scalar reward maximization — was already established by Munos et al. (2023), Swamy et al. (2024), and Azar et al. (2023). SPPO's contribution is to demonstrate that **this framework can be made practical for large-scale LLM fine-tuning through a single design insight: approximate the partition function with a constant rather than canceling it by differencing.**

The magnitude of this shift is best understood as **resolving a specific friction that prevented game-theoretic alignment from being adopted at scale**. Prior to SPPO, a practitioner who accepted the theoretical argument for Nash equilibrium alignment faced an implementation dilemma: use IPO (simple loss, but suffers from the likelihood-gap failure mode documented by Pal et al., 2024), implement Nash-MD with tabular representations (provable but intractable beyond toy settings), or deploy PPO/SAC with the exponential weight update (complex, unstable at LLM scale, as Swamy et al., 2024 attempted for robotics). SPPO eliminates this dilemma by showing that a regression loss — exactly the kind of objective that standard autoregressive fine-tuning pipelines handle naturally — can approximate the exponential update with sufficient fidelity to achieve strong empirical results. The loss is four lines of code; the training loop is iterative supervised fine-tuning with a specific target.

This reframes which research directions are attractive. **Before SPPO**: work on general preference alignment split into two tracks — theory (Munos et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024) that proved convergence but didn't scale, and practice (iterative DPO, Snorkel) that scaled but abandoned theoretical grounding. **After SPPO**: the two tracks converge. The regression-based approximation strategy means theoretical advances in game-theoretic alignment (better convergence rates, alternative solution concepts, partial-information settings) can potentially be translated into practical algorithms by following the same pattern: derive an update rule, express it as a log-ratio target, and regress. Conversely, empirical challenges observed in SPPO's behavior — the alignment tax on knowledge benchmarks, the varying optimal iteration count across base models — now motivate theory: what regularizers or early-stopping criteria preserve the convergence guarantee while mitigating capability degradation?

The paper also **reconciles a specific empirical contradiction** that was previously unexplained. Iterative DPO and IPO had shown mixed results: some studies (Xu et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024) reported improvements from iterative training, while others found plateauing or degradation. SPPO's analysis in Section 4.5 provides a mechanistic explanation: methods that optimize only the pairwise gap $(a - b)$ can achieve their training objective through loser suppression without winner improvement. On benchmarks where judge models are sensitive to the difference between "actively good" and "merely not bad" responses, iterative DPO/IPO appear to improve; on benchmarks that can distinguish these, they plateau. SPPO's per-response absolute targets avoid this failure mode, which explains why it shows monotonic improvement on AlpacaEval 2.0 LC and Arena-Hard while DPO/IPO do not (Figure 1, Figure 2). This explanation is testable and actionable: future methods should report not just win rates but also whether winner likelihoods actually increase, and practitioners should be skeptical of pairwise loss methods that show large raw win rate gains without corresponding LC improvements.

A less obvious but equally important shift: SPPO demonstrates that **a 0.4B preference model is sufficient to align a 7–8B policy to outperform much larger models** (Table 2: Mistral-7B-SPPO at 28.53% LC win rate beats Llama 3 70B Instruct at 34.4% raw but 22.9% LC? No — 28.53% vs. 34.4% for Llama 3 70B on LC metric; Llama-3-8B-SPPO at 38.77% surpasses GPT-4 0314 at 35.3%). The preference model is ~20× smaller than the policy being aligned. This inverts the conventional wisdom that alignment requires a judge at least as capable as the policy (the "you can't supervise a superintelligence" argument). SPPO shows that **iterative self-play against a fixed, weak judge can drive substantial alignment gains** because each iteration generates new responses that reveal new weaknesses in the current policy, and the judge only needs to provide relative comparisons within the policy's current output distribution, not absolute quality assessments. This is a concrete existence proof that weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2023) is achievable in the preference optimization setting: the 0.4B PairRM "supervises" the 7B Mistral to GPT-4-competitive performance.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**Directly measuring whether SPPO increases winner likelihood vs. DPO/IPO.** The paper's central theoretical claim — that SPPO's per-response targets force the winner's log-ratio toward +1/2 while DPO/IPO only widen the gap — is supported by the algebraic analysis (Section 4.5) and indirectly by the length-control results (SPPO's moderate length growth vs. IPO's explosion). But the claim is never directly tested. A clean experiment: run SPPO and iterative DPO/IPO for three iterations from the same base model, save all generated responses, and at each iteration, compute the log-probability the *current* policy assigns to the *winning* responses from the *previous* iteration. Under DPO/IPO, the winner's log-probability may increase, stay flat, or even decrease (as long as the gap to the loser widens). Under SPPO, it should increase toward the target. This experiment requires only logging probabilities during training — no additional infrastructure — and would provide definitive evidence for or against the paper's mechanistic explanation. A negative result (SPPO also only increases the gap, not absolute winner likelihood) would force a reinterpretation of where SPPO's empirical gains come from.

**Scaling SPPO to more iterations and characterizing the convergence trajectory.** The paper runs exactly three iterations and shows diminishing but positive gains on AlpacaEval 2.0 (Figure 1). What happens at iteration 10? At iteration 50? Theorem 4.1 guarantees $O(1/\sqrt{T})$ convergence of the duality gap for the idealized algorithm, but the practical algorithm introduces unquantified approximation error. Running SPPO for 10–20 iterations on a moderate-scale model (1B–3B parameters, to make the experiment feasible) and measuring both PairRM win rates and GPT-4-judged win rates at each iteration would reveal: (a) whether SPPO eventually plateaus or continues improving, (b) whether the improvement rate empirically matches $O(1/\sqrt{T})$, (c) whether the alignment tax on knowledge benchmarks (Table 3) worsens with more iterations, and (d) whether the partition function approximation with $\eta/2$ causes systematic drift when accumulated over many iterations. If SPPO degrades after some iteration count, identifying the inflection point and its cause (over-optimization of PairRM? accumulation of approximation error? catastrophic forgetting?) would define the practical limits of the method.

**Replacing $\eta/2$ with a learned or estimated baseline.** The partition function approximation is the clearest heuristic in SPPO's derivation. Appendix A provides a theoretical justification but no empirical validation. A strong follow-up would test alternatives: (1) **estimate $\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$ from the $K$ samples** — it's an expectation $\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_t}[\exp(\eta \hat{P}(y \succ \pi_t|x))]$, computable from the same responses already generated, with $O(K)$ additional computation; (2) **train a lightweight value function** $V_\phi(x)$ alongside the policy to predict $\eta^{-1}\log Z_{\pi_t}(x)$, updated at each iteration, giving a per-prompt baseline that can adapt as the policy improves; (3) **sweep the constant coefficient** from $\eta/2$ to $\eta$ (the two extremes from Appendix A) and measure how sensitive final performance is to this choice. The third option is minimal-cost (just a hyperparameter sweep) and would reveal whether SPPO's performance owes anything to the specific constant choice or whether any reasonable baseline works — a diagnostic for how much the partition function matters. If performance is flat across the range, the policy gradient interpretation (any baseline reduces variance) is the dominant explanation and the partition function discussion is theoretically elegant but practically irrelevant.

**SPPO with alternative preference models: human feedback, ensemble judges, and stronger models.** All experiments use PairRM-0.4B. The natural extensions test generalization: (1) **Replace PairRM with a stronger preference model** (UltraRM-13B, or GPT-4-Turbo as a judge during training) and measure whether SPPO's alignment ceiling rises with judge quality. The prediction from the paper's framework is that a better preference oracle should yield a better Nash equilibrium — but it might also change the over-optimization dynamics (a more accurate judge might allow more iterations before degradation). (2) **Use an ensemble of preference models** (PairRM + UltraRM + a safety classifier) and aggregate their judgments via averaging or voting. This tests whether SPPO's per-response target can naturally incorporate multi-dimensional preferences without the Bradley-Terry scalar reduction — a key claimed advantage of the general preference framework. (3) **Run a small-scale human evaluation** where SPPO training uses actual human pairwise judgments (crowdworkers, not AI) for 100–200 prompts, then evaluate whether the alignment transfers to held-out prompts better than PairRM-trained SPPO. This would close the most important generalization gap: we currently don't know whether SPPO's gains are real alignment or PairRM-specific exploitation.

**Combining SPPO with SFT data mixing to mitigate the alignment tax.** The paper observes the alignment tax on Open LLM Leaderboard (Table 3) and speculates that "incorporating high-quality SFT annotations could play a significant role." A concrete experiment: during each SPPO iteration, mix the SPPO preference data with a subset of high-quality SFT data (e.g., the original instruction-tuning data, or a curated factual QA dataset) in varying proportions (10%, 25%, 50% SFT). Train on the combined loss: SPPO regression loss on preference data + standard cross-entropy on SFT data. Measure whether this preserves or improves knowledge benchmark performance while retaining the alignment gains. If a 25% SFT mixture eliminates the MMLU/Winogrande degradation while maintaining >90% of the AlpacaEval gain, it would provide a practical recipe for practitioners and validate the hypothesis that the alignment tax is caused by insufficient capability-preserving regularization, not by an inherent tradeoff.

**SPPO for multi-turn and interactive alignment.** All experiments evaluate single-turn response quality (AlpacaEval 2.0, Arena-Hard) or two-turn conversations (MT-Bench). But SPPO's game-theoretic formulation naturally extends to multi-step interactions: the "response" becomes a trajectory of turns in a conversation, and the preference oracle judges entire dialogues. The MT-Bench results (Figure 2, left) show that SPPO initially degrades second-turn performance (Iter1: 6.79 vs. base 7.25) before recovering (Iter3: 7.34). This suggests the single-turn optimization may temporarily disrupt multi-turn conversational skills. A direct extension would train SPPO on multi-turn dialogue data where the "response" is a full conversation, and the preference oracle compares complete interaction histories. This would test whether the Nash equilibrium framework scales to sequential interaction without collapsing into single-turn myopia — a critical capability for real-world deployment.

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**Bootstrapping high-quality alignment for open-source models without proprietary supervision.** The most immediate application is the one the paper demonstrates: taking an instruction-tuned open model (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, Llama-3-8B-Instruct) and a lightweight preference model (PairRM-0.4B), and producing an aligned model competitive with proprietary systems (28.53%–38.77% LC win rate against GPT-4-Turbo on AlpacaEval 2.0, Table 1) without using any GPT-4-generated responses or human labels during training. For organizations that cannot or will not depend on API access to stronger models for alignment (due to cost, privacy, or reproducibility concerns), SPPO provides a self-contained recipe: deploy your base model, run PairRM locally, iterate three times, and achieve competitive alignment with purely internal resources. The total inference cost is approximately 300k policy generations and 1.5M PairRM queries — manageable on a small GPU cluster.

**Improving instruction-following in specialized domains with custom preference judges.** SPPO decouples the preference oracle from the policy, which means the framework can be deployed with any pairwise judge. For a legal tech company building an LLM for contract analysis, the "preference oracle" could be a set of domain-specific heuristics (does response A cite correct case law more often than response B? does it use proper legal terminology?) or a fine-tuned legal preference classifier. Running SPPO with this domain-specific judge on a base legal LLM would iteratively improve alignment with legal practice norms without requiring expensive attorney-annotated preference data at scale — the judge provides the signal, and SPPO's self-play loop generates the training data. The key requirement is that the judge provide reliable *relative* comparisons within the policy's output distribution, not absolute quality scores, which is often easier to build for specialized domains.

**Data generation for weak-to-strong distillation pipelines.** The finding that a 0.4B judge can guide a 7B policy to GPT-4-competitive performance suggests a specific distillation architecture: train a small (0.5B–1B) student model using SPPO-generated data from a larger (7B–13B) teacher that has been SPPO-aligned. The SPPO-aligned teacher produces high-quality outputs across diverse prompts; these outputs, paired with the teacher's estimated win rates as soft targets, can train the student via standard distillation. Because SPPO's win-rate targets are continuous (not binary win/loss), they provide a richer training signal than hard preference labels. This pipeline could produce compact, aligned models suitable for on-device deployment where running the full 7B model and the self-play loop is infeasible.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper does not articulate an explicit decision rule comparing SPPO against named alternatives in different deployment scenarios. The experimental comparison is against iterative DPO and iterative IPO under matched conditions, and the message is that SPPO outperforms both for the configuration tested (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, PairRM-0.4B, UltraFeedback prompts, three iterations). The paper does not provide the kind of tradeoff analysis (e.g., "prefer SPPO when you have a weak judge but need strong alignment; prefer DPO when you have high-quality offline preference pairs and limited generation budget") that would support a conditional recommendation matrix. Forcing such a matrix would fabricate guidance not present in the paper's analysis or results. The practical takeaway is narrower: if you are currently using iterative DPO or IPO with a general preference model for chat alignment, and you observe plateauing win rates or excessive length growth, switching the loss function to SPPO's per-response regression target (Equation 4.6) while keeping everything else identical is likely to improve length-controlled performance based on the consistent evidence in Table 1 and Figure 1. Beyond this specific comparison, the generalization evidence is insufficient to recommend SPPO over other method classes.
