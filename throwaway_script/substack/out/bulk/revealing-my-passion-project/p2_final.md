Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman essay (The Staff Engineer's Process for Building Your Own Hardware Product):

**Spotting the Gap in the Market**

After years of wrist problems from tennis and a long software career ahead, Ryan became a heavy ergonomic keyboard user—but could never find exactly what he wanted. His criteria were specific: a split keyboard with tenting for wrist positioning, a standard 75% layout like a MacBook so he didn’t have to relearn anything, and genuinely beautiful design and build quality. The market fell into three frustrating buckets: keyboards that were "too ergonomic" (bizarre layouts contoured to hand shape, requiring a steep relearning curve), keyboards that were "not ergonomic enough" (popular models with slight tenting but no split, which didn’t solve his actual wrist pain), and keyboards with the right ergonomics but "poor design and build quality." His daily driver, the Kinesis Freestyle2, epitomized the last category—all plastic, lots of wasted space, multiple wires, and tenting legs sold separately at a price that "feels like price gouging." He asked himself, "Can you imagine Apple or Logitech putting out a product like this?" When serious searching turned up nothing perfect, he decided to build the ergonomic keyboard he wished existed.

**Setting Direction with a Strategy Doc**

The first move was writing a direction doc, which is what he does whenever he faces ambiguity in a project—at work or outside it. He wrote down the problem, why it should be solved, and then pulled in all his research and opportunity sizing. "Many people think that writing docs is just overhead that big tech engineers are forced to do," but to him writing forces clarity. "As I flushed out the document a lot more became clear. It also became a central place to pull in all my research." The act of writing helped him think through whether the project was worth doing at all—he felt he had decent odds of making back his initial investment (roughly $30,000 so far), and money aside, he was genuinely excited: "there’s something satisfying about building what you want." These reasons made him feel it was a worthwhile project, so he started thinking through the roadmap.

**Recruiting a Team Without Authority**

He knew he couldn't build a prototype alone. Writing firmware was within his wheelhouse, but learning PCB layout and CAD from scratch would take too long and carry unacceptable quality risk if it was his first attempt. His approach to staffing mirrored the **influence without authority** skill he considers essential for growing to Staff: convince people to join your vision when you have no organizational power over them. For electrical engineering, he rejected Upwork because "quality was important"—the risk of an unknown hire was too high. Instead he reached out to electrical engineers he'd studied with at UCLA. He shared the strategy doc with one who was excited by the vision precisely because engineering quality was a stated priority, and he "could see the logic behind the plan." For industrial design, he found a designer whose work he admired online and cold-reached. Again, the doc was "critical for communicating the project vision, which he thought was compelling." Both joined. Writing gave him a clearer picture of what he wanted and why it seemed like a good opportunity; that clarity made him "much more compelling in conversation," and sharing the document was an effortless way to convey the vision consistently. "Without their help, this project would not be possible."

**Execution as Ambiguity Reduction**

Once the team was in place, execution became the "easy" part—in quotes, because the challenging work remains, but "the remaining problems have a lot less ambiguity." This is a pattern from big tech: "you often see high-level engineers come in and resolve all the ambiguity, then leave projects once they are on a steady course." For this project, execution has meant doing whatever the overall initiative needs to succeed, not just writing firmware—learning how manufacturing works (including a trip to China), "owning and protecting the product vision," doing project management across functions, and reviewing work outside his domain in hardware, mechanical engineering, and industrial design. This level of ownership, "drives me to solve whatever problem is needed," and he traces it directly back to what he learned at Instagram as part of technical leadership. The main difference here is that the work is "a lot less software engineering"—a shift he's been having a lot of fun with. No one knows if the project will succeed, but the process itself demonstrates that Staff-level behaviors are portable: "A lot of what you need to lead in big tech will help you elsewhere too." He plans to write more about the project separately, since not all of it is relevant for software engineering career growth.

---

His **influence without authority** playbook—recruiting UCLA classmates and a designer he admired by sharing a strategy doc that made the vision compelling—is the same skill he names as essential for growing to Staff. That skill flows directly from his stance that writing is not overhead but a tool for **resolving ambiguity**; the doc created clarity for himself and became an effortless artifact to convey the vision to others. The entire arc—from direction doc to ambiguity reduction to doing whatever the team needs—mirrors the high-level steps of technical leadership he outlined in a prior article, now stress-tested on a hardware product built on nights and weekends.
