Here are the takeaways from this John Myles White interview (director of engineering at Meta who ran promotions for AI Infra, built Deltoid 3, was a Julia core contributor, and later worked at MSL):

**Javi’s office hours**

His clearest, most rehearsed career regret: "Javi was my skip." Javi (now COO of Meta, then head of growth and ads) held open office hours, and John’s manager Danny told the team weekly that Javi wanted people to show up and no one did. John declined every time, rationalizing that he had nothing valuable to say and didn’t want to waste Javi’s time. Later, as a director running his own office hours, the symmetry hit hard — he desperately wanted junior people to show up and they didn’t. He now sees it as a decision "that basically harmed both parties that I did out of fear." His advice: when a senior leader says "please come talk to me," go. Directors and above are so many layers removed from the IC reality — what’s happening in the codebase, what team dynamics are — that they genuinely hunger for ground truth. "Don’t show up and be like, I want you to tell me how to get promoted" — but if you say "I think this part of our org could be better, could you help me?", leaders love that.

**Running promotions at Meta**

When John moved from Data Infra to AI Infra, the culture shock was total: "there was not a single person I would meet on any team in AI infra whose first and foremost goal wasn’t promotion." By contrast, when he started at Meta under Adrien Friggeri (a previous podcast guest), they were told their org had never had anyone above IC7 — so promotions simply weren’t the dominant frame. The promo-driven culture, he argues, **decouples real skill development from career advancement**. The promo bar often demanded running rollouts that touched ten other systems, which incentivized building maximally-coupled systems rather than clean ones. And the most toxic manifestation was teams shipping code they knew was bad because "that’s my promo bar," even agreeing to delete it the next day. In monetization, the playbook was explicit: "a very detailed plan of work you will do in order to get promoted" — effective at retention but, by his account, "wrecked tons of teams." Yet he is pragmatic: "unless your VP is ready to fix that and totally shut it down, you have to play the game. It’s totally irrational not to."

**The PyTorch counterculture**

PyTorch was the exception — a team where "people were just genuinely in it for the love of the craft" and "loved engineering as engineering." They held a brutally high promotion bar, and he was candid with candidates: "our eights are as good as you’re going to get anywhere in this whole world," but if you want to be a 10, maybe go to a team that promotes faster. This created a **reputation premium**: because the rest of Meta knew PyTorch didn’t inflate levels, their promotion cases sailed through calibration where other teams’ cases were doubted ("oh, you guys are not well known for holding a high bar"). The trade-off is real — he acknowledges he may have left 20% compensation on the table — but "I was willing to get 20% less compensation to be in a place that I was more proud of." Whether the "promote fast, get fired fast" orgs actually maximize lifetime earnings is an open question he can’t fully resolve.

**Surviving the emotional-contagion crisis**

John joined Meta as an IC4 on Core Data Science, but arrived into chaos: a colleague published "Emotional Contagion in Social Networks" in PNAS, which became "the absolute singular worst piece of PR for Meta as a business that year." Suddenly the team was in meetings with the head of legal, fielding company-wide backlash, with the author doing a Q&A that was essentially a public apology. John had tried to prevent the publication but lacked authority; he was lucky to be affiliated with the experimentation-tools work rather than the paper’s direct fallout. The experience was formative: it taught him that a team could very nearly get itself disbanded even in the pre-layoffs era. "It blew over," and the scare gave way to several deeply fulfilling years building A/B testing infrastructure.

**Deltoid 3 and the accident of becoming TL**

He was a main developer of Deltoid 3, Meta’s A/B testing framework. The career-growth mechanism was accidental: "almost all the senior engineers left the team in the span of six months," and he went from random IC5 to "the only one who remembered how anything worked." He became de facto tech lead, drove the vision for experimentation tools for years, and calls it some of the most fulfilling work of his career. He notes that people "understate how often these things can happen in tech" — sudden attrition creating vacuum promotions. One of his pangs: Deltoid’s feature set is essentially Statsig’s product (and before that, Optimizely’s). "You know, probably should have built a Deltoid startup many years earlier and I did not have the wisdom to do that." Ryan notes the repeatable playbook of taking a big-tech internal tool and productizing it for the industry — the product-market fit is largely proven. John adds his own parallel: he also managed DataSwarm, and "when people are like, what’s DataSwarm? It is literally Airflow. The guy who wrote Airflow built DataSwarm, quit, and a week later open-sourced Airflow."

**The Julia language and the fight against slowness**

Between grad school and Facebook, he took six months to work on Julia, becoming a core contributor. Julia’s founding pitch: you should be able to write high-level code (like Python or MATLAB — MATLAB was the original target to destroy) that runs at C speed. The thing that "drove me insane" was that naive R code that looked 80% identical to its C implementation ran 1,000–10,000× slower. The reason is invariants: fast languages have invariants the compiler can rely on; slow languages have **maximal dynamic uncertainty**. In R, even a brace block can be redefined by the user, so the runtime must check every time. R’s lazy-evaluation promise objects, per a paper he cites (by Jan Vitek’s students, evaluating the design of the R language), were unnecessary in 70–90% of cases but imposed enormous overhead. "Lack of invariance — that’s what makes a language fast." Julia is "the only people still actually fighting that fight," he says. It didn’t win the data-science wars, but it’s a top-25 language and doing okay.

**The academic arrogance toward industry**

His most viral tweet was about how grad students and postdocs treat industry as "a safe position of last resort" and assume it will be trivial. He once interviewed a CS professor who could not pass values between functions — the professor literally proposed printing output to a REPL, reading it as a human, and typing it into the next function. "You are better at programming than this, right?" The candidate said no, that was how they worked. He attributes the mentality partly to academic culture selling the story that "smart people are in academia and dumb people are in industry," so competing with the dumb people should be easy — and then finding it is not.

**Statistics as the crazy field**

Statistics fascinates him because it’s the discipline that "sells people the idea that they can use statistical methods in real life but in reality what they do is do pure mathematics." It’s caught between pure math’s internal coherence and software engineering’s empirical "the code runs" pragmatism, making claims about the real world that often don’t hold up under scrutiny. The difference between great statistics books and bad ones is **hyper-intense honesty**: the great ones tell you exactly what they’re assuming and exactly what they’re not claiming. His top recommendation is Larry Wasserman — "literally anything you can get by Larry Wasserman. Buy and read" — whom he’s never met but whose books he considers unmatched. A second strong recommendation is Peter Aronow’s *Introduction to Agnostic Statistics* for social scientists and practitioners (less math-heavy). This rigor matters practically: he’s seen a team pressured to ship code to hit a half-goal they planned to delete on July 2nd, and a manager who blocked a Deltoid 3 launch because it made a borderline-significant holdout win flicker — "your win is so close to the border between you did nothing and won that literally mild tweaks in our code have turned it off. I feel like that’s not a win." A lack of statistical understanding and evaluation-driven pathologies "become extra dangerous" together.

**The changing labor dynamic**

John is "very bullish on Meta as a company" and continues to invest, but "very bearish on Meta if you’re an employee who’s not a stockholder — which just turns out to not be really anyone." The senior ranks are more stockholder than employee; the tension is real. Across all of Silicon Valley, the labor supply-demand equation has flipped: "the supply of engineers is way oversupplied," and employers no longer feel they must make sacrifices to acquire and retain talent. This manifests in compensation but also in subtler ways — "do we do things that upset the employees or do we not? How much do we give them a voice?" The layoffs loom largest of all: "the possibility you might get laid off is the number one emotional thing that causes people stress," and once that fear arrived, people stopped freaking out about perks. "Prior to the layoffs, when those things got taken away, people freaked out and were like, this is unforgivable. But then once they’re like, oh wait, actually you guys might just fire me. So now I’m much more tolerant of you taking my food away."

**Advice to his younger self**

He would tell 20-year-old him: "have more confidence that you can do bigger stuff." He sees a repeated pattern across his life of not being ambitious enough, of over-convincing himself of other people’s greatness and under-convincing himself of his own potential. Many successful people, he says, are not actually doing that much better — "they just tried and you didn’t try."

---

John’s career is a study in the tension between the **promo-driven culture** that decouples skill from advancement and a **reputation premium** earned by teams like PyTorch that refuse to inflate levels — a long-game bet he acknowledges may have left compensation on the table but left his pride intact. His Julia story applies the same diagnostic lens to programming languages that he applies to A/B testing and evaluation culture: **lack of invariance** is what makes something slow or unreliable, whether it’s a language runtime or a metrics-review culture that can’t distinguish a real win from statistical noise. And his most portable regret — not walking into Javi’s office hours — distills into one rule: when a leader opens a door, walk through it; the **hyper-intense honesty** he admires in Wasserman’s books is the same thing directors are desperate to hear from the ground floor.
