Here are the takeaways from this Igor interview (Senior Staff Engineer who left Google for Meta, sought a demotion, and returned to Google at a lower level):

**Why he wanted a demotion at Meta**

Igor joined Meta at Senior Staff (E7), a level where you’re expected to be “a big expert in the field,” know the infrastructure deeply, and have broad trust across surrounding teams—all things he found nearly impossible to build from zero in a short ramp. He describes the process of starting at a new company at a high level as beginning “from zero” and needing to climb the ladder as fast as possible: first to intern-level productivity, then junior, then progressively more senior. “Within this year and two months that I spent in Meta, maybe if I’m generous with myself, I maybe achieved like E6. So I don’t feel like I reached E7.” Meta had also recently introduced a policy of cutting the bottom 10% of performers, which meant you had “at most one year to ramp up to be comparable in performance to other old timers.”

Beyond the performance pressure, Igor discovered something about the work itself: as he climbed toward E7 territory, he realized that “my happiest time was when I was working in a relatively small team, doing a lot of coding, debugging, designing, mentoring more junior folks.” The higher-level role meant “spends most of the time in meetings and design docs and touches much less code.” He was programming from age 12 and genuinely loved the hands-on work. So he asked management if he could drop a level—and was told it simply wasn’t possible: “staying in the same job category and dropping level, just like they said, it’s not possible.” He didn’t push harder because “I also felt like I’m too far outside of my comfort zone,” and a Google recruiter had been contacting him periodically. Taking the “easy way out,” he went back.

**Self-judgment and the project that sealed the decision**

Igor’s assessment of his own performance was largely self-driven, though he did receive some feedback he preferred not to share. In his final months, he was working on a project he initially thought would take two weeks; it turned out to be far more complex. Even if he finished and launched it successfully—which he nearly did—“it is still not an E7 level project. You cannot justify my level with the project being completed.” He acknowledged this was partly his own fault in which projects he picked and how he underestimated complexity. Ryan notes the pattern here of senior IC job mobility becoming progressively riskier; Igor had seen colleagues at Google leave and return a year or two later after things didn’t work out, but “you hear the success stories, you don’t hear the failures.”

**Why Senior Staff at Meta was different from Cruise and Google**

The central contrast: at Google, Igor started at L3 and spent 14.5 years climbing the ladder, failing promotion cycles and retrying. When he finally reached Senior Staff, it was because he’d worked on the same project for several years—“I was already like an expert. I knew everybody, everybody knew me.” At Cruise, the ramp-up was also difficult initially, but Cruise was a smaller company with “less infrastructure to learn” and “less crowded space”; you could “easily carve out space for you to grow into.” Meta, by contrast, felt crowded—“especially among the IC7 folks. It felt a little bit like they have too many, frankly.” You had to actively find scope for yourself.

Igor thinks Meta also “doesn’t know how to onboard senior folks because most of the senior folks in the company, they grew within the company to those senior levels.” Even if someone came from another org internally, they at least had “the meta knowledge.” His own onboarding leaned too heavily on reading docs and building foundational knowledge; “it is much better to just get your hands dirty and just try doing stuff.” He wished management had given him a concrete recipe: “this other person just ramped up three quarters ago and here’s what they did.”

**Meta vs. Google culture**

Igor found a distinct cultural difference in how deadlines work. Meta “tends to set up very ambitious goals,” giving arbitrary deadlines like “you need to finish it by September 15th.” Everyone works hard under pressure, sends constant updates to leadership, and then—when the date arrives—the project is still unfinished “and it just keeps dragging on. And everybody is fine with that.” After experiencing this cycle five times, “people just say okay, you know what, 5pm I’m going home… I know that this whole pressure is artificial.” He describes it as almost like “elementary school, where the teacher is yelling but kids are still playing. If you’re yelling too much, it stops working essentially.” Google, at least as of a decade ago, was “much more reasonable”—pressure came only in genuinely exceptional cases, not as a constant operating mode. This cultural mismatch contributed to his feeling that Meta “didn’t fit me the best.”

**Downleveling at Google**

When Igor reached out to return to Google, he explicitly asked to come back as L6 rather than his previous L7. This turned out to be nearly as unusual as demoting in place: “they don’t have a process for bringing people at a level down, but they made it possible for me.” He recognizes how it might look to a hiring manager—“it does sound fishy. Like what’s wrong with you?” He had to explicitly assure his recruiter he’d accept the lower compensation, which was a significant drop. Google didn’t require him to re-interview despite a 3.5-year absence, but did note that if he was coming back as L6, “you might need to do a coding interview because who knows if you wrote code while you were out.” He got an exception. “You got promoted out of competence in the lower levels,” Ryan jokes.

**Why he’s transparent**

Igor attributes his willingness to share candidly to both personality and privilege: “I’m a generally very open person” and “quite confident.” More concretely: “I don’t need the work visa… My spouse has health insurance, my kids are already grown up and off to college, I don’t have mortgage on my house. I can afford not to work for a few years.” Google could rescind his offer and he’d be fine. “That’s a position of privilege that many other people wouldn’t have.” His advice to others: “I would probably say no, unless you feel so safe and secure.”

**The best quality-of-life engineering level**

“Senior engineer like E5, L5. That’s probably the least pressure.” At that level, you’re shielded by a TL above you who absorbs the status updates and upper-management pressure, and by lower-level managers. “You can just do the stuff that hopefully you enjoy.” Igor got into programming at age 12 and “would do it even if it wasn’t paying well.” While he could enjoy E6 and might have eventually grown comfortable at E7, the imposter syndrome was real—but he cautions against comparing yourself to a composite of peers: “one person is good at this, another person is good at this and you’re comparing yourself against a team of people.” Friends and family told him he was crazy to leave voluntarily—“nobody showed you the door”—but his response was: “I’m capable of running a marathon, but I don’t like running a marathon.”

**The Senior Staff promo at Google: TPU migration for Ads ML**

The project that earned him L7 was building a new ML training infrastructure for Google Ads to run on TPUs instead of CPUs. When he joined, the team’s CPU-based training system had been polished over roughly 10 years to be “super reliable, well monitored, unit tested, very rigid, very polished.” His task was to build a completely new piece of infrastructure for TPUs that had to match that reliability. It began as a small exploratory effort—the TL plus one person building a prototype—with heavy cross-team support from the TPU team, compiler team, and TensorFlow team. “That’s why knowing people and them knowing you is very important… If you don’t have that, you cannot succeed.”

The hardware planning was a high-stakes bet: you had to order chips 18 months in advance, before you could try them, and “those chips are super expensive.” A key testing technique: run the system with all computation removed to see how fast data could be fed in and out—effectively testing as if the TPU were infinitely fast. This surfaced bottlenecks constantly, like a data scheduler that had “previously was never an issue and then suddenly this is your bottleneck.” He also ran into a physical-world constraint few engineers consider: training on petabytes of data stored on spinning disks. Disk capacity kept growing but rotational speed didn’t, so “your data center has a lot fewer disks to store the same amount of data, but the throughput is an order of magnitude less.” Asking for more disks for throughput, not storage, met resistance: “disks are very cheap compared to everything else in the data center, super cheap. But you cannot get them.” Igor usually under-ordered chips, being conservative in a mature business that needed to remain profitable—unlike today’s LLM world where “they’re all losing money and they’re all fine with that.”

**Mentorship: don’t underestimate your interns**

As an L4, Igor hosted an intern who later converted to full-time and, a few years later, “became the manager of the team that he interned in.” By that time Igor had already switched projects, so he was never in the position of reporting to his former intern—but the lesson stuck: “don’t underestimate your interns. They can be really, really good.”

**Biggest career regret: disillusionment**

Igor joined Google believing “it’s a really positive force in the world” and that he’d “work here just because it’s Google, not because it pays more.” Over the years, he became disillusioned: “today it is a corporation just like any other corporation.” The turning point was watching Google shut down an Atlanta office of a few hundred people—roughly the same size as his own Pittsburgh office. The explanation from leadership: the senior VP sponsoring Atlanta’s headcount pulled out, and “nobody else was willing to take over the headcount of that office.” The company offered relocation or an exit package. “You feel like, yeah, at the end of the day, I’m just a cell in the spreadsheet.” This happened many years before the post-COVID layoffs made such moves routine.

**Advice to his younger self**

“Maybe work on what matters. Ask yourself, does my company really benefit from what I’m doing? And if not, then maybe you shouldn’t.” As a junior engineer at Google, he sometimes worked on projects where he privately thought, “why are we building this in the first place? Who needs this?” A year later, leadership would reach the same conclusion and shut them down. His practical advice for a junior in that situation: talk to other managers and find another project. He notes this only works at companies with fluid internal mobility—Google and Meta are good for this; Intel and IBM, by reputation, made switching “almost impossible. It’s much easier to just quit and then reapply.”

Throughout the conversation, Igor returns to a core insight: the E5/E6 zone is where hands-on engineers who love coding and mentoring can be happiest, and the industry’s default assumption—that everyone wants to climb—doesn’t fit everyone. He was confident enough in his own preferences, and privileged enough in his circumstances, to act on that conviction even when everyone around him thought he was crazy.
