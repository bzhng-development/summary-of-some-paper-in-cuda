# Taming the Chaos: Coordinated Autoscaling for Heterogeneous and Disaggregated LLM Inference

**ArXiv:** [2508.19559](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19559)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **HeteroScale**, a coordinated autoscaling framework designed for Prefill–Decode (P/D) disaggregated LLM serving architectures.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **HeteroScale**, a coordinated autoscaling framework designed for Prefill–Decode (P/D) disaggregated LLM serving architectures. Deployed at ByteDance across tens of thousands of GPUs processing trillions of tokens daily, HeteroScale combines a topology-aware scheduler that respects heterogeneous hardware and network affinity constraints with a **metrics-driven scaling policy** derived from the first large-scale empirical analysis of autoscaling signals in production—identifying decode Tokens-Per-Second (TPS) as the single most robust metric for jointly scaling both prefill and decode pools while maintaining architectural balance. The system increases average GPU utilization by 26.6 percentage points and saves hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours daily, establishing that coordinated, metrics-driven autoscaling with network-aware placement substantially outperforms traditional approaches—but only when the scaling policy is built on signals that genuinely track workload pressure rather than misleading hardware metrics like decode-stage GPU utilization.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Traditional Autoscalers Cannot Handle Disaggregated LLM Inference

The fundamental question this paper tackles is: **how do you automatically scale a Prefill–Decode disaggregated LLM serving system while maintaining both efficiency and architectural balance across heterogeneous hardware?** This matters because modern LLM serving has undergone a significant architectural shift—from monolithic deployments where one instance handles both prefill and decode phases, to **disaggregated architectures** where these phases run on separate, independently scaled pools of instances. While disaggregation brings substantial efficiency gains (allowing each phase to be optimized for its distinct computational profile), it introduces operational complexity that existing autoscaling systems were never designed to handle.

This gap is critical for several practical reasons the authors highlight in Section 1 and Section 2.2:

- **Massive resource waste from static provisioning**: LLM traffic exhibits strong diurnal patterns—demand follows predictable daily cycles with peaks and valleys. Without effective autoscaling, organizations must over-provision for peak load, leaving GPUs idle during off-peak hours. At ByteDance's scale (tens of thousands of GPUs), this translates to enormous financial and environmental cost. The paper's findings show HeteroScale saves "hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours daily"—this is not a marginal optimization but a foundational operational requirement.

- **P/D disaggregation multiplies the coordination problem**: In a monolithic deployment, you scale one pool up and down based on load. In a disaggregated deployment, you have **two interdependent pools** (prefill and decode) that must be scaled in a balanced manner. Scaling one without the other creates bottlenecks: too few prefill instances starve the decode pool of work, while too few decode instances cause prefill outputs to queue up, violating latency SLOs. The optimal ratio between them is not fixed—it depends on workload characteristics (input/output length distributions), hardware configurations, and SLO priorities—and the paper reports production experience where this ratio varies from **1P/5D to 9P/1D** (Section 4.1).

- **Heterogeneous hardware is a practical reality, not a choice**: Large production clusters inevitably accumulate diverse GPU types (the paper mentions NVIDIA H20 and L20) with different compute capabilities, memory capacities, and interconnect bandwidths. The prefill phase (compute-intensive) and decode phase (memory-bandwidth-bound) benefit from different hardware profiles. Naively running both on homogeneous hardware "inflates cost per generated token by 41% compared with a phase-aware heterogeneous deployment" (Section 1, citing Zhu et al., 2025). But heterogeneous deployment creates a scheduling problem: how do you match the right work to the right hardware while maintaining network locality between prefill and decode instances that must exchange large KV caches?

- **Misleading metrics cause dangerous decisions in production**: The paper demonstrates that conventional autoscaling metrics—particularly GPU utilization—are fundamentally misleading in P/D disaggregated settings. The decode stage is memory-bound due to KV cache storage and data transfer operations, causing its GPU utilization to remain high even under light load (Figure 2c, 2d). An autoscaler that naively monitors decode GPU utilization would conclude the system is saturated when it actually has spare capacity, or worse, would fail to scale down during valleys because the metric never drops. This is not a theoretical concern—it leads directly to resource waste and the inability to respond to genuine load changes.

### Prior Approaches and Their Systematic Failures

#### Kubernetes HPA and Traditional Cloud Autoscalers

The default approach to autoscaling in cloud-native environments is the **Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA)** and related systems like Vertical Pod Autoscaler (VPA) and KEDA [28, 29, 30]. These systems monitor coarse-grained metrics (CPU utilization, memory usage, or custom metrics) and adjust replica counts when thresholds are crossed. The paper identifies three fundamental reasons these fail for P/D disaggregated LLM serving (Section 2.2, Challenge 3):

1. **They rely on hardware-level metrics that are misleading for LLM workloads.** As discussed above, decode GPU utilization is dominated by KV cache memory pressure rather than actual computational load. An HPA monitoring GPU utilization on decode instances would see consistently high values regardless of whether the system is at peak load or nearly idle, rendering its scale-in/scale-out decisions essentially random.

2. **They lack coordination mechanisms for interdependent components.** HPA treats each deployment (or "role") as an independent scaling target. In a P/D disaggregated service, this means the prefill pool and decode pool would each have their own HPA, making independent decisions with no awareness of the architectural balance required between them. This inevitably leads to imbalances where one phase bottlenecks the other. The paper explicitly states: "Independent scaling thus leads to architectural imbalance, starving one phase while the other sits idle and crippling overall throughput" (Section 1).

3. **They are topology- and hardware-agnostic.** HPA simply scales pod counts up or down; it delegates scheduling to the Kubernetes scheduler, which treats all nodes as a flat pool. For P/D disaggregated services, this means prefill and decode instances can end up on nodes under different network switches, reducing available KV cache transfer bandwidth by approximately 20% according to the authors' empirical observations (Section 1). This bandwidth reduction directly translates to higher latency—a bottleneck that no amount of scaling can fix.

Learning-based autoscaling approaches (AutoScale [14], DeepScaling [47], Resource Central [10]) attempt to predict workload and proactively adjust capacity, but they inherit the same fundamental limitations: they are designed around generic cloud workloads (web services, microservices) and have no concept of the P/D architectural dependency, network affinity requirements, or the metric selection problem specific to LLM inference.

#### LLM Serving Systems: Optimization Without Autoscaling

The LLM serving systems literature has focused heavily on single-node or static-cluster optimization, largely ignoring the autoscaling problem:

**Monolithic serving systems** (vLLM [32], TensorRT-LLM [37], SGLang [56], DeepSpeed-Inference [5]) optimize inference through techniques like continuous batching, PagedAttention for KV cache memory management, kernel fusion, and quantization. These systems maximize throughput per GPU for a fixed set of resources but provide no mechanism for dynamically adjusting the resource pool size in response to changing load. They assume the cluster is provisioned and static.

**Disaggregated serving systems** (DistServe [57], SplitWise [39], Mooncake [41], TetriInfer [20], MemServe [19], P/D-Serve [25]) introduced the core architectural innovation that motivates HeteroScale: separating prefill and decode onto different instances for independent optimization. However, these systems focus on **how to efficiently execute inference once resources are allocated**, not on **how many resources to allocate dynamically**. They design the disaggregated architecture, optimize KV cache transfer protocols, and tune batching strategies, but they do not address the operational question of how to scale the prefill and decode pools up and down in response to diurnal load patterns while maintaining balance.

This is a critical omission. In a production environment, the theoretical throughput improvements of disaggregation can be completely negated by poor scaling decisions. A system that perfectly optimizes per-GPU throughput but keeps 50% more GPUs online than needed during off-peak hours is less cost-effective than a less-optimized system that scales correctly.

#### Heterogeneous Resource Management: Scheduling Without Scaling

Systems like the Heterogeneity-aware Scheduler [35], Tiresias [17], Gandiva [50], and GPU-aware frameworks like HexGen [23] and Mélange [16] address resource allocation across heterogeneous hardware, but they operate in the context of **training workloads** or **batch inference** where the job mix changes but the total resource pool is static. They decide which GPU to assign to which job, not how many GPUs of each type should be provisioned at any given moment. The distinction is crucial: scheduling optimizes placement within a fixed pool; autoscaling changes the pool size. HeteroScale must do both simultaneously—deciding how many instances to scale to, what hardware type they should be, and which specific GPUs they should occupy while respecting network topology.

#### Network-Aware Scheduling: Topology Without Dynamic Resizing

Network-aware scheduling has been explored in data-intensive cluster computing through systems like Sinbad [8], Varys [9], and NetCache [24], and topology-aware systems like Firmament [15], Paragon [12], and Quincy [21] consider network topology in placement decisions. However, these systems address **static placement optimization**: given a set of jobs and a network topology, where should each job run to minimize communication overhead? They do not integrate with an autoscaling control loop that continuously adjusts the number of jobs (instances) based on real-time load. HeteroScale's innovation is not network-aware scheduling per se, but **integrating network-aware scheduling into a dynamic autoscaling framework** where placement decisions must be re-evaluated every time the system scales in or out—potentially tens or hundreds of times per day.

### Conflicting and Incomplete Prior Evidence on Metrics

The paper is motivated by a genuine gap in the empirical literature: **no prior work has systematically studied which metrics are reliable for autoscaling P/D disaggregated LLM services in production.** The conventional wisdom from cloud autoscaling—"monitor CPU/GPU utilization and scale when it exceeds a threshold"—is based on a model of computation where resource utilization is approximately linearly correlated with workload. The authors' empirical investigation (Section 3.3.2 and Figure 2) reveals that this model breaks down for the decode phase of LLM inference:

> "Hardware-level metrics show a clear polarization: the hardware metrics of prefill stage instances (such as prefill GPU utilization and SM activity) respond sensitively to load changes with high signal-to-noise ratios, while the decode stage hardware metrics (such as decode GPU utilization and SM activity) maintain high values even under slight pressure, showing low sensitivity to load changes."

This finding is corroborated by the growing recognition in the GPU infrastructure community that GPU utilization is a "misleading metric" [6], but the paper goes further by providing the first large-scale empirical quantification of *why* and *how* it fails for LLM-specific workloads, and more importantly, by identifying **decode TPS as the robust alternative**.

Equally important, the authors show that latency metrics (TTFT and TBT)—which might seem like natural candidates for SLO-driven autoscaling—exhibit a "cliff-like transition" (Section 3.3.2): they remain flat across a wide range of load levels and then shoot up abruptly when saturation is reached. This non-linearity makes them unsuitable for proportional control. A controller that scales proportionally to latency would over-provision massively during the early, flat portion of the curve and then scramble to catch up when the cliff is reached. The paper's use of latency metrics in a **negative feedback safety mechanism** (triggering fixed incremental adjustments only when thresholds are crossed) rather than as primary scaling signals is a key design insight derived from this empirical characterization.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

HeteroScale positions itself not as a new LLM serving system (it does not compete with vLLM or DistServe on inference efficiency) nor as a new scheduling algorithm in isolation (network-aware placement draws on established ideas), but as a **comprehensive autoscaling framework that bridges the gap between the inference engine layer and the cluster management layer.** The paper's conceptual contribution is the recognition that autoscaling for disaggregated LLM serving is not merely a matter of adding an HPA on top of existing serving infrastructure—it requires a fundamentally different approach that:

1. **Selects metrics based on empirical validation, not convention.** The paper's metric analysis (Section 3.3.2) is framed as the "first large-scale empirical analysis of autoscaling metrics for P/D disaggregated serving" and represents a deliberate break from the hardware-utilization-centric autoscaling paradigm. By demonstrating that decode TPS is robust while GPU utilization is not, the paper establishes that **the first design decision in an autoscaling system is metric selection**, and this decision requires workload-specific investigation, not assumption.

2. **Treats P/D balance as a first-class constraint, not an afterthought.** Prior autoscaling approaches scale each component independently. HeteroScale's coordinated scaling policy (Section 3.3.2) jointly computes required capacity for both pools from a single metric (decode TPS), always enforcing the target P/D ratio. This transforms scaling from a set of independent decisions into a coordinated architectural action that preserves the disaggregated system's performance characteristics under dynamic load.

3. **Elevates network topology and hardware heterogeneity to scheduling constraints.** The Deployment Group and RDMA Subgroup abstractions (Section 3.4) formalize the requirements that prior systems handled ad hoc: network affinity between prefill and decode instances, and priority-ordered allocation of scarce heterogeneous hardware. These abstractions make it possible to express policies like "place prefill and decode roles on different GPU types but under the same switch" as configurable constraints rather than manual operational procedures.

4. **Demonstrates at real production scale.** The paper's evaluation is not simulation-based or small-scale. HeteroScale manages tens of thousands of GPUs processing trillions of tokens daily, and the reported results (26.6 percentage point GPU utilization increase, hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours saved daily) come from production deployments. This scale is important because many challenges of autoscaling (oscillation, metric noise, coordination failures) only manifest at large scale with real workload variability—a system that works on a 16-GPU testbed may collapse under production dynamics.

The intellectual thread connecting all four of these positions is that **autoscaling for LLM serving is a systems integration problem that spans metric selection, policy design, scheduling, and operational stability**, and that addressing only one layer (e.g., better scheduling) while relying on naive autoscaling policies (e.g., independent HPAs with GPU utilization) yields most of the efficiency gains of disaggregation to be lost to poor resource allocation. The paper's unified approach—validated at the scale where these problems become existential rather than academic—is what distinguishes it from prior work that advanced individual components in isolation.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

HeteroScale is a production autoscaling system that functions as a **closed-loop controller** sitting between an organization's service-level objectives (SLOs) and the physical GPU cluster—it continuously monitors workload signals, decides how many prefill and decode instances should be running on which hardware, and executes those decisions through the underlying Kubernetes infrastructure. The system solves the problem of **coordinated resource allocation under architectural constraints**: unlike traditional autoscalers that treat each service component as an independent scaling target, HeteroScale recognizes that prefill and decode pools in a disaggregated LLM serving architecture are coupled—scaling one without the other in the right ratio creates bottlenecks—and that the placement of those instances across heterogeneous hardware and network topology directly determines whether the disaggregated architecture achieves its promised efficiency or collapses under communication overhead.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

HeteroScale is organized as a three-layer system with clear separation of concerns, illustrated in Figure 1:

1. **Autoscaling Layer with Policy Engine** — This is the brain of the system. It collects real-time metrics from running services, evaluates them against configurable policies (periodic time-based schedules or metrics-driven proportional/negative-feedback controllers), and produces scaling *decisions*: "Service X needs to scale out to N prefill instances and M decode instances at a P/D ratio of R." It does not decide *where* on the physical cluster to place those instances—that is delegated downward.

2. **Federated Pre-Scheduling Layer** — This layer translates the "what to scale" decisions from above into "where to place" decisions. It maintains a live topological view of all GPU resources organized hierarchically (data center → cluster → S2 switch → S1 switch → node → GPU), applies network affinity constraints (ensuring prefill and decode instances that must communicate are placed under the same switch), manages the priority-ordered allocation of heterogeneous hardware (giving scarce high-performance resources to services that require them), and enforces P/D ratio maintenance during scaling operations. It assembles scaling actions into concrete resource allocations and passes them downward for execution.

3. **Sub-cluster Scheduling Layer** — This is the execution layer that interfaces directly with the Kubernetes API server. It receives resource allocation instructions from the pre-scheduling layer, creates or updates the corresponding Custom Resource Definitions (CRDs), and exposes the node API upward for topology assembly. It also handles the lifecycle of Deployment Groups—the logical containers that bundle prefill and decode roles sharing a common scheduling domain.

The layers communicate through well-defined interfaces: the policy engine pushes scaling decisions to the pre-scheduling layer; the pre-scheduling layer resolves those decisions into specific placement instructions; the sub-cluster layer executes them against Kubernetes. A monitoring feedback loop closes the circle—metrics from running instances flow back to the policy engine, enabling it to evaluate whether its previous decisions achieved the desired state and adjust accordingly.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the policy engine layer, because it is where the core intellectual contribution lies: the empirical analysis that identified decode TPS as the robust scaling metric, and the two complementary control algorithms (proportional control for throughput metrics, negative feedback for latency metrics) that together implement coordinated scaling. Understanding *why* decode TPS was chosen and *how* it drives joint prefill-and-decode scaling is fundamental to everything else.

- **Second**, the workload-centric policy curation pipeline (Algorithm 1), because it explains how the system arrives at the optimal P/D ratio and scaling policy for a given service—a prerequisite for the coordinated scaling mechanism to function correctly at deployment time.

- **Third**, the federated pre-scheduling layer with its three key abstractions: the Deployment Group (enforcing network affinity), the RDMA Subgroup (prioritizing heterogeneous hardware), and the affinity-aware scheduling algorithm (Algorithm 4). This order is natural because it follows the flow of a scaling decision: first you decide *how much* to scale, then you decide *where* to place the new instances, and the placement decision must respect both network topology and hardware priority.

- **Fourth**, the P/D ratio maintenance mechanism—both the policy-layer enforcement of the target ratio during scaling decisions and the service-framework-level soft ratio maintenance that handles startup-order imbalances. This connects back to the policy engine (which enforces the ratio in its calculations) and forward to deployment (which must handle the reality that instances start asynchronously).

- **Fifth**, the system stability mechanisms (anti-flapping, disaster recovery) because these are the operational safeguards that make the theoretical control algorithms work in practice under noisy production metrics and unpredictable failures.

- **Sixth**, the extension to disaggregated Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) serving, which demonstrates the generality of the abstractions beyond the basic P/D disaggregation case.

This is primarily a **systems design and empirical validation paper** whose core idea is that autoscaling for disaggregated LLM serving requires coordinated, metrics-driven policies that maintain architectural balance between prefill and decode pools, combined with topology-aware scheduling that respects both network affinity for KV cache transfer and priority-ordered allocation of heterogeneous GPU hardware.

#### 3.4.1 The Autoscaling Layer and Policy Engine: Configuration Management and Control Loop

The autoscaling layer serves as the decision-making core of HeteroScale, integrating configuration management with a policy evaluation engine to create a closed-loop control system. The configuration management module provides an interface for backend engineers and operators to define per-service parameters aligned with desired SLOs and performance targets—this is where the target P/D ratio, scaling thresholds, cooling periods, and metric choices are specified. The policy engine runs as a periodic evaluation loop: it wakes up at a configurable interval, fetches the current real-time metric observations for each managed service, evaluates the configured policy against those observations, and issues scaling decisions (scale-out, scale-in, or no-change) to the federated pre-scheduling layer below.

The critical design choice here is that the policy engine does **not** execute scaling actions itself—it only computes what the target state should be. This separation of decision (what to scale) from execution (where to place) is what enables the federated pre-scheduling layer to optimize placement across the global resource pool without the policy engine needing to understand hardware topology. The policy engine operates on a logical view of the service: "this service needs X prefill instances and Y decode instances." The pre-scheduling layer resolves those logical counts onto physical hardware.

The monitoring component feeding back into the policy engine creates the closed-loop property: after a scaling action is executed, the policy engine observes the resulting metrics on the next evaluation cycle. If the scaling action was insufficient (metrics still above target), it can issue additional scale-out; if it overshot (metrics far below target), it can issue scale-in. The anti-flapping mechanisms (Section 3.6) ensure that this feedback loop does not oscillate.

#### 3.4.2 Empirical Metric Selection: Why Decode TPS and Not GPU Utilization

The paper's most important pre-design contribution is the empirical analysis in Section 3.3.2 that determines *which metric should drive scaling decisions*. This analysis is not an afterthought—it is the foundation upon which the entire policy design rests, because choosing the wrong metric would make any control algorithm unstable or ineffective regardless of its sophistication.

The authors group candidate metrics into three classes:

- **Throughput metrics**: Prefill TPS and decode TPS
- **Hardware-level metrics**: Prefill and decode GPU SM activity (the fraction of streaming multiprocessor cycles that are active) and GPU utilization (as reported by NVIDIA's metric, which measures the fraction of time the GPU was busy executing kernels)
- **Latency metrics**: Time-To-First-Token (TTFT), measuring initial response latency, and Time-Between-Tokens (TBT), measuring per-token generation speed

The empirical analysis uses metric traces from a production open-domain dialogue service with hundreds of GPUs (shown in Figure 2), with additional validation on a vision-language search service (Appendix A, Figure 8) and reported consistency across web search, long-form content understanding, real-time audio conversation, real-time video processing, and code generation workloads.

**Throughput metrics analysis.** Throughput metrics (Figure 2a) exhibit a high signal-to-noise ratio with clear differentiation between peak and off-peak periods. They respond quickly to traffic changes, enabling timely scaling operations. However, there is a subtlety: prefill TPS measurements can be unreliable when KV cache hits occur, because cached prefill operations bypass the full computation. The authors therefore restrict consideration to **"KV cache missed prefill TPS"** and decode TPS. Both are viable, but decode TPS is preferred for production deployment because it can be uniformly distributed across instances, whereas prefill instances often have heterogeneous hardware configurations that complicate metric normalization—a decode instance processing tokens at rate R has a well-defined meaning regardless of GPU type, while a prefill instance's throughput depends on both the prompt characteristics and the hardware.

**Hardware-level metrics analysis.** This is where the key negative result emerges. Prefill-stage hardware metrics (prefill GPU utilization, prefill SM activity) respond sensitively to load changes with high signal-to-noise ratios—the prefill phase is compute-intensive, so its hardware utilization is approximately linearly correlated with the workload it processes. However, **decode-stage hardware metrics (decode GPU utilization, decode SM activity) maintain persistently high values even when workload is low** (Figures 2c, 2d). The authors explain this as a consequence of the decode phase being memory-bound: a large fraction of the GPU's activity comes from KV cache storage management and data transfer operations. These memory operations keep the GPU's execution units busy (or at least, keep the GPU in a state that reports high utilization) regardless of whether the system is processing many requests or few. The practical consequence is that an autoscaler monitoring decode GPU utilization would observe 85% utilization during both peak load (where the system genuinely needs all its instances) and valley load (where it could easily scale down by 50%), making it impossible to distinguish when scaling is needed.

The SM activity metric on decode instances exhibits the same problem: it remains high even during low request volumes, making it unsuitable as a scaling signal.

**Latency metrics analysis.** Latency metrics (TTFT and TBT, Figure 2b) exhibit what the authors describe as a "cliff-like transition": they remain nearly flat across a wide range of workload levels, then shoot upward abruptly when the system approaches saturation. This non-linear behavior makes proportional control dangerous—a controller that tried to scale proportionally to latency would see flat latency during moderate load increases and do nothing, then suddenly see a spike and attempt a massive scale-out, likely overshooting. The authors note that TBT provides a clearer signal than TTFT, which suffers from a lower signal-to-noise ratio. However, both require fundamentally different control strategies than throughput metrics.

**The resulting design decision.** The analysis drives a bifurcation in the policy design. Throughput metrics (which vary proportionally with load) are suited for **proportional control**—the controller directly computes how many instances are needed based on the ratio of current throughput to target throughput per instance. Latency metrics (which exhibit threshold-driven non-linear behavior) require **negative feedback control**—the controller makes fixed incremental adjustments only when thresholds are crossed, functioning as a safety mechanism rather than a primary scaling driver.

For production deployment, **decode TPS is selected as the primary scaling metric.** The justification is multi-dimensional: (1) it has high signal-to-noise ratio and responds proportionally to load changes; (2) it directly aligns with business objectives (throughput is what users care about); (3) it can be uniformly distributed across instances without hardware-specific normalization; (4) it is simpler to configure than latency-based controllers, which require tuning multiple interdependent thresholds. The paper reports that the TPS-based policy manages 64% of the total GPU fleet under HeteroScale's control (Section 4.3).

#### 3.4.3 Coordinated Scaling with Proportional Control (Algorithm 2)

The proportional control algorithm (Algorithm 2 in Appendix B) is the primary scaling mechanism in HeteroScale. Its key innovation is not the proportional formula itself—which is standard—but its **coordinated application across the prefill and decode pools from a single metric.**

The algorithm operates as follows. Given a service, the system maintains:

- `$I_{\text{curr}}$`: the current total number of instances (across both prefill and decode pools)
- `$M_{\text{curr}}$`: the currently observed value of the scaling metric (e.g., aggregate decode TPS across all decode instances)
- `$M_{\text{target}}$`: the target value of the metric per instance, derived from pressure testing (Section 3.4.4)

The expected total instance count is computed as:

$$I_{\text{expected}} = I_{\text{curr}} \times \frac{M_{\text{curr}}}{M_{\text{target}}}$$

where `$I_{\text{expected}}$` is the total number of instances needed to process the current workload at the target per-instance utilization, `$I_{\text{curr}}$` is the current total instance count, `$M_{\text{curr}}$` is the current observed aggregate metric value, and `$M_{\text{target}}$` is the target metric value per instance (determined offline through pressure testing).

**What it computes:** The formula calculates the number of instances that would be needed if the per-instance throughput remained constant at `$M_{\text{target}}$` and the workload scaled linearly. If current throughput is twice the target, the system needs roughly twice as many instances. If throughput is half the target, it needs roughly half. The ratio `$R = I_{\text{expected}} / I_{\text{curr}}$` is computed, and if `$R$` exceeds `$1 + \theta_{\text{out}}$` (the scale-out threshold, providing a buffer to prevent oscillation), a scale-out is triggered; if `$R$` drops below `$1 - \theta_{\text{in}}$` (the scale-in threshold), a scale-in is triggered. Cooling periods `$C_{\text{out}}$` and `$C_{\text{in}}$` enforce minimum intervals between successive scaling actions of the same direction.

**Why this form:** The proportional formula assumes a linear relationship between instance count and throughput capacity, which is approximately valid for throughput metrics (doubling instances roughly doubles throughput, assuming the workload is parallelizable). It would be inappropriate for latency metrics, where the relationship between instances and latency is non-linear (adding instances reduces latency only up to the point where the system is no longer saturated, after which further instances have no effect). The hysteresis provided by `$\theta_{\text{out}}$` and `$\theta_{\text{in}}$` being non-zero (creating different trigger points for scaling up vs. down) is a standard anti-flapping measure that prevents the system from oscillating when the metric hovers near the target.

**The coordination mechanism.** After computing `$I_{\text{expected}}$` (the total instance count needed), the system applies the **target P/D ratio** to split this total into prefill and decode instance counts. Specifically:

- Let `$r_{\text{PD}}$` be the configured P/D ratio (e.g., 1P/3D means `$r_{\text{PD}} = 1/3$`)
- Prefill instances: `$I_{\text{prefill}} = I_{\text{expected}} \times \frac{r_{\text{PD}}}{1 + r_{\text{PD}}}$`
- Decode instances: `$I_{\text{decode}} = I_{\text{expected}} \times \frac{1}{1 + r_{\text{PD}}}$`

Both counts are scaled simultaneously—prefill and decode instances are always added or removed together in the appropriate ratio. This is the crucial design choice that prevents architectural imbalance: the system never scales one pool independently, which would risk starving one phase while the other sits idle. The metric driving this coordinated decision is decode TPS, measured on the decode pool, but the scaling action affects both pools jointly. The implicit assumption—validated by the observation that input-output length distributions remain relatively stable for a given service—is that decode TPS is a reliable proxy for total system load, and that prefill load scales proportionally with decode load under stable workload characteristics.

#### 3.4.4 Negative Feedback Control for Latency Metrics (Algorithm 3)

For latency metrics (TTFT and TBT), HeteroScale employs a fundamentally different control strategy based on **negative feedback** rather than proportional control. Algorithm 3 in Appendix B details this approach.

The algorithm monitors current latency `$L_{\text{curr}}$` against a target latency `$L_{\text{target}}$` (derived from SLO requirements). Instead of computing a continuous scaling factor, it uses a **multi-tier threshold system**:

- If `$L_{\text{curr}} \geq L_{\text{target}} \times \alpha_{\text{out}}$` (a severe breach, e.g., `$\alpha_{\text{out}} = 1.5$`), scale out by 20%: `$I_{\text{expected}} = I_{\text{curr}} \times 1.2$`
- Else if `$L_{\text{curr}} \geq L_{\text{target}} \times \beta_{\text{out}}$` (a moderate breach, e.g., `$\beta_{\text{out}} = 1.2$`), scale out by 10%: `$I_{\text{expected}} = I_{\text{curr}} \times 1.1$`
- Else if `$L_{\text{curr}} \leq L_{\text{target}} \times \gamma_{\text{in}}$` (latency is well below target, e.g., `$\gamma_{\text{in}} = 0.5$`), scale in by 5%: `$I_{\text{expected}} = I_{\text{curr}} \times 0.95$`
- Otherwise, make no change

Cooling periods `$C_{\text{out}}$` and `$C_{\text{in}}$` are enforced as in the proportional controller.

**Why this stepped, threshold-based approach instead of proportional control.** The justification is rooted in the "cliff-like" behavior of latency metrics observed in Figure 2b. Latency does not vary smoothly with instance count—it remains approximately constant across a wide range of under-saturated conditions, then increases sharply when the system approaches or exceeds its capacity. A proportional controller would observe flat latency during the under-saturated regime and conclude no scaling is needed, even as load increases toward the cliff. When latency finally spikes, the proportional formula would attempt a large correction (because the observed-to-target ratio would be large), likely overshooting—adding far more instances than necessary and wasting resources. The negative feedback approach avoids this by making **fixed, conservative adjustments only when SLO risk is detected**, regardless of the magnitude of the latency violation. It functions as a safety mechanism: the primary scaling decisions are made by the throughput-based proportional controller, and the latency-based negative feedback controller only intervenes when SLOs are threatened despite those decisions.

The authors explicitly acknowledge that this approach is difficult to tune in practice: "the nonlinear relationship between latency and resource allocation necessitates a multi-tier feedback-driven scaling mechanism. This design inevitably introduces numerous hyperparameters... achieving a balance between meeting SLOs and maximizing throughput demands that the system operates within a narrow and highly sensitive configuration range" (Section 4.2.2). This tuning difficulty is precisely why the TPS-based proportional controller is preferred as the primary mechanism and the latency-based controller is relegated to a backup safety role.

#### 3.4.5 The Periodic Scaling Policy: Time-Based Proactive Scaling

In addition to the reactive metrics-driven policies, HeteroScale supports a **periodic scaling policy** that adjusts resources based on time-of-day patterns. This policy enables proactive scaling based on expected workload patterns—for instance, scaling up before the morning traffic ramp begins, rather than waiting for metrics to detect the increase. The policy is configured as a schedule of time periods, each with static target instance counts and P/D ratios. At the configured times, the system automatically adjusts the service to the specified capacity.

The paper notes (Section 3.3.1) that periodic scaling is used in production for "services that operate under specific constraints or involve experimental configurations, which are not amenable to metrics-driven scaling policies." In the production deployment analysis (Section 4.3), the TPS-based policy yielded 10.0 percentage points higher GPU utilization and 11.1 percentage points higher SM activity compared to the periodic policy, confirming that the dynamic, metrics-driven approach is more efficient because it can maintain higher resource pressure by reacting to real-time fluctuations rather than following static schedules that must include safety margins for unexpected load.

#### 3.4.6 Workload-Centric Policy Curation (Algorithm 1)

Before a service can be deployed with HeteroScale, the system must determine the optimal P/D ratio and the best scaling policy for that service's specific workload profile. Algorithm 1 formalizes this as a three-stage pipeline:

**Stage 1: Pressure Testing.** Given a service `$S$` and its workload profile `$W$` (characterizing input length distribution, output length distribution, and request arrival patterns), the system runs a controlled pressure test to empirically determine:

- `$r_{\text{opt}}$`: the optimal P/D ratio that maximizes throughput while maintaining SLO compliance
- `$\hat{m}$`: the expected performance metric under load (e.g., decode TPS per instance at the optimal ratio)

The pressure test varies the P/D ratio across a range while subjecting the system to representative load, measuring throughput at each ratio. As shown in Figure 4 (Section 4.1), the relationship between P/D ratio and throughput is non-monotonic: there is a clear midrange peak, with underperformance on either side. At ratios that are too low (too few prefill instances), TTFT violations cap throughput despite idle decode capacity—requests queue up waiting for prefill processing. At ratios that are too high (too many prefill instances), TBT violations dampen throughput because the decode pool is overwhelmed by the prefill output rate.

The paper reports (Section 4.1) two concrete examples from experimentation on 16 nodes with eight H20 GPUs each:

- Service A: average input length ~3k tokens, output length ~350 tokens, I/O ratio 8.5, SLOs TTFT ≤1s and TBT ≤40ms. An optimal P/D ratio exists in the midrange.
- Service B: average input length ~7.8k tokens, output length ~700 tokens, I/O ratio 11, SLOs TTFT ≤1s and TBT ≤20ms. Again a midrange peak, but at a different ratio due to different workload characteristics.

The authors emphasize that in production experience, the optimal P/D ratio spans "a considerable range—from 1P/5D to 9P/1D—depending on the input-output length distribution, hardware configurations, and SLO priorities" (Section 4.1). This variability is what makes the pressure-testing step essential rather than relying on a fixed default ratio.

**Stage 2: Policy Simulation.** For each candidate scaling policy `$p$` in the set `$P$` (e.g., TPS-based proportional, GPU-utilization-based proportional, latency-based negative feedback), the system simulates the policy's behavior under the baseline conditions established in Stage 1. The simulation evaluates how each policy would respond to workload fluctuations, measuring metrics like stability (absence of oscillation), responsiveness (time to reach target capacity), and efficiency (resource utilization achieved).

**Stage 3: Policy Selection.** The system selects the policy `$p_{\text{opt}}$` that maximizes the chosen objective—specifically, "throughput while maintaining SLO compliance" (Section 3.3.3). The output of this pipeline is a triple: the optimal policy, the optimal P/D ratio, and the expected per-instance metric value. These become the configuration parameters fed into the policy engine at deployment time.

**Why this pipeline exists.** This curation process addresses a fundamental challenge: there is no universal "best" scaling policy or P/D ratio. A service with highly variable, unpredictable traffic (like an open-domain chatbot) might benefit most from reactive TPS-based proportional control, while a service with rigid diurnal patterns and experimental model configurations might be better served by periodic scaling. The curation pipeline makes the choice systematic rather than ad hoc, ensuring that each service gets a policy and ratio tuned to its specific workload characteristics before it enters production.

#### 3.4.7 The Federated Pre-Scheduling Layer: Translating Decisions into Placements

The federated pre-scheduling layer is the bridge between the logical scaling decisions made by the policy engine ("this service needs N more instances") and the physical reality of the GPU cluster ("these specific GPUs on these specific nodes under these specific switches are available and compatible"). It performs three functions, each implemented through a distinct abstraction.

**Topology Discovery and Resource Tree Construction.** At the start of each scheduling cycle, the layer builds a fresh **topological resource tree** (illustrated in Figure 3). This tree represents the entire available GPU capacity organized hierarchically: at the top are Virtual Data Centers (VDCs), which contain physical Kubernetes clusters, which contain S2 switches (bigpods), which contain S1 switches (minipods), which contain S0 switches (racks), which contain individual nodes with GPUs. Each node in the tree records the available GPU count and type at that level of aggregation.

The tree is built from information exposed upward by the sub-cluster scheduling layer's node API. It provides a live, eventually-consistent view of the cluster state. This view is **not** incrementally updated—it is rebuilt from scratch at the beginning of each scheduling cycle to avoid the state drift that would accumulate from incremental updates over many iterations. The cost of this rebuild is amortized across all scheduling decisions made within the cycle.

**Request Sorting and Prioritization.** All pending scaling requests generated by the policy engine are collected and sorted, primarily by **service priority**. This ensures that critical production services get first access to scarce resources before lower-priority services are considered. Within the same priority level, the ordering ensures deterministic scheduling behavior.

**Candidate Evaluation and Priority-Based Selection.** For each scale-out request, the scheduler identifies all valid placement options by evaluating:

- **Expansion of existing Deployment Groups:** Can the requested instances be added to a Deployment Group the service already has? This is the preferred option because it reuses existing network-domain placements and avoids creating new management overhead.

- **Creation of new Deployment Groups:** If existing groups lack capacity, the scheduler considers creating a new Deployment Group in a different, compatible network domain. This involves selecting an appropriate RDMA Subgroup (explained below) and establishing the network affinity constraint for the new group.

Each candidate placement is scored based on the priority of its associated RDMA Subgroup. The scheduler selects the placement that satisfies the service's affinity constraints while **minimizing the consumption of high-priority resources**. The key insight: a service with loose affinity requirements (e.g., only needing cluster-level co-location of prefill and decode) should be placed in a low-priority subgroup even if high-priority capacity is available, preserving scarce high-performance hardware for services that genuinely require tight network coupling.

**Virtual Allocation.** Once a placement decision is made, the chosen resources are virtually deducted from the topological tree for the remainder of the scheduling cycle. This atomic reservation prevents the same GPUs from being assigned to multiple requests within the same cycle. Resources freed by scale-in operations are NOT immediately added back—the entire tree is rebuilt at the next cycle, ensuring a consistent view.

**Scale-In Handling.** Scale-in is simpler: the scheduler selects one or more of the service's existing Deployment Groups to reduce, targeting those occupying **high-priority resource pools first**. The rationale: freeing scarce, high-value resources has greater benefit for the global cluster than freeing abundant, low-priority resources. This creates a natural "gravity" that pulls services away from premium hardware when they need to contract, making that hardware available for other services that require it.

#### 3.4.8 The Deployment Group Abstraction: Bundling Roles Under Network Affinity

The Deployment Group is the central scheduling abstraction in HeteroScale. It is a **logical container** that bundles the different roles of a P/D disaggregated service (prefill instances and decode instances) that must share a common scheduling domain. Its key characteristics:

**Shared Scheduling Domain.** All instances within a Deployment Group are bound by a common **network affinity constraint**. This constraint specifies the scope within which the instances must be co-located. The constraint is configurable per-service:

- For high-performance services where KV cache transfer latency is critical, the constraint may mandate placement under the **same S2 switch** (the highest level of aggregation within a data center, providing the lowest latency and highest bandwidth interconnect between the instances).
- For services with less stringent requirements, the constraint can be relaxed to the **same physical cluster** level (which may span multiple S2 switches with lower inter-switch bandwidth).

The purpose is to ensure that the large KV cache transfers between prefill and decode instances do not become a bottleneck. The authors' empirical observation (Section 1, Challenge 2) is that topology-agnostic placement—where prefill and decode instances land under different switches—can reduce available KV cache transfer bandwidth by approximately 20%, directly increasing transfer latency. By constraining placement to a single switch domain, the Deployment Group ensures that KV cache transfers occur over the highest-bandwidth, lowest-latency links available.

**Independent Scaling Roles.** Within a Deployment Group, the prefill and decode roles function as **independent deployment units** that can be scaled out or in separately, subject to the system's P/D ratio maintenance logic. This means a Deployment Group might start with a 1P/3D configuration and later scale to 2P/6D while remaining under the same S2 switch. The scaling of individual roles within a group does not create new Deployment Groups—it expands or contracts the existing group up to the capacity limits of its scheduling domain.

**Priority-Aware Group Creation.** When a service requests a scale-out and its existing Deployment Groups lack capacity in their scheduling domains, the scheduler faces a choice: create a new Deployment Group in a different network domain. The RDMA Subgroup priority system (Section 3.4.9) guides this decision. A service with low-affinity requirements (only cluster-level co-location) should have its new Deployment Group placed in a low-priority subgroup, even if this means the new group is in a different physical location or uses different hardware. This preserves the high-priority resources in the existing group's domain for other services that require them.

**Extending to Disaggregated MoE.** The Deployment Group abstraction generalizes naturally to disaggregated Mixture-of-Experts serving. In this architecture, the prefill stage itself is further disaggregated into attention (attn) instances and feed-forward network (ffn) instances. The Deployment Group for an MoE service includes these sub-roles and enforces a **hierarchical** affinity constraint: attn and ffn instances are co-located under the same S1 switch (tighter coupling, since they must communicate frequently during prefill), while the prefill instances (both attn and ffn) and decode instances are co-located under the same S2 switch. This enables dual-ratio control—maintaining the attn-to-ffn ratio within prefill replicas and the prefill-to-decode ratio across the service—while respecting the different communication bandwidth requirements at each level of the architecture.

#### 3.4.9 RDMA Subgroups: Prioritizing Heterogeneous Hardware Resources

In a large-scale production cluster, not all GPU capacity is equal. A rack containing multiple GPU types under a single S1 switch is a strategically valuable resource because it allows prefill and decode roles to be placed on **different, specialized hardware** (e.g., compute-optimized GPUs for prefill, memory-bandwidth-optimized GPUs for decode) while maintaining minimal network latency for KV cache transfer. HeteroScale formalizes this value through a **priority system implemented as RDMA Subgroups**.

An RDMA Subgroup is a logical collection of one or more S1 or S2 switches, classified into a distinct priority tier based on the hardware they contain. The paper defines three priority tiers, ranked from lowest to highest:

- **Low Priority: S2 Homogeneous GPU Subgroups.** These contain S2 switches where all underlying GPUs are of a single type (e.g., all H20s or all L20s). They are the most common and abundant resource configuration and are suitable for services that do not require heterogeneous hardware within their network domain. Most services running homogeneous P/D configurations should be placed here.

- **Medium Priority: S2 Heterogeneous GPU Subgroups.** In these subgroups, an S2 switch manages a mix of GPU types, but each of its underlying S1 switches remains homogeneous. For example, an S2 switch might have one S1 switch populated entirely with H20 GPUs and another S1 switch populated entirely with L20 GPUs. Prefill instances can be placed on the H20 S1 and decode instances on the L20 S1, achieving hardware specialization while staying within the same S2 domain for KV cache transfer. The S2-level co-location provides sufficient bandwidth for most workloads.

- **High Priority: S1 Heterogeneous GPU Subgroups.** These are the most valuable pools, containing S1 switches that directly connect machines with different GPU types. For example, a single S1 switch might have some nodes with H20 GPUs and others with L20 GPUs. This enables the most demanding heterogeneous P/D configurations with the tightest possible network affinity—prefill and decode instances communicate within a single S1 switch, providing the absolute minimum latency and maximum bandwidth for KV cache transfer.

**How priority guides scheduling.** When the affinity-aware scheduling algorithm (Algorithm 4) evaluates placement options for a scale-out request, it scores candidate placements based on the priority of the RDMA Subgroup they would consume. The scheduler then applies a **least-privilege principle**: it selects the lowest-priority placement that satisfies the service's affinity requirements. For a service with only cluster-level affinity needs, it will prefer to place instances in a low-priority homogeneous S2 subgroup, even if high-priority S1 heterogeneous capacity is available. The high-priority resources are reserved for services that explicitly require heterogeneous hardware under tight network coupling—for example, a service that specifies "prefill on H20, decode on L20, co-located under S1 switch."

This priority system prevents a common failure mode in shared clusters: services with loose requirements inadvertently consuming scarce, specialized hardware, forcing services that genuinely need that hardware into suboptimal placements. By making the priority of resource consumption an explicit scheduling consideration, HeteroScale ensures that the most valuable hardware is used where it provides the most benefit, maximizing global cluster efficiency.

#### 3.4.10 P/D Ratio Maintenance: Enforcement at Multiple Levels

Maintaining the target P/D ratio during dynamic scaling is non-trivial because of a fundamental tension: the ideal ratio is computed for the steady state, but scaling operations involve transient states where instances start asynchronously and may become ready in an unpredictable order. HeteroScale addresses this through a two-level mechanism.

**Policy-Level Enforcement During Scaling Decisions.** When the policy engine computes a scaling decision using the proportional control algorithm (Section 3.4.3), the P/D ratio is enforced mathematically: the total instance count `$I_{\text{expected}}$` is split into prefill and decode counts according to the target ratio, and both counts are scaled **simultaneously**. The system never issues a "scale only prefill" or "scale only decode" decision. The paper states this explicitly: "The prefill and decode instances are always scaled in or out simultaneously" (Section 3.4, P/D Ratio Maintenance). This is a deliberate design choice to prevent a scenario where one role scales successfully but the other fails due to resource constraints, creating an imbalanced state.

**Service-Framework-Level Soft Ratio Maintenance.** Even when both roles are scaled simultaneously, they may not become ready at the same time. Differences in container startup times, model loading sequences, and health check configurations can cause prefill instances to become ready before decode instances, or vice versa. This creates a temporary window where the effective P/D ratio—the ratio of *ready* prefill instances to *ready* decode instances—deviates from the target.

HeteroScale addresses this at the service framework level through a **service discovery gating mechanism.** If the ratio of ready prefill instances to ready decode instances deviates significantly from the configured ratio, service discovery registration for the **role with the larger quantity** is suspended. For example, if decode instances come up faster than prefill instances, creating a temporary ratio of 1P/5D when the target is 2P/5D, the "extra" decode instances will not be registered for service discovery. They will be held in a pending state until the prefill instances complete their registration and the ratio returns to within a tolerable range. The paper describes this as: "if the ratio of prefill to decode instances in ready state deviates significantly from the configured ratio, service discovery for the role with a larger quantity will be suspended, and registration will proceed only after the other instances from the other role complete their service discovery registration" (Section 3.4).

This mechanism ensures that traffic is only routed to a balanced set of instances, preventing SLO violations (particularly TTFT spikes) that would occur if, for example, many decode instances were ready and accepting requests but too few prefill instances were available to process the input prompts.

**Ratio Derivation.** The target P/D ratio used by both mechanisms is derived from service pressure tests and historical empirical data (Section 3.4, P/D Ratio Maintenance). In the online environment, the ratio is fixed—the paper does not implement dynamic ratio adaptation, though it is listed as future work (Section 6). The current approach applies a smooth transition when adjusting the ratio to avoid abrupt changes that could cause instability.

#### 3.4.11 System Stability Mechanisms: Anti-Flapping and Disaster Recovery

Autoscaling systems operating in production with real, noisy metrics face a persistent challenge: **oscillation.** If the controller reacts too aggressively to metric fluctuations, it can repeatedly scale up and scale down in rapid succession—a phenomenon known as flapping—wasting resources, creating operational churn, and potentially destabilizing the service. HeteroScale implements a multi-layered defense against this.

**Cooling Periods.** Both the proportional control (Algorithm 2) and negative feedback control (Algorithm 3) algorithms enforce minimum intervals between successive scaling actions: `$C_{\text{out}}$` for scale-out operations and `$C_{\text{in}}$` for scale-in operations. After any scaling event, the system will not issue another event of the same direction until the cooling period has elapsed. This prevents the controller from responding to the same metric observation multiple times before the effect of its previous action has propagated through the system.

**Hysteresis Thresholds.** The proportional controller uses different trigger points for scaling out (`$\theta_{\text{out}}$`) and scaling in (`$\theta_{\text{in}}$`), creating a buffer zone where neither action is taken. For example, if `$\theta_{\text{out}} = 0.2$` and `$\theta_{\text{in}} = 0.1$`, then the computed ratio `$R = I_{\text{expected}} / I_{\text{curr}}$` must exceed 1.2 to trigger scale-out but must drop below 0.9 to trigger scale-in. This means that after a scale-out event (which reduces `$R$` because more instances are now available), the new `$R$` value will naturally fall into the buffer zone rather than immediately triggering a scale-in. The hysteresis creates a stable region where minor metric fluctuations do not cause any action.

**Dampening Factors.** The system applies dampening factors to moderate the magnitude of scaling adjustments, preventing the controller from making drastic changes in a single step. Rather than scaling directly to `$I_{\text{expected}}$` (which might represent a large jump), the system may scale to a dampened target that moves only partway toward the computed value, smoothing the trajectory.

**Soft Scaling-In for Disaster Recovery.** A distinctive feature of HeteroScale's operational safety design is the **soft scaling-in** mechanism. When the policy engine decides to scale in (remove instances), the targeted instances are not immediately terminated. Instead, they are first **withdrawn from service discovery**—they stop receiving new requests—but kept running in an observation state. During this observation period, the system continuously monitors SLOs (particularly latency). If performance remains within targets, the instances are subsequently terminated. However, if performance degradation is detected during the observation window—suggesting that the scale-in decision was premature or that workload has unexpectedly increased—the instances are **reinstated immediately** into service discovery. This avoids the penalty of a full startup cycle (which can take minutes for large models) if the scale-in turns out to be wrong.

The paper notes additional disaster recovery measures: preserving critical state information to enable fast resumption after failures, and applying graceful degradation strategies (maintaining essential functionality while temporarily reducing non-critical services) when resources become constrained. These are described at a high level without detailed algorithmic specification, reflecting their nature as operational practices rather than algorithmic contributions.

#### 3.4.12 Extending to Disaggregated Mixture-of-Experts Serving

The paper notes that HeteroScale's abstractions extend naturally to disaggregated MoE serving (Section 3.4), where the prefill stage is further decomposed into attention (attn) and feed-forward network (ffn) sub-components. The Deployment Group abstraction accommodates this by bundling three roles (attn, ffn, decode) with hierarchical affinity constraints:

- Attn and ffn instances (both part of prefill) are co-located under a **high-affinity S1 switch**, since they must communicate intensively during the prefill computation.
- The entire prefill component (attn + ffn instances) and the decode instances are co-located under a **common S2 switch** for KV cache transfer.

This hierarchical scheduling enables **dual-ratio control**: maintaining a strict attn-to-ffn ratio within prefill replicas (for balanced prefill computation) and a proportional balance between prefill and decode components (for end-to-end architectural stability). The paper does not provide a detailed evaluation of the MoE extension, presenting it as a demonstration of the abstraction's generality rather than a separately validated contribution.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Autoscaling Metric Selection Is a First-Order Design Problem, Not an Implementation Detail

The paper's most conceptually distinctive contribution is not any specific algorithm but the **empirically-driven reframing of metric selection as the foundational design decision in an LLM autoscaling system.** Before HeteroScale, the dominant assumption across cloud autoscaling—inherited from Kubernetes HPA [29], cloud provider auto-scalers, and the broader systems community—was that hardware utilization metrics (CPU, GPU, memory) are adequate scaling signals, and that the interesting engineering challenge lies in the control algorithm that responds to those signals. This assumption is so deeply embedded that it is rarely questioned: you monitor GPU utilization, and when it crosses 80%, you scale out. The algorithm might be sophisticated (predictive, learning-based, PID-controlled), but the metric is taken as given.

HeteroScale's Section 3.3.2 analysis—visualized in Figure 2—demonstrates that this assumption is **fundamentally broken for the decode phase of LLM inference.** Decode GPU utilization and SM activity remain persistently high (around 85% and 53% in the non-autoscaled baseline) regardless of whether the system is serving peak traffic or sitting nearly idle. The root cause is architectural: the decode phase is memory-bandwidth-bound due to KV cache storage and data transfer operations, which keep the GPU's memory subsystem busy and report high utilization even when the compute units are underutilized and the actual token throughput is low.

This is not an incremental observation—it is a **diagnostic reframing** with cascading implications. If GPU utilization is misleading, then an entire class of autoscaling systems (everything built on hardware metric thresholds) is architecturally misguided for LLM serving. The metric selection problem moves from "pick a metric and set a threshold" to "validate that your metric is causally connected to actual workload pressure before designing any control logic." The paper's systematic comparison of three metric classes (throughput, hardware, latency) across their signal-to-noise ratios, linearity of response, and sensitivity to load changes establishes a **methodology for metric validation** that generalizes beyond the specific metrics studied. Other serving architectures or workload types might require different metrics, but the principle—empirically validate before designing—is the transferable insight.

The practical significance is demonstrated in production: the TPS-based policy manages 64% of the GPU fleet under HeteroScale and delivers 10.0 percentage points higher GPU utilization than the periodic policy (Section 4.3). The specific choice of decode TPS over prefill TPS (due to uniform distributability across heterogeneous instances) is a secondary refinement on top of the primary insight that **you must measure what actually tracks load, not what is conventionally measured.**

**Comparison to prior work:** Traditional autoscalers (Kubernetes HPA, VPA, KEDA [28, 29, 30]) treat metric selection as a configuration option, not a design problem. Learning-based autoscalers (AutoScale [14], DeepScaling [47]) focus on predicting future metric values but still operate on conventional hardware metrics. The growing recognition that "GPU utilization is a misleading metric" [6] exists in the infrastructure community as folk wisdom, but HeteroScale provides the first large-scale empirical characterization of *why* it fails for LLM decode specifically, and more importantly, demonstrates that the alternative (decode TPS) works robustly at scale. This transforms a vague concern into an actionable design principle.

**Evidence anchor:** Figure 2 (c, d) showing decode GPU utilization and SM activity remaining high under low load, contrasted with Figure 2a showing throughput metrics tracking load faithfully. Figure 6 (d, f) confirming that utilization-based and SM-activity-based autoscaling on decode instances produces poor scaling behavior in controlled experiments.

**Nature of contribution:** This is a **diagnostic reframing**—it changes what the field considers the central design question for LLM autoscaling (from "what control algorithm?" to "what metric?"). It is fundamental rather than incremental because it invalidates the default assumption underlying an entire class of existing systems, and it provides a principled methodology (empirical characterization across metric classes) rather than just a point solution (use decode TPS).

---

### Innovation 2: Coordinated Scaling from a Single Metric Maintains Architectural Balance Where Independent Scaling Cannot

The second conceptual contribution is the recognition that **in a disaggregated architecture with interdependent components, scaling decisions must be coordinated and derived from a unified signal rather than made independently per component.** Prior to HeteroScale, the natural approach to scaling a multi-component service was to attach an independent autoscaler to each component—each deployment gets its own HPA with its own metric, making its own decisions about when to scale up or down. This approach works for loosely coupled microservices where components can scale independently without creating systemic bottlenecks. It fails catastrophically for P/D disaggregated LLM serving because prefill and decode pools are **architecturally coupled**: the throughput of the entire system is determined by the bottleneck of whichever pool is under-provisioned relative to the other.

The paper's insight is that this coupling can be exploited rather than fought. Because prefill load and decode load scale together under stable workload characteristics (the input-output length distribution of a given service is relatively constant), a **single metric measured on one pool**—specifically, decode TPS—can serve as a sufficient statistic for the total load on both pools. The proportional control algorithm (Algorithm 2) uses this single metric to compute the required total capacity, then splits it into prefill and decode instance counts according to the pre-determined optimal P/D ratio. This transforms the autoscaling problem from "coordinate two independent controllers" to "drive everything from one signal with a fixed architectural split."

What makes this intellectually distinctive is that it reverses the conventional wisdom about separation of concerns. The standard argument for independent scaling is modularity: each component should manage its own resources based on its own observed load. HeteroScale argues that for this specific architectural pattern (P/D disaggregation), the **modularity is illusory** because the components are not truly independent—they are coupled by a hard architectural constraint (the P/D ratio), and independent decisions that violate this constraint produce worse outcomes than coordinated decisions based on a partially observed signal. The paper demonstrates this empirically through the P/D ratio sweep experiments (Figure 4, Section 4.1): deviations from the optimal ratio on either side cause SLO violations and throughput collapse, confirming that balance is a hard requirement, not a soft preference.

The decoupling of the P/D ratio from the scaling metric is also significant. The ratio is determined offline through pressure testing (Algorithm 1) based on workload characteristics and hardware configurations, while the scaling metric (decode TPS) captures real-time load. This separation means the ratio can be re-tuned when workload patterns shift without changing the autoscaling logic, and the autoscaling logic can operate on a uniform metric across services with different ratios. In production, the paper reports optimal ratios ranging from 1P/5D to 9P/1D (Section 4.1), but all these services can use the same TPS-based proportional controller—only the ratio parameter differs.

**Comparison to prior work:** Kubernetes HPA and similar autoscalers [29, 30] scale deployments independently with no coordination mechanism. Disaggregated serving systems (DistServe [57], SplitWise [39], Mooncake [41]) design the optimal static allocation but do not address dynamic scaling. The concept of coordinated scaling from a unified signal is not present in prior LLM serving or autoscaling literature. The closest analog might be coordinated scaling in multi-tier web applications, but those typically use response time or request rate as the unified metric—HeteroScale's contribution is the demonstration that this pattern applies to LLM inference with a domain-specific metric (decode TPS) and a domain-specific coupling constraint (P/D ratio).

**Evidence anchor:** Figure 4 showing throughput collapse when P/D ratio deviates from optimum; Figure 7a (production) showing prefill and decode instance counts tracking TPS variations in lockstep with the P/D ratio maintained; Section 4.3 reporting that the coordinated TPS-based policy achieves 10.0 pp higher GPU utilization than the static periodic policy.

**Nature of contribution:** This is a **architectural insight** about how to structure control for coupled components—it identifies that coordinated scaling from a unified signal is both necessary (because independent scaling causes imbalance) and sufficient (because decode TPS captures total load under stable workload characteristics). It is fundamental rather than incremental because it challenges the default modular scaling paradigm and provides an alternative that demonstrably works at production scale.

---

### Innovation 3: Network Topology and Hardware Heterogeneity as First-Class Scheduling Constraints United Under a Single Abstraction Hierarchy

The third conceptual contribution is the **unified treatment of network topology and hardware heterogeneity as a single, priority-ordered resource hierarchy** expressed through the Deployment Group and RDMA Subgroup abstractions. Prior work addressed these concerns separately: network-aware schedulers (Firmament [15], Paragon [12], Quincy [21]) optimized placement to minimize communication overhead but operated on homogeneous resource assumptions and did not integrate with autoscaling; heterogeneous resource managers (Heterogeneity-aware Scheduler [35], Tiresias [17], Gandiva [50]) optimized allocation across GPU types but treated network topology as a secondary concern or ignored it entirely. The P/D disaggregated LLM serving context demands both simultaneously because the two concerns are **coupled**: the value of heterogeneous hardware placement (prefill on compute-optimized GPUs, decode on memory-bandwidth-optimized GPUs) is only realized if prefill and decode instances are networked closely enough to transfer KV caches without becoming a bottleneck.

HeteroScale's innovation is to formalize this coupling through a **priority hierarchy of network-hardware combinations** (RDMA Subgroups). The three-tier classification—S2 homogeneous, S2 heterogeneous, S1 heterogeneous—encodes both the diversity of hardware available within a network domain and the tightness of the network coupling. An S1 heterogeneous subgroup (different GPU types under the same S1 switch) is the most valuable because it enables optimal hardware specialization with minimal KV cache transfer latency. An S2 homogeneous subgroup (all same-type GPUs under an S2 switch) is the least valuable—abundant but unable to support hardware specialization. By assigning these tiers explicit priorities and having the scheduler prefer lower-priority placements when service requirements permit, HeteroScale prevents the common cluster pathology where services with loose requirements consume scarce specialized resources.

What makes this more than a classification scheme is its integration into the autoscaling control loop. The scheduler (Algorithm 4) evaluates placement options at each scaling decision by considering both the service's affinity requirements and the priority of the resources that would be consumed. This means the system makes **different placement choices at different times** depending on current cluster state—a scale-out during off-peak hours might land in a high-priority subgroup because capacity is abundant, while the same service scaling during peak congestion might be directed to a lower-priority subgroup. The priority system provides the mechanism for this dynamic resource stewardship.

The Deployment Group abstraction complements this by making network affinity a **property of the scaling unit** rather than an external scheduling constraint. When a service scales, it either expands an existing Deployment Group (preserving its established network affinity) or creates a new one (incurring the cost of establishing a new scheduling domain). The choice between these options is not hard-coded but driven by the priority system, enabling the scheduler to make globally optimal tradeoffs between resource conservation and placement quality.

**Comparison to prior work:** Network-aware schedulers [8, 9, 15, 12] consider topology in placement but do not have a concept of heterogeneous resource priority, do not integrate with autoscaling, and do not provide the Deployment Group abstraction for maintaining affinity across scaling operations. Heterogeneous resource managers [35, 17, 23] handle GPU diversity but treat network topology as orthogonal. The paper's contribution is the **synthesis**—showing that these concerns are coupled in P/D disaggregated serving and providing a unified abstraction hierarchy that addresses both simultaneously within a dynamic scaling framework. This is a systems integration insight rather than a novel algorithm, but it is significant because without this unification, either network bottlenecks or hardware inefficiency would limit the gains achievable from disaggregation.

**Evidence anchor:** The empirical observation (Section 1, Challenge 2) that topology-agnostic placement reduces KV cache transfer bandwidth by ~20%; the 41% cost inflation figure for homogeneous vs. phase-aware heterogeneous deployment (Section 1, citing Zhu et al.); the production deployment managing tens of thousands of GPUs across heterogeneous hardware while maintaining SLO compliance (Section 4.3).

**Nature of contribution:** This is a **systems architecture contribution**—it provides abstractions that make a complex coupled constraint (network topology + hardware heterogeneity + dynamic scaling) tractable and optimizable. It is a refinement of existing ideas (network-aware scheduling, heterogeneous resource management) but the integration of these into a unified priority framework that operates within an autoscaling control loop is a non-trivial synthesis that enables practical deployment at scale.

---

### Innovation 4: Latency Metrics Require Fundamentally Different Control Strategies Than Throughput Metrics—And Should Be Safety Mechanisms, Not Primary Drivers

The fourth conceptual contribution is the **explicit bifurcation of control strategies based on the mathematical relationship between the metric and resource allocation**, and the specific recognition that latency metrics' "cliff-like" behavior (Figure 2b) makes them categorically unsuitable for proportional control and better suited as safety mechanisms. This is not merely an empirical observation about TTFT and TBT—it is a **control-theoretic insight** with implications for how any latency-sensitive LLM autoscaling system should be designed.

The core observation is that latency metrics exhibit a **non-linear, threshold-driven response** to resource changes: they remain approximately flat across a wide range of under-saturated load levels, then increase sharply when the system approaches saturation. This shape makes proportional control dangerous. A proportional controller computes `$I_{\text{expected}} = I_{\text{curr}} \times (L_{\text{curr}} / L_{\text{target}})$`. During the flat region, `$L_{\text{curr}} \approx L_{\text{target}}$`, so the controller computes `$I_{\text{expected}} \approx I_{\text{curr}}$` and takes no action—even as load increases and the system approaches the cliff. When latency finally spikes, the ratio `$L_{\text{curr}} / L_{\text{target}}$` becomes large, the controller requests a massive scale-out, the system overshoots, and the cycle repeats. The result is oscillation around the saturation point combined with chronic under-provisioning during the approach to saturation.

The paper's insight is that this is not a tuning problem (adjust thresholds, add dampening) but a **structural mismatch** between the metric's behavior and the control algorithm's assumptions. Proportional control assumes a roughly linear relationship between the controlled variable and the control input—an assumption that holds for throughput metrics (doubling instances roughly doubles throughput capacity) but fundamentally fails for latency metrics. The appropriate control structure for cliff-like metrics is **negative feedback with fixed incremental adjustments triggered by threshold crossings** (Algorithm 3), which makes conservative, bounded responses regardless of the magnitude of the latency violation.

But the deeper insight is that even with an appropriate control structure, latency-based autoscaling is **inherently harder to tune and less efficient** than throughput-based autoscaling. The paper explicitly notes: "tuning the associated hyperparameters poses a significant challenge... achieving a balance between meeting SLOs and maximizing throughput demands that the system operates within a narrow and highly sensitive configuration range" (Section 4.2.2). The multi-tier threshold design (severe breach → 20% scale-out, moderate breach → 10% scale-out, well below target → 5% scale-in) requires tuning the thresholds themselves, the scaling percentages, and the cooling periods—a combinatorially large and workload-sensitive parameter space. The production deployment analysis (Section 4.3) confirms this practical difficulty by showing that the TPS-based policy is overwhelmingly preferred (64% of the fleet) and achieves substantially higher utilization than the periodic policy.

The paper's resolution—use latency metrics as a **safety mechanism** (negative feedback controller that only activates when SLOs are threatened) while relying on throughput metrics as the **primary scaling driver** (proportional controller that efficiently tracks load)—is a principled division of labor that recognizes the complementary strengths of each metric class. Throughput metrics are efficient but do not directly capture user experience; latency metrics directly capture SLO compliance but are inefficient for continuous load tracking. The hybrid approach uses each where it is best suited.

**Comparison to prior work:** Traditional autoscalers typically use a single metric (CPU utilization or request latency) with a single control algorithm. Kubernetes HPA supports custom metrics but does not distinguish between metric types in its control logic—it applies the same proportional algorithm regardless of whether the metric is utilization, throughput, or latency. Learning-based approaches attempt to learn the relationship between metrics and optimal capacity but do not explicitly model the structural difference between linear and non-linear metrics. The paper's contribution is the **explicit categorization** of metrics by their mathematical relationship to resource allocation and the design of **fundamentally different controllers** for each category, with a clear argument for why latency metrics should be safety mechanisms rather than primary drivers.

**Evidence anchor:** Figure 2b showing the cliff-like latency behavior; Figure 6 (g, h) showing the oscillatory, overshooting behavior of TTFT-based and TBT-based autoscaling in controlled experiments; Section 4.2.2 documenting the hyperparameter tuning difficulty for latency-based controllers.

**Nature of contribution:** This is a **control-theoretic design principle** derived from empirical characterization. It is a refinement of existing control approaches (proportional control, negative feedback control are both well-known) but the insight that metric type determines controller type—and that latency metrics should be safety mechanisms, not primary drivers—is a meaningful conceptual contribution that would guide the design of any future LLM autoscaling system.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper does not use a static benchmark dataset in the traditional sense. Instead, it evaluates on **production workloads** at ByteDance, primarily an open-domain dialogue service using the Doubao-Seed-1.6-thinking model (Section 4 introduction). For controlled experiments, the authors replay production workload traces: an 8-hour segment from morning to mid-afternoon containing two prominent peaks and valleys (Section 4.2.1). Additional validation is performed on a vision-language search service (Appendix A, Figures 8–9), with the paper reporting that results hold for "web search, long-form content understanding, real-time audio conversation, real-time video processing, and code generation" workloads (Section 3.3.2). There is no mention of standard academic benchmarks (e.g., no MATH, HumanEval, or similar static datasets), which reflects the paper's focus on operational autoscaling rather than model quality evaluation.

- **Base model(s).** All experiments use the **Doubao-Seed-1.6-thinking** model (Section 4 introduction), a production LLM developed at ByteDance. The paper provides no architectural details (parameter count, architecture type, training data) beyond the model name. This is consistent with the paper's scope: the autoscaling system is model-agnostic, and the relevant properties for evaluation are the workload characteristics (input/output length distributions, diurnal patterns) rather than model internals. The absence of multi-model evaluation is a limitation discussed in the Critical Assessment. The FLOPs-matched comparison against larger pretrained models (which is central to some LLM inference papers) is entirely absent—this paper compares autoscaling strategies against each other, not inference efficiency against alternative serving architectures.

- **Metrics.** The primary evaluation metrics are **GPU utilization** (as reported by NVIDIA's metric, measuring the fraction of time the GPU was busy executing kernels) and **SM activity** (the fraction of streaming multiprocessor cycles that are active, from NVIDIA's `sm__cycles_active` counter). These are reported as percentage point changes (e.g., "26.6 percentage points increase") rather than relative improvements. Secondary metrics include **GPU-hours saved daily** (operational cost metric), and for controlled autoscaling policy comparisons, **throughput in Tokens Per Second (TPS)** measured separately for prefill and decode pools, **latency** (Time-To-First-Token, TTFT; Time-Between-Tokens, TBT), and qualitative assessment of scaling behavior (responsiveness to load changes, absence of oscillation) from the scaling event traces in Figure 6. The paper does not report standard deviation, confidence intervals, or statistical significance for any metric—all numbers are presented as point estimates from production observation or controlled trace replay.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against several baselines, though the comparison structure differs between production deployment analysis and controlled experiments:
  - **No autoscaling (static provisioning):** Services running without HeteroScale enabled, used as the baseline for the production deployment analysis (Section 4.3). This represents the state prior to HeteroScale deployment.
  - **Periodic (time-based) scaling policy:** HeteroScale's own periodic policy (Section 3.3.1), which adjusts resources according to pre-configured time-of-day schedules with static target instance counts and P/D ratios. Compared against the metrics-driven TPS-based policy in production (Section 4.3), showing that the dynamic policy achieves 10.0 pp higher GPU utilization and 11.1 pp higher SM activity.
  - **Various metric-driven policies (controlled experiments):** In the controlled trace-replay experiments (Section 4.2.2, Figure 6), eight candidate autoscaling metrics are each evaluated as the primary scaling signal: prefill TPS, decode TPS, prefill GPU utilization, decode GPU utilization, prefill SM activity, decode SM activity, TTFT, and TBT. All experiments began with identical prefill/decode instance counts, shared the same resource quotas, and used uniform scaling thresholds (Section 4.2.2). These are intra-system comparisons rather than comparisons against external autoscaling systems like Kubernetes HPA—the paper does not implement or evaluate an HPA baseline in the experiments, despite extensively motivating HPA's inadequacy in Section 2. This is a notable gap discussed in the Critical Assessment.
  - **Pre-HeteroScale vs. post-HeteroScale deployment:** A before/after comparison analyzing performance "on a recent date with a date prior to the scaled deployment" (Section 4.3), showing overall GPU utilization increased by 8.6 pp and SM activity by 6.5 pp across the fleet.

- **Compute accounting.** The paper measures compute in terms of **GPU count and GPU-hours.** The production deployment manages "tens of thousands of GPUs" (Abstract, Section 4). Efficiency is reported as GPU-hours saved daily ("hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours saved each day," Section 4.3), and as GPU count reduction: "overall GPU usage is reduced by 41.3%" for the specific dialogue service shown in Figure 7a (Section 4.3). For the controlled policy experiments, compute is measured in instance counts—the trace replay experiments track the number of prefill and decode instances over time as the autoscaler adjusts capacity (Figure 6). The paper does not use FLOPs or token-based compute accounting, which is appropriate given the systems-level focus: the cost of interest is GPU rental time, not computational operations. However, there is no accounting for the computational cost of the autoscaling system itself (metric collection, policy evaluation, scheduling cycles).

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not employ cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or repeated trials in the traditional ML sense. For the production deployment analysis (Section 4.3), the evaluation is observational: metrics are collected from live production traffic on a representative day and compared against a baseline day. The controlled policy experiments (Section 4.2.2) use a single trace replay—an 8-hour segment of production workload replayed under standardized initial conditions—with no reported repetition or variance analysis. The paper does not report error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical tests for any quantitative result. This is partly inherent to the systems evaluation context (production A/B testing at this scale is the "experiment," and statistical significance is established by the magnitude of effect over extended operation rather than repeated trials), but it limits the ability to assess whether observed differences (e.g., 26.6 pp vs. a hypothetical 24 pp) are reliable or within measurement noise. The paper also does not discuss potential confounding factors in the before/after production comparison—changes in workload patterns, model versions, or infrastructure between the two dates could influence utilization independently of HeteroScale.

---

### Main Quantitative Results

The evaluation is organized around two axes: (1) controlled trace-replay experiments that isolate the effect of metric choice on autoscaling behavior (Section 4.2), and (2) production deployment analysis that measures real-world efficiency gains at scale (Section 4.3). The P/D ratio sensitivity analysis (Section 4.1) provides necessary context for understanding why the ratio must be maintained during scaling. Unlike an ML paper with clear train/test splits, the experimental structure reflects an operational system where the "evaluation" is the system's behavior under production conditions.

---

#### P/D Ratio Sensitivity: Establishing the Necessity of Coordinated Scaling

Before evaluating autoscaling policies, the paper establishes why coordinated scaling (maintaining a specific P/D ratio) is necessary in the first place. Two services with different workload profiles are subjected to controlled experiments on 16 nodes, each with 8 H20 GPUs (Section 4.1, Figure 4):

- **Service A:** Average input ~3k tokens, output ~350 tokens (I/O ratio 8.5), SLOs TTFT ≤1s, TBT ≤40ms.
- **Service B:** Average input ~7.8k tokens, output ~700 tokens (I/O ratio 11), SLOs TTFT ≤1s, TBT ≤20ms.

For each service, the P/D ratio is swept while measuring maximum achievable throughput (TPS) under SLO constraints. The headline finding (Figure 4): **both services exhibit a clear midrange peak in throughput, with performance collapse on either side.** At ratios that are too low (too few prefill instances relative to decode), TTFT violations become the bottleneck—"scarce prefill instances cause TTFT to exceed preset threshold, capping throughput despite idle decode capacity." At ratios that are too high, TBT violations dominate—"excess prefill instances overwhelm decode resources, pushing TBT beyond its limit, and dampening maximum throughput."

The paper does not report exact throughput values at each ratio point in the text, but the key qualitative result is Figure 4's inverted-U shape: throughput increases from low ratios up to an optimum, then decreases. The optimal ratio differs between the two services—the paper states in production experience the ratio spans "from 1P/5D to 9P/1D" (Section 4.1), though specific optimal ratios for Services A and B are not numerically stated.

**Why this matters for the autoscaling evaluation:** This experiment establishes that deviating from the optimal P/D ratio—which is exactly what would happen if prefill and decode pools were scaled independently by separate autoscalers—directly causes SLO violations and throughput loss. The experiment thus provides empirical motivation for HeteroScale's coordinated scaling design, where both pools are always scaled together at the target ratio.

**Limitation:** The experiment is conducted on a fixed 16-node cluster with homogeneous H20 GPUs. It does not explore how the optimal P/D ratio changes with heterogeneous hardware configurations, different GPU types, or different cluster scales—all of which are central to HeteroScale's deployment context. The paper also does not quantify how sensitive throughput is to small ratio deviations (e.g., 5% off optimal vs. 20% off), which would inform how precisely the ratio must be maintained during scaling operations.

---

#### Controlled Policy Comparison: Which Metric Makes the Best Autoscaling Signal?

The core controlled experiment (Section 4.2.2, Figure 6) evaluates eight candidate metrics as autoscaling signals by replaying an 8-hour production workload trace from an open-domain dialogue service. All experiments begin with identical prefill and decode instance counts, share the same resource quotas, and use uniform scaling thresholds calibrated to induce scaling behavior under equivalent load conditions (Section 4.2.2). The output is scaling event traces—timing and magnitude of instance additions/removals—visualized in Figure 6 (and Appendix C, Figures 10–17). The evaluation is qualitative: which metrics produce scaling behavior that tracks workload, avoids oscillation, and maintains efficiency?

**TPS-based autoscaling (Figures 6a, 6b; Appendix Figures 10–11).** Prefill TPS and decode TPS both produce scaling behavior that "responds effectively to workload dynamics": during peak periods, the system promptly scales out; during valley periods, it efficiently scales in. The scaling traces closely follow the workload curve without visible oscillation or overshooting. The paper notes that within a given service, prefill and decode TPS are correlated (due to stable input-output length distributions), so either can serve as the scaling signal in principle. However, decode TPS is preferred for production because it "can be uniformly distributed across instances," whereas prefill instances often have heterogeneous hardware configurations that complicate metric normalization (Section 4.2.2).

**Prefill GPU utilization and SM activity (Figures 6c, 6e; Appendix Figures 12, 14).** Prefill GPU utilization "exhibits reasonable validity as a scaling signal" but is "less sensitive to workload changes compared with TPS-based metrics" (Section 4.2.2). The scaling trace shows some responsiveness to load, but the magnitude of scaling actions is smaller and the timing is delayed relative to TPS. Prefill SM activity "correlates well with workload fluctuations and may be a strong candidate for further investigation." The paper does not quantify the sensitivity difference (e.g., correlation coefficient, response time delay) beyond the qualitative comparison of the scaling traces.

**Decode GPU utilization and SM activity (Figures 6d, 6f; Appendix Figures 13, 15).** These are the **clear failures.** Decode GPU utilization "remains at a high level even when the workload decreases," producing scaling behavior that is essentially non-responsive to load changes. The scaling trace in Figure 6d shows almost no scale-in events during valleys—the metric never drops below the scale-in threshold because decode GPU utilization stays high due to KV cache memory pressure, exactly as predicted by the metric analysis in Section 3.3.2 (Figure 2c). Decode SM activity shows the same limitation. The paper's language is definitive: these metrics are "unsuitable as a reliable autoscaling indicator" and "ineffective for guiding scaling decisions."

**Latency-based autoscaling (Figures 6g, 6h; Appendix Figures 16–17).** TTFT-based autoscaling (Figure 6g) exhibits the oscillatory, overshooting behavior predicted by the cliff-like latency analysis: "scale-out actions tend to overshoot the required capacity, followed by frequent corrective adjustments." The trace shows multiple rapid scale-out/scale-in cycles. TBT-based autoscaling (Figure 6h) demonstrates "relatively smoother responses and fewer fluctuations compared to TTFT, reacting reasonably to workload variations." However, the paper emphasizes the **hyperparameter tuning difficulty** as a practical barrier: the multi-tier negative feedback design requires tuning threshold intervals, scaling percentages at each tier, and cooling periods—a "narrow and highly sensitive configuration range" (Section 4.2.2) that makes reliable deployment at scale "prohibitively difficult."

**Summary of the metric comparison.** The quantitative results are primarily visual (Figures 6, 10–17) rather than numerical. No single summary statistic (e.g., average latency, resource utilization, scaling accuracy) is reported for each metric. The conclusion is qualitative: TPS-based and prefill SM activity-based autoscaling "exhibit the most reliable responsiveness to workload variations" (Section 4.2.2). Decode TPS is selected as the production standard due to "strong alignment with business objectives and straightforward configurability."

**What is missing.** The controlled experiments do not report: (1) end-to-end SLO compliance rates under each policy; (2) total GPU-hours consumed over the 8-hour trace; (3) response time distributions; (4) any quantitative measure of oscillation (e.g., number of scaling events, time between direction changes). The evaluation is thorough as a qualitative characterization of scaling behavior but thin as a quantitative comparison. A reader looking for a table showing "Policy A: 92% SLO compliance, 850 GPU-hours; Policy B: 88% SLO compliance, 720 GPU-hours" will not find it.

---

#### Production Deployment Analysis: Real-World Efficiency Gains at Scale

The production evaluation (Section 4.3) reports results from HeteroScale's deployment across "tens of thousands of GPUs" processing "trillions of prefill tokens and hundreds of billions of decode tokens" daily. The evaluation takes three forms: a fleet-wide comparison, a policy-type comparison, and a granular single-service analysis.

**Fleet-wide comparison (before/after).** Comparing a representative day with HeteroScale enabled against a baseline prior to deployment:
- **26.6 percentage points increase in GPU utilization** for services with HeteroScale enabled compared to services without autoscaling (Section 4.3). This is the headline number.
- **9.2 percentage point increase in SM activity** for the same comparison.
- An additional before/after analysis comparing "a recent date with a date prior to the scaled deployment" shows overall GPU utilization increased by **8.6 percentage points** and SM activity by **6.5 percentage points** across the fleet (Section 4.3). The difference between the 26.6 pp number (comparing services with/without HeteroScale at the same time) and the 8.6 pp number (comparing the entire fleet before/after deployment) suggests that not all services adopted HeteroScale, so the fleet-wide average gain is diluted by services still running without autoscaling.
- **"Hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours are saved each day"** (Section 4.3). For context: 100,000 GPU-hours is equivalent to approximately 4,167 GPU-days or running roughly 4,167 H100-class GPUs continuously. At public cloud GPU pricing (~$2–4/GPU-hour for H100 equivalents), this represents daily savings in the range of $200K–$400K, though ByteDance runs on proprietary infrastructure so the cost is opportunity cost (freed GPUs available for other workloads) rather than cloud spend.

**Policy-type comparison.** Among services managed by HeteroScale:
- The **TPS-based policy manages 64% of the total GPU fleet** (Section 4.3).
- The TPS-based policy delivers **10.0 percentage points higher GPU utilization** and **11.1 percentage points higher SM activity** compared to the periodic policy (Section 4.3). This confirms that dynamic, metrics-driven scaling is substantially more efficient than static time-based schedules.

**Single-service granular analysis (Figure 7).** For the same open-domain dialogue service shown in Figure 2 (the non-autoscaled baseline):
- **GPU usage reduced by 41.3%** (Section 4.3). This is measured as total GPU count over time: the autoscaled service uses fewer GPUs during off-peak hours while maintaining capacity during peaks.
- Prefill GPU utilization increased from **46.8% to 76.2%** (a 29.4 pp increase). Prefill SM activity increased from **36.6% to 62.5%** (a 25.9 pp increase).
- Decode GPU utilization changed from **86.0% to 82.2%** (a slight decrease of 3.8 pp). This is expected and actually desirable—the decode stage is memory-bound, so its GPU utilization should remain high regardless of load; the small decrease reflects that the autoscaler is maintaining fewer decode instances during valleys while each instance remains saturated.
- Decode SM activity increased from **53.0% to 61.6%** (an 8.6 pp increase).
- Latency metrics (TTFT and TBT, Figure 7d) "both vary within a smaller, more stable range compared to the non-autoscaled service." The paper notes that the apparent large fluctuations in Figure 7d are "an artifact of normalization" (the y-axis scale amplifies small variations). Occasional TTFT spikes are attributed to "temporary P/D ratio imbalances that can occur during scaling operations," which the soft P/D ratio maintenance mechanism at the service discovery level addresses.
- Figure 7a shows that "the number of both prefill and decode instances closely tracks the TPS variations, confirming that the essential P/D ratio is well-maintained throughout the workload fluctuations." This is the key validation of the coordinated scaling design—the instance counts follow the load curve while maintaining the target ratio.

**Vision-language search service validation (Appendix A, Figures 8–9).** A separate vision-language search service shows "a similar pattern of improved performance and stability." The paper notes that "the metrics for the autoscaled service may appear similar to the non-autoscaled service at times because the policy's minimum instance count is set to a relatively high threshold"—a deliberate configuration to maintain higher instances during off-peak hours for service stability and readiness, prioritizing responsiveness over efficiency. This is an important operational detail: the autoscaling policy is not purely efficiency-maximizing; it incorporates a floor on instance count that trades some GPU savings for reduced cold-start latency during traffic surges.

**What is not reported.** The production evaluation does not include: (1) SLO violation rates (what fraction of requests exceed TTFT or TBT targets under HeteroScale vs. baseline); (2) tail latency (p95, p99 TTFT/TBT); (3) scaling responsiveness (time from load increase to capacity increase); (4) any measure of system stability during scaling events (e.g., request error rates during scale-in/scale-out transitions); (5) comparison against alternative autoscaling systems like Kubernetes HPA deployed on the same workload (the paper motivates HPA's inadequacy theoretically but never demonstrates it empirically with a side-by-side production comparison running HPA on the same services); (6) breakdown of GPU-hour savings by service type or workload characteristic; (7) any cost-benefit analysis of running the HeteroScale infrastructure itself (metric collection, policy engine, scheduling overhead).

---

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

The paper does not conduct traditional ablation studies in the ML sense (removing components and measuring degradation). The evaluation structure is closer to a systems paper, where different configurations are compared:

- **Metric selection as implicit ablation:** The controlled experiments (Section 4.2.2, Figures 6, 10–17) can be viewed as an ablation over the choice of scaling metric. Each of the eight candidate metrics is tested in isolation, demonstrating that metric choice fundamentally determines autoscaling quality. The key finding: **decode-stage hardware metrics (GPU utilization, SM activity) are effectively non-functional as scaling signals**, while throughput metrics (particularly decode TPS) are robust. This is the closest the paper comes to a component ablation, but it ablates the *input* to the system rather than internal components.

- **P/D ratio as implicit ablation:** The P/D ratio sweep experiments (Section 4.1, Figure 4) demonstrate that deviating from the optimal ratio degrades throughput. This validates the necessity of the coordinated scaling mechanism—without ratio maintenance, performance would collapse. It is not, however, an ablation of HeteroScale's ratio maintenance mechanism itself; it demonstrates *why* the mechanism is needed but not *how well* it works under dynamic conditions. The production traces in Figure 7a show qualitatively that the ratio is maintained, but there is no quantitative measurement of ratio drift (e.g., maximum deviation from target during scaling events).

- **Policy-type comparison (TPS-based vs. periodic):** The production comparison (Section 4.3) showing TPS-based policy achieves 10.0 pp higher GPU utilization and 11.1 pp higher SM activity than periodic policy is a comparison between two of HeteroScale's own policy modes, not a comparison against an external baseline. It demonstrates the value of dynamic, metrics-driven scaling over static schedules but does not isolate any specific component of the metrics-driven system.

- **Cross-modality validation (text dialogue vs. vision-language search):** The paper demonstrates that the same TPS-based autoscaling approach works for both an open-domain dialogue service and a vision-language search service (Appendix A, Figures 8–9). This provides evidence of modality-agnostic behavior, though both services use the same base model (Doubao-Seed-1.6-thinking) and the evaluation is qualitative (visual inspection of metric traces) rather than quantitative comparison. The paper claims in Section 3.3.2 that the same qualitative patterns hold for "web search, long-form content understanding, real-time audio conversation, real-time video processing, and code generation," but data for these additional modalities is not shown.

- **Negative result: latency-based autoscaling is impractical to tune at scale.** The controlled experiments (Figure 6g, 6h; Figures 16–17) and the associated discussion in Section 4.2.2 constitute a documented negative result: latency metrics (TTFT, TBT) can work as scaling signals in principle (TBT shows reasonable behavior) but the hyperparameter tuning burden makes them "prohibitively difficult" to deploy reliably at large scale. This negative result is arguably as informative as the positive TPS finding—it warns practitioners away from an approach that might seem natural (scale based on SLO latency) and explains why HeteroScale relegates latency to a safety mechanism role.

- **Missing: ablation of the coordinated scaling mechanism itself.** The paper never evaluates what happens if you run the TPS-based proportional controller on prefill and decode pools *independently* (i.e., each with its own TPS metric and independent scaling decisions) rather than coordinated through a single metric with enforced P/D ratio. This would be a natural ablation—demonstrating that independent scaling leads to ratio drift, SLO violations, or throughput loss compared to coordinated scaling—and its absence is notable. The P/D ratio experiments (Figure 4) demonstrate *why* ratio matters, and the production traces (Figure 7a) show that HeteroScale maintains it, but the causal chain "coordinated scaling → ratio maintained → SLOs met" is not closed with a controlled comparison against independent scaling.

- **Missing: ablation of the network-aware scheduling.** The paper provides no experiment comparing network-aware placement (Deployment Groups under same switch) against topology-agnostic placement (random pod distribution) in terms of KV cache transfer latency or end-to-end throughput. The ~20% bandwidth reduction figure cited in Section 1 is described as an "empirical observation" but is never reproduced in a controlled experiment or linked to a specific figure. The RDMA Subgroup priority system's effect on cluster-level resource efficiency is asserted but not quantified—there is no comparison of global GPU utilization with and without the priority-based placement logic.

- **Missing: ablation of the service-framework-level soft P/D ratio maintenance.** The paper describes the service discovery gating mechanism that prevents P/D ratio imbalance during asynchronous instance startup, and attributes occasional TTFT spikes in Figure 7d to temporary imbalances. However, there is no comparison of TTFT behavior with this mechanism enabled vs. disabled, making it impossible to assess how much of the latency stability is attributable to this mechanism vs. the policy-level coordinated scaling.

- **Missing: sensitivity to hyperparameter choices.** The controlled experiments use "uniform scaling thresholds calibrated to induce scaling behavior under equivalent load conditions" (Section 4.2.2), but there is no sweep over threshold values, cooling periods, or dampening factors. The production success of the TPS-based policy depends on these hyperparameters being well-tuned, but the paper provides no guidance on how to set them, no sensitivity analysis, and no discussion of failure modes when they are misconfigured.

---

### Critical Assessment

The paper makes four central claims: (1) metric selection is the foundational design decision for LLM autoscaling, and decode TPS is the robust choice; (2) coordinated scaling from a single metric maintains P/D balance better than independent scaling; (3) network topology-aware scheduling with heterogeneous hardware priority improves resource efficiency; (4) the system achieves 26.6 pp GPU utilization improvement and saves hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours daily in production. The experimental evidence supports these claims with varying degrees of strength, and several important gaps exist.

**Claim 1: Metric selection is foundational, and decode TPS is robust.** The evidence for this claim is the strongest in the paper. The empirical metric analysis (Section 3.3.2, Figure 2) clearly demonstrates that decode GPU utilization and SM activity are non-responsive to load, while throughput metrics track load faithfully. The controlled trace-replay experiments (Section 4.2.2, Figure 6) confirm that this translates directly into autoscaling behavior: decode-GPU-utilization-driven autoscaling fails to scale down during valleys, while TPS-driven autoscaling responds appropriately.

However, the strength of this evidence must be qualified. The metric characterization in Figure 2 is from a single service (open-domain dialogue) with a single model (Doubao-Seed-1.6-thinking). The paper asserts that the same patterns hold across multiple modalities (Section 3.3.2 lists six workload types) but only shows data for two (dialogue in Figure 2, vision-language search in Appendix Figure 8). The claim that decode GPU utilization is universally misleading is plausible given the architectural reason (the decode phase is memory-bound due to KV cache, so its GPU utilization reflects memory operations rather than computational load), but the paper does not demonstrate this across GPU architectures (the experiments use H20 and L20 GPUs; behavior on H100, A100, or other architectures with different memory bandwidth characteristics could differ). The selection of decode TPS over prefill TPS is motivated by practical concerns (uniform distributability across heterogeneous instances) rather than empirical superiority—the controlled experiments show both prefill and decode TPS perform well (Figures 6a, 6b).

**What would strengthen this claim:** Quantitative metrics comparing the eight candidate signals (correlation with ground-truth load, response delay, oscillation count, SLO compliance rate during trace replay) rather than qualitative visual assessment of scaling traces. Validation on additional GPU architectures and model families.

**Claim 2: Coordinated scaling maintains P/D balance.** The evidence for this claim is indirect. The P/D ratio sweep experiments (Figure 4) convincingly demonstrate that the P/D ratio matters—throughput collapses when the ratio deviates from optimal. This establishes the *necessity* of ratio maintenance but does not evaluate HeteroScale's *mechanism* for maintaining it. The production trace in Figure 7a shows prefill and decode instance counts tracking TPS variations in lockstep, which is consistent with coordinated scaling working correctly, but there is no controlled comparison showing that independent scaling of prefill and decode pools (each with its own TPS-based HPA) would lead to ratio drift and throughput loss. This is a significant gap because the entire motivation for coordinated scaling is that independent scaling causes architectural imbalance—but the paper never demonstrates this imbalance experimentally. The P/D ratio experiments (Figure 4) show what happens at *static* imbalanced ratios, not what happens when independent autoscalers cause the ratio to *dynamically drift*.

The service-framework-level soft ratio maintenance is mentioned as addressing startup-order imbalances that cause temporary ratio drift, but its effectiveness is not quantified. The paper notes that occasional TTFT spikes in Figure 7d are "caused by temporary P/D ratio imbalances that can occur during scaling operations" and states that the soft ratio mechanism "controls the registration of new instances to prevent such imbalances." This implies that the mechanism reduces but does not eliminate the problem—if it eliminated it entirely, there would be no spikes to observe. The residual spike frequency and magnitude are not characterized.

**What would strengthen this claim:** A controlled experiment running the same workload trace with coordinated scaling (HeteroScale's approach) vs. independent scaling (separate TPS-based HPAs on prefill and decode pools), measuring P/D ratio drift, SLO violation rates, and total GPU consumption. A comparison of TTFT stability with the soft ratio maintenance mechanism enabled vs. disabled.

**Claim 3: Network topology-aware scheduling improves resource efficiency.** This is the least empirically supported claim in the paper. The ~20% KV cache transfer bandwidth reduction from topology-agnostic placement is cited (Section 1) but never traced to a specific experiment, figure, or measurement methodology. The Deployment Group and RDMA Subgroup abstractions are described in detail (Section 3.4), and their integration into the scheduling algorithm is specified (Algorithm 4), but there is **no experimental evaluation of their impact.** No experiment compares network-aware placement against topology-agnostic placement. No experiment measures KV cache transfer latency under different placement strategies. No experiment quantifies how much the RDMA Subgroup priority system improves global resource efficiency or prevents high-priority resource consumption by low-affinity services.

The production deployment results (Section 4.3) show that HeteroScale as a whole improves GPU utilization by 26.6 pp, but this is the combined effect of the metrics-driven coordinated scaling policy AND the network-aware scheduling. It is impossible to attribute how much of the gain comes from better scaling decisions vs. better placement decisions. The paper does not attempt to disentangle these effects through ablation or component-level measurement.

**What would strengthen this claim:** A controlled experiment measuring end-to-end throughput and latency with network-affine placement (same S2 switch) vs. cross-switch placement, holding all other factors constant. A production analysis showing GPU utilization or throughput differences between services placed with RDMA Subgroup priority optimization vs. random placement within the same autoscaling policy. Any quantification of the fraction of GPU-hour savings attributable to scheduling improvements vs. scaling policy improvements.

**Claim 4: 26.6 pp GPU utilization improvement and hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours saved daily.** These production numbers are impressive in magnitude and reflect real operational impact. However, the methodology for arriving at them is not fully specified, which limits their interpretability.

The 26.6 pp figure compares "services with HeteroScale enabled" against "services without autoscaling" on a "representative day" (Section 4.3). Several methodological questions are unanswered: Were the services compared serving similar workloads? Were they using the same hardware? Was the comparison done on the same day (controlling for diurnal patterns and any infrastructure changes) or on different days? How many services and GPUs contributed to each side of the comparison? If the "without autoscaling" services were statically provisioned for peak load (which is typical), then some of the 26.6 pp gain reflects the autoscaler scaling *down* during valleys—a baseline that any autoscaler (including a simple time-based schedule) could improve upon. The additional comparison against the periodic policy (10.0 pp gain) isolates HeteroScale's metrics-driven approach from the time-based baseline, which is more informative about the system's specific contribution.

The "hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours saved daily" is a consequential number but is reported without the denominator: what is the total GPU-hour consumption of the fleet? Savings of 200K GPU-hours on a fleet consuming 2M GPU-hours daily (10% savings) has different implications than savings of 200K GPU-hours on a fleet consuming 500K GPU-hours daily (40% savings). The paper also does not specify whether "saved" means GPUs were powered down or simply made available for other workloads—in a shared cluster, freed GPUs are typically reallocated to other services, so the savings represent increased cluster-wide throughput capacity rather than reduced energy consumption or hardware count.

The before/after fleet-wide comparison (8.6 pp GPU utilization increase, 6.5 pp SM activity increase) is a weaker but more comprehensive metric—it includes services that did not adopt HeteroScale, so the gain is diluted. The paper does not report the adoption rate (what fraction of the fleet runs HeteroScale), which would allow back-calculation of the per-service gain.

**What would strengthen this claim:** A more detailed methodology section for the production evaluation specifying comparison dates, service selection criteria, hardware homogeneity between compared groups, and statistical methodology (were metrics averaged over the day? over a week?). Reporting the total fleet GPU-hours to contextualize the "hundreds of thousands" figure. A breakdown of utilization gains by service type or workload characteristic. An SLO compliance comparison (not just utilization) to ensure the efficiency gains did not come at the cost of degraded user experience.

**General methodological limitations across all experiments:**

- **No error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical tests anywhere in the paper.** Every number is a point estimate. For the production deployment analysis, this is partially defensible—when you observe hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours saved daily over extended operation, the effect is real regardless of p-values. But for the controlled experiments (P/D ratio sweep, trace replay), the absence of any variance characterization makes it impossible to distinguish genuine differences from noise, particularly for the finer-grained comparisons (e.g., prefill SM activity vs. prefill TPS, which are described as both being viable).

- **Single model (Doubao-Seed-1.6-thinking).** The paper's claims about metric behavior (decode GPU utilization being misleading, TPS being robust) depend on the decode phase being memory-bound, which is a property of the model architecture and serving configuration. Larger models with different KV cache sizes, different batch sizes, or different quantization schemes could exhibit different GPU utilization patterns. The paper does not evaluate on multiple model families or scales.

- **No external baseline comparison.** The paper extensively motivates why Kubernetes HPA and traditional autoscalers fail for P/D disaggregated LLM serving (Section 2), but never deploys an HPA on the same workload and compares it against HeteroScale. The controlled experiments compare HeteroScale's own policies against each other (TPS vs. utilization vs. periodic) but not against an external system. This means the paper demonstrates that HeteroScale works well, not that it works *better than the alternative*—the alternative (HPA) is only argued against theoretically. Given that HPA supports custom metrics (including TPS), a reader might reasonably ask: if you configure an HPA with decode TPS as the custom metric, does it still fail? If so, why—is it the lack of coordination, the lack of network-aware scheduling, or something else? The paper does not answer this.

- **The P/D ratio experiments (Figure 4) use homogeneous H20 GPUs**, while HeteroScale's key value proposition includes heterogeneous hardware support. The optimal P/D ratio almost certainly depends on the relative compute characteristics of the prefill and decode hardware—prefill on a compute-optimized GPU vs. a general-purpose GPU would shift the ratio. The paper does not explore this interaction.

- **Production evaluation does not report SLO compliance.** The entire purpose of autoscaling is to maintain performance while reducing cost, but the production evaluation only reports cost-side metrics (GPU utilization, GPU-hours saved). The paper states in Section 4.3 that "these efficiency improvements are achieved while upholding all Service Level Objectives (SLOs)" (from the Abstract) and that "TTFT and TBT both vary within a smaller, more stable range" (Section 4.3), but there is no quantification of SLO violation rates, p95/p99 latency, or throughput under peak load relative to SLO targets. The reader must trust that SLOs were maintained based on the latency traces in Figure 7d, which show occasional TTFT spikes that the paper acknowledges are "caused by temporary P/D ratio imbalances." Without SLO compliance data, it is possible that the 41.3% GPU reduction for the dialogue service (Section 4.3) came with a small but real degradation in tail latency that was deemed acceptable in production—the paper does not provide the evidence to evaluate this.

Despite these limitations, the paper's experimental contribution is substantial: it provides the first large-scale empirical characterization of autoscaling metrics for P/D disaggregated LLM serving, convincingly demonstrates that conventional hardware metrics fail for the decode phase and that TPS-based proportional control works robustly in production, and reports real operational savings at a scale that most systems papers cannot match. The weaknesses are concentrated in the evaluation of the scheduling components (network affinity, RDMA Subgroup priority) and in the absence of external baseline comparisons and SLO compliance quantification—areas where the paper asserts benefits that the experiments do not directly test.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### Limitation 1: The Difficulty Estimation Cost Is Unaccounted For in Reported Efficiency Gains

**The assumption or constraint.** HeteroScale's compute-optimal policy selection relies on estimating each prompt's difficulty *before* allocating the inference budget. The paper's method for doing so—generating 2048 samples per question and averaging either ground-truth correctness (oracle) or PRM final-answer scores (predicted)—is extraordinarily expensive. The paper acknowledges this explicitly (Section 3.2):

> "estimating difficulty in this way still incurs additional computation cost during inference... our experiments do not account for this cost largely for simplicity"

At 2048 samples per question, the difficulty estimation step alone consumes more compute than the largest test-time budgets studied (256–512 generations). This means the reported 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N are computed *after* difficulty is known, without amortizing the cost of learning it.

**The consequence.** In a realistic deployment, the total cost would be difficulty estimation + strategy execution, and the former could dominate the latter—potentially eliminating or even reversing the claimed efficiency gains. A practitioner who reads "4× more efficient than best-of-N" and deploys the system naively would discover that the difficulty estimation overhead consumes more budget than the "savings" from adaptive allocation. The paper's predicted difficulty bins (using PRM scores instead of ground-truth labels) partially address the oracle problem but do not address the cost problem—they still require generating and scoring 2048 samples per prompt.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 3.2 describes the 2048-sample estimation procedure. The 4× efficiency claims appear in Figures 4 and 8, where the compute-optimal curves are compared against best-of-N at equal generation budgets, with no cost term for difficulty estimation. There is no experiment that measures total cost (estimation + execution) or that sweeps the number of estimation samples to find a cost-quality tradeoff.

**Mitigation status.** The paper flags this as "a key avenue for future work" (Section 3.2) and suggests training models to predict difficulty directly from the question text, or using adaptive estimation that amortizes difficulty assessment into the solution process. No such model is developed or evaluated. Until this gap is closed, the 4× figure should be understood as an **upper bound on achievable efficiency** in a deployment where difficulty estimation cost is externalized.

---

### Limitation 2: Hard Problems Remain Essentially Unsolved—Test-Time Compute Cannot Create Capability

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper's entire framework assumes the base model has a non-trivial probability of producing correct solutions on a given problem. The compute-optimal strategies work by amplifying this existing capability—search finds correct solutions already present in the proposal distribution, and revisions refine answers that are roughly on the right track. For problems where the base model's pass@1 is near zero, no amount of test-time compute helps.

**The consequence.** Across all methods—search, revisions, and their compute-optimal combinations—the hardest questions (difficulty bin 5) show **near-zero improvement** regardless of compute budget. In Figure 3 (right), bin 5 accuracy hovers at 1–3% for all search methods and all budgets. In Figure 7 (right), bin 5 shows roughly 2–3% accuracy irrespective of the sequential-to-parallel ratio. In the FLOPs-matched comparison (Figure 9), the bin 5 scaling line is essentially flat near 0–5%. A practitioner deploying this system on a problem distribution that skews toward genuinely hard problems—those outside the base model's training distribution or requiring capabilities it has not acquired—will see essentially no benefit from any amount of test-time compute. The only path forward for such problems is scaling pretraining (larger model or more data).

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The difficulty-bin breakdowns in Figures 3 (right), 7 (right), and 9 all show bin 5 performance flat and near zero. The paper is transparent about this (Section 7 takeaway box) and explicitly frames test-time compute as "amplifying existing capability rather than creating it from nothing."

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to solve this—it is a fundamental boundary condition, not a fixable limitation of the method. The implication for practitioners is that difficulty estimation serves a dual purpose: it not only selects the right test-time strategy but also identifies problems where test-time compute is futile and the only recourse is a larger model. The ambiguity is whether there exists an intermediate regime where problems are slightly too hard for the current base model but could become solvable with a different form of test-time compute (e.g., tool use, retrieval, code execution) that the paper does not explore.

---

### Limitation 3: Revisions and PRM Search Are Studied Independently, Not Combined

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper studies two complementary axes—PRM-guided search (modifying output selection) and iterative revisions (modifying the proposal distribution)—but never combines them. Section 8 explicitly acknowledges:

> "we did not experiment with PRM tree-search techniques in combination with revisions"

Each mechanism shows complementary strengths: revisions help most on easy problems (local refinement of roughly-correct answers), while PRM search helps most on medium problems (global exploration to find correct solutions the model wouldn't produce by random sampling alone). The paper demonstrates that each mechanism individually, when applied through a compute-optimal difficulty-conditioned policy, achieves roughly 4× efficiency gains over best-of-N. But the two mechanisms could potentially be combined: use the revision model as the proposal distribution within beam search, or use the PRM to guide which revisions to pursue rather than blindly generating long revision chains.

**The consequence.** The reported results represent a **lower bound** on what a fully integrated system could achieve. A practitioner reading the paper might reasonably conclude that revisions and search are alternative strategies to be chosen between, when in fact they operate on different parts of the inference pipeline and could be composed. The gap between what the paper demonstrates and what is possible with full integration is unknown—it could be marginal (if the mechanisms' benefits overlap) or substantial (if they are truly complementary). The paper's compute-optimal policy selects the best *single* strategy per difficulty bin, not the best *combination*.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly states the omission in Section 8 (Future Work). There is no experiment combining the two mechanisms, no ablation showing the marginal benefit of one given the other, and no discussion of what the integration would look like architecturally.

**Mitigation status.** This is listed as future work. The paper's conceptual framework (proposal distribution vs. verifier as independent scaling axes) provides the intellectual scaffolding for combination, but no implementation or evaluation exists. For practitioners, this means the paper's 4× efficiency figure may be conservative—but also that deploying both mechanisms simultaneously requires engineering work and performance characterization that the paper does not provide.

---

### Limitation 4: Single Benchmark, Single Model Family, and Unvalidated Generalization

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use the MATH benchmark (500 test questions) with PaLM 2-S* as the base model. The authors state they "believe this model is representative of the capabilities of many contemporary LLMs" (Section 4), but this claim is unverified. The findings could be model-specific or benchmark-specific in several concrete ways:

- The PRM's quality and over-optimization behavior depend on PaLM 2-S*'s output distribution. A model with different calibration properties, error patterns, or base accuracy on MATH could exhibit different difficulty-dependent scaling curves.
- The revision model's ability to learn from incorrect in-context examples depends on the base model's in-context learning capabilities, which vary substantially across model families (some models are much better at in-context learning than others).
- The MATH benchmark consists of competition-level math problems requiring multi-step symbolic reasoning. It is unclear whether the difficulty-dependent patterns (beam search hurting easy problems due to PRM over-optimization, revisions helping easy problems, no strategy helping hard problems) generalize to other reasoning domains.

**The consequence.** A practitioner deploying this approach on a different model (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, Llama) or a different task (code generation, logical reasoning, scientific QA) cannot assume the specific findings will transfer. The optimal policy—which search algorithm, what beam width, what sequential-to-parallel ratio—is selected per difficulty bin based on PaLM 2-S*'s behavior on MATH. Different models and tasks may have different optimal strategies even at the same difficulty level. The paper's *methodology* for discovering the optimal strategy (difficulty estimation, policy sweep, cross-validation) would need to be re-executed, not just the *results* applied.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 4 describes the model and dataset. The paper provides no experiments on other benchmarks, other model families, or other task types. The appendices use a single model variant (the revision model and PRM are both fine-tuned from PaLM 2-S*).

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim generality beyond its experimental setup, and the methodology is designed to be re-applicable—the compute-optimal framework can be instantiated on any model and benchmark by re-running the pressure tests and policy sweeps. However, the cost of doing so (2048 samples per question for difficulty estimation, plus policy sweeps) makes this non-trivial. A practitioner porting HeteroScale to a new domain faces a substantial upfront characterization investment with no guarantee that the qualitative patterns will replicate.

---

### Limitation 5: The FLOPs-Matched Comparison Uses a Weak Pretraining Baseline

**The assumption or constraint.** The FLOPs-matched comparison in Section 7 pits PaLM 2-S* with compute-optimal test-time scaling against a model with approximately 14× more parameters trained on the same data and evaluated with **greedy decoding only**—no majority voting, no best-of-N, no test-time compute augmentation of any kind. Furthermore, the larger model scales only parameters (not training data), which the authors acknowledge departs from compute-optimal pretraining (Hoffmann et al., 2022):

> "We choose this setting as it is representative of a canonical approach to scaling pretraining compute and leave the analysis of compute-optimal scaling of pretraining compute where the data and parameters are both scaled equally to future work."

**The consequence.** The reported advantages of test-time compute over pretraining—particularly the headline results showing the smaller model with test-time compute outperforming the 14× larger model on easy-to-medium questions—are measured against a baseline that is likely **weaker than a properly optimized pretraining investment**. A Chinchilla-optimal model (scaling both parameters and data) trained with 14× more total FLOPs would outperform a parameter-only-scaled model. Giving the larger model even a modest test-time compute budget (best-of-8 or best-of-16) would create a much stronger baseline. The paper's finding that "test-time compute can substitute for pretraining" must therefore be qualified: it substitutes for a *specific form* of pretraining scaling (parameter-only, greedy decoding) but may not substitute for compute-optimal pretraining combined with its own test-time compute augmentation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 7 describes the FLOPs-matched methodology and acknowledges the parameter-only scaling choice. Figure 9 shows the comparison results, with the 14× larger model's greedy performance shown as stars at three R values. The paper does not include a variant where the larger model gets any test-time compute budget.

**Mitigation status.** The paper explicitly flags the parameter-only scaling as a limitation and frames the comparison as a first step rather than a definitive answer. The authors suggest future work on compute-optimal pretraining baselines. For practitioners, the practical takeaway—that test-time compute is most valuable on easy-to-medium problems and at low R ratios—is directionally useful regardless of baseline strength, but the quantitative claim that test-time compute can match a 14× larger model should be treated as an **upper bound** on the substitution rate that would likely shrink against a stronger pretraining baseline.

### Limitation 6: The Revision Model Has a 38% Correct-to-Incorrect Reversion Rate with No Principled Fix

**The assumption or constraint.** The revision model is trained exclusively on sequences where all in-context answers are incorrect, followed by a correct target answer. During training, the model never sees a sequence where the current answer is already correct and should be preserved. At test time, when the revision chain encounters a correct answer (produced in an earlier revision step), the model has no learned behavior for recognizing and preserving it, and may incorrectly "revise" it into a wrong answer.

**The consequence.** The paper reports that approximately **38% of correct answers get converted back to incorrect ones** during revision chains (Section 6.1). This creates a fundamental tension: longer revision chains improve the probability of reaching a correct answer at some point in the chain (Figure 6, left, shows pass@1 improving through step 20), but also increase the probability that a correct answer, once found, will be subsequently destroyed. The paper mitigates this with post-hoc selection across the chain (majority voting or verifier-based selection), which picks the best answer from any point in the chain rather than always taking the final revision. This is a patch, not a fix—it means the system generates many revisions, most of which are wasted because they overwrite already-correct answers, and then relies on the verifier or voting to recover the correct answer from earlier in the chain. This is inherently inefficient: compute is spent generating revisions that are counterproductive.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Section 6.1 reports the 38% reversion rate. The ReST^EM experiment (Appendix K, Figure 16) provides additional evidence of revision training fragility: attempting to further optimize the revision model with on-policy RL-style training caused performance to **degrade** with sequential revisions, suggesting the revision approach is sensitive to training methodology in ways that are not fully understood.

**Mitigation status.** The paper acknowledges the problem and applies selection mechanisms as a workaround, but does not develop a principled solution—such as training the model to recognize when no revision is needed, or adding "no change needed" trajectories to the training data. The dependence on post-hoc selection means that if the verifier used for selection is imperfect (and the paper demonstrates PRM over-optimization is a real problem), correct answers rescued from earlier in the chain might be misranked and discarded. The negative ReST^EM result further suggests that the revision training recipe is brittle and that naively attempting to improve it can backfire. A practitioner deploying revision-based test-time strategies must accept that roughly a third of the revision compute budget is spent destroying good answers, and that the recovery of those answers depends on a reliable verifier or voting mechanism.
