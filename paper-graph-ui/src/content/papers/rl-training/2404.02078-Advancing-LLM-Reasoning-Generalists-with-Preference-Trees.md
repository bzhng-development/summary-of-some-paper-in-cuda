# Advancing LLM Reasoning Generalists with Preference Trees

**ArXiv:** [2404.02078](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02078)

## 🎯 Pitch

This paper introduces **EURUS**, a suite of LLMs finetuned from Mistral-7B and CodeLlama-70B that achieve state-of-the-art open-source performance on diverse reasoning benchmarks, and the accompanying dataset **ULTRAINTERACT**, a tree-structured alignment corpus specifically designed for complex reasoning.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper introduces **EURUS**, a suite of LLMs finetuned from Mistral-7B and CodeLlama-70B that achieve state-of-the-art open-source performance on diverse reasoning benchmarks, and the accompanying dataset **ULTRAINTERACT**, a tree-structured alignment corpus specifically designed for complex reasoning. ULTRAINTERACT constructs a **preference tree** for each instruction—a binary tree where correct and incorrect actions are paired at every turn to facilitate preference learning—encompassing three design elements: diverse planning strategies with code-as-action execution (e.g., chain-of-thought or modularization programming followed by Python code generation), multi-turn interaction trajectories with environment feedback and GPT-4 critique (e.g., the model refines solutions across up to five turns after receiving execution errors and expert suggestions), and pairwise correct-incorrect action data organized across turns rather than only at trajectory endpoints. EURUS-70B achieves 33.3% pass@1 on LeetCode and 32.6% on TheoremQA, substantially outperforming existing open-source models by margins exceeding 13.3%, and matches GPT-3.5 Turbo in aggregate reasoning performance across 12 benchmarks. The paper further derives a novel reward modeling objective that augments the Bradley-Terry loss with an explicit term to increase chosen-action rewards and decrease rejected-action rewards, establishing that the absolute value of chosen-data rewards—not merely the margin between chosen and rejected—correlates positively with reasoning performance, and that preference learning algorithms like DPO which optimize only relative differences degrade on reasoning tasks while KTO and NCA, which increase absolute chosen rewards, yield consistent gains.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: Open-Source LLMs Lag in General Reasoning

The fundamental gap this paper addresses is the persistent underperformance of open-source LLMs on complex, general-purpose reasoning tasks compared to proprietary models like GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4. While the open-source community has made remarkable progress in building *specialists*—models that excel at either math (Luo et al., 2023a; Yue et al., 2023; Toshniwal et al., 2024) or coding (Wei et al., 2023; Guo et al., 2024a; Zheng et al., 2024)—these models remain, as the authors state, "well behind proprietary models" when it comes to tackling a diverse range of challenging problems that require *general* reasoning capability. The paper's Figure 1 illustrates this vividly: on LeetCode and TheoremQA, two challenging out-of-distribution benchmarks, existing open-source models cluster far below GPT-3.5 Turbo, with even 70B-parameter models failing to bridge the gap.

This problem matters for several reasons that extend beyond academic benchmarking:

- **Reproducibility and transparency**: The most advanced open-source general-purpose models (DeepSeek-AI, 2024; Bai et al., 2023; Touvron et al., 2023) maintain their alignment recipes confidential, which the paper notes "further hinders the replication and development of open-source reasoning models." Without understanding *how* alignment contributes to reasoning capability, the open-source community cannot systematically improve.
- **Specialization without generalization**: Specialist models that excel at math but fail at coding, or vice versa, are impractical for real-world deployment where users pose diverse problems. A model that can solve competition-level math but cannot write correct Python code to implement its solution is incomplete.
- **The capability ceiling**: Even the best open-source models at the time of this work were far from matching GPT-4's 74.8% average across the 12 benchmarks evaluated (Table 3). The best ~70B open-source model, DeepSeek-LM-67B-Chat, achieves 53.5%—a gap of over 21 percentage points. Closing this gap is practically significant for applications where proprietary API access is infeasible due to cost, latency, privacy, or regulatory constraints.

### Why This Gap Exists: Two Interlocking Shortfalls

The paper identifies two specific causes for this performance gap, framing them as the twin bottlenecks that ULTRAINTERACT and EURUS are designed to address:

**Shortfall 1: Lack of high-quality alignment data specifically designed for reasoning.** Existing alignment datasets are overwhelmingly oriented toward open-ended conversation and general helpfulness. The paper contrasts three data paradigms in Figure 2:

- Datasets like HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022) provide single-turn preference pairs where human annotators compare two complete responses—this captures general conversational preference but provides no signal about intermediate reasoning steps, no multi-turn interaction with tools, and no structured feedback on *where* a solution went wrong.
- Datasets like CodeActInstruct (Wang et al., 2024) and Code-Feedback (Zheng et al., 2024) provide trajectories where the model interacts with a code interpreter, but these focus narrowly on code execution feedback without incorporating the diversity of reasoning patterns (chain-of-thought, modularization, tool creation) that complex problems require.
- Neither paradigm provides **preference annotations at intermediate steps within a trajectory**. HH-RLHF compares only final outputs; CodeActInstruct provides execution feedback but not structured pairwise data (correct vs. incorrect actions at each step) suitable for preference learning algorithms like DPO or KTO.

The consequence is that when models are fine-tuned on existing alignment data, they learn to produce *conversationally appropriate* responses but do not learn the structured reasoning patterns—decomposing problems, generating code as executable actions, interpreting error messages, refining based on critique—that complex math, coding, and logic problems demand.

**Shortfall 2: Underexploration of preference learning for reasoning tasks.** Preference learning algorithms—DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024), NCA (Chen et al., 2024a)—have been extensively studied and validated in the context of open-domain chatbots, where they effectively align models with human preferences for helpfulness and harmlessness. However, their behavior on reasoning tasks is poorly understood. The paper cites recent research showing that DPO can actually *degrade* performance on reasoning tasks, while newer algorithms like KTO and NCA show positive effects. But as the authors note, "a deep understanding of preference learning, specifically its efficacy on complex reasoning, is not yet established."

This is a genuinely puzzling situation. In open-domain chat, DPO is highly effective—the relative preference between two responses (this one is more helpful than that one) is a sufficient signal. But reasoning tasks have a fundamentally different structure: there are objectively correct and incorrect answers, the space of correct answers is much smaller than the space of incorrect ones, and the *absolute* quality of a solution matters—not just whether it is better than a worse alternative. The paper's hypothesis (developed in Section 6.1) is that DPO's focus on optimizing *relative* differences between chosen and rejected responses, while ignoring the absolute reward values, makes it ill-suited for reasoning where a model needs to produce solutions that are *unconditionally* correct, not just better than a particular incorrect alternative.

### Prior Approaches and Their Limitations

The paper positions itself against several strands of prior work, each of which has significant limitations:

**Specialist models that sacrifice generality.** Models like WizardMath-7B (Luo et al., 2023a), MAmmoTH-7B (Yue et al., 2023), and OpenMath-Mistral-7B (Toshniwal et al., 2024) achieve strong math performance but cannot handle coding tasks. Magicoder-S-DS-6.7B (Wei et al., 2023) and OpenCodeInterpreter (Zheng et al., 2024) excel at coding but underperform on math. Table 3 illustrates this: MAGicoder achieves 75.6% on HumanEval (coding) but only 16.4% on GSM-Plus (math); WizardMath achieves 54.6% on GSM-Plus but only 50.0% on HumanEval. A general reasoning model needs to perform well across *both* domains simultaneously.

**General-purpose models with opaque alignment recipes.** Models like DeepSeek-LM-67B-Chat and QWen1.5-72B-Chat achieve relatively strong overall performance (53.5% and 52.2% averages in Table 3), but their alignment procedures are confidential. The open-source community cannot replicate, analyze, or improve upon these recipes. This opacity is a barrier to scientific progress in understanding how alignment data and preference learning contribute to reasoning capabilities.

**Existing preference data that doesn't capture reasoning structure.** The dominant preference dataset used in the open-source community, UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023), provides high-quality pairwise preferences for general conversation. But its pairs compare *complete responses* from different models on open-ended prompts. This structure is mismatched for reasoning in two ways: (1) it provides no intermediate step-level signal—the model cannot learn *where* in a solution it went wrong, only that one entire solution is preferred over another; (2) it is annotated by LLM-as-a-judge (GPT-4), which may not be reliable for evaluating the correctness of complex mathematical or logical reasoning, especially when the judge model itself makes errors.

**Preference learning algorithms validated only for chat.** DPO, the most widely adopted preference learning algorithm, was developed and validated in the context of dialogue alignment. Its derivation from the Bradley-Terry model optimizes the *relative likelihood* of chosen over rejected responses. The paper's analysis in Section 6.1 (Figure 5) reveals a critical problem in the reasoning context: during DPO training, the rewards of *chosen* data actually *decrease* (going below zero), even as the margin between chosen and rejected increases and the loss goes down. The model learns to distinguish correct from incorrect, but its absolute confidence in correct answers drops. For reasoning tasks where the model must generate correct answers from scratch (not just select between options), this decreasing confidence in correct solutions is detrimental—a phenomenon that prior work had not characterized.

### How This Paper Positions Itself

The paper frames its contributions as addressing both bottlenecks simultaneously through a unified data-and-algorithm approach:

**On the data side**, ULTRAINTERACT is positioned as a new kind of alignment dataset that synthesizes three design principles missing from prior work: (1) it is domain-diverse, spanning math, coding, and logic with problems selected for difficulty (specifically, problems that GPT-3.5 Turbo fails to solve); (2) it is structured as *preference trees* where correct and incorrect actions are paired at every turn in a multi-turn trajectory, unlike prior datasets that only compare final outputs; (3) it encodes *interaction*—the model learns not just to generate solutions but to interpret execution feedback (from a Python interpreter) and textual critique (from GPT-4 given ground-truth answers) and refine its solutions across multiple turns.

The tree structure (Figure 2) is a deliberate conceptual innovation. A standard preference dataset is flat: pairs of (chosen, rejected) at the trajectory level. ULTRAINTERACT's tree means that if a model makes a mistake at turn 2, that mistake is explicitly paired with the correct action from turn 2 of another trajectory branch. The model can learn *per-step* corrections rather than only trajectory-level preferences. This is computationally analogous to process supervision (Lightman et al., 2023) but constructed through interaction with an environment and critique model rather than human annotation.

**On the algorithm side**, the paper positions its contribution as both empirical analysis and methodological innovation. The empirical analysis (Section 6.1) explains *why* DPO fails on reasoning while KTO and NCA succeed—the absolute reward values matter, not just relative differences—providing a diagnostic that future work can build on. The methodological innovation is the augmented reward modeling objective ($\mathcal{L}_{\text{ULTRAINTERACT}}$) that combines the Bradley-Terry loss (for relative preference) with an explicit term $\mathcal{L}_{\text{DR}}$ that directly pushes chosen-action rewards up and rejected-action rewards down. This objective is motivated by the empirical finding that higher absolute chosen rewards correlate with better reasoning performance, and it represents a concrete algorithmic fix for the DPO limitation.

The paper also positions EURUS as a demonstration that these two innovations—tree-structured reasoning alignment data and reward modeling that respects absolute values—can close a significant portion of the gap between open-source and proprietary models. The 7B EURUS variant outperforming models 5× larger and the 70B variant matching GPT-3.5 Turbo on aggregate reasoning is presented not just as a new SOTA but as evidence that the alignment data and preference learning recipe are the key differentiators, not raw model scale.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

This paper constructs a **data generation and model training pipeline** that produces LLMs capable of solving complex math, coding, and logic problems through multi-step reasoning, code execution, and iterative refinement from feedback. The core idea is that existing alignment datasets focus on single-turn conversational preference, but complex reasoning demands a fundamentally different data structure—a **preference tree** where correct and incorrect actions are paired at every step of a multi-turn solution trajectory, allowing models to learn per-step corrections rather than just trajectory-level preferences.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The system has three major components connected in a pipeline:

1. **ULTRAINTERACT Data Generation Engine** — Takes diverse reasoning instructions (from 12 established datasets spanning math, coding, and logic) and produces a preference tree for each instruction by: (a) having an actor model (GPT-3.5 Turbo) generate step-by-step solutions using randomly chosen planning strategies and code-as-action execution, (b) interacting with a Python interpreter ("environment") and a GPT-4 critique model across multiple turns, and (c) pairing correct and incorrect actions at each turn into structured preference pairs. The output is a dataset of 86K instructions with 220K action pairs organized as imbalanced binary trees.

2. **EURUS Model Training Pipeline** — Takes the ULTRAINTERACT dataset and combines it with existing open-source data (UltraChat, ShareGPT, OpenOrca) to perform: (a) supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on all 287K correct actions from the preference trees, (b) preference learning using KTO, NCA, or DPO on all 220K multi-turn trajectory pairs (augmented with UltraFeedback pairs), and (c) reward model training using a novel objective that combines Bradley-Terry with an explicit absolute-reward term. Initialization is from Mistral-7B or CodeLlama-70B.

3. **EURUS-RM-7B Reward Model** — A separate model trained on ULTRAINTERACT action pairs (both multi-turn trajectories and augmented single-turn pairs) plus UltraFeedback and UltraSafety data, using the novel combined objective. This model scores candidate solutions for reranking and is evaluated on standard RM benchmarks.

Information flows: raw instructions from 12 datasets → GPT-3.5 Turbo generates solutions with environment/critique interaction → correct and incorrect actions are paired at each turn → preference trees are stored → SFT trains on all correct leaf nodes (discarding interaction history) → preference learning trains on the pairwise data → EURUS models are evaluated on OOD reasoning benchmarks.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the ULTRAINTERACT data construction pipeline (§3.4 ULTRAINTERACT Construction), because the entire approach rests on the quality and structure of this data. We trace the full generation process: instruction selection, action generation with environment/critique interaction, and preference pair construction across multiple turns.
- **Second**, the supervised fine-tuning recipe (§3.4 Supervised Fine-Tuning), since SFT establishes the base reasoning capability before any preference learning is applied. We examine what data is used, why interaction history is discarded, and how general-purpose data is mixed in.
- **Third**, the preference learning investigation (§3.4 Preference Learning), including why DPO degrades reasoning performance while KTO and NCA improve it, and the empirical analysis that motivates the novel reward modeling objective.
- **Fourth**, the reward modeling objective and training (§3.4 Reward Modeling), since the augmented loss function is a methodological contribution derived from the preference learning analysis.
- **Fifth**, the evaluation framework (§3.4 Evaluation Design), to understand how the models are tested and why the chosen benchmarks constitute a rigorous OOD assessment.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **data-and-training methodology paper** whose core idea is that tree-structured, multi-turn reasoning trajectories with per-step preference pairs—combined with preference learning algorithms that respect absolute reward values—produce LLMs that generalize across diverse reasoning domains.

---

#### 3.4.1 ULTRAINTERACT Construction

The ULTRAINTERACT dataset is constructed through a multi-stage pipeline that transforms raw reasoning problems into structured preference trees. The pipeline has four stages: (1) instruction selection, (2) action generation with environment interaction, (3) critique generation, and (4) preference pair construction across turns.

**Instruction Selection (Section 2.1).** The paper targets three reasoning domains—math problem-solving, code generation, and logical reasoning—and selects 12 established datasets (Table 6). The selection criteria are described explicitly: "We select challenging problems that GPT-3.5-Turbo fails to solve" and "We intentionally restrict the selection of the datasets to those with ground-truth solutions, aiming to ensure high-quality oversight signals rather than relying on LLM-as-a-judge annotation." This is a deliberate design choice: by filtering for problems that are difficult for a strong model, the resulting data provides training signal on the types of problems where improvement is most needed. By requiring ground-truth solutions, the paper avoids the reliability issues of using LLMs to judge correctness—a known weakness for complex reasoning tasks where even GPT-4 makes errors.

The diversity mechanisms are explicit at the instruction level. For each dataset, the paper includes "distinct reasoning patterns based on question categories or formulations necessary to solve the problems." For example, MathQA divides problems into categories by topic and annotates a formula indicating the reasoning pattern; the paper applies stratified sampling to select "at most five problems for each pattern" and prioritizes long-tail categories. For TabMWP, only difficulty levels 4 and 5 are kept since easier problems are "too easy for current state-of-the-art models." For NumGLUE, three of the eight reasoning tasks are discarded due to simplicity. This curation ensures that the instructions are not merely numerous but are concentrated in the difficulty regime where training signal is most valuable.

**Code-focused datasets require special handling for evaluation.** CodeContest and TACO provide competition-level programming problems with test cases, but "part of the questions in TACO only contain ground-truth solutions and do not contain test cases for evaluation." For these, GPT-4 generates 12 test case inputs (4 basic, 4 edge cases, 4 large numbers), and the ground-truth solution is executed to produce expected outputs. Magicoder-Evol-Instruct, the only dataset without test cases or ground-truth solutions, is used exclusively for SFT (not preference learning) with GPT-4 Turbo judging correctness during interaction—a necessary compromise that the paper explicitly acknowledges limits preference pair construction for this subset.

**Action Generation and Environment Interaction (Section 2.2).** For each selected instruction, the paper generates solution trajectories through an actor model (GPT-3.5 Turbo, unless otherwise specified) connected to two external components: a Python interpreter (the "environment") and a GPT-4 critique model (a "proxy for user"). The generation process at each turn follows a specific sequence:

First, the actor model decomposes the input problem. The paper states: "the actor model first decomposes the input problem into several sub-problems and then solves each by generating Python code pieces as actions and using the environment to execute them." To promote solution diversity—critical for preventing the model from memorizing answer patterns—"the actor model randomly samples one reasoning schema in the form of either CoT (Wei et al., 2022) or modularization programming (Qian et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2023)." Chain-of-thought (CoT) means the model reasons step-by-step in natural language before writing code; modularization programming means the model creates reusable tools (functions) to solve sub-problems. The choice is randomized per trajectory to ensure the training data covers both strategies.

The actor generates "actions in text or code to solve each sub-problem, with each step being marked by explicit notations." The paper's Figure 3 shows the notation scheme: `<thought>` tags enclose natural language reasoning steps like "Step 1: First, let's calculate the total...," `<execute>` tags enclose Python code blocks like `total_books_child = x * 7`, and `<solution>` tags enclose the final answer. This structured formatting makes the reasoning process machine-readable—the Python interpreter can directly execute `<execute>` blocks, and the evaluation system can extract `<solution>` blocks for answer checking.

**The environment provides two types of observation** when it receives an action: (1) Python execution results, which are "either program outputs or error traceback messages," and (2) binary feedback indicating "whether the solution is correct or not." The binary feedback is determined by comparing the executed output against the ground-truth answer (for math) or test case outputs (for coding). For example, in Figure 3, the first turn produces a `NameError: name 'x' is not defined` because the code attempted to use variable `x` before defining it, and the binary feedback is "Your answer is wrong." In the second turn, after the model defines `x` as `(78 - 8) / 7`, execution produces `Out: 10.0` and binary feedback is "Your answer is correct."

**Critique generation** follows the environment observation. The observations plus interaction history are passed to GPT-4, which "locates the errors and provides suggestions for improvements." The paper explicitly justifies using GPT-4 as the critique model: "To avoid potential bias introduced by self-correction (Wang et al., 2023b; Xu et al., 2024), we adopt a stronger model, GPT-4, as the critique and ensure critique quality by providing GPT-4 with ground truth answers as references." This addresses a known failure mode of self-correction: when models critique their own outputs, they tend to either miss errors or introduce new ones. Using a stronger external critic with access to ground truth produces higher-quality feedback. The critique in Figure 3 is illustrative: GPT-4 says "Your thought process is accurate and your error lies in your code. You need to define `x` first," pinpoints the specific error location, and the second-turn critique confirms "Good job! You have fixed the error in last turn. Now your answer is correct."

The paper distinguishes its approach from Wang et al. (2024) on three points: "we adopt more diverse reasoning patterns to teach LLMs to learn rationales rather than simply memorizing answers," "learn to create and use tools," and "we believe that it is important for LLMs to learn from the feedback provided by the critique rather than solely from observations of the environment." The last point is important: environment observations tell the model *what* went wrong (e.g., `NameError`), but critique tells the model *why* and *how to fix it* in pedagogically useful language.

**Preference Tree Construction (Section 2.3).** The critical structural innovation of ULTRAINTERACT is that preference pairs are constructed at *every turn* in a multi-turn trajectory, not just at the final turn. The process works as follows:

For each instruction, the paper samples "a pair of correct and incorrect actions" from the actor model. The sampling follows Cui et al. (2023) to "sample the pair from different actor models to ensure response diversity." Specifically, one model is randomly chosen from Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct, Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, and DeepSeek-LLM-67B-Chat to generate the incorrect action, while GPT-3.5 Turbo generates the correct action. Using different models for correct and incorrect actions prevents the preference pairs from being trivially separable by surface features of a single model's output distribution. The paper also excludes instances that "fail to pass the Python syntax check" to prevent models from exploiting syntax errors as shortcuts for distinguishing correct from incorrect.

**The most challenging aspect of construction is obtaining correct actions for difficult problems.** The paper reports that "certain challenging problems in ULTRAINTERACT pose difficulties in obtaining correct actions, even using strong actors such as GPT-4, with nearly zero pass@100 accuracies." To address this, the paper implements a three-stage escalation process:

- **Stage 1:** Directly sample 20 actions and randomly keep a correct one, if any.
- **Stage 2:** If no correct action is obtained, repeat the above process up to three times, "progressively switching from more cost-effective models to the strong yet expensive GPT-4 Turbo."
- **Stage 3:** For remaining difficult problems where no correct action is acquired after previous steps, "we provide the actor with ground-truth rationales and answers, and then apply various techniques to elicit correct actions." The specific information provided varies by task: for coding, the actor receives full access to the solution code (adding step marks and explanations to make it understandable); for tool-free math, the answer numbers in the rationale are masked "to avoid the actor model directly copying the answers to pass the correctness checking"; for program-enhanced math, the textual rationale is first translated into code, then either provided directly or converted to modularization programming.

This three-stage process reveals a practical tension in the data construction. Stage 3's use of ground-truth rationales means that for the hardest problems, the "correct" action is not independently discovered by the actor model—it is elicited by providing the answer. The paper addresses this by noting that the actor must still generate the step-by-step reasoning and code, even when given the ground truth, which prevents trivial memorization. However, this means the SFT data for the hardest problems may teach the model to follow a provided rationale rather than to independently discover the solution path, a subtle distinction that could affect generalization.

**Tree expansion across turns.** After obtaining a correct-incorrect pair at turn 1, the correct action concludes its trajectory (it is a leaf node in the preference tree). The incorrect action is expanded into the next turn: "We expand the incorrect action into the next turn, and have the actor interact with the environment and the critique to refine its solution." At turn 2, a new correct-incorrect pair is sampled following the same procedure. This expansion continues up to a maximum of five turns. The result is an imbalanced binary tree where each internal node is an incorrect action, each correct action is a leaf, and each turn adds a new layer.

Conceptually, this means that ULTRAINTERACT provides supervised signal at multiple levels of granularity. An SFT model can learn from: (1) complete correct trajectories (root-to-correct-leaf paths), (2) individual correct actions at any turn, or (3) the correction process itself (how an incorrect action at turn $n$ is refined into a correct action at turn $n+1$). A preference learning model can learn from pairs of (correct, incorrect) at the same turn—for example, at turn 2, comparing a correct refinement against an incorrect one—which is a much finer-grained signal than trajectory-level preferences.

**Additional instruction-action pairs for challenging problems.** For problems that reach Stage 3 and have multiple ground-truth solutions, the paper samples additional correct actions to cover all ground truths, then samples additional incorrect actions to pair with them. This ensures that the hardest problems—which provide the most valuable training signal because they are at the frontier of the model's capability—contribute proportionally more data points to the preference learning dataset.

The final dataset statistics (Table 1, Table 7) show: 85,918 total instructions, 219,819 total action pairs, 286,979 correct answers, an average of 1,201.8 tokens per trajectory, and an average of 2.3 trajectories per instruction. The breakdown by task and tool usage reveals that math problems dominate (approximately 32K with tools, 25K without), followed by coding (approximately 28K with tools, 8.5K without), and logic (approximately 4.5K with tools, 2K without). The average trajectory length is substantially longer for coding problems with tools (1,070.4 tokens for the tool-free variant, 1,728.5 for tool-using) than for math (439.1 for tool-free, 1,521.9 for tool-using), reflecting the verbosity of code generation.

#### 3.4.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The supervised fine-tuning stage takes base models (Mistral-7B for EURUS-7B, CodeLlama-70B for EURUS-70B) and trains them to generate correct reasoning trajectories.

**Data selection and preprocessing.** The SFT stage uses "all correct actions (287K) in ULTRAINTERACT." However, the paper reports an important empirical finding about how this data is used: "We find it yields better performance to discard interaction history and train only on correct leaf nodes in each tree." In practical terms, this means that rather than training the model to reproduce the entire multi-turn interaction (including the incorrect actions, environment feedback, and critique that preceded the final correct action), the SFT data consists only of the final correct sequences—the root-to-correct-leaf paths. The model sees the instruction followed by the correct step-by-step solution, without the intermediate failures.

This design choice is notable because it means the SFT model does not explicitly learn from the correction process during this stage. The interaction history is discarded to prevent the model from learning to generate the pattern of "make error → receive feedback → correct error" as a fixed script, which could lead to the model artificially inserting errors to then "correct" them. By training only on the final correct outputs, the SFT stage focuses on teaching the model to generate correct solutions directly.

**Data mixture with general-purpose datasets.** To prevent the model from becoming a reasoning specialist that loses general instruction-following ability, the paper mixes in existing alignment datasets. For EURUS-7B: "32K UltraChat, 30K ShareGPT, and 50K OpenOrca." For EURUS-70B: "63K UltraChat, 30K ShareGPT, and 70K OpenOrca." UltraChat (Ding et al., 2023) provides diverse multi-turn conversational data; ShareGPT contains real user-assistant interactions; OpenOrca (Lian et al., 2023) provides augmented reasoning traces from FLAN. The mixture ratios are not explicitly justified beyond the empirical results in Section 6.2 (Table 5), which show that training on ULTRAINTERACT alone produces strong reasoning (47.7% average) but substantially worse instruction-following (17.4% on IFEval), while the full mixture balances both (53.6% average, 44.0% IFEval). The OpenOrca portion is particularly important: Table 5 shows that training only on open-source data without ULTRAINTERACT yields only 37.0% average, confirming that the ULTRAINTERACT data provides the bulk of the reasoning capability.

**Training hyperparameters.** The paper reports: "We finetune base models for 1 epoch with a 2e-5 learning rate and 0.1 warmup ratio using a cosine scheduler." The single epoch suggests that the data volume is sufficient that multiple epochs would risk overfitting—a standard practice in instruction tuning where the model should learn the output format and reasoning patterns without memorizing specific problems. The learning rate of 2e-5 is modest, appropriate for fine-tuning a pretrained model without catastrophic forgetting of pretrained knowledge. The 0.1 warmup ratio means 10% of training steps use a linearly increasing learning rate before the cosine decay begins, which stabilizes early training.

#### 3.4.3 Preference Learning

After SFT, the paper applies preference learning algorithms to further improve the model. The key finding is that not all preference learning algorithms work for reasoning tasks—a result that leads to the paper's analysis of reward patterns and the novel reward modeling objective.

**Data used.** The preference learning stage uses all 220K multi-turn trajectory pairs from ULTRAINTERACT and all 340K pairs from UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023). The inclusion of UltraFeedback is important: it provides general conversational preference signal that complements ULTRAINTERACT's reasoning-specific pairs. Without UltraFeedback, the model might over-optimize for reasoning at the expense of general helpfulness—a trade-off the paper explicitly tries to avoid.

The paper notes a key difference from SFT: "Differently from SFT, here we include all multi-turn trajectory pairs in our ULTRAINTERACT (220K)." This means preference learning uses the full tree structure with pairs at every turn, not just the final correct actions. The model sees examples like "at turn 2, this correct refinement is preferred over this incorrect one" which teaches it to distinguish good from bad intermediate steps—capability that SFT alone cannot provide since it only sees correct outputs.

**Algorithms tested.** Three preference learning algorithms are evaluated: DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024), and NCA (Chen et al., 2024a). The hyperparameters are: "all β is set to 0.1, and λ+/λ−in KTO is set to 1.33 as recommended." The β parameter controls how strongly the model is regularized toward the SFT policy—a lower β allows more deviation from the SFT model. Training uses "a 5e-7 learning rate and 0.1 warmup ratio using a cosine scheduler" for 1 epoch. The learning rate is substantially lower than SFT (5e-7 vs. 2e-5), which is standard for preference learning since the model is already initialized from the SFT checkpoint and only needs to be nudged toward preferred outputs.

**Empirical results and analysis.** Table 3 shows the performance of each algorithm. The SFT baseline (EURUS-7B-SFT) achieves 46.5% average. DPO degrades this to 44.5%: "Surprisingly, we observe that DPO hurts model performance on most benchmarks." Specifically, DPO causes drops on HumanEval (55.5% → 50.6%), MBPP (59.1% → 52.1%), LeetCode (20.0% → 8.3%), MATH (32.6% → 28.3%), and several math benchmarks. KTO improves to 48.8% and NCA to 48.1%. For the 70B model, "DPO training of our 70B model fails since the rewards go down to −∞."

**Diagnosis of DPO failure (Section 6.1, Figure 5).** The paper traces the performance difference to the behavior of implicit rewards during training. Figure 5 shows the reward trajectories throughout the training process for DPO, KTO, and NCA, plotting both the rewards assigned to chosen data, rewards assigned to rejected data, and the margin between them. The observations are:

- **For DPO:** "Rewards for chosen rejected data both keep decreasing through DPO, though the rewards for chosen data is still higher hence the loss decreases." The chosen data rewards drop from positive values to approximately -1.26 by the end of training. The rejected data rewards drop even further, so the *margin* between chosen and rejected increases and the DPO loss decreases, but the *absolute* chosen reward is negative.
- **For KTO:** "The rewards of chosen data keep increasing with those of rejected data decreasing." Chosen rewards end at approximately +0.4 (positive), rejected rewards are negative, and the margin increases.
- **For NCA:** Similar to KTO: chosen rewards end at approximately +0.16 (positive), rejected rewards decrease.

The paper's hypothesis is: "the distinction in the trend of rewards that leads to the performance gap between DPO and the other two algorithms. This distinction can be attributed to that DPO, derived from the Bradley-Terry model, only optimizes the relative differences between chosen and rejected data overlooking the absolute values of the rewards." The Bradley-Terry model, which underlies DPO, models the probability that one item is preferred over another as:

$$P(y_c \succ y_r | x) = \frac{\exp(r(x, y_c))}{\exp(r(x, y_c)) + \exp(r(x, y_r))}$$

where the preference depends only on the *difference* $r(x, y_c) - r(x, y_r)$. This means DPO can achieve low loss even when both $r(x, y_c)$ and $r(x, y_r)$ are highly negative, as long as their difference is positive—exactly what Figure 5 shows.

**Why absolute rewards matter for reasoning.** The paper argues that reasoning tasks have a fundamentally different structure from open-ended conversation: "This is a non-issue in alignment with general human values where preference is 'relative' and there can be many valid answers to the same input. However, in reasoning tasks, the space of correct answers is much smaller than that of incorrect ones." In plain language: for chat, being "better than a bad response" is a meaningful signal because there are many acceptable responses. For math, being "less wrong than a very wrong answer" does not guarantee being correct—a solution that is marginally less incorrect can still be completely wrong. The model needs to produce solutions that are *unconditionally* correct, which requires the reward for correct solutions to be high in absolute terms, not just relative to incorrect ones.

The paper provides empirical support: "we notice that the rewards of chosen data in the last training step follow the ranking order of KTO > NCA > DPO, positively correlate with their performance trends." The final chosen reward values (KTO: +0.4, NCA: +0.16, DPO: -1.26) directly predict the performance ranking in Table 3 (KTO: 48.8%, NCA: 48.1%, DPO: 44.5%).

**Why KTO and NCA avoid this problem.** KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization) is derived from prospect theory rather than the Bradley-Terry model; it optimizes a value function that is asymmetric—losses (rejected outputs) are weighted more heavily than gains (chosen outputs), but it also includes a reference point that prevents chosen outputs from being driven arbitrarily low. NCA (Noise Contrastive Alignment) uses a contrastive objective that explicitly pushes chosen and rejected representations apart while maintaining chosen representations near the SFT initialization. Both algorithms, by design, preserve or increase absolute chosen rewards while DPO does not.

#### 3.4.4 Reward Modeling

The paper trains a separate reward model, EURUS-RM-7B, for use in reranking (selecting the best response from a set of candidates). The reward model training incorporates the insights from the preference learning analysis into a novel objective function.

**Training data.** The reward model uses a combination of datasets:
- All 220K multi-turn trajectory pairs from ULTRAINTERACT
- An augmented set of 240K single-turn action pairs derived from ULTRAINTERACT. The augmentation procedure: "we scale up the pairs by matching every correct action for each instruction with one incorrect action of other turns. This leads to NxN pairs of single-turn actions for a trajectory of depth N. We remove the action pairs consisting of nodes at the same turn, as they are already part of the multi-turn trajectory pairs we included." To prevent overfitting: "we only select instructions with NxN ≤ 10, and for these instructions, we randomly sample at most 9 pairs with each action occurring no more than 3 times." This creates 240K additional single-turn preference pairs.
- All 340K pairs from UltraFeedback
- One pair per instruction from UltraSafety (Guo et al., 2024b), totaling approximately 3K pairs. UltraSafety provides harmlessness preference data to prevent the reward model from assigning high scores to harmful outputs.

**Model initialization.** EURUS-RM-7B is initialized from EURUS-7B-SFT "with a new linear layer" added on top to produce scalar reward predictions. The base model provides the representation learning from SFT; the new linear layer maps from the final hidden state to a single reward value.

**The novel reward modeling objective.** The paper's key algorithmic contribution is an augmented objective function for reward modeling:

$$L_{\text{ULTRAINTERACT}} = -\log \left( \sigma \left(r_\theta(x, y_c) - r_\theta(x, y_r)\right) \right) - \log \left( \sigma \left(r_\theta(x, y_c)\right) \right) - \log \left( \sigma \left(-r_\theta(x, y_r)\right) \right)$$

Where `$r_\theta(x, y_c)$` is the reward model's predicted reward for the chosen action `$y_c$` given instruction `$x$`, `$r_\theta(x, y_r)$` is the predicted reward for the rejected action `$y_r$`, and `$\sigma(\cdot)$` is the sigmoid function `$\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$`.

**What the three terms compute, separately and together:**

- **First term** `$-\log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_c) - r_\theta(x, y_r))$`: This is the standard Bradley-Terry loss, denoted `$L_{\text{BT}}$`. It penalizes the model when the chosen action does not receive a sufficiently higher reward than the rejected action. If `$r_\theta(x, y_c) \gg r_\theta(x, y_r)$`, then `$\sigma(\text{large positive}) \approx 1$` and `$-\log(1) = 0$`. If the rewards are equal or reversed, the loss is large. This term optimizes *relative* preference.

- **Second term** `$-\log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_c))$`: This term penalizes the model when the chosen action's reward is not sufficiently positive. If `$r_\theta(x, y_c) \gg 0$`, then `$\sigma(\text{large positive}) \approx 1$` and `$-\log(1) = 0$`. If `$r_\theta(x, y_c) < 0$`, the loss increases. This term explicitly *pushes chosen action rewards upward* in absolute value.

- **Third term** `$-\log \sigma(-r_\theta(x, y_r))$`: This term penalizes the model when the rejected action's reward is not sufficiently negative. If `$r_\theta(x, y_r) \ll 0$`, then `$-r_\theta(x, y_r)$` is large positive, `$\sigma(\text{large}) \approx 1$`, and `$-\log(1) = 0$`. If `$r_\theta(x, y_r) \geq 0$`, the loss increases. This term explicitly *pushes rejected action rewards downward* in absolute value.

The second and third terms together form `$L_{\text{DR}}$` (Direct Reward), the novel contribution. The paper states the intuition: "We therefore augment the established Bradley-Terry (BT) objective `$L_{\text{BT}}$` with an additional term `$L_{\text{DR}}$` to directly increase the reward of the chosen actions for instances from ULTRAINTERACT, and decrease those of the rejected ones."

**Why this form:** The standard Bradley-Terry loss alone optimizes only the *difference* `$r_\theta(x, y_c) - r_\theta(x, y_r)$`. This can be minimized when both rewards are very negative (e.g., `$r_\theta(x, y_c) = -1$`, `$r_\theta(x, y_r) = -5$`) as long as the chosen is higher. The added terms enforce additional constraints: `$L_{\text{DR}}$` requires `$r_\theta(x, y_c) > 0$` and `$r_\theta(x, y_r) < 0$`. This prevents the reward collapse observed in DPO training (Figure 5) by directly penalizing negative chosen rewards and positive rejected rewards.

**Selective application.** The paper notes that `$L_{\text{DR}}$` is applied only to "instances from ULTRAINTERACT," while "for instances from other datasets, we train with `$L_{\text{BT}}$`." This selective application reflects the paper's core insight: in reasoning tasks (ULTRAINTERACT data), absolute correctness matters and the reward model should reflect this; in general conversation (UltraFeedback, UltraSafety), relative preference is sufficient and the BT loss alone is appropriate.

**Training hyperparameters.** The paper reports: "We train RM for 1 epoch with lr=1e-5 learning rate. We also use a cosine scheduler with a warmup ratio of 0.1."

**Ablation results for the objective (Table 4).** The paper evaluates the contribution of each term:

- **w/o L_DR (BT only):** Reasoning performance drops from 87.0% to 77.5% on RewardBench's Reasoning split, confirming that the absolute reward term is critical for reasoning domain performance.
- **w/o L_BT (L_DR only):** Chat performance drops on RewardBench's Chat-Hard split from 65.3% to 58.5%, confirming that the relative preference term remains important for general conversation. Reasoning performance is 84.2%, which is higher than BT-only (77.5%) but lower than the combined objective (87.0%), suggesting both terms contribute to reasoning.
- **w/o UltraSafety:** Safety performance drops (67.7% vs. 80.7% on RewardBench Safety), but reasoning actually improves slightly on some metrics. This confirms that safety data introduces a trade-off: including it improves harmlessness evaluation at some cost to reasoning accuracy on certain benchmarks.
- **w/o UltraFeedback + UltraSafety (ULTRAINTERACT only):** Performance drops substantially on Chat (95.1% → 73.4% on Chat) but Reasoning remains relatively strong (78.0%), confirming that ULTRAINTERACT provides reasoning-specific reward modeling capability while UltraFeedback provides conversational reward modeling.

#### 3.4.5 Evaluation Design

The evaluation framework is designed to test generalization—most benchmarks are out-of-distribution relative to the training data.

**Single-turn evaluation benchmarks (Section 4).** The evaluation covers five capability dimensions:

- **Coding:** HumanEval (164 handwritten programming problems), MBPP (427 entry-level Python problems), and LeetCode (180 recent contest problems from LeetCode, curated by Guo et al., 2024a). LeetCode is explicitly described as a challenging OOD benchmark; it contains problems from 2023-2024 contests that postdate the training data. All coding evaluations use pass@1 accuracy—the model generates one solution and it is checked against test cases.
- **Math:** GSM-Plus (an adversarially perturbed version of GSM8K designed to test robustness—Li et al., 2024), MATH (competition-level math problems from Hendrycks et al., 2021b), TheoremQA (university-level STEM theorem-driven questions—Chen et al., 2023), SVAMP (simple math word problems with varying structures), and ASDiv (diverse math word problems). The paper tests "both textual reasoning and program-enhanced settings and report the best performance of the two," meaning for each math benchmark, the model can solve problems either through natural language reasoning or by writing and executing Python code to compute the answer.
- **Reasoning:** BBH-Hard (23 challenging tasks from BIG-Bench that require multi-step reasoning), evaluated with 3-shot prompting.
- **Instruction-following:** IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), reporting the "prompt-level loose score" which measures adherence to formatting and content constraints.
- **Multi-turn:** MINT (Wang et al., 2023b), evaluating the model's ability to interact with tools and feedback across multiple turns. The paper reports "success rate at Turn 5," and only considers coding and math problems within MINT.

**Out-of-distribution emphasis.** The paper explicitly states: "All test sets except MATH are out-of-distribution to our models and most baselines." The decontamination procedure is described in Appendix A.3: for LeetCode, an Exact Substring Matching Algorithm is used to compare against ULTRAINTERACT instructions (finding no overlaps); for other benchmarks, 8-gram exact matching is used, and "We remove those instructions that overlap 8 grams with any test sample." This is a strong decontamination protocol that supports the claim that the evaluation measures genuine generalization.

**A note on contaminated baselines.** Table 3 notes that "MAmmoTH, OpenChat, and Starling-LM have been trained on TheoremQA test sets. We strikethrough the contaminated numbers." This is disclosed in the table metadata and the numbers are excluded from analysis, demonstrating awareness of the data contamination issue that plagues LLM evaluation.

**Multi-turn evaluation specificity.** For MINT (Appendix C), the paper selects "MATH, TheoremQA, and MMLU-math from 'reasoning' as a new 'math' split." This creates separate code and math multi-turn evaluations, with success rates reported at Turn 5—after the model has had five turns of interaction with tools and feedback to refine its solution.

**Baseline selection (Table 2).** The baselines span three categories: general-purpose models (Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2, Zephyr-7B-β, OpenChat-3.5-1210, Starling-LM-7B-α, Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, DeepSeek-LLM-67B-Chat, QWen1.5-72B-Chat), coding specialists (Magicoder-S-DS-6.7B, OpenCodeInterpreter-DS-6.7B/CL-70B, DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct, CodeLLaMA-70B-Instruct), and math specialists (MAmmoTH-7B-Mistral, WizardMath-7B-v1.1, OpenMath-Mistral-7B/CL-70B). This comprehensive baseline set allows comparison against both generalist models (to show EURUS's reasoning advantage) and specialist models (to show EURUS does not sacrifice generality for domain-specific performance).

**Reward model evaluation (Section 5).** EURUS-RM-7B is evaluated on three RM benchmarks: RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024), which tests preference prediction across Chat, Chat-Hard, Safety, and Reasoning splits; AutoJ (Li et al., 2023a), which tests LLM-as-a-judge correlation with human experts across Code, Math, and Other splits; and MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023), which tests the correlation between the RM's scores and human preference judgments. The paper "excludes the 'prior sets' split from RewardBench, since many baselines train on the datasets that this split contains"—maintaining the OOD evaluation philosophy.

**Reranking evaluation (Section 5.1, Figure 4).** EURUS-RM-7B is used to rerank responses from Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 on HumanEval, MBPP, GSM8K, and MATH. For each instruction, 1, 2, 4, 8, or 16 responses are sampled, and the RM selects the best one. The baselines are random sampling, self-consistency (majority voting), and Starling-RM-34B (a 5× larger RM). This evaluation tests whether the RM's preference judgments translate to improved downstream task performance.

**Prompting conventions.** "All evaluations are conducted in 0-shot CoT with two exceptions: BBH uses 3 shots and IFEval does not use CoT." The 0-shot CoT means the model is prompted to reason step-by-step without demonstration examples, which tests its ability to apply reasoning patterns learned during training rather than mimicking in-context examples.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: Preference Trees as a New Data Structure for Reasoning Alignment

The paper's most conceptually distinctive contribution is the **preference tree**—a structured alignment data format where correct and incorrect actions are paired at *every turn* of a multi-turn reasoning trajectory, not just at the final output. This is a fundamental reframing of what alignment data looks like for reasoning tasks, and it departs sharply from both dominant paradigms in the literature.

**What the field did before.** Alignment datasets for LLMs have overwhelmingly adopted one of two structures. The first, exemplified by HH-RLHF (Bai et al., 2022) and UltraFeedback (Cui et al., 2023), provides **trajectory-level preference pairs**: a human or LLM judge compares two complete responses and selects the preferred one. This captures global preference—"this answer is better than that one"—but provides zero signal about *where* in the solution the error occurred, *which step* was suboptimal, or *how* to correct it. The second paradigm, exemplified by CodeActInstruct (Wang et al., 2024) and Code-Feedback (Zheng et al., 2024), provides **interaction trajectories** where models execute code and receive interpreter feedback, but these datasets lack structured pairwise preferences—they show the model what happened (this code produced an error) without explicitly comparing correct and incorrect actions at the same point in the reasoning process.

ULTRAINTERACT's preference tree (Figure 2) synthesizes the strengths of both paradigms while addressing their limitations. The tree structure means that if a model makes an incorrect action at turn 2, that action is explicitly paired with a correct action *from an alternative branch at the same turn*. The model learns per-step corrections—at turn 2, this refinement approach is correct, this one is incorrect—rather than only trajectory-level preferences. This is conceptually analogous to **process supervision** in the verifier literature (Lightman et al., 2023), where a reward model scores each intermediate step rather than just the final answer. But ULTRAINTERACT's innovation is in *how* this per-step signal is generated: through interaction with an environment (Python interpreter) and a critique model (GPT-4 with ground truth), rather than through expensive human annotation. The preference pairs are a byproduct of the interaction process—the environment provides binary correctness feedback automatically, and the tree structure emerges naturally from expanding incorrect actions into refinement turns.

**Why this is a conceptual advance, not just a data engineering contribution.** The preference tree isn't merely a format; it encodes a *theory of what reasoning alignment requires* that differs from both conversational alignment and specialist training. For conversational alignment, relative preference is sufficient because there are many acceptable responses—knowing that response A is better than response B provides useful training signal even if both are flawed. For specialist training (e.g., training on correct math solutions via SFT), only correct outputs are shown, which teaches the model to generate correct answers but provides no discriminative signal about *what makes an answer incorrect*. The preference tree combines both signals: SFT on correct leaf nodes teaches the model to generate correct solutions, while preference pairs at every turn teach the model to *distinguish correct from incorrect intermediate actions*. This dual signal is what the paper argues is missing from prior approaches—and it's encoded in the tree structure itself, not just in the training algorithm applied to it.

**Evidence tying this to results.** The ablation in Table 5 provides support: training on ULTRAINTERACT alone (which includes the preference tree structure) yields 47.7% average compared to 37.0% for open-source data alone. More tellingly, the preference learning results in Table 3 show that KTO and NCA applied to ULTRAINTERACT's multi-turn trajectory pairs (which are only possible because of the tree structure) improve over SFT by 2.3 and 1.6 percentage points respectively, with the largest gains in multi-turn interaction ability (MINT coding: 15.4 → 19.1 for KTO, MINT math: 28.4 → 43.6). SFT alone uses only correct leaf nodes (discarding interaction history); the multi-turn pairs from the preference tree are what enable the preference learning stage to teach per-step correction, and the multi-turn evaluation gains directly reflect this.

### Innovation 2: The Absolute-Reward Diagnostic for Preference Learning in Reasoning

The paper's second major conceptual contribution is a **diagnostic insight** about why preference learning algorithms behave differently on reasoning tasks versus open-domain conversation, and the accompanying hypothesis that the absolute value of chosen-action rewards—not merely the margin between chosen and rejected—is what drives reasoning performance.

**The puzzle this resolves.** Prior to this work, the field had conflicting signals about whether preference learning helps or hurts reasoning. DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), the most widely adopted preference learning algorithm, had been shown to sometimes *degrade* reasoning performance, while newer algorithms like KTO (Ethayarajh et al., 2024) and NCA (Chen et al., 2024a) showed positive effects. But no one had explained *why*. The dominant assumption—implicit in the widespread adoption of DPO—was that optimizing relative preferences is sufficient regardless of domain, because the Bradley-Terry model from which DPO is derived only depends on the difference between chosen and rejected rewards. If the loss goes down, the reasoning should be improving. EURUS's results showed this assumption was wrong.

**The diagnostic move (Figure 5).** Rather than treating the DPO failure as a mysterious empirical result, the paper traces it to a specific, observable mechanism: during DPO training on reasoning data, the *implicit rewards for chosen actions decrease* and become negative, even as the reward *margin* between chosen and rejected increases and the DPO loss decreases. The model becomes better at distinguishing correct from incorrect in a relative sense—it can tell that solution A is better than solution B—but its absolute confidence in correct solutions drops below zero. For reasoning tasks where the model must *generate* correct answers from scratch (not merely select between options), this decreasing absolute confidence is detrimental: the model has learned that correct answers are "less bad" rather than "good," and this manifests as degraded generation quality.

This diagnostic move is significant because it **provides a mechanistic explanation, not just a correlation**. The paper doesn't just report that DPO underperforms KTO and NCA—it shows *why* by inspecting the intermediate reward trajectories and connecting them to the mathematical structure of the DPO objective. DPO's derivation from Bradley-Terry means it optimizes `$P(y_c \succ y_r | x) \propto \exp(r(x, y_c) - r(x, y_r))$`, which depends only on the reward difference. The loss can be minimized with `$r(x, y_c) = -1$` and `$r(x, y_r) = -5$` just as well as with `$r(x, y_c) = +5$` and `$r(x, y_r) = +1$`. The paper's key empirical finding is that in practice, DPO training *does* drive chosen rewards negative—it takes the low-reward path—while KTO and NCA preserve or increase absolute chosen rewards. The final chosen reward values directly predict the performance ranking: KTO (+0.4) > NCA (+0.16) > DPO (-1.26), matching the accuracy ordering in Table 3.

**The conceptual reframing.** This diagnostic implies a reframing of what preference learning means for reasoning. In open-domain chat, preference is genuinely relative—there are many valid ways to respond helpfully, and learning that response A is *preferred to* response B is sufficient because both might be acceptable, and the model just needs to select the better one. In reasoning, correctness is largely absolute—an answer is either right or wrong, and the space of correct answers is much smaller than the space of incorrect ones. Learning that a solution is "less incorrect" than an alternative does not make it correct. The model needs high absolute confidence in correct solutions to generate them reliably. This reframing explains why preference learning algorithms that preserve absolute chosen rewards (KTO, NCA) succeed on reasoning while those that don't (DPO) fail—a finding with immediate practical implications for algorithm selection in reasoning pipelines.

**Evidence.** Table 3 shows EURUS-7B-DPO at 44.5% average vs. SFT's 46.5% (a degradation), while KTO reaches 48.8% and NCA reaches 48.1%. The 70B model fails entirely with DPO ("rewards go down to -infinity"). Figure 5 visualizes the reward trajectories that explain these performance differences.

### Innovation 3: The Augmented Reward Modeling Objective Derived from the Absolute-Reward Insight

Building directly on the diagnostic insight above, the paper's third contribution is a **novel reward modeling objective** (`$L_{\text{ULTRAINTERACT}}$`) that operationalizes the absolute-reward hypothesis as a training criterion. This is a methodological innovation motivated by the conceptual finding, not an arbitrary modification.

**What distinguishes this from standard reward modeling.** Standard reward model training uses the Bradley-Terry loss:

$$L_{\text{BT}} = -\log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_c) - r_\theta(x, y_r))$$

This optimizes only the relative difference between chosen and rejected rewards—exactly the same mathematical structure that the paper showed causes DPO's failure mode. A reward model trained with pure BT loss can achieve low loss while assigning low absolute scores to correct solutions, as long as incorrect solutions receive even lower scores. The paper's augmented objective adds two explicit regularization terms:

$$L_{\text{DR}} = -\log \sigma(r_\theta(x, y_c)) - \log \sigma(-r_\theta(x, y_r))$$

These terms directly penalize the model when `$r_\theta(x, y_c) \leq 0$` (chosen reward not positive enough) or `$r_\theta(x, y_r) \geq 0$` (rejected reward not negative enough), forcing the reward model to maintain absolute calibration—correct solutions should score above zero, incorrect ones below zero—in addition to maintaining the correct relative ordering.

**The conceptual significance is the translation of a diagnostic observation into a training objective.** The paper didn't just observe that absolute rewards matter; it derived a loss function that explicitly enforces the desired absolute-reward property. This is a clean example of a diagnostic leading to an algorithmic fix: the problem (DPO drives chosen rewards negative) → the diagnosis (Bradley-Terry only constrains relative differences) → the solution (add terms that constrain absolute values). The selective application of `$L_{\text{DR}}$` only to ULTRAINTERACT instances (not UltraFeedback or UltraSafety) reflects the paper's core insight that absolute correctness is specific to reasoning, while relative preference is appropriate for general conversation—the objective is domain-adaptive, applying different criteria to different data sources.

**Why this is more than an incremental tweak.** Adding regularization terms to loss functions is common; what makes this significant is the *justification* and the *specificity*. The regularization isn't generic (e.g., L2 weight decay) but targets the exact failure mode the paper diagnosed. The ablation in Table 4 confirms that the terms are not redundant: removing `$L_{\text{DR}}$` drops reasoning performance from 87.0% to 77.5% on RewardBench's Reasoning split, while removing `$L_{\text{BT}}$` drops Chat-Hard performance from 65.3% to 58.5%. The two terms serve complementary functions—one for relative ordering, one for absolute calibration—and both are necessary for the RM to excel across both conversational and reasoning domains.

**Evidence.** Table 4 shows EURUS-RM-7B with the full objective achieves 82.4% average on RewardBench, substantially outperforming Starling-RM-7B (76.2%), UltraRM-13B (69.8%), and even GPT-4 on AutoJ (64.5% vs. 61.9%) and MT-Bench (72.9 vs. 63.9). The ablation rows show that removing either `$L_{\text{DR}}$` or `$L_{\text{BT}}$` hurts specific capabilities, and the full combination yields the best overall performance.

### Innovation 4: The Finding That Interaction History Should Be Discarded During SFT

This is a **negative result with practical force**—the paper's empirical discovery that supervised fine-tuning on complete multi-turn interaction trajectories (including environment observations and critique) produces worse results than training only on the final correct outputs with interaction history stripped away.

**What makes this non-obvious.** The intuitive expectation would be that training on the full interaction trajectory would teach the model to *learn from feedback*—if the SFT data shows the model making an error, receiving critique, and then correcting itself, the trained model should internalize this correction ability. This is how multi-turn conversational SFT works: training on full dialogue histories teaches models to maintain context and respond appropriately to user feedback. The ULTRAINTERACT data contains rich interaction trajectories with Python interpreter observations and GPT-4 critique, and a natural assumption would be that including this data during SFT would improve the model's ability to interact with tools and refine its outputs.

The paper's empirical finding contradicts this: "We find it yields better performance to discard interaction history and train only on correct leaf nodes in each tree." The SFT model is trained only on the root-to-correct-leaf paths—the instruction followed by the correct step-by-step solution, without the intermediate failures. This means the interaction ability that EURUS demonstrates (the 31.6% multi-turn coding success rate for EURUS-70B-SFT in Table 3) is not directly taught during SFT but is *transferred* from the preference learning stage (which does use the multi-turn trajectory pairs) or emerges from the model's base reasoning capabilities.

**The diagnostic implication.** The paper doesn't fully explain why discarding interaction history helps, but the result suggests a specific failure mode: training on full interaction trajectories might teach the model to *expect* failure and correction as part of the normal problem-solving process, leading it to artificially generate errors to then "correct"—a form of mode collapse where the model learns the interaction *pattern* rather than the reasoning *skill*. By training only on correct outputs, the SFT stage teaches the model to aim directly for correct solutions, while the preference learning stage separately teaches the discriminative ability to distinguish correct from incorrect actions and refine failures. Separating these two learning objectives—generating correct solutions versus learning from mistakes—may prevent destructive interference between them.

**Evidence.** This finding is mentioned in Section 3 ("Supervised Fine-Tuning") as a design choice, and its justification is implicit in the performance trajectory: SFT establishes strong single-turn reasoning (46.5% average), preference learning adds discriminative and interactive capability on top (48.8% for KTO), and the multi-turn evaluation specifically benefits from the preference learning stage (MINT coding from 15.4% SFT to 19.1% KTO; MINT math from 28.4% SFT to 43.6% KTO). If SFT had been trained on full interaction trajectories, the reasoning baseline might have been stronger on multi-turn but weaker on single-turn—and the paper's design choice prioritizes a clean separation of concerns.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Datasets.** The paper evaluates on 12 benchmarks spanning coding (HumanEval, MBPP, LeetCode), math (GSM-Plus, MATH, TheoremQA, SVAMP, ASDiv), logical reasoning (BBH-Hard), instruction-following (IFEval), and multi-turn interaction (MINT). All test sets except MATH are out-of-distribution relative to the training data. HumanEval contains 164 handwritten programming problems; MBPP contains 427 entry-level Python problems; LeetCode contains 180 recent contest problems from 2023–2024; GSM-Plus is an adversarially perturbed GSM8K designed to test robustness; MATH contains competition-level mathematics problems; TheoremQA contains university-level STEM theorem-driven questions; SVAMP and ASDiv contain math word problems with varying structures; BBH-Hard comprises 23 challenging BIG-Bench tasks requiring multi-step reasoning; IFEval measures instruction-following via prompt-level loose accuracy; MINT evaluates multi-turn tool interaction success rate at Turn 5 on coding and math subsets. Decontamination uses 8-gram exact matching against ULTRAINTERACT training instructions; overlapping instances are removed. For LeetCode specifically, an Exact Substring Matching Algorithm is applied; no overlaps were found. Three baselines (MAmmoTH, OpenChat, Starling-LM) are flagged as having been trained on the TheoremQA test set; their contaminated TheoremQA numbers are struck through in Table 3.

- **Base models.** EURUS-7B variants are fine-tuned from Mistral-7B (Jiang et al., 2023a); EURUS-70B variants are fine-tuned from CodeLlama-70B (Roziere et al., 2023). The authors do not explicitly justify these base model choices beyond their availability as strong open-source foundation models. Mistral-7B provides a widely-used 7B-scale starting point; CodeLlama-70B provides a coding-pretrained 70B-scale starting point. The SFT stage uses a 1-epoch fine-tuning protocol with learning rate 2e-5, 0.1 warmup ratio, and cosine scheduler. Preference learning uses learning rate 5e-7 with the same warmup and scheduler configuration, β = 0.1 for all algorithms, and λ+/λ− = 1.33 for KTO as recommended.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric across all single-turn benchmarks is pass@1 accuracy—the model generates one solution and it is evaluated against ground-truth answers (for math, using exact match via grading functions provided by the original dataset authors; for coding, using test case execution). For math benchmarks, the paper tests both textual reasoning and program-enhanced settings (where the model writes and executes Python code) and reports the best performance of the two; this dual-mode evaluation inflates the reported math scores relative to models evaluated in text-only mode, though the paper does not discuss this inflation explicitly. For multi-turn evaluation on MINT, the metric is success rate at Turn 5—the fraction of problems correctly solved after up to five turns of interaction with tools and feedback. For IFEval, the metric is prompt-level loose accuracy. For reward model evaluation, the metrics are pairwise preference accuracy on RewardBench, human correlation on AutoJ (reported as percentage agreement across Code, Math, and Other splits), and agreement with human judgments on MT-Bench. For reranking experiments, the metric is pass@1 accuracy after the reward model selects one response from N candidates.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against three categories of open-source models (Table 2). General-purpose models: Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 (Jiang et al., 2023a), Zephyr-7B-β (Tunstall et al., 2023), OpenChat-3.5-1210 (Wang et al., 2023a), Starling-LM-7B-α (Zhu et al., 2023), Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct (Jiang et al., 2024), DeepSeek-LLM-67B-Chat (DeepSeek-AI, 2024), QWen1.5-72B-Chat (Bai et al., 2023). Coding specialists: Magicoder-S-DS-6.7B (Wei et al., 2023), OpenCodeInterpreter-DS-6.7B and OpenCodeInterpreter-CL-70B (Zheng et al., 2024), DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct (Guo et al., 2024a), CodeLLaMA-70B-Instruct (Roziere et al., 2023). Math specialists: MAmmoTH-7B-Mistral (Yue et al., 2023), WizardMath-7B-v1.1 (Luo et al., 2023a), OpenMath-Mistral-7B and OpenMath-CodeLLaMA-70B (Toshniwal et al., 2024). Proprietary model results for GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4 are sourced from prior work and cited in Table 3; the paper does not independently re-evaluate these models. For reward model evaluation, baselines include PairRM (Jiang et al., 2023b), Starling-RM-7B and Starling-RM-34B (Zhu et al., 2023), UltraRM-13B (Cui et al., 2023), and GPT-3.5 Turbo / GPT-4 as proprietary reference points.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper does not use a standardized compute budget for fair comparison across methods during model evaluation—each model is evaluated under its standard inference protocol (typically single-pass greedy or temperature-based sampling for pass@1). For SFT and preference learning, all models are trained for exactly 1 epoch, which standardizes the training compute but does not control for differences in model size, data volume, or inference-time compute. For the reranking experiments with EURUS-RM-7B, the compute budget is parameterized by N, the number of candidate responses per instruction (N ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}), with the reward model selecting the best candidate among N. The paper does not discuss FLOPs- or wall-clock-matched comparisons between EURUS models and baselines of different sizes.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation, statistical significance testing, or confidence intervals for any of its evaluation results. All numbers in Tables 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, and 10 are reported as point estimates without variance or error bars. The decontamination protocol (Appendix A.3) is described in detail: for LeetCode, Exact Substring Matching is applied; for other benchmarks, "We perform 8-gram exact matching to compare ULTRAINTERACT instructions with test sets of the same task. We remove those instructions that overlap 8 grams with any test sample." However, the paper does not report how many instructions were removed by this process or what percentage of the training data was affected. For the ablation study in Table 5, three data configurations are tested (Ground-truth, Open-source Only, ULTRAINTERACT Only) but no repeated runs or statistical comparisons are reported.

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Overall Reasoning Performance (Table 3)

The headline result is that EURUS models achieve the best average performance among open-source models of comparable size across the 12 evaluated benchmarks. EURUS-7B-KTO achieves 48.8% average across all 12 benchmarks, the highest among ~7B models, compared to OpenChat-3.5-1210 at 46.2%, OpenCI-DS-6.7B at 40.5%, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 at 28.5%. EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 59.0% average, compared to DeepSeek-LLM-67B-Chat at 53.5% and QWen1.5-72B-Chat at 52.2%. GPT-3.5 Turbo scores 57.0% and GPT-4 scores 74.8%. The paper claims EURUS-70B "achieves better performance than GPT-3.5 Turbo" (Section 4.1), but this claim merits scrutiny: EURUS-70B-NCA's 59.0% average exceeds GPT-3.5 Turbo's 57.0% by 2.0 percentage points, but this aggregate masks substantial per-benchmark variation where GPT-3.5 Turbo outperforms EURUS-70B on several individual benchmarks (MBPP: 82.5 vs. 71.9; HumanEval: 76.8 vs. 79.3, where EURUS leads; GSM-Plus: 61.2 vs. 62.8; TheoremQA: 35.6 vs. 32.6)—the aggregate is dominated by EURUS's substantial leads on SVAMP (90.3 vs. 83.0), ASDiv (89.0 vs. 90.6, where GPT-3.5 Turbo leads slightly), and BBH (80.0 vs. 70.1). The claim of "comparable performance to GPT-3.5 Turbo" (Section 1) is more accurate than "outperforms."

#### Coding Benchmarks (Table 3)

On HumanEval, EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 79.3%, the highest among open-source models and exceeding GPT-3.5 Turbo's 76.8%. However, DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct achieves 82.3%, and OpenCI-CL-70B achieves 77.4%—EURUS-70B-NCA is not the outright leader here. EURUS-7B-KTO achieves 56.1%, compared to Magicoder-S-DS-6.7B at 75.6% and OpenCI-DS-6.7B at 76.8%—substantially lower than coding-specialized models of similar size, though higher than general-purpose models like Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 (39.0%) and Zephyr-7B-β (29.3%). On MBPP, EURUS-70B-SFT achieves 74.2% (higher than any preference learning variant), compared to GPT-3.5 Turbo's 82.5% and DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct's 73.9%. On LeetCode—the paper's most emphasized coding benchmark due to its recency and difficulty—EURUS-70B-SFT and EURUS-70B-NCA both achieve 33.3%, compared to DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct at 27.8% and GPT-3.5 Turbo at 23.3%. The 33.3% pass@1 on LeetCode is the strongest single-benchmark result and represents a 13.3-percentage-point margin over the best prior open-source model (DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct at 27.8% is 5.5 points behind). However, DPO degrades LeetCode performance dramatically: EURUS-7B-DPO achieves only 8.3% on LeetCode compared to EURUS-7B-SFT's 20.0%, a 11.7-point drop that the paper attributes to the absolute-reward failure mode analyzed in Section 6.1.

#### Math Benchmarks (Table 3)

EURUS models show strong performance across all five math benchmarks. On GSM-Plus (adversarially perturbed GSM8K), EURUS-7B-KTO achieves 55.0%, substantially behind WizardMath-7B-v1.1 at 54.6% (marginally ahead) and OpenMath-Mistral-7B at 59.4%, but ahead of Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 at 15.7%. EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 62.8%, exceeding GPT-3.5 Turbo's 61.2% and trailing DeepSeek-LM-67B-Chat's 65.0%. On MATH, EURUS-7B-NCA achieves 34.2%, behind MAmmoTH-7B-Mistral at 36.0% and OpenMath-Mistral-7B at 39.1%. EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 41.7%, behind QWen1.5-72B-Chat at 43.4% and OpenMath-CL-70B at 45.9%. This is a notable weakness: EURUS models are not the top performers on MATH, the one benchmark that is in-distribution (MATH training data is included in ULTRAINTERACT). The paper does not discuss why EURUS underperforms math specialists on the one in-distribution math benchmark.

On TheoremQA, EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 32.6%, the highest among open-source models (MAmmoTH-7B-Mistral's 26.3% is flagged as contaminated and struck through) and approaching GPT-3.5 Turbo's 35.6%. EURUS-7B-KTO achieves 20.6%, compared to MAmmoTH-7B-Mistral's contaminated 26.3% and OpenChat-3.5-1210's contaminated 19.1%. The 32.6% on TheoremQA is the paper's second highlighted result alongside LeetCode, representing a substantial improvement over uncontaminated open-source baselines. On SVAMP and ASDiv (simpler math word problems), EURUS models achieve scores in the 82–90% range, well above most baselines but with several specialist models achieving comparable results (OpenMath-Mistral-7B at 83.4% SVAMP; WizardMath-7B-v1.1 at 73.5% ASDiv)—these benchmarks may be approaching saturation for strong models.

#### Logical Reasoning and Instruction-Following (Table 3)

On BBH-Hard, EURUS-70B-KTO achieves 80.8%, exceeding DeepSeek-LM-67B-Chat at 78.9% and GPT-3.5 Turbo at 70.1%. EURUS-7B-KTO achieves 67.6%, the highest among ~7B models. The BBH results show consistent improvement from SFT to preference learning across both model sizes. On IFEval (instruction-following), EURUS-7B-SFT achieves 44.0%, competitive with general-purpose models like Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 (44.4%) and OpenChat-3.5-1210 (50.3%) but substantially ahead of specialists like MAGicoder-S-DS-6.7B (21.1%) and OpenMath-Mistral-7B (15.0%). EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 49.2%, behind QWen1.5-72B-Chat at 53.4% and DeepSeek-LM-67B-Chat at 52.7%, but vastly ahead of specialist models like OpenMath-CL-70B (15.7%) and OpenCI-CL-70B (26.8%). The instruction-following performance demonstrates that mixing ULTRAINTERACT with general-purpose SFT data (UltraChat, ShareGPT, OpenOrca) successfully preserves general capabilities while adding reasoning skills—a finding confirmed by the ablation in Table 10, where ULTRAINTERACT-only SFT achieves only 17.4% on IFEval.

#### Preference Learning Performance (Table 3, Figure 5)

The comparison of preference learning algorithms constitutes the paper's central algorithmic finding. Across all three EURUS variants evaluated, KTO and NCA produce consistent improvements over SFT, while DPO either degrades performance or fails entirely.

For EURUS-7B: SFT baseline averages 46.5% across all benchmarks. KTO improves to 48.8% (+2.3 percentage points), NCA to 48.1% (+1.6 points), and DPO degrades to 44.5% (-2.0 points). The degradation from DPO is not uniform: DPO improves MINT coding (15.4 → 20.6) and MINT math (28.4 → 32.4), but substantially hurts HumanEval (55.5 → 50.6), MBPP (59.1 → 52.1), LeetCode (20.0 → 8.3), and several math benchmarks. The multi-turn improvement under DPO is interesting because it suggests DPO's discriminative training helps the model correct errors in interactive settings, even as it degrades single-turn generation quality—a nuanced pattern the paper does not fully explore.

For EURUS-70B: SFT averages 57.1%. KTO improves to 58.4% (+1.3 points). NCA reaches 59.0% (+1.9 points). DPO is reported as follows: "DPO training of our 70B model fails since the rewards go down to -infinity." No DPO results are reported for the 70B model. The failure mode—rewards collapsing to negative infinity—is consistent with the diagnosis in Section 6.1 that DPO does not constrain absolute reward values, and at larger model scales (where the policy can move further from initialization due to higher capacity) this collapse becomes catastrophic rather than merely performance-degrading.

Figure 5 provides mechanistic evidence for the performance differences. During DPO training of EURUS-7B, chosen-action rewards decrease from approximately 0 to -1.26 (final value), while rejected-action rewards decrease even further, producing an increasing margin but negative absolute chosen rewards. In KTO training, chosen rewards increase to approximately +0.4 (final value) while rejected rewards decrease, producing both an increasing margin and positive chosen rewards. In NCA training, chosen rewards increase to approximately +0.16 (final value) with rejected rewards decreasing, an intermediate pattern. The final chosen reward values (KTO +0.4 > NCA +0.16 > DPO -1.26) directly predict the overall accuracy ranking in Table 3.

#### Multi-Turn Interaction (Table 3)

Multi-turn evaluation on MINT reveals large improvements from preference learning. EURUS-7B-SFT achieves 15.4% on MINT coding and 28.4% on MINT math. EURUS-7B-KTO improves coding to 19.1% and math to 43.6%—a dramatic +15.2 percentage point improvement in math, the largest single-benchmark gain from preference learning. EURUS-7B-NCA achieves 21.3% coding and 38.7% math. The paper attributes multi-turn improvement specifically to the inclusion of multi-turn trajectory pairs from ULTRAINTERACT during preference learning: "Since SFT models only use the single-turn data from ULTRAINTERACT while preference learning uses the multi-turn ones, the improvements in interaction ability should also be attributed to ULTRAINTERACT rather than the algorithms alone" (Section 4.1). However, EURUS-7B-DPO also improves multi-turn performance (20.6% coding, 32.4% math) despite degrading single-turn performance, suggesting that the multi-turn benefit comes from the data (exposure to interaction trajectories) while the algorithm choice (DPO vs. KTO vs. NCA) modulates whether single-turn capability is preserved or degraded.

EURUS-70B models show a similar pattern. EURUS-70B-SFT achieves 31.6% coding and 40.4% math on MINT. EURUS-70B-KTO improves to 39.0% coding and 49.8% math—the 49.8% math score is the highest multi-turn result in the table, exceeding DeepSeek-LLM-67B-Chat (30.9% coding, 41.8% math) by large margins. EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 38.2% coding and 39.6% math. The multi-turn math improvement for the 70B model (+9.4 percentage points from KTO) is smaller than for the 7B model (+15.2 points), suggesting that the larger base model already possesses stronger interactive capability from pretraining, leaving less room for preference learning to improve.

#### Reward Model Evaluation (Table 4, Figure 4)

EURUS-RM-7B achieves 82.4% average accuracy on RewardBench, outperforming Starling-RM-7B (76.2%), UltraRM-13B (69.8%), and PairRM (58.7%), and comparable to Starling-RM-34B (84.0%). The strongest result is on the Chat-Hard split (65.3%), where EURUS-RM-7B exceeds all baselines including Starling-RM-34B (59.0%). On the Reasoning split, EURUS-RM-7B achieves 87.0%, behind Starling-RM-34B (90.3%) but well ahead of Starling-RM-7B (74.6%). On AutoJ, EURUS-RM-7B achieves 67.5% on Code, 62.5% on Math, and 63.6% on Others (overall 64.5%), exceeding GPT-4's 69.2% on Code but trailing on Math (51.4%) and Others (61.4%). On MT-Bench, EURUS-RM-7B achieves 72.9%, substantially exceeding all baselines including GPT-4 at 63.9%. The paper claims EURUS-RM-7B "achieves a better correlation with human annotators than all existing models on AutoJ and MT-Bench" (Section 5.1), which is accurate for MT-Bench (72.9 > 63.9 for GPT-4) and overall AutoJ (64.5 > 61.9 for GPT-4), though GPT-4 leads on the Code split (69.2 vs. 67.5).

The ablation of the training objective reveals the complementary roles of the BT and direct reward terms. Removing LDR (training with BT loss only) drops Reasoning performance from 87.0% to 77.5% on RewardBench—a 9.5-percentage-point decrease, confirming the importance of the absolute-reward signal for reasoning tasks. Removing LBT (training with direct reward terms only) drops Chat-Hard from 65.3% to 58.5%—a 6.8-point decrease, confirming that relative preference modeling matters for conversational evaluation. Removing UltraFeedback + UltraSafety (training on ULTRAINTERACT only) drops Chat from 96.5% to 73.4% while Reasoning remains at 78.0%, confirming that ULTRAINTERACT provides reasoning-specific reward modeling capability while UltraFeedback is necessary for conversational reward modeling.

The reranking results (Figure 4, Table 9) show that EURUS-RM-7B consistently improves pass@1 accuracy when used to select among N candidate responses from Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2. On HumanEval, EURUS-RM-7B achieves 47.0% at N=16, compared to 40.9% for self-consistency and 45.7% for Starling-RM-34B. On MBPP, EURUS-RM-7B achieves 43.9% at N=16 vs. 37.1% self-consistency and 40.4% Starling-RM-34B. On GSM8K, EURUS-RM-7B achieves 57.3% at N=16 vs. 52.8% self-consistency and 56.5% Starling-RM-34B. On MATH, EURUS-RM-7B achieves 17.3% at N=16 vs. 16.8% self-consistency, but Starling-RM-34B degrades MATH performance to 7.7%—a striking negative result where a reward model actively hurts performance by selecting incorrect answers. The paper notes that "EURUS-RM-7B's reranking performance scales well with #responses per instruction, except a slight decrease in HumanEval when increasing response number from 8 to 16" (from 47.6% at N=8 to 47.0% at N=16), and that "Starling-RM-34B suffers from severe performance drop on HumanEval and it consistently hurts model accuracy on MATH" (from random sampling's 8.5% at N=16 to 7.7%).

#### MMLU and MT-Bench Results (Table 8)

The paper evaluates EURUS on MMLU and MT-Bench to assess whether reasoning capability comes at the cost of general knowledge and conversational ability. On MMLU, EURUS-7B-SFT achieves 61.8%, comparable to general-purpose models like OpenChat-3.5-1210 (63.4%) and Starling-LM-7B-α (64.0%), and higher than the base Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 (58.9%). EURUS-70B-SFT achieves 59.1%, trailing DeepSeek-LM-67B-Chat (72.3%) and QWen1.5-72B-Chat (72.9%) by large margins—the paper attributes this to the base model: "CodeLLaMA-70B has not been intentionally optimized for knowledge." On MT-Bench, EURUS-7B-SFT achieves 7.15, behind Zephyr-7B-β (7.34) and OpenChat-3.5-1210 (7.81), but KTO improves this to 7.38, matching GPT-3.5 Turbo's 7.94 within the range of typical MT-Bench score variance (which the paper does not estimate). EURUS-70B-KTO achieves 7.93 on MT-Bench, effectively matching GPT-3.5 Turbo's 7.94.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**SFT data composition (Table 5, Table 10):** Training on ULTRAINTERACT alone produces 47.7% average accuracy across coding, math, BBH, and IFEval, but IFEval drops catastrophically to 17.4% (compared to 44.0% with the full data mixture). This confirms that ULTRAINTERACT does not provide instruction-following training signal and must be complemented with general-purpose alignment data. Training on open-source data alone (UltraChat, ShareGPT, OpenOrca, without ULTRAINTERACT) produces only 37.0% average, with coding at 31.2%, math at 33.5%, and BBH at 65.3%. This 10.7-point gap between open-source-only and ULTRAINTERACT-only (on non-IFEval benchmarks) quantifies the reasoning-specific contribution of ULTRAINTERACT. The full SFT mixture achieves 53.6%—higher than either data source alone—demonstrating that ULTRAINTERACT and general-purpose data are complementary rather than redundant.

**ULTRAINTERACT vs. ground-truth rationales (Table 5, Table 10):** Replacing ULTRAINTERACT's generated actions with original ground-truth rationales and answers from the source datasets ("Ground-Truth" row) produces only 44.0% average accuracy, substantially lower than EURUS-7B-SFT's 53.6%. The largest gaps appear on LeetCode (8.9% vs. 20.0%), GSM-Plus (62.2% vs. 73.7%), MATH (15.0% vs. 32.6%), and TheoremQA (9.6% vs. 20.0%). The paper attributes this advantage to "ULTRAINTERACT's designs of divide-and-conquer and code-as-action patterns"—training on generated solutions that use structured decomposition and code execution produces models that generalize better than training on ground-truth rationales, even though ground-truth rationales are guaranteed correct. This is a non-obvious finding: synthetic training data with specific formatting and execution patterns can outperform human-authored solutions as training material.

**Reward model objective ablation (Table 4):** Five ablation configurations are evaluated for EURUS-RM-7B. Removing LDR (direct reward term): Reasoning drops from 87.0% to 77.5% (-9.5 points); Chat-Hard drops from 65.3% to 59.9% (-5.4 points); AutoJ overall increases slightly from 64.5% to 65.0%. Removing LBT (Bradley-Terry term): Reasoning drops from 87.0% to 84.2% (-2.8 points); Chat-Hard drops from 65.3% to 58.5% (-6.8 points); AutoJ Math increases from 62.5% to 66.7%. These asymmetric effects confirm that LDR primarily benefits reasoning while LBT primarily benefits conversational evaluation, and that using both terms together yields the best overall performance (82.4% vs. 78.3% w/o LDR and 80.8% w/o LBT on RewardBench average). Removing UltraSafety: Safety drops from 80.7% to 67.7% (-13.0 points), but Reasoning drops slightly from 87.0% to 81.7%, and AutoJ Math improves from 62.5% to 61.1% while Code improves from 67.5% to 66.7%—a trade-off consistent with the hypothesis that safety training introduces a conservative bias that slightly suppresses reasoning calibration. Removing both UltraFeedback and UltraSafety: Chat drops from 96.5% to 73.4% (-23.1 points); Reasoning drops from 87.0% to 78.0% (-9.0 points); overall RewardBench average drops from 82.4% to 78.0%. The Chat collapse is expected since UltraFeedback provides conversational preference signal; the moderate Reasoning drop suggests ULTRAINTERACT alone provides substantial reasoning reward modeling capability.

**Preference learning algorithm comparison on multi-turn (Table 3):** DPO improves multi-turn performance on MINT for EURUS-7B (coding: 15.4% SFT → 20.6% DPO; math: 28.4% → 32.4%) despite degrading overall single-turn average. This within-algorithm pattern—DPO helps interaction but hurts generation—is not analyzed in detail by the paper but suggests that the absolute-reward collapse affects single-turn generation quality more than interactive correction ability. KTO produces the largest multi-turn gains (coding: 19.1%; math: 43.6%), consistent with its preservation of positive absolute chosen rewards.

**Preference learning algorithm comparison by benchmark (Table 3):** Not all benchmarks respond uniformly to preference learning. On HumanEval, both KTO and NCA produce minimal or negative changes from SFT for EURUS-7B (55.5% SFT → 56.1% KTO, 55.5% NCA) while DPO degrades to 50.6%. On LeetCode, all preference learning algorithms degrade performance for EURUS-7B (20.0% SFT → 8.3% DPO, 18.9% KTO, 14.4% NCA), while for EURUS-70B, NCA preserves LeetCode performance (33.3% SFT → 33.3% NCA) and KTO degrades it moderately (33.3% → 26.1%). On BBH, KTO improves EURUS-7B from 64.6% to 67.6% while DPO produces a smaller gain to 65.0%. This benchmark-level variation suggests that the optimal preference learning algorithm depends on the specific reasoning task, but the paper does not provide a systematic analysis of which task characteristics predict algorithm effectiveness.

**Data scaling for preference learning:** The paper does not ablate the quantity of preference learning data (e.g., training with subsets of the 220K ULTRAINTERACT pairs or the 340K UltraFeedback pairs). The contribution of data scale versus data quality (tree structure, per-turn pairs) versus algorithm choice is therefore not disentangled—it is possible that the KTO/NCA advantage over DPO is partly attributable to the specific data mixture rather than the algorithms per se.

**Base model scaling:** The paper trains both 7B and 70B variants, providing a partial view of scaling behavior. However, there is no intermediate scale (e.g., 13B or 33B) and no ablation of how the optimal data mixture, learning rate, or preference learning algorithm changes with model size. The catastrophic DPO failure at 70B (rewards → -infinity) versus performance degradation at 7B suggests an interaction between model scale and algorithm stability that is not systematically explored.

**Interaction history in SFT:** The paper reports that discarding interaction history during SFT yields better performance, but this is stated as an empirical finding without ablation results comparing SFT with and without interaction history. The claim "it yields better performance to discard interaction history" is provided without a corresponding table row showing the performance of SFT trained on full trajectories, making it an assertion rather than a demonstrated result within the paper.

### Critical Assessment

The experiments demonstrate that EURUS models achieve strong performance across diverse reasoning benchmarks, validating the overall claim that ULTRAINTERACT-based training produces generalist reasoning models competitive with GPT-3.5 Turbo in aggregate. However, several claims in the paper are supported with qualifications that the presentation sometimes glosses over, and certain central claims would benefit from experimental designs not present in the paper.

**Claim: "EURUS-70B outperforms GPT-3.5 Turbo in reasoning."** The evidence in Table 3 shows EURUS-70B-NCA achieving 59.0% average across 12 benchmarks versus GPT-3.5 Turbo's 57.0%. This 2.0-percentage-point difference is small relative to the per-benchmark variance, and on several individual benchmarks GPT-3.5 Turbo leads (MBPP: 82.5 vs. 71.9; TheoremQA: 35.6 vs. 32.6; GSM-Plus: comparable at 61.2 vs. 62.8). The aggregate advantage is driven primarily by EURUS's stronger performance on BBH (80.0 vs. 70.1) and SVAMP (90.3 vs. 83.0)—benchmarks where strong open-source models often excel due to training data similarity. The claim is *technically supported by the reported numbers* but would be stronger if accompanied by statistical testing (is the 2.0-point aggregate difference significant given per-benchmark variance?) and per-benchmark analysis showing where the advantage comes from.

**Claim: "DPO hurts reasoning performance while KTO and NCA improve it."** The evidence in Table 3 and Figure 5 provides strong support for this claim at the aggregate level. However, the mechanism—that absolute chosen rewards matter and DPO allows them to collapse—is demonstrated convincingly for the 7B model (Figure 5) but not for the 70B model (where DPO simply fails to converge). The paper's additional claim that "the rewards of chosen data in the last training step follow the ranking order of KTO > NCA > DPO, positively correlate with their performance trends" is supported by the 7B evidence but is correlational: the paper does not experimentally manipulate absolute chosen rewards independently of the algorithm to demonstrate causation. A stronger test would be to train DPO with an explicit regularization term preventing chosen rewards from dropping below zero and show that this rescues DPO's performance—an experiment the paper does not conduct.

**Claim: "Preference trees facilitate preference learning for reasoning."** The evidence supporting this claim is indirect. ULTRAINTERACT's tree structure provides per-turn action pairs, and preference learning with these pairs improves multi-turn performance (Table 3: MINT scores increase). However, the paper does not include an ablation comparing tree-structured pairs against a flat baseline—for example, training preference learning on only the final-turn pairs (as in standard HH-RLHF-style data) versus the full multi-turn pairs. Without this ablation, the specific contribution of the tree structure (per-turn pairs) versus simply having more preference data (220K pairs, which is substantial) is not isolated. The improvement in multi-turn performance could be attributed to the multi-turn trajectory data per se (which teaches interaction patterns) rather than to the tree-organized pairwise structure specifically.

**Claim: "The augmented reward modeling objective is beneficial."** Table 4 provides clear ablation evidence that removing LDR hurts reasoning (87.0% → 77.5% on RewardBench Reasoning) and that removing LBT hurts chat (65.3% → 58.5% on Chat-Hard). The complementarity is well-demonstrated. However, the paper does not explore alternative instantiations of the absolute-reward idea: would a simple L2 penalty on `|r_θ(x, y_c)|` or a hinge loss `max(0, -r_θ(x, y_c))` work equally well? The specific form of L_DR (-log σ(r_c) - log σ(-r_r)) is presented as the single instantiation without comparison to alternative absolute-reward formulations. The selective application of L_DR only to ULTRAINTERACT instances is sensible but also not ablated—the paper doesn't show what happens if L_DR is applied to UltraFeedback pairs as well.

**Strengths of the experimental design:** (1) The 12-benchmark evaluation suite covers diverse reasoning domains (coding, math, logic) plus general capabilities (instruction-following, multi-turn interaction, MMLU, MT-Bench), providing a genuinely comprehensive assessment. (2) The decontamination protocol (8-gram matching, Exact Substring Matching for LeetCode) is explicitly described and applied, and contaminated baseline numbers are flagged rather than silently reported—a level of rigor above typical practice. (3) The inclusion of both single-turn and multi-turn evaluation captures the interaction capability that ULTRAINTERACT is specifically designed to provide. (4) The ablation of SFT data composition (Table 5) cleanly separates the contributions of ULTRAINTERACT, ground-truth rationales, and general-purpose data. (5) The preference learning analysis in Figure 5 moves beyond reporting final accuracy to inspecting intermediate reward trajectories, providing mechanistic insight rather than just outcome comparisons.

**Weaknesses and missing experiments:**

- **No measure of variance:** All results in Tables 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, and 10 are point estimates. For a 500-question test set (MATH test split) or a 164-question test set (HumanEval), differences of 1–2 percentage points (e.g., EURUS-70B-NCA's 59.0% vs. GPT-3.5 Turbo's 57.0%) may not be statistically significant. Confidence intervals or standard errors are essential for interpreting whether the reported ranking is robust, yet none are provided. The MT-Bench scores (reported to two decimal places, e.g., 7.38) are particularly likely to be noisy given the small number of judgments per model, and small score differences should not be over-interpreted.

- **The "discard interaction history" claim is unsubstantiated by evidence:** The paper states as a finding that SFT works better when trained only on correct leaf nodes rather than full interaction trajectories, but no ablation table compares these two SFT configurations. The omission is significant because it's a counterintuitive result that directly informs the training recipe. Without the ablation, the reader cannot assess whether the improvement is marginal or substantial, or whether it interacts with model scale or data mixture.

- **No ablation isolating the tree structure:** The paper claims the preference tree is a key innovation, but never ablates it. A comparison of preference learning with (1) full multi-turn tree-structured pairs versus (2) only final-turn pairs versus (3) random turn sampling would isolate what the tree structure contributes beyond simply having more data.

- **Single base model families:** All experiments use Mistral-7B and CodeLlama-70B. The strong results might be partly attributable to these specific base models (Mistral-7B is known for strong instruction-following; CodeLlama-70B is pretrained on code). Training EURUS variants from other base models (e.g., Llama-2-7B, DeepSeek-7B) would test whether the ULTRAINTERACT recipe generalizes across model families.

- **MATH performance is not state-of-the-art:** EURUS-7B-NCA achieves 34.2% on MATH, behind MAmmoTH-7B-Mistral (36.0%, though contaminated on TheoremQA) and OpenMath-Mistral-7B (39.1%). EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 41.7%, behind QWen1.5-72B-Chat (43.4%) and OpenMath-CL-70B (45.9%). MATH is the only in-distribution benchmark, and EURUS's underperformance on it relative to math specialists is not discussed. This could indicate that ULTRAINTERACT's code-as-action format (generating Python to solve math problems) is suboptimal for MATH problems that are more naturally solved through symbolic manipulation, or that the SFT data mixture dilutes math-specific capability relative to specialist training.

- **Comparison against a strong DPO baseline is absent:** The paper shows DPO degrades reasoning performance, motivating KTO and NCA as alternatives. However, the DPO training uses a fixed β = 0.1 with no hyperparameter sweep. DPO is known to be sensitive to β—a larger β keeps the model closer to the SFT initialization and might prevent the chosen-reward collapse. The paper's claim that DPO is "less suitable for reasoning tasks" would be substantially stronger if it showed that the degradation persists across reasonable β values.

- **The FLOPs- or parameter-matched comparison with baselines is absent:** Table 3 compares EURUS models against baselines of similar size categories (~7B, ~40B, ~70B), but these categories are broad. For example, DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct at ~33B achieves 82.3% on HumanEval versus EURUS-7B-KTO's 56.1%. A reader might ask: if we fine-tuned DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct on ULTRAINTERACT, would it outperform EURUS-7B-KTO by an even larger margin? The paper answers this partially by comparing within size categories, but the absence of controlled experiments where the same training recipe is applied to different base models leaves open the question of whether ULTRAINTERACT or the base model choice is the primary driver of performance.

- **GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4 evaluation is not independently replicated:** The paper cites GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4 results from prior work rather than re-evaluating them under the same prompting conditions. Evaluation protocols (prompt format, CoT instructions, sampling temperature, answer extraction) can substantially affect benchmarks like MATH and LeetCode, and comparing across papers introduces uncontrolled protocol variance. The strong claim that EURUS-70B "matches GPT-3.5 Turbo" is therefore comparing numbers reported under potentially different evaluation conditions.

- **The reranking improvement does not scale uniformly:** The claim that "EURUS-RM-7B improves LLMs' reasoning performance by a large margin through reranking" is supported by the GSM8K results (57.3% at N=16 vs. 45.7% self-consistency) but the margin on HumanEval (47.0% vs. 40.9%) and MATH (17.3% vs. 16.8%) is much smaller. The paper acknowledges the HumanEval decrease from N=8 to N=16 but does not investigate why reranking saturates or degrades at higher N on certain benchmarks. The Starling-RM-34B results on MATH (7.7% at N=16, worse than random sampling's 8.5%) demonstrate that reward model reranking can actively harm performance—a finding the paper notes but does not diagnostically explain beyond attributing it to the reward model's training data and objective differences.

- **The selective application of L_DR is not ablated:** The paper applies L_DR only to ULTRAINTERACT instances and L_BT to all instances. An ablation applying L_DR uniformly to all data (UltraFeedback, UltraSafety, and ULTRAINTERACT) would reveal whether absolute-reward pressure on conversational preference data hurts or helps conversational reward modeling—a natural question given the paper's hypothesis that absolute correctness is specific to reasoning.

- **The correctness of the DPO diagnosis relies on observing implicit rewards, but the paper does not establish that implicit rewards faithfully represent the model's generation quality:** Figure 5 shows reward trajectories that correlate with downstream performance, but the rewards are derived from the DPO/KTO/NCA objective functions and represent the policy's *implicit* valuation of outputs. Whether these implicit rewards directly measure the model's probability of generating correct answers is not validated. A stronger diagnostic would compare implicit rewards against actual generation accuracy on a held-out set throughout training to confirm that negative chosen rewards correspond to degraded generation quality—the paper's argument rests on this correspondence but does not directly verify it.

In summary, the experiments provide solid evidence that EURUS models achieve state-of-the-art open-source performance on diverse reasoning benchmarks and that preference learning with KTO or NCA outperforms DPO on these tasks. The evidence for the tree structure specifically and for the absolute-reward mechanism specifically is correlational and would benefit from controlled ablations that are absent from the paper. The comparison with GPT-3.5 Turbo is credible at the aggregate level but the small margin and per-benchmark variation warrant caution in interpreting the claim of "outperforming" versus "matching." The paper's transparency about data contamination and its comprehensive benchmark suite are methodological strengths; the absence of variance estimates and certain key ablations are the most significant experimental limitations.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### 6.1 The Preference Tree Structure Is Asserted but Never Ablated

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper introduces the preference tree—a binary tree where correct and incorrect actions are paired at every turn of a multi-turn trajectory—as a central conceptual innovation. The paper states that "ULTRAINTERACT enables comparisons at every turn, in contrast to comparing only at the last turn (Bai et al., 2022), and thus can improve the models' interaction ability" (Section 2.3). This claim rests on the assumption that per-turn preference pairs provide training signal that final-turn pairs do not capture, and that this signal is causally responsible for the observed gains in multi-turn interaction performance.

**The consequence.** Without an ablation comparing tree-structured pairs against a flat baseline (e.g., preference learning using only final-turn pairs from the same trajectories, or pairs sampled randomly from any turn), the specific contribution of the tree structure is unidentifiable. The multi-turn performance improvements from preference learning—EURUS-7B-KTO achieving 19.1% on MINT coding and 43.6% on MINT math versus SFT's 15.4% and 28.4% (Table 3)—could be attributed to (a) the tree structure specifically, (b) simply having more preference data (220K pairs is substantial), or (c) exposure to multi-turn interaction trajectories during training (the model sees environment feedback and critique, teaching it to interpret such signals), independent of whether pairs are organized per-turn or only at trajectory endpoints. A practitioner implementing a similar system cannot determine whether the engineering investment in constructing preference trees—which requires maintaining and pairing actions across turns—is necessary, or whether simply collecting more flat preference pairs from multi-turn trajectories would suffice.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper acknowledges that preference learning uses multi-turn trajectory pairs while SFT uses only single-turn data: "Since SFT models only use the single-turn data from ULTRAINTERACT while preference learning uses the multi-turn ones, the improvements in interaction ability should also be attributed to ULTRAINTERACT rather than the algorithms alone" (Section 4.1). This correctly attributes multi-turn improvements to the ULTRAINTERACT *data* (which contains interaction trajectories) rather than to the preference learning *algorithms* per se. However, this observation does not distinguish between the contribution of multi-turn trajectory exposure and the contribution of the tree organization specifically. The paper provides no experiment comparing: (1) preference learning with full tree-structured per-turn pairs, (2) preference learning with only final-turn pairs sampled from the same trees, (3) preference learning with per-turn pairs but randomly paired (not preserving the tree structure). Without such an ablation, the tree structure remains an asserted but unverified contributor to performance.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this as a limitation, does not ablate the tree structure, and does not suggest future work to isolate its contribution. This is a significant gap because the tree is presented as the paper's primary conceptual innovation (Figure 2, Section 2.3) yet its causal role is not experimentally established.

### 6.2 Difficulty Estimation and Data Construction Cost Is Unaccounted For

**The assumption or constraint.** The ULTRAINTERACT dataset is constructed using GPT-3.5 Turbo as the actor model, GPT-4 as the critique model, and a three-stage escalation process to generate correct actions for difficult problems (Appendix A.2). For problems where even GPT-4 fails to produce correct actions (the paper reports "nearly zero pass@100 accuracies" for the hardest problems—Section 2.3), the construction process provides the actor with ground-truth rationales and answers to elicit correct actions. The paper describes this as a necessary step to handle "challenging problems that GPT-3.5-Turbo fails to solve" (Section 2.1). This data construction pipeline incurs substantial API costs that are not reported or analyzed: generating 20+ samples per instruction across two model tiers (GPT-3.5 Turbo and GPT-4 Turbo), plus GPT-4 critique generation for every turn of every trajectory.

**The consequence.** The headline results—EURUS-70B matching GPT-3.5 Turbo in aggregate reasoning, EURUS-RM-7B achieving state-of-the-art reward modeling—are achieved using training data whose construction cost is unknown and likely substantial. A practitioner attempting to replicate or extend this work faces an unbudgeted expense. More fundamentally, the reliance on GPT-4 for critique and GPT-4 Turbo for correct action generation on hard problems means that ULTRAINTERACT is bootstrapped from proprietary models that may themselves be unavailable or change behavior (API updates, deprecation, cost increases). This introduces a reproducibility risk: if GPT-4's critique quality changes, or if GPT-4 Turbo's output distribution shifts, the resulting ULTRAINTERACT data—and consequently EURUS model performance—may differ from what the paper reports. The paper's framing of EURUS as an "open-source" advance is partially undercut by the dependence on proprietary models during data construction—the training *recipe* is open, but the data generation *process* requires API access that may not be replicable by all researchers.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper explicitly describes the use of GPT-3.5 Turbo as the actor model (Section 2.2), GPT-4 as the critique model (Section 2.2), and the escalation to GPT-4 Turbo for hard problems: "progressively switching from more cost-effective models to the strong yet expensive GPT-4 Turbo" (Section 2.3). The three-stage process is described in Appendix A.2, including details on how ground-truth rationales are provided to the actor. However, the paper provides no accounting of total API calls, token consumption, approximate cost, or computational budget required to construct the 86K-instruction dataset. The 2048 samples per question used for difficulty estimation in conceptually similar prior work (as discussed in the \u0013reference example\u0013 paper summary) would represent an enormous expense at GPT-4 API pricing; ULTRAINTERACT's construction cost, while structured differently, is likely comparable in magnitude and is entirely unquantified.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the cost of data construction, does not report API usage statistics, and does not frame the proprietary model dependence as a limitation. The models and data are released (the paper states "Checkpoints of our EURUS models, accompanying ULTRAINTERACT alignment data to reproduce this research, will be publicly available"), which partially mitigates the reproducibility concern for users who can download pre-built data. However, for researchers who wish to extend the approach to new domains or datasets, the cost barrier remains unknown. The paper does not suggest future work on reducing construction cost, e.g., through self-consistency-based filtering or smaller open models as actors and critics.

### 6.3 MATH and Coding Benchmarks Do Not Saturate, and Gains Are Concentrated on Specific Benchmarks

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper presents EURUS as a suite of "reasoning generalists" (title) achieving state-of-the-art performance across diverse benchmarks. The aggregate average across 12 benchmarks (Table 3) is used as the primary summary metric, and the claim that EURUS-70B "achieves better performance than GPT-3.5 Turbo" (Section 4.1) rests on this aggregate. This framing assumes that the 12-benchmark average represents a meaningful summary of general reasoning capability and that improvements are broadly distributed rather than concentrated on a subset of benchmarks.

**The consequence.** The aggregate metric masks substantial per-benchmark variation that complicates the "generalist" narrative. EURUS models are not the top open-source performers on several key benchmarks:

- **MATH (in-distribution):** EURUS-7B-NCA achieves 34.2%, behind MAmmoTH-7B-Mistral (36.0%) and OpenMath-Mistral-7B (39.1%). EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 41.7%, behind OpenMath-CL-70B (45.9%) and QWen1.5-72B-Chat (43.4%). EURUS underperforms math specialists on the *only in-distribution* math benchmark, which is unexpected given that ULTRAINTERACT includes MATH training data.
- **HumanEval:** EURUS-7B-KTO achieves 56.1%, substantially behind Magicoder-S-DS-6.7B (75.6%) and OpenCI-DS-6.7B (76.8%)—both coding specialists of similar size. EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 79.3%, behind DeepSeek-Coder-33B-Instruct (82.3%), a model with roughly half the parameters.
- **MBPP:** EURUS-7B-KTO achieves 58.6%, behind Magicoder-S-DS-6.7B (70.4%) and OpenCI-DS-6.7B (66.2%). EURUS-70B-SFT achieves 74.2% —and KTO degrades this to 68.2%—versus GPT-3.5 Turbo's 82.5%.

The aggregate advantage over GPT-3.5 Turbo (59.0% vs. 57.0%) is driven by EURUS's strong performance on BBH (80.0 vs. 70.1, a 9.9-point margin) and the simpler math benchmarks SVAMP/ASDiv (89.5/90.3 vs. 83.0/90.6), while GPT-3.5 Turbo leads or ties on several others. A practitioner evaluating whether EURUS meets their needs cannot rely on the aggregate: if their task distribution skews toward competition-level coding (HumanEval, MBPP) or pure mathematical reasoning (MATH), specialist models or GPT-3.5 Turbo may be preferable. The "generalist" claim is true in the sense that EURUS performs reasonably across all benchmarks (unlike specialists that crater on out-of-domain tasks—e.g., MAGicoder at 16.4% on GSM-Plus), but it is not the best model on any individual benchmark where strong specialists exist.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 provides the per-benchmark breakdown, and the paper does not hide these numbers. However, the narrative text (Section 4.1) emphasizes the aggregate: "EURUS, both the 7B and 70B variants, achieve the best overall performance among open-source models of similar sizes" and "EURUS even outperform specialized models in corresponding domains in many cases." The "in many cases" qualifier is doing significant work—the cases where EURUS outperforms specialists are primarily on benchmarks outside the specialist's domain (e.g., EURUS outperforms coding specialists on math benchmarks), which is the expected pattern for a generalist versus specialists. The per-benchmark analysis provided in this limitation section is not conducted in the paper; the paper reports the numbers but does not discuss the pattern of where EURUS leads versus trails.

**Mitigation status.** Partially addressed through disclosure. The paper presents the full per-benchmark results in Table 3, allowing readers to conduct their own analysis. The paper does not claim EURUS is the best model on every benchmark—it claims "best overall performance" and "achieve state-of-the-art results among open-source models on a diverse set of benchmarks" (Abstract). However, the paper does not discuss *why* EURUS underperforms specialists on in-domain benchmarks (MATH for math, HumanEval for coding), which would help practitioners understand the tradeoff between specialization and generalization in the ULTRAINTERACT training recipe. The SFT data mixture (ULTRAINTERACT + UltraChat + ShareGPT + OpenOrca) likely dilutes domain-specific capability relative to specialist training on domain-only data, but this mechanism is not explored.

### 6.4 The DLDR Objective Requires Reasoning-Specific Data Partitioning, Limiting Generality

**The assumption or constraint.** The novel reward modeling objective L_ULTRAINTERACT applies the direct reward term L_DR only to instances from ULTRAINTERACT, while using the Bradley-Terry loss alone for instances from UltraFeedback and UltraSafety (Section 3.4). The paper states: "For instances from other datasets, we train with L_BT." This design choice embodies the paper's central insight: that absolute reward values matter for reasoning tasks (where correctness is objective) but relative preferences suffice for general conversation. However, this means the objective requires a priori knowledge of which training instances are "reasoning" versus "general conversation"—a data partitioning that must be done correctly for the method to work.

**The consequence.** The augmented objective does not provide a general-purpose replacement for Bradley-Terry reward modeling; it provides a domain-conditional objective that requires the user to correctly classify each training pair as reasoning or non-reasoning. If this classification is wrong—for example, if a math problem with conversational framing is classified as general chat, or if an open-ended preference pair has an objectively correct answer that goes unrecognized—the wrong loss term is applied and the benefit may be lost or reversed. More broadly, the objective does not address the harder question: for tasks that fall on a spectrum between pure reasoning (objective correctness) and pure conversation (subjective preference), which loss function is appropriate? The paper's binary partitioning (ULTRAINTERACT gets L_DR, everything else gets L_BT) sidesteps this question but does not resolve it.

The ablation in Table 4 shows that applying L_DR without L_BT (i.e., direct reward terms on all data) hurts Chat-Hard performance (58.5% vs. 65.3% with both terms), while applying L_BT without L_DR hurts Reasoning (77.5% vs. 87.0%). This confirms that the loss terms are domain-specific in their benefits and that a uniform application of either term degrades some capability. The implication is that building a reward model that excels at both reasoning and conversation requires knowing which data is which—a requirement that may not generalize to new datasets where the reasoning-versus-conversation boundary is ambiguous.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The ablation results in Table 4 demonstrate the complementarity and the cost of uniform application. The paper does not discuss the data partitioning requirement as a limitation, nor does it explore what happens if the partitioning is imperfect or if L_DR is applied to UltraFeedback pairs (which contain some reasoning-adjacent prompts). The selective application is presented as a feature—"For instances from ULTRAINTERACT, we train with [the full objective]"—but the dependence on correct classification is not analyzed.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not frame the data partitioning requirement as a limitation, does not ablate the effect of misclassification (e.g., applying L_DR to a subset of UltraFeedback pairs that have objective correctness criteria), and does not suggest methods for automatically determining which loss function to apply to new data. Future work could explore adaptive or learned weighting between L_BT and L_DR based on instance-level features, but the paper does not point in this direction.

### 6.5 No Statistical Significance or Variance Estimates Are Reported

**The assumption or constraint.** All experimental results in the paper are reported as point estimates without confidence intervals, standard errors, or any measure of statistical reliability. This is true for every table: Table 3 (12-benchmark evaluation, 500-question MATH test set, 164-question HumanEval, etc.), Table 4 (reward model benchmarks), Table 5 (ablation study), Tables 8-10 (supplementary results). The paper implicitly assumes that differences between point estimates—some as small as 1-2 percentage points—are meaningful and that the ranking of models by these point estimates reflects true performance differences rather than sampling noise.

**The consequence.** Several of the paper's headline claims involve small numerical margins whose statistical significance is unknown:

- EURUS-70B-NCA achieves 59.0% average versus GPT-3.5 Turbo's 57.0%—a 2.0 percentage point difference. On a 500-question test set, the standard error of a proportion is approximately `√(p(1-p)/n)`. At p ≈ 0.57, n ≈ 500 (per benchmark), the standard error for a single benchmark is roughly 2.2 percentage points. The aggregate across 12 benchmarks reduces this somewhat, but the per-benchmark variance is substantial (EURUS-70B-NCA ranges from 32.6% on TheoremQA to 90.3% on ASDiv), making a simple average potentially misleading.
- EURUS-7B-KTO achieves 48.8% versus EURUS-7B-NCA's 48.1%—a 0.7 percentage point difference. This is almost certainly within sampling noise, yet the paper presents KTO as the best 7B variant based on this ordering.
- KTO versus NCA differences for the 70B model (58.4% vs. 59.0%) reverse the 7B ordering, with NCA now ahead by 0.6 percentage points. Without variance estimates, it is impossible to determine whether KTO and NCA are distinguishable in performance or whether the apparent differences are noise.

The MT-Bench scores (Table 8) are reported to two decimal places (e.g., 7.38, 7.93), implying a precision that is unlikely to be justified given the small number of judgments per model on MT-Bench (typically 80-160 judgments across 80 questions with 1-2 judges each). The paper's claim that EURUS-70B-KTO "achieves the score of GPT-3.5 Turbo" on MT-Bench (7.93 vs. 7.94) involves a 0.01 difference—far below any reasonable confidence interval.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The absence of variance reporting is systematic across all results. The paper provides no discussion of statistical methodology, no bootstrap confidence intervals, and no mention of test set size in the context of statistical power. The number of test instances is available for some benchmarks (HumanEval: 164, MBPP: 427, MATH: 500) from their original papers, but the paper does not use these to estimate uncertainty.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not mention this as a limitation, does not provide confidence intervals, and does not suggest that future work should incorporate statistical rigor. This is a common practice in the LLM evaluation literature (many papers in this space report point estimates without uncertainty), but it is a genuine limitation: without variance estimates, readers cannot assess whether the reported performance differences—particularly the small margins on which several claims rest—are reliable or within sampling noise.

### 6.6 The Catastrophic DPO Failure at 70B Scale Is Neither Explained Nor Resolved

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper reports that "DPO training of our 70B model fails since the rewards go down to -infinity" (Section 4.1) and provides no DPO results for EURUS-70B in Table 3. This catastrophic failure is invoked to support the paper's central claim that DPO is unsuitable for reasoning tasks because it does not constrain absolute rewards. However, the paper does not diagnose *why* the failure is catastrophic at 70B when it is merely performance-degrading at 7B, and does not explore whether standard remedies (different β values, learning rate schedules, gradient clipping, or initialization from a different SFT checkpoint) could stabilize DPO training at scale.

**The consequence.** The DPO diagnosis in Section 6.1 and Figure 5 is based entirely on the 7B model. The 70B model's catastrophic failure—rewards collapsing to negative infinity, training failing to converge—is a qualitatively different failure mode than the 7B model's performance degradation (rewards becoming negative but finite, training loss decreasing). The paper's explanation for DPO's failure (Bradley-Terry only constrains relative differences) does not fully account for the scale-dependent transition: if the mechanism were solely relative-preference optimization, DPO should show performance degradation at 70B similar to 7B, not complete training collapse. The collapse suggests an additional mechanism—perhaps related to the larger model's greater capacity to move away from the SFT initialization, or to numerical stability issues specific to the β=0.1 setting at scale—that the paper does not investigate.

For practitioners, the practical implication is ambiguous. The paper's recommendation is to use KTO or NCA for reasoning preference learning. But a practitioner with a 70B-scale model who has already invested in a DPO pipeline might wonder whether DPO could be made to work with better hyperparameters rather than requiring a switch to a different algorithm. The paper provides no evidence either way—no hyperparameter sweep, no stability analysis, no discussion of whether the "-infinity" collapse is a fundamental limitation of DPO or an artifact of the default β=0.1 setting applied at scale.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper reports the failure in one sentence (Section 4.1) and provides the 7B reward trajectories in Figure 5. There is no corresponding figure for the 70B DPO training dynamics, no analysis of the collapse, and no hyperparameter sweep. The β value is fixed at 0.1 for all algorithms and both model scales (Appendix B). DPO is known in the literature to be sensitive to β—higher β values keep the policy closer to the reference model and can prevent reward collapse—but the paper does not test β values other than 0.1.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper presents the DPO failure as confirmatory evidence for its absolute-reward hypothesis but does not investigate scale-dependent effects, does not attempt to stabilize DPO at 70B, and does not discuss whether the 7B diagnosis fully explains the 70B collapse. This leaves open the possibility that properly tuned DPO could match or exceed KTO/NCA at 70B scale—a possibility the paper's strong claims about DPO's unsuitability for reasoning do not rule out.
