Here are the takeaways from this Ryan Peterman article (a Staff Engineer's pattern for finding promo-driving scope):

**The two-step promotion model**

Getting promoted, boiled down, is two things: finding next-level scope and then solving the problems to land impact. Finding the scope is often harder than the solving part itself—especially for Staff-and-above promotions. After watching many engineers cross that threshold, Ryan noticed a recurring pattern in how they surface the right work.

**Brainstorm with engineers one or two levels above you**

The pattern is dead simple: brainstorm project ideas with engineers who are already one or two levels more senior than you. Since those engineers are extremely picky with their time, the projects they *don't* have capacity for are still almost certainly big enough to get you promoted. They've already triaged out the small stuff; what remains in their backlog has legitimate scope by definition. The higher-level engineer's selectivity acts as a natural filter—if it's worth their attention but not their calendar, it's likely calibrated to the next level.

**Built-in mentorship and a future champion**

Taking on one of their shelved projects comes with structural advantages beyond the scope itself. Because the senior engineer already understands the problem, they're the most relevant person to review your designs and code, and they'll naturally mentor you through execution. It lifts the quality of your work and accelerates your own growth. And if the collaboration goes well, you walk away with an obvious champion—someone who can write consequential peer feedback when your promotion packet eventually goes to committee. You don't need to manufacture advocacy; it emerges from the working relationship.

**Concrete example: the tech lead's backlog item**

For his own Senior (L5) promotion at Meta, one of the contributing projects was literally just something sitting in his tech lead's backlog: addressing shortcomings in the video ads pipeline. Ryan thought it looked impactful and volunteered to take it. The tech lead connected him to the ads team to start the collaboration, then reviewed his design and code until it was clear Ryan had a full grasp of the work. That initial handoff grew into a larger project he eventually owned end-to-end. Ryan doesn't know exactly what went into his Senior promo packet, but he suspects the tech lead's positive feedback was in there, since they'd worked together so closely. "The collaboration grew into a larger project, which I took full ownership of, but you can see how it all started by taking on a stronger engineer's backlog project."

**Acknowledging the privilege of a strong team**

Ryan is candid that he got lucky: he was on a team with much stronger engineers, so project opportunities were "free-flowing" and he didn't need to plan this explicitly. The pattern was ambient, not intentional. His advice is aimed at engineers who aren't in that situation—if you're stuck trying to find next-level scope, explicitly go talk to more senior engineers. Their expertise will help generate good ideas, and their support will "make it much easier for you to finish the job too."

---

Ryan's **two-step promotion model** (find next-level scope, then solve) puts a name to what many engineers feel but can't articulate: the search is often harder than the execution. His **brainstorm-with-seniors pattern** operationalizes that search by piggybacking on the triage that higher-level engineers have already done—their backlog is effectively a pre-vetted scope inventory. The pattern also quietly encodes a **champion-through-collaboration** dynamic: advocacy isn't something you lobby for separately; it's a byproduct of doing real work side-by-side with someone whose judgment the committee already trusts.
