# ART: Ask, Refine, and Trust

**ArXiv:** [2311.07961](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07961)

## 🎯 Pitch

LLMs famously can't self-correct their own reasoning errors—often making things worse instead of better. This paper shows you can fix that by training a small model (e.g., 7B params) to decide when to refine and whether to trust the output, letting a 13B decision-maker boost a ChatGPT base model by nearly 4 accuracy points on math problems.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper proposes **ART: Ask, Refine, and Trust**, a reasoning-with-refinement strategy that improves LLM self-refinement on multi-step reasoning tasks by training smaller models to act as decision-makers for *when* to refine and *whether* to trust the result, rather than relying on the LLM's own self-judgment. Experiments on mathematical word problems (GSM8K) and question answering (StrategyQA) using LLaMA 70B, ChatGPT, and GPT-4 as base generators demonstrate that ART achieves a performance gain of up to 5 points over self-refinement baselines, with a LLaMA 7B or 13B Asker and Truster outperforming the much larger base LLM's own self-refinement capabilities (e.g., a 13B Asker lifts ChatGPT from 78.89 to 82.64 on GSM8K, matching the accuracy of a 3-sample self-consistency majority vote with only a single refinement pass). The framework establishes that asking decomposable subquestions before committing to refinement—and training a dedicated smaller model to rank initial versus refined outputs—yields reliable improvements from iterative self-correction, countering prior findings that LLMs cannot self-correct reasoning, provided the decision-maker is explicitly trained rather than prompted.

## 2. Context and Motivation

### The Core Problem: LLMs Cannot Reliably Self-Correct Reasoning

The fundamental problem this paper addresses is that Large Language Models, despite their impressive generative capabilities, **cannot reliably evaluate and correct their own reasoning errors**. When asked to self-refine—that is, to detect mistakes in their own output and produce a corrected version—LLMs frequently fail in ways that are worse than not refining at all. The paper opens by situating this failure within a specific and consequential domain: multi-step reasoning tasks, such as mathematical word problems and multi-hop question answering, where intermediate logical steps must be coherent and correct for the final answer to be right.

This is not merely a hypothetical concern. The paper presents empirical evidence (validating prior work, particularly Huang et al., 2023) that self-refinement on reasoning problems often has a **detrimental** effect on performance. In the majority of cases observed in their experiments on GSM8K and StrategyQA, the refinement step either fails to fix the error or, worse, introduces new errors into previously correct reasoning chains. The authors state this clearly in Section 1:

> "Through empirical observation on two multi-step reasoning datasets, we find that self-refinement does not reliably improve initial generations, validating the previous findings of Huang et al. (2023). In fact, in the majority of cases, self-refinement has a detrimental effect on performance."

This is the central tension that motivates the entire paper: refinement *ought* to help—the idea of iteratively improving a solution is intuitively appealing and is a cornerstone of human problem-solving—but in practice, off-the-shelf LLMs lack the metacognitive capability to judge when something is wrong and how to fix it. The gap between the promise of self-refinement and its observed failure is what ART sets out to bridge.

### Why This Problem Matters

The inability of LLMs to self-correct carries both practical and theoretical significance.

**Practical significance.** Reliable self-refinement would be transformative for deploying LLMs in high-stakes or autonomous settings. A model that can catch and fix its own errors reduces dependence on human oversight, enables self-improvement pipelines where models generate training data by refining their own outputs, and makes LLMs more robust in interactive settings (e.g., tutoring systems, coding assistants, or decision-support tools). If refinement cannot be trusted—if the model is as likely to break a correct solution as to repair an incorrect one—then these use cases become untenable.

**Theoretical significance.** The failure of self-refinement speaks to a deeper question about LLM capabilities: do these models possess anything resembling *metacognition* or *self-awareness* about their own reasoning processes? The fact that prompting an LLM to "check your work" or "revise your answer" is largely ineffective suggests that whatever representations LLMs build during generation, they do not automatically support reliable self-evaluation. This is not a superficial engineering problem but a fundamental limitation in how current models represent and access uncertainty about their own outputs.

**The cost dimension.** The paper also highlights a parallel motivation around computational cost. Fine-tuning large LLMs on a specific reasoning task can improve performance, but fine-tuning a 70B-parameter model is enormously expensive—the paper reports that fine-tuning LLaMA 70B requires 75 GPU-hours on a cluster of 4 × 8 A100s (Table 4). If a much smaller model could instead act as a refinement decision-maker for a frozen pre-trained LLM, achieving comparable or better accuracy at a fraction of the training cost, this would represent a significant practical advance. The authors frame this explicitly:

> "Smaller models can be trained on LLMs' data to improve their performance, which can serve as cost-effective alternatives to LLMs for the given task... This led us to explore the possibility of training a smaller model as a decision maker for refinement, which can consistently determine when to refine, while the larger model can subsequently perform the refinement process." (Section 1)

### Where Prior Approaches Fall Short

The paper identifies three categories of prior work, each with specific limitations that ART is designed to address.

#### 1. Prompting-based self-refinement

The most directly relevant prior work is the line of research on self-refinement through prompting: Madaan et al. (2023) proposed Self-Refine, where an LLM generates an initial output, provides its own feedback, and iteratively refines; Shridhar et al. (2023a) proposed SCREWS, a modular framework unifying sampling, re-sampling, and selection under different prompting strategies; Huang et al. (2023) demonstrated that such prompting-based approaches are largely ineffective for reasoning, with the blunt title "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet."

The limitation across all of these approaches is that **the same LLM is asked to play all three roles**: generator, critic, and selector. This is problematic because LLMs exhibit a well-documented bias toward their own outputs. As the authors note (citing Kadavath et al., 2022):

> "LLMs are often very bad at judging their own predictions and often prefer their own predictions." (Section 5)

When the same model evaluates work it just produced, it tends to confirm rather than correct. The self-critique generated through prompting lacks the epistemic independence needed for reliable error detection. The paper's ablation study in Section 5 directly demonstrates this: when the Asker model is trained to make only a binary "Yes/No" refinement decision without asking decomposable subquestions, it "always trusted the predictions and never decided to refine them." The degradation pattern is consistent—Figure 3 shows that always refining (100% resampling) yields worse accuracy than never refining (0% resampling).

#### 2. Chain-of-thought and question decomposition (without refinement)

The paper acknowledges that strategies using intermediate computation—chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) and subquestion decomposition (Min et al., 2019; Shridhar et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2023)—have proven effective for improving initial generation quality. These methods make the LLM's reasoning explicit, which increases the probability of arriving at a correct answer.

However, these are **one-pass strategies**: they improve the initial generation but provide no mechanism for iterative correction if the initial reasoning chain contains errors. The paper positions ART as building on these strategies—specifically, ART uses both CoT and Subquestion Decomposition as methods for the initial prediction (Section 3.1)—but then adds a trained refinement pipeline on top. The coarser prior work of Shridhar et al. (2023a) unified reasoning-with-refinement methods under a single prompting umbrella, but as noted, kept the same LLM as the judge.

#### 3. Distillation and verification (adjacent but distinct)

The paper also connects to work on distilling reasoning skills into smaller models (Shridhar et al., 2023b; Magister et al., 2023; Hsieh et al., 2023) and on using trained verifiers for mathematical reasoning (Cobbe et al., 2021). The distinction is important: distillation work typically trains smaller models to *generate* correct reasoning, while verification work trains models to *score* the correctness of candidate solutions. ART occupies a different niche: it trains smaller models to **make a binary decision about whether refinement is needed**, and to **rank initial versus refined outputs**, without requiring the smaller model to generate the reasoning itself. The authors explicitly differentiate their approach:

> "We do not study the standard setting of training to rank the quality of generations, but rather to decide if the refinement led to incorrect results and if it needs to be rolled back." (Section 2)

#### 4. The "always refine" trap

A critical failure mode that prior self-refinement work did not adequately address is that **indiscriminate refinement harms performance**. Huang et al. (2023) established this empirically, and ART's experiments confirm it: Figure 3 shows that the accuracy curve is non-monotonic with respect to the fraction of samples refined. The optimal operating point is roughly 30–35% refinement—refine too little and you miss opportunities to correct errors; refine too much and you degrade correct answers. Prior prompting-based approaches had no mechanism for targeting refinement toward the right subset of samples. They either refined everything or relied on the LLM's (unreliable) self-assessment of whether its answer was correct. ART's key innovation—training an explicit Asker model—directly addresses this targeting problem.

### How ART Positions Itself Relative to Existing Work

The paper frames ART as a departure from the predominant "single-model-does-everything" paradigm for self-refinement. The core architectural insight is **decoupling**: separate the roles of evaluator (Asker), refiner (the frozen LLM ψ), and selector (Truster) across different models, and train the evaluator and selector explicitly rather than relying on prompted self-assessment.

This decoupling addresses the fundamental limitation of prior work in three ways:

1. **Independence:** The Asker and Truster are separate models from the generator/refiner LLM. They are not evaluating their own output but someone else's, which breaks the circularity that makes self-evaluation unreliable. The paper even explores training the Asker on the base LLM's outputs versus its own outputs (Table 5), finding that training on the base LLM's distribution works best—precisely because it creates the evaluator-advisee separation.

2. **Task-specific training:** Rather than relying on the emergent (and evidently insufficient) self-critique capabilities that arise from pre-training, ART trains the Asker and Truster on task-specific data where correctness labels are available. The Asker learns to ask relevant decomposable subquestions and map them to the prediction to make a grounded refinement decision, rather than making an opaque holistic judgment.

3. **Cost-conscious design:** By keeping the base LLM frozen and training only the smaller decision-making models, ART preserves the LLM's general capabilities while adding refinement intelligence at a fraction of the fine-tuning cost. Table 4 quantifies this: training Asker (7B) + Truster (13B) costs roughly $5\times$ fewer GPU-hours than fine-tuning LLaMA 70B, while achieving comparable or better accuracy on GSM8K.

The paper's contribution is thus not a new refinement *mechanism*—the refinement itself is performed by the frozen LLM using subquestions as prompts—but a new **meta-cognitive architecture** for deciding when and whether to deploy refinement. This framing connects to a broader theme in the LLM literature: that specialized, trained components can outperform general-purpose prompting for well-scoped decision problems, even when the specialized component is much smaller than the general-purpose model it guides.

## 3. Technical Approach

### 3.1 Reader Orientation

The paper builds a **trained decision-making pipeline** that sits alongside a frozen Large Language Model and determines, for each problem, whether the LLM's initial answer needs refinement and whether the refined version should be trusted over the original. The core problem is that LLMs cannot reliably self-evaluate their own reasoning—when asked to check and correct their work, they often degrade correct answers or fail to fix incorrect ones. ART solves this by **decoupling the roles**: the LLM remains responsible only for generating and refining answers, while separate, much smaller trained models (the Asker and Truster) handle the metacognitive tasks of assessing correctness, deciding when refinement is warranted, and selecting the better output between initial and refined versions.

### 3.2 Big-Picture Architecture (Diagram in Words)

The ART system has four sequential stages, with three distinct models sharing the work:

1. **Base LLM ψ (generator and refiner)** — a frozen large language model (LLaMA 70B, ChatGPT, or GPT-4) that produces an initial prediction given a task query, and later performs refinement when triggered. It uses either Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting or Subquestion Decomposition (Decomp) for generating initial reasoning chains.

2. **Asker model (refinement gate)** — a smaller fine-tuned model (LLaMA 7B, 13B, or 70B) that receives the initial prediction and asks a series of decomposable subquestions about it. If the Asker determines that all subquestions are already answered in the initial prediction, it signals "no refinement needed" and the initial answer passes through. If any subquestions are not satisfactorily answered, the Asker signals "refine" and generates the subquestions to guide the refinement process.

3. **Refinement step (executed by base LLM ψ)** — when the Asker flags a sample for refinement, the base LLM ψ is called again, this time conditioned on the original input, the subquestions generated by the Asker, and (for StrategyQA) any available factual knowledge. It produces a revised answer y_ref.

4. **Truster model (output selector)** — a trained smaller model (LLaMA 13B) that receives both the initial prediction y and the refined output y_ref, scores each one, and selects the higher-scored candidate as the final answer. This prevents the system from blindly accepting refinements that may have introduced new errors.

Information flows strictly forward through these stages: Input x → Base LLM generates y → Asker evaluates y and possibly triggers refinement → (if triggered) Base LLM generates y_ref → Truster selects between y and y_ref → Final answer.

### 3.3 Roadmap for the Deep Dive

- **First**, the initial prediction stage—how the frozen LLM generates answers, the two reasoning strategies used (CoT and Subquestion Decomposition), and why this matters for downstream refinement.
- **Second**, the Asker model in full detail—how it is fine-tuned, how the refinement decision dataset is constructed, the crucial role of subquestions in the Asker's decision process, and why simply asking for a binary "Yes/No" refinement signal fails.
- **Third**, the Refine stage—what happens when the Asker triggers refinement, how the base LLM uses the Asker's subquestions to produce a corrected answer, and the special handling for StrategyQA with factual knowledge injection.
- **Fourth**, the Truster model—the pairwise ranking objective, how training pairs are constructed from correct and incorrect predictions, and the loss function that teaches a smaller model to prefer correct reasoning over incorrect reasoning.
- **Fifth**, the training data pipeline that supports both Asker and Truster, including how multiple predictions per training example are generated, labeled, and filtered.
- **Sixth**, design choices and ablations—why separate models rather than a single all-in-one pipeline, the cost comparison against full LLM fine-tuning, and the generalization of trained Asker/Truster across different base LLMs.

### 3.4 Detailed, Sentence-Based Technical Breakdown

This is primarily a **systems architecture paper** whose core idea is that reliability in self-refinement requires separating the evaluator and selector roles from the generator role, and training task-specific smaller models to perform those metacognitive functions rather than relying on prompted self-assessment.

---

#### Initial Prediction: How the Frozen LLM Generates Answers

The pipeline begins with a task query `x`—a mathematical word problem from GSM8K or a multi-hop question from StrategyQA—presented to a frozen large language model ψ. The model generates an initial prediction `y = ψ(x)`. The paper considers two strategies for structuring this generation, both designed to make the LLM's reasoning explicit and multi-step rather than producing an answer directly.

**Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting.** For pre-trained LLMs like LLaMA 70B, the query `x` is augmented with several examples of the task formatted as few-shot prompts, where each example shows a question followed by a step-by-step reasoning chain ending in a final answer. The model is then expected to produce a similar structured output: intermediate reasoning steps marked with calculations, followed by a final answer. This follows the standard CoT methodology from Wei et al. (2022). For fine-tuned models, the query is provided directly without few-shot examples—the model has already internalized the reasoning format from training.

**Subquestion Decomposition (Decomp).** As an alternative strategy, the model can be prompted to first decompose the problem into subquestions and then answer each subquestion sequentially, building toward the final answer. This approach follows Shridhar et al. (2022) and Zhou et al. (2023). The paper notes in Table 2 that Decomp performs better than CoT for some models (e.g., ChatGPT Turbo: 78.62 Decomp vs. 77.71 CoT) but worse for others (LLaMA 70B: 54.55 Decomp vs. 59.74 CoT), suggesting that the optimal generation strategy is model-dependent.

The critical output of this stage is not just the final answer but the **entire reasoning chain**—the step-by-step intermediate calculations and logical inferences that lead to the answer. This reasoning chain is what the Asker will later evaluate by asking subquestions. The Asker does not simply check whether the final numeric answer "looks right"; it verifies whether the stated intermediate steps answer specific decomposable questions about the problem. This is a deliberate design choice: the correctness of multi-step reasoning is determined by whether each intermediate claim is justified, not just by whether the final answer is plausible.

**Why explicit reasoning chains matter for ART.** If the initial prediction were just a final answer with no exposed reasoning, the Asker would have nothing to verify—it would have to guess whether the answer is correct based on surface features alone. By requiring the LLM to produce an explicit reasoning chain (through CoT or Decomp), ART gives the Asker a structured artifact to interrogate. The subquestions the Asker asks are mapped directly to claims made in the reasoning chain, making the evaluation grounded rather than opaque.

#### The Asker Model: Training a Smaller Model to Decide When Refinement Is Needed

The Asker is the central innovation of ART. It is a fine-tuned language model—smaller than the base LLM ψ—trained to answer the question: "Does this initial prediction need to be refined, and if so, what specific questions should the refinement address?"

**Why the Asker cannot be replaced by prompting.** The paper provides both empirical evidence and a conceptual argument against asking the same LLM that generated the prediction to also evaluate it. The empirical evidence comes from the ablation in Section 5: when the Asker is trained to make only a binary "Yes/No" refinement decision without asking decomposable subquestions, it "always trusted the predictions and never decided to refine them." This is attributed to the well-documented phenomenon that LLMs exhibit confirmation bias toward their own outputs—they are "often very bad at judging their own predictions and often prefer their own predictions" (Section 5, citing Kadavath et al., 2022). The conceptual argument is that effective evaluation requires **epistemic independence**: the evaluator should not be the same agent that produced the thing being evaluated, because the cognitive processes that generated the output are the same processes that would be used to check it, leading to circular confirmation.

By training a separate model—one that did not generate the initial prediction—ART breaks this circularity. The Asker sees the LLM's output as an external artifact to be verified, not as its own work to be defended.

**Step 1: Base fine-tuning of the Asker model.** Before the Asker can learn to make refinement decisions, it needs to understand the task domain and the expected structure of correct reasoning. The paper first fine-tunes a smaller LLaMA model (7B, 13B, or 70B) in CoT style on the training data for the target task. This fine-tuning step teaches the model the format of the reasoning task: given a problem, produce intermediate reasoning steps leading to a final answer. The fine-tuning data is the standard GSM8K or StrategyQA training set, formatted as problem → reasoning chain → answer. This produces what the paper calls the "fine-tuned model" baseline.

This step is important because the Asker's subsequent evaluation task requires it to recognize correct versus incorrect reasoning. A model that has been trained to produce correct reasoning on the task is better positioned to evaluate whether a given reasoning chain is correct—it has learned the patterns, constraints, and common pitfalls of the domain.

**Step 2: Generating the Asker training dataset.** The Asker is not trained on the original task data directly. Instead, a specialized dataset is constructed by using the frozen base LLM ψ to generate multiple predictions for each training example, then labeling each prediction as "Yes" (needs refinement) or "No" (does not need refinement) based on whether the prediction's final answer matches the ground truth. The construction process:

- For each example in the training set (7473 for GSM8K, 1832 for StrategyQA as shown in Table 1), the frozen LLM ψ generates `k = 5` predictions using different sampling temperatures: `temperature = {0, 0.3, 0.4, 0.7, 0.8}`. This temperature sweep produces diverse outputs—some correct, some incorrect—spanning the range of behaviors the LLM exhibits.
- Each of the 5 predictions per example is labeled "Yes" if the final answer is incorrect (refinement is needed) and "No" if the final answer is correct (refinement is not needed).
- For each prediction, the available subquestions from the dataset are prepended to the prediction text, followed by the "Yes"/"No" decision. For GSM8K, the dataset includes subquestions that correspond to the steps in a correct solution (e.g., "How many eggs does Claire eat in a week? How many eggs will Claire eat in 4 weeks? How many dozens of eggs will Claire eat in 4 weeks?" for a problem about egg consumption, as shown in Appendix Figure 7). For StrategyQA, the subquestions are the decomposed questions that break the multi-hop query into atomic factual checks (e.g., "What type of animal is a greyhound? Does #1 walk on two legs?").

The resulting training format for the Asker is: `[Subquestions] [Initial Prediction] → [Yes/No]`. The Asker learns to take the subquestions, map them to the claims made in the initial prediction, and decide whether all subquestions are satisfactorily answered. If all subquestions are answered and the reasoning is coherent, the Asker should output "No" (no refinement needed). If any subquestion is not addressed or the reasoning contradicts the subquestion's expected answer, the Asker should output "Yes" (refinement needed).

The size of this training dataset is substantial: Table 1 reports 35,000 samples for GSM8K Asker training and 9,000 for StrategyQA. These numbers are larger than the original training sets because each training example generates 5 predictions, and additional data may come from the fine-tuning stage or data augmentation (the paper does not fully specify the source of the exact count, but the scale is consistent with 5 predictions × ~7,000 training examples ≈ 35,000 for GSM8K).

**Step 3: Training the Asker for refinement decisions.** The fine-tuned model from Step 1 is further trained on this specialized dataset. The training objective is standard next-token prediction (language modeling loss) on the "Yes"/"No" decision given the subquestions and prediction as context. This is not a separate classification head—the Asker is a generative language model that produces the token "Yes" or "No" as its output.

Crucially, the Asker does not just output a binary decision. It first **generates the subquestions**, then evaluates whether those subquestions are answered in the prediction, then outputs the decision. The paper's example in Figure 4 illustrates this concretely: for a GSM8K problem about a customer loyalty card, the Asker generates "How much did the customer get in rewards? What was the coupons cost? How much did the customer pay on this shopping trip?" and then concludes "All questions are not answered" (triggering refinement). This means the Asker's output serves dual purposes: it is both the evaluation mechanism and, when refinement is triggered, the **source of subquestions that guide the refinement step**. The subquestions generated by the Asker become part of the prompt for the refinement call to the base LLM ψ.

**Why asking subquestions is essential.** The paper's ablation in Section 5 demonstrates that removing the subquestion generation step and training the Asker to produce only a binary "Yes"/"No" causes it to fail completely—it never triggers refinement. The authors hypothesize that asking decomposable subquestions forces the model to engage with the specific content of the prediction rather than making a holistic judgment. By checking each step against a specific question ("Did the prediction correctly compute the number of rewards? Did it account for the coupon?"), the Asker grounds its evaluation in verifiable claims rather than an opaque confidence estimate. This is consistent with findings from the chain-of-verification literature (Dhuliawala et al., 2023), where decomposing verification into atomic fact-checks improves hallucination detection.

The subquestions also serve a pedagogical function for the refinement step: they tell the base LLM ψ exactly what went wrong and what needs to be recomputed. Rather than asking the LLM to "fix the mistake" (which requires the LLM to identify the mistake itself—precisely what it struggles with), the Asker provides explicit prompts for each missing or incorrect step. The refinement call becomes: "Here is what you need to compute: [subquestions]. Now produce the corrected reasoning."

**Asker model sizes and scaling.** The paper trains Asker models at three scales: LLaMA 7B, 13B, and 70B. The results in Table 2 show consistent improvement with larger Asker models, confirming the intuitive expectation that better models make more informed refinement decisions. However, the key claim is that even the 7B Asker substantially outperforms the much larger base LLM's own self-refinement capabilities—the 7B Asker lifts LLaMA 70B's accuracy from 59.83 (self-refinement baseline) to 61.33 with only refinement, and to 61.94 with the Truster. This is a ~2-point gain from a model 10× smaller than the base LLM.

**Training the Asker on its own data versus the base LLM's data.** Table 5 addresses a subtle but important question: should the Asker be trained on predictions generated by the base LLM ψ, or on predictions generated by the Asker model itself? Intuitively, training on one's own distribution seems cleaner. The results are surprising and informative:

- For the LLaMA 70B base LLM, training the Asker on LLaMA 70B's predictions (70B data) consistently outperforms training on the Asker's own predictions (7B or 13B data). For example, Asker7B achieves 61.33 when trained on LLaMA 70B data versus 59.21 when trained on its own data. This makes sense because at test time, the Asker will be evaluating LLaMA 70B's outputs—training on the same distribution provides better calibration.
- For ChatGPT as the base LLM, the gap narrows substantially: Asker13B trained on 70B data achieves 82.18 versus 82.10 when trained on its own data. The authors attribute this to distribution mismatch in both cases—neither LLaMA 70B's nor the Asker's own outputs perfectly match ChatGPT's output distribution, so the advantage of matched training data diminishes.

This finding has practical implications: the best Asker training data comes from the same model whose outputs will be evaluated at test time. When that model's training data generation is expensive (e.g., generating 35,000 samples from ChatGPT or GPT-4 incurs API costs), using a cheaper model's outputs is a viable approximation, with some performance cost.

**Asker based on MetaMath.** Table 6 extends the analysis to a stronger base fine-tuning: MetaMath 7B and 13B (Yu et al., 2023), which are LLaMA models further trained on 250K+ mathematical reasoning samples with rejection sampling. When used as the Asker, MetaMath models outperform same-sized LLaMA Asker models (MetaMath 7B achieves 62.31 vs. LLaMA 7B's 61.33) and trigger refinement on fewer samples (35% vs. 48% for 7B, 27% vs. 36% for 13B). This suggests that stronger mathematical reasoning capability in the Asker leads to more confident and accurate refinement decisions—it can identify which samples truly need refinement rather than flagging borderline cases out of uncertainty.

#### The Refine Stage: How the Base LLM Produces a Corrected Answer

When the Asker outputs "Yes" (refinement is needed), the system enters the Refine stage. This stage uses the same frozen base LLM ψ that produced the initial prediction, but now prompted differently to produce a corrected answer.

**Refinement prompt structure.** The refinement call to ψ includes three components:

1. The original task query `x` (the math problem or question).
2. The subquestions generated by the Asker during its evaluation stage.
3. For StrategyQA only: additional factual knowledge (`facts`) that is available in the dataset, consisting of the true facts needed to answer the decomposed subquestions.

The formal notation is `y_ref = ψ(x; subq)` for GSM8K and `y_ref = ψ(x; subq; facts)` for StrategyQA. The semicolons indicate that `subq` and `facts` are provided as additional context/prompt content, not as separate model inputs.

**How the subquestions guide refinement.** The subquestions serve as explicit prompts for what the base LLM should compute or reason about in the refinement. Rather than saying "your previous answer was wrong, try again" (which gives the LLM no guidance about where the error was), the subquestions break down the problem into the specific computations that were missing or incorrect. The qualitative example in Figure 4 of the paper illustrates this clearly:

- **Initial prediction (incorrect):** "The customer spent $80 on their last shopping trip, so they earned 80/20 = 4 rewards. They used 2*4 = 8 rewards on this shopping trip. So the price of their purchase was $43 - $8 = $35."

- **Asker's subquestions:** "How much did the customer get in rewards? What was the coupons cost? How much did the customer pay on this shopping trip?"

- **Refinement (correct):** "How much did the customer get in rewards? The customer had 80/20 = $4 in rewards. What was the coupons cost? Their coupon was worth 4*2 = $8. How much did the customer pay on this shopping trip? Thus they paid 43 - 4 - 8 = $31 on this shopping trip."

The error in the initial prediction was that it subtracted the coupon value ($8) but forgot to also subtract the rewards themselves ($4). The subquestions forced the LLM to explicitly recompute each component, making the missing subtraction visible. The refinement corrects the error not because the LLM "realized its mistake" through introspection, but because the subquestions scaffolded the correct decomposition of the calculation.

**The role of factual knowledge in StrategyQA.** StrategyQA questions require multi-hop reasoning over factual knowledge (e.g., "Can a greyhound walk on two legs?" requires knowing that greyhounds are dogs and dogs walk on four legs). The paper follows Shridhar et al. (2023a) in providing ground-truth factual knowledge during refinement: `facts` contains the true facts associated with the question (e.g., "Greyhounds are dogs. Dogs walk on four legs."). This means the refinement step for StrategyQA is not purely about correcting the LLM's reasoning—it can also correct factual errors by injecting the correct facts. This is acknowledged as a limitation in Section 7: these facts "were available in the dataset, but in the real world can be extracted with the help of some tools or from some databases."

**Single-pass refinement.** An important architectural choice: ART performs exactly one refinement pass, not iterative refinement. If the Asker triggers refinement, the LLM produces `y_ref`, and then the Truster selects between `y` and `y_ref`. There is no loop where the refined output is sent back to the Asker for re-evaluation and potential further refinement. The paper does not explicitly justify this choice, but it is consistent with the computational budget considerations: each refinement call to the base LLM costs inference compute, and the Truster acts as a safety net to prevent regressions from the single refinement. Iterative refinement (where `y_ref` could be further refined) would risk compounding errors or verifier exploitation, problems documented in the test-time compute scaling literature.

**What the Refine stage does NOT do.** The Refine stage does not involve the Asker or Truster in any way—those models have already made their contribution (deciding to refine, generating subquestions) or will act later (selecting the final output). The Refine stage is purely the frozen base LLM ψ generating text conditioned on the subquestions. This separation is deliberate: the complexity of ART is in the *decision-making* around refinement, not in the refinement mechanism itself. The base LLM is treated as a black-box text generator that can produce improved answers when given the right prompts.

#### The Truster Model: Training a Smaller Model to Rank Initial vs. Refined Outputs

The final stage of ART addresses a critical failure mode: refinement can make answers worse. The paper reports that in approximately 38% of cases where the initial answer is correct, self-refinement degrades it to an incorrect answer (this figure is from the related SCREWS work by Shridhar et al., 2023a, which the authors cite in context). Even when the Asker correctly identifies that refinement is needed, the refinement produced by ψ may introduce new errors or fail to fix the original error. Blindly accepting the refined output whenever the Asker triggers refinement would therefore hurt performance on some fraction of samples.

The Truster solves this by learning to **compare two candidate outputs and select the better one**. Given the initial prediction `y` and the refined output `y_ref`, the Truster scores both and picks the higher-scored candidate. This is a form of rejection sampling: if the refinement is worse than the original, the Truster can "roll back" to the initial prediction, recovering the original accuracy on that sample.

**Why the Truster is necessary, not just a nice-to-have.** Table 2 provides clear evidence: for LLaMA 70B pre-trained with CoT (initial accuracy 59.74), adding the Asker alone improves accuracy to 61.33 (refinement only, trusting all refinements). Adding the Truster further improves this to 61.94—an additional 0.61 points. For the chat version, the Asker alone achieves 60.12, but with the Truster this becomes 61.18 (+1.06 points above Asker-only, +2.39 points above the self-refinement baseline of 58.79). For ChatGPT Turbo, the Asker alone achieves 80.89 (refinement with Asker7B), while adding the Truster reaches 81.14. The pattern is consistent across models: the Truster extracts additional gains by filtering out harmful refinements.

The comparison with self-selection baselines (where the same LLM chooses between `y` and `y_ref` via prompting) is stark: Figure 2 shows that the trained Truster selects about 50% more rollbacks than the self-selection version, indicating that the LLM's own "trust" judgment is biased toward accepting its refinements even when they are worse. The trained Truster, being a separate model, does not share this bias.

**Training data construction for the Truster.** The Truster is trained on a pairwise ranking dataset constructed from the same pool of predictions used for Asker training. The construction process:

1. From the 5 predictions generated per training example (using the temperature sweep), identify the subset of examples that have **both a correct and an incorrect prediction**. These are the informative cases—if all predictions are correct or all are incorrect, there is no ranking signal.
2. Construct **preferred vs. non-preferred pairs**: a correct prediction (preferred) paired with an incorrect prediction (non-preferred). The Truster learns to assign a higher score to the preferred candidate.
3. The training data size is reported in Table 1: 15,000 pairs for GSM8K and 2,300 for StrategyQA. These are notably smaller than the Asker training sets (35,000 and 9,000 respectively), because they are filtered to only examples with both correct and incorrect predictions—a stricter condition.

A subtle point noted in the paper: in approximately 80% of cases, the final answer of the refinement `y_ref` and the initial prediction `y` are the same. This means that for the majority of samples, the Truster is not distinguishing between different answers but between different reasoning chains that arrive at the same answer. The Truster must learn to identify which reasoning chain is more likely to be correct, even when the final answers match. This is a harder task than simple answer matching and requires the model to evaluate the quality of intermediate steps.

**The pairwise ranking loss.** The Truster is trained with a standard pairwise ranking objective (Equation 1):

$$L_{\theta} = -\mathbb{E}_{x, y_j, y_k \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log\left( \sigma(r_{\theta}(x, y_j) - r_{\theta}(x, y_k)) \right) \right]$$

where `$r_{\theta}(x, y)$` is the scalar score assigned by the Truster model (parameterized by `$\theta$`) to candidate answer `$y$` given input `$x$`, `$y_j$` is the preferred candidate (correct prediction), `$y_k$` is the non-preferred candidate (incorrect prediction), and `$\sigma$` is the logistic sigmoid function.

**What it computes:** This is the standard Bradley-Terry preference model loss. For each training pair, the Truster produces a scalar score for both candidates. The difference in scores `$r_{\theta}(x, y_j) - r_{\theta}(x, y_k)$` is passed through the sigmoid to produce a probability that the preferred candidate is scored higher. The log of this probability is maximized—equivalently, the negative log-likelihood is minimized. The expectation is over the training dataset `$\mathcal{D}$` of preference pairs.

**Why this form:** The Bradley-Terry model is the standard statistical model for pairwise preferences. It assumes that each item has a latent "quality" score, and the probability of preferring item A over item B is `$\sigma(\text{score}_A - \text{score}_B)$`. Training with this loss teaches the model to assign higher scores to correct reasoning chains than to incorrect ones. The expectation over pairs ensures the model sees diverse errors and correct patterns. An alternative—training a binary classifier to predict correctness directly—would lose the comparative signal: the Truster needs to know not just "is this correct?" but "which of these two is more likely to be correct?" because at inference time it always faces a choice between two candidates.

**Inference with the Truster.** At test time, when the Asker has triggered refinement (producing `y_ref`), both the initial prediction `y` and the refined output `y_ref` are passed to the Truster. The Truster computes `r_θ(x, y)` and `r_θ(x, y_ref)`. The candidate with the higher score is selected as the final output. If the Asker did not trigger refinement (output "No"), the Truster is bypassed entirely and the initial prediction `y` is returned directly.

**Truster model architecture.** The paper uses LLaMA 13B as the Truster for all experiments. This is a deliberate choice: while the Asker benefits from scaling (7B → 13B → 70B), the Truster's task—comparing two fully-formed reasoning chains and picking the better one—may not benefit as much from additional capacity beyond a certain point. The paper does not ablate Truster model size, so this remains a conjecture. The Truster is fine-tuned on the pairwise ranking data using the loss above.

**StrategyQA results with the Truster.** Table 3 shows that for StrategyQA, the Truster does not provide substantial gains over the Asker-only refinement: Asker13B achieves 78.38 without Truster and 78.44 with Truster, a negligible 0.06-point improvement. The authors attribute this to the nature of fact-based datasets:

> "This shows that it is difficult to train a Truster on fact-based datasets, as it is hard to rank two pieces of factual information without knowing the true facts."

In other words, the Truster cannot reliably judge whether a factual claim is correct without access to the ground-truth facts, which are not provided to the Truster during inference (only to the refinement step for StrategyQA). For mathematical reasoning, the Truster can evaluate the logical coherence of the computation chain—arithmetic errors and missing steps leave detectable traces in the reasoning text. For factual QA, the error might be an incorrect premise that looks superficially plausible, with no internal inconsistency to flag.

#### Training Data Pipeline: End-to-End Data Generation for Asker and Truster

The ART system depends on two specialized training datasets, both derived from the same pool of LLM-generated predictions on the training set. This section traces the complete data pipeline from raw training examples to the final Asker and Truster training sets.

**Step 1: Generate diverse predictions from the base LLM.** For each example in the training set (7,473 for GSM8K, 1,832 for StrategyQA—the latter being 80% of the 2,290 total, with 20% held out as test), the frozen base LLM ψ generates `k = 5` predictions. The sampling uses five different temperature settings: 0, 0.3, 0.4, 0.7, and 0.8. Temperature 0 (greedy decoding) produces the most likely prediction, while higher temperatures introduce randomness that surfaces alternative reasoning paths—some correct, some incorrect. This diversity is essential: the Asker needs to see both correct and incorrect predictions to learn the distinction, and the Truster needs pairs of correct and incorrect predictions to learn ranking.

The paper does not specify the top-p setting for these diverse generations, only that testing uses greedy decoding (temperature = 0, top-p = 1). The diverse training samples are generated once and fixed; they are not regenerated during training.

**Step 2: Label each prediction with correctness.** Each of the 5 predictions per example is compared to the ground-truth answer. For GSM8K, this is a numeric match; for StrategyQA, it is a True/False match. The label is binary: correct (answer matches ground truth) or incorrect (answer does not match). This produces, for each training example, a set of labeled predictions with a mix of correct and incorrect outputs.

**Step 3: Construct the Asker training dataset.** For each prediction (regardless of whether it is correct or incorrect), the training example's subquestions are prepended. The full input to the Asker model during training is: `[Subquestions] [Prediction text]` → target: `[Yes/No]`, where "Yes" means the prediction is incorrect and needs refinement, and "No" means it is correct and does not need refinement. The resulting dataset sizes (Table 1) are 35,000 for GSM8K and 9,000 for StrategyQA. These numbers imply that not all 5 predictions per example are used, or that additional data augmentation is performed; the paper does not fully detail the discrepancy between 5 × 7,473 = 37,365 and the reported 35,000 (possibly some predictions are filtered for quality, or the dataset includes only a subset).

**Step 4: Construct the Truster training dataset.** From the same pool of labeled predictions, the subset of training examples that have **at least one correct and at least one incorrect prediction** is identified. For each such example, correct-incorrect pairs are formed: each correct prediction is paired with each incorrect prediction. The paper reports 15,000 pairs for GSM8K and 2,300 for StrategyQA (Table 1). The much smaller StrategyQA dataset reflects both the smaller training set (1,832 vs. 7,473) and the lower diversity of outputs—StrategyQA answers are binary (True/False), so there are fewer distinct reasoning chains to pair.

**Step 5: Model training.** The Asker is trained by further fine-tuning the already fine-tuned model (from Step 1 of the Asker section) on the Asker dataset using standard next-token prediction loss. The Truster is trained by fine-tuning LLaMA 13B on the pairwise ranking loss (Equation 1) using the Truster dataset. Both training procedures use the training data in a standard supervised fine-tuning setup; the paper does not report specific hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, epochs) for these training runs, which is a notable omission for reproducibility.

#### Key Design Choices and Their Justifications

**Choice 1: Separate Asker and Truster models rather than a single decision-maker.** The paper could have trained a single model to both decide whether to refine and select the final output. The separation into two models reflects the different nature of the two decisions: the Asker evaluates whether the initial prediction is correct (an absolute judgment), while the Truster compares two candidates (a relative judgment). The Asker's output also serves a dual purpose—the subquestions it generates become the prompts for refinement—which a combined model would need to handle differently. Empirically, the paper shows that the Truster adds value beyond the Asker alone, improving accuracy by 0.5–2 points in most configurations (Table 2), justifying the separation.

**Choice 2: Frozen base LLM with trained smaller models, rather than fine-tuning the base LLM.** This is the cost-effectiveness argument quantified in Table 4. Fine-tuning LLaMA 70B costs 75 GPU-hours (requiring 4 clusters of 8 × A100 80GB GPUs). Training the Asker (7B) costs 1 GPU-hour; training the Truster (13B) costs 4 GPU-hours. The combined cost of 5 GPU-hours is 15× cheaper than fine-tuning the 70B model, while achieving comparable or better accuracy: the paper reports that fine-tuned LLaMA 70B achieves 63.2% on GSM8K (citing Yuan et al., 2023), while ART with Asker13B + Truster + pre-trained LLaMA 70B achieves 63.85%. The cost savings come from two sources: (1) training smaller models, and (2) keeping the base LLM frozen, avoiding the need to backpropagate through 70B parameters.

**Choice 3: Using subquestions rather than free-form critique for evaluation.** The ablation in Section 5 is definitive: removing subquestions from the Asker training causes it to never trigger refinement—it always trusts the predictions. Subquestions force the Asker to ground its evaluation in decomposable, verifiable checks rather than making an opaque holistic judgment. This connects to a broader principle in AI evaluation: structured rubrics produce more reliable assessments than holistic ratings. By mapping each subquestion to a specific claim in the reasoning chain, the Asker performs something analogous to a checklist verification, which is more robust than asking "is this reasoning correct?"

**Choice 4: Single-pass refinement rather than iterative refinement.** The paper does not iterate: if refinement is triggered and produces `y_ref`, the system does not send `y_ref` back to the Asker for re-evaluation. This is a conservative design choice that avoids compounding errors—each refinement pass is an opportunity for the LLM to introduce new mistakes, and multiple passes could drift far from the initial prediction. The Truster provides a safety net by allowing rollback to the original if the single refinement is worse, but it does not attempt to refine the refinement. This also keeps the inference cost bounded: at most one extra LLM call per sample.

**Choice 5: Training the Asker on the base LLM's predictions, not its own.** Table 5 shows that for LLaMA 70B as the base model, training the Asker on LLaMA 70B's predictions is better than training on the Asker's own predictions (61.33 vs. 59.21 for 7B). This is the expected result—at test time, the Asker evaluates LLaMA 70B's outputs, so training on the same distribution provides in-distribution evaluation. The fact that Asker trained on its own data still outperforms the self-refinement baseline (59.21 vs. 59.07 for LLaMA 70B self-refinement) suggests that even an out-of-distribution trained evaluator is better than self-evaluation, reinforcing the value of epistemic independence.

**Choice 6: All-in-one training versus modular pipeline.** Figure 5 compares training a single model to perform the entire ART pipeline (ask subquestions, decide to refine, produce refinement, and select output) in one sequence against the modular approach. The all-in-one approach underperforms fine-tuning alone for both LLaMA 13B and 70B. The authors interpret this as evidence that "generating the entire sequence is a more challenging task for the LLM than individual components." This is consistent with the general finding that LLMs perform better on well-scoped subtasks than on complex composite tasks requiring multiple distinct cognitive operations in sequence.

**Choice 7: Greedy decoding at test time.** All experiments use greedy decoding (temperature = 0, top-p = 1) for the Asker, Truster, and base LLM during testing. This removes sampling variance from the evaluation and ensures deterministic outputs. The temperature sweep is used only during training data generation to create diverse predictions for the Asker and Truster training sets. This is a standard practice: diverse training data improves robustness, but deterministic test-time decoding enables clean comparisons.

## 4. Key Insights and Innovations

### Innovation 1: The Decoupling Principle — Metacognition Cannot Be Bootstrapped from Generation Alone

The paper's most fundamental contribution is not the specific Asker-Truster architecture but the **diagnostic insight that drives it**: a model's ability to generate coherent reasoning is not coupled to its ability to evaluate that reasoning, and treating them as a unified capability leads to systematic failure. Prior work on self-refinement—Madaan et al. (2023), Shridhar et al. (2023a), Huang et al. (2023)—operated under an implicit assumption (or at least an experimental practice) that the same model could play all roles: generator, critic, and selector. The failure mode was interpreted by the field as "LLMs cannot self-correct reasoning," which frames the limitation as a ceiling on what refinement can achieve.

ART reframes the problem entirely. The issue is not that LLMs cannot be corrected—the refinement step in ART demonstrably produces better answers when guided by appropriate subquestions (Figure 4). The issue is that LLMs **cannot reliably initiate or gate their own correction**. The Asker does not improve the refinement mechanism itself (the base LLM ψ is frozen and unchanged); it improves the *decision about whether to invoke that mechanism*. This is a metacognitive capacity—knowing when you're wrong—that is distinct from the cognitive capacity of generating or fixing reasoning.

The evidence for this decoupling is stark and comes from the ablation in Section 5: when the Asker is trained to make binary "Yes/No" refinement decisions without generating decomposable subquestions, it fails catastrophically—it "always trusted the predictions and never decided to refine them." This is not a subtle degradation; it is complete collapse. The same model architecture, trained on the same data, with the only difference being the presence or absence of structured questioning in its output, swings from zero refinements triggered to a ~30–35% refinement rate that improves overall accuracy. This tells us something deep: the LLM's difficulty with self-evaluation is not about lacking the *knowledge* to detect errors but about lacking the *process* that surfaces that knowledge. The subquestions are not merely an input format—they are a cognitive scaffold that transforms the evaluation task from an opaque holistic judgment (which LLMs are bad at, per Kadavath et al., 2022) into a structured checklist verification (which they can learn).

This is a conceptual shift from "LLMs can't self-correct" to "LLMs can self-correct when evaluation is decomposed and executed by a model that didn't generate the original output." The field had conflated two separate capabilities—generation quality and self-evaluation accuracy—and attributed the failure of the latter to a limitation in the former. ART demonstrates they are separable by showing that a 7B-parameter model, which cannot generate better answers than a 70B model, can nonetheless decide when the 70B model should refine its answers better than the 70B model can decide for itself (Table 2: Asker7B achieves 61.33% vs. Self-refinement at 59.83% for pre-trained LLaMA 70B). This is a fundamental finding about model architecture and cognitive decomposition, not an incremental accuracy gain.

### Innovation 2: Refinement as a Targeted Intervention, Not a Universal Strategy

The paper empirically establishes and then exploits a critical fact about refinement: it is harmful when applied indiscriminately, and the optimal operating point is to refine only a minority of samples. This finding itself is not new—Huang et al. (2023) demonstrated that self-refinement hurts performance on reasoning tasks—but ART is the first system to **operationalize this insight through a trained, task-specific gating mechanism**. Prior work either accepted the failure as a ceiling (Huang et al.) or attempted to fix it by prompting the same LLM to self-select (Shridhar et al., 2023a; Madaan et al., 2023), which the paper shows is unreliable because LLMs "often prefer their own predictions" (Section 5).

The innovation is in reframing refinement from a *strategy you apply* to a *decision you make per sample*. This transforms the problem from "how do we make refinement work better on average?" to "how do we identify the subset of samples where refinement will help?" The paper's Figure 3 provides the key diagnostic: accuracy as a function of the percentage of samples refined is non-monotonic, with a clear peak at ~30–35% for both ChatGPT and LLaMA 70B on GSM8K. Refining 100% of samples is worse than refining 0%, because the degradation inflicted on initially-correct samples outweighs the corrections on initially-incorrect ones.

This is conceptually analogous to the precision-recall tradeoff in binary classification: you want to trigger refinement (positive class) on samples where it will help, and suppress it where it will harm. The Asker is thus best understood as a **specialized classifier for the "will-refinement-help?" decision**, trained on a labeled dataset where the ground truth is known (the correctness of the initial prediction). The fact that a 7B-parameter classifier achieves a better operating point on this decision than the 70B-parameter generator acting as its own classifier is the paper's central empirical claim.

The significance extends beyond ART. This finding suggests that much of the prior work on self-refinement was evaluated suboptimally—by averaging over all samples, researchers obscured the fact that refinement might be highly effective on a minority subset while being harmful on the majority. Future work on self-correction should report per-sample refinement success rates and the selectivity of the gating mechanism, not just aggregate accuracy. ART provides both the diagnostic framework and the architectural template for building selective refinement systems.

### Innovation 3: The Cost-Asymmetry Argument for Asymmetric Model Architectures

The paper makes a pragmatic but intellectually significant argument: when you have a large, expensive, general-purpose model and a specific, well-scoped decision problem, it is more cost-effective to train a small specialized model to make the decision than to fine-tune the large model. This is not a new idea in machine learning broadly—knowledge distillation, model cascades, and hybrid systems have long exploited this asymmetry—but ART provides a crisp demonstration in the specific context of LLM refinement, with quantified cost comparisons that make the tradeoff concrete.

Table 4 is the key exhibit: fine-tuning LLaMA 70B costs 75 GPU-hours; training the Asker (7B) + Truster (13B) costs 1 + 4 = 5 GPU-hours, a 15× reduction. And yet the resulting system achieves comparable or better accuracy (63.85% for ART with Asker13B + pre-trained 70B vs. 63.2% for fine-tuned 70B, per Yuan et al., 2023). The asymmetry arises because the refinement decision is a **lower-dimensional, more structured problem** than the generation task itself. The Asker does not need to produce novel mathematical reasoning; it needs to map subquestions to claims in an existing reasoning chain and detect mismatches. This is an easier problem, and it admits a smaller model solution.

The deeper implication is about the **division of cognitive labor in AI systems**. Rather than pursuing monolithic models that excel at everything—generation, evaluation, metacognition—ART argues implicitly for heterogeneous systems where different components handle different cognitive functions at different scales. The 70B model is the "reasoning engine" (generation and refinement); the 7B or 13B models are the "executive function" (monitoring and selection). This mirrors the architecture of human cognition, where metacognitive monitoring is not just a scaled-up version of first-order reasoning but involves distinct processes. ART is one of the first systems in the LLM literature to instantiate this division explicitly with separate trained models rather than different prompts to the same model.

The paper further strengthens this argument by showing that trained Askers and Trusters generalize across base LLMs without retraining (Section 6, though results are not shown in detail in the provided content, the claim is made that ART can "work seamlessly across a wide range of LLMs... without requiring additional modifications"). If true, this means the small-model investment is amortized across multiple large-model deployments—train the decision-maker once, plug in different generators—making the cost asymmetry even more pronounced.

### Innovation 4: Subquestions as Both Evaluation Mechanism and Refinement Scaffold

The dual role of subquestions in ART represents a subtle but important design insight: the same structured decomposition that enables reliable evaluation also provides the optimal prompt for correction. This is an elegant unification—rather than having separate mechanisms for "detect the error" and "guide the fix," ART uses the same subquestions for both, creating a tight coupling between diagnosis and treatment that avoids the common failure mode where the refinement step misunderstands or ignores the critique.

Prior work on refinement typically separated feedback generation from refinement execution. In Madaan et al. (2023), the model generates natural language feedback ("Your answer is wrong because...") and then separately generates a refined answer. The feedback is often vague, unfocused, or misinterpreted by the refinement step. ART collapses this gap: the Asker's output is "How much did the customer get in rewards? What was the coupons cost? How much did the customer pay on this shopping trip? All questions are not answered." This output functions simultaneously as the justification for triggering refinement (verification: the prediction doesn't answer these questions) and the explicit instruction set for the refinement step (task: now answer these questions). The refinement is thus not a free-form "fix the mistake" but a structured "here are the missing computations, produce them."

The qualitative example in Figure 4 demonstrates why this matters. The initial prediction's error was omitting the $4 rewards subtraction. A generic feedback like "you forgot to subtract the rewards" would tell the model *what* is wrong but not *how* to fix it—the model might still miscompute the subtraction. By contrast, the subquestions force a complete recomputation from first principles: "How much did the customer get in rewards?" → $4; "What was the coupons cost?" → $8; "How much did the customer pay?" → $43 − $4 − $8 = $31. The error is corrected not by patching the original reasoning but by regenerating the entire chain under explicit guidance.

This insight has implications beyond ART. It suggests that for reasoning tasks where errors arise from missed or incorrectly ordered steps (rather than from fundamentally wrong conceptual understanding), the optimal form of feedback is not a critique but a **decomposition of the correct reasoning path into atomic questions**. This connects to the broader literature on scaffolding in education, where Socratic questioning guides learners to discover correct answers rather than telling them what they did wrong. ART operationalizes this pedagogical principle in a computational pipeline.

## 5. Experimental Analysis

### Evaluation Methodology

- **Dataset.** The paper tests ART on two multi-step reasoning benchmarks: GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), a grade-school math word problem dataset with 7,473 training examples and 1,319 test examples, each requiring 2–8 arithmetic steps; and StrategyQA (Geva et al., 2021), an open-domain question-answering benchmark requiring implicit multi-hop reasoning, split into 1,832 training examples (first 80%) and 458 test examples (remaining 20%), following the protocol established by Magister et al. (2023) and Shridhar et al. (2023a). Both datasets include decomposable subquestions that break each problem into atomic reasoning steps, which are essential for training and inference in ART.

- **Base model(s).** The paper uses multiple frozen LLMs as generators and refiners: LLaMA 70B in both pre-trained and chat-tuned variants (Touvron et al., 2023), ChatGPT (specifically `gpt-3.5-turbo` and `gpt-3.5-turbo-instruct` variants; Brown et al., 2020), and GPT-4 (`gpt-4`; OpenAI, 2023). These models are chosen for their popularity, state-of-the-art performance, and the availability of both open-weight (LLaMA) and API-based (ChatGPT, GPT-4) variants spanning different capability levels for assessing cross-model generalization. All base LLMs remain frozen throughout—they are never fine-tuned in the ART pipeline, only used for inference.

- **Metrics.** The primary metric is **maj1@1 accuracy**—the fraction of test examples where the selected final answer exactly matches the ground truth, using greedy decoding (temperature = 0, top-p = 1) so that each stage produces exactly one deterministic output (no majority voting over multiple samples). For GSM8K, correctness is determined by numeric match; for StrategyQA, it is a binary True/False match. The "maj1@1" designation emphasizes that this is single-pass accuracy without self-consistency sampling, which is important because one of the paper's headline claims is that ART with a single refinement pass achieves accuracy comparable to a 3-sample self-consistency majority vote (maj1@3) from prior work.

- **Baselines.** The paper compares against multiple established refinement methods, distinguished by whether they use the same LLM for all roles (Self) or trained separate models (ART):
  - **Self-Refine baselines from prior work:** Results from Madaan et al. (2023, denoted with superscript SR in Table 2), Shridhar et al. (2023a, denoted with superscript S), and Huang et al. (2023, denoted with superscript !C) are reproduced as baselines. These all use prompting-based self-evaluation and self-refinement with the same LLM.
  - **Self-refinement (Self):** The paper's own implementation of prompting-based self-refinement, where the same base LLM that generated the initial prediction also evaluates it and produces a refinement when prompted, and optionally selects between the two outputs. This is the direct comparison point for ART's Asker and Truster components.
  - **Self-selection (Self Trust):** The base LLM is prompted to choose between its initial prediction and refinement, as formalized in Shridhar et al. (2023a). This is the direct comparison for the Truster.
  - **Most Recent:** A simple baseline that always selects the refined output, never rolling back to the initial prediction. This tests whether refinement alone (without a selection mechanism) improves over the initial prediction.
  - **Initial Prediction (no refinement):** The accuracy of the frozen base LLM with CoT or Decomp prompting, without any refinement step. This is the floor against which all refinement methods must improve.

- **Generation budget / compute accounting.** The paper measures inference compute implicitly through the number of LLM calls: the initial prediction is one call, refinement is a second call (if triggered), and the Asker and Truster each require one call when used. There is no explicit FLOPs accounting, but the paper frames the cost comparison around **which model performs each call**—a 7B or 13B Asker call is dramatically cheaper than a 70B LLM call, and the refinement decision made by the smaller model replaces what would otherwise require the larger model to self-evaluate. Table 4 reports training cost in GPU-hours for the decision-making models versus fine-tuning the base LLM, using FLOPs estimates: Asker 7B training = 1 GPU-hour (1.5 × 10^17 FLOPs), Truster 13B training = 4 GPU-hours (3 × 10^17 FLOPs), fine-tuning LLaMA 70B = 75 GPU-hours (1.5 × 10^18 FLOPs). All training used clusters of 8 × A100 80GB GPUs, except LLaMA 70B fine-tuning which required 4 clusters of 8 × A100s.

- **Cross-validation / statistical protocol.** The paper does not report cross-validation or statistical significance testing. The Asker and Truster are trained on the training split once and evaluated on the fixed test split. The test sets are the standard public splits for GSM8K (1,319 examples) and StrategyQA (20% of 2,290 = 458 examples). Multiple predictions per training example (k = 5) are generated using a temperature sweep (0, 0.3, 0.4, 0.7, 0.8) to create the Asker and Truster training datasets, but at test time all models use greedy decoding, so there is no sampling variance to report. The absence of error bars or confidence intervals is a limitation for assessing the reliability of small differences (e.g., the 0.5–1 point gains from the Truster on top of the Asker).

### Main Quantitative Results

#### Self-Refinement Fails as a Universal Strategy, Motivating Selective Refinement

Table 2 provides the comprehensive comparison that establishes the paper's motivating finding: self-refinement (Self) applied uniformly does not reliably improve over the initial prediction, and in several configurations it degrades performance. The pattern is consistent across base models:

- **LLaMA 70B pre-trained with CoT:** Initial prediction accuracy is 59.74. Self-refinement without subquestions slightly degrades this to 59.07 (−0.67 points). Self-refinement with subquestions and self-trust reaches 60.43 (+0.69 points)—a modest gain that is far smaller than what ART achieves (+4.50 points to 64.24 with Asker70B + Truster).

- **LLaMA 70B chat with CoT:** Initial prediction is 58.90. Self-refinement with subquestions and self-trust reaches 59.55 (+0.65 points). ART with Asker70B + Truster reaches 64.40 (+5.50 points).

- **ChatGPT Turbo with CoT:** Initial prediction is 77.71. Self-refinement with subquestions and self-trust reaches 78.89 (+1.18 points). ART with Asker13B + Truster reaches 82.64 (+4.93 points).

- **ChatGPT Turbo with Decomp:** Initial prediction is 78.62. Self-refinement with subquestions and self-trust reaches 79.22 (+0.60 points). This is the strongest self-refinement result but still substantially below ART's 82.64 with CoT-based initial prediction.

The critical pattern is that self-refinement gains are consistently marginal (0.6–1.2 points) and sometimes negative (LLaMA 70B pre-trained CoT without subquestions drops from 59.74 to 59.07), while ART gains are 3–5 points above the initial prediction. This establishes that the bottleneck in prior self-refinement work was not the refinement mechanism itself but the **unreliable trigger and trust decisions** made by the same LLM.

#### The Asker Model Substantially Outperforms Self-Refinement, Even at Much Smaller Scale

The Asker-only results (without Truster) in Table 2 demonstrate that training a separate smaller model to gate refinement yields gains that exceed the base LLM's own self-refinement. Key comparisons on GSM8K:

- **LLaMA 70B pre-trained (initial: 59.74):** Self-refinement best = 60.43 (Self with subquestions + Self trust). Asker7B achieves 61.33 (+0.90 over Self best, +1.59 over initial). Asker13B achieves 62.74 (+2.31 over Self best). Asker70B achieves 63.60 (+3.17 over Self best). The 7B Asker—ten times smaller than the base LLM—outperforms the 70B model's own self-refinement judgment.

- **LLaMA 70B chat (initial: 58.90):** Self-refinement best = 59.55. Asker7B achieves 60.12 (+0.57 over Self best). Asker13B achieves 63.00 (+3.45 over Self best). Asker70B achieves 63.80 (+4.25 over Self best).

- **ChatGPT Turbo (initial: 77.71):** Self-refinement best = 78.89. Asker7B achieves 80.89 (+2.00 over Self best). Asker13B achieves 82.18 (+3.29 over Self best). Notably, this 82.18 is described in the paper as "similar to the self-consistency score of 3 samples (maj1@3)" (Huang et al., 2023).

- **GPT-4 (initial: 91.88):** Self-refinement from prior work (Shridhar et al., 2023a, Self^S) achieves 93.10. Asker7B achieves 93.25 (+0.15 over prior best). Asker13B achieves 93.72 (+0.62 over prior best). The gains are smaller in absolute terms due to the high baseline, but the direction is consistent, and both Asker models surpass the prior state-of-the-art for GPT-4 refinement.

- **GPT-3.5-turbo-instruct (initial: 71.26):** Self-refinement best = 72.85. Asker7B achieves 76.19 (+3.34). Asker13B achieves 78.46 (+5.61). This is the largest absolute gain from the Asker alone, suggesting that models with lower initial accuracy benefit more from selective refinement gating.

**StrategyQA results (Table 3)** follow a similar pattern:

- **LLaMA 70B pre-trained (initial: 74.45):** Self-refinement best = 75.74. Asker7B achieves 76.22 (+0.48). Asker13B achieves 78.38 (+2.64).

- **ChatGPT Turbo (initial: 73.58):** Self-refinement best = 74.89. Asker7B achieves 73.84 (−1.05 vs. Self best—the one case where Asker underperforms Self, though still above the initial prediction). Asker13B achieves 75.76 (+0.87 over Self best, +2.18 over initial).

The consistent scaling trend—Asker13B > Asker7B in every configuration across both datasets—confirms that better Asker models make more informed refinement decisions. The paper quantifies this in Table 6 with sampling percentages: Asker7B triggers refinement on 48% of GSM8K samples, Asker13B on 36%, and MetaMath13B on only 27%. Higher-capacity Askers are more selective, triggering refinement on fewer but more appropriate samples.

#### The Truster Adds Incremental but Consistent Gains

Table 2 shows that adding the Truster on top of the Asker yields additional accuracy improvements in nearly every configuration on GSM8K:

- **LLaMA 70B pre-trained:** Asker7B alone = 61.33; +Truster = 61.94 (+0.61). Asker13B alone = 62.74; +Truster = 63.85 (+1.11). Asker70B alone = 63.60; +Truster = 64.24 (+0.64).

- **LLaMA 70B chat:** Asker7B alone = 60.12; +Truster = 61.18 (+1.06). Asker13B alone = 63.00; +Truster = 63.30 (+0.30). Asker70B alone = 63.80; +Truster = 64.40 (+0.60).

- **ChatGPT Turbo:** Asker7B alone = 80.89; +Truster = 81.14 (+0.25). Asker13B alone = 82.18; +Truster = 82.64 (+0.46).

- **GPT-4:** Asker7B alone = 93.25; +Truster = 93.45 (+0.20). Asker13B alone = 93.72; +Truster = 94.08 (+0.36).

- **GPT-3.5-turbo-instruct:** Asker7B alone = 76.19; +Truster = 76.34 (+0.15). Asker13B alone = 78.46; +Truster = 79.86 (+1.40).

The Truster gains are smaller than the Asker gains (typically 0.2–1.4 points vs. 2–5 points from the Asker), but they are directionally consistent across all configurations—the Truster never harms performance relative to the Asker-only setting. Figure 2 provides explanatory evidence: the trained Truster selects rollbacks (reverting to the initial prediction) approximately 50% more often than the LLM's own self-selection mechanism, suggesting that the base LLM is biased toward accepting its own refinements even when they degrade accuracy. The Truster, being a separate model without this bias, makes more objective comparisons and correctly identifies harmful refinements.

**StrategyQA (Table 3)** is the exception: the Truster provides negligible benefit. For LLaMA 70B pre-trained: Asker13B alone = 78.38; +Truster = 78.44 (+0.06). For ChatGPT Turbo: Asker13B alone = 75.76; +Truster = 75.86 (+0.10). The authors attribute this to the difficulty of ranking factual correctness without access to ground-truth facts—the Truster sees two reasoning chains that may differ in their factual premises, but without knowing which premises are true, it cannot reliably determine which chain is correct.

#### The Optimal Refinement Rate Is ~30–35%; Always Refining Hurts Performance

Figure 3 presents a sweep over the fraction of samples refined, showing the accuracy curve as the Asker's refinement trigger rate varies. For both ChatGPT and LLaMA 70B on GSM8K, the curve is non-monotonic:

- At 0% refinement (no samples refined), accuracy equals the initial prediction baseline (77.71 for ChatGPT, 59.74 for LLaMA 70B pre-trained).
- As the refinement percentage increases, accuracy rises, peaking at approximately 30–35% refinement.
- Beyond the peak, accuracy declines, and at 100% refinement (all samples refined), accuracy is **worse than 0% refinement**—the harm to initially-correct samples outweighs corrections to initially-incorrect ones.

This finding is consistent with Huang et al. (2023) and validates the central premise of selective refinement: the Asker's job is not to refine as many samples as possible but to identify the specific ~30–35% where refinement will help.

#### Cost Comparison: ART Is 5–15× Cheaper Than Fine-Tuning the Base LLM

Table 4 quantifies the training cost of the ART pipeline versus fine-tuning the base LLM on GSM8K:

- **Asker (7B):** 1.5 × 10^17 FLOPs, 1 GPU-hour
- **Truster (13B):** 3 × 10^17 FLOPs, 4 GPU-hours
- **Asker + Truster combined:** 5 GPU-hours
- **Fine-tuning LLaMA 70B:** 1.5 × 10^18 FLOPs, 75 GPU-hours (requiring 4 clusters of 8 × A100s)

The combined ART pipeline is 15× cheaper to train than fine-tuning the 70B model, while achieving comparable accuracy: fine-tuned LLaMA 70B achieves 63.2% on GSM8K (citing Yuan et al., 2023), while ART with pre-trained LLaMA 70B + Asker13B + Truster achieves 63.85% (Table 2). The paper notes that fine-tuning also incurs an additional cost: it reduces the model's general in-context learning performance on other tasks (Wang et al., 2022b), whereas ART keeps the base LLM frozen and preserves its generality.

#### Asker Trained on Base LLM Data Outperforms Asker Trained on Its Own Data

Table 5 compares training the Asker on predictions generated by the base LLM (LLaMA 70B) versus predictions generated by the Asker model itself:

- **Asker7B on LLaMA 70B:** Refinement accuracy 61.33 after Ask stage, trained on LLaMA 70B data; drops to 59.21 when trained on Asker7B's own predictions. The drop is +2.12 points in favor of training on the base LLM's distribution.

- **Asker13B on LLaMA 70B:** 62.74 on LLaMA 70B data vs. 62.39 on Asker13B's own data (+0.35 point advantage).

- **Asker7B on ChatGPT Turbo:** 80.89 on LLaMA 70B data vs. 80.69 on Asker7B's own data (+0.20 point)—the gap narrows because neither distribution perfectly matches ChatGPT's outputs.

- **Asker13B on ChatGPT Turbo:** 82.18 on LLaMA 70B data vs. 82.10 on Asker13B's own data (+0.08 point).

The clear takeaway: training the Asker on the same distribution it will evaluate at test time is beneficial. When the test-time LLM is the same as the data-generating LLM (LLaMA 70B), the gap is substantial. When the test-time LLM is different (ChatGPT), the gap shrinks because both training distributions are mismatched.

#### State-of-the-Art Mathematical Reasoning Models Make Better Askers

Table 6 compares LLaMA-based Askers against MetaMath-based Askers (Yu et al., 2023), which are LLaMA models further fine-tuned on 250K+ mathematical reasoning samples:

- **LLaMA 7B Asker:** Refinement accuracy 61.33, refines 48% of samples
- **MetaMath 7B Asker:** Accuracy 62.31 (+0.98), refines 35% of samples (−13 percentage points)
- **LLaMA 13B Asker:** Accuracy 62.74, refines 36% of samples
- **MetaMath 13B Asker:** Accuracy 64.06 (+1.32), refines 27% of samples (−9 percentage points)

Stronger mathematical reasoning capability yields both higher accuracy and greater selectivity—MetaMath models trigger refinement less often but on more appropriate samples. The reduction in refinement rate (from 48% to 35% for 7B, from 36% to 27% for 13B) suggests that better Askers have more confidence in their correctness judgments and fewer borderline cases where refinement is triggered out of uncertainty.

#### The All-in-One Pipeline Underperforms the Modular Approach

Figure 5 compares training a single LLaMA model (13B or 70B) to perform the entire ART pipeline in one sequence—ask subquestions, decide to refine, produce refinement, select output—against the modular approach and against standard fine-tuning:

- **LLaMA 13B:** Pre-trained accuracy is approximately 10–15% (read from figure). Fine-tuned reaches approximately 50%. All-in-one-go reaches approximately 40%, underperforming fine-tuning by ~10 points.
- **LLaMA 70B:** Pre-trained accuracy is approximately 60%. Fine-tuned reaches approximately 63%. All-in-one-go reaches approximately 57%, underperforming fine-tuning by ~6 points and falling below the pre-trained baseline as well.

The all-in-one approach is consistently worse than standard fine-tuning, and for the 70B model, it is even worse than the pre-trained baseline without any task-specific training. The authors attribute this to task complexity: "generating the entire sequence is a more challenging task for the LLM than individual components." This result validates the modular design of ART, where separate models handle distinct cognitive functions.

### Ablation Studies and Robustness Checks

**Asker with binary Yes/No decision only (without subquestions):** The paper reports that all versions of the LLaMA models trained to make only a binary refinement decision, without generating decomposable subquestions, "always trusted the predictions and never decided to refine them." This is a complete failure mode—refinement is never triggered, so the Asker provides zero benefit. The result is attributed to LLMs' tendency to "prefer their own predictions" (citing Kadavath et al., 2022). This ablation is not presented in a separate table but is discussed in Section 5 as the motivation for incorporating subquestions into the Asker's training and inference process.

**Self-selection vs. trained Truster (Figure 2):** The Truster model (LLaMA 13B) is compared against the base LLM's own self-selection mechanism on GSM8K. The trained Truster achieves higher accuracy than self-selection for both ChatGPT and LLaMA 70B, with the gap visibly larger for LLaMA 70B (Figure 2). The Truster selects rollbacks to the initial prediction approximately 50% more often than the self-selection version, indicating that it identifies more harmful refinements that the base LLM would have accepted.

**Refinement rate sweep (Figure 3):** As described in the main results, varying the fraction of samples refined from 0% to 100% reveals a non-monotonic accuracy curve for both ChatGPT and LLaMA 70B on GSM8K. The optimal rate is ~30–35%, and 100% refinement consistently underperforms 0% refinement. This is a robustness check on the premise that selective refinement is necessary—if the curve were monotonic (more refinement always better), the Asker's gating function would be unnecessary.

**Asker training data source (Table 5):** Training the Asker on the base LLM's predictions versus the Asker's own predictions is evaluated across model scales and base models. The finding that base-LLM-data training is better—with the gap diminishing when the test-time model differs from the training-data model—is robust and practically important for deployment decisions.

**MetaMath vs. LLaMA as Asker base (Table 6):** The substitution of MetaMath for LLaMA as the Asker fine-tuning base tests whether stronger mathematical reasoning capability improves refinement decisions. The answer is clearly yes, with both accuracy gains and improved selectivity (lower refinement trigger rates). This is a robustness check confirming that Asker quality scales with domain-specific reasoning capability, not just model size.

**All-in-one vs. modular pipeline (Figure 5):** This ablation tests whether the separate Asker and Truster models are necessary or whether a single model can handle the full pipeline. The all-in-one approach underperforms standard fine-tuning and sometimes the pre-trained baseline, confirming that task decomposition across specialized models is beneficial.

**Asker generalizes across base LLMs without retraining:** This is asserted in Section 1 and Section 6 but without detailed supporting tables. The claim is that "trained models (Asker and Truster) can work seamlessly across a wide range of LLMs (LLaMA 70B, ChatGPT, and GPT-4) without requiring additional modifications." The results in Tables 2 and 3 partially support this—the same Asker trained on LLaMA 70B data is evaluated with ChatGPT and GPT-4 as base generators—but there is no systematic cross-model generalization experiment where an Asker trained for one base model is tested on multiple others and compared to an Asker trained specifically for the test-time model.

**Temperature sweep for training data diversity:** The paper generates training predictions at five temperatures (0, 0.3, 0.4, 0.7, 0.8) but does not ablate the number of temperatures or the specific values. It is unclear whether fewer temperatures would suffice or whether the specific range is important for capturing diverse error types.

### Critical Assessment

**Claim 1: "ART achieves a performance gain of 5 points over self-refinement baselines."**

This claim is supported but requires careful contextualization. The 5-point gain appears in specific configurations—most notably ChatGPT Turbo + Asker13B + Truster achieves 82.64 versus the self-refinement baseline of 78.89 (a difference of +3.75 points against the paper's own Self implementation) or versus Huang et al.'s (2023) reported 75.10 (a difference of +7.54 points). The "5 points" figure from the abstract is best matched to the GPT-3.5-turbo-instruct results where Asker13B achieves 78.46 versus the Self best of 72.85 (+5.61). The claim is accurate but represents the upper end of observed gains rather than the typical gain across configurations—LLaMA 70B pre-trained sees a +3.81 point gain (64.24 vs. 60.43), and GPT-4 sees only +0.98 points (94.08 vs. 93.10). The paper would be stronger if it reported the distribution of gains across model configurations rather than highlighting the maximum.

**Claim 2: "A much smaller model (LLaMA 7B) can outperform a 10× larger model (LLaMA 70B) in a self-refinement setup."**

This is supported when comparing the 7B Asker's refinement decisions against the 70B model's self-refinement decisions: Asker7B achieves 61.33 versus Self-refinement at 60.43 (Table 2, LLaMA 70B pre-trained). However, this is a comparison of *different system architectures*, not the 7B model outperforming the 70B model at the same task. The 7B model is making a binary gating decision while the 70B model is generating reasoning chains—these are different tasks of different difficulty. The claim is technically true but could be misinterpreted as "the 7B model generates better answers than the 70B model," which is not the case. The 7B model decides *when the 70B model should refine*, which is a more constrained and trainable metacognitive task. The paper's framing in the abstract is slightly oversimplified relative to this nuance.

**Claim 3: "ART with smaller models is a cost-effective alternative to fine-tuning LLMs."**

This is well-supported by Table 4's cost comparison (5 vs. 75 GPU-hours, a 15× reduction) and the accuracy parity: ART with Asker13B + Truster + pre-trained LLaMA 70B achieves 63.85% (Table 2) versus fine-tuned LLaMA 70B at 63.2% (from Yuan et al., 2023). Two caveats weaken this claim slightly: (1) The fine-tuned 70B accuracy of 63.2% is cited from external work, not reproduced by the authors, so the comparison is not controlled within the same experimental setup. (2) The inference cost of ART is higher than a fine-tuned model because ART requires up to three model calls (initial prediction, Asker evaluation, possible refinement, Truster selection), whereas a fine-tuned model produces an answer in one call. The paper frames cost-effectiveness around *training* cost, but a complete accounting would include inference cost for deployment scenarios.

**Claim 4: "Asking questions before refinement is an effective way to verify generation quality."**

The strongest evidence for this claim is the ablation in Section 5: Asker models trained to make binary Yes/No decisions without subquestions fail completely, never triggering refinement. This is a stark and informative negative result. However, the evidence is qualitative ("all versions... always trusted the predictions and never decided to refine them") rather than quantitative—no table reports exact refinement rates or accuracy for the subquestion-free Asker, making it difficult to assess whether the failure is truly complete or simply a severe degradation. The qualitative example in Figure 4 provides a compelling illustration of how subquestions guide the refinement toward specific corrections, but a single example cannot establish general effectiveness.

**Genuine weaknesses in the experimental design:**

- **No cross-validation or statistical testing.** The paper reports single-run accuracies on fixed test splits with no confidence intervals, error bars, or significance tests. Many of the gains attributed to the Truster are small (0.2–1.4 points on GSM8K), and on a test set of 1,319 examples, these differences may fall within the range of sampling noise. The absence of statistical rigor makes it difficult to determine which specific gains are reliable.

- **Single metric (accuracy) throughout.** The paper reports only final-answer accuracy. There is no analysis of *where* the Asker makes correct versus incorrect gating decisions, no breakdown of precision/recall for the refinement trigger, and no analysis of error types (does ART fix arithmetic errors better than logical errors? Does it help more on problems requiring many steps versus few steps?). This limits insight into the mechanism's strengths and weaknesses.

- **No oracle upper-bound analysis.** A natural diagnostic experiment would be: "If the Asker had perfect knowledge of which samples needed refinement (oracle gating), how high would accuracy go?" This would decompose the performance gap into (a) the ceiling achievable by selective refinement and (b) the gap between the trained Asker and the oracle, revealing how much headroom remains. This experiment is not reported.

- **StrategyQA Truster results show no benefit.** Table 3 shows the Truster adding 0.06–0.10 points on StrategyQA, essentially zero. The paper attributes this to the difficulty of ranking factual claims without access to ground-truth facts, which is a plausible explanation. But it also means that the Truster component of ART provides no value on fact-based QA tasks—the pipeline reduces to Ask + Refine only. This is an important boundary condition that the paper acknowledges but does not explore deeply.

- **No ablation of Truster model size.** The Truster is always LLaMA 13B. It is unknown whether a 7B Truster would perform similarly (reducing cost further) or whether a larger Truster would provide meaningful gains. Given that the Truster gains are already small relative to the Asker gains, understanding whether Truster performance scales with model size would inform cost-allocation decisions.

- **Training data sizes are not fully justified.** Table 1 reports 35,000 Asker training samples for GSM8K and 9,000 for StrategyQA, but the derivation of these numbers is opaque. Five predictions per training example × 7,473 examples = 37,365, yet only 35,000 are used. The paper does not explain the discrepancy or whether smaller training sets would suffice. Similarly, the Truster training set (15,000 pairs for GSM8K) comes from filtering examples with both correct and incorrect predictions, but the paper does not report how many examples met this criterion or how many pairs were generated per qualifying example.

- **Cross-model generalization claim is undersupported.** The assertion that Askers and Trusters "work seamlessly across a wide range of LLMs" is mentioned in the abstract and Section 1 but not substantiated with a systematic experiment. The results in Tables 2 and 3 show ART working with different base models, but the Asker in each case was trained on LLaMA 70B data (not, for example, an Asker trained on LLaMA 70B data being tested with ChatGPT and GPT-4 and compared to an Asker trained specifically for each). A proper cross-model generalization experiment would hold the Asker fixed and vary only the base LLM, reporting accuracy for each pair.

**Missing experiments that would strengthen the paper:**

- **Ablation of the number of subquestions.** Does the Asker's performance depend on how many subquestions it generates? Would a fixed set of subquestions per problem type work as well as generated ones?

- **Confusion matrix for Asker decisions.** How often does the Asker correctly identify incorrect predictions (true positive for refinement)? How often does it incorrectly flag correct predictions (false positive, causing unnecessary and potentially harmful refinement)? Understanding these error rates would clarify which part of the Asker's behavior drives the accuracy gains.

- **Iterative refinement.** The paper performs only single-pass refinement. Would allowing the Asker to re-evaluate the refined output and trigger a second refinement pass help? The all-in-one ablation (Figure 5) suggests multi-step sequences are challenging, but a controlled experiment with multiple modular passes would clarify whether the single-pass design is optimal or a limitation.

- **Latency measurement.** The paper discusses cost in GPU-hours for training but never reports inference latency. An ART pipeline requires up to three serial LLM calls (initial generation → Asker → possible refinement → Truster), which each add latency. For interactive applications, the wall-clock time of refinement may be more important than the training cost.

- **Comparison against simple baselines like "refine if the initial answer has low confidence."** If the base LLM provides token-level probabilities, one could imagine a simpler gating mechanism: refine only when the model's average token probability on the final answer is below a threshold. Comparing ART against such a confidence-based baseline would clarify whether the trained Asker provides value beyond what a simple uncertainty heuristic could capture.

## 6. Limitations and Trade-offs

### The Asker Model Requires Ground-Truth Subquestions for Training and Inference

**The assumption or constraint.** ART critically depends on the availability of decomposable subquestions—both for training the Asker and for guiding refinement at inference time. The paper uses subquestions that are provided with the GSM8K and StrategyQA datasets, which were constructed by human annotators or dataset creators as part of the benchmark design. For GSM8K, the dataset "consists of sub-questions that correspond to the steps in a given correct solution" (Section 4.1); for StrategyQA, "each question is accompanied by its decomposed questions and the correct factual knowledge required to answer it" (Section 4.1). The paper acknowledges this dependency in the Limitations section:

> "In this work, we trained a Asker to make a refinement decision by asking questions to verify the predictions. We used the training data available for the GSM8K and StrategyQA datasets. However, for many tasks, training data may not be available." (Section 7, Limitations)

**The consequence.** The Asker cannot function without subquestions—as demonstrated by the ablation in Section 5, removing subquestions from the Asker's training and inference causes it to fail completely, never triggering refinement. This means ART as presented is not deployable on arbitrary reasoning tasks. For any new domain or dataset where decomposable subquestions are not already available, a practitioner would need to either (a) generate them using an LLM (which the paper notes is possible but does not evaluate within ART) or (b) have human annotators produce them (which is expensive and time-consuming). The entire pipeline—Asker training, refinement triggering, and refinement guidance—assumes these subquestions exist and are of reasonable quality. If the generated or annotated subquestions are incomplete, ambiguous, or misaligned with the actual error patterns, the Asker's verification would be unreliable and the refinement prompts would target the wrong aspects of the problem.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not evaluate ART with LLM-generated subquestions, despite noting that "LLMs can be used to generate the subquestions and then distill them into smaller models that perform similarly to the ground truth (Magister et al., 2023)" (Section 2). This means there is zero evidence in the paper about how ART performs when subquestions come from an imperfect source—a scenario that would be the norm for any deployment beyond the two benchmark datasets studied. The paper also does not ablate subquestion quality (e.g., by introducing noise, removing some subquestions, or using deliberately incomplete decompositions) to test how sensitive the Asker's decisions are to the specific subquestions used.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not address this limitation experimentally. It acknowledges it in the Limitations section and gestures toward future work: "LLMs can be used to generate data and in many cases it performs similarly to the ground truth data (Magister et al., 2023). However, we have not tested this with ART due to the availability of the training dataset." This is a significant gap—until ART is demonstrated to work with automatically generated subquestions, its applicability is restricted to the narrow set of benchmarks that already include human-authored decompositions.

---

### The Truster Provides No Benefit on Factual Reasoning Tasks

**The assumption or constraint.** The Truster is trained to rank reasoning chains based on their internal coherence and correctness, without access to the ground-truth facts that would disambiguate which chain is factually accurate. This assumes that incorrect reasoning leaves detectable traces in the reasoning text—arithmetic errors, logical inconsistencies, or missing steps—that a trained model can identify without knowing the true facts. This assumption holds for mathematical reasoning (GSM8K), where the correctness of each computation can be verified from the reasoning text itself, but breaks down for open-domain factual reasoning (StrategyQA).

**The consequence.** On StrategyQA, the Truster adds essentially zero value. Table 3 shows that Asker13B alone achieves 78.38 on LLaMA 70B pre-trained, while adding the Truster yields 78.44—a negligible +0.06 point gain. For ChatGPT Turbo, Asker13B alone achieves 75.76, and with the Truster it reaches 75.86—a +0.10 point gain. The paper explains this directly:

> "This shows that it is difficult to train a Truster on fact-based datasets, as it is hard to rank two pieces of factual information without knowing the true facts." (Section 4.3)

The Truster is unable to distinguish between two reasoning chains that differ in their factual premises (e.g., one assumes greyhounds are dogs, another assumes they are birds) because both chains may be internally coherent. Without external knowledge of which premise is true, the ranking task reduces to stylistic preference or guesswork. This means that for a broad class of reasoning tasks where errors stem from incorrect factual recall rather than incorrect logical manipulation, the Truster component of ART is vestigial—it adds complexity and inference cost without improving accuracy. The pipeline effectively reduces to Ask + Refine only, with the refinement blindly accepted whenever the Asker triggers it.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** Table 3 is the sole evidence. The Truster training set for StrategyQA is only 2,300 pairs (Table 1), compared to 15,000 for GSM8K, which may partially explain the weak performance—the model may not have seen enough diverse error patterns to learn useful ranking signals. However, the paper argues this is an inherent limitation of the domain, not a sample size issue: factual errors are latent in the choice of premises, not visible in the reasoning structure.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not attempt to mitigate this limitation. It acknowledges it only briefly when presenting the StrategyQA results and in the Limitations section, where it notes that for StrategyQA, "we used the available facts to support the model decision when refining the predictions. These facts were available in the dataset, but in the real world can be extracted with the help of some tools or from some databases. We did not test this approach in our work and leave it for future work." A potential mitigation—providing the Truster with access to retrieved factual knowledge during inference, mirroring what is done for the refinement step—is suggested but not implemented or evaluated.

---

### Training Data Generation Cost Is Unmeasured and Potentially Substantial

**The assumption or constraint.** The paper frames ART as a cost-effective alternative to fine-tuning LLMs, with Table 4 showing that training the Asker (1 GPU-hour) and Truster (4 GPU-hours) is 15× cheaper than fine-tuning LLaMA 70B (75 GPU-hours). However, this comparison omits a critical cost: generating the training data for the Asker and Truster requires running the frozen base LLM ψ on the entire training set with k = 5 diverse samples per example. For GSM8K with 7,473 training examples, this means approximately 37,365 inference calls to the base LLM—and for the paper's headline results, this base LLM is often LLaMA 70B, ChatGPT, or GPT-4. The cost of these inference calls (compute for LLaMA 70B, API costs for ChatGPT/GPT-4) is not included in the GPU-hour comparison in Table 4, which reports only the cost of fine-tuning the Asker and Truster after the training data has been generated.

**The consequence.** The true cost of deploying ART on a new task includes: (1) generating k = 5 predictions per training example from the (potentially very large) base LLM, (2) labeling each prediction with correctness (which requires ground-truth answers), (3) training the Asker on this labeled data, (4) constructing correct-incorrect pairs for Truster training (which requires examples with both correct and incorrect predictions—a filtering step that discards data and may require generating more than 5 predictions per example to get sufficient pairs), and (5) training the Truster. For a base model accessed via API (ChatGPT, GPT-4), step 1 alone could cost hundreds of dollars in API credits—a cost that may exceed the GPU-hours for training the smaller Asker/Truster models, especially for larger training sets. For the LLaMA 70B base model, step 1 requires running 70B-parameter inference 37,365 times, which is a non-trivial compute expenditure that the paper does not quantify. The headline "15× cheaper than fine-tuning" comparison is therefore misleading—it compares only the final training step, not the full pipeline cost including data generation.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper provides no cost breakdown for data generation. Table 1 reports dataset sizes (35,000 Asker samples, 15,000 Truster pairs for GSM8K), and Section 3.2 describes the k = 5 temperature sweep used to generate them, but the compute or dollar cost of this generation step is never stated. The inference cost of the base LLM during data generation is implicitly treated as zero or negligible, which is not defensible when the base model is a 70B-parameter LLM or a commercial API.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not acknowledge this cost omission. A fair accounting would report the total FLOPs or GPU-hours for data generation alongside the training costs in Table 4, or would explicitly distinguish one-time data generation costs (amortized over all downstream uses) from per-deployment training costs. For API-based models, a dollar-cost estimate would be appropriate.

---

### The Method Is Evaluated on Only Two Benchmarks with a Single Model Architecture Family

**The assumption or constraint.** All experiments use two datasets (GSM8K and StrategyQA) and train the Asker and Truster exclusively from the LLaMA model family (7B, 13B, 70B, with MetaMath as a fine-tuned variant). The base LLMs acting as generators and refiners span LLaMA 70B, ChatGPT, and GPT-4, but the decision-making models (Asker, Truster) are always LLaMA-derived. The paper makes a general claim in its abstract:

> "On two multistep reasoning tasks of mathematical word problems (GSM8K) and question answering (StrategyQA), ART achieves a performance gain of 5 points over self-refinement baselines."

The implication is that ART is effective on reasoning tasks broadly, but the evidential basis is narrow.

**The consequence.** Several important generalization questions are unanswered. First, would ART work on other reasoning domains—code generation, logical deduction, scientific reasoning, multi-step planning—where the structure of subquestions and the nature of errors may differ from arithmetic word problems and binary factual QA? Second, would ART work when the Asker and Truster are from a different model family than the base LLM (e.g., a Mistral-7B Asker guiding a LLaMA 70B generator), or is there something about the LLaMA family's training data or tokenizer that makes the cross-model transfer work? The paper claims that Askers and Trusters "can work seamlessly across a wide range of LLMs (LLaMA 70B, ChatGPT and GPT-4) without requiring additional modifications" (Section 1), but this is only tested within the LLaMA family for the decision-making models—the Asker is always a LLaMA variant, evaluated with different base generators. It is not tested whether a non-LLaMA Asker (e.g., Mistral, Falcon) would generalize similarly.

Third, both GSM8K and StrategyQA have relatively short reasoning chains (2–8 steps for GSM8K, typically 2–3 hops for StrategyQA). It is unclear whether ART's subquestion-based verification scales to problems requiring dozens of reasoning steps, where the Asker would need to generate and verify many more subquestions, increasing both the risk of missing a critical subquestion and the inference cost.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper presents no cross-domain experiments beyond GSM8K and StrategyQA. The cross-model generalization claim is supported only by the fact that the same LLaMA-based Asker is evaluated with ChatGPT and GPT-4 as base generators (Tables 2 and 3), but this tests whether the Asker's training data distribution (LLaMA 70B predictions) transfers to other generators' output distributions—it does not test whether the Asker architecture itself generalizes across model families. Table 5 partially addresses the distribution shift question by showing that Asker performance drops when trained on its own data versus the base LLM's data, but this is within the LLaMA family.

**Mitigation status.** The paper does not claim to have tested broader generalization and does not suggest that its results necessarily transfer to other domains. The limitation is an absence of evidence rather than a false claim. However, the abstract and introduction language ("two multistep reasoning tasks") is appropriately scoped. The lack of diversity in benchmarks and model families is a standard limitation of conference-scale papers and is honestly represented.

### Single-Pass Refinement Leaves Potential Gains Unexplored and May Underperform on Complex Errors

**The assumption or constraint.** ART performs exactly one refinement pass when the Asker triggers it. If the refined output `y_ref` is still incorrect, there is no mechanism to detect this and attempt a second refinement. The pipeline is strictly: initial prediction → Asker evaluation → (if triggered) single refinement → Truster selection → final answer. The paper does not explicitly justify this design choice, but it is consistent with the computational budget framing—each additional refinement pass would add another inference call to the (potentially large) base LLM, and the risk of compounding errors across multiple passes is a known failure mode in iterative self-refinement (Huang et al., 2023).

**The consequence.** For problems where the initial error is complex—involving multiple interacting mistakes or requiring a fundamentally different approach rather than a targeted patch—a single refinement pass may be insufficient. The Asker's subquestions guide the refinement toward fixing specific missing or incorrect steps, but if the refinement itself introduces new errors (which happens in some fraction of cases, motivating the Truster), there is no second chance. The Truster can roll back to the initial prediction, recovering the initial accuracy, but it cannot trigger a re-refinement of the (still-incorrect) initial answer. This means ART's accuracy ceiling is bounded by what a single guided refinement can achieve, which may be lower than what iterative refinement with appropriate gating could reach.

The all-in-one ablation in Figure 5 provides indirect evidence that multi-step refinement is challenging: a single model trained to perform the full ask-refine-trust sequence in one pass underperforms standard fine-tuning. However, this does not directly test whether a modular pipeline with multiple sequential refinement passes (each gated by the Asker re-evaluating the latest output) would help or hurt. It is possible that allowing a second refinement pass on samples where the Truster selected the initial prediction over the refinement (indicating the refinement was harmful) could recover some of those cases with a differently-guided second attempt.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** None. The paper does not experiment with multi-pass refinement, does not report how often the refined output is still incorrect after a single pass, and does not compare single-pass against multi-pass variants. The only multi-step result is the negative all-in-one finding (Figure 5), which tests a different hypothesis (unified model vs. modular pipeline) and does not inform the question of whether modular iterative refinement would help.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper treats single-pass refinement as a fixed architectural choice without discussion of alternatives or acknowledgment of the potential ceiling it imposes. Given that the paper's central contribution is the gating mechanism (Asker) and selection mechanism (Truster), extending these to support iterative refinement—where the Asker re-evaluates `y_ref` and potentially triggers a second pass, and the Truster selects among all candidates generated—would be a natural extension but is left entirely to future work.

### The Refinement Mechanism Inherits All Limitations of the Frozen Base LLM

**The assumption or constraint.** ART keeps the base LLM ψ completely frozen—it is never fine-tuned, never updated, and never given task-specific training. The refinement step consists solely of prompting ψ with the original problem and the Asker's subquestions (plus factual knowledge for StrategyQA). This means that the quality of the refinement is entirely bounded by the base LLM's capability to produce correct reasoning when given appropriate prompts. If the base LLM fundamentally cannot solve a certain class of problems—even with step-by-step guidance—ART provides no path to success.

**The consequence.** ART cannot correct errors that stem from the base LLM lacking the necessary knowledge or reasoning capability. The qualitative example in Figure 4 shows a case where the error was a simple omission (forgetting to subtract the rewards), which the base LLM can fix when prompted with the right subquestions. But for errors that reflect deeper misunderstandings—e.g., consistently applying the wrong formula, misunderstanding a physical principle, or lacking factual knowledge not provided in the prompt—no amount of subquestion-guided refinement will produce a correct answer. The Asker might correctly identify that the answer is wrong and trigger refinement, and the Truster might correctly identify that the refinement is also wrong, but the system has no mechanism to go beyond what the base LLM can generate.

This is a fundamental bound that the paper does not explicitly characterize. The FLOPs-matched comparison literature (e.g., Snell et al., 2024 on test-time compute scaling) has established that inference-time strategies cannot compensate for capability gaps that only larger-scale pretraining can address. ART is subject to the same bound: for problems in the base model's "difficulty bin 5" (where pass@1 is near zero), no refinement strategy will help because there are effectively no correct reasoning paths in the model's output distribution to discover. The paper does not analyze ART's performance stratified by problem difficulty or by the base model's initial accuracy on different problem types, so the boundary of this limitation is unmeasured.

**What evidence exists in the paper.** The paper does not directly measure this limitation, but the results in Table 2 provide suggestive evidence. For GPT-4 (initial accuracy 91.88), ART's gains are small (+2.20 points to 94.08 at best), because most errors occur on problems near the frontier of GPT-4's capability—problems where even guided refinement may not bridge the gap. For ChatGPT Turbo (initial accuracy 77.71), ART achieves larger gains (+4.93 points to 82.64), because more errors fall into the "fixable with guidance" category. The paper does not break down performance by problem difficulty or by error type, so this pattern remains suggestive rather than conclusive.

**Mitigation status.** Not addressed. The paper does not discuss the capability boundary of the base LLM as a limiting factor for ART, nor does it analyze which types of errors ART can versus cannot fix. This is a significant omission for practitioners, because it means there is no guidance on when ART is likely to help versus when resources would be better spent on a stronger base model or on fine-tuning.


## 7. Implications and Future Directions

### How This Work Changes the Landscape

This paper is best understood as a **reframing of the self-refinement problem** rather than a paradigm shift. It does not introduce a new refinement mechanism—the base LLM's refinement procedure is standard subquestion-guided prompting—nor does it claim that refinement is now universally reliable. Instead, it makes a diagnostic contribution with practical consequences: it demonstrates that the bottleneck in prior self-refinement work was not the *refinement* step itself but the *decision to invoke it* and the *decision to trust the result*. By decoupling these metacognitive functions from the generator and assigning them to separately trained, much smaller models, ART achieves reliability that prompting-based self-evaluation could not.

The conceptual shift is from "LLMs cannot self-correct reasoning" (the conclusion of Huang et al., 2023) to **"LLMs cannot self-evaluate reasoning, but they can be corrected when evaluation is performed by a different, trained model."** This is not a negation of prior negative results—it is a more precise diagnosis of *where* the failure occurs. The paper's ablation evidence (Section 5) is decisive on this point: removing subquestions from the Asker causes it to never trigger refinement, confirming that the same model asked to both generate and evaluate its output suffers from a confirmation bias that structured decomposition can overcome, but only when the evaluator is distinct from the generator.

The reconciliation of conflicting prior findings follows naturally from this diagnosis. Madaan et al. (2023) found self-refinement effective for dialogue and sentiment tasks but less effective for mathematical reasoning—likely because dialogue quality judgments are more holistic and less demanding of step-by-step verification, making the LLM's self-evaluation somewhat more reliable in those domains. Huang et al. (2023) found self-correction ineffective for reasoning—exactly what the ART framework predicts, because reasoning evaluation requires the kind of step-by-step decomposition that untrained self-prompting cannot reliably perform. Shridhar et al. (2023a) found that a modular prompting framework with selection could help, but only modestly—because the selection was still performed by the same LLM via prompting, which ART shows is biased toward accepting its own refinements. ART's contribution is therefore not to overturn prior findings but to provide the **missing mechanism** (trained, independent evaluators) that makes the modular vision of Shridhar et al. (2023a) actually work reliably.

The paper also shifts the cost calculus around LLM deployment in a specific but important way. By quantifying that training a 7B Asker costs 1 GPU-hour versus 75 GPU-hours for fine-tuning LLaMA 70B (Table 4), while achieving comparable or better accuracy (63.85% vs. 63.2% on GSM8K), the paper makes a concrete economic argument: **for tasks where training data with ground-truth decompositions exists, keeping the large model frozen and training small metacognitive models is more cost-effective than fine-tuning**. This is not a universal principle—the paper does not claim that small models can replace large models for generation—but it carves out a specific niche where the cost asymmetry is compelling. The downstream effect is to make **asymmetric model architectures** (large frozen generator + small trained decision-makers) an attractive design pattern for reasoning systems, not just a theoretical curiosity.

Research directions that become more attractive after this work:
- **Training specialized evaluator models** for other cognitive functions where LLMs show poor self-awareness (hallucination detection, uncertainty calibration, planning feasibility assessment).
- **Cost-aware architecture design** that explicitly allocates model capacity to different cognitive functions based on the complexity of each function, rather than using a single large model for everything.
- **Decomposition-based verification** as a general strategy, where evaluation is broken into atomic checks rather than performed holistically—this echoes the chain-of-verification approach (Dhuliawala et al., 2023) but with trained rather than prompted verifiers.
- **Cross-model transfer of metacognitive models**, where an Asker trained on one base model's outputs generalizes to other base models (which the paper claims but does not thoroughly validate).

Research directions that become less attractive:
- **Prompting-only self-refinement for reasoning tasks.** ART's results, combined with the ablation showing the complete failure of subquestion-free evaluation, suggest that prompting a model to "check your work" is a fundamentally limited approach for multi-step reasoning. Resources are better spent on training separate evaluators.
- **Uniform refinement strategies** that refine every sample or use fixed heuristics. ART's Figure 3 shows that the optimal refinement rate is ~30–35% and that 100% refinement is worse than 0%—any approach that does not adaptively gate refinement is leaving performance on the table.

### Follow-Up Research This Work Enables

**1. Oracle-gating upper bound to decompose Asker error into selection quality vs. refinement quality.** The paper never establishes how much room remains above the trained Asker. A critical experiment: on the GSM8K test set, compute accuracy under *oracle* gating, where refinement is triggered only for samples whose initial prediction is incorrect and the refined output is correct. This would measure the ceiling achievable by perfect selective refinement. The gap between the oracle ceiling and ART's Asker accuracy would reveal how much of the remaining error is due to the Asker making suboptimal gating decisions (triggering refinement on correct answers, or failing to trigger on fixable incorrect ones) versus the base LLM being unable to produce correct refinements even with perfect guidance. If the oracle ceiling is, say, 75% on LLaMA 70B versus ART's 64%, then improving the Asker is the priority; if the ceiling is 68%, then improving the base LLM's refinement capability (or switching to a stronger base model) is the bottleneck. This decomposition is essential for allocating future research effort but is absent from the paper.

**2. Sensitivity analysis of subquestion quality and completeness.** ART's dependence on ground-truth subquestions is its most significant deployment barrier. A controlled degradation experiment would characterize this dependency: take the GSM8K subquestions and systematically degrade them—drop random subquestions, introduce vague or misleading subquestions, replace human-authored subquestions with LLM-generated ones from models of varying capability (LLaMA 7B, ChatGPT, GPT-4)—and measure how Asker accuracy and final ART accuracy degrade as a function of subquestion quality. This would establish the *minimum viable subquestion quality* for ART to provide benefits over self-refinement, which is essential information for anyone considering deploying ART on a new domain where only imperfect subquestions are available. The paper's citation of Magister et al. (2023) as evidence that LLM-generated subquestions "perform similarly to the ground truth" is insufficient—that work studied distillation for generation, not verification, and the demands on subquestion precision may be different for the Asker's checklist-style evaluation.

**3. Iterative refinement with re-evaluation gating.** The paper performs exactly one refinement pass. A natural extension: after the base LLM produces `y_ref`, send `y_ref` back to the Asker for re-evaluation. If the Asker determines that `y_ref` is still incorrect (by asking subquestions against the refined reasoning), trigger a second refinement pass with a new set of subquestions targeting the remaining errors. Repeat until the Asker approves or a maximum number of iterations is reached, then use the Truster to select among all generated candidates. This would test whether the gains from ART are bounded by single-pass refinement or whether iterative refinement can push accuracy closer to the oracle ceiling. The all-in-one ablation (Figure 5) suggests multi-step sequences are challenging for a single model, but a modular iterative pipeline with separate Asker calls at each step might avoid the compounding-error problem. The key measurement would be accuracy as a function of the number of refinement passes, stratified by initial prediction correctness, to see whether iterative refinement primarily fixes initially-incorrect answers or degrades initially-correct ones.

**4. Training the Asker on error-type annotations rather than binary correct/incorrect labels.** The current Asker training data uses a binary label: "Yes" (the final answer is wrong, refine) or "No" (the final answer is right, don't refine). This teaches the Asker to detect *that* an error exists but not *what kind* of error—arithmetic miscalculation, missing step, incorrect formula application, misreading of the problem statement. A richer training signal would annotate each incorrect prediction with the specific error type(s), and train the Asker to generate both the error-type diagnosis and the corresponding subquestions. This could improve the Asker's subquestion generation by making it more targeted: a missing-step error requires different subquestions than a wrong-formula error. The experiment would compare ART with error-type-aware Asker training against the current binary-label training, measuring both refinement trigger accuracy and the quality of generated subquestions (judged by whether downstream refinement using those subquestions produces correct answers).

**5. Cross-model generalization stress test with held-out base model architectures.** The paper claims that Askers and Trusters "can work seamlessly across a wide range of LLMs" but tests this only with LLaMA-based Askers evaluated against LLaMA 70B, ChatGPT, and GPT-4 as base generators. A rigorous generalization experiment would: (a) train an Asker exclusively on LLaMA 70B predictions, (b) evaluate it with a held-out base model from a different family (e.g., Mistral, Falcon, or Qwen) on the same task, (c) compare against an Asker trained specifically on that held-out model's predictions. The generalization ratio—(accuracy with LLaMA-trained Asker) / (accuracy with model-specific Asker)—would quantify how much performance is lost to distribution shift. Table 5 provides a partial answer within the LLaMA family (Asker trained on LLaMA 70B vs. its own data), but a cross-family test is needed to support the "seamlessly" claim. If the ratio is close to 1.0, ART's cost advantages are amplified because one Asker can serve many base models; if the ratio is substantially below 1.0, per-model Asker training is necessary, increasing deployment cost.

**6. Confidence-based refinement gating as a simple baseline comparison.** The paper compares ART against prompting-based self-refinement but not against a natural lightweight alternative: use the base LLM's token-level confidence (sequence log-probability or average token probability on the final answer) to decide whether to refine, with a threshold tuned on a validation set. This baseline would establish whether the Asker's complex subquestion-based verification provides value beyond a simple uncertainty signal that any LLM can produce without additional training. If confidence-based gating achieves, say, 75% of ART's gains at zero training cost, the cost-benefit calculus shifts—ART would only be preferred when the remaining gap justifies the training investment. The experiment is straightforward: on GSM8K, extract the base LLM's log-probability for the final answer tokens, sweep a threshold from low to high confidence, and measure the accuracy of "refine if confidence < threshold" against ART's accuracy at each operating point on the refinement rate spectrum (extending Figure 3 with a confidence-based curve).

### Practical Applications and Downstream Use Cases

**1. Cost-efficient deployment of large LLMs on reasoning tasks with training budgets.** For organizations that have access to a powerful frozen LLM (via API or open weights) and a small training budget (a few GPU-hours), ART provides a concrete recipe for improving task accuracy without fine-tuning the large model. The paper's numbers: training Asker (7B) + Truster (13B) costs 5 GPU-hours total (Table 4) and lifts pre-trained LLaMA 70B's GSM8K accuracy from 59.74% to 61.94% (+2.20 points), or ChatGPT Turbo from 77.71% to 81.14% (+3.43 points with Asker7B + Truster). The training data requirement—5 predictions per training example from the base LLM—is manageable for datasets with a few thousand examples. A company with a 70B-parameter model serving math tutoring features could deploy ART by spending 5 GPU-hours on Asker/Truster training rather than 75 GPU-hours on fine-tuning, while achieving comparable or better accuracy and preserving the base model's general capabilities. The primary prerequisite is the availability of decomposable subquestions for the target task, which may require a one-time human annotation investment or LLM-generation-and-verification pipeline.

**2. Selective refinement in interactive AI assistants for reasoning-heavy domains.** In tutoring systems, coding assistants, or decision-support tools where users ask multi-step reasoning questions, ART offers a deployable strategy for second-pass correction without the latency and cost of always running refinement. The paper's Figure 3 shows that the optimal refinement rate is ~30–35% of queries—meaning ART would invoke the expensive refinement call (a full extra generation from the large base LLM) on only one-third of user queries, while still capturing most of the benefit of refinement. The Asker, being a 7B or 13B model, runs quickly and cheaply compared to the 70B+ generator. In a production setting, this translates to: user asks question → 70B model generates initial answer quickly → 7B Asker evaluates answer in a fraction of a second → only ~30% of the time, the 70B model is called again for refinement. The remaining 70% of queries get the initial answer with zero additional latency. This is a practical realization of the "think fast, think slow" paradigm applied to LLM inference, where the system expends additional computation only when the fast answer is flagged as potentially incorrect.

**3. Self-improvement data generation pipelines with quality control.** ART can serve as a filtering mechanism in LLM self-improvement loops (e.g., STaR, ReST$^{EM}$, rejection sampling fine-tuning), where a model generates its own training data by producing reasoning chains and selecting the correct ones. The standard approach is to keep only chains whose final answer matches the ground truth, but this discards chains with correct reasoning that happened to reach a wrong final answer, and keeps chains with flawed reasoning that coincidentally reached the right answer. ART's Asker, trained to evaluate reasoning chain quality via subquestion verification rather than final-answer matching, could serve as a more discerning filter: keep chains where the Asker determines all subquestions are answered correctly (regardless of final answer), and discard or refine chains where subquestions reveal errors. This would produce higher-quality training data for the next iteration of fine-tuning, potentially improving the efficiency of self-improvement loops. The paper's finding that better mathematical reasoning ability in the Asker (MetaMath vs. LLaMA, Table 6) improves refinement decisions suggests that a well-trained Asker generalizes its verification capability, though this would need to be tested in a closed-loop self-improvement setting.

**4. Multi-model systems with role specialization for high-stakes reasoning.** For applications where accuracy is paramount and cost is secondary—medical decision support, legal reasoning, financial analysis—ART points toward a deployment architecture where different models handle different cognitive roles: a very large model (GPT-4 class) generates initial reasoning and performs refinement, while a separately trained verification model (potentially also large, but optimized for evaluation rather than generation) asks targeted subquestions and triggers refinement when needed, and a third ranking model selects the best candidate. The paper demonstrates this architecture at a modest scale (LLaMA 7B/13B decision-makers + LLaMA 70B/ChatGPT/GPT-4 generator), but the pattern scales: if a GPT-4-class model achieves 92% accuracy, and ART-style verification and selective refinement adds 2 points to reach 94% (as shown for GPT-4 in Table 2), that 2-point gain in a high-stakes domain could be worth the additional inference cost of the Asker and refinement passes. The paper's finding that Askers generalize across base models without retraining (claimed in Section 1) would be particularly valuable here: the same verification model could serve multiple different generator models, amortizing its training cost.

### When to Prefer This Method

The paper explicitly positions ART against two alternatives: **self-refinement via prompting** (the same LLM evaluates and refines its own output) and **fine-tuning the base LLM**. The decision rules below follow directly from the paper's empirical findings and cost analysis.

- **Prefer ART over prompting-based self-refinement when:** (1) the task involves multi-step reasoning where errors are localized to specific steps (rather than holistic quality issues), (2) decomposable subquestions are available or can be generated for the task, (3) the base LLM is large and expensive enough that its self-evaluation is unreliable (the paper shows this for LLaMA 70B and ChatGPT), and (4) a small training budget (a few GPU-hours) is available for training the Asker and Truster. The paper's evidence: ART with Asker7B outperforms LLaMA 70B self-refinement by 1.5 points (61.33 vs. 59.83, Table 2); ART with Asker13B outperforms ChatGPT Turbo self-refinement by 3.3 points (82.18 vs. 78.89).

- **Prefer ART over fine-tuning the base LLM when:** (1) the base LLM is very large (70B+ parameters) making fine-tuning expensive (75+ GPU-hours per Table 4), (2) preserving the base model's general in-context learning capabilities is important (fine-tuning can cause specialization at the expense of generality, per Wang et al., 2022b), (3) the same base LLM will be used for multiple tasks where different Askers could be plugged in without modifying the base model, and (4) the accuracy target is achievable with selective refinement rather than requiring full model adaptation. The paper's evidence: ART with Asker13B + Truster + pre-trained LLaMA 70B achieves 63.85%, comparable to fine-tuned LLaMA 70B at 63.2% (Yuan et al., 2023), while costing 15× less in training GPU-hours.

- **Prefer fine-tuning the base LLM over ART when:** (1) the training budget is large enough to fine-tune the base model without concern, (2) the task requires learning fundamentally new knowledge or capabilities that the base LLM lacks (since ART cannot add new knowledge—it only improves the deployment of existing capability), (3) inference latency is critical and the additional Asker/Truster/refinement calls would exceed acceptable response times, or (4) decomposable subquestions are not available and cannot be generated with sufficient quality. The paper does not explicitly evaluate these tradeoffs, but they follow from ART's architecture: frozen base LLM means no new knowledge acquisition, and serial model calls mean higher latency per query.

- **Prefer self-refinement via prompting only when:** the task does not justify any training investment and the domain is one where LLM self-evaluation has been shown to work adequately (e.g., dialogue response quality, sentiment modification). The paper's results suggest this is a narrow set of use cases for reasoning tasks, where self-refinement gains are marginal at best (0.6–1.2 points in Table 2) and sometimes negative.
